# Already Perishing: The Grammar That Ends a World

## The Ayah

**وَلَا تَدْعُ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا ءَاخَرَ ۘ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۚ كُلُّ شَىْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُۥ ۚ لَهُ ٱلْحُكْمُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ**

*Wa lā tadʿu maʿa-llāhi ilāhan ākhar. Lā ilāha illā huwa. Kullu shayʾin hālikun illā wajhah. Lahu al-ḥukmu wa ilayhi turjaʿūn.*

> And do not call upon any other god alongside Allah. There is no god but He. Every single thing is perishing except His Face. To Him belongs the decree, and to Him you will be returned.

---

## Introduction — The World Before This Sentence

Before we open this ayah, we have to walk through the surah that produced it. Because what Allah is about to say at the end will only land if we have lived through what He said before it.

Surah Al-Qasas opens with a baby in a basket. A mother who has been told to nurse her child and then, when she fears for him, to *cast him into the river*. A throne so vast it seems to fill the earth — Pharaoh's throne — and a child placed by the water at the foot of that throne, knowing not even his own name. The surah builds an entire world. Magicians. Exile. A burning bush. A staff that becomes a serpent. A man fleeing alone across a desert with no provision. Then the rise of Musa from fugitive to messenger. Then the drowning of Pharaoh and his armies in a sea that closes like a fist.

And just when you think the surah is about empires, it pivots. It tells you about a man named Qarun. From the people of Musa. Given so much wealth that the *keys* to his treasure houses required strong men to carry them. He walked out among his people in his finery and the people whose hearts were attached to this world said: *if only we had what Qarun had.* And when his wise neighbors warned him, he said the line that has echoed across every century since:

> "I was given this only because of knowledge I have."

[PAUSE]

Then the earth opened beneath his feet. And his treasures with him. And there was no one — *no group, no faction, no party* — to come to his aid against Allah.

The next morning the very people who had said *if only we had what Qarun had* said: *Glory be to Allah — He expands provision for whom He wills and constricts it. Had Allah not been gracious to us, He would have caused the earth to swallow us too.*

This is the world we are living in when the surah's final words arrive. We have just watched solidity dissolve. We have watched a throne sink. We have watched treasure swallowed. We have watched the man who said *I built this myself* descend into the dirt. And then the surah, having made us watch all of this, turns and says:

**كُلُّ شَىْءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجْهَهُ.**

Every single thing is perishing — except His Face.

Here is the question I want you to carry into this reflection. We hear this ayah as a future statement: *one day everything will perish.* We file it away with our other someday-truths. But what if Allah did not say "will perish"? What if the grammar Allah chose says something far stranger and far more urgent? What if He is telling us — right now, at this very moment, in the chair you are sitting in — that everything around you is *already* in the act of dissolving, and you simply cannot see it?

Let's go there.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1: The Word That Refuses To Be Future — هَالِكٌ

Open your hand and look at it. The skin you see right now is not the skin you had a year ago. The cells have been dying and replacing themselves the entire time. Your bones are being rebuilt. The matter that composes you today is largely not the matter that composed you a decade ago. Modern biology gives us a strange truth: you are not a thing. You are a process. A pattern that something is moving through. The "you" of solid edges is a story the brain tells you — useful, but not literally true.

Now hold that in your mind, and let me teach you something about Arabic grammar before we go to the word.

Arabic has a category of words called the **active participle** — *ism al-fāʿil*. In English we have something similar with our "-ing" forms: *running, sleeping, dying.* The active participle is not exactly a verb and not exactly a noun. It describes someone or something *in the state of doing the action.* It is the verb caught mid-motion and frozen into a quality. A *kātib* is not "one who wrote" and not "one who will write" — it is *one who is, in their being, a writer.* The action has become an attribute. It is happening, has been happening, and will continue.

Now look at the word Allah uses. Not *sa-yahliku* — "will perish." Not *halaka* — "perished." Not *yahliku* — "perishes." He chose:

**هَالِكٌ** — *hālikun.*

An active participle. *Perishing.* In the state of perishing. Currently undergoing the process of dissolution as an intrinsic quality of being.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Allah did not say *every thing will perish.* He said every thing **is** *perishing — right now.* The grammar does not point at a distant future. It points at *this moment.* The pen on your desk. The wall behind you. The body you inhabit. The relationship you are worried about. The wealth you have been counting. The reputation you have been building. *In the very second you are reading these words,* every one of those things is in the active state of decay. You are not waiting for them to end. They are ending. They have been ending the whole time you have been calling them solid.

Notice something. We have a deep psychological need to believe in solid things. The reason we panic at death is not that death exists — it is that death *interrupts* our model of permanence. We think: this is real, this is mine, this will hold. And then it ends, and we are shocked. The shock comes from the gap between the model and the reality. The Quran's grammar is closing that gap. It is telling us: the model was false from the beginning. Nothing was ever holding. The thing you are clutching has been dissolving in your hand the entire time.

Listen also to the *sound* of the word. *Hā-li-kun.* Three hard, unyielding consonants — the breathy *ḥā* of Arabic, the sharp *lām*, the cutting *kāf.* Compare it to the word that follows: *waj-ha-hu.* Soft. Flowing. Open at the end. The mouth itself enacts the contrast. You feel the collapse, then the opening. Allah is making the very texture of the recitation perform the meaning.

[PAUSE]

What this single word reveals is that Allah is not telling us about an event in the future. He is telling us about the *nature of every created thing.* Perishing is not what will happen to creation — perishing is *what creation is doing.* That is its grammar. That is its mode of being. Solidity was never a feature of the world. It was a feature of our perception.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element 2: The Asymmetry of the Exception — وَجْهَهُۥ

Here is something you may never have noticed. When Allah makes the exception, He does not say *kullu shayʾin hālikun illā Huwa* — "everything is perishing except *Him.*" He says *illā wajhah* — "except His **Face.**"

Why?

Why insert this word? Why not the simpler, more obvious *Him*? The grammar would have been cleaner. The theology would have been clearer. *Everything perishes except Allah.* Done. But that is not what Allah said. He said *His Face.* And in the Quran, when a word is chosen over a simpler alternative, that choice is doing work.

Let me build this slowly because it matters.

The classical scholars of tafsīr were not unanimous on what *wajh* means here, and that is not a problem to solve — it is a richness to inhabit. Three readings stand alongside each other, and rather than pick one, I want us to see how they fan out from the same center, like three angles on the same statue.

The first reading: *wajh* means **the Essence** — *al-Dhāt.* In Arabic idiom, *the face of a thing* is sometimes its self, its core. To say "I want the face of so-and-so" can mean *I want the person himself.* On this reading, "everything is perishing except His Face" simply means "everything is perishing except Him." The word *wajh* is a way of saying *He, in His very self.*

The second reading — and this one rises directly from the Companions and many of the early scholars: *wajh* means **what is done for His sake.** The face you turn something toward. The direction you offer your action. On this reading, the ayah says: every thing is perishing — *every* thing — except that which was *turned toward Him.* Your prayer. Your patience. Your unseen kindness. The whisper you made in the dark that no one heard. These do not perish. The deed turned toward His Face takes on something of His Face's permanence. Everything else dissolves; what was for Him remains.

The third reading is the one Ahl al-Sunnah preserve as a matter of doctrine: *wajh* is an attribute Allah has affirmed for Himself, in a manner befitting His majesty, *bi-lā kayf* — without our asking *how.* We affirm what He affirmed. We do not picture it. We do not deny it. The Face is His, in a way only He knows.

Here is what is staggering. **All three readings are saying the same thing from different distances.** The Essence does not perish. What is offered to that Essence does not perish. And the very Face we cannot picture is the only point of contact in the universe with permanence. Whether you read *wajh* as essence, as direction-of-deed, or as attribute, the conclusion is identical: *the only thing that survives the universal verb of perishing is what is on the side of Allah.*

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the asymmetry. Allah names two categories: *every thing* and *His Face.* Notice He did not balance them. He did not say "everything perishes except what is eternal." He gave the perishing category an exhaustive label — *kullu shayʾin* — *every single thing.* And He gave the surviving category one specific phrase — *His Face.* The list of perishables is total. The list of survivors is exactly one item long.

What this asymmetry teaches us is that *survival is not a property things can have.* You cannot make something permanent. You cannot build something that lasts. The wealth Qarun built did not last. The throne Pharaoh sat on did not last. The pyramids will not last. The library will not last. The album will not last. The institution will not last. The only way anything you do escapes the universal verb of *perishing* is if it is **on the side of His Face** — turned toward Him, done for Him, anchored in Him. There is no other survival path. There has never been another survival path.

And here is the second hidden teaching of *wajh.* A face is the part of a being that you *meet.* It is the locus of relationship. Allah did not say the only thing surviving is His power, or His knowledge, or His throne — though those too are eternal. He named the attribute of *meeting.* The part of Him that is *toward you.* What survives the end of all things is the very capacity to encounter Him. The relationship survives. The face-to-face survives. Everything else collapses around it, but the meeting remains.

[PAUSE — long]

That is what the word *wajh* is doing here that *Huwa* could never have done. It is telling you that on the day every solid thing has dissolved, *there is still a Face turned toward you.* And that Face is the only thing that was ever real.

### Element 3: The Verb You Did Not Conjugate — تُرْجَعُونَ

We come to the last word of the surah. *Turjaʿūn.* "You will be returned."

Teach yourself one feature of Arabic before we step into the word. Arabic, like English, distinguishes active and passive verbs. *Rajaʿa* — *he returned* — is active. The person did the returning. *Rujiʿa* — *he was returned* — is passive. Something or Someone returned him. The agent has been removed from the sentence, but the action still happened, and it happened *to* the subject, not *by* him.

Allah could easily have said *wa ilayhi tarjiʿūn* — "and to Him you will return." Active. You walking back. You choosing the path home. He has used that construction elsewhere in the Quran. It is grammatically clean. It is theologically true. But here, at the end of this surah — the surah of Pharaoh, the surah of Qarun, the surah of every being who imagined himself the author of his own life — Allah chose:

**وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ.**

*And to Him you will be returned.* Passive.

[PAUSE]

You did not return. You will not return. You will be *returned.*

Stay here, because this is one of the gentlest and most devastating choices in the Quran. Pharaoh thought he was the agent of his life. Qarun thought he was the agent of his wealth. They lived their entire lives in the active voice — *I built, I conquered, I earned, I deserved.* And the surah closes by quietly stripping the active voice out of every reader's grip. *To Him you will be returned.* Not by your decision. Not on your timeline. Not under your steam. The most important journey of your existence is one in which you are not the verb's actor. You are its object.

Notice what this does to the entire moral architecture of the surah. Pharaoh was a king. He decided. He commanded. He pursued. And in the end, he too was *returned* — pulled by the sea, dragged into the ground that swallowed his armies. Qarun was a self-made man. He built. He boasted. *I was given this for what I know.* And in the end, he too was *returned* — into the very earth he had walked across in his finery. The passive *turjaʿūn* is the great equalizer. Every active life ends in a passive verb.

And listen — this is not a *threat.* It is a mercy. Read the surah again with this in mind. We have been watching exhausted, striving people: Musa fleeing into the desert with no provision, then helped to water by an unseen hand. Musa fleeing again from Pharaoh's court, *afraid and waiting to see what comes next* — and Allah is the one moving every piece. The mother of Musa, instructed to *cast* her child into the river, and Allah is the one who returns the child to her arms. The whole surah is about the futility of trying to author your own delivery, and the *kindness* of being delivered. The final verb keeps the same theology: you are not the one who makes the journey home. You are the one who is brought home.

[PAUSE]

There is also a shift inside the ayah itself that you should hear. It opens with a singular command: *wa lā tadʿu* — "you (singular) do not call." Allah is speaking, in the second person singular, to a single soul — perhaps the Prophet ﷺ, perhaps you the reader, perhaps any one human being wrestling with the temptation to scatter their devotion. But by the end of the ayah He has shifted to the plural: *wa ilayhi turjaʿūn* — "and to *all of you* will be returned." The singular tightens into intimacy; the plural opens into universality. He came close to whisper a personal warning, then widened His gaze to gather every human being into the same final passive verb.

What this last word reveals is that the Quran is not asking you to be powerful enough to choose Allah. It is telling you that, powerful or not, you are coming back to Him. Your only real freedom — the only thing the active voice still controls — is what you are turning your face toward in the time before the passive verb arrives.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So we have stood inside three small grammatical decisions. *Hālikun* — the participle that pulled the future of perishing into the present. *Wajhah* — the asymmetric exception that named not Him but His meeting-face as the sole survivor. *Turjaʿūn* — the passive that strips us of authorship at the very moment we thought the journey was ours.

Now let us ask what these grammatical decisions reveal not just about words — but about the texture of reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1: The Things You Are Calling Real

Modern psychology has a name for the silent project that runs underneath most human lives. They call it *terror management.* The theory is simple and uncomfortable. Human beings, alone among creatures, know they are going to die. That knowledge is unbearable in its raw form. So we manage the terror by pouring our energy into what the researchers call *symbolic immortality projects* — things that feel like they outlive us. Career. Wealth. Reputation. Buildings. Family name. Followers. A book. A company. Anything we can point to and say: *this will remain when I am gone.* The whole construction is a defense against the fact that we, personally, will not.

This is what Qarun was doing.

Read his words again. *I was given this for what I know.* He is not really talking about a treasure. He is talking about a *self.* He has built a self — a brilliant, self-made, knowledge-deserving self — and the treasure is its proof. Take the treasure away and the self collapses. So when the earth opens, it does not just swallow gold. It swallows *Qarun* — the entire architecture of who he thought he was. There is nothing left to send for help, no faction, no party. The self he built was made of the same dissolving stuff as everything else.

Now hear the ayah again, with this in mind. *Every single thing is perishing.* Not "will perish." *Is perishing.* The terror management project was already failing the entire time it was being built. The wealth was decaying as Qarun was counting it. The reputation was eroding as he was burnishing it. The self was disintegrating as he was constructing it. He was building a sandcastle with his back to the tide and calling himself a king.

This is not abstract. Stop right now and ask: *what am I building that I am secretly hoping will outlive me?* The career. The platform. The house. The body. The image. The achievements I rehearse to myself when I cannot sleep. The Quran is not telling you those things will end one day. It is telling you they are ending right now, and the only reason you cannot see it is that the timescale of their dissolution is slower than the timescale of your awareness.

Here is the strange gift the ayah gives us. Once you accept that *kullu shayʾin hālik* is a present-tense reality, the terror management project loses its grip. You stop asking *how do I make this last.* You start asking the only question that makes sense in a perishing world: *which of my actions is being turned toward His Face?* Because that is the only thing that escapes the verb. Not the action's bigness. Not its visibility. Not its measurability. *Its direction.*

Two people pray the same prayer. One is praying so people will think he is religious. One is praying because there is nothing in this universe more deserving of his face than Allah. The first prayer is *hālik* — perishing as it is being uttered, because it was an instrument of his terror management, an offering to the self he is building. The second prayer is on the side of *wajh Allāh* — turned toward the only Face that survives. The deed is the same. The destiny is opposite.

What this changes about how you live tomorrow is not the size of your work. It is the *direction.* The same email written for Allah's face survives in a way that the same email written for your reputation does not. The same parenting done for His face survives. The same conversation, the same patience with a difficult person, the same act of putting down your phone to listen — all of it can be on either side of the line. The line is *who you are turning your face toward as you do it.* That is the only audit that matters. Everything else is dissolving, including the version of yourself you are doing it for.

[PAUSE]

### Theme 2: The Relief of Not Being the Author

There is a particular exhaustion that lives in the modern soul. It is the exhaustion of self-authorship. We have been told, all our lives, that we are the architects of our destinies. That we choose our story. That we are, each of us, the protagonist of an unfolding narrative we are responsible for crafting well. And many of us — the most diligent, the most conscientious, the most spiritually serious — have absorbed this so deeply that even our religion becomes another arena for the same exhausting project. *I must build a good Muslim. I must construct a worshipper. I must author my own salvation.* And it crushes us, because we know in our bones that we are not equal to the task.

Hear the last word of the surah again. *Turjaʿūn.* You will be **returned.**

Not "you will return." You will be returned. The verb is passive. The subject is being acted upon. The agent — Allah — is the one bringing you home.

Sit with the relief that is hidden inside this passive voice.

You are not the author of your own return. You did not initiate this journey. You did not choose to be born, you did not choose your parents, you did not choose the time and place into which you were placed. And — this is what the ayah is whispering to you — *you do not have to be the engine of your homecoming either.* The same Hand that put you here is bringing you back. Your job is not to manufacture a journey. Your job is to stop calling on other gods, to recognize the Face that is the only survivor, to turn your face toward His Face, and to let the verb of return happen to you.

This is not a defeat. This is a liberation.

Look at how the surah began. A mother in terror, told to put her infant son into a *river.* She had no plan. She had no path. She did exactly what she was told and *let go of the basket.* And the surah tells us: *We returned him to his mother, that her eye might be cooled and that she might not grieve.* That verb — *radadnāhu* — is the same root as *turjaʿūn.* The mother of Musa was not the author of the return of her son. She was the recipient of it. She did not bring him back. He was *brought back to her.* The whole surah is rehearsing the grammar of the final verb. Stop trying to author. Start trusting the Hand that returns.

This does not mean inaction. It does not mean the singular command at the start of the ayah disappears — *do not call upon another god alongside Allah* is still an active verb, still a real choice you make, still a moral demand. The active voice has its work. But the active voice's work is *small and clear*: do not divide your face. Do not scatter your devotion. Do not pretend there is more than one Face worth turning toward. *Everything else,* the cosmos handles. The return is not your project.

For the soul that has been white-knuckling its own salvation, this is water in a desert. *To Him you will be returned.* You will not have to invent the road. You will not have to engineer the arrival. The Owner of the verb is bringing you home. Your work is to keep your face from drifting in the meantime.

[PAUSE]

What this changes about how you live tomorrow is the *quality of your striving.* You can still strive — Musa strove, the mother of Musa strove, the Prophet ﷺ strove more than any of us — but you can strive without the terror that says *if I do not get this right, no one will.* You can do your work and trust the verb. You can release the white knuckles. You can let yourself be returned.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that the work is done, look at the surah from above. Surah Al-Qasas opens with three disjointed letters — *Ṭā Sīn Mīm.* Sounds without graspable meaning. The very first thing the surah does is name the limits of human grasp: *here are letters whose secret you do not know, even though they are speech you can pronounce.* The surah opens by humbling our capacity to comprehend.

It closes with a sentence about the limits of all things. *Every single thing is perishing except His Face.*

The surah's beginning names the ceiling of human knowing. Its end names the ceiling of created being. And in between, it tells stories of two men — Pharaoh and Qarun — who refused both ceilings. Who thought they comprehended. Who thought they would last. The frame of the surah was warning us about them from the very first letter and the very last word.

### Bringing It Together

The three grammatical decisions and the two themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Hālikun* says: nothing solid is solid. *Wajhah* says: only what is turned toward the Face survives. *Turjaʿūn* says: even the journey home is not your verb.

Theme one says: stop building immortality projects on a dissolving foundation; choose direction over durability. Theme two says: stop carrying the entire weight of your salvation; the One who put you here is bringing you back.

Underneath both: *you are not, and never were, the author of permanence. You are the one who chooses, in the small and shrinking window of your life, what your face is turned toward as the verb of return arrives.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What am I building right now that I am secretly hoping will outlive me — and would I still build it if I knew, in my bones, that it is *already* dissolving?
2. Of the actions I will take today, which ones are turned toward His Face, and which ones are turned toward an audience that is itself perishing?
3. Where am I exhausting myself trying to author a homecoming that is, in fact, being authored for me?
4. If the only survivor is what is on the side of Allah, what would change about how I spend the next hour of my life?
5. The ayah whispers a singular command before it widens to a plural fate. What is the *one* other-than-Allah that I am still quietly calling upon, and am I willing to stop?

### The Distillation

The grammar of this ayah teaches you that the world is dissolving in real time, that the only survivor is what is turned toward the Face of Allah, and that your homecoming is not a project you author but a verb that is happening to you — so spend your shrinking life turning your face toward the only Face that does not dissolve.

### The Invitation

You do not need to do something large after this reflection. You do not need to overhaul your life. The ayah does not ask for that. It asks for *direction.* It asks you, in the next thing you do today — the next conversation, the next prayer, the next moment of work, the next interaction with someone who is hard to love — to do it *on the side of His Face.* That is all. One act, turned toward the only Face that survives. Then another. The accumulation of those acts is the only thing of yours that the universal verb of perishing cannot touch.

### Du'ā

> O Allah, You whose Face alone survives the perishing of every thing — turn our faces toward Your Face before our breath is taken. Loosen our grip on the dissolving things we have been calling permanent, and let our smallest deeds be on the side of what does not perish. And when You return us to You — for we will be returned — let us arrive with a face that recognized Your Face while there was still time. Āmīn.
