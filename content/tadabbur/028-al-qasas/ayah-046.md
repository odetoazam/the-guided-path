# The Witness Who Was Not There

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 46

**Arabic:**
وَمَا كُنتَ بِجَانِبِ ٱلطُّورِ إِذْ نَادَيْنَا وَلَـٰكِن رَّحْمَةً مِّن رَّبِّكَ لِتُنذِرَ قَوْمًا مَّآ أَتَىٰهُم مِّن نَّذِيرٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
Wa mā kunta bi-jānibi-ṭ-ṭūri idh nādaynā, wa lākin raḥmatan min rabbika li-tundhira qawman mā atāhum min nadhīrin min qablika laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn.

**Translation:**
*And you were not on the side of the Mount when We called out — but [it was sent] as a mercy from your Lord, so that you may warn a people to whom no warner had come before you, that perhaps they may take heed.*

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Ayah

Picture Makkah in the years before the migration. A man in his forties is standing in the marketplace, or by the Kaʿbah, or at the edge of a small gathering, and he is telling a story.

The story is about a baby placed in a basket and floated down a river. About a young man who killed someone in anger and fled into the desert. About a stranger who arrived at a well and was offered a daughter's hand in marriage. About a shepherd who, years later, saw a fire on the side of a mountain and walked toward it — and was met there by the voice of God.

The man telling the story has never read a book. There are very few books in Makkah to read. He has not traveled to the great centers of Jewish or Christian learning. He has not, as far as anyone in his city knows, sat at the feet of any teacher who specializes in the lives of the ancient prophets.

And yet the story he tells is *correct* — correct in a way that the Jewish tribes of Madinah will later confirm. Correct in details that the Christian monks of Najran will recognize. Correct in a way that the surrounding pagans cannot account for.

So they have a question. It is the obvious question. It is the question that, if you were standing in Makkah in 615 CE, you would also be asking:

**How does he know?**

Where is this information coming from? He wasn't there. None of his ancestors were there. The events happened in another land, in another language, more than a thousand years before he was born. Either someone is feeding him this material — in which case, *who*, and why has no one seen them? — or he is making it up — in which case, why does it keep matching the records of the older communities? Or…

Or there is a third option.

And that third option is what this ayah is built to deliver.

But Allah does not deliver it like a defense lawyer. He does not say, "I revealed it to him, full stop, end of argument." He delivers it in a way that is so quiet, so understated, that you can read it three times and miss what's happening. He delivers it by stating something that nobody would ever bother to deny.

**He says: "And you were not on the side of the Mount when We called out."**

[PAUSE]

Why on earth would Allah tell the Prophet ﷺ that he was not at a mountain in the Sinai peninsula during an event that took place over a thousand years before his birth? Of course he wasn't there. Nobody thinks he was there. Why state the obvious?

Stay with that question. That is the door into this ayah.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Wa mā kunta* — The Argument from Absence

Let me first teach you a way of arguing that, once you see it, you will start to notice everywhere.

Imagine a detective walks into a room where a robbery has just been committed. The detective points at a man and says, "You weren't in the lobby when the alarm went off." The accused man frowns. He says, "Of course I wasn't in the lobby. Why are you saying that?" And the detective replies, calmly, "Because the only way you could know what I'm about to tell you is if you weren't in the lobby — *and yet you described what happened in the lobby in perfect detail*."

The denial of presence is not a defense of innocence. It is the setup for a much sharper question: **how, then, do you know?**

This is what Arabic rhetoricians sometimes call a *muqaddimah* — a premise that looks like a concession but is actually a trap door. The speaker lays down what seems like an obvious negative, and then the listener falls through into the only conclusion that explains the data.

Now look at the opening of our ayah.

**وَمَا كُنتَ بِجَانِبِ ٱلطُّورِ إِذْ نَادَيْنَا**
*And you were not on the side of the Mount when We called out.*

Notice the structure. *Mā kunta* — "you were not." It's a flat denial. Allah is denying something. But what He's denying is something nobody ever claimed. Nobody in Makkah — not the Prophet's friends, not his enemies — ever claimed that Muhammad ﷺ had been physically present at the Mount of Sinai during the calling of Musa.

So why deny it?

Let's sit with this for a moment. In Arabic rhetoric, when a speaker negates something that no one asserted, the negation is not really about the literal claim. It is doing something else. It is **isolating an explanation by elimination**.

Here is the logic, made explicit:
- You are telling them about a private conversation between God and Musa on the side of a mountain.
- You were not on that mountain.
- You were not even alive when that mountain was called to.
- Nobody else who *was* there is whispering it to you, because that generation is gone.
- And yet — you are telling them what happened.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what *mā kunta* is doing? It is not informing the Prophet ﷺ of where he was not. It is informing *the listener in Makkah* — and every listener after — that the only remaining explanation for the Prophet's knowledge of these events is the one he has been claiming all along. Revelation.

Here's what's remarkable. This is the third time in just three ayahs that Allah has done this. Look at the rhythm:

- Ayah 44: *Wa mā kunta bi-jānibi-l-gharbiyy* — "And you were not on the western side [of the mountain] when We decreed the matter to Musa."
- Ayah 45: *Wa mā kunta thāwiyan fī ahli madyan* — "And you were not a resident among the people of Midian."
- Ayah 46: *Wa mā kunta bi-jānibi-ṭ-ṭūr* — "And you were not on the side of the Mount when We called out."

Three denials. Three locations. Three moments in Musa's life. And every single one of them is detailed in this very surah. Allah has just spent more than forty ayahs telling the story of Musa in cinematic detail — and now, in three quick strokes, He places the Prophet ﷺ outside every camera angle of that story.

What this invites us to consider is this: **the argument is not stated. It is built in your mind.** Allah does not say, "Therefore I am the source." He says, "You weren't there, you weren't there, you weren't there" — and lets the listener complete the sentence.

This is the architecture of the ayah's first half. A denial that forces a conclusion.

### Element Two: *Idh nādaynā* — The Verb of the First Person

Now zoom in on three words: *idh nādaynā*. "When We called out."

The root *n-d-w* in Arabic carries the meaning of calling from a distance — a call that has range, a call that reaches across space. It's not whispering. It's not a private murmur. It's the kind of call you'd make to someone across a valley. And it appears here in the first-person plural: *nādaynā* — "We called."

Here is where you have to slow down. Because something happens grammatically in this ayah that is easy to miss.

Through most of the surah, the story of Musa has been told in the third person. *Allah said* to Musa. *He was told*. *He was called*. The narrator is hovering above the scene, telling us what happened.

But here — right at the moment of the calling itself — the grammar shifts. Allah does not say, "When *He* called out." He says, **"When *We* called out."**

In classical Arabic rhetoric, this kind of shift is called **iltifāt** — a sudden change of grammatical person inside a single sentence. And iltifāt is never accidental. It is one of the most carefully studied features of the Quran. When the speaker shifts from third person to first person, He is doing something specific: He is reaching out of the narrative frame and identifying Himself as a participant.

Let me give you a small example so you can feel it. Imagine you are reading a history book about a great battle. The author writes, "The general commanded his troops to advance. They marched into the valley. They were ambushed." That's third person. The author is outside the story. Now imagine you turn the page and suddenly the prose changes: "*I* watched them march into the valley. *I* saw what was coming." Your whole relationship to the text just changed. The author is no longer the narrator — he is now a witness, possibly a participant. The story is no longer something he is reporting. It is something he is testifying to.

That is what *nādaynā* does here.

Allah has been telling the story of Musa. And at the precise moment of the calling — the moment of revelation, the moment when the divine reached into the human — He shifts from the cool distance of narration to the first-person plural of testimony. "We called out."

Notice something. The denial in the first part of the ayah is about the Prophet's absence. *You* were not there. But the verb in the dependent clause is about Allah's presence. *We* called. So you have, in a single sentence, the human absent and the Divine present at the very same moment.

[PAUSE]

What this is doing — quietly, structurally — is dismantling the alternative explanations. If you were going to argue that Muhammad ﷺ got his information from human sources, then the ayah is saying: there were no human witnesses for him to get it from. And if you were going to argue that the information is just legend retold, the ayah is saying: the source is not memory of an event, the source is *the One who was the event.* "We called out."

The plural *nādaynā* — the royal "We" of majesty in Quranic usage — is doing one more thing. It is binding the call at the Mount to the call of this very Quran. The same "We" who called Musa is the "We" who is speaking right now. The voice that reached out of the burning bush is the voice that is reaching, in this moment, out of this surah, into the ear of the listener.

The argument from absence has become an argument from presence. *You weren't there — but We were. And We are here now.*

### Element Three: *Wa lākin raḥmatan min rabbika* — The Pivot

Now here is where the ayah turns. And the turn is one of the most beautiful in the Quran.

After two denials — *you were not on the Mount, We were the ones who called* — Allah pivots with a single particle: *wa lākin* — "but."

What would you expect after such an argument? You would expect a conclusion that crowns the argument. Something like: "but We have made you Our messenger." Or, "but We have given you knowledge of the unseen." Or, "but We have raised you to the rank of those before you." Something about *the Prophet's* status. Something about *the Prophet's* qualification. Something about the *evidentiary triumph* of this rhetorical setup.

That is what we expect.

That is not what Allah says.

He says: **وَلَـٰكِن رَّحْمَةً مِّن رَّبِّكَ** — *"but [it was sent] as a mercy from your Lord."*

Stay with me. The pivot of the entire argument — the place where the verse should crown the Prophet ﷺ with the rank of recipient of revelation — is given over instead to the word ***raḥmah***. Mercy.

And notice the case of the word. *Raḥmatan* — accusative, indefinite. Why indefinite? Why not *al-raḥmah*, "*the* mercy"? In Arabic, indefiniteness in this construction often signals magnification — a mercy of such a kind that no specific article could contain it. Not just mercy. A mercy. A particular, unbounded, immeasurable mercy.

And notice what is left unsaid grammatically. The sentence is elliptical. The classical grammarians point out that there is an implied verb here — *but we sent it / but you were sent* as a mercy from your Lord. Arabic permits this kind of compression because the meaning is clear from context. But the compression itself has a function. It makes mercy not a side note — it makes mercy the *entire content* of the missing main clause.

In other words: when Allah finally gets to the main point of the verse, the main point is not "We made you a prophet." The main point is **"this whole thing — your knowing, your speaking, your warning — is mercy."**

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable about this. The verse began as an evidentiary argument — almost a courtroom argument — for the truth of the Prophet's mission. *You weren't there. We were. Therefore the source of your knowledge is verified.* But the moment Allah arrives at the conclusion, He does not deliver a courtroom verdict. He delivers a gift.

The proof of prophecy and the meaning of prophecy turn out to be **the same thing**. The fact that you have access to what you could not have known is the proof. And the fact that you have access to what you could not have known is the mercy. The proof *is* the mercy.

Most arguments, when they are won, leave you feeling either vindicated or owed. This argument, when it is won, leaves you feeling *given to*. That is the pivot. From evidence to gift.

And notice: it is *raḥmatan min rabbika* — mercy from *your Lord*. Not "from Allah." Not "from God." From *your Lord*. The word *rabb* in Arabic carries the meaning of one who nurtures, who raises up, who brings something from incompleteness to completeness. The Prophet ﷺ is being told: this mercy is not coming from some distant cosmic authority. It is coming from the One who has been your nurturer your entire life — who raised you as an orphan, who guided you through the desert, who placed you in this exact moment so that this mercy could come through you.

This is a Lord speaking to His own. This is not the language of decree. It is the language of intimacy.

### The Bridge

So look at what these three linguistic features have shown us:

- **Mā kunta** built an argument by absence — proof through what wasn't there.
- **Nādaynā** placed the divine speaker inside His own argument — first-person presence answering human absence.
- **Wa lākin raḥmatan min rabbika** turned the evidentiary triumph into a gift, naming mercy as the meaning of the whole.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Knowledge You Did Not Earn Is the Mercy You Did Not Ask For

Begin with a moment that you have lived. Not a moment from a scripture. A moment from your life.

You are sitting somewhere quietly — maybe in a car, maybe on a walk, maybe waiting for sleep — and something occurs to you. A way of understanding a situation that has been troubling you. A name for an emotion that you have been feeling for weeks and have not been able to name. A connection between two things that have seemed unrelated. And the moment it arrives, you feel a small jolt: *where did that come from?*

You did not produce it. You were not working on it. It came to you. You were on the receiving end of an insight that you could not trace.

Now multiply that experience. Take it from the level of a passing insight to the level of an entire worldview. Take it from the level of "where did that thought come from?" to the level of "where did the *capacity to think about that at all* come from?" Take it from the level of an idea to the level of a whole system of meaning that explains your existence.

That is what Allah is naming in this ayah. The knowledge that the Prophet ﷺ has of Musa, of the Mount, of the call across the valley — that knowledge did not come to him by study. It did not come by inheritance. It did not come by travel. It came as a *gift*. And the gift, Allah tells us, has a name. It is called **raḥmah**.

Stay with this. Because there is something here that addresses one of the deepest assumptions of the modern self.

We are raised, especially in this age, to believe that what we have earned is what we own, and what we have not earned is suspicious. Knowledge in particular is treated as something you must work for, document, source, defend. If you say something insightful, the first question is, "Where did you read that?" The assumption is that any knowledge worth having has a paper trail.

But the Quran is making a different claim. It is saying: **some of the deepest knowledge in your life has no paper trail because the paper trail is divine.** And the proper response to that kind of knowledge is not to demand a citation. It is to recognize a gift.

How does this land in your life? Think of the moments when you have known something you should not have known. The moment of insight in a relationship you didn't have the maturity for. The moment of correct intuition about a person you had only just met. The moment when, in the middle of confusion, a verse you had memorized as a child suddenly opened in your heart and said exactly what you needed to hear. Those moments are not coincidences in the worldview of this ayah. They are samples of the same phenomenon. They are local instances of the global truth that this verse is naming. **Knowledge that arrives without being earned is a mercy from your Lord.**

What does this change about how you live? It changes how you receive understanding. When the next moment of clarity comes to you — about your work, about a person you love, about your own heart — instead of crediting yourself, instead of moving past it quickly to use it, you might pause. You might say *al-ḥamdu lillāh*. You might recognize, even for ten seconds, that the capacity to see what you just saw is not something you generated. It is something that was *called* to you, across the distance, from a Lord who calls.

And that is a small habit. But it accumulates. It accumulates into a life that knows the difference between insight as performance and insight as mercy.

### Theme Two: The Warner for a People with No Warner

Look at the second half of the ayah again:

**لِتُنذِرَ قَوْمًا مَّآ أَتَىٰهُم مِّن نَّذِيرٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ**
*…so that you may warn a people to whom no warner had come before you, that perhaps they may take heed.*

There is something here that, if you are not careful, you will read past too quickly. Allah says: "to warn a people to whom no warner had come *before you*."

Wait.

The Quran itself, in many other places, names a long line of warners — Hud, Salih, Shuʿayb, Ismaʿil — sent to the Arabs and to nearby peoples. The Kaʿbah itself was built by Ibrahim and Ismaʿil as a house of monotheism. So in what sense had *no warner* come to this people?

The classical scholars hold these meanings together rather than choosing between them. One angle: no warner had come *in their direct ancestral memory*, in the centuries immediately before the Prophet ﷺ. Another angle: no warner had come *in their language*, with a scripture in their tongue, addressing them as *their* community. Another angle: no warner had come *with a finality* that closed the chain.

All of these readings converge on a single human reality: the people the Prophet ﷺ was sent to had been left, for generations, **without a living voice from the unseen**. The lineage was distant. The scriptures were in other tongues. The prophetic line had gone quiet. And in that quiet, they had drifted.

Now think about your own life. Think about the people you know who have grown up without a living voice of guidance.

This is not just a description of pre-Islamic Makkah. It is a description of so much of our modern world. There are entire generations being raised right now in homes where no one prays, no one recites, no one names God. There are children growing up in communities where the only voices speaking to them are voices selling them something. There are countries that were once full of scholars and have, within living memory, become deserts of meaning. There are *parts of our own families* who, by no fault of their own, have grown up in a silence where the divine was never named.

These are *qawm mā atāhum min nadhīrin*. They are not bad people. They are not stubborn rejectors. They are people who simply, structurally, have not had a warner come to them.

And how does Allah speak about them? Listen again. He does not say, "to punish them for their ignorance." He does not say, "to condemn them for the silence they inherited." He says, **"so that you may *warn* them — *perhaps* they may take heed."**

Stay with the word *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn*. *Perhaps they may remember.* The root is *dh-k-r* — to recall, to bring back to mind. It is not *yataʿallamūn*, "perhaps they may learn." It is *yatadhakkarūn*, "perhaps they may *remember*."

What this invites us to consider is one of the most remarkable claims of the Quran: the message of revelation is not new information. It is **reminded information**. It is something the soul already knows and has forgotten. The warner does not arrive with content the soul has never encountered. The warner arrives with content the soul, deep down, already carries — and the warner's job is to make that buried knowing audible again.

There is a hadith qudsi that resonates with this: Allah created humans upright, and the shayāṭīn turned them away. The default position of the human soul is the position of recognition. Forgetfulness is what was added on top.

So when Allah says *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn*, He is naming hope. Even for a people who have had no warner. Even for a generation that has been raised in silence. The buried knowing is still there. The seed is still in the soil. And the warner's voice — including, in our own way, *your* voice when you speak to someone you love about meaning — is not introducing something foreign. It is calling something to remember itself.

What does this change about how you live tomorrow? It changes who you give up on.

There is a kind of quiet despair that creeps into religious people when they look at family members or friends who seem completely closed to the message. The despair says: *they will never come back.* But this ayah is saying something different. It is saying: **they were given to a Prophet whose entire mission is to warn a people to whom no warner had come.** The Prophet ﷺ did not write off such a people. He warned them with hope. He warned them *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* — perhaps they may remember.

And what is asked of us, in our own small way, is the same posture. Not to give up on the one who has no warner. To be willing to be, in our own corner of the world, the small voice that says — *do you remember? You knew this once. Do you remember?*

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Look at how the ayah is built, now that we have walked through it.

It begins with **absence**: *you were not there.*
It moves through **presence**: *We called out.*
It pivots into **gift**: *but it is mercy from your Lord.*
It opens outward into **mission**: *so that you may warn a people.*
And it closes in **hope**: *perhaps they may remember.*

That is the entire arc of prophecy in twenty-eight words. From the Prophet's absence at the mountain, through Allah's presence in the call, through mercy as the meaning of the whole, into a mission addressed to the forgetful, ending in hope that they will recall what was always in them.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions. The grammatical denial of presence is the structural foundation for mercy as gift. The first-person *nādaynā* is the cosmological foundation for the warner-to-a-silent-people. The pivot of *wa lākin raḥmatan* is the entire theology of how revelation reaches a forgetful world.

What Allah has shown is this: **the absence of the Prophet at the Mount and the absence of warners among his people are the same absence — and the answer to both is the same answer. Mercy.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life have you received knowledge that you did not earn? How do you usually account for it — and how would your life feel different if you accounted for it as *raḥmah min rabbik*?
2. Who in your life is a *qawm mā atāhum min nadhīr* — a person or a community to whom no warner has come? What would it mean to speak to them *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn*, rather than expecting immediate change?
3. When you tell someone about your faith, are you delivering information or are you helping them *remember*? What changes when you assume the seed is already there?
4. The ayah names mercy as the *content* of the prophetic mission, not merely its *style*. What in your own life have you been treating as a task, that might actually be a mercy?
5. Allah did not say to the Prophet ﷺ, "We have raised you." He said, "this is mercy *from your Lord*." How does it change your relationship to your gifts to see them as flowing from a Lord, not as belonging to you?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The proof of prophecy and the meaning of prophecy are the same thing: a knowing that the human could not have earned, sent as mercy to a people who could not have remembered without it.

### The Closing Invitation

There is one thing this ayah invites — not commands, invites.

The next time understanding comes to you, especially understanding you cannot trace, pause for the length of a single breath and recognize where it came from. Not as a theological exercise. As an act of return. Say, even silently: *raḥmatan min rabbī.* A mercy from my Lord.

And the next time you are with someone who seems to have no access to meaning, do not write them off. Speak to them as if a seed is buried in them. Because the Quran says it is. Speak *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn*. Perhaps they will remember.

### Du'ā

*O Allah, You called from the side of the Mount, and Your call reached across centuries to find us. We were not there, and yet we received it. Make us recognize that every gift of understanding in our lives is mercy from You — and make us, in our small way, voices that help the forgetful remember what You once placed in them. Āmīn.*
