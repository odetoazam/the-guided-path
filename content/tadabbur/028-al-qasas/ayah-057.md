# The Excuse That Hides the Fear

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 57

### Arabic

> [ayah:28:57] وَقَالُوٓا۟ إِن نَّتَّبِعِ ٱلْهُدَىٰ مَعَكَ نُتَخَطَّفْ مِنْ أَرْضِنَآ ۚ أَوَلَمْ نُمَكِّن لَّهُمْ حَرَمًا ءَامِنًا يُجْبَىٰٓ إِلَيْهِ ثَمَرَٰتُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ رِّزْقًا مِّن لَّدُنَّا وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

### Transliteration

*Wa qālū in nattabiʿi-l-hudā maʿaka nutakhaṭṭaf min arḍinā. Awa-lam numakkin lahum ḥaraman āminan yujbā ilayhi thamarātu kulli shay'in rizqan min ladunnā wa lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn.*

### Translation

> *"And they said: 'If we follow the guidance with you, we will be snatched from our land.' Have We not established for them a safe sanctuary, to which the fruits of all things are drawn — a provision from Us? But most of them do not know."*

## INTRODUCTION — The World Behind the Excuse

Before we can hear this ayah properly, we have to sit in a particular room.

The room is in Makkah. The year is somewhere late in the prophetic mission. The Quraysh have heard the message for years now. They have argued against it publicly with poetry, with philosophy, with accusations of madness and magic. But sometimes — and this ayah captures one such moment — they come close to the Prophet ﷺ in private, and the public argument falls away.

And what comes out, when the public mask drops, is not theology.

It is fear.

They say something like: *"Muhammad, we know what you are saying. We can hear it. But if we follow you, the Arabs will rise against us. We will lose our position. We will lose our caravans. We will be ripped out of this place. We can't afford to be right."*

That is the moment this ayah enters. Allah is not addressing them in public this time. He is letting the Prophet ﷺ — and us — hear what they said behind closed doors. And then He answers the fear directly.

Notice something already. The most common excuse for refusing truth, across human history, is not "I don't believe it." It is **"I cannot afford to believe it."**

That is what this ayah is about.

[PAUSE]

There is something else in the air of this surah that we have to feel before we open the words. Al-Qasas has just spent fifty-six verses on the story of Musa. A man who *was* snatched from his land. A man who fled Egypt with nothing, who arrived in Madyan as a refugee, who had no place. And what did Allah do for him? He gave him water, then food, then shelter, then a wife, then a mission, then a kingdom of meaning.

So when the Quraysh say *"if we follow the guidance, we will be snatched from our land,"* — they are saying the one thing this entire surah has been quietly preparing to answer.

The surah has been waiting for this sentence.

And here's the hook: when Allah answers, He does not say *"trust Me, I will protect you."* He does not promise them anything new. He says something far stranger. He says, **look at the ground beneath your feet right now.** The very fear you are using as an excuse — I have already, in this exact moment, disproven it. And you cannot see it.

What does it mean that the answer to our deepest fear is *already underneath us* and we cannot see it?

That is what we are going to sit with.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Nutakhaṭṭaf* — The Word That Means "Snatched Like Prey"

Let me teach a concept first, before we touch the word.

In Arabic, when you want to describe an action — say, "to take" — you have a range of verbs. You can say *akhadha* (to take). You can say *naqala* (to move). You can say *salaba* (to plunder). Each verb carries a different *weight*. Each one summons a different image. The Quran chooses with surgical precision among these options. A word is never just chosen for its dictionary meaning. It is chosen for the *world* it pulls into the sentence.

Now look at what the Quraysh say. They don't say *we will be defeated*. They don't say *we will lose*. They don't say *they will fight us*. They say a very specific word:

**نُتَخَطَّفْ** — *nutakhaṭṭaf*.

This verb comes from the root خ-ط-ف (*kh-ṭ-f*). The basic meaning, attested in the classical lexicons, is **to snatch suddenly, to seize quickly, to pluck away in an instant.**

In the Arabic of the Quran itself, this same root is used in two telling places. In Surah Al-Ḥajj, Allah describes idols as so powerless that *if a fly were to snatch* — *yakhṭafhum* — *something from them, they couldn't reclaim it.* The word is used for the predatory fly. And in Surah Al-Ṣāffāt, the shayāṭīn who try to climb up to eavesdrop on the heavens are described as being *pursued by a shihāb that snatches* — *al-khaṭfata* — them away.

Stay with the image. *Khaṭf* is what a hawk does to a sparrow. *Khaṭf* is what a wave does to a child on a beach. *Khaṭf* is sudden, predatory, total. You do not survive being *khaṭafed*. You are simply *gone*.

Now notice what they are saying about themselves. *"If we follow the guidance with you, we will be snatched like prey from our land."* They are not picturing themselves as warriors who will lose a battle. They are picturing themselves as **small animals** caught in the talons of a much larger world.

Here's what's remarkable. This word reveals more about the speaker than about the threat.

A person who chooses the word *nutakhaṭṭaf* is a person who, deep down, already sees themselves as small and the world as a predator. They are not just afraid of a specific outcome. They are afraid of being **erased**. And that fear — the fear of erasure — is what is sitting underneath their stated objection.

The Quraysh think they are giving a strategic argument. *We have political concerns. We have trade interests.* But the verb they chose has betrayed them. The verb has revealed that this isn't a calculation. It is a primal terror of being plucked out of existence.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is even more astonishing. The Prophet ﷺ never said, *follow me and you will be physically removed from Makkah.* He said, *follow the guidance.* So where did the violence come from? It came from inside them. They imagined the threat. They projected the predator. The word *nutakhaṭṭaf* is not describing a real bird circling overhead. It is describing **a bird that lives inside their own chest.**

Notice something Allah does not do. He does not say to them, *no, you won't be snatched.* He does not contradict the *fear*. He goes deeper. He goes after the **picture of reality** that makes the fear feel reasonable.

What this single word reveals: most of the time, when we refuse guidance, we are not refusing because of an argument. We are refusing because of an image — an image of ourselves as small, an image of the world as predator. And until that image is dismantled, no argument will reach us.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Numakkin* — The Verb That Means "We Set Down Roots For"

The Quraysh have used a verb of motion and violence — *nutakhaṭṭaf*, to be snatched. Allah's reply uses a verb that does the exact opposite.

**أَوَلَمْ نُمَكِّن لَّهُمْ** — *Awa-lam numakkin lahum* — *Have We not established for them...*

The verb *numakkin* comes from the root م-ك-ن (*m-k-n*), and it is one of the most architecturally interesting verbs in the Quran. The basic noun, *makān*, simply means "place." But the verb in this form — Form II, *makkana* — means something stronger. It means **to give someone a place**, to root them in it, to make their position firm, to *plant* them so that they cannot easily be removed.

When Allah says of Yusuf, *wa kadhālika makkannā li-Yūsufa fī-l-arḍ* — *thus did We establish Yusuf in the land* — He is not saying *We let Yusuf visit Egypt.* He is saying *We rooted him into it, we gave him standing in it, we made his position immovable.*

So look at what is happening in our ayah. The Quraysh have used a word that means **to be torn out**. And Allah replies with a word that means **to be planted into**.

*Nutakhaṭṭaf*. *Numakkin*. They are linguistic opposites.

[PAUSE]

This is the rhetorical move. Allah is not arguing with their fear. He is showing them that the fear is **already factually contradicted by their own lives.** *You are afraid of being snatched? Look at where you are standing. I have already made you the most rooted, most established, most unsnatchable tribe in the entire Arabian peninsula. The thing you fear has not happened. The opposite of what you fear is already true.*

And notice — this is the bridge between the linguistic and the theological — Allah uses *numakkin* in the first-person plural. *We* established them. He does not say "you established yourselves through clever trade routes." He does not say "your ancestor Ibrāhīm built this place." He says *We* did it. The very stability they think they are protecting by *refusing* the guidance — was given to them by the very God whose guidance they are refusing.

The architecture of the irony is breathtaking. *They are refusing the Giver in order to protect the gift.*

[PAUSE]

Let me give you one more thing about this verb that the classical grammarians notice. *Makkana* takes the preposition *li-* — *makkana li-fulān* — "we established *for* someone." This is significant. It means the establishment was **deliberate, intentional, for the sake of the one established.** It was not an accident. It was not a coincidence of geography. It was a gift directed *at them*.

Which means when they refuse the Giver, they are refusing the One who specifically chose to root them. The very ground under their feet is *for them* — *lahum*. Personal. Targeted. A gift addressed by name.

What this verb reveals: when we are afraid that following truth will cost us our stability, we have forgotten that the stability we are clinging to was never ours in the first place. It was a gift. And the same hand that gave it can hold it. We are protecting an inheritance from the very One who left it to us.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Yujbā* and the Unspecified Bounty

Now we come to the strangest, most quietly stunning piece of the ayah.

Allah describes their *ḥaram* — their sanctuary — and He says: *yujbā ilayhi thamarātu kulli shay'in* — *to which the fruits of every thing are drawn.*

Let's open this slowly.

The verb is **يُجْبَىٰ** — *yujbā*. The root ج-ب-ي (*j-b-y*) means **to gather, to collect, to bring in from many places to one place.** In classical Arabic, *al-jibāya* is the technical term for collected taxes or tribute — wealth that flows *toward* a center. The verb is passive: *yujbā* — *are drawn, are gathered, are brought*.

The grammar matters. Allah does not say *they bring the fruits.* He does not say *the people gather the fruits.* He uses the **passive voice**. The fruits *are drawn*. The agent is invisible.

Why does this matter?

Because the Quraysh's entire self-conception is built on the idea that **they** make the trade happen. **They** have the caravans. **They** have the contracts. **They** have the cleverness. Their economy is *their* achievement. That is the very thing they are afraid of losing — *our* land, *our* trade, *our* position.

Allah uses a verb that quietly erases them as the agent. The fruits *are drawn*. By whom? The verb does not say. The verb leaves the agent unstated — but the very next phrase fills the gap: *rizqan min ladunnā* — *as a provision from Us*.

The fruits are not being drawn by Quraysh's commercial genius. They are being drawn by Allah. The Quraysh are not the magnet. **The sanctuary is the magnet, and Allah is the one who built the magnet.**

[PAUSE]

Now look at what's being drawn. *Thamarātu kulli shay'in* — *the fruits of every thing*.

Pause on that. Not *the fruits of every tree*. Not *the fruits of every region*. **The fruits of every thing.** *Kulli shay'in.* Of *everything*.

This is a stunning phrase, and the classical commentators dwelt on it. Makkah itself, geographically, grows almost nothing. It is a stone valley. And yet — every fruit, every product, every kind of good from every part of the world is *drawn* to this place. The poverty of the soil is reversed by the wealth of the gathering. The least fertile spot on earth becomes the most stocked marketplace.

How? Because Allah did not make this place productive. He made this place a **center**. He made everything else come *to* it.

Here's the unseen reality the physical image is pointing toward. The Quraysh think their security comes from *what their land produces*. Allah is telling them that their security comes from *what their land attracts*. And the magnet is not in the soil. The magnet is in *Allah's decision* that this place be a *ḥaram āmin* — a sacred, safe sanctuary.

If the magnet is divine, then refusing the divine is the only thing that could ever turn the magnet off.

[PAUSE]

And now — the absence that I want you to feel.

Look at the phrase again: *thamarātu kulli shay'in*. The fruits of *every thing*. Allah does not specify. He does not say *figs and dates and grain*. He does not give a list. He leaves it utterly open.

This is an absence that speaks. By leaving it unspecified, Allah is saying: *every conceivable form of provision, every kind of good you have ever received in this place, every gift that has ever come to your door — was Mine.* He does not need to name them, because **all of it is included.** There is nothing in the marketplace of Makkah, nothing on the caravan, nothing in the granary, that is outside this verb.

The unspecified object is the universal claim. *Everything you have. All of it. Mine.*

And then He closes with two words that change the temperature of the entire ayah:

**رِّزْقًا مِّن لَّدُنَّا** — *rizqan min ladunnā* — *a provision from Our presence.*

*Min ladunnā* — "from Our presence" — is one of the most intimate phrases in the Quran. *Ladun* is not just "from." It is **"from right beside, from immediately at, from the personal store of."** When Allah teaches Khiḍr knowledge, He calls it *ʿilman min ladunnā* — knowledge *from Our very presence*. When the believers ask for mercy, they ask for *raḥmatan min ladunka* — mercy *from right beside You*.

So here, the provision is not just *from Allah* in a general sense. It is **handed directly from His presence.** Personally. Closely. As if from His own hand.

Imagine the picture this builds. The Quraysh think their food comes from caravans. Allah is saying: **every fruit that ever reached your table, I handed to you Myself.**

[PAUSE]

What this construction reveals: when we are afraid of losing what we have, we have already misidentified where it came from. We think we are protecting our own work. We are actually clinging to a gift while pushing away the Giver's hand. The fear of loss is the fear of an *imaginary* loss — because the thing we think is ours was never ours to lose. It was a daily, intimate gift from a Presence we have been trained not to see.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have three linguistic discoveries.

*Nutakhaṭṭaf* — the word that revealed the fear was not strategic but primal: a self-image of small prey in a predatory world.

*Numakkin* — the word that showed the fear was already factually contradicted: you have already been rooted by the very hand you are rejecting.

*Yujbā ... rizqan min ladunnā* — the construction that erased the Quraysh as the agent of their own provision and named the real Source: a Presence that has been handing them every fruit, daily, intimately, while they imagined it was their own cleverness that brought it.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Stated Reason Is Almost Never the Real Reason

There is a moment that happens in nearly every human life. You are talking to someone — a friend, a parent, a spouse — and they give you a reason for something. *I can't take that job because of the commute. I can't leave that relationship because of the kids. I can't pray because I'm too busy.*

You listen. The reason sounds rational. It has logic. It has structure. And yet, somewhere in your body, you know that **this is not the real reason.** The real reason is sitting underneath the stated reason, like a stone underneath water. You can see the shape of it through the surface, but the speaker has covered it with something more presentable.

We do this to ourselves constantly. We are *experts* at this. Our minds are exquisite manufacturers of reasonable-sounding cover stories for fears we cannot face directly.

Modern psychology has a word for this — *rationalization*. We construct a logical-sounding justification for an emotional decision we have already made. The justification is real to us. We believe it. We may even pass a polygraph saying it. But it is a screen.

Here is the question this ayah opens: **what would it look like if Allah peeled the screen back?**

[PAUSE]

That is exactly what is happening in this verse.

The Quraysh give the Prophet ﷺ a stated reason. *We can't follow the guidance because we'll be snatched from our land.* It sounds geopolitical. It sounds strategic. It is the kind of thing a serious person says to another serious person. *Look, we'd love to. We can't. There are real-world consequences.*

Allah's reply does something none of us would expect. He does not engage the stated reason on its own terms. He does not say *let me show you that, actually, you won't be snatched.* He doesn't even argue the prediction.

He pulls the screen back.

He says, in effect: *you are afraid of losing your sanctuary. But look — I am the One who gave you this sanctuary in the first place. The very thing you say you are protecting is a thing you did not make, do not sustain, and cannot truly own. Your fear is not about a future loss. Your fear is about admitting a present truth — that everything you have was already a gift, and you have spent your whole life pretending it was yours.*

The real fear was never *we will be snatched*. The real fear was **what would it mean if everything we have was already given?** Because if everything is already a gift, then we owe gratitude. And we have been refusing to be grateful for our entire lives.

The stated fear is *I will lose what is mine.*
The real fear is *I might have to admit that nothing was ever mine.*

[PAUSE]

Now bring this home.

What are *your* stated reasons? The next time you find yourself saying *I can't pray right now because of work,* or *I can't fast this year because of my health,* or *I can't sit with the Quran because I'm too tired,* or *I can't forgive that person because of what they did* — sit for one second longer than you usually would. And ask: *what is the real reason, sitting under the stated one?*

The Quraysh's stated reason was *we will lose our land*. The real reason was *we will have to admit we never owned it.*

What is your real reason?

Allah, in this ayah, is showing us that He sees both. He hears the stated reason — He even quotes it back to us in the Quran. *Wa qālū* — *and they said.* He honors that we said something. But then He gently, terrifyingly, gives the answer to what we actually meant.

That is the kind of mercy that requires honesty. Because the mercy of Allah is not the mercy of a friend who lets us keep our cover story. It is the mercy of a Beloved who lifts the cover off because He knows we will not heal until we can see what is underneath.

**The practical application:** when you next refuse something you know is good for your soul, write down the stated reason. Then, before going to sleep, ask Allah to show you what is underneath it. Do not be surprised when, sometime in the next few days, the real reason surfaces. That is the door this ayah opens — the willingness to be seen.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: We Are Protecting the Wrong Thing

There is an old story — you've probably heard versions of it — about a man who is so afraid of being robbed that he never leaves his house. He spends his life inside, guarding his treasure. Decades pass. He dies. They open the safe. And inside is a stack of money from a currency that no longer exists.

He guarded a thing he could not lose, because what he was guarding was already worthless — and the life he could have lived was the actual treasure.

This is what the Quraysh are doing.

They are guarding their sanctuary by refusing the One who made it a sanctuary. They are protecting their trade by rejecting the One who draws every fruit to their doors. They are clinging to a gift while spitting at the hand that gave it.

And we — sitting fourteen hundred years later, in our houses, in our cars, in our jobs — we do this every single day.

[PAUSE]

Think about what you are actually afraid of losing. Your health. Your job. Your reputation. Your relationships. Your savings. The respect of your family. The opinion of your community.

Now ask yourself: *who gave me these things?*

Not who *bought* them. Not who *worked* for them. Not who *earned* them. Who *gave* them?

Because if you trace the chain of causation back far enough — back through the coincidences, back through the doors that opened at the right moment, back through the people who came into your life at the exact time you needed them, back through the body that was healthy enough to do the work, back through the mind that was clear enough to make the decisions — at some point the chain reaches a place where **you were not the cause.**

Allah was.

And here is the disorienting part. The very things we are most afraid of losing are the things we did the least to earn. The accident of being born in a particular family. The accident of a healthy body. The accident of an opportunity that landed in our lap. The accident of meeting the right person at the right party. The very ground we are standing on, in the most literal sense — the very fact that the earth beneath us is solid right now and not shaking — is a gift we did not arrange.

The Quraysh thought they had a sanctuary because of their cleverness. Allah told them: *I made this a sanctuary. I drew the fruits here. I established you in it.*

You think you have your job because of your résumé. You think you have your family because of your love. You think you have your stability because of your planning.

Some of that is true at one level. But underneath all of it — Allah established it for you. *Numakkin lahum* — *We rooted them.* Past tense. Already done. Before you noticed.

[PAUSE]

So what are we *actually* afraid of losing?

If we tell the truth: we are afraid of losing the *illusion that we did it ourselves*. We are afraid of losing the *narrative* that we are the cause of our own life. Because if Allah was the cause all along, then the entire architecture of our identity — *I built this, I earned this, I made this happen* — collapses. And we have to admit something we have spent our entire lives avoiding: **we are recipients, not authors.**

The ayah ends with the most quietly devastating line in the entire surah:

**وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ** — *but most of them do not know.*

Not *most of them refuse*. Not *most of them deny*. Not *most of them argue*. Most of them **do not know**. They live their whole lives inside a sanctuary built by Allah, eating fruits drawn by Allah, breathing air given by Allah — and they *do not know*. The gift is so total, so constant, so atmospheric, that they cannot see it. They are fish who do not know they are in water.

And when they are told — when the Prophet ﷺ comes and says *follow the guidance* — they react as if accepting the truth would *cost* them something. When in reality the truth is the thing that would let them, for the first time in their lives, see what they had been receiving all along.

[PAUSE]

**The practical application:** for the next twenty-four hours, every time you receive something — food, a kind word, a moment of ease, a green light, an answered prayer, a breath that came easily — pause for two seconds and consciously trace it back. Not in words, just in awareness. *This came from Allah, through that hand, through that opening, through that mercy.* Do not try to feel grateful. Just try to *see*. The seeing itself is the worship. The Quraysh's tragedy was not that they were ungrateful. It was that they *did not know.* Knowing is the first half of gratitude. Most of us are still inside the first half.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Let me show you something now that I held back.

The ayah is built in a perfectly mirrored structure.

The Quraysh's statement has **three movements**:
1. *If we follow the guidance with you* — the conditional act.
2. *We will be snatched* — the verb of loss.
3. *From our land* — the object of fear.

Allah's reply has **three corresponding movements**:
1. *Have We not established for them* — the prior act (His action came *first*).
2. *A safe sanctuary* — the verb of stability (the opposite of snatching).
3. *To which fruits are drawn as a provision from Us* — the object of gift (their "land" is not theirs; it is *His* delivery system).

Each of their three pieces is answered by an opposite piece. The conditional verb is answered by the completed verb. The verb of being snatched is answered by the verb of being rooted. The land they call *theirs* is revealed to be *His*, with everything in it.

Their entire fear is structurally inverted, line by line. The ayah is a mirror, and what they see in it — when Allah holds it up — is the exact opposite of the reality they thought they were defending.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Nutakhaṭṭaf* showed us that the stated reason hides a primal fear of erasure.
The first theme showed us that the real reason for refusing truth is rarely the one we voice.

*Numakkin* showed us that the very stability we cling to was given by the One we reject.
The second theme showed us that we spend our lives protecting things we did not make, from a Giver who could give us so much more.

*Yujbā ... rizqan min ladunnā* showed us that every gift came directly from Allah's presence, through an invisible chain.
And the closing — *most of them do not know* — showed us that the entire tragedy is one of unseeing. The water was always there. The fish refused to admit they were swimming.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the stated reason you have been giving for not doing the thing your soul knows it needs to do? And — sitting with it now — what is the real reason underneath it?

2. Trace the last week of your life. What came to you that you did not earn? What landed in your lap, what door opened, what person showed up? When did you last name those as *rizqan min ladunnā* — provisions from His presence?

3. What in your life are you *protecting* that you did not *make*?

4. If Allah were to lift the cover off your stated reasons today, what do you think He would show you? Are you willing to be shown?

5. The Quraysh said *we will be snatched from our land*. What is your *land* — the thing whose loss feels unsurvivable? And what would change if you could see, for one moment, that the land was a gift, not a possession?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The reason we refuse guidance is almost never the reason we say — and the things we are most afraid of losing are gifts we never owned, given by the very One we are pushing away to keep them.

### The Closing Invitation

Sit somewhere, for five minutes, sometime in the next twenty-four hours. Look at the ground. Look at the walls. Look at whatever fruit is in your kitchen. And try — just try — to see it for what it is. Not yours. *Min ladunhu*. From His presence. Delivered to you.

This is not an obligation. It is an invitation to *see what has been here all along*. The Quraysh's tragedy was not that they were bad people. It was that they lived inside a sanctuary and did not know they were in one. May we, even for one afternoon, know it.

### Du'ā

Ya Allah, You who rooted us in places we did not build, who draws to our doors fruits we did not plant, who hands us provision from Your own presence in ways we have failed to see — open our eyes to what is already underneath us. Lift the cover from the reasons we have been giving ourselves. Let us see what is real. And give us the courage to follow the guidance, even when our stated reasons say we cannot afford to.

Āmīn.
