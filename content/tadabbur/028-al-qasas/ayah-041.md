# The Imams Who Call to the Fire

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 41

**Arabic:**
وَجَعَلْنَٰهُمْ أَئِمَّةً يَدْعُونَ إِلَى ٱلنَّارِ ۖ وَيَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ لَا يُنصَرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan yadʿūna ilā an-nār, wa yawma al-qiyāmati lā yunṣarūn.*

**Translation:**
And We made them leaders inviting to the Fire, and on the Day of Resurrection they will not be helped.

---

## Introduction: The World Behind the Verdict

Before we step into this ayah, we have to step into the world it ends.

Imagine you are sitting in Makkah, in the years when the Prophet ﷺ is speaking to a tribe that owns the keys to the most sacred sanctuary on earth. The Quraysh are not just powerful — they are *leaders*. They are the appointed custodians of pilgrimage. They are admired. They are imitated. The Arabs look at them the way the world looks at empires.

And the Quran, in this surah, sits them down to listen to a long story. Not a sermon. A story. The story of another tribe, another sanctuary, another civilization — Egypt. The story of a man named Mūsā ʿalayhi as-salām who grew up inside the very palace of the people who hunted his life. The story of a Pharaoh whose throne was so absolute that the rivers of his land seemed to flow because he commanded them to.

The surah builds slowly. We see Pharaoh's cruelty. We see Mūsā's escape. We see the years in Madyan. We see the return. We see the staff. We see the magicians. We see the chase. We see the sea.

And then — the sea closes.

Pharaoh drowns. His armies drown. The chapter of his life is over.

And right here, just as the listener exhales, just as the ear expects a moral — *"so be warned"*, *"so reflect"*, *"so do not be like him"* — Allah does something else.

He does not address us.

He delivers a verdict.

> **"And We made them leaders inviting to the Fire, and on the Day of Resurrection they will not be helped."**

[PAUSE]

Eleven words. And in those eleven words, Pharaoh stops being a person and becomes a *category*. He stops being history and becomes a *type*. He stops being a body in the sea and becomes a *door* — a door that other people, in other times, are still walking through.

Here is what should unsettle us before we even open the words: the Quran does not say *Pharaoh was punished*. It does not say *Pharaoh was destroyed*. It says something far stranger. It says **Pharaoh was made into something**. Made into what? Made into an *imām*. Made into a leader. Made into the kind of person other people follow.

What does it mean for Allah to *make someone* a leader to ruin? And why is this said about Pharaoh *after* he is already dead?

Stay with that question. We will spend the next hour answering it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Jaʿalnāhum* — "We Made Them"

Let's begin somewhere unexpected. Not with the most dramatic word in the ayah, but with the most ordinary one. The verb at the very beginning: **جَعَلْنَا** — *jaʿalnā* — "We made."

Before we open it inside the ayah, let me teach you something about this word in Arabic.

In English, "made" is mostly a manufacturing word. You make a chair. You make dinner. You make a mistake. It's about producing something that wasn't there.

In Arabic, *jaʿala* is wider and stranger. Yes, it can mean to manufacture, but it can also mean **to render**, **to designate**, **to appoint**, **to assign a status**, **to cause something to become something else**. When the Quran says Allah *jaʿala* the night a covering and the day for seeking provision, it doesn't mean He fabricated them as objects. It means He *assigned them their function*.

So *jaʿala* is not just creation. It is **categorization**. It is the act of placing a thing inside a role, inside a meaning, inside a destiny.

Now bring that into our ayah.

> **"And We *made* them — *jaʿalnāhum* — leaders inviting to the Fire."**

Notice something. Allah is not saying *they became* leaders. He is not saying *they appointed themselves*. He is saying **We assigned them this role**.

Stay with me, because this is one of the hardest moments in this ayah, and we have to be careful.

Pharaoh chose his arrogance. Pharaoh chose his oppression. Pharaoh chose, in front of his court, to say *"I am your lord, the most high."* Every act of his tyranny was his own free decision, and the Quran is unambiguous about that elsewhere — *they wronged themselves*, *they earned this*, *Allah did not wrong them but they wronged their own souls*.

So when Allah says *We made them leaders to the Fire*, what is this?

Here is what the construction is pointing toward. There is a distinction in the Quran between two kinds of leadership. There is the leadership that Allah grants as **honor** — like when He tells Ibrāhīm, *"Indeed I am making you an imām for the people"* (2:124). And there is the leadership that Allah *designates as consequence* — leadership that is, in its essence, a kind of judgment.

Pharaoh chose to be the front of the line. He pushed his way to the front. He claimed the position with violence. And Allah, in His justice, says: *very well. You wanted the front of the line. You will have it. But you will not be leading where you thought you were leading. You will be leading to the Fire — and history will see you do it.*

[PAUSE]

This is a terrifying form of divine justice. Not the kind that interrupts. The kind that **lets the trajectory complete itself**. You wanted to be an imām. You are an imām. You wanted followers. You have followers. You wanted history to remember your name. History remembers your name. *Everything you asked for, you have been given.*

And it is the punishment.

There is an Arabic phrase the scholars use for this: *al-jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal* — "the recompense is from the same genus as the deed." You asked to lead. You will lead. You will lead exactly where your leading was always going to end.

So *jaʿalnāhum* is not Allah forcing Pharaoh into a role. It is Allah **confirming Pharaoh's chosen trajectory and crystallizing it into a permanent station in the moral universe**.

This is what should make us pause. Because the question this opens is no longer about Pharaoh. The question is: *what trajectory am I confirming with my choices today, that Allah may one day crystallize into who I have become?*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Aʾimmatan* — The Inverted Imāms

Now we come to the word that should make every Muslim ear sit up straight.

**أَئِمَّةً** — *aʾimmatan*. The plural of *imām*.

Let me teach you what this word means in its bones, because we use it so casually that it has lost its weight.

The root is **أ-م-م** (*ʾ-m-m*), and the noun *umm* from this root means **mother**. *Ummah* means a community. *Imām* means the one who stands in front — the one toward whom others orient themselves.

But there is a deeper sense in the lexicons. *Imām* is connected to the idea of **a path, a road, a way that is plainly seen**. In some classical usages, the word *imām* itself can refer to the **clear road** — the road so visible that you orient yourself by it. So an *imām* is not just a person at the front. An *imām* is **a visible direction**. A *living road sign*.

This is why the Quran calls Ibrāhīm an *imām*. He was a walking road. People who looked at him knew where to go.

Now look at what this ayah does.

> **"We made them *aʾimmatan* — imāms — inviting to the Fire."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the violence of that.

The word designed to describe Ibrāhīm — the word that points to mothers, to clarity, to the visible road of guidance — is here applied to Pharaoh. And it is not metaphorical. It is structural. The Quran is saying that **Pharaoh and Ibrāhīm occupy the same architectural slot in human history**. Both are *imāms*. Both are visible directions. Both are road signs.

The difference is what they point toward.

This is the chilling symmetry. Leadership is not a moral category by itself. It is a **structure** — a position, a slot, an architecture of influence. And that slot can be filled either way. There is an imāmah of light, and there is an imāmah of fire. There is a mother of guidance, and there is a mother of ruin. The structure is the same. The content is everything.

Notice what this does to a category we treat as automatically positive. *Leader*. *Influencer*. *The one in front*. We assume these words carry a moral weight. They do not. Being at the front means nothing. The only question is: **what is at the end of the line you are leading?**

Stay with the linguistic detail. The word is *aʾimmatan* — **indefinite**, **plural**. Not *al-aʾimmah* — *the* imāms. Just *aʾimmatan* — imāms, of a kind. Why does this matter?

Because indefiniteness in Arabic often opens a category. It is not naming specific individuals. It is opening a **type**. Allah is not just saying *We made these specific Egyptians into imāms*. He is saying *We made them into a kind of imām that exists in the world, that is reproducible, that has a name, that has a category in the moral architecture of history*.

Pharaoh is not unique. Pharaoh is a **slot**. And the slot can be filled again. And it has been. And it will be.

This is why the Quran tells this story to the Quraysh. The point is not *look at the bad man from long ago*. The point is *this category is still open*. This template is still available. There are imāms in your time, sitting in seats of power, who fill exactly this slot. They call the people somewhere. And what they call them toward — *that* is the question.

[PAUSE]

And let me give you the linguistic detail that I think is the most haunting.

Look at what is **absent** in the description.

Allah says they call **"to the Fire"** — *ilā an-nār*. He does not say they call *to disbelief*. He does not say they call *to oppression*. He does not say they call *to evil*. He skips every middle term and names only **the destination**.

Why?

Because they themselves did not call people to "the Fire." They called people to *glory*. They called people to *the river*. They called people to *the empire*. They called people to *prosperity, security, civilization, order*. No leader ever stands on a platform and says *follow me to ruin*. They stand on a platform and say *follow me to greatness*.

But the Quran does not name the surface call. The Quran names the actual destination. **The advertising is irrelevant. The destination is what's real.**

[PAUSE]

That is the second word. We will return to it. Sit with it for now: the slot of imāmah is open. The advertising is not the destination. The destination is the only thing that matters.

### Element Three: *Yadʿūna* — The Ongoing Calling

Now I want you to notice something so small you might miss it, and yet it is the bone of this ayah.

**يَدْعُونَ** — *yadʿūna* — "they call" or "they invite."

This is a **present-tense** verb. The form *yafʿulūn* in Arabic — what grammarians call the *muḍāriʿ* — is the verb of ongoing, repeating, continuous action. It is not "they called" (past). It is not "they will call" (future only). It is **they call** — they keep calling — they are *still calling*.

Now hold that against what we already know from the surah.

Pharaoh is dead.

The man drowned in the previous ayah. His body, the Quran will tell us elsewhere, was preserved as a sign. He is gone. His tongue does not speak. His arm does not gesture. His court is dust.

And yet — *yadʿūna*. They are *still* calling.

[PAUSE]

How?

This is the moment the ayah cracks open. Because the present-tense verb forces us to ask a question we would never have asked otherwise: **how does a dead leader keep recruiting?**

And the answer is the most important political and spiritual insight in this entire surah.

A leader is not just a person. A leader is **a pattern that outlives them**. When a person stands in front of others and calls them somewhere, what they leave behind is not just a memory. They leave behind a *model* — a way of being, a way of arranging power, a way of justifying violence, a way of speaking to subjects, a way of imagining the self. That model continues to call.

Pharaoh's body died. But *pharaonism* did not die. The *template* did not die. Every tyrant who has stood since and said *I am the state*, *I am the law*, *those who oppose me are vermin* — has been answering Pharaoh's call. They are not original. They are responding. They are downstream. They are the followers of an imām whose body has been dust for three thousand years.

This is what *yadʿūna* — *they are still calling* — actually means.

And it is true in the small as well as the large. A father who built a house of cruelty does not stop calling when he dies. His children carry the call. His grandchildren carry the call. The pattern *yadʿū* — keeps inviting — keeps recruiting — keeps reaching toward new generations from beyond the grave. Until someone, somewhere in the bloodline, has the courage to break the pattern.

Pharaoh is the archetype. But the structure he exposes is universal. **Every life is a call. The call does not stop when the life stops.**

[PAUSE]

And this is why the Quran told the story in the first place. Not because Pharaoh is a curiosity. Because the call is *still in the room*. Right now. In our time. From the dead. The question for any listener — the Quraysh then, us now — is *which call am I answering?* Because we are always answering one.

[PAUSE]

Let me hold the three elements together before we move.

*Jaʿalnāhum* — Allah confirmed Pharaoh's chosen trajectory and crystallized it into a permanent station.

*Aʾimmatan* — the slot Pharaoh fills is the same architectural slot as Ibrāhīm. Leadership is a structure; the question is its content. The advertising is not the destination.

*Yadʿūna* — the call does not die with the caller. A leader is a pattern. A pattern is a call. A call outlives the mouth that spoke it.

Now let's ask what all this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Kinds of Front

Let me start somewhere familiar.

Think of someone you know who is *in front*. A boss. A teacher. An older sibling. A figure on a screen with millions of followers. A parent. An imām in a masjid. A senator. A father at the head of his family table.

These people are all *in front*. They all occupy that architectural slot the Quran is naming. And we tend, in our culture, to evaluate them on one axis: are they competent? Are they successful? Do they get results?

But this ayah breaks that frame in half. Because it shows us that *being in front* is not, by itself, anything. It is a position. A geometry. A slot. The question is not whether someone is in front. The question is: **what direction does the front point?**

There are two kinds of front. There is the front that leads people **upward** — toward something better than themselves, toward courage they did not know they had, toward humility, toward the willingness to take blame, toward worship, toward truth-telling, toward fear of Allah, toward mercy. That front is the imāmah of Ibrāhīm. That front, the Quran tells us, was granted to him *after* he had been tested in his own self — *"And when his Lord tested Ibrāhīm with words and he fulfilled them, He said: I am making you an imām for the people"* (2:124). The order matters. The imāmah came *after* the self was disciplined. The man led others because he had first led himself.

And then there is the front that leads people **downward** — toward greater appetite, toward greater fear, toward the willingness to harm an outsider, toward worship of the leader's image, toward enemy-making, toward the sense that everything is permitted because we are us. This front is the imāmah of Pharaoh. And the Quran tells us how it forms: the man takes the front *without* having disciplined himself. He pushes to the position from the appetite of the nafs, not from the testing of the heart. He does not *receive* leadership from Allah after passing through the fire of self-mastery. He **seizes** leadership in flight from that fire.

You can recognize the difference even from the outside. The Ibrāhīm imām, when challenged, says *I do not love things that set.* He renounces what he has. The Pharaoh imām, when challenged, says *do you dare to speak before I have given you permission? I will cut off your hands and your feet.* He grasps at what he has.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this home.

What kind of front are you?

You are in front of someone. There is someone who looks at you and orients themselves to your model. A child. A younger sibling. A coworker who is newer than you. A student. A person who reads what you write online. A person who watches how you treat your spouse and quietly decides whether marriage is safe. You are an imām to someone, in some scale.

And the question this ayah forces is not *am I successful*. The question is: **the people who follow my model — where does my model take them?**

Not where do I tell them I am taking them. Where am I actually taking them.

Because remember — Pharaoh did not advertise the Fire. Pharaoh advertised greatness, security, civilization, prosperity. The destination was the Fire. The advertising was the empire.

So this is the practical work this ayah opens.

Stop and ask, of every position of leadership you hold — every front you occupy, however small — *where does this lead the people who follow me?* If I were to imagine my child, ten years older, having absorbed everything I have modeled — what kind of human are they? If I were to imagine my employees, having internalized the values I project — what kind of workplace have I built? If I were to imagine my students, my readers, my friends, my family — having taken me as a road sign — where have they ended up?

This is the discipline of being a front. You cannot afford to evaluate your leadership by your *intention*. You must evaluate it by your **destination**.

And here is the practical step: **for the next week, do not ask yourself whether you are leading well. Ask yourself where you are leading.** Pick one person who follows you, in any sense. Imagine the trajectory of becoming like you, sustained over twenty years. Look at what that person becomes. That is your leadership. Adjust accordingly.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: *Lā Yunṣarūn* — The Unhelped

Now we come to the second half of the ayah, and we have not yet sat with it.

> **"And on the Day of Resurrection, *lā yunṣarūn* — they will not be helped."**

I want to teach this through a feeling that every human being knows.

There is a moment, in every crisis, when you reach out. You may not even know who you are reaching for. But the hand goes out. The chest opens. The mouth forms a word. You are looking for *help*. You are looking for someone to stand with you, intervene for you, take your side, speak for you, defend you, save you, even hold you.

This reflex is wired into us. It is one of the most basic human acts. When the fire is at the door, *we call*.

The Quran knows this. The Quran is built on this. The Quran is, in many ways, the long answer to that human reaching — *I am here. Reach for Me. I will not abandon you.* The whole architecture of the relationship between Allah and the believer is the certainty that **when you reach, there is Someone to receive your reaching**.

Now hear what this ayah does.

It looks at Pharaoh — and his pattern, and everyone who fills his slot — and it says: **on the Day of Resurrection, they will not be helped.**

[PAUSE]

Notice what the verb is in the **passive**. *Lā yunṣarūn* — "they will not be helped." Not "no one will help them" — though that is the meaning. The passive is intentional. It does not name a refuser. It just names an absence.

In Arabic, when the passive is used like this, it often signals a kind of universal absence. Not *I refused to help them*. Not *the angels did not help them*. Not *their followers did not help them*. Just — **the act of being-helped does not occur**. The universe does not produce help for them. The category of help, for them, is empty.

Now feel what this means.

The reflex of a human being in extremity is to reach out. To call. To cry. The Pharaoh-type, in life, did not need to. He had his guards. He had his court. He had his river. He had his ideology. He had a state apparatus that responded to his every gesture. Help was *automatic*. Help was *infrastructure*. He did not have to ask for help because help was always already arriving.

And then comes the Day when all of that infrastructure is gone. The state has dissolved. The court is judging him. The river has dried up. The guards are themselves on trial. And he reaches, the way every human reaches — and there is **nothing**.

This is the deepest justice of this ayah. The man who built an entire system of help around himself, who was helped by everyone, who never had to do without help — *will not be helped*. The man who lived as if he were the source of help to others — finds that he himself is the one in need, and no source exists.

[PAUSE]

But this is not just about Pharaoh.

This is about a category of life — a way of living — that builds its security on **created help** and forgets that all help is, in the end, from Allah.

Listen to the contrast. The believer is the one who, even in the height of his ease, says *iyyāka nastaʿīn* — *You alone we ask for help*. Every day, seventeen times in his obligatory prayers. He is the one who, when the world is going well, still locates the source of help in Allah. He does not let his eyes lie to him. He sees the doctor, the friend, the parent, the salary, the system — and behind all of them, he sees the Hand that arranges them. So when the systems collapse, his reaching does not collapse with them. His hand knows where to go.

The Pharaoh-type is the opposite. He has lived his whole life so embedded in created help that he cannot see anything else. He thinks the help *is* the doctor, the friend, the army, the state. So when those things go — his reaching has nowhere to go. He does not know where Allah is, because he has spent his whole life with his eyes on the intermediaries.

This is what *lā yunṣarūn* names. It is not arbitrary punishment. It is **the natural consequence of building a life that did not know where help came from**.

[PAUSE]

So the practical work of this theme is this.

Right now, in your life, where is your help coming from?

Not in your theory. In your *reflex*. When something goes wrong, who do you reach for *first*? When the fear comes, what is the first hand you grab? Is it the phone? Is it the friend? Is it the bottle? Is it the screen? Is it your own mind, trying to think its way out?

That first reach is the truth of where your help lives.

The Pharaoh-pattern is to live with so much created help that the reflex of the heart toward Allah atrophies. The Ibrāhīm-pattern is to keep that reflex alive, even in the middle of ease — to say *iyyāka nastaʿīn* even when nothing seems to be wrong, so that the muscle stays strong for the day when everything is wrong.

And there will be a day when everything is wrong.

The work is to make sure that, on that day, your hand knows where to go.

The practical step: **for the next seven days, before any other reach — before the phone, before the friend, before the conversation — make a small private reach toward Allah first.** Even if it is two words. *Yā Rabb.* Then move. Train the reflex. Build the muscle. Make sure that the deepest hand in your chest knows the address.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look back at the whole ayah and see what it has been doing.

> **"And We made them leaders inviting to the Fire — and on the Day of Resurrection they will not be helped."**

There are two halves. They look like two separate statements. They are not. They are one structure.

The first half names what this kind of leader **did in life**: he called others. He pulled others toward him. He was the destination toward which other people moved. He was the recipient. The one to whom motion flowed.

The second half names what this kind of leader **receives in the next life**: he calls for help, and no one comes. No motion flows toward him. He is the one reaching, and the reach goes empty.

The symmetry is exact. **In this life, the Pharaoh-type was the one others moved toward. In the next life, no one moves toward him.** The current of the universe, which he organized around himself, reverses. The followers who came to him — they are not there. The infrastructure of help that surrounded him — it is not there. He stands in the architectural slot of *the one others come to*, and the architecture is empty.

This is the divine pedagogy of the ayah. It is not arbitrary. It is symmetric. It is *al-jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal* — the recompense from the same genus as the deed. He lived as the magnet. He dies as the magnet that has lost its field.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us that *jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan* is a confirmation of trajectory — Allah crystallizing a chosen direction into a station. That *aʾimmatan* is a structural slot that can be filled with light or fire. That *yadʿūna* is a present-tense call that does not die.

The thematic side showed us that we ourselves occupy that slot in small scale, and that the destination of our model is the only honest evaluation of our leadership. And that the way we live now is teaching our hand where to reach when extremity comes.

Together they form one teaching: **a human being is, every day, building a pattern of life that will either be confirmed by Allah as guidance or confirmed as ruin — and the test is not what we call ourselves, but where our actual call takes the people downstream of us, and where our actual hand reaches when the systems we depend on collapse.**

### Questions to Carry

1. The people who follow me — in any sense, in any scale — *where does my actual model take them?* Not where do I tell them I am taking them. Where am I taking them.

2. What pattern am I living that, if Allah were to crystallize it into a permanent station, I would not want to be?

3. When fear comes, what is the first hand my heart reaches for? Is that hand attached to anything real on the Day when everything else is gone?

4. Whose call am I answering? In what I admire, in what I imitate, in what I aspire to — whose *yadʿū* is reaching me from across the years, and where is it taking me?

5. Where in my life is my advertising different from my destination? What am I saying I am building, while actually building something else?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Every life is a road that calls others to walk it; the only honest question is where that road ends — and whether, when our own infrastructure collapses, we have built a reach toward the only Helper who remains.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not a verdict on people far away. It is a mirror.

You are an imām in some scale. Your life is calling people somewhere. Your reach has been trained, by years of practice, to land somewhere. The work of this ayah is to look honestly at both — the call you are sending, and the reach you have built — and to bring them into alignment with the Ibrāhīm-pattern instead of the Pharaoh-pattern.

This is not a one-time decision. It is a daily discipline. It is what every *iyyāka nastaʿīn* in every salāh is training. It is what every *Ihdinā aṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* is asking — *make me, Allah, an imām on the road that leads upward; not the road that leads to fire; and let my hand reach toward You when everything else falls away*.

That is the invitation. Not obligation. **Invitation**. The slot is open. Both slots are open. Choose which one you will fill, every day, in the small choices that nobody sees.

### Du'ā

O Allah, do not let us be among those whose lives call others to ruin while their tongues claim to call to good. Train our reach, in the days of ease, toward You alone — so that on the Day when no created helper remains, our hand still knows the address of its Help. And place us, by Your mercy, in the imāmah of Ibrāhīm — not the imāmah of Pharaoh — so that the people downstream of our lives find, at the end of the road we built for them, **You**.

Āmīn.
