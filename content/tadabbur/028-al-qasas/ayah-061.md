# The Promise You Cannot Cash and the Promise You Already Spent

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 61

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> أَفَمَن وَعَدْنَاهُ وَعْدًا حَسَنًا فَهُوَ لَاقِيهِ كَمَن مَّتَّعْنَاهُ مَتَاعَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا ثُمَّ هُوَ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ مِنَ الْمُحْضَرِينَ

**Transliteration:**
> Afaman waʿadnāhu waʿdan ḥasanan fahuwa lāqīhi kaman mattaʿnāhu matāʿa l-ḥayāti d-dunyā thumma huwa yawma l-qiyāmati mina l-muḥḍarīn

**Translation:**
> Is one to whom We have made a beautiful promise — and he will meet it — like one whom We have given the enjoyment of worldly life, then on the Day of Resurrection he will be among those summoned forth?

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To feel this ayah land the way it landed on its first listeners, we need to step back and look at the surah it lives inside.

Surah Al-Qasas — "The Stories" — is a long surah. It tells you the story of Musa, a baby cast into water with nothing, raised in a palace he didn't belong to, exiled into a desert with empty pockets, married into a household by being trusted, and finally returned to confront a Pharaoh who thought his wealth and his army were his identity.

It is, from start to finish, **a surah about who you really are when everything is stripped away.**

And then, just after the Musa story winds down — before the surah reaches its final, devastating climax with the story of Qarun, the man swallowed by his own treasure — Allah inserts a kind of pause. A breath. A few ayahs of pure comparison. Two ayahs before ours, Allah said:

> *"And whatever you have been given is but the enjoyment of worldly life and its adornment. And what is with Allah is better and more lasting. Will you not then use reason?"* (28:60)

That word — **"use reason"** — is doing something. It's telling the listener: *what comes next is not a sermon. It is a question I want you to think about.*

And then comes our ayah.

[PAUSE]

### The Strand This Ayah Belongs To

The surrounding surahs — Al-Naml before, Al-ʿAnkabūt after — are running a strand the Quran returns to over and over: **two men, two ledgers, two destinations.** The strand surfaces in 13:26, in 57:20, in 20:131. It surfaces here in 28:61 — but here it is pressed into its most compact, most surgical form.

Twenty-one Arabic words. One question. Two men.

And one of them is you.

### The Hook

Here is what we are going to discover together.

There is one word in this ayah that does not have an answer.
There is one word that has been **chosen to be ugly**.
There is one structure that **refuses to give the second man a future tense.**
And there is one missing thing — one absence — that, once you see it, will change the way you read every promise Allah has ever made to you.

Let us begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Has No Question Mark — *Afaman*

The ayah opens with one tiny particle: **أَفَمَن** — *afaman*.

Before we touch the word, let us teach the concept.

In English, when someone wants to challenge you with a comparison, they will sometimes ask a question they don't actually want answered. "Is a doctor the same as a witch doctor?" "Is gold the same as gravel?" You hear the question. You know there's no answer expected. The question **is the answer.**

Arabic has a name for this. It is called **istifhām inkārī** — a question whose form is a question, but whose function is denial. It is a rhetorical structure that says: *I'm not really asking. I'm telling you, by asking, that there is no comparison possible.*

Now look back at the ayah.

**Afaman** = "Is one who..."

It is a question. It opens a comparison. It says: "Is the one to whom We made a beautiful promise... like the one whom We gave a little enjoyment..."

And then — notice this — the question **never closes.** There is no question mark. There is no "yes" or "no" expected. The verse simply *describes* the two men, side by side, and then ends.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because something is happening here that is easy to miss.

Allah is not asking *you* to compare them. Allah is **placing them next to each other and walking away** — leaving the comparison standing in the air, like a scale you have to look at yourself.

Here's what is remarkable. In a normal rhetorical question, the speaker still does the work. The speaker raises their eyebrow at you. "Is gold the same as gravel?" — and you can almost see the speaker's expression, doing the comparison *for* you.

But the Quran does not raise its eyebrow. It just says: *one received a promise. One received an enjoyment. One will meet his promise. One will be summoned.* And then it stops.

Why?

Because Allah knows that **a comparison you make yourself stays with you longer than a comparison told to you.** A judgment a parent makes on your behalf, you can rebel against. A judgment you arrived at on your own, sitting in your own silence — that one you live with.

The *afa-* at the start of the ayah is not asking. It is **handing you the scale.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Waʿdan Ḥasanan* — The Promise That Is "Beautiful"

Look at how Allah describes what He gave the first man.

**وَعْدًا حَسَنًا** — *waʿdan ḥasanan*. "A beautiful promise."

Now stop. This is strange wording.

You would expect "a true promise." Or "a great promise." Or "a generous promise." Or "a guaranteed promise." Those would be the natural words to use about Paradise, about the meeting with Allah, about reward.

Instead, Allah uses **ḥasan** — *beautiful*.

Why beautiful?

Let us slow down. *Ḥasan* in classical Arabic does not just mean pretty. It means something that is **good in a way that pleases the soul as it pleases the eye.** It is goodness with aesthetic completeness. It is the word used for someone whose face is balanced, but it is also the word used for a deed that is right in *every* dimension — right in itself, right in timing, right in proportion.

So when Allah calls His promise *ḥasan*, He is saying something very specific: **this is a promise that, when it arrives, will not just be true. It will be beautiful.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment.

Most of us have received promises in our lives that came true but did not come true *beautifully*. A job came through, but six months too late. A relationship was repaired, but with scars. A long-awaited reunion happened, but you were exhausted by the time it did.

We know what it is to have a promise *fulfilled but disappointed*. The fulfillment came, but the beauty leaked out somewhere along the way.

Allah is telling us that His promise is **not like that.**

When it lands — and we will see in a moment how the word *lāqīhi* says it will land — it will land with its beauty intact. The promised meeting will not be a transaction at a counter. It will be the kind of arrival that, looking back, you will say: *this was worth every moment of waiting. This is what I was waiting for and I did not even know.*

That is what *ḥasan* is doing in this ayah.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Lāqīhi* — "He Will Meet It"

Now look at what comes next.

**فَهُوَ لَاقِيهِ** — *fa-huwa lāqīhi*. "And he will meet it."

Teach the concept first. In Arabic, the verb **laqiya** (root: ل-ق-ي) does not just mean "to encounter." It is the word used for **a meeting between two faces.** Two people who turn toward each other. When you "meet" someone in Arabic with *laqiya*, you are not just bumping into them — you are coming face to face.

So when Allah says the first man **"will meet"** the promise — *lāqīhi* — Allah is not saying he will receive a delivery. Allah is saying **he will come face to face with what was promised.**

The promise is not a package. The promise is a *meeting*.

[PAUSE]

And now notice the grammatical form. *Lāqīhi* is an **active participle** — *ism al-fāʿil*. In Arabic, this form has a very particular quality. It is not a verb saying "he will do" something in the future. It is a *describing word* that names him as **the one who, in his very nature, is the meeter of this promise.**

It is the difference between saying "he will swim" and saying "he is a swimmer." The first describes an action. The second describes an identity.

So Allah is not saying "this man will eventually meet the promise." Allah is saying "**this man — this is who he is — he is a meeter of the promise.**"

His meeting with the promise is not an *event* in his future. It is a *fact* in his being. The promise has already, in some sense, become part of who he is. His arrival at it is just the unfolding of what was always true.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Four: The Brutal Asymmetry — *Mattaʿnāhu Matāʿa l-Ḥayāti d-Dunyā*

Now let us turn to the second man.

**كَمَن مَّتَّعْنَاهُ مَتَاعَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا** — *kaman mattaʿnāhu matāʿa l-ḥayāti d-dunyā*. "Like the one whom We gave the enjoyment of worldly life."

Notice something. **There is no promise.**

The first man received *waʿd ḥasan* — a beautiful promise.
The second man received... *mataʿ*. Enjoyment. A loan of pleasure.

There is no covenant in his hand. There is no guarantee. There is just a temporary handful of things.

[PAUSE]

The word *mataʿ* (root م-ت-ع) is a load-bearing word in the Quran. It means **provision that is meant to be used up.** It is the word used for travel provisions — what you pack for a journey, knowing you will eat it before you arrive. It is the word for goods that depreciate. The opposite of *mataʿ* in the Quran's vocabulary is not poverty — it is **what is lasting** (*bāqī*, *khayrun wa-abqā*).

So Allah is not saying the second man was deprived. Allah is saying he was given **the kind of thing that comes with a deadline written into it.**

[PAUSE]

But here is where the ayah cuts deepest. Listen to the **shape** of what the first man got versus what the second man got, side by side.

**First man:** *waʿdan ḥasanan fa-huwa lāqīhi* — a promise, beautiful, and he is its meeter.
**Second man:** *mattaʿnāhu matāʿa l-ḥayāti d-dunyā* — We gave him enjoyment, the enjoyment of the life of this world.

The first man's clause **ends with the meeting.** It pulls forward. It has a destination.

The second man's clause **ends with "this world."** It pulls inward. It has no destination. It terminates in the very thing it was made of.

Read it again in Arabic and you can almost hear it — the first phrase opens outward into the future; the second phrase closes shut around itself. The first phrase has a horizon. The second phrase has a fence.

[PAUSE]

### Element Five: The Word Allah Chose to Be Ugly — *Muḥḍarīn*

And now we come to the most carefully chosen word in the ayah.

**ثُمَّ هُوَ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ مِنَ الْمُحْضَرِينَ** — *thumma huwa yawma l-qiyāmati mina l-muḥḍarīn*. "Then, on the Day of Resurrection, he will be among the summoned-forth."

*Muḥḍarīn*.

Let us teach the concept first. The root is ح-ض-ر — *ḥaḍara* — to be present, to come to attendance. From this root come gentle, ordinary words. *Ḥāḍir* means "the one present." *Ḥaḍrah* means "presence" — even an honored, respected presence.

But *muḥḍar* is different. *Muḥḍar* is the **passive participle of Form IV** (*afʿala*) — *aḥḍara*. And Form IV in this verb adds a sense of **being made to come, being brought, being produced under compulsion.**

This is not "he attended." This is **"he was hauled in."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. Because Allah could have used so many other words. He could have said "he will be in the Fire." He could have said "he will be punished." He could have said "he will be questioned."

Instead, Allah uses a word whose entire meaning is: **he did not come on his own.**

The first man **meets** his promise — *lāqīhi*, active, embracing, face to face, of his own being.

The second man **is brought** to his accounting — *muḥḍar*, passive, hauled, summoned, no agency, no dignity in the entry.

[PAUSE]

And listen to the **sound** of the word. *Muḥḍarīn*. There is something harsh in it. The *ḥ* sound is from deep in the throat — guttural, scraping. The *ḍ* is one of the heaviest letters in Arabic, called *al-mufakhkham*, the "thickened" letter. The whole word has a weight, a resistance, a kind of dragging quality.

It sounds the way it means.

Compare it to *lāqīhi* — soft, flowing, two long vowels, the word almost sings.

The Quran is doing in **sound** what it is doing in **meaning.** The first man's arrival sounds beautiful because his arrival *is* beautiful. The second man's arrival sounds heavy because his arrival *is* heavy.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Six: The Absence That Speaks the Loudest

Now let us read the whole ayah one more time and ask the question we have been building toward:

**What is missing from the second man's description?**

The first man got:
- A promise (*waʿdan*)
- Beautiful (*ḥasanan*)
- A face-to-face meeting (*lāqīhi*)
- A destination (the promise itself)
- Agency (he is the *meeter*)

The second man got:
- Enjoyment (*matāʿ*)
- "Of the life of this world" (a description of what kind of enjoyment, not a promise)
- A summoning (*muḥḍar*)
- The Day of Resurrection (as a venue, not a destination)
- No agency (he is *brought*)

Do you see what is missing?

**The second man was never promised anything.**

Allah never said "We made him a promise." Allah said "We gave him enjoyment." Pleasure was loaned. Nothing was promised.

[PAUSE]

This is the asymmetric absence we were told to look for.

Most of us, when we live the *mataʿ* life — the life of accumulating enjoyments, of chasing what *ḥayāt al-dunyā* offers — feel as though we are working toward something. Feel as though there is a promise on the other side. *If I just earn enough. If I just achieve enough. If I just experience enough — there will be an arrival.*

The ayah is telling us, in its grammatical silence: **there is no promise on the other side of *mataʿ*.**

The first man has a promise to meet. The second man has nothing to meet — and so, having nothing to walk toward, he has to be **dragged** to where the ledger is opened.

That is what *muḥḍar* is doing. It is the grammatical fingerprint of a life that had no destination.

### The Bridge

Look at what we have found. We have found that:

- The ayah is a question Allah refuses to answer for us
- The first man's reward is called *ḥasan* — beautiful — not just true
- The first man's arrival is a **meeting** of two faces
- The first man's meeting is part of his **identity**, not just his future
- The second man's gift has no destination — it terminates in itself
- The second man's arrival is **passive, heavy, dragged**
- And the second man was **never promised anything in the first place**

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about how a human life works.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Kinds of Receiving

Let me start somewhere ordinary.

There is a difference between **receiving a paycheck** and **receiving a gift.**

A paycheck arrives because you worked. It is owed to you. It is *yours* the moment it lands. There is no waiting after it arrives — you cash it, you use it, and it is gone. The next month you have to earn it again.

A gift, on the other hand — a real gift, from someone who knows you — does something different. It arrives, and then it keeps arriving. Years later, you can be sitting at a table and your eye lands on the gift and you remember the person and the moment. The gift didn't deplete. It deepened.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what is strange about being human. **We treat almost all of life like a paycheck and almost nothing like a gift.**

We collect experiences and consume them. We collect achievements and forget them by the next quarter. We get the promotion, and three weeks later we are restless again. We have the dinner we waited for and the next morning we are scrolling for the next one. The *mataʿ* depletes. It is *meant* to deplete. That is what *mataʿ* is.

What this ayah is showing us is that **there is another way to receive.** There is a kind of receiving where the thing received does not deplete — it deepens. And that kind of receiving has a name: **a promise from Allah.**

When Allah promises you something, **He is not paying you a wage.** He is gifting you a meeting.

[PAUSE]

Notice what changes when you live as someone who is *lāqīhi* — a *meeter* of a promise.

Your prayers stop being a transaction. They become a *waiting*. You are not "earning" Paradise — you are *moving toward a face you have been told you will see.*

Your patience in hardship stops being endurance. It becomes *attention.* You are not "putting up" with the world. You are *walking through it on your way somewhere.*

Your generosity stops being a cost. It becomes a *deposit toward a meeting.* When you give for the sake of Allah, you are not losing — you are arranging to be met.

This is what *waʿdan ḥasanan fahuwa lāqīhi* does to a life. **It re-genres your existence.** You stop living as someone who pays bills and starts living as someone who is going to meet a Beloved.

[PAUSE]

**The practical shift:** Tonight, before you sleep, look back on something good that happened today. Most of us, looking back on the good, ask *what did I earn?* Try, instead, to ask: *what was I being shown? What promise was being prepared?* The first question makes your day a paycheck. The second makes it a love letter from someone arranging a meeting.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: How You Arrive Is Who You Are

There is a strange truth about human life: **the way you walk into a room tells the room who you are.**

Watch someone enter a hospital. The patient walks in carrying his diagnosis like he is the one in control of it — he is one kind of person. The patient who is wheeled in unconscious is a different kind of person, even if the body is the same. The entrance reveals the relationship between the person and what is happening to them.

Now look at what this ayah is doing with two entrances.

The first man **walks into his promise**. He is *lāqī* — actively meeting, face turning toward face, arriving as himself.

The second man **is hauled into his accounting.** He is *muḥḍar* — produced, brought forth, no agency at the moment of entrance.

These are not just two outcomes. **They are two postures.** Two relationships between a soul and what is happening to it.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question this puts to us — and I want you to feel it before I unpack it:

**How am I arriving at things in my life?**

Am I *meeting* my Fajr — turning toward it, face to face — or am I *hauled* into it by the alarm clock and my own dragging body?

Am I *meeting* my Quran — sitting before it as someone walking toward a promise — or am I *brought to it* by guilt, by Ramadan pressure, by the obligation to "get through" my daily portion?

Am I *meeting* my own death — preparing for it as one prepares for an awaited meeting — or am I going to be *muḥḍar*, dragged into it kicking, because I never let myself look at it while I was alive?

[PAUSE]

The ayah is teaching us that **how you arrive at the Day of Judgment is a habit you are practicing right now, in how you arrive at everything else.**

The man of the promise has been *practicing meeting* his whole life. Every prayer, every fast, every small turning of the heart toward Allah is rehearsal for the great meeting. By the time the great meeting comes, his nature has been so shaped by meeting that he simply walks in — *fa-huwa lāqīhi* — because that is who he has become.

The man of *mataʿ* has been *practicing consuming* his whole life. He has been receiving and using up, receiving and using up, receiving and using up — and at no point did he turn his face toward anything beyond the thing in his hand. By the time the Day comes, he has no rehearsal for entering anywhere except passively. So he is brought. *Muḥḍar.*

[PAUSE]

This is the terrifying gift of the ayah. It is telling us: **your eternity does not start at death. It starts in how you greet the next thing in front of you.**

The next adhan. The next conversation. The next moment of being alone with your phone in your hand.

Are you meeting it — or are you being dragged into it?

[PAUSE]

**The practical shift:** Pick **one** thing tomorrow that you usually have to be dragged into. The morning prayer. The phone call with the relative you avoid. The reading of a few ayahs before bed. And do this: **before** you do it, pause for ten seconds and *turn your face toward it.* Do not just begin it. *Meet* it. Practice being *lāqī* in something small. Because if you cannot practice meeting in small things, you will not be among the meeters in the big one.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now, having walked through the ayah word by word, look at how it is built. Stand back and see the shape.

The ayah has two halves, and they are designed to **mirror each other in their differences**:

| First Man | Second Man |
|---|---|
| **promise** (*waʿd*) | **enjoyment** (*mataʿ*) — no promise |
| **beautiful** (*ḥasan*) | "of the life of this world" — no beauty named |
| **he is meeter** (active, identity) | **he is among the brought** (passive, no agency) |
| **arrival** is forward | **arrival** is dragged |
| **destination** is the promise | **destination** is the summons |

Notice the **chiasmus** at the heart of the ayah — the way the two phrases are structured as inverse images of each other. The first man's clause moves *outward* toward a beautiful meeting. The second man's clause turns *inward* toward this world and then is *yanked* forward to the Day. The first phrase has gravity pulling it toward Allah. The second has gravity pulling it back to itself — until it is forcibly pulled.

And the entire ayah is wrapped inside one **unanswered question** — *afaman*. Allah has built a comparison so perfectly balanced that the only sane response is *of course these two are not the same.* But He never says it. He lets the architecture say it for Him.

### Bringing It Together

What the linguistic journey discovered, and what the themes discovered, are the same thing said from two directions.

**From the linguistics:** the words Allah chose carry, in their very form, the truth that one life ends in a beautiful meeting and the other ends in a heavy dragging.

**From the themes:** the way you receive and the way you arrive are not separate from each other. The man of the promise is the man who knows how to be met; the man of *mataʿ* is the man who only knows how to consume.

In both cases: **the difference between the two men is not what they had. It is what they were oriented toward.** One was facing Allah his whole life. The other was facing his hand.

### Questions to Carry

1. If someone observed how I receive my blessings — without knowing my prayers — would they see a *meeter* of promises, or a *consumer* of enjoyments?

2. When was the last time I treated something good in my life as a **rehearsal for a meeting** rather than a paycheck to be cashed?

3. What in my daily life am I currently being **dragged into** that I should learn to **meet** instead?

4. If I knew, with absolute certainty, that Allah had made me a *waʿdan ḥasanan* — a beautiful promise — would the way I live the next twenty-four hours change?

5. What is the *mataʿ* in my life that I have started to mistake for a promise?

### One-Sentence Distillation

There are two kinds of lives — one that walks toward a meeting it has been promised, and one that is dragged toward a reckoning it never noticed was coming — and the difference between them is decided not at the end, but in how you receive the next ordinary thing in front of you.

### The Closing Invitation

Allah did not ask you the question in this ayah and then answer it for you. He placed two men in front of you and walked away. He trusted you to look.

So look.

Not in fear. In honesty. Which man's grammar is your life written in?

If you find that, in too many places, your life is being lived in the passive voice — being dragged, being summoned, being pulled into worship and goodness rather than walking toward it — you do not have to despair. You have to **rewrite one verb.**

Tomorrow, pick one thing. And *meet* it.

That is how a *muḥḍar* slowly, by Allah's mercy, becomes a *lāqī*.

### Du'a

> Yā Allah — You who made the promise *ḥasan* and made the meeting real — let me not be among those who are dragged. Teach my face to turn. Teach my heart to walk toward You while I still have legs. And when the Day of meeting comes, let me arrive as one who has been practicing arriving my whole life. Āmīn.
