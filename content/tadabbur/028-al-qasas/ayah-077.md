# The Forgotten Portion: Seeking the Hereafter Without Forgetting Your Share of This World

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to these words:

**وَٱبْتَغِ فِيمَآ ءَاتَىٰكَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلدَّارَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ ۖ وَلَا تَنسَ نَصِيبَكَ مِنَ ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ وَأَحْسِن كَمَآ أَحْسَنَ ٱللَّهُ إِلَيْكَ ۖ وَلَا تَبْغِ ٱلْفَسَادَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُفْسِدِينَ**

*Wabtaghi fīmā ātāka Allāhu ad-dāra al-ākhirata, wa lā tansa naṣībaka mina ad-dunyā, wa aḥsin kamā aḥsana Allāhu ilayka, wa lā tabghi al-fasāda fī al-arḍi, inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-mufsidīn.*

*"But seek, through what Allah has given you, the Home of the Hereafter — and do not forget your share of this world. And do good as Allah has done good to you. And do not seek corruption in the land. Indeed, Allah does not love the corrupters."*

### The World Behind the Words

Before we open a single word of this ayah, I want you to see the room it was spoken in.

There is a man named Qarun. The Quran tells us he was *min qawmi Mūsā* — from the people of Musa, peace be upon him. From the inside. From the believing community. He was not Pharaoh. He was not the enemy across the line. He was one of them.

And Allah had given him — listen to this — treasures so immense that the *keys alone* required a band of strong men to lift. Not the treasures. The keys. [PAUSE]

Try to feel that. The wealth was so beyond ordinary scale that even the apparatus of accessing it had become its own burden. He had crossed the threshold from "wealthy" into a category that almost has no name — a category modern people only meet when we read about certain technology founders, certain oil heirs, certain men whose net worth has more zeroes than their lifespan has days.

And here is the moment the ayah opens into.

His own people — not Musa, not a prophet, but ordinary believers from his community — come to him. They have watched him strut. They have watched him say things like *"I was given this because of knowledge I have"* — the next ayah will record those exact words from his mouth. They have watched the keys grow heavier. And they sense, with the quiet instinct that ordinary people sometimes have when standing near a man about to fall, that something is coming.

So they speak to him. Gently. Without prophetic authority. Without the thunder of revelation. Just with the wisdom Allah has placed in the hearts of His servants.

And what they say to him — *this ayah* — is the wisdom that the entire Quran offers to any human being who has ever had more than they needed and felt the soul slipping.

### The Hook

Here is what is strange about what they tell him. They do not say: *"Give it all up. Renounce the world. Throw the keys into the river."*

They tell him something much harder. They tell him **to use it.** They tell him to seek the Hereafter *through* what he has. And then — and this is the line that stops every careful reader in their tracks — they tell him:

**"Do not forget your share of this world."**

Wait. He is *drowning* in the world. His keys weigh more than the average household. And the counsel to him is *"do not forget your share of this world"*?

What share has he forgotten? [PAUSE]

That is what we are going to spend this hour discovering. Because the answer is not what you expect, and once you see it, you cannot un-see it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ibtaghi* — The Verb of Active Pursuit

The ayah begins with a command: **وَٱبْتَغِ** — *wabtaghi*. "And seek."

Before we touch the word itself, let me teach you something about Arabic verbs that you may not know.

In Arabic, most verbs have a basic form — what scholars call Form I. A simple three-letter root doing simple work. But Arabic has nine derived forms beyond that, and each one *bends* the meaning in a specific direction. Form II often intensifies. Form III often involves another party. Form IV often causes the action. Form V often turns the action back on yourself. And so on.

Form VIII — which is the form *ibtaghi* belongs to — has a very specific flavor: **the agent putting deliberate, sustained, often inward effort into the action.** It is not casual. It is not passive. It is the form you reach for when you want to say someone is *applying themselves* to the verb.

The simple root here is **ب-غ-ي** (*b-gh-y*), which in its base form just means "to want, to desire, to seek." If the counsel had simply said *abghi* — Form I — it would mean "want the Hereafter," "wish for it."

But it says *ibtaghi*. Form VIII. **Pursue it. Apply yourself to it. Make it the object of your active, ongoing, deliberate striving.** [PAUSE]

Now stay with me, because the construction does something even more remarkable.

It does not say *ibtaghi ad-dāra al-ākhirah* — "seek the Hereafter." That would be a clean, direct sentence.

It says *wabtaghi **fīmā ātāka Allāhu** ad-dāra al-ākhirah* — "seek, **in what Allah has given you,** the Home of the Hereafter."

Notice what has been inserted between the verb and its object. *Fīmā ātāka Allāh.* In what Allah has given you. That phrase is shoved right into the middle of the sentence, breaking the natural flow, demanding your attention.

Why?

Because the ayah is not telling Qarun what to seek. It is telling him **where to seek it.**

The Hereafter — that vast, eternal Home — is not somewhere you go *after* leaving the world. It is something you pursue *inside* what Allah has already given you. The wealth itself is the field. The keys themselves are the soil. The treasures themselves are where the seeds of the akhirah are planted.

[PAUSE]

This is the first quiet revolution of the ayah. Renunciation is not the path. **Re-orientation** is the path. The same money, the same house, the same career, the same talent — but pointed in a different direction. Used as the vehicle, not mistaken for the destination.

And the *fī* — that little preposition, "in" — is doing enormous work. The Hereafter is not pursued *with* what Allah has given you. It is not pursued *using* what He has given you. It is pursued *in* it. Inside it. Through it. As if the wealth is a medium, and the Hereafter is a current flowing through that medium toward Allah.

What this single Form VIII verb and that one preposition reveal: **the dunya is not the obstacle to the akhirah — it is the instrument of it, when held correctly.**

### Element Two: *Naṣīb* — A Portion That Has Already Been Apportioned

Now the most famous half of this ayah. The line that has been quoted by every preacher, every parent, every well-meaning relative trying to convince a son or daughter to enjoy life:

**وَلَا تَنسَ نَصِيبَكَ مِنَ ٱلدُّنْيَا** — *wa lā tansa naṣībaka mina ad-dunyā*. "And do not forget your share of this world."

Before we touch *naṣīb*, look at the verb. **Lā tansa.** "Do not forget."

The root is **ن-س-ي** — *n-s-y* — to forget. And forgetting in Arabic has a specific flavor that English flattens out. In English, "forget" is mostly neutral — a mental lapse. In Arabic, *nisyān* can carry an almost moral weight: to lose track of something that should have stayed present to you. To let slip something you had a duty to remember.

So the counsel is not "do not be unaware of your share." It is *"do not let your share slip from your active attention."*

Now the word that has been mistranslated more than almost any word in the Quran: **نَصِيبَكَ** — *naṣībak*. "Your share."

The root is **ن-ص-ب** — *n-ṣ-b*. And here the lexicons all converge: *naṣīb* is not "what you grab" or "what you accumulate." It is **a portion that has been set up, fixed, designated, apportioned by another.**

A *naṣīb* is not something you take. It is something that has been *allotted to you.* [PAUSE]

Stay with that. The whole moral universe of this word lives in that distinction.

A grabber has no *naṣīb* — a grabber has loot. *Naṣīb* implies a Giver. *Naṣīb* implies a portion that exists because Someone set it up for you, fixed it in place, designated it as yours and not someone else's.

So when the ayah says "do not forget *your naṣīb* from the dunya," it is not saying "do not forget to enjoy yourself." It is saying:

**Do not forget that Allah has already apportioned to you a specific share of this world — and that share has limits, has shape, has been designated for purposes only He knows. Stay aware of what He has placed in your hand. Don't reach past it. Don't trample it. Don't lose track of it.**

And here is where the classical scholars give us a sketch in three angles, each one revealing a different face of the same truth.

**One angle**, voiced by mufassirūn like Ibn Kathir and many of the salaf: your *naṣīb* from this world is what you send ahead. The only portion that is truly yours is what you spend in the path of Allah, what you give in charity, what you build for the akhirah. Everything else will be left behind for inheritors. So "do not forget your share" means: do not forget that your real share of this dunya is what you forwarded to the next.

**Another angle**, voiced by other classical voices: your *naṣīb* is the lawful enjoyment of what Allah permitted — food, marriage, rest, beauty, the company of family. Do not become so consumed with accumulation that you forget you were also given a body that needs rest, a soul that needs joy, relationships that need presence. Hoarding is not the same as living.

**A third angle**, which the structure of the ayah itself seems to suggest: your *naṣīb* of this world is the *opportunity* of this world — the brief, finite window in which work can still be done. Time itself is the *naṣīb*. The years are the share. Do not forget that you have been allotted a specific number of breaths in which to seek the Hereafter — and once those breaths run out, the share is closed.

These are not three competing readings. They are three camera angles on one reality. **Your portion of this world is what you do with it before it ends.** What you send forward, what you live within lawfully, what you accomplish in the window you were given — all three are *the same naṣīb*, seen from three sides.

[PAUSE]

And now — *now* — return to Qarun.

Why is *he*, of all people, being told not to forget his share of the dunya?

Because here is the strange truth the Quran is laying bare: **a man can be soaked in dunya and have entirely forgotten his share of it.** He can be drowning in wealth and have completely lost track of *naṣīb* — the apportioned portion. He thought the dunya was an open buffet to be devoured without limit. The counselors are telling him: it was never that. It was always a *portion* — bounded, designated, set up by Another for a purpose. And in your grasping, you have forgotten the very thing that grasping was supposed to be about.

You cannot consume your *naṣīb* by overconsumption. You can only consume it by *forgetting what it was for.*

### Element Three: *Ahsin kamā ahsana Allāhu ilayk* — The Mirror Command

The third movement of the ayah:

**وَأَحْسِن كَمَآ أَحْسَنَ ٱللَّهُ إِلَيْكَ** — *wa aḥsin kamā aḥsana Allāhu ilayk*. "And do *ihsan* as Allah has done *ihsan* to you."

I am going to leave *ihsan* in Arabic for a moment, because no English word holds it.

The root **ح-س-ن** — *ḥ-s-n* — means beauty, excellence, goodness. *Ihsan* is the verbal noun of Form IV (*aḥsana*), which here means "to do something with excellence, to do good, to make beautiful, to confer benefit."

The Prophet ﷺ defined *ihsan* in the famous hadith of Jibril as: *"that you worship Allah as though you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He sees you."*

So *ihsan* is not just "be nice." It is to act with the quality of a presence — to do the right thing as if the One who sees everything is right there watching. Because He is.

Now look at the structural symmetry of the command:

**Aḥsin — kamā — aḥsana Allāhu ilayk.**
*Do ihsan — just as — Allah has done ihsan to you.*

The same verb appears twice. Once as a command to the human being. Once as a statement of what Allah has already done. The grammatical structure puts them on the same line, looking at each other across the conjunction *kamā* — "just as."

The Arabic *kamā* is a particle of comparison — *like, as, in the manner of.* It draws a parallel. It says: *what you do here should mirror what was done there.*

[PAUSE]

This is what I want you to feel. The ayah is not commanding you to be a good person in the abstract. It is showing you a mirror.

It is saying: Allah has already poured *ihsan* on you. Excellence, beauty, benefit, generosity beyond what you earned. Your existence is *ihsan*. Your faculties are *ihsan*. Your wealth — *Qarun's* wealth, the keys weighing down strong men — that itself is *ihsan* from Allah, freely given to one who did nothing to deserve treasures of that scale.

Now look in the mirror. Your treatment of others should mirror His treatment of you. The flow of *ihsan* that came down to you from above should flow outward from you to those around you. **You are not the terminus of generosity. You are a conduit of it.**

And here is something subtle. The ayah does not say *aḥsin kamā uḥsina ilayk* — "do good as good has been done to you" — in a passive, neutral way. It names the Source. *Kamā aḥsana **Allāhu** ilayk.* Just as **Allah** has done good to you.

Because if you forget the Source, you will think you are the source. You will start saying — exactly what Qarun is about to say in the next ayah — *"I was given this because of knowledge I have."* You will mistake the gift for the proof of your own greatness. And the moment you mistake the gift for evidence of self, the gift becomes a weight that will break you.

The mirror has to stay clear: *Allah* did this *to me*. Therefore *I* do this *to others*.

This third element reveals: **the ethical life is not invention — it is reflection. Your treatment of the world should be a mirror of how the world has been treated to you.**

---

### Bridge

We have walked through three elements:

A verb of active, deliberate pursuit — *ibtaghi* — placed not against the dunya but *inside* it.

A noun of designated portion — *naṣīb* — that re-frames "your share" as something allotted, not seized.

A command of mirrored excellence — *aḥsin kamā aḥsana* — that turns ethics from invention into reflection.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Forgettings

I want you to think of someone you know — or maybe yourself — who has been *too busy.*

Not lazy. Not careless. Not selfish. *Busy.* In the very particular modern way that has eaten the souls of a generation.

The kind of busy where you scroll the calendar and every block is colored in. Where you fall asleep mid-conversation with your spouse because the day was that long. Where your children stopped asking you for things because they learned you would say "later." Where the gym membership is paid and unused. Where the Quran on the shelf has a thin film of dust because the project at work was on fire, and then the next project was on fire, and then the next.

That person is not seeking dunya for its own sake. They will tell you, with sincerity, that they are working *for their family.* They are saving *for the kids' education.* They are building *something that will outlast them.*

And yet — somewhere in the middle of all that — something has been forgotten.

This is what the ayah is naming when it tells Qarun **lā tansa naṣībaka mina ad-dunyā.**

Because there are two ways a human being can lose this world.

**The first** is the way we expect: by clinging to it, drowning in it, devouring it, mistaking it for the destination. This is the obvious failure. This is Qarun on the surface — the man with the heavy keys.

**The second** is the way we don't expect: by *grinding through it without ever inhabiting it.* By treating the dunya as a long, joyless treadmill on which you accumulate without ever pausing to notice that you are *alive*, that your wife is right there, that your child just said something beautiful, that the sun is going down and you have not yet thanked Allah for the day He gave you.

Both of these are *forgetting your naṣīb.* [PAUSE]

The first man drowns in the dunya because he does not see it as a portion — he sees it as an open buffet.

The second man drowns in the dunya because he does not see it as a portion — he sees it as a relentless accumulation engine with no off switch.

Neither one is *living within their naṣīb.* Neither one is treating the dunya as what it actually is: **a defined, bounded, gift-given share that has within it the materials for the akhirah, the materials for joy, the materials for rest, the materials for relationship — and that runs out the moment the breath does.**

The ayah's miracle is that it addresses both forgettings at once. *Wabtaghi fīmā ātāka Allāhu ad-dāra al-ākhirah* — don't drown in the dunya, seek the akhirah through it. *Wa lā tansa naṣībaka mina ad-dunyā* — don't disappear from the dunya either, don't forget the share that has been put in your hand.

You are not asked to renounce. You are asked to **inhabit.** Inhabit the portion. Live inside the share. Notice what has been given. Use it the way a gift should be used — with gratitude, with awareness, with the quiet recognition that the Giver has not been forgotten.

**The practical edge:**

Tomorrow, before you start the day, ask yourself one question that this ayah has placed in your hand: *what part of my share am I currently forgetting?*

Is it the prayer that has shrunk because the meetings grew? That is forgetting your *naṣīb* of the akhirah-pursued-through-dunya.

Is it the meal you eat standing up, scrolling? That is forgetting your *naṣīb* of permitted enjoyment.

Is it the body you have not moved in weeks because you are "too busy"? That is forgetting your *naṣīb* of the trust Allah placed in your physical form.

Is it the parent you have not called, the child you have not really looked at, the spouse you have not asked a real question of in months? That is forgetting your *naṣīb* of relationship — which is one of the heaviest, most undervalued shares Allah apportions.

The ayah is not asking you to add one more item to a busy life. It is asking you to **stop forgetting** what was already there.

### Theme Two: The Mirror and the Counter-Mirror

Now I want to take you to the second insight, which lives in the second half of the ayah.

**Wa aḥsin kamā aḥsana Allāhu ilayk. Wa lā tabghi al-fasāda fī al-arḍ.**

*"Do ihsan as Allah has done ihsan to you. And do not seek fasād — corruption — in the land."*

Notice the structure of the second half. There is a mirror — *aḥsin kamā aḥsana.* Do good as good was done to you.

And then immediately, the ayah names what would happen if you broke that mirror. *Lā tabghi al-fasāda fī al-arḍ.* "Do not seek corruption in the earth."

Look at the verb. *Tabghi.* From the same root we started with — **ب-غ-ي** — *to seek, to pursue, to aim at.* The same verb that opened the ayah, telling Qarun to pursue the akhirah, now appears in its negative form: *do not pursue fasād.*

The architecture is staggering. The ayah opens by telling him **what to seek** — the akhirah, through what Allah has given him. And it closes by naming **what not to seek** — corruption in the land. The same verb of pursuit, bracketing the entire counsel. Whatever Qarun is going to actively chase, the ayah is putting up two signposts: *this direction, yes. That direction, no.*

And the *fasād* he is told not to pursue — what is it? [PAUSE]

The root **ف-س-د** — *f-s-d* — means corruption, spoilage, the breaking down of what was once whole. *Fasād* is what happens to milk left in the sun. *Fasād* is what happens to a wound that festers. *Fasād* is what happens to a society where injustice has worked its way into the foundations.

It is the opposite of *ihsan* in a very particular way. *Ihsan* makes things better, more beautiful, more whole. *Fasād* makes things worse, uglier, broken. *Ihsan* is the flow of beauty outward. *Fasād* is the flow of decay outward.

And here is what I want you to see: **the man who does not mirror Allah's ihsan to him will not be neutral.** He will not stand in the middle, neither helpful nor harmful. He will become a *fasād-spreader.* The wealth that did not flow outward in *ihsan* will rot in the hand that holds it, and that rot will spread.

This is the lesson Qarun is about to embody in the most literal way possible. The ayah right after this one records him saying *"I was given this because of knowledge I have."* And then the earth swallows him. The man who would not mirror Allah's *ihsan* becomes the corruption he refused not to seek — and the earth itself, the *arḍ* in which he would not stop sowing *fasād*, opens up and takes him in.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this home.

Every time you receive something — a paycheck, a kind word, a hour of free time, a moment of unexpected health, a child's laugh, a sentence of the Quran that landed in your heart — Allah has done *ihsan* to you. The mirror is being held up.

What you do next is the test. Do you reflect it outward? Or does it stop with you?

If it stops with you, the *ihsan* does not stay neutral. The blessing does not just sit there inert. Unspent goodness, hoarded goodness, ungrateful goodness — it *turns.* It begins to ferment. It begins to corrupt the heart that holds it. And eventually it spills outward as *fasād* — as the small cruelties of the comfortable, the casual injustices of the secure, the slow contempt of those who have forgotten that anything was ever a gift.

This is the spiritual physics the ayah is naming. *Ihsan* received must become *ihsan* given, or it becomes *fasād* spread. There is no third option. The wealth in the keys does not stay still. It either flows out in beauty, or it ferments into corruption.

**The practical edge:**

Tonight, take five minutes. Count three pieces of *ihsan* Allah has done to you today. Big or small. The cup of coffee that was warm. The lung that took a breath. The friend who texted. The roof. The pillow. The names of your children that you still remember.

Then ask: for each one, what mirror have I held up? Where has the *ihsan* I received flowed back out today?

If you cannot answer for even one — that is not a moral failure. That is a *naṣīb* not yet inhabited. The mirror is still in your hand. You can lift it tomorrow.

Because here is the secret of this ayah: **you do not have to be Qarun to learn from Qarun. You only have to be willing to see that the keys you hold — however small, however ordinary — were not yours to begin with. They were ihsan. And ihsan, once received, asks one thing of you. To pass it on.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we have walked it, look back.

The ayah opens with **a positive command** — *wabtaghi.* Seek.
The ayah closes with **a negative command** — *wa lā tabghi.* Do not seek.

Both commands use the *same verb root* — ب-غ-ي. The pursuit.

Between them, four clauses pulse outward in pairs:

**Seek the Hereafter** (eternal) — *and do not forget your share of this world* (temporal).
**Do ihsan as Allah has done ihsan to you** (positive flow) — *and do not seek fasād in the land* (negative flow).

The structure itself is teaching you the lesson. The Muslim life is not lived by choosing one pole over the other. It is lived in the *space between the commands* — pursuing what should be pursued, not pursuing what should not, holding both worlds in one hand, mirroring outward what was poured inward.

And right at the center, the pivot: **wa aḥsin kamā aḥsana Allāhu ilayk.** The mirror. The whole ayah turns on it.

### Bringing It Together

What did we discover?

From the linguistics: that the verb *ibtaghi* is active pursuit *inside* the wealth, not against it; that *naṣīb* is an apportioned share, not loot; that *ihsan* is a mirror command, not an invention.

From the themes: that there are *two* ways to forget your share — by drowning and by grinding; that ungifted *ihsan* does not stay still — it ferments into *fasād*.

Both directions converge on a single insight:

**The dunya was never the problem. The orientation was. The same keys that crushed Qarun could have built a path to the akhirah. He just held them wrong.**

### Questions to Carry

These are for you, not for me. Sit with them when you are alone.

1. What in your hand right now is *ihsan* from Allah that you have started mistaking for the proof of your own knowledge, your own skill, your own deserving?

2. If your *naṣīb* of this world is what you forward to the next — what have you forwarded this month? This year? In your entire life?

3. There are two ways to forget your share: by overconsumption and by joyless accumulation. Which way are *you* forgetting?

4. Allah has poured *ihsan* on you today. Trace one stream of it. Where has that stream flowed out from you to someone else?

5. The earth swallowed Qarun whole. What in *you*, if it continues, would the earth eventually be asked to swallow?

### The One Sentence

*Allah is not asking you to leave the world — He is asking you to stop forgetting that the world He has placed in your hand is a portion, a mirror, and a path to Him.*

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to be wealthy to be Qarun. You only have to hold what Allah gave you as if you earned it, as if it is owed to you, as if the mirror is yours.

And you do not have to be a saint to escape Qarun's fate. You only have to remember three things, three times a day, three weeks in a row, until they become a habit of the heart:

What Allah gave me is a *portion* — bounded, designated, on loan.

What Allah gave me is a *mirror* — to be reflected outward, not absorbed inward.

What Allah gave me is a *path* — through the dunya, not against it, toward the Home.

That is the invitation. Not renunciation. **Inhabitation.** Live inside your share — fully, gratefully, generously, with one eye on the Giver — and you will have already begun to seek the Hereafter through what He has given you.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, the keys we hold are heavier than we admit. Teach us to see them as Yours. Teach us to use them as paths to You. Make us mirrors of Your ihsan and never carriers of fasād. And when our portion of this world runs out, let us have already forwarded the better share to the Home You are calling us to.*

*Āmīn.*
