# The Pull You Cannot Afford
## A Tadabbur of Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 87

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let's begin with the ayah.

**Arabic:**
وَلَا يَصُدُّنَّكَ عَنْ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ بَعْدَ إِذْ أُنزِلَتْ إِلَيْكَ ۖ وَادْعُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ ۖ وَلَا تَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ

**Transliteration:**
Wa lā yaṣuddunnaka ʿan āyāti-llāhi baʿda idh unzilat ilayk(a), wa-dʿu ilā rabbik(a), wa lā takūnanna mina-l-mushrikīn.

**Translation:**
"And let them not turn you away from the signs of Allah after they have been revealed to you. And invite to your Lord. And never be among those who associate others with Him."

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, you have to stand for a moment in late-Makkan air.

The Prophet ﷺ has been preaching for years. The early thrill is gone. The novelty has been replaced by something heavier — a slow grinding of social pressure. His uncle Abu Talib has died. His wife Khadijah has died. The tribe of Quraysh, instead of weakening in its opposition, has hardened. They have tried mockery. They have tried boycott. They have tried bribery — sending Utbah ibn Rabīʿah to offer him wealth, leadership, marriage, anything — if he would just *soften*. Just *adjust*. Just *stop calling their idols false*.

The pressure was not always violent. Often it was social. Polite. Reasonable.

*Can't you meet us halfway? Can't you stop with the absolutism? Can't you acknowledge our ancestors had something? Can't you just modify the message — a little?*

And around this, the whole surah of Al-Qasas has been unfolding — story after story of what *pull* does to people. Pharaoh, pulled by power, drowns. Qarun, pulled by wealth, gets swallowed by the earth. Musa, pulled toward Madyan, becomes a prophet. The surah is, at one level, a meditation on the gravitational fields that shape a human life.

And then, near the end, the Quran turns and addresses the Messenger ﷺ directly.

### The Hook

What is remarkable is what Allah chooses to warn about, and *how* He chooses to phrase the warning. He doesn't say "do not abandon the signs." He doesn't say "hold firmly." He uses a grammatical structure in Arabic that, once you see it, will change the way you read this ayah forever — and a warning that, by its very phrasing, reveals something disturbing about how human beings actually lose their way.

Most people don't walk away from truth. They get *pulled* away. And the verb Allah uses here is built precisely for that distinction.

Let's sit with this.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to look closely at three things: a verb, a grammatical particle attached to the verb, and an absence.

### Element One: The Verb يَصُدُّنَّ — "to turn away"

#### Teaching the concept first

In English, when we say someone "turned away" from something, we usually picture the person turning themselves. The agency is theirs. *He turned away from his old friends. She turned away from her career.*

Arabic has a different verb for that — and that's not the verb here.

The verb here is from the root **ص-د-د** (ṣād-dāl-dāl). And this root, in its core meaning, does not describe a person turning. It describes a person *being turned*. It describes obstruction. Deflection. Being pushed off a path you were already walking.

Think of a river flowing toward the sea, and a boulder dropped in front of it. The river doesn't *decide* to change course. The boulder *deflects* it. That deflection is ṣadd.

So the verse is not saying "do not turn yourself away." It is saying "do not let them deflect you."

#### Opening the word

Now let's look at what Allah actually says:

وَلَا يَصُدُّنَّكَ

*Wa lā yaṣuddunnaka.*

Notice the grammatical addressee. Allah is speaking *to* the Prophet ﷺ, but the verb is in the third person plural — *they*. "Let them not deflect you."

But — and this is critical — Allah has not yet named *them*. There is no specified subject before this verb. There is no "the disbelievers" or "the people of Quraysh" or "your enemies." The deflectors are grammatically anonymous.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment.

Why would Allah leave the subject unspecified?

Because the threat is not from one group. It's from *anyone*. *Anyone* who might pull at you. The hostile pagan. The well-meaning relative. The respected elder. The friend who says *just calm down a little*. The grammar is open-ended because the threat is open-ended.

This is what the linguists call a generalized agent — Allah has left the door wide so that every deflector who has ever existed or will ever exist walks through it.

#### Deepening

Here is something even more remarkable. The Arabic verb يَصُدُّنَّ does not require the deflectors to be malicious. The root ص-د-د in classical usage simply means to obstruct, to turn aside, to keep someone from reaching a destination. The motive of the obstructer is not part of the word's meaning. A loving mother can be a ṣādd. A respected teacher can be a ṣādd. A government policy can be a ṣādd. Even a polite social convention can be a ṣādd.

The verb describes the *effect*, not the *intent*.

This is staggering when you think about it. Allah is not warning the Prophet ﷺ primarily about persecution. He is warning him about *being moved*. And being moved can happen through love just as much as through hate. Through kindness just as much as through cruelty.

Notice something. This verb is not "do not abandon." It is "do not be deflected." The frame is not *your willful turning*. The frame is *their pull on you*.

And here's what this single word reveals: **the primary spiritual danger for someone who has received Allah's signs is not that they will reject them — it is that they will be slowly pulled off course by people who never explicitly asked them to.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Nūn al-Tawkīd — The "Heaviness" of Certainty

#### Teaching the concept first

Arabic has a grammatical feature that English doesn't really have. In English, if I want to make a command more emphatic, I use my voice, or I add words. *Do NOT do that. Don't you DARE do that.*

Arabic does something different. It attaches a particle directly *into the body of the verb* — a particle called the **nūn al-tawkīd**, the "nūn of emphasis." There are two forms: the heavy nūn (with shaddah) and the light nūn. The heavy nūn produces a doubled n-sound that, in spoken Arabic, lands with a kind of percussive weight. It is the grammatical equivalent of someone gripping your shoulder while they speak.

#### Opening the word

Look again:

وَلَا يَصُدُّنَّكَ
*Wa lā yaṣud-**dunna**-ka.*

That doubled **-dunna-** in the middle of the verb? That is the heavy nūn of emphasis. It is grammatically embedded *inside* the verb itself.

And then, at the end of the ayah:

وَلَا تَكُونَنَّ مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ
*Wa lā takūn-**anna** min al-mushrikīn.*

Again — the heavy nūn. Embedded. Pressing.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepening

Why does this matter?

In classical Arabic grammar, the nūn al-tawkīd is not used casually. It is reserved for moments where the speaker needs the listener to feel the *seriousness* of what is being said — to feel that this is not optional, not soft, not conditional. It functions almost like an oath embedded inside the verb.

Now consider who is being addressed. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ — the most spiritually anchored human being on earth. The one who has *received* the revelation. The one who has *seen* angels. The one whose entire being is oriented toward Allah.

And Allah uses the nūn of emphasis with him.

Stay with what that implies.

If the most spiritually grounded human being needs this level of emphatic warning, what does that say about the gravitational pull being described? It says the pull is *real*. The pull is *strong*. The pull is *capable of moving even him* — not because of any weakness in him, but because of the sheer force of what is being aimed at him.

The nūn al-tawkīd is Allah's way of saying: *I know this pressure is enormous. I know what they are doing. I want you to feel the weight of my warning the way you feel the weight of their pull.*

Here is what this grammatical feature reveals: **steadfastness on revelation is not a default state. It is an active resistance against a real, measurable force, severe enough that Allah Himself uses His grammar's strongest emphasizer when commanding it.**

And it reveals something else. If steadfastness required such an emphatic warning for the Prophet ﷺ, then for us — who are not prophets, who do not receive revelation, who live surrounded by far more sophisticated deflectors than the Quraysh ever dreamed of — the work of *not being moved* must be a serious, conscious, ongoing exertion. Not a feeling. Not a vibe. An active resistance.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Absence — "After They Have Been Revealed to You"

This is where we have to read what is *not* there.

#### Opening the absence

Allah says:

بَعْدَ إِذْ أُنزِلَتْ إِلَيْكَ
*baʿda idh unzilat ilayk.*

"After they have been revealed to you."

Notice the structure. The warning against being deflected is grounded by this phrase — *after they have been revealed*. After. Once you have received them.

[PAUSE]

Now ask the question that the grammar is forcing on you.

Why specify *after*? Why does Allah anchor the prohibition specifically to the post-revelation state?

#### Deepening through the gap

Because — and this is the silent insight Allah is gesturing at — there is a particular kind of spiritual peril that does not exist *before* receiving guidance. It only exists *after*.

Before guidance, you are searching. You are open. There is nothing yet to be deflected *from*.

But after guidance — after the signs have arrived, after you have seen them, after they have been *unzilat ilayk*, "sent down upon you" — now you have something to lose. Now there is a path you are on. Now there is a direction you are facing. And only now does deflection become possible.

This is the spiritual physics: **deflection is only meaningful once direction exists**. Allah is naming a danger that only the guided can experience.

Notice what this implies. The person at greater risk in this ayah is not the unbeliever — it is the believer. The one who has received. The one who knows. The one who has the signs. *They* are the ones who can be deflected, because they are the only ones who had a trajectory in the first place.

And one more layer. Look at the verb *unzilat* — "revealed" or "sent down." It is passive. Allah does not say "after you acquired them" or "after you studied them." He says "after they have been sent down to you." This is a gift framing. The signs came *to* you. You did not climb up and take them.

Which means: deflection after revelation is not just a personal misstep. It is a turning away from something that *came to you as a descent of mercy*. The phrase *baʿda idh unzilat ilayk* is doing emotional work, not just chronological work. It is saying: *don't let them deflect you from something that was given*.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this absence reveals: **the verse is not addressed to the lost. It is addressed to the found. And the found are warned because the found are the ones who have something a deflector can pull at.**

### Bridge

So we have three linguistic discoveries:

A verb that locates the danger in *being pulled*, not in your turning.
A grammatical emphasizer that signals the pull is severe enough to require Allah's strongest warning.
An absence that reveals the warning is meaningful only because you have already received what you might lose.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how human beings actually fall, and what it takes to stand.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Will Not Be Persuaded — You Will Be Pulled

Let me begin somewhere mundane.

Think of someone you knew who started a fitness habit. They were running. Eating clean. Sleeping. They felt the change in their body. They were *on a path*.

Six months later, the habit is gone.

If you asked them when it ended, they would not be able to tell you. There was no day they sat down and said, "I have decided to abandon health." There was a friend's birthday. A late night. A travel week. A sick child. A stressful project. A *we're just going to skip today*. And then a *we'll start back Monday*. And then Monday came and went.

They were not persuaded out of it. They were *pulled* out of it.

This is the most common architecture of human failure. We almost never *decide* to abandon what is good for us. We *drift* away from it under the steady pull of competing gravities.

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again with this in mind.

وَلَا يَصُدُّنَّكَ عَنْ آيَاتِ اللَّهِ بَعْدَ إِذْ أُنزِلَتْ إِلَيْكَ

The verb is ṣadd. *Deflection*. And Allah is naming the precise spiritual mechanism that destroys people who *had the signs*.

The Quraysh did not need to argue Muhammad ﷺ out of Islam. They only needed to wear him down. To pull him. To extract small concessions. To make every day of standing firm a little more expensive. They were betting — and this was their entire strategy — that *gravity wins*. That given enough time, enough pressure, enough social cost, the human heart bends.

And Allah's response — embedded in this single verb with its embedded emphatic nūn — is essentially: *I see what they are doing. The pull is real. The pull is strong. And you are not to bend.*

But notice what Allah does *not* say. He does not say, "and you will not bend." He says, "let them not deflect you." The grammar leaves the work to the Prophet ﷺ. The pull is acknowledged. The resistance is commanded. The agency stays with the one being warned.

#### Going deeper

There is a category of pull that almost no one warns about, and which this verb captures perfectly.

It is the pull of *being reasonable*.

You receive the signs. You know what they say. And then someone — someone you respect, someone you love, someone whose approval matters to you — looks at your conviction and says, *that's a bit much*. Or *you've gotten very intense*. Or *you used to be more open*. Or simply doesn't say anything, but raises an eyebrow when the topic comes up.

That eyebrow is a boulder in the river.

You don't even realize you're adjusting. You start phrasing things more softly. You start qualifying. You start strategically forgetting parts of what you know. You start being *moderate* — which is sometimes a virtue, but is also sometimes the precise word for being slowly deflected from what was revealed to you.

The Prophet ﷺ was not at risk of becoming an atheist. The Quraysh weren't trying to make him one. They were trying to make him *more reasonable*. More flexible. More willing to acknowledge that maybe their gods had *some* validity. Just a little. Just enough.

And Allah's grammar in this ayah closes that door with a slammed nūn al-tawkīd.

#### What this changes about how I live tomorrow

Identify your deflectors. Not the dramatic ones. The quiet ones. The relationships, the rooms, the conversations, the platforms where, when you leave, you notice you have spoken about your dīn more softly than you actually believe it. That softening is not humility. It is ṣadd, and Allah named it before it ever reached you.

You don't have to confront the deflector. You only have to *notice the pull*. Awareness of the gravity is half of the resistance.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Antidote Is Outward, Not Inward

Look at what Allah commands *after* the warning.

وَادْعُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ
*Wa-dʿu ilā rabbik.*

"And invite to your Lord."

[PAUSE]

This is unexpected.

If someone is being deflected, the obvious instruction would be defensive: *hold firm*, *anchor yourself*, *retreat*, *fortify*. Allah does not say any of that. He says: *and invite*.

The antidote to being pulled is not to brace. It is to *call others*.

This deserves real attention, because it inverts the way most of us instinctively respond to spiritual pressure.

#### Going deeper

When we feel our faith being pulled at, the instinctive move is inward. We retreat. We isolate. We stop talking about it. We protect what's left.

Allah's prescription is the opposite. He prescribes outward motion — *udʿu*, "call," "invite." From the root **د-ع-و**, the same root as *duʿāʾ*, which in its core meaning is to call, to summon, to address. When you call someone, you are facing *toward* them. You are extending. You are not withdrawing.

Why is this the antidote?

Because *deflection* and *calling* are vectors pointing in opposite directions. Deflection is something done *to* you — you are the object, the river being pushed. Calling is something done *by* you — you are the subject, the voice reaching out. The two cannot coexist in the same moment. The act of inviting someone toward Allah is, structurally, the act of refusing to be moved away from Him. You cannot point others toward a destination you are walking away from.

This is why Allah pairs them. *Wa lā yaṣuddunnaka* and *wa-dʿu ilā rabbik* are not two separate commands. They are the same posture described from two sides — *don't be deflected* / *be the one calling*. Defense by direction.

#### Now go deeper still

Notice the phrasing: *udʿu ilā **rabbik*** — "invite to **your** Lord." Not "invite to Allah," not "invite to Islam," not "invite to the truth." *Your Lord.*

This is intimate language. The Prophet ﷺ is being told to call people not to a system or an institution but to a relationship he himself is in. To the Lord he himself knows. The invitation has to come from a real, lived nearness, or it has no power.

And here is the layer beneath that. The act of inviting others to *your Lord* keeps reminding *you* that you have a Lord. It re-anchors you every time you do it. Daʿwah is not just about the listener. It is, profoundly, a form of self-preservation for the caller. Every time you point at the source of light, you remember where the light is.

This is one of the most quietly merciful instructions in the Quran. Allah does not say: *don't be pulled — and now go fix yourself*. He says: *don't be pulled — and call others to Me*. Because the calling is part of the not being pulled.

#### What this changes about how I live tomorrow

When you feel the pull — the soft shame, the social pressure, the embarrassment, the *just-keep-it-private* instinct — do not interpret it as a sign to retreat. Interpret it as a sign to *speak*. Not loudly. Not defensively. Not combatively. But to be the one in your room, your family chat, your workplace, your timeline, who points at the source of light. The invitation is the protection.

And one more thing. The end of the ayah seals it: *wa lā takūnanna mina-l-mushrikīn* — "and never be among those who associate." Notice the construction — "among those who" — *mina-l-mushrikīn*. Allah warns not just against shirk as an act but against being *of that company*. Because identity is downstream of association. The one who keeps inviting toward Allah keeps locating himself among those who call toward Allah. The grammar of the ayah is constructing the soul's address.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now that we've done the work, look at the structure of the ayah itself.

It has three commands, in this order:

1. **Don't be deflected** from the signs (negative — defensive)
2. **Invite** to your Lord (positive — outward)
3. **Don't be among** the mushrikīn (negative — directional)

This is not a list. This is an architecture.

The first command names the threat: *being moved off the signs*.
The second command names the antidote: *be the one calling toward Allah*.
The third command names the destination of the failure to do both: *you will end up among those who associate*.

The three commands are not parallel. They are *sequential*. They describe what happens when ṣadd succeeds — you do not become an atheist. You become a *mushrik*. Because shirk is not always the dramatic worship of an idol. Sometimes shirk is the slow accumulation of small deferences — to public opinion, to social approval, to professional advancement, to family expectation — until those things share the throne in your heart with Allah, or quietly replace Him.

The ayah's architecture is the warning's anatomy: *deflection → silence → association*. Each step is small. The end is everything.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

The verb ṣadda told us the danger is being *pulled*, not turning. The theme of social gravity told us this is exactly how the human heart actually loses its way — not through argument but through wear.

The nūn al-tawkīd told us the warning is *severe*. The theme of pairing daʿwah with steadfastness told us *why* the warning needs to be severe: because the antidote requires courage. You cannot invite if you are hiding. You cannot speak if you are bracing.

And the phrase *baʿda idh unzilat ilayk* told us this ayah is addressed only to those who already received the signs. The theme of identity-by-association told us where the un-received-from end up: among those who associate. Not because they chose to. Because they were pulled, and they did not call back.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I being deflected — not argued with, but slowly pulled — and have I been mistaking it for *being reasonable*?

2. Who in my orbit is a ṣādd — a person, a feed, a room, a job — that, without ever telling me to abandon my dīn, has made me softer in describing it than I actually believe?

3. When was the last time I *called* — not argued, not posted, not signaled — but actually invited a specific human being toward my Lord? And is the absence of that calling correlated with the increase of my own deflection?

4. If shirk is the destination of unanswered ṣadd, what are the smaller gods that have accumulated authority in my life — approval, image, professional standing, family peace — and whose throne are they sitting on?

5. The signs were *sent down* to me. *Unzilat ilayk* — they came. Am I living like I received a gift, or like I picked up a habit?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You will not be argued out of Allah's signs — you will be slowly pulled, and the only force strong enough to resist the pull is to keep facing outward, inviting others toward the very Lord they are trying to pull you away from.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't need to overhaul your life after this ayah. You only need to do one thing this week.

Identify the pull. Name it specifically. *The room where my dīn gets quieter. The relationship where my certainty gets softer. The platform where I edit out the parts I actually believe.*

And then, somewhere — even in a small way, even to one person — invite. Speak the name of your Lord aloud. Mention an ayah. Send a verse. Recommend a passage. Be, for one moment, the one calling rather than the one being pulled. And see what that single act of *udʿu ilā rabbik* does to the gravity around you.

Because Allah did not tell His Messenger to brace.

He told him to call.

### Du'a

O Allah, You have sent Your signs down to us as a gift. Do not let us be deflected from them — not by enemies, not by friends, not by the slow gravity of being wanted by everyone. Make us those who call to You, so that we keep facing You. And never let us become among those who, by small concessions, ended up among those who associate.

Āmīn.
