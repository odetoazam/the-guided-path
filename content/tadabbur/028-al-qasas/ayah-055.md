# The Dignified Turn: How the Believer Walks Past Noise

## Introduction

**Arabic:**
وَإِذَا سَمِعُوا اللَّغْوَ أَعْرَضُوا عَنْهُ وَقَالُوا لَنَا أَعْمَالُنَا وَلَكُمْ أَعْمَالُكُمْ سَلَامٌ عَلَيْكُمْ لَا نَبْتَغِي الْجَاهِلِينَ

**Transliteration:**
Wa idhā samiʿū al-laghwa aʿraḍū ʿanhu wa qālū lanā aʿmālunā wa lakum aʿmālukum salāmun ʿalaykum lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn.

**Translation:**
"And when they hear vain talk, they turn away from it and say: 'For us are our deeds, and for you are your deeds. Peace be upon you — we do not seek out the ignorant.'"

---

### Rebuild the World

Place yourself in Makkah, somewhere in the middle of the Prophet ﷺ's mission there. Surah Al-Qasas is a Makkan surah — heavily Makkan in tone, in pressure, in the particular weight it carries. The believers are a tiny, embattled minority. Their faith is mocked daily in the streets. Walk to the market and someone laughs. Walk past the Kaʿbah and someone insults you. Walk past a circle of poets and they recite a poem they composed last night that turns your beliefs into a punchline.

There is no escape from it. You cannot move to another neighborhood. You cannot block them on a feed. You cannot un-hear what was said. The mockery is in the air you breathe.

Now — into this atmosphere — a delegation arrives from outside Makkah. They are People of the Book. They hear the Quran. They recognize it. They believe. And the Quran pauses, in the middle of a surah about Musa and Pharaoh, to draw a portrait of these particular believers — and to hold them up as an example.

[PAUSE]

What is the portrait? It is not their theology that gets described. It is not their rituals. It is not even, primarily, their faith.

It is **their composure**.

It is how they **walk past noise**.

### The Hook

Notice something strange before we even enter the words. In a surah dominated by the great confrontation between Musa and Pharaoh — staff vs. magicians, child in a basket vs. king on a throne — Allah pauses to describe a small, almost domestic moment. Someone hears something foul. And they... turn away. And they say something. And they walk on.

Why is this small moment placed at this height in the surah? Why is this — of all responses — held up as the mark of those whose reward will be **doubled** (the ayah right before this)? What does Allah see in this turn that we usually miss?

Stay with that question. We will arrive at it together.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: **al-Laghw** — The Word Itself

Before we approach what they turned away from, we need to understand what *kind* of speech is being described. Because Arabic — and the Quran specifically — does not use one flat word for "bad speech." It uses different words for different injuries. And the word here is precise.

The word is **اللَّغْوُ — *al-laghw***.

#### Let's first sit with the concept

In English, when someone says something we don't want to hear, we have a few buckets: insults, gossip, lies, profanity, slander. Each of these is *about something*. Each is, in its own way, a claim — a claim that wounds, a claim that's false, a claim that exposes.

But there is another category of speech that doesn't fit any of those. It's not exactly a lie. It's not exactly slander. It's just... **empty**. It is words that carry no weight, no benefit, no truth being asserted, no real meaning being transferred. It is speech that, if you removed it from the world, the world would lose nothing.

You know this category. You've sat in a room while it happened. You've scrolled through a feed full of it. It's the comment thread that's just noise. It's the gossip that has no real subject. It's the mockery that isn't even *clever* mockery — it's just sound, signifying nothing.

That is the territory **laghw** maps.

#### Now open the word

The classical lexicographers, when they describe the root **l-gh-w** (ل-غ-و), reach for a striking image. They describe it as speech that is *cast aside* — speech you would discard. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, in his *Mufradāt*, ties it to anything that has no consideration paid to it, anything one says without thinking. Some lexicographers connect the root to the foam on water — that thin, hissing layer that has no substance, that disappears the moment you reach for it.

[PAUSE]

Foam.

Think about that. Allah did not describe what they heard as "insults." He did not call it "lies." He called it **foam**. The image is doing real work. Because foam looks like something. It looks, on the surface of the water, like it has body, like it occupies space, like it matters. But it is air wrapped in a thin skin of water. Reach for it and there is nothing in your hand.

That is what they were hearing. That is what we hear all day.

#### Here's what's remarkable

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say "when they hear **insults**." He did not say "when they hear **lies**." He did not say "when they hear **mockery of Allah**."

If He had said those things, the ayah would be about a particular *content* of speech. It would be: when the content is bad, turn away. But because He chose **laghw**, the ayah is about a particular *weight* of speech. It is about speech that has no weight at all.

And this matters enormously. Because some of the most addictive speech in our lives is not lies and not insults — it is **laghw**. It is the endless scroll of opinions we don't need. It is the argument we keep having in our head with someone who doesn't even know we exist. It is the YouTube comment we cannot stop thinking about. None of it has weight. All of it consumes us.

The believer Allah is describing has developed a particular sense. He can **feel the weightlessness of speech**. He can hear, in the first three seconds, that this is foam. And the moment he feels that — something in him **turns**.

That is element one. The first quality of the believer in this ayah is not knowledge. It is not eloquence. It is **a trained ear** — an ear that can recognize foam.

### Element Two: **Aʿraḍū ʿanhu** — The Quality of the Turn

Now we come to what they *do* when they hear it. The verb is **أَعْرَضُوا — *aʿraḍū***.

#### Let's first sit with the concept

In English, when someone speaks foam at us, we have several options. We can argue. We can mock back. We can roll our eyes. We can stay and listen. We can engage. We can disengage. Each of these is a different motion of the soul.

The motion the believer makes here is none of those, exactly. It is its own specific thing. And Arabic — gloriously precise about the motions of the soul — gives it its own word.

#### Now open the word

The root is **ʿ-r-ḍ** (ع-ر-ض). At its base, it carries the sense of *width*, of *breadth*, of *the side of a thing*. From this root come words for the **side** of a body, the **width** of a road, the **broad** dimension of anything.

When the root takes the form *aʿraḍa* — Form IV — it means literally **to present one's side to something**. To turn so that you are no longer facing it. To put your width — your shoulder — between yourself and it.

Stay with that physical image for a moment. Because the Arabic has chosen, very specifically, **not** to use a word that means "fight back," "respond," "argue with," "answer." It has chosen a word that physically describes a **body that has turned**.

You were facing this speech. Now you have turned. Your shoulder is to it. You are still in the world. You have not fled. You have not run. You have not hidden. You have simply **rotated**.

[PAUSE]

#### Notice what this is not

This is not avoidance, in the cowardly sense. The believer here is not pretending he did not hear. He heard. The ayah tells us he heard — *idhā samiʿū*, "when they hear." He did not put his fingers in his ears. He did not stay home so he would not encounter this.

He went out. He heard. And then, having heard, he **turned**.

And this is not silence, either. Because the ayah continues — he speaks. *Wa qālū* — and they say. So the turn is not muteness. The turn is a turn *toward* a different kind of speech. Away from the foam. Toward something else.

What Form IV (*aʿraḍa*) carries that the simpler verbs do not is **a deliberate, intentional, conscious presenting of the side**. This is not absent-minded ignoring. This is a chosen posture. The believer has *decided* where to put his body and where to put his attention. He has decided that this speech does not get the gift of his face.

#### Here is what is remarkable

There is a difference between being *unable* to engage and being *unwilling* to engage. The first is weakness. The second is mastery.

The believer Allah is describing is not the believer who cannot answer back. He could answer back. He could probably **win** the argument. But he has perceived something deeper than the argument: the argument is foam. And foam does not deserve the attention that would give it the appearance of substance.

To engage foam is to **make it not-foam**. The moment you argue with a thing, you have conceded that it had weight enough to be argued with. The turn is a refusal to make that concession.

That is element two. The believer does not just refrain from engaging — he engages a **different motion of the body**. He turns his side. He keeps walking. He stays in the world but does not face the noise.

### Element Three: **Lā Nabtaghī al-Jāhilīn** — What We Do Not Seek

The ayah closes with a phrase so quiet that it can pass us by. *Lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn* — "we do not seek out the ignorant." But sit with this verb. Because this verb is doing extraordinary work.

#### Let's first sit with the concept

If someone insults you, what is the natural answer? "I do not want to argue with you." "I do not want to listen to you." "I will not respond to you." Each of these is a refusal of a *current* interaction.

But the believer does not say that. He says something larger. He says: *lā nabtaghī* — "we do not **seek out**."

#### Now open the word

The verb is from the root **b-gh-y** (ب-غ-ي). In Form VIII — **ibtaghā** — the root carries the meaning of *to seek*, *to desire*, *to actively pursue*, *to go out looking for*. Al-Rāghib notes that this form intensifies the seeking — it is not passive wanting, it is active pursuit. You seek something out. You go after it. You hunt for it.

Allah uses this same root in some of the most beautiful verses of the Quran. *Wabtaghū min faḍli-llāh* — "and seek of Allah's bounty" (62:10). *Yabtaghūna faḍlan min rabbihim* — "they seek bounty from their Lord" (5:2). The verb describes the deep, oriented hunger of the soul for something.

And the believer in our ayah, in one phrase, declares the **direction** of his hunger. He says: *lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn*. "We are not in pursuit of the ignorant. They are not what we are hunting for. They are not the prey our soul is chasing."

[PAUSE]

#### Stay with this

This is so much larger than "I will not argue with you right now." This is: *the company of the ignorant is not a thing my soul is reaching toward. It is not on my menu. It is not in my budget of attention. It is not in the geography of where I am going.*

In a single phrase, the believer has redrawn the map of his desires.

And notice — and this is crucial — he has not said "we do not seek out **you**." He has said "we do not seek out **the ignorant** (*al-jāhilīn*)." This is gentler. He has not labeled this specific person an ignoramus to his face. He has spoken in the abstract — about a *category* he is not chasing. The person standing in front of him can hear the statement and is free to draw his own conclusion. The believer has refused to make the personal accusation while still naming, clearly, the principle by which he lives.

This is dignity. This is the verbal equivalent of *aʿraḍū ʿanhu* — turning the side. He has not faced the man with an accusation. He has stated a principle and let the man stand in front of it as he wishes.

#### And one more thing

The word *al-jāhilīn* — "the ignorant" — is not, in classical Arabic, primarily an intellectual category. *Jahl*, in the Quran's vocabulary, is not the opposite of *ʿilm* (knowledge) in the simple academic sense. The pre-Islamic age was called **al-Jāhiliyyah** not because people did not know facts. They knew genealogies, they knew poetry, they knew astronomy, they knew trade. *Jahl* describes a *posture of the soul* — recklessness, hot-headedness, refusal to weigh consequences, ignorance of one's place before Allah.

So when the believer says "we do not seek out the *jāhilīn*," he is not saying "we do not seek out the uneducated." He is saying: **we do not seek out those whose souls are reckless. We are not pursuing the company of the heedless. Our orbit is not in that direction.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

#### Bridge

Three words. Three movements.

*Laghw* — they have learned to **hear the weight of speech**. They can feel foam.
*Aʿraḍū* — they have learned to **turn the body**. Their attention has a side to present.
*Lā nabtaghī* — they have learned to **direct their hunger**. Their soul has a map, and the heedless are not on it.

Three words, and we have a portrait of a complete inner discipline — a *discipline of attention*. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how to live in a world full of noise.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: **The Discipline of Attention**

There is something you carry with you all day that is more valuable than money, more valuable than time, more renewable than energy and more depletable than any of them. It is your attention.

Begin where you actually live. You wake up. Before your feet have hit the floor, your hand has found your phone. Before you have spoken to Allah, you have read what some stranger said about some other stranger. By the time you have brushed your teeth, you have absorbed seventeen opinions you did not ask for. By noon, a comment someone made — about you, about your community, about something you care about — is still circling in your head like a fly that will not land.

None of it had weight.

All of it took weight from you.

[PAUSE]

This is the modern situation. We are not, mostly, being insulted to our face. We are not, mostly, being persecuted. What we are being subjected to — at industrial scale, by algorithms designed to do exactly this — is **laghw**. Foam. Speech with no substance, engineered to occupy us.

And here is the cruel mechanism: foam expands when you engage it. The moment you reply, the algorithm shows you more. The moment you argue, the comment thread grows. The moment you mentally rehearse the perfect comeback in the shower, the foam has filled the space of your mind where remembrance of Allah was supposed to live.

The believer in this ayah has perceived the mechanism. He has seen what it does. And he has developed a counter-discipline.

The counter-discipline is **the turn**.

#### What the turn does

When you turn your shoulder to a thing, you do three things at once.

First, you have **denied it your face**. The face is where attention lives. The eyes that engage, the ears that lean in, the mouth that opens to reply. When you turn, you remove those instruments from the foam's reach.

Second, you have **kept walking**. You did not stop. The foam was on the side of the road. You acknowledged it was there. You did not pretend it did not exist. You simply did not let it become the road.

Third, you have **kept your direction**. Wherever you were going, you are still going there. The foam tried to redirect you. It tried to make itself the new center of your attention. You did not let it.

This is what *aʿraḍū* is. Not closing your eyes. Not running away. Just — keeping your direction, with your shoulder presented to the noise.

#### The practical application

Tomorrow you will encounter laghw. Probably within twenty minutes of waking. The discipline is simple to name and difficult to practice: **the first three seconds**. In the first three seconds of any input, you have a moment to ask: *does this have weight?* If it is foam, **turn**. Do not engage. Do not refute. Do not even mentally compose a reply. Present your shoulder. Keep walking.

The reward of this practice is not, primarily, that you avoid bad speech. The reward is that the **space inside you stays clear**. The space where *dhikr* can live. The space where Quran can echo. The space where, when you sit to pray, you are actually able to be present.

Allah, in this ayah, is not just describing a polite response to insults. He is describing the **interior architecture of a soul** that has kept itself uncluttered.

### Theme Two: **Salām — The Strange Greeting**

We have not yet sat with the strangest word in this ayah. *Salāmun ʿalaykum*. "Peace be upon you."

Begin again with how this would feel. Imagine someone has just mocked you. They have said something foul about what you hold sacred. You have turned your shoulder. You have stated your principle — *lanā aʿmālunā wa lakum aʿmālukum* — "for us are our deeds, for you are your deeds." And now you are about to walk away.

What is the last word you say to them?

In our world, the last word is usually a parting shot. A cutting line. A "have a nice life." Even when we are dignified, the last word usually carries a final little blade — *something* to make sure they know we got the last word.

The believer in this ayah says: *Salām*.

[PAUSE]

#### Sit with how strange that is

This is the word that, between believers, is the greeting of love. This is the word the angels say in Paradise. This is the word Allah Himself addresses to the people of Jannah — *salāmun qawlan min rabbin raḥīm* (36:58). This is one of Allah's own Names — *as-Salām*.

And the believer is giving it to the mocker.

Why?

The classical commentators have explored this gift carefully. Many — including al-Ṭabarī and others — read this *salām* as what is sometimes called the *salām al-mutāraka*, the "peace of disengagement" — not the warm greeting between believers, but a verbal declaration that *I am withdrawing from hostility*. It is the word that closes the door without slamming it. It says: I am not coming with violence. I am not coming with curses. I am leaving. Peace.

[PAUSE]

But sit with what it means that **this** is the word chosen. Allah could have given the believer many phrases. *Wadāʿan* — farewell. *Inṣarafnā* — we depart. Some neutral term. He chose the word that **also** means the deep greeting. He chose the word that **also** names a Name of Allah.

#### Here is what is remarkable

The believer, in the moment of being mocked, **does not let the mocker pull him down to mockery**. He does not match the energy. He responds to *laghw* with *salām*. He responds to foam with weight. He responds to the worst word with one of the best words.

This is what 41:34 names directly: *Repel evil with that which is better, and then the one between whom and you was enmity will become like a devoted friend.*

The believer in our ayah has learned this. Not as a slogan. As a posture. The way he leaves a hostile encounter is by **dropping a word of peace into it as he goes**.

#### What this does

Two things, at least.

First — it protects **his own heart**. He does not leave that encounter carrying the mocker's bile. He leaves carrying his own salām. The encounter could have poisoned his afternoon. Instead, it became another opportunity to practice the speech of Paradise.

Second — and this is the deeper one — it **plants a seed**. Because the mocker, who was expecting a fight, did not get one. He was expecting an enemy. He got a man saying peace. That word, dropped into hostile soil, may not germinate that day. It may not germinate that year. But it has been planted. And Allah is the one who makes seeds grow.

Many of the early Muslims came to Islam after being mocked by a believer they had insulted and receiving, in return, only dignity. The mocker thinks he is hardening you. He is, in fact, often, softening himself — though neither of you may know it for years.

#### The practical application

Tomorrow, when foam reaches you — and it will — you have a choice not just about whether to turn, but about **what word to leave behind as you turn**. The Quran is teaching you that the believer's exit line is not a parting shot. It is a small dropping of peace into the soil of the encounter.

This is hard. Most of us, when wounded, want to wound back. But the believer in this ayah has been trained. He has *aʿmālunā* — "our deeds" — to attend to. He cannot afford to spend his deeds inside someone else's argument. He has somewhere to be. And the way he leaves is with peace.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look back at the ayah as a whole. Notice the shape.

> *And when they hear laghw — they turn from it — and they say: "For us our deeds, for you yours — peace upon you — we do not seek out the ignorant."*

The ayah is a sequence of four motions, in perfect order:

1. **Hearing** (*samiʿū*) — the world reaches them.
2. **Turning** (*aʿraḍū*) — they move the body.
3. **Boundary** (*lanā aʿmālunā wa lakum aʿmālukum*) — they name the principle.
4. **Peace and Direction** (*salām...lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn*) — they leave a blessing and declare their orbit.

This is not just an instruction. This is a **complete encounter, fully resolved**, in thirteen words. The Quran has given us, in one verse, an entire method for moving through a hostile world without becoming hostile.

And here is the architectural beauty: the verse begins with the believer *receiving* — he hears. It ends with the believer *giving* — he gives salām. The hostile speech entered him; what comes out of him is peace. The transformation is the ayah.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight from two directions.

The linguistic work showed us that this ayah is built around three precise verbs of the soul — recognizing foam, turning the body, directing hunger. The thematic work showed us that this ayah is about **a discipline of attention** and **a refusal to be poisoned by what one passes through**.

These are the same insight. Both are saying: *the believer is the master of what enters him and the master of what leaves him*. Foam enters; salām leaves. Mockery enters; principle leaves. Noise enters; clarity leaves.

This is sovereignty over the self. And in Surah Al-Qasas — a surah about how the powerless inherit the earth — it is exactly this inner sovereignty that comes before any outer inheritance.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the foam I keep reaching for — even though I know, when I touch it, there is nothing in my hand?
2. When I last walked through a hostile encounter, what word did I leave behind? Was it salām — or a parting shot?
3. What am I currently *seeking out* (*nabtaghī*) that I should not be? Whose conversations am I orbiting that are not on the map of where I am trying to go?
4. Where in my day could I install the first-three-seconds practice — the moment of asking "does this have weight?" before I engage?
5. When I read this ayah, do I read it as a description of *those people* — or as a portrait of the person Allah is shaping *me* to become?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The believer does not need to win every encounter — he only needs to keep his attention clear and leave peace behind him as he passes.

### The Closing Invitation

This is an invitation, not an obligation. To begin practicing a single, small motion of the soul: the turn. To begin to feel, when foam approaches, the slight rotation that puts your shoulder to it. To begin to notice the word you leave behind when you exit a hard conversation. To begin to draw the map of where your soul is reaching — and to gently, gently, withdraw it from the places that are not on the way to Allah.

This is not a posture for the strong. It is the practice that *makes* you strong.

### Du'a

Yā Allāh, give us the ear that can feel the weight of words. Give us the shoulder that can turn from what does not deserve our face. And when we walk through hostile ground, let what leaves our mouths be salām — and let our hunger be only for what brings us closer to You. Āmīn.
