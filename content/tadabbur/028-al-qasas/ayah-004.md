# When Power Divides a People in Two

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 4

---

### The Arabic

> إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهْلَهَا شِيَعًا يَسْتَضْعِفُ طَائِفَةً مِّنْهُمْ يُذَبِّحُ أَبْنَاءَهُمْ وَيَسْتَحْيِي نِسَاءَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّهُ كَانَ مِنَ الْمُفْسِدِينَ

### Transliteration

> *Inna Firʿawna ʿalā fil-arḍi wa jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa yastaḥyī nisāʾahum, innahu kāna minal-mufsidīn.*

### Translation

> "Indeed, Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and made its people into factions, oppressing a group among them — slaughtering their sons and keeping their women alive. Indeed, he was of the corrupters."

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Story Begins

Stay with me for a moment before we even open the words.

Surah Al-Qasas — *al-Qaṣaṣ*, "The Stories" — is the longest sustained narrative about Musa in the entire Quran. And Allah, before He tells us anything about the child who will be set adrift in a basket, before He introduces the mother whose heart will be emptied, before He even names the desert in Madyan or the well or the two women — does something that should arrest us.

He spends one ayah — *one ayah* — on the world Musa is born into.

That's it. The entire backdrop. The whole reason this story needs to exist. Compressed into a single sentence.

So we have to ask: **what does Allah choose to put in that single sentence?** Because whatever He includes is what we are supposed to understand. Whatever He leaves out is not needed for what comes next.

Notice what He does *not* mention. He does not mention Egypt's wealth. He does not mention its agriculture, its monuments, its priesthood, its mythology, its slave economy in detail. He does not list Pharaoh's military strength or his court or his magicians. All of that is in the Quran elsewhere — but not here, not in the opening shot.

What He mentions instead is something more diagnostic. Something almost clinical. He gives us, in one breath, the **anatomy of tyranny itself**.

This is not a story about ancient Egypt. This is Allah teaching us how to *recognize* a Pharaoh — in any age, in any land, wearing any uniform, speaking any language. The features are listed. The diagnosis is given. The pattern is named.

And here is the hook I want you to carry as we walk through this: the first thing Allah names about Pharaoh is not what he did to the Bani Isra'il. It is what he did to *himself*. Everything else flows from that.

Stay with that. Then let's look.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to look at three things. Three words — really three movements — that, taken together, are the entire skeleton of how tyranny works. Once you see them, you can never unsee them.

---

### Element One: *ʿalā fil-arḍ* — "He exalted himself in the land"

Before we open this phrase, let me teach you something about Arabic verbs.

In English, when we say "he rose," we are describing motion. He went from a lower position to a higher one. Neutral. He could be rising in a building, rising in his career, rising from bed.

Arabic is more careful than that. There is a family of verbs in Arabic — *ʿalā*, *istakbara*, *ṭaghā* — that all involve elevation, but each one carries a moral charge built into its grammar. *ʿalā* is the most basic of them. From the root *ʿ-l-w*. It is the same root that gives us *al-ʿAlī* — the Most High — one of Allah's names.

So when Allah uses *this* verb for Pharaoh, something has just happened that we should not miss.

Allah is *al-ʿAlī*. The Most High. Highness, in its absolute and rightful form, belongs to Him alone.

And then Allah says of Pharaoh: *ʿalā fil-arḍ*. He exalted himself — *in the land*.

Notice something. *Fil-arḍ.* In the land. Not "in the heavens." Not "absolutely." Pharaoh's elevation has a ceiling on it that he himself cannot see. He is rising within a box. Within dirt. Within the very *arḍ* — earth, ground — that he will one day be buried in.

Here is what is remarkable: Allah does not say *takabbara* (he was arrogant) or *istakbara* (he made himself big), though He uses those verbs elsewhere for Pharaoh. He says *ʿalā*. He *rose*. He *elevated*.

Why? Because this verb describes something deeper than arrogance. Arrogance is an attitude. *ʿAlā* is a position. Pharaoh has not merely *felt* superior — he has *occupied* a place in the social order that does not belong to him. He has placed himself above other human beings as a matter of structure. Not just opinion. Structure.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because here is where it gets uncomfortable.

The root *ʿ-l-w* is morally neutral in itself. Allah's *ʿuluww* — His highness — is beautiful, true, and the natural order of reality. The problem is not elevation. The problem is *misplaced* elevation. A creature occupying the throne of the Creator. A human being arrogating to himself a vantage point that is not his to take.

What this single verb is doing — and you have to feel the precision here — is naming the **root cause** of everything that follows. Pharaoh did not start with slaughtering children. He started with a *posture*. A claim about where he stood in relation to other people, and where he stood in relation to Allah. The children come later. The factions come later. The corruption is downstream.

Tyranny does not begin with violence. **It begins with a self-elevation that has lost its ceiling.**

That is what *ʿalā fil-arḍ* names. And Allah names it first because everything else is a consequence.

[PAUSE]

Notice one more thing before we move on. *ʿAlā* is past tense, perfect aspect — a completed act. He *did* this. It is already done by the time the ayah opens. But the consequences that follow — *yastaḍʿifu*, *yudhabbiḥu*, *yastaḥyī* — those are all in the present imperfect. Ongoing. Habitual. Repeated.

The grammar itself is showing you the architecture: one completed act of self-elevation, then a stream of continuous oppressions flowing out of it.

The Arabic is teaching you the diagnosis. One root sin. Many fruits.

---

### Element Two: *jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan* — "He made its people into factions"

Now the second movement. And we have to be very careful here, because this is the most modern-feeling word in the entire ayah.

*Shiyaʿan* — plural of *shīʿah*. From the root *sh-y-ʿ*, which classical lexicographers tell us carries the basic sense of a group that follows together, partisans, a band that walks behind something.

A *shīʿah* is not just a group. It is a **partisan** group. A faction defined by what it is *against* as much as what it is *for*. A team. A camp. A side.

And Allah says Pharaoh *jaʿala* — he *made* — the people of the land into *shiyaʿan*. Plural. Multiple factions.

Pause on this verb. *Jaʿala*. He *made* them. This is constructed. Engineered. The factions are not natural. They did not arise organically from differences of opinion or geography or family. Pharaoh *manufactured* them.

This is one of the most chillingly specific words in the entire Quranic vocabulary of oppression.

Here is the everyday version, so you can feel it. Imagine a workplace where, on day one, everyone gets along. People disagree about small things, but they share lunch. They cover each other's shifts. Now a new manager arrives. Within six months, the floor is split. The morning shift hates the night shift. The veterans resent the new hires. The men suspect the women, the women resent the men. Nobody can quite remember when this started. Nobody can point to a single moment.

That manager has done what Pharaoh did. *Jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan.* The factions did not exist. He *made* them.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what this reveals about how power works. Because here is what tyrants understand that we sometimes forget: **a unified people cannot be oppressed for long.** If the entire population of Egypt — Egyptians and Israelites alike — had stood together, Pharaoh's throne could not have held. His army was not large enough. His magicians were not powerful enough.

So the first thing he had to do, *before* the violence, was destroy the possibility of solidarity. He had to convince the Egyptians that the Israelites were Other. He had to convince the privileged Egyptians that the poor Egyptians were beneath them. He had to convince the soldiers that the children of the slaves were a threat to national security. He had to make the people of one land into *shiyaʿan*.

And only *then* — only after the factions were in place — could he move to the next step.

Here is what is remarkable: notice that Allah uses the word *ahlahā*. Its *people*. The people *of the land*. Not "the Israelites" yet. Not "the foreigners." *Ahl al-arḍ* — everyone living there, Egyptians and Israelites together. The crime is not just what was done to the Bani Isra'il. The crime is what was done to **the social fabric itself**. The whole land was fractured. The whole community was poisoned. Everyone was made into someone's enemy.

[PAUSE]

And then — *only then* — does Allah narrow the camera.

*Yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum.* He oppresses *a group among them*.

Look at the precision. First the factions are made. *Then* one is targeted. Stay with this sequence because it is the entire playbook.

The verb *yastaḍʿifu* is form X of the root *ḍ-ʿ-f*, weakness. Form X in Arabic often carries the sense of *seeking* or *treating as*. So *yastaḍʿifu* does not just mean "he weakened them." It means he **treated them as weak**. He *positioned* them as the weak ones. He *cast* them in the role of the powerless.

This is not a description of their actual state. This is a description of the role assigned to them by the tyrant.

The Bani Isra'il were not inherently weak. Allah will later call them — through their own deliverance — those whom He chose to make leaders and inheritors of the earth (and you can hear this in 28:5, the very next ayah). Their weakness was *manufactured*, just as the factions were manufactured. *Made*. *Constructed*. *Imposed*.

And here is the uncomfortable mirror this holds up to every age: the question is not whether some group is "really" weak. The question is — *who is doing the casting?* Who is sorting people into the role of the weak? Who benefits when this group is positioned as less-than? Whose throne is held up by their being held down?

That is what *yastaḍʿifu* names. Not weakness as a fact. Weakness as a *project*.

---

### Element Three: *yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa yastaḥyī nisāʾahum* — The Asymmetry

Now the third movement. And this is where I want you to slow your breathing, because what Allah does in this phrase is one of the most quietly devastating things in the Quran.

He uses two verbs side by side: *yudhabbiḥu* and *yastaḥyī*.

*Yudhabbiḥu* — from the root *dh-b-ḥ*, the verb used for ritual slaughter. The slow, deliberate cutting of the throat. Not killing in battle. Not collateral damage. *Slaughter.* And notice the form: it is form II — *yudhabbiḥu*, not *yadhbaḥu* — which in Arabic carries intensification or repetition. He was not just slaughtering. He was slaughtering *thoroughly, repeatedly, systematically*. The form itself is doing the work of describing an industry of killing.

And then, paired with it, *yastaḥyī*. From the root *ḥ-y-y*, life. Form X again — *seeking* or *causing*. He *keeps alive*. He *spares*. He *lets live*.

Now here is what I want you to feel. The sentence has a structure that, on the surface, looks almost balanced. Killing the sons / keeping the women. Two actions, almost parallel.

But it is not balanced. Listen to it again.

*Yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum* — he **slaughters** their sons.
*Yastaḥyī nisāʾahum* — he **keeps alive** their women.

[PAUSE]

What is the relationship between these two verbs?

In English, "kept alive" sounds like mercy. It sounds like the women were spared. As if Pharaoh, even in his cruelty, drew a line.

This is exactly what Allah wants you to feel — and then to see through.

Because *yastaḥyī* — kept them alive — in this context is not mercy. It is the *other half* of the same crime. To kill the sons of a people and keep their women alive is not to spare half the population. It is to **make the women available**. To turn them into servants. To leave them without protectors. To break the line of inheritance, the line of resistance, the line of fatherhood, the line of the future.

The sons are slaughtered so the line dies. The women are kept alive so the labor continues, so the bodies remain, so the population can be used without ever being allowed to *renew itself*. To kill all of them would be genocide. To do this — kill the future, keep the present — is something more particular. It is the engineering of a permanent underclass. A population kept alive only as raw material.

[PAUSE]

Stay with the asymmetry. Notice what Allah does *not* say. He does not say "he killed all of them." He does not say "he expelled them." He says — with the precision of a surgeon — *he slaughtered the sons and kept the women alive.*

And here the linguistic reading and the moral reading converge into one terrible insight: in the architecture of oppression, *which half* you kill matters as much as the killing itself. Pharaoh did not want the Israelites *dead*. He wanted them *manageable*. Permanently weakened. Useful. The killing of the sons was not the goal. The goal was the *shape* of the surviving population.

This is the most chilling phrase in the ayah because it tells you that the tyrant has *thought about this*. He has done the math. He has calculated which deaths will produce the social structure he wants.

It is, in the most literal sense, the industrialization of cruelty.

And notice — one more time — the grammar. *Yudhabbiḥu... yastaḥyī.* Both present-imperfect. Both ongoing. Both habitual. This is not one incident. This is a policy. A standing order. A routine.

The grammar is showing you that what we are looking at is not an outburst of violence. It is **a system**.

---

### Bridge

So we have three movements. Three precise linguistic naming acts.

*ʿAlā fil-arḍ* — he placed himself above. The posture.
*Jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan* — he made the people into factions. The fracturing.
*Yudhabbiḥu... yastaḥyī* — he killed selectively to engineer a population. The system.

[PAUSE]

Posture → fracture → system. Each one made the next possible. None could exist without the one before.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about Pharaoh — but about reality itself. About every age. About *our* age. About the human heart that is capable of building such things, and the human heart that is capable of living in such a system without noticing.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Three-Step Architecture of Every Tyranny

Let me start where you already live.

You have seen, in your own lifetime — in the news, in history class, in the headlines of last week — what happens when a society turns on a part of itself. You have seen the rhetoric warm up. You have seen the *us* harden against the *them*. You have seen the laws shift, then the language, then the violence.

If you have studied any 20th century atrocity — any of them, in any continent — you will recognize a pattern that historians and political scientists describe over and over again. They use phrases like "the eight stages of genocide." Or "the construction of the enemy." Or "demonization." Or "dehumanization." Whole academic fields have been built around describing this pattern.

And here is what should arrest you: **Allah described it in one ayah, fourteen centuries ago, in the order in which it happens.**

Posture. Fracture. System.

Let me show you what I mean.

**Step one** is always a leader — a regime, a movement, a class — that places itself *above* the moral order. Not above other people in talent or wealth, but above the rules that bind human beings to each other. A claim that *we* are not subject to the constraints that govern others. *We* answer to history, or to destiny, or to the nation, or to the race, or to the market, or to nothing. *We* are exempt. This is *ʿalā fil-arḍ*. The posture.

Before any violence, there is this posture. Always. The leader who speaks as if he is not bound by truth. The state that signals it is not bound by law. The class that signals it is not bound by the dignity of the others. The self-elevation comes first, and it is verbal and symbolic long before it is physical.

**Step two** is the division of the people into camps. *Shiyaʿan.* And notice — this is not natural diversity. This is *manufactured* faction. The population that yesterday lived alongside each other is taught, gradually, to see each other as threats. The media changes tone. The schools change curriculum. The neighbors stop greeting each other. Old friendships go cold. The new categories — these people, those people — become the only categories that matter.

You will find this in every regime that committed atrocities in the 20th century. There was always a generation of fracturing *before* the killing. The killing was unthinkable until the fracturing made it thinkable.

**Step three** is the selective violence that engineers the future. *Yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa yastaḥyī nisāʾahum.* This is the most sophisticated stage, and it is rarely as crude as outright extermination. It is more often a targeted breaking of one part of the community so the rest can be used. Kill the leaders. Imprison the young men. Disperse the families. Take the land but leave a labor force. Forbid the language but allow the bodies. Each tyrant has his own version, but the logic is the same: **shape the survivors so they cannot resist.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question Allah's ayah is asking us — and this is where it stops being a history lesson and starts being a mirror.

Where do *we* fit in this architecture?

Because every tyranny, throughout history, has been made possible by three groups of people: the architects, the enforcers, and the *bystanders*.

The architects are few. The enforcers are some. The bystanders are everyone else.

And the bystanders are not bad people. They are usually decent people. They are people who do not personally hate anyone. They are people who would, if asked directly, say that killing children is wrong. But they are also people who have absorbed the *posture* without noticing. Who have come to accept the *factions* without questioning. Who have stopped seeing the *system* because it is the air they breathe.

The most uncomfortable reading of this ayah is not "look at how bad Pharaoh was." The most uncomfortable reading is: **the Egypt described in this ayah had millions of people in it. And almost none of them stopped this from happening.**

What allowed that? The same thing that allows it now. Step one had been completed before they were born. Step two was finished while they were growing up. By the time step three was running at full capacity, it was just *the way things were*.

This is why Allah opens the story this way. Not so we can shake our heads at ancient Egypt. So we can ask, with terror — *what step is being completed around me right now, and have I noticed?*

The practical application is uncomfortable but clear: **train yourself to notice posture before you wait for violence**. When you hear a leader, a movement, a class, a regime speak as if it is not bound by the same moral order as everyone else — that is *ʿalā fil-arḍ* warming up. By the time it reaches *yudhabbiḥu*, it is too late. The work of resistance is done in step one.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: *Mufsidūn* — The Word Allah Reserves for the Last Sentence

Now turn with me to the last phrase of the ayah. We have spent the whole reflection inside Pharaoh's actions. And then Allah closes with a single judgment.

*Innahu kāna minal-mufsidīn.*

"Indeed, he was of the corrupters."

Look at the structure. The ayah opened with *inna* — "indeed, Pharaoh exalted himself." It closes with *innahu* — "indeed, he..." Two emphatic bookends. Allah does not say this casually. He frames the entire description between two affirmations.

And the word He chooses, in the closing — the word He reserves for the *summary* of everything Pharaoh did — is *mufsid*.

From the root *f-s-d*. Corruption. Decay. The opposite of *ṣalāḥ* — wholeness, soundness, the state of being well-ordered.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what I want you to feel. Allah has many words for evil-doers. *Ẓālimūn* — wrongdoers. *Kāfirūn* — concealers. *Mujrimūn* — criminals. *Fāsiqūn* — those who break out. *Mustakbirūn* — the arrogant.

He could have used any of these. Pharaoh fits every category.

But for *this* ayah — for the ayah that summarizes Pharaoh's entire tyranny — Allah chooses *mufsidīn*.

Why?

Because *fasād* — corruption — is not just moral wrongness. It is moral wrongness that **spreads**. It is wrongness that *disorders* what was ordered. It is a process. A spoilage. A rot.

When meat is *fāsid*, it has gone bad — but it has also become a *source* of badness. It will sicken whoever eats it. It will spoil what it touches. It cannot just be removed; what it has touched must also be discarded.

That is what Allah is naming.

Pharaoh is not just a wrongdoer. He is a *spreader of disorder*. He has not just committed individual sins. He has *poisoned the order of a society*. He has taken the *fiṭrah* — the natural state in which people would have lived together — and corrupted it into factions, into fear, into industries of slaughter.

And here is the lexical thread that should make you sit upright: *fasād* is the antonym of *ṣalāḥ*. The verb root of *ṣalāḥ* — *ṣ-l-ḥ* — is the same root from which we get *Aṣ-Ṣāliḥūn*, the righteous. The ones whose *being* makes the world *better-ordered*. Whose presence *heals*.

Allah's universe is structured around this opposition. Some people leave reality more whole than they found it. Some people leave it more broken. Pharaoh was the master of the second kind.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the bridge to your own life — because this is the most personal turn this ayah can make.

We tend to think of corruption as something *big people* do. Tyrants, dictators, billion-dollar fraudsters. Pharaoh-scale corruption.

But the word *mufsid* does not specify scale. It specifies *direction*. A *mufsid* is someone whose actions, large or small, leave the order of things *more broken* than they found it.

You can be a *mufsid* in your marriage by introducing suspicion that did not need to be there. You can be a *mufsid* in your workplace by feeding a rumor mill. You can be a *mufsid* in your family by playing siblings against each other. You can be a *mufsid* in your masjid by importing factionalism into a community that was finding its way to peace.

The difference between you and Pharaoh, in this reading, is one of degree. Not of kind. He fractured a kingdom. You can fracture a household. The verb is the same. The direction of the action is the same.

And Allah's closing judgment on Pharaoh is, with terrifying simplicity: *innahu kāna minal-mufsidīn*. Indeed, he was *of* the corrupters. Plural. *Min* — "of." He belongs to a category. Allah is placing him in a family, a tribe, a lineage of people across time who all do the same thing in their different scales.

The question the ayah leaves you with is whether your name belongs in that category — at any scale — or in its opposite. *Aṣ-Ṣāliḥūn*. Those whose presence reorders. Those whose touch heals. Those whose direction in the world is *toward* wholeness, not away from it.

The practical application: **stop measuring yourself only by what you have not done wrong**. Start measuring yourself by what your presence *did* to the order around you. Did the people in your home today, your office today, your phone screen today — did they become *more* whole because of you, or *less* whole? That is the question *mufsid* is asking. Every day. In small denominations. Of the same currency Pharaoh dealt in.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at the whole ayah with everything we have walked through.

*Inna Firʿawna ʿalā fil-arḍ* — the **posture**. The self-elevation. The root cause.

*Wa jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan* — the **fracture**. The making of factions. The destruction of solidarity.

*Yastaḍʿifu ṭāʾifatan minhum* — the **targeting**. The assignment of one group to the role of the weak.

*Yudhabbiḥu abnāʾahum wa yastaḥyī nisāʾahum* — the **engineering**. The selective violence that shapes the surviving population.

*Innahu kāna minal-mufsidīn* — the **diagnosis**. The summary judgment that names the entire process as one thing: corruption.

Five movements. One sentence. Bookended by two emphatic *inna*s — one naming the posture at the start, one naming the corruption at the end. The two *inna*s clasp the ayah closed like two hands meeting around the entire architecture of tyranny.

And what sits inside those two hands is not just a description of one man. It is a **diagnostic instrument**. A way to *see* the same pattern wherever it appears.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic journey showed you that the ayah is built as a sequence: one completed act of self-elevation, then a flood of present-tense oppressions flowing out of it.

The thematic journey showed you that this is not a story about ancient Egypt — it is a permanent template for how tyrannies work, and a permanent mirror for the small ways we ourselves participate in fracture.

Both are saying the same thing: **the visible violence is downstream of an invisible posture**. And the invisible posture — *ʿalā fil-arḍ* — is the thing the Quran wants you to *see first*. Before it has even introduced Musa. Before any prophet has spoken. Allah has shown you what the prophet is *for*: to undo what *ʿalā fil-arḍ* sets in motion.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life — in my speech, in my judgments, in my online presence — have I been doing a small version of *ʿalā fil-arḍ*? Placing myself, even subtly, above the moral order that binds me to others?

2. What factions has my generation been *taught* to see as natural? Which of the categories I use every day to sort people would not exist if a Pharaoh somewhere had not benefited from their existence?

3. When I hear someone speaking as if they are exempt from the rules — exempt from honesty, exempt from accountability, exempt from compassion — do I notice it as step one of something, or do I dismiss it as just talk?

4. In the rooms I walk into, in the conversations I enter — am I a *muṣliḥ* or a *mufsid*? Do I leave the order of the place more whole or more broken?

5. If Allah wrote a one-sentence diagnosis of *my* life — bookended by two emphatic *inna*s — which verbs would He put in the middle?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Tyranny is a posture before it is a policy, a fracture before it is a slaughter, and a system before it is a name — and Allah teaches us to recognize it at the root, not at the harvest.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this ayah with the comfortable feeling of having understood Pharaoh. Pharaoh is not the point. Pharaoh is the *case study*. The point is that Allah has handed you, in fifteen Arabic words, the diagnostic kit for every age — including yours, including the one you are walking through right now.

Carry this:
- Watch for *posture*. Long before violence.
- Watch for *fracture*. Especially the kinds you are told are natural.
- Watch for *systems* of selective harm — the killings that are not killings, the silencings that are not silencings, the engineerings of who survives and how.
- And every morning, ask yourself which side of *fasād* / *ṣalāḥ* your day is going to move things toward.

The world has had many Pharaohs. It has had fewer *muṣliḥūn*. The shortage is not accidental. It is what the ayah is *recruiting* you to address.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah — the only One who is truly *al-ʿAlī* — keep our hearts low before You so that we never rise falsely over others. Let the order we leave behind in this world be *ṣalāḥ*, not *fasād*. And in every age in which a Pharaoh rises, place us among those who see it at step one, name it at step two, and resist it before step three becomes the air everyone is breathing.

Āmīn.
