# The Curse That Walks Beside Them

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 42

> وَأَتْبَعْنَاهُمْ فِي هَـٰذِهِ الدُّنْيَا لَعْنَةً ۖ وَيَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ هُم مِّنَ الْمَقْبُوحِينَ

> *Wa atbaʿnāhum fī hādhihi'd-dunyā laʿnah, wa yawma'l-qiyāmati hum mina'l-maqbūḥīn.*

> "And We caused a curse to follow them in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection they will be among the despised."

---

## Introduction: The World Behind the Verdict

Before we step into this ayah, we have to feel where it sits.

Surah Al-Qasas has just spent forty-one ayāt building one of the most carefully constructed stories in the Quran. We watched a mother place her infant in a river. We watched that infant grow up inside the palace of the very man who was killing children like him. We watched that grown child flee to Madyan, marry, work for ten years, and then — at a bush in a valley — receive a command to return.

Then we watched Pharaoh.

We watched him gather his court. We watched him say to his minister, "Build me a tower so I can climb up and look at the God of Musa." We watched him stand on the balcony of his empire and declare:

> *Anā rabbukumu'l-aʿlā.*
> "I am your lord most high."

And then — silence. The army moves. The sea opens. The waters close.

The ayah right before our ayah ends like this:

> *Fa-naẓar kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu'ẓ-ẓālimīn.*
> "So look — how was the end of the wrongdoers."

You'd expect the story to end there. That's how stories end. The villain drowns. The credits roll. We move on.

But Allah does not move on. [PAUSE]

He says: there is something I attached to them *after* the drowning. Something that did not sink with their bodies. Something that is still happening — *fī hādhihi'd-dunyā* — "in this world." In this very world that you and I are sitting in right now.

Here's what I want you to feel before we open a single word. The original listener — the Quraysh of Makkah, the tribe of Bani Hashim, the small group around the Prophet ﷺ — they knew Pharaoh's name. Egypt was right there. The pyramids were already over a thousand years old. The mummies were already in their tombs. The hieroglyphs were already carved.

Pharaoh had won, in a sense. He had built the tallest buildings on earth. His face was on the walls. His name was on the obelisks. He was the most documented human being who had ever lived.

And yet — when Allah names him in His Book, He doesn't say "the one whose monuments are still standing." He says: "We attached a curse to him that follows him."

Stay with that gap. The Pharaohs of Egypt won the contest of architecture. They lost the contest of memory. How? Why? What exactly did Allah *do* to them that He didn't do to other tyrants? And what is the difference between the punishment in this world and the punishment on the Day of Resurrection — because Allah is going to give us both, and He is going to give them to us in a very specific order.

That's what we're going to sit with today.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We have three doors to walk through. Each one will open something the translation cannot show you.

### Element One: *Atbaʿnāhum* — "We caused [it] to follow them"

Before we look at the word, let me teach you the concept.

In Arabic, there is a family of verbs built from the root **ت-ب-ع** (*t-b-ʿ*). The basic meaning is "to follow." If you walk into a room and your child walks in behind you, your child is *tābiʿ* — a follower. The footprint follows the foot. The shadow follows the body. The echo follows the sound.

Now Arabic has different *forms* of the same root that change what the verb does. The simplest form, *tabiʿa*, means "he followed." The fourth form — *atbaʿa* — means "he caused [something] to follow," or "he attached [something] as a follower."

So when a shepherd brings his sheep, he is *tābiʿ* — following them. But when the shepherd attaches a bell to a sheep's neck so the bell goes wherever the sheep goes — that's a fourth-form action. He has *caused* something to follow.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah says:

> *Wa atbaʿnāhum fī hādhihi'd-dunyā laʿnah.*

He doesn't say "We cursed them." He doesn't say *laʿannāhum*. He says: **We caused a curse to follow them**. *Atbaʿnāhum… laʿnah.*

Notice something. A curse, in our usual way of thinking, is a thing pronounced *on* someone. It hits them. It lands. It's over.

But Allah is using the language of attachment. Of a thing trailing behind. Of a companion. As if the curse is walking behind them down the corridors of history.

Let's sit with this. Pharaoh drowned around — by most historical reconstructions — somewhere between three thousand and three thousand five hundred years ago. He is, at this point, a body. A corpse. By any normal measure, nothing further can happen to him in this world. He is past the reach of dunya.

And yet Allah says: *fī hādhihi'd-dunyā* — "in **this** dunya" — there is a curse that is still attached to him. Still following him. Still active.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah did not say *atbaʿathum laʿnah* — "a curse followed them," as if it were doing its own thing. He said *atbaʿnāhum* — "**We** caused [it] to follow them." The first-person plural pronoun of divine majesty. Allah is the one attaching the curse. Allah is the one keeping it walking behind them.

This is not a passive consequence. It is not karma. It is not what naturally happens to bad people. It is something Allah specifically, actively, deliberately, **attached**.

[PAUSE]

Think about what this means. If you and I curse someone, our curse lasts as long as our memory lasts, and our memory lasts as long as our life. When we die, the curse dies with us. But Allah is *al-Ḥayy* — the Living. His attachment does not expire. The curse does not run out of fuel.

And here is the second layer — and this is where we have to be careful, because this is what scholars throughout the tradition have observed.

Al-Rāzī, Ibn Kathīr, Al-Qurṭubī — when they comment on *atbaʿnāhum laʿnah*, they offer different cameras on the same reality. One opinion: the curse is that every generation that comes after Pharaoh names him with contempt. Another: the curse is that he is held up as the symbol of human evil — the very word "pharaoh" becomes synonymous with tyranny. A third: the curse is the disgrace attached to his memory in every revealed scripture that came after him.

These are not three different curses. They are three sides of the same curse: **a name that becomes its own punishment**.

Look at our own moment. Five thousand years of human civilization. Hundreds of thousands of kings, emperors, sultans, generals, presidents. How many of them does the average person on earth know by name? Maybe twenty. Maybe thirty.

And one of those names — guaranteed — is **Pharaoh**.

But notice *how* you know it. You don't know it the way you know Alexander or Napoleon, with a kind of mixed admiration. You know it as a category of evil. When someone wants to call a tyrant the worst thing they can call them, they call him a Pharaoh. The Quran itself uses *firʿawn* almost as a common noun — *firʿawn* and *kullu firʿawn* — Pharaoh and every Pharaoh.

What this invites us to consider is this: Allah did not just kill the man. He converted his name into a warning sign that every human being for the rest of history would read and recoil from. The man wanted to be a god. Allah made him the universal symbol of what happens when you try.

And the verb form tells us — this is not over. *Atbaʿnāhum*, in classical Arabic, can carry the sense of an action whose effects continue. The curse is *still* following. Right now. As we speak.

---

### Element Two: *Laʿnah* — The Indefinite Curse

Now look at the word *laʿnah* itself.

The root **ل-ع-ن** (*l-ʿ-n*) in classical Arabic — as documented in *Lisān al-ʿArab* and *Mufradāt al-Rāghib* — does not primarily mean "to wish evil." Its core meaning is **distancing**. To curse someone is to drive them far away — to place them outside the circle of mercy, outside the circle of acceptance, outside the circle of being-with.

A *laʿīn* is one who has been pushed out. Iblīs is called *al-laʿīn* — the distanced one. He is not primarily someone who has been wished evil upon. He is someone who has been pushed beyond the threshold.

So when Allah attaches *laʿnah* to Pharaoh in this world, the meaning is: I have placed him outside. Outside what? Outside the company of those whose memory humans honor. Outside the company of those whom hearts return to with affection. Outside the gathering of human beings.

Notice something now. *Laʿnah* is **indefinite**. Allah did not say *al-laʿnah* — "**the** curse," with the definite article. He said *laʿnah* — "a curse." Some curse. An unspecified, unbounded curse.

Stay with me on this, because Arabic does this deliberately.

In Arabic, when you make a noun indefinite in a context like this, you can be doing one of two things: you can be making it vague, or you can be making it **vast**. Grammarians call this *tanwīn al-taʿẓīm* — the indefiniteness of magnification. The idea is: I am not specifying it, because no specification could contain it.

If I say, "I have **a** problem" — I might mean a small problem. But if I say, "Brother, I have *a problem*" — with the right tone — you understand: this thing is bigger than I can name. The indefinite, used this way, doesn't shrink the thing. It bursts it open.

When Allah says *atbaʿnāhum laʿnah* — "We caused **a** curse to follow them" — the indefinite here is doing the second thing. Not a small curse. Not a defined curse. A curse so vast that to name it would be to limit it. Every kind of disgrace. Every form of being-pushed-out. Every variety of historical contempt. All of it. Unspecified — because it is too much to specify.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing in this word that you cannot see in the English.

In Arabic, *laʿnah* is a noun built on the pattern *faʿlah* — a pattern that, among other things, often denotes a single instance of an action, but in poetic and Quranic usage can also carry a connotation of completeness or finality. The shape of the word — short, sharp, decisive — *laʿ-nah* — sounds like a verdict being pronounced. There is a phonetic finality to it. Listen: *atbaʿnā-hum… fī hādhihi'd-dunyā… laʿ-nah.*

It lands. It doesn't roll. It doesn't trail. The sentence has a hammer at the end.

This is what Arabic does when the meaning is final. The sound carries the verdict.

---

### Element Three: *Maqbūḥīn* — The Word That Sounds Like What It Means

Now we come to the third door. This is the most extraordinary word in the ayah, and I want to teach you why before you see it.

Allah is now speaking about the Day of Resurrection:

> *Wa yawma'l-qiyāmati hum mina'l-maqbūḥīn.*
> "And on the Day of Resurrection they will be among the **maqbūḥīn**."

The root is **ق-ب-ح** (*q-b-ḥ*). It means: ugly. Disfigured. Repulsive. Something whose appearance causes the eye to recoil.

In classical Arabic lexicons — *Lisān al-ʿArab*, *Tāj al-ʿArūs*, *Mufradāt al-Rāghib* — *qabīḥ* is the standard opposite of *ḥasan*. *Ḥasan* is beautiful, pleasing, good to look upon. *Qabīḥ* is ugly, repellent, painful to look upon.

But Allah does not use the simple form *qabīḥīn*. He uses *maqbūḥīn* — a passive participle on the *mafʿūl* pattern.

This is critical, and it's where most translations lose the meaning.

*Qabīḥ* means: ugly, as an inherent quality. The thing is ugly by its nature.

*Maqbūḥ* means: **made ugly**. Rendered ugly. Disfigured. The ugliness has been *done to it*.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference? A person can be plain. That's *qabīḥ*. But a person whose face has been twisted, whose features have been deformed, who has been *turned into* something repellent by an outside hand — that is *maqbūḥ*.

The passive participle tells us: someone did this to them. They were *made* this way. They did not arrive at the Day of Resurrection ugly by nature. They were *rendered* ugly by judgment.

And there is something else.

Listen to the word. Say it aloud. *Maq-bū-ḥīn.* 

Feel what your mouth does. The *q* — *qāf* — is a deep guttural, pulled from the back of the throat. The *ḥ* — *ḥā'* — is a constricted breath, almost a wheeze. The whole word is built from sounds that the human mouth produces with difficulty, with constriction, with a kind of involuntary recoil.

This is what classical Arabic rhetoricians called *munāsabat al-lafẓ li'l-maʿnā* — the correspondence of sound and meaning. The word *maqbūḥīn* is itself unpleasant to pronounce. It is, phonetically, ugly. The mouth resists it.

Allah is not just telling you these people are disfigured. He is making you feel the disfigurement in your own mouth as you say the word.

Here's what's remarkable. *Ḥasan* — beautiful — flows. *Jamīl* — beautiful — sings. But *maqbūḥ* — disfigured — chokes. You can hear the verdict in the consonants.

Now look at the larger structure. Look at what Allah did across the two halves of the ayah:

**In dunya**: He attached *laʿnah* — distancing. A curse that pushes them out of human memory.

**In ākhira**: He places them among *al-maqbūḥīn* — the disfigured. A judgment that pushes them out of human appearance.

Pushed out of memory in this world. Pushed out of beauty in the next. The same divine action, working on two different planes.

[PAUSE — major pause]

But here is the question that has been waiting all along, and now we can finally ask it.

**Why does the Day of Resurrection get the second clause?**

Look at the structure of the ayah again. Allah is the One who designs every word in this Book. He could have said simply: "We caused a curse to follow them in this world and in the Hereafter." That would have been theologically sufficient. Done.

But He didn't. He gave dunya one verdict — *laʿnah* — and ākhira another verdict — *maqbūḥīn*. Two different words. Two different consequences.

Why?

Because in this dunya, you cannot see them. Pharaoh is in a tomb. You cannot look at his face and recoil. The curse on him in this world is a curse on his *name*, on his *memory*, on his *legacy* — because that is all that is accessible to other humans. We can only encounter him through report.

But on the Day of Resurrection, every soul is resurrected with a face. With a body. With an appearance. And on that day, what was hidden becomes visible. What was a curse on the name in this dunya — becomes a curse on the face in the next.

The disfigurement, the *maqbūḥ*-ness, is what divine distancing *looks like* when there is finally something to look at.

[PAUSE]

That is the linguistic architecture of the ayah. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Way Allah Punishes Memory — and What That Says About What You Are Building Right Now

Let me start somewhere that has nothing to do with Pharaoh.

I want you to think about your grandparents — the ones you knew, or the ones you heard about. Picture them. Now think about *their* grandparents. Can you name them? Can you describe their faces?

For most of us, the answer is no. Three or four generations back, and the people who built our bloodlines become a fog. Names get lost. Faces get lost. The specifics of what they cared about, what they fought for, what made them laugh — gone.

This is not a tragedy. This is just what time does. Time is a kind of soft erasure. It blurs everyone.

Now here is the strange thing about Pharaoh. Time did not blur him.

Three thousand five hundred years later — long after every other person in his entire kingdom has been forgotten — his name is on the lips of children in Indonesia, in Senegal, in Brazil, in upstate New York. Children who will never visit Egypt know the word *Firʿawn*.

Time did not erase him. Time made him *more* visible.

This is what the ayah is pointing at. *Atbaʿnāhum fī hādhihi'd-dunyā laʿnah.* When Allah attaches a curse to someone in this dunya, the curse is not that they suffer in their lifetime. They might suffer, they might not. The curse is that **their memory becomes the very mechanism of their disgrace**.

What people normally want — to be remembered — Allah turns into the instrument of his punishment. You wanted your name on the obelisk? Granted. Now every human being who reads it for the next ten thousand years will read it as a warning.

[PAUSE]

Now let me bring this close to home. Because in our moment — in this exact moment of human history — every single one of us is building a memory of ourselves. Not on obelisks. On screens. On profiles. On the trail of digital exhaust that follows every click, every post, every comment, every photo.

We are, all of us, more documented than any pharaoh ever was.

And we tell ourselves the documentation is for *us*. It's how we keep our friends. It's how we remember our trips. It's how we share our lives.

But pay attention to what the ayah is showing us. Pharaoh thought his monuments were for *him*. He thought his face on the wall was *his* face. What Allah is teaching us is that the moment you build a public memory of yourself, that memory passes out of your hands. It becomes a thing Allah can use. To honor you. Or to attach something to you that follows you long after you cannot defend yourself.

This is not a warning about social media. This is older than social media. It's a warning about *the structure of memory* — that what you broadcast about yourself, you are handing to a process you do not control.

Consider: every prophet in the Quran is also remembered three thousand years later. Mūsā is remembered. Ibrāhīm is remembered. Yūsuf is remembered. They are remembered with *raḥmah* — mercy — on their names. People say *ʿalayhi as-salām* — peace be upon him — after their names, by reflex, for thirty centuries.

Pharaoh is remembered too. Just as long. With the opposite trailing behind his name.

The same length of memory. Two different reputations. Two different curses or blessings *attached*.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow?

Here is one thing. Pay attention to what you are choosing to be **remembered for**. Not "what do I want to be known for among my friends." Not "what's my brand." But: if Allah decided that your name would last for three centuries after you, what would He attach to it?

You don't get to control whether you are remembered. You don't even fully get to control *what* you are remembered for. But you can shape, through your actual life, the kind of person whom memory will find when it reaches you.

The companions of the Prophet ﷺ did not try to be famous. Most of them avoided it. And yet — fourteen hundred years later — Muslims still name their children Abū Bakr and ʿUmar and ʿUthmān and ʿAlī, with love. Memory found them. And what memory found, was something worth following them with.

The opposite of *atbaʿnāhum laʿnah* is what Allah said of His prophets in another place: *wa jaʿalnā lahum lisāna ṣidqin ʿaliyyā* — "We made for them a tongue of truth, exalted" (Maryam 19:50). A tongue. Out in the world. Speaking well of them. Long after they are gone.

A name is a thing that walks behind you in this dunya. Allah decides what walks with it.

### Theme Two: The Justice of Two Verdicts — Why Allah Does Not Settle Accounts All at Once

Now let me ask you something that probably bothered you when we first opened the ayah, even if you didn't say it out loud.

Why two punishments? Pharaoh has already drowned. Then a curse follows him in dunya. *Then* he is disfigured on the Day of Resurrection. That is — if you count carefully — *three* things. Drowning. Worldly curse. Resurrection disfigurement.

Is this not… excessive?

Let me sit with this honestly, because I think this is a question that quietly haunts many believers, and we don't usually let ourselves ask it.

Here is what I want you to notice first. We have a deep intuition that justice should be *one* event. The verdict is read. The sentence is carried out. It's over. We close the book.

But human justice is *one* event because human beings cannot reach beyond death. Human courts only have access to a person's body and a person's lifetime. So they have to compress everything into one window.

Allah is not bounded that way.

What this ayah is showing us — and this is something the classical mufassirūn point to consistently — is that Allah's justice has multiple layers because **the harm itself was multi-layered**.

Think about what Pharaoh actually did. He didn't just commit one act. He built a *system*. He killed babies. He enslaved a nation. He persecuted believers. He claimed divinity. He built monuments to himself with the labor of the oppressed. He corrupted his own people by training them to worship him.

The harm continues *after* him. His system shaped Egyptian religion for centuries. His example inspired other tyrants. His architecture sat there for three thousand years saying "this is what greatness looks like" — and that lie required correction.

So Allah's justice unfolds at the scale of the crime.

The drowning — that's the personal accountability. The man dies for what the man did.

The worldly curse — that's the systemic accountability. The *example* he set has to be unraveled. Every generation that might have looked at his architecture and thought "I want that" — Allah attaches a curse to the example so that the lesson is the opposite.

The Day of Resurrection — that's the cosmic accountability. The lie he told — *anā rabbukum al-aʿlā* — was a cosmic lie. It claimed something only Allah can claim. A cosmic lie requires a cosmic verdict. And that verdict has to happen in front of all the witnesses, in the place where everyone can see.

[PAUSE]

Now hold this against your own life.

There is something deeply consoling here, and something deeply sobering.

The consoling thing is this: when you have been wronged — really wronged, in the way that breaks something in you that no apology can mend — you may have noticed that worldly justice cannot reach it. The court date passes. The settlement is signed. And the wound is still there.

Allah is telling you: I see the harm in three dimensions. I see the act. I see what the act set into motion. I see what the act said about the order of things. And I have a verdict for each layer. None of the layers will be missed. None of the wounds you carry are too subtle for me. None of the systems that hurt you are too entrenched for me. None of the cosmic distortion is too large for me.

The sobering thing is this: when you do harm — really do harm, in the way that breaks something in someone else — you cannot settle it by surviving the moment. You cannot settle it by escaping the consequences in dunya. There is a layer that walks behind you in this world. And there is a layer that waits in the next.

This is why the believers' relationship to wrongdoing is so urgent. It is not enough to say "I got away with it." Nobody gets away with it. The accounts are spread across more dimensions than the wrongdoer can see.

[PAUSE]

But here is the mercy that lives inside this verse — and you have to know where to look.

The verdict in this ayah is on people who *did not turn back*. Pharaoh, until his last breath, was claiming divinity. The ayāt right before this one show that even when the waters were rising around him and he tried to say "I believe" — Allah said: *now*? After all this time?

The two-fold punishment is for the one who did not seek shelter.

But the door of *tawbah* — turning back — is, in the same Quran, described as wider than the heavens and the earth. The same Allah who attaches *laʿnah* to Pharaoh is the One who says: *qul yā ʿibādiya'lladhīna asrafū ʿalā anfusihim lā taqnaṭū min raḥmati'llāh* — "Say: O My servants who have wronged themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (Az-Zumar 39:53).

The two verdicts in this ayah are not Allah's first response to a wrongdoer. They are His final response to a wrongdoer who never turned.

So what changes about how I live tomorrow?

When I see myself drifting — when I see myself building a small Pharaoh inside myself, the part of me that wants to be its own god, that wants to be the highest authority in my own life — I should not say "I'll deal with this later." Because *later* is the dimension Allah controls.

I should turn back now. While the door is still wider than the sky.

That's the practical application. Not fear of disfigurement. Fear of *delay*. Fear of waiting until the waters are at my neck before I remember Whose I am.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now let's pull back and see the whole shape of what Allah built.

The ayah has two halves, and they mirror each other with precision:

**Half one:** *Wa atbaʿnāhum fī hādhihi'd-dunyā laʿnah.* — Verb of attachment + this world + curse.

**Half two:** *Wa yawma'l-qiyāmati hum mina'l-maqbūḥīn.* — Day of Resurrection + they + among the disfigured.

But notice something. The first half uses **action language**: *We caused [it] to follow*. Allah is the active subject. The curse is the object He is moving.

The second half uses **state language**: *They will be among the disfigured.* The verb has receded. The state remains.

This is the architecture. In dunya, Allah is *still working* on them — the curse is in motion, the attachment is happening, the action is ongoing. In ākhira, the work is done. They have arrived at the state. The verbs are over. The condition simply *is*.

This is what divine justice looks like in time. It begins as action — moving, attaching, following. It ends as a settled reality.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two directions.

Linguistically, we saw that Allah did not curse Pharaoh — He **attached** a curse. The fourth-form verb did not erase his name; it kept his name moving through history, carrying its disgrace with it.

Thematically, we saw that this is how Allah punishes memory itself. Not by erasing the tyrant. By *preserving* him — in such a way that his preservation becomes his curse.

Linguistically, we saw that *maqbūḥīn* is not "ugly" but "**made** ugly" — passive participle, done-to, rendered.

Thematically, we saw that this corresponds to the cosmic layer of justice — the layer where Allah does, in front of all witnesses, what He had been doing quietly through history.

The whole ayah is one truth told twice. The verdict is announced in this world through memory, and the verdict is enacted in the next world through appearance. Same verdict. Different visibility.

### Questions to Carry

1. If Allah were to attach something to your name and let it walk through three centuries after you — what would He find to attach? Be honest. What is the texture of the life you are leaving in your trail?

2. When you imagine "getting away with" something — what specifically are you imagining escaping? And which of the three layers of justice does your imagination simply forget exists?

3. Pharaoh's first declaration was *anā rabbukumu'l-aʿlā* — "I am your lord most high." Where, in your interior life, is there a quiet version of this same claim — the part of you that wants the final say in your own life?

4. The ayah says the curse *follows*. What in your life right now is following you because of something you set into motion long ago? And what would it take to set something better into motion today?

5. The opposite of *atbaʿnāhum laʿnah* is what Allah did for the righteous: a tongue of truth, exalted. What would you have to *do* in this dunya — not say, do — for Allah to attach that to your name instead?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah does not erase the tyrant — He preserves him as his own punishment, attaching disgrace to his memory in this world and disfigurement to his form in the next, because cosmic crimes require cosmic answers and the One who answers them is not bound by the moment of death.

### The Closing Invitation

If you take one thing from this ayah, let it be this:

The most dangerous thing you can do in this life is to assume that what survives you, survives intact. Pharaoh survived. His body survived. His monuments survived. His name survived. Everything survived — except his honor.

What walks behind your name is being shaped, right now, by what you are doing right now. Not by what you are posting. Not by what you are claiming. By what you are actually *doing*, in the quiet places where no human is watching.

The verdict of this ayah is not just about Pharaoh. It is the disclosure of how reality works. Every human being is being *followed*. Either by *raḥmah* — mercy that catches up to you in dunya and welcomes you in ākhira — or by *laʿnah* — distancing that disgraces your name in dunya and your face in ākhira.

The invitation is not to be afraid. The invitation is to walk in such a way that what follows you is something you would be honored to have catch up.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, do not attach to our names a curse that walks behind us — but attach to our names a tongue of truth that prays for us in places we will never see. Do not make us among those whose memory is their punishment, but among those whose memory becomes a path of light for those who come after. And on the Day of Resurrection, when faces are made manifest, let our faces be among those You have lit, not among those You have rendered.

*Āmīn.*
