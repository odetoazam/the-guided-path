# And We Wanted: The Divine Reversal That Rewrites History

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 5

### The Arabic

> وَنُرِيدُ أَن نَّمُنَّ عَلَى الَّذِينَ اسْتُضْعِفُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَنَجْعَلَهُمْ أَئِمَّةً وَنَجْعَلَهُمُ الْوَارِثِينَ

### Transliteration

*Wa nurīdu an namunna ʿalā alladhīna ustuḍʿifū fī al-arḍi wa najʿalahum a'immatan wa najʿalahumu al-wārithīn.*

### Translation

*And We wanted to bestow favor upon those who were oppressed in the land, and to make them leaders, and to make them the inheritors.*

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to stand inside the moment it answers.

The surah opens with three Arabic letters — *Ṭā Sīn Mīm* — and then plunges us into a world. Not a parable. Not a metaphor. A specific place, a specific time, and a specific terror.

Pharaoh has divided his people into factions. He has *weakened* one group among them. He is slaughtering their sons. He spares their women — which is not mercy, it is a slow strangling of a people. A nation without sons is a nation that dies in one generation. The math of genocide is patient.

Now picture the woman whose son was just born this morning. Picture the father who buried his last boy a week ago. Picture an entire people moving through their days under a power so total, so embedded in the machinery of the state, that there is no one to appeal to. The judge is Pharaoh. The army is Pharaoh. The temple is Pharaoh. The Nile itself, the very source of life, has been turned into a tomb for their children.

The Quran spends four verses building this world. The tyranny. The faction-making. The slaughter. And then [PAUSE] — verse five.

Here is what I want you to notice before we even open the words. The four verses before this one are in the *past tense*. Pharaoh **exalted** himself. He **made** the people factions. He **was weakening** them. He **was slaughtering** their sons. All past. All done. Closed history.

Then verse five arrives, and the very first word breaks the tense. *Wa nurīdu* — **And We are wanting**. Present tense. Active. Ongoing. Right now.

Something is happening underneath the visible history. Something is moving while the slaughter is still fresh. Allah is about to tell us what was *really* going on — not just on the surface of Egypt, but in the unseen layer where He was already writing a different ending.

Here's what's stunning, and what I want you to hold as a question through this entire reflection: **why does Allah want us to know what He** ***wanted***? He could have simply told us what He did. He could have skipped ahead to Musa floating in a basket. Instead, He pauses the story, looks straight at us, and tells us about His own intention — using a verb shape that keeps that intention alive, present, available, *now*.

This is the question. Sit with it. We'll come back.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Verb That Refuses to Close — *Nurīdu*

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we go to this one.

Arabic has two main tense forms. *Māḍī* is the past tense — completed action. Done. Sealed. The wave has already broken. *Muḍāriʿ* is the present-future tense — ongoing, recurring, alive, still in motion. The wave is still cresting. It is named *muḍāriʿ* because it *resembles* — it resembles the active, the immediate, the not-yet-finished.

In English, we mostly choose between "I wanted" and "I want." Arabic gives you a third thing: a verb shape that means **I am wanting and continue to want**. A wanting that doesn't close.

Now look at the four verses before this one. *Innaʾ Firʿawna ʿalā* — **Pharaoh exalted himself**. *Wa jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan* — **and he made its people factions**. These are *māḍī*. Past tense. They describe Pharaoh's tyranny as a closed, finished event in history.

Then verse five. *Wa nurīdu*. **And We are wanting.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah didn't say *aradnā* — "We wanted" — past tense, parallel to Pharaoh's past-tense tyranny. He could have. The grammar would have worked. The story would have been clean: Pharaoh did X, We did Y, the end.

Instead, He chose the *muḍāriʿ*. **We are wanting.**

Let's sit with this. What does it do?

It does something almost impossible to do in English. It takes a divine intention that was active *fourteen hundred years before the Quran was revealed* — already fulfilled, already historical, already documented in the story of Musa that everyone knew — and it *reopens* it. It refuses to close it.

It's as if Allah is saying: yes, this happened to the Children of Israel. Yes, I lifted them. Yes, I drowned Pharaoh. But the verb I use to describe what I was doing then is the same verb I would use to describe what I am doing *now*. The wanting did not end with the drowning. The wanting is a permanent posture of Mine toward a permanent category of people.

Stay with me. Here's what this means.

When you read this verse, you are not reading a history book. You are reading a divine standing rule. The tense itself is telling you: **this is not a one-time event. This is how I move through history. This is who I am.**

The classical commentators noticed this immediately. They pointed out that the *muḍāriʿ* here generalizes the specific. It takes the Children of Israel as one instance of a deeper pattern. The pattern is the rule. The Children of Israel are the case study.

And then there is one more thing the *muḍāriʿ* does. It puts the verb into the same time as the reader. Whenever you open this page — whether you are a Companion in Madinah hearing Surah Al-Qasas for the first time, or a slave in 16th century Andalusia, or a Palestinian child reading by candlelight tonight — *Wa nurīdu*. **We are wanting.** Right now. The verb meets you in your present.

[PAUSE]

This is what this single verb does: it takes Pharaoh's tyranny and seals it in the past, and it takes Allah's response and keeps it perpetually open.

One verb. Two different relationships with time. The oppression is finite. The divine intention is permanent.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Passive That Refuses to Name a Villain — *Ustuḍʿifū*

Now I want to show you something even more remarkable. It is hidden inside the word that describes the oppressed.

The word is *ustuḍʿifū*. The root is *ḍ-ʿ-f*, which means weakness. From this root, classical Arabic builds several forms:

- *Ḍaʿufa* (Form I) — **he became weak**. Just weakness as a state.
- *Ḍaʿʿafa* (Form II) — **he doubled, he multiplied**. (Different shading — but watch this root flex.)
- *Istaḍʿafa* (Form X) — **he sought to make someone weak; he treated someone as weak; he counted them as weak.**

That last form, Form X, is the active verb. *Istaḍʿafa* — to *deem* someone weak and to *treat* them according to that deeming. To look at a human being and assign them the status of weak — and then act on that assignment.

But the word in our verse is not the active *istaḍʿafa*. It is the *passive*: *ustuḍʿifū*. **Those who were deemed-weak. Those who were treated-as-weak.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question I want you to hold. Why didn't Allah say *al-ḍuʿafāʾ* — **the weak ones**? That's a perfectly normal Arabic word. It just means the weak. The vulnerable. The lesser.

He didn't use it. He used a passive verb in Form X.

This is doing two things at once, and I want to take them slowly.

**First**: the form *istaḍʿafa* does not say they *were* weak. It says they were *treated as* weak. *Counted as* weak. *Deemed* weak. The weakness is not in them. The weakness is in the eye that looked at them and the hand that held them down.

Think about what that means. Allah is refusing to grant Pharaoh's verdict. Pharaoh looked at these people and saw weak ones. Allah looks at the same people and says: no — they are those upon whom weakness was *imposed*. The grammar itself rejects Pharaoh's assessment. The grammar says: their weakness was a label, not a reality. It was something done to them, not something they were.

This is enormous. Before Allah lifts them in the next clause, He has already lifted them grammatically. He has refused to call them what their oppressor called them.

**Second** — and this is the absence I want you to notice — the verb is *passive*. *Ustuḍʿifū*. **Those who were weakened.** Weakened by *whom*?

Arabic, when it uses the passive, has a name for an agent that goes unnamed: *al-fāʿil maḥdhūf* — the doer is *deleted*. Suppressed. Removed from the sentence.

Where is Pharaoh in this verse?

He is not here.

He was named in the previous verses. *Innaʾ Firʿawna*. *Pharaoh exalted himself.* *Pharaoh made factions.* In those verses he is the subject, he is the agent, he is the grammatical center.

Then verse five arrives, and Allah removes him from the sentence entirely. The verb is passive. The oppressor is grammatically dissolved. The people who were oppressed are present — *alladhīna ustuḍʿifū*, **those who were oppressed** — but the one who oppressed them is *gone*.

[PAUSE]

Let me make sure you feel this.

In verses three and four, Pharaoh is the sun the sentence rotates around. He is named. He is the subject. The grammar gives him weight.

In verse five — the verse where Allah declares His own intention — Pharaoh has vanished from the syntax. He is not even worth being a named agent. The grammar treats him the way Allah will treat him on the Day of Judgment: not as a power to be reckoned with, but as a deleted variable.

This is what the passive voice is doing here. It is performing, in syntax, what the surah will later perform in history. Pharaoh's name will be erased. The waters will close over him. He will become a *sign* rather than an actor. And it begins right here, in this verb.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer. The classical scholars noted that the passive *ustuḍʿifū* opens the category wider than any name could. It does not say *Banī Isrāʾīl* — the Children of Israel. It says **those who were deemed weak**.

That phrase has no national boundary. It has no ethnic boundary. It has no century. *Those who were treated as weak in the land* — anywhere, any time, anyone.

Combine this with the *muḍāriʿ* of *nurīdu*. The verb of divine wanting is in a permanent present. The object of that wanting is a permanent category. The result is a divine standing promise that meets every oppressed person in every age. Allah's grammar built that on purpose.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Gift Before the Inheritance — *Namunna*

There is one more word I have to open with you, because this word is the *engine* of the entire verse. Without it, the rest of the verse would mean something else.

The word is *namunna*. The root is *m-n-n*. Allah says *an namunna ʿalayhim* — **that We bestow** *mann* **upon them**.

*Mann* is one of the most theologically saturated words in the Quran. It is hard to translate. Classical lexicons document several layers of meaning, and we have to take them carefully because each one matters.

At its base, *mann* means a *favor freely given* — a gift extended without prior obligation on the part of the giver, and without prior earning on the part of the receiver. It is *gratuitous grace*. Pure bestowal.

But it carries another classical sense — heaviness, weight, that which is substantial. As if to say: this is not a token. This is a load-bearing favor. A favor that *changes* the one who receives it.

And there is one more sense documented in the classical lexicons that you have to hear. The word *mann* is the same word used for the food Allah sent down to the Children of Israel in the desert — *al-mannu wal-salwā*. The manna and the quails. Food that fell from the sky. Food that arrived not because they planted it, not because they worked for it, not because they deserved it, but because Allah wanted to feed them.

That is the texture of the word.

Now look at where Allah places this word in the verse.

*Wa nurīdu an **namunna** ʿalā alladhīna ustuḍʿifū fī al-arḍ.*

**And We are wanting to bestow** *mann* **upon those who were deemed weak in the land** — *and* then to make them leaders, *and* then to make them inheritors.

Notice the order. The *mann* comes *first*. Before the leadership. Before the inheritance. Before the lifting up.

[PAUSE]

What this is saying is profound. The leadership and the inheritance are not the *mann*. They are the *consequence* of the *mann*. The *mann* is something *prior* — something that has to happen *first* before any of the lifting becomes possible.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah is telling us that the rescue of the oppressed begins with a gift they do not deserve and cannot earn. It does not begin with their virtue. It does not begin with their patience surpassing some threshold. It does not begin with them becoming worthy.

It begins with Allah *wanting to give*. The wanting is His. The gift is His. The choosing of *them* — *these* people, the ones the world labeled weak — is His.

The Children of Israel in the time of Musa were not a particularly righteous people. Read the rest of the Torah-narrative in the Quran. They argue. They complain. They worship a calf. They demand to see Allah. They refuse to enter the Holy Land. Even *after* the rescue.

And yet Allah's verse here, before any of that, says: **I want to bestow** mann **upon them.**

The lifting is not a reward. The lifting is a gift.

This is the same texture that runs through the entire Quran when Allah describes how He rescues people. He does not say "and We rescued them because they deserved it." He says *mann* — I *gave*. And the giving was Mine to give, and the choosing was Mine to make.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing about *mann* that has to be said.

The word also carries — in some usages — the texture of something that *binds* the giver to the receiver. A gift so substantial that it places the receiver inside a relationship of indebtedness and intimacy with the one who gave. When Allah bestows *mann*, He is not just transferring resources. He is *claiming* the one He gives to.

This is why, after the rescue from Pharaoh, the Children of Israel are addressed throughout the Quran with the phrase *yā banī Isrāʾīl, udhkurū niʿmatī allatī anʿamtu ʿalaykum* — **O Children of Israel, remember My favor that I bestowed upon you.** The favor is not a transaction that ended. It is a relationship that began.

The *mann* of verse five opens the relationship. Everything that follows in the surah — Musa being saved, Musa being trained, Musa being sent, the people being delivered — is the unfolding of one gift Allah decided, before any of it happened, to give.

[PAUSE — major pause]

A verb of wanting that refuses to close.

A gift that comes before any earning.

Three linguistic features. One reality underneath them. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how Allah moves through history itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Invisible Counter-Decree

Let me start with something every human being knows.

There is a kind of suffering that does not just hurt — it *closes the sky*. It convinces you that whatever is happening is the only thing happening. That power is what you see, and there is nothing underneath it. That the people who are in charge are in charge, and the people who are crushed are crushed, and that is simply the geometry of the world.

Everyone who has lived through real oppression knows this feeling. Refugees know it. Abused children know it. People watching their cities burn on a phone screen know it. There is a flat, exhausted certainty that the visible world *is* the world.

Now look at what verse five does.

The first four verses of the surah describe the visible world. Pharaoh rises. Pharaoh divides. Pharaoh slaughters. Pharaoh spares the women so the people will die out in one generation. This is what you see. This is what the surveillance state is doing. This is the news.

Then verse five tells you what is happening *underneath*.

*Wa nurīdu.* **And We are wanting.**

It is the same time. The same moment. The mothers are still hiding their sons. The blood is still on the riverbank. The visible world has not changed by a single atom. But Allah pulls back a curtain and shows us that, in the very same instant, a divine wanting is moving — and that wanting will, in time, *eat* the visible reality from underneath.

[PAUSE]

This is what the classical scholars call the *bāṭin* of history — the inner layer. The Quran is constantly insisting that history has two layers. There is the layer of cause and effect that you see. And there is the layer of divine intention that you do not see. The visible layer looks total. The unseen layer is what actually decides what is happening.

Pharaoh does not know about verse five. Pharaoh, in the moment he is signing the slaughter decree, is acting as if he is the only writer of history. He cannot see *nurīdu*. He cannot see the *mann* that is already being woven for the people he is crushing. He thinks his decree is the final word. He has no idea that *another* decree — quieter, slower, deeper — has already been issued.

And here is the staggering thing. The mothers of the slaughtered children also do not know about verse five. The grandmother who has buried three sons does not know. The father who hides his newborn in a basket in the reeds, his hands shaking, does not know that the basket he just released into the Nile is *the* basket that Allah's *nurīdu* is carrying.

The verse five reality is real *whether or not anyone inside the story can see it*.

Stay with me here. This is the door.

When you are inside the suffering, you cannot see verse five. By design. The whole testing of faith is that the divine wanting is *hidden* in the moment it is most active. You don't get to see it operating. You see the slaughter. You see the visible. You feel the closed sky.

And then, sometimes years later, sometimes generations later, sometimes never in this life, the verse five layer surfaces. And every event that looked like defeat is revealed to have been one of the threads being woven into a *mann*.

The Children of Israel did not know, while they were burying their sons, that Musa was being born. They did not know that the very Nile being used to drown them was going to carry the deliverer. They did not know that the daughter of Pharaoh — the daughter of the oppressor himself — was going to lift the basket out of the water. They did not know that Pharaoh's own household, his own palace, his own table, would feed and educate and shelter the prophet who would dismantle him.

None of that is visible from inside verse three. All of it is operating from verse five.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this is asking of us.

It is asking us to develop a kind of *peripheral vision* for divine action. Not to deny what is in front of us — Pharaoh was real, the slaughter was real, the suffering was real, and the Quran never minimizes any of it. But to hold, alongside that reality, the certainty that there is another layer running underneath it. To live with both eyes open. To grieve the visible and to trust the unseen.

This is not optimism. Optimism is a feeling that things will be okay. This is something different. This is *iman* in the *muḍāriʿ* tense of *nurīdu*. It is the conviction that Allah's wanting is *currently active* — right now, this minute, in the situation you cannot see a way out of — and that the visible layer is not the only layer.

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes what you do when you cannot see how a situation could resolve. When the news breaks something inside you. When you watch a people being crushed and there is no human power that can lift them. The verse does not tell you to look away. It tells you to look *deeper*. To know that *nurīdu* is operating in the same time as the suffering. That the basket is already being woven. That the daughter of Pharaoh is already walking toward the river.

You will not see it. You are not supposed to see it. But you can know that it is there.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Reversal — From Beneath to Above

There is a particular kind of move that Allah makes in the Quran, over and over, that almost no one notices until it is pointed out. Once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

The move is this: Allah takes the *exact category* that was used to crush a people, and He turns that category into the throne He seats them on.

Let me show you what I mean.

Pharaoh divided the people into factions. He took one group and *istaḍʿafa* them — he deemed them weak. He made them the bottom of his pyramid. He built his entire kingdom on the principle that *these* people are below. Below his army, below his priests, below his free citizens. The *istiḍʿāf* — the weakening — was the *category* of their existence in his empire.

Now read verse five again. **We are wanting to bestow** *mann* **upon those who were deemed weak, and to make them** *a'immah* **— leaders — and to make them the** *wārithīn* **— the inheritors.**

Look at the order. *A'immah* — leaders. Imams. The ones in front. The ones the others look to. The exact opposite position from *istiḍʿāf*. And then *wārithīn* — those who inherit. The ones who *end up* with what someone else *built*.

[PAUSE]

This is what I want you to feel. Allah does not merely lift these people. He lifts them *into the exact position their oppressor occupied*. They were below — He makes them leaders. They were dispossessed — He makes them inheritors. The category of their suffering becomes the dimension of their elevation. The very axis along which they were crushed becomes the axis along which they are raised.

And — this is the breathtaking part — they inherit *Pharaoh's land*. The same Egypt. The same fields. The same houses. The same wealth that was built on their backs is going to pass into their hands.

This is what *wārithīn* means in this context. The classical commentators are explicit. They inherit the land of Pharaoh. They inherit the kingdom of the very king who decreed their deaths. The pyramid does not get torn down — it gets *flipped*. The slaves become the holders of the keys. The ones whose sons were drowned become the ones whose descendants will pray on the same ground.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where this becomes a divine pattern, not a single episode.

The Quran tells us, in another verse — *wa naridu an namunna* echoes through the entire book — that Allah's pattern is to take the category the world rejects and make it the category He elevates. The Companions of the Prophet ﷺ were largely slaves, freedmen, orphans, the poor of Makkah. The aristocracy laughed at them. *Look at the people Muhammad has gathered around him* — Bilāl the Abyssinian slave, Suhayb the foreigner, Ammār whose mother was tortured to death. The Quraysh used these names as insults.

Within twenty years, Bilāl is calling the adhan on top of the Kaʿbah while the sons of the Quraysh stand below listening. Suhayb's descendants are governors. The category that was used to dismiss them became the throne they were seated upon.

This is *nurīdu* moving through another century.

Look across the Quran and you will see this pattern repeating. Yusuf — thrown into a well by his brothers, sold as a slave, imprisoned on a lie — becomes the second in command of Egypt. The mother of Musa — hiding her infant in terror — becomes the woman whose nursing milk Allah orchestrates to be the milk the prophet drinks. Maryam — accused, shamed, isolated — becomes the woman whose name is the title of a surah.

Every single one of these is verse five operating in a different century. The grammar changes. The names change. The principle does not.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing I want you to feel about *wārithīn* — **the inheritors**.

The word inheritance, in Arabic, has a specific texture. You do not inherit something you took. You do not inherit something you bought. You inherit something that *passes to you* because the one who held it before *can no longer hold it*. The previous owner is gone.

This is the quiet violence inside the word. When Allah says He will make the oppressed *the inheritors*, He is saying — without saying — that the oppressors will be *gone*. Their hands will be opened. They will have to release what they were clutching. The pyramid will pass to the people they crushed *because the ones who built it will no longer be there*.

And this happens — read the surah — exactly the way the word predicted. Pharaoh's hands open in the sea. The wealth he amassed becomes ownerless. The land becomes available to be inherited. *Wārithīn* describes the moment the empire's grip is broken.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

If you are someone who has been counted weak — by your family, by your culture, by the systems you grew up inside — verse five is asking you to refuse the verdict. The grammar of the verse already refused it on your behalf. *Ustuḍʿifū* — you *were treated as* weak. The weakness was a label assigned to you. It was never your nature.

And if you are someone who is comfortable, who is not being crushed, who is on the upper side of the pyramid — verse five is asking a different question of you. The verse warns you what *category of human being* Allah elevates. He does not elevate the comfortable. He does not elevate the dominant. The throne in His economy is reserved for those underneath. If you want to be near where Allah is *wanting*, the verse tells you where to go. Go down. Go to the deemed-weak. Go to the place where the *mann* is being prepared.

That is where verse five is operating. That is where Allah's wanting is currently being woven into history.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Step back and look at the structure of the verse one more time.

> *Wa nurīdu — an namunna ʿalā alladhīna ustuḍʿifū fī al-arḍ — wa najʿalahum a'immatan — wa najʿalahumu al-wārithīn.*

Look at the architecture.

- **One verb of divine intention**: *nurīdu*.
- **Three actions that flow from it**: *namunna* (bestow), *najʿalahum a'immah* (make them leaders), *najʿalahumu al-wārithīn* (make them inheritors).

The intention is one. The expressions of it are three.

And those three are a *sequence*. First the *mann* — the gift, the favor, the prior grace. Then the *imamah* — the leadership, the standing in front, the dignity. Then the *wirāthah* — the inheritance, the actual material transfer of the world.

You cannot skip stages. The leadership does not come without the *mann* coming first. The inheritance does not come without the leadership coming first. Allah is showing you the *order* of His lifting. It begins in unseen favor. It moves through visible dignity. It ends in material reality.

This is how *nurīdu* works in every age. The gift comes first, in secret. The dignity comes next, in struggle. The inheritance comes last, in time.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The *muḍāriʿ* of *nurīdu* tells us the wanting is permanently active — and the theme of the invisible counter-decree tells us we are supposed to trust it when we cannot see it.

The passive *ustuḍʿifū* tells us Allah refuses Pharaoh's verdict on the oppressed — and the theme of the reversal tells us those very oppressed become the inheritors of the system that crushed them.

The *mann* before the leadership tells us elevation begins in unearned grace — and the architecture of the verse tells us this is the sequence of every lifting Allah performs.

One insight, viewed from two windows.

3. If Allah elevates those who are counted weak — am I living near them, or far from them? Whose company would put me closer to where His *mann* is being prepared?

4. Am I treating someone in my life the way Pharaoh treated his people — *istaḍʿafa*, deeming them weak? Reading them by my own assignment rather than by what Allah has placed in them?

5. When I am suffering, am I able to hold the visible and the unseen at the same time — grieving what is real *and* trusting what is hidden — or do I collapse one into the other?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

While the visible world is closed by the powerful, Allah's wanting is permanently open — and the people the world counts as weak are the ones He has already decided to lift, by a gift they did not earn, into a leadership and inheritance their oppressors never imagined possible.

---

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not asking you to imagine a future. It is asking you to recognize a *posture* — Allah's permanent posture — and to align yourself with it.

If you carry pain that the world cannot see, this verse meets you in it. The *muḍāriʿ* of *nurīdu* is present tense. The wanting is happening right now. You do not have to manufacture hope. You have to remember a grammar.

If you have power, if you are comfortable, if you are anywhere near the upper side of any pyramid — this verse asks you to pay attention to the lower side. That is where Allah is currently active. That is where the *mann* is being prepared. Stand near it. Let it work on you.

And if you are anywhere in between — read this verse, every time history feels closed, as a quiet correction. The closed sky is not the sky. The visible decree is not the only decree. *Wa nurīdu*. **And We are wanting.** The verb has not finished.

---

### Du'a

O Allah, the One whose wanting refuses to close — let us trust You in the layer of history we cannot see, even as we grieve what we can. Place us near those You are lifting, so that we may be in the company of Your *mann* as it is being woven. And refuse, on our behalf, every verdict of weakness that any tongue has spoken over us — for You are the One who looks at the deemed-weak and writes them into the line of inheritors. Āmīn.
