# The Empty-Handed Prayer: Musa at the Water of Madyan

## Introduction

Before we read a single word, I want you to feel where this man is standing.

He is a wanted man. Days ago he was a prince — raised in the palace of the most powerful ruler on earth, eating from Pharaoh's table, wearing Pharaoh's linen. Then, in a single unplanned instant, he struck an Egyptian and the man died. And the whole architecture of his life came down. The morning after, he heard the whisper in the city: *they are conspiring to kill you.* So he ran. Not with provisions, not with a plan, not with a caravan. He ran on foot, out of the only civilization he had ever known, into open desert, with nothing.

The Qur'an tells us he left the city **خَآئِفًا يَتَرَقَّبُ** — frightened, watching over his shoulder. And as he walked away from Egypt, all he could say was, *"My Lord, save me from these wrongdoing people."*

Now picture the days that follow. A man on foot crossing the wilderness between Egypt and Madyan — that is a journey measured not in hours but in a week or more of walking. No shoes fit for it. Some of the narrations say his feet were torn, that by the time he reached Madyan he was eating the leaves of trees, that he was so depleted the greenness of the leaves could be seen through the thinness of his belly. Whether or not every detail is precise, hold the shape of it: **this is a man at the absolute floor of his life.** Yesterday, royalty. Today, a hunted foreigner collapsing at the edge of a strange town, with no name, no money, no home, and no idea what happens next.

And it is *this* man — this emptied-out, sunburned, grieving fugitive — that we are about to watch. Because what he does in the next sixty seconds of his life, and the eleven words he says to his Lord, will turn out to be the hinge on which his entire destiny swings.

Six verses. A well, two women, a stranger, a prayer, a father, a marriage.

Now here is what I want you to hunger for. Between the moment Musa arrives with *nothing* and the moment he walks away with a home, a wife, ten years of security, and — eventually, at that very region — the voice of God calling him to prophethood, there is one sentence. One prayer. And in that prayer, **he asks for nothing.** He names no need. He requests no item. He does not say *feed me* or *shelter me* or *give me work.*

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### The Two Women "Holding Back": تَذُودَانِ and مِن دُونِهِمُ

Let me first hand you a small tool from the Arabic language, and I'll do it with something you already know.

Imagine a crowded doorway after a concert — everyone pushing out at once, shoulder to shoulder, nobody yielding. Now imagine, off to the side, two people standing back against the wall, waiting for the crush to pass before they move. You didn't need any words to understand the difference between the crowd and the two who held back. You understood it from *position* and from *restraint.* Arabic can do that same thing in a single verb and a single preposition — it can tell you where someone is standing and what they are holding themselves back from, all at once.

Watch it happen.

The verse says the crowd was **يَسْقُونَ** — *yasqūn*, "watering," an open plural, everyone doing the thing freely, elbowing in. And then: **وَوَجَدَ مِن دُونِهِمُ ٱمْرَأَتَيْنِ تَذُودَانِ**  — "and he found, *min dūnihim*, two women, *tadhūdān.*"

Take **مِن دُونِهِمُ** first. <!-- morphology:28:23:18 root=دون pos=N --> The word *dūn* means apart-from, aside-from, at a remove. They were not in the crowd. They were held at the margin — near the water but not at it, present but separated. The preposition places them before the verse tells you anything about them. Position first. **They are the ones standing apart.**

Now the verb: **تَذُودَانِ**. <!-- morphology:28:23:21 root=ذود pos=V --> The root is *dhāl–wāw–dāl*, and *dhāda / yadhūdu* means to drive back, to fend off, to hold something away. Notice the sound of it — *tadhūdān* — it isn't smooth. It catches in the throat, that *dh* pressing against a *d*, a small effort in the mouth. And that is exactly the meaning: **it is effortful to hold back when everyone else is pushing forward.** The word is doing the work of the thing it describes. These two women are not passively waiting. They are actively fending off — keeping their flock back from the men's flocks, restraining their own animals from the water, spending energy on *not going forward.*

Let's sit with why that matters. When Musa asks them, **مَا خَطْبُكُمَا** — "what is your circumstance?" — they answer with total dignity and zero complaint: *"We do not water until the shepherds drive off — and our father is a very old man."*  Read the whole picture the language has painted: two women, keeping themselves apart from a crowd of men, doing hard work under difficult constraint, not asking anyone for help, explaining themselves in one clean sentence without a drop of self-pity. The verb *tadhūdān* and the phrase *min dūnihim* are not scene-decoration. They are a portrait of **restraint that costs something** — of people who could have pushed to the front and chose to hold back and wait their turn.

And here is the thread I want you to hold, because it runs straight through this entire passage: **everyone in this scene who receives is someone who first held back.** The women held back their flocks. In a moment, we will see Musa hold himself back from asking anyone for anything. Keep that in your hand.

One clear statement, then we move: the language of verse 23 is the language of dignified restraint — the discipline of not shoving to the front even when you are thirsty.

### The Prayer That Asks for Nothing: فَقِيرٌ and the Unspecified خَيْر

This is the heart. Everything in the passage leans toward this one sentence.

Let me first give you the tool, again from your own world. In English, there is a difference between two ways of asking. One person walks into a room and says, *"I need forty dollars for gas."* Specific. Bounded. If you give him forty dollars, the request is closed. The other person walks in, sets down his hands, and says only, *"I'm in a hard place — I'll take whatever help you think is right."* Notice what the second person just did. **He didn't limit the answer to the size of his own imagination.** The first man can only receive forty dollars. The second man can receive forty dollars, or a job, or a place to stay, or a friendship that changes his life. By *not naming* the need, he left the answer open.

Now watch Musa.

He waters the flocks — **فَسَقَىٰ لَهُمَا**  — *fa-saqā lahumā.* <!-- morphology:28:24:2 root=سقي pos=V --> One act of service. Done for two strangers. Done by a man who himself has not eaten. Then: **ثُمَّ تَوَلَّىٰٓ إِلَى ٱلظِّلِّ** — "then he turned away to the shade."  The verb *tawallā* <!-- morphology:28:24:6 root=ولي pos=V --> is a turning — a deliberate turning of the whole self *away* from the crowd and the well, *toward* the shade.

And I want you to feel that shade for a second, because the Qur'an rarely mentions a physical thing for no reason. **ٱلظِّلِّ** <!-- morphology:28:24:9 root=ظلل pos=N --> — *aẓ-ẓill*, the shade. In the furnace of that desert, the shade is the one place of mercy, of relief, of shelter from a sun that has been punishing him for days. He turns from the place of *people* to the place of *shelter* — and it is there, in that little pocket of mercy, that he turns to the source of all mercy. The physical shade he walks into is the doorway to the true shelter he is about to reach for. He doesn't collapse and beg the crowd. He withdraws to the shade, and there, alone, he speaks upward.

And what he says is this:

**رَبِّ إِنِّى لِمَآ أَنزَلْتَ إِلَىَّ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَقِيرٌ** 

*"My Lord — indeed I am, of whatever good You send down to me, in need."*

Look at the shape of it, because the word order is not accidental. In Arabic he could have simply said *"innī faqīr"* — "I am needy" — and stopped. Instead, everything he is needy *for* is placed **before** the word "needy." *Li-mā anzalta ilayya min khayrin* — "of whatever good You send down to me" — all of that is fronted, and then, at the very end, landing last, the single word: **فَقِيرٌ.**

Two things are happening, and both are extraordinary.

First, the object of his need is **مِنْ خَيْرٍ** — "of *good*," any good, unspecified. <!-- morphology:28:24:23 root=خير pos=N --> He does not say "for food." He does not say "for shelter." He does not say "for work." He uses the widest, most open word available and leaves it completely undefined. Remember our two ways of asking? Musa is the second man. **He refuses to shrink the answer to the size of his own hunger.** His stomach is screaming for bread — and he will not even say the word bread, because he knows that the One he is asking sees needs Musa himself cannot see. So he leaves the door open: *whatever good.* And notice something almost unbearable in its trust: he says **أَنزَلْتَ** — *anzalta*, "You send *down.*" <!-- morphology:28:24:18 root=نزل pos=V --> Good, to Musa, is something that *descends.* It comes from above. It is not something he seizes; it is something that is lowered to him. A starving fugitive still speaks of good as rain from the sky.

Second — and here is the resting note of the whole prayer — the last word out of his mouth is **فَقِيرٌ.** <!-- morphology:28:24:24 root=فقر pos=N --> The root is *fā–qāf–rā*, and the lexicographers connect *faqr* — poverty — to *faqār*, the vertebrae of the spine. The *faqīr*, in the deepest reach of the word, is *the one whose backbone is broken* — bent double under the weight of his need. Whether or not we lean on that image, the plain meaning is stark enough: he ends not with a demand but with a **confession of emptiness.** The final word he leaves ringing in the air is not "give me." It is "I am poor."

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you the absence — because what is *missing* here is the whole miracle.

**There is no request in this prayer.** Grammatically, this is where you expect an imperative: *give me, feed me, help me, send me.* It never comes. He describes his Lord (*Rabbi*), he describes God's giving (*whatever good You send down*), and he describes himself (*faqīr*). Subject, gift, self — and no verb of asking anywhere. He has performed the most powerful supplication in the passage by **declining to make a demand at all.** He simply stood in the shade, told his Lord the truth about his own emptiness, and left the rest to Him.

And now hold this against something the same surah will show us later, because the Qur'an is having a conversation with itself. Further into Al-Qasas, a man named Qārūn — buried in wealth — will be told to be grateful, and he will sneer: **إِنَّمَآ أُوتِيتُهُۥ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ عِندِىٓ** — *"I was only given this because of knowledge I have."* *I earned it. It's mine. I'm self-sufficient.* That is the exact opposite pole of the human heart. Qārūn, drowning in provision, says *"I need no one."* Musa, dying of hunger in the shade, says *"I am in need of whatever You send."* And the surah is going to swallow Qārūn and his treasure into the earth, and raise Musa to speak with God. **The one who confessed his poverty was the richer man.** That contrast is not my invention laid onto the text — it is the architecture of the surah itself.

One clear statement: the most complete prayer in this passage is the one that names no want, sets down empty hands, and simply tells God, *I am poor, and whatever good You choose to lower to me, I will receive.*

### Walking Upon Modesty: تَمْشِى عَلَى ٱسْتِحْيَآءٍ

Watch how fast the sky answers. The prayer ends in verse 24. Verse 25 opens with **فَ** — *fa*, "and then, immediately" — and the answer walks toward him on two feet.

**فَجَآءَتْهُ إِحْدَىٰهُمَا تَمْشِى عَلَى ٱسْتِحْيَآءٍ** 

*"Then one of the two came to him, walking upon shyness."*

Let me give you the tool. In Arabic, prepositions are load-bearing — the tiny word can change everything. The verse does not say she came *with* shyness (*bi-*), as if shyness were a thing she carried in her hand. It says **عَلَى ٱسْتِحْيَآءٍ** — *ʿalā*, "*upon.*" You walk *upon* the ground. You stand *upon* a foundation. To walk *upon* shyness is to make modesty **the very surface you move across** — the ground under your steps, not an accessory in your bag. Her *ḥayāʾ* is not something she is wearing. It is the earth she is walking on.

And the word itself: **ٱسْتِحْيَآءٍ.** <!-- morphology:28:25:8 root=حيي pos=N --> The root is *ḥāʾ–yāʾ–yāʾ* — the same root as *ḥayāh*, **life** itself. Modesty and *living* spring from one source in Arabic; the ancients understood *ḥayāʾ* as the sign of a heart that is still spiritually alive. And this is form ten — *istifʿāl*, the pattern of *seeking, drawing something up from within.* Her modesty is not a timid blank; it is an active, living dignity that she is drawing up out of herself as she crosses that open ground toward a strange man.

Now feel the tension the language is holding, because this is the point most people miss. **She still came.** She still walked across the open. She still spoke — clearly, with a purpose: *"My father invites you, to reward you for having watered for us."*  This is not paralysis. This is not a woman too shy to function. **This is strength and modesty in the same body at the same time** — she does the necessary, difficult, forward thing, and she does it *upon* the ground of dignity. The Qur'an is refusing the lie that modesty and capability are enemies. She is capable *and* modest. She fended off a flock in verse 23; she carries a message in verse 25. And she does all of it *ʿalā istiḥyāʾ.*

And do you hear the echo of the whole passage in her? Musa held back from asking. The women held back their flocks. And now the answer to his prayer arrives in the form of a person who is herself **the walking image of dignified restraint** — someone who moves toward what must be done without ever pushing, without ever losing her poise. The prayer of the man who wouldn't shove to the front is answered by the woman who walks upon modesty. Like calls to like.

One clear statement: *ʿalā istiḥyāʾ* tells us that true modesty is not a fence that stops you from acting — it is the ground you stand on *while* you act.

[PAUSE]

**Bridging out of the words:** three pieces of language — two women *holding back*, a fugitive who *asks for nothing*, a woman *walking upon modesty* — and every one of them is a study in the same quiet virtue: restraint that does not shrink, dignity that does not demand. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about six verses of Arabic, but about the way provision actually reaches a human life.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Anxiety of the Broadcast, and the Rest of the Empty Hand

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a need — a real one. Rent is tight this month. Or the job is drying up. Or you're single and aching about it. Or someone you love is sick. And notice what your generation has been *trained* to do with a need: **broadcast it.** Post the vague-but-visible caption. Network the room. Send the "just checking in!" text to the contact who might have a lead. Optimize the profile. Run the numbers again at 2 a.m. There is an entire economy built on the premise that if you are not *constantly signaling and specifying and pursuing* your need to every possible human channel, it will not be met. And underneath all that motion is a very particular kind of exhaustion — the exhaustion of a person who believes that the meeting of their needs depends *entirely on how well they manage other people's perception of them.*

There's a version of this even inside our spiritual lives now — the "manifest it, name it precisely, ask the universe for the exact car and the exact salary" instinct. Specify your desire, we're told, down to the make and model, or you won't get it.

And here comes a starving man in the shade who does the exact opposite of all of it.

He served *before* he asked. He withdrew from the crowd *instead of* working it. He addressed *One* audience, not a hundred. And when he finally spoke his need, he **refused to specify it** — he handed it over open-ended: *whatever good You send.* Everything the anxious, broadcasting heart is sure will leave you empty, Musa did — and the sky opened over him within a single verse.

Let this land somewhere specific. Picture yourself at the end of a genuinely brutal day — the kind where you've been "on" for everyone, performing your competence, managing your image, and you finally close the door of your room and the mask comes off and you're just *tired.* That collapse, that moment when there's no one left to perform for — the Qur'an is telling you that *this* is not the end of your resources. **It is the doorway to the shade.** That exact posture — spent, un-performing, honest about your emptiness — is the posture from which Musa prayed the most answered prayer of his life. You do not have to arrive before God polished and specific. You are allowed to come to Him the way you actually are: *Rabbi, innī faqīr* — my Lord, I'm poor, I've got nothing, and I'll take whatever good You know I need better than I do.

There is a rest in that you cannot manufacture by broadcasting harder. Because the broadcast says *my provision depends on me reaching enough people.* And the empty hand in the shade says *my provision depends on the One who was already watching me water two strangers' flocks when I didn't know anyone was looking.*

So here is the thing to actually change tomorrow. The next time you feel the pull to broadcast a need — to send the anxious text, to run the 2 a.m. numbers again, to specify to the universe exactly what you demand — try, once, doing what Musa did in order. **Serve someone quietly first.** Then withdraw somewhere no one is watching. And say the open-handed prayer — not the itemized invoice, but the surrender: *whatever good You send down, I'm in need of it.* And then get up, and go do your work, and watch which door opens. It may not be the door you would have specified. It will, very often, be a better one.

### Theme Two: The Door Out Is Disguised as a Small Kindness

Now let me put a different scene in front of you.

You are depleted. Genuinely running on empty — the kind of low where you feel you have nothing left to give anyone. And in that state, you pass someone who could use a hand. Not a grand rescue — a small thing. Helping someone lift a bag. Staying ten extra minutes for a coworker who's drowning. Watering someone's flock, so to speak, when your own belly is empty. And every calculating instinct says: *not now. You have nothing to spare. Conserve. It's not your problem. You're the one who needs help here.*

Here is what Musa did not know when he stepped up to that well.

He did not know that watering two flocks would get him invited to a home. He did not know the invitation would bring him to a righteous old man — the Qur'an's Shu'ayb, in the traditional reading — who would say to him the exact words his terrified heart needed most: **لَا تَخَفْ ۖ نَجَوْتَ مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ** — *"Do not fear. You have escaped the wrongdoing people."*  Do you understand what that sentence *meant* to a man who had spent a week walking and glancing over his shoulder, certain Pharaoh's men were behind him? **Safety.** The one thing money can't buy for a fugitive. He didn't know the small kindness would lead there. He didn't know it would lead to being *seen* accurately — to a woman describing him as **ٱلْقَوِىُّ ٱلْأَمِينُ**, "the strong, the trustworthy."  <!-- morphology:28:26:15 root=قوي pos=N -->  After days of being no one, of being hunted human refuse, someone looked at him and named his worth in two words. He didn't know it would lead to marriage, to a home, to ten years of stable work, to a family. And he certainly did not know that this obscure region on the far side of nowhere was the very ground where, years later, God Himself would call to him from the burning tree and make him a prophet.

**All of it — every single piece of it — came through the door of watering two strangers' flocks when he had nothing to spare.**

I don't want to leave that floating in the abstract, so let me bring it down to your Tuesday. The favor you're being asked for when you're at your most depleted — the one your calculating mind says you can't afford — you are evaluating it by its *immediate cost* because you can only see the immediate. You cannot see the invitation on the other side of it. You cannot see the person who is watching how you treat someone with nothing to offer you back. **The Qur'an is showing you that the exit from your own crisis is very often disguised as a small service to someone else's.** Not because service is a transaction — Musa did not water those flocks *in order to* get a wife. That's the whole point. He did it as a decent man does a decent thing, expecting nothing, *"lahumā"* — for *them.* And precisely because it was clean, unbargained, done for the sake of two strangers and their God, it became the hinge.

Notice, too, how the provision actually arrived. Not as manna from heaven. Not as a bag of gold appearing in the sand. It came through **a young woman with the courage to speak, an old man with a generous heart, and an ordinary employment contract.** Allah answered *"whatever good You send down"* — and what descended looked, on the surface, entirely ordinary: a job, a marriage, a roof. This is *how rizq moves.* We wait for the miracle and miss the invitation. We're scanning the sky for angels and the answer walks up to us on two feet, *upon shyness,* and says *my father would like to see you.* The good that "comes down" almost always comes down *through people and through the plainest of doors.*

So, concretely, tomorrow: when you are running on empty and someone small needs something small from you — do the thing. Water the flock. Not as a strategy, not as an investment with an expected return, but as a decent person who trusts that the God who sees the hidden act is arranging doors you cannot see from the shade. And when good does come to you through a plain, unremarkable channel — a job, an introduction, a neighbor's kindness — do not fail to recognize it as **exactly the "khayr" you asked to be sent down.** Don't demand it arrive as lightning. Learn to see the answer in the ordinary.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Now that we've done the climbing, let me show you the shape of the mountain, because the design of these six verses is almost too perfect to be accidental.

Stand back and look at the whole passage, and you'll see it folds around a single center.

Before the prayer, Musa is entirely a **giver who asks for nothing.** He arrives, he sees a need, he waters the flocks — **فَسَقَىٰ لَهُمَا** — and he asks not one thing of anyone. He turns away to the shade.

At the exact center sits **the prayer** — *"My Lord, I am, of whatever good You send down, in need."* The hinge. The one moment he turns from all people to God alone, and even then, he demands nothing. He only confesses his poverty.

And after the prayer, Musa becomes entirely a **receiver who need not ask.** Watch how the giving reverses and floods back. He watered — **سَقَيْتَ** — and *she comes to reward him for having watered.* The very word bends back on him: *saqā* becomes *saqayta.* He fended for two strangers, and now a household opens to shelter *him.* He gave water; he receives safety, dignity, a wife, a home, ten years of provision. **Before the hinge, he pours out and asks nothing. After the hinge, everything is poured onto him and he still asks nothing** — for even in the marriage contract he is the generous one: *"if you complete ten years, that is from you… I do not wish to make it hard for you."*

The design is the proof of the theme. The empty hand that serves and then turns to God in the shade — *that* is the hand that gets filled to overflowing. He asked for an unspecified *"khayr,"* one open word — and God answered with safety, and honor, and marriage, and home, and work, and finally, in that very land, prophethood. **The unspecified request received the unimaginable answer.** Had he asked for bread, he might have received only bread. He asked for *"whatever good,"* and received a life.

### Where the Two Journeys Meet

The words and the themes were the same truth approached from two sides.

The linguistic journey showed us three portraits of **restraint that does not shrink** — women holding back their flocks, a man asking for nothing, a woman walking upon modesty. The thematic journey showed us that this exact restraint — refusing to broadcast, refusing to grasp, serving before asking — is the posture that provision answers. The grammar and the life are one lesson: **the hand you hold open and empty before God is the hand He fills. The hand that grasps and demands and broadcasts stays clenched and stays empty.**

We began with a broken man in the dirt at the edge of a strange town, watching over his shoulder. We end with him settled, married, safe, and standing at the threshold of the greatest calling a human being has ever received. And the whole turn happened in the shade, in eleven words, with empty hands.

### Questions to Carry

- When you are in real need, where do you instinctively turn *first* — to the crowd at the well, or to the shade? And what would change if you reversed the order?
- Is there a need you have been *specifying* to God — demanding it arrive in one exact shape — that He might answer far more generously if you handed it over as *"whatever good You send down"*?
- When was the last time good came to you through a completely ordinary door — a person, a job, a plain kindness — and you failed to recognize it as an answered prayer?
- Musa served two strangers when he himself had nothing. Who is the flock at your well right now — the small service you're avoiding because you feel too empty to give?
- If someone watched you the way that household watched Musa, would they name you *al-qawiyy al-amīn* — capable *and* trustworthy? Which of the two are you neglecting?

### The One Sentence to Carry

**Set down your empty hands in the shade, ask God not for the thing you can name but for the good only He can see, and then go water the nearest flock — for that is the exact posture that Heaven answers.**

### The Invitation

You do not have to arrive polished. You do not have to broadcast louder, specify harder, or perform your competence one more exhausting time. Somewhere today there is a shade waiting for you — a quiet corner, a closed door, a moment no one is watching — and you are invited to walk into it the way Musa did: emptied out, honest, and unafraid to say *I am poor before You.* Serve quietly. Ask openly. And then keep your eyes open for the ordinary door that opens, because the good is already on its way down.

### A Closing Du'a

O Allah, You are the One who watered the fugitive from the sky of Your mercy and turned his poverty into prophethood. We come to You the way Musa came — with empty hands and honest hearts. We do not tell You what to send; we only confess that of whatever good You send down, we are in desperate need. Make us of those who serve before we ask, who hold back from the crowd without shrinking, and who recognize Your answer even when it walks up to us in the plainest of clothes. And when we are afraid and watching over our shoulders, say to our hearts what You said to his: *do not fear — you have escaped.* Āmīn.
