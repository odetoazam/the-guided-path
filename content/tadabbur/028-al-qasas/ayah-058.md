# The Cities That Forgot They Were Guests

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 58

### Arabic

> [ayah:28:58] وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا مِن قَرْيَةٍۭ بَطِرَتْ مَعِيشَتَهَا ۖ فَتِلْكَ مَسَـٰكِنُهُمْ لَمْ تُسْكَن مِّنۢ بَعْدِهِمْ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا ۖ وَكُنَّا نَحْنُ ٱلْوَٰرِثِينَ

### Translation

> "And how many a city did We destroy that had become reckless in its way of life. Those are their dwellings — never inhabited after them except a little. And it is We who are the inheritors."

---

## Introduction: The Argument Before This Ayah

Before we open this verse, we need to hear the argument it answers.

One verse earlier — in 28:57 — the Quraysh of Mecca had said something to the Prophet ﷺ that, if you listen carefully, sounds painfully modern:

*"If we follow this guidance with you, we will be snatched from our land."*

Translate that into the language we actually speak: *If we change, we lose everything. Our position. Our trade. Our standing in the world. Our children's future. The only thing keeping us safe is the system we have. Don't ask us to risk it.*

It is the oldest objection there is. Not "I don't believe you." Something quieter and more honest: *"I'm afraid of what believing you would cost me."*

The Quraysh weren't stupid. They could see Mecca was prospering. Caravans came. Pilgrims came. The sanctuary held. Why would anyone risk an arrangement that was clearly working?

So Allah answers them. But notice how He answers. He does not argue with their economics. He does not promise them better trade. He does not even reassure them.

He takes them on a walk.

[PAUSE]

If you have ever driven through a desert and seen, off the highway, the outline of something that used to be a city — broken walls, a half-standing arch, weeds growing up through what was once a marketplace — you know what this ayah is doing. The Quraysh were on the trade route. They had passed these ruins their whole lives. The remains of ʿĀd. The remains of Thamūd. The houses of Madyan. Cities that had once said exactly what the Quraysh were now saying — *we have everything we need, don't disturb it* — and were now wind and stone.

The ayah doesn't argue. It points.

And what it asks us to notice in those ruins is not just *that* they fell. It asks us to notice **why**. And — this is the part that will undo us if we let it — it uses a single Arabic word to name the reason. A word so precise that once you understand it, you will recognize the disease in yourself before the ayah is over.

That word is **batirat**.

Let's earn it.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Kam Ahlaknā* — The Grammar of the Uncountable

Let me start with the structure of the opening, because the Quran is doing something with grammar here that English cannot easily carry.

The ayah opens: **wa kam ahlaknā** — *and how many did We destroy.*

In Arabic, **kam** is what grammarians call *kam al-khabariyyah* in this construction — it means "how many" but not as a question. It is an exclamation. It is the "how many" of someone standing in a graveyard and gesturing at the headstones. It is not asking for a number. It is overwhelmed by the number.

Think about the difference in English between "How many cookies are in the jar?" and "*How* many times have I told you?" The second one isn't a question. It's a sigh. It's the speaker giving up on counting because the count has become the point.

That is **kam** here.

Notice something — Allah does not give us a list. He does not name the cities. He does not say "ʿĀd and Thamūd and Madyan and the people of Lūṭ." He just says **kam** — and lets the word do the weight of all of them at once.

Why?

Let's sit with this. If He had given a list, the Quraysh could have said *those are old stories.* They could have categorized them, distanced them, made them archaeology. But **kam** does not let you do that. **Kam** says: *there are more than you can list, and you are looking at one of them right now if you only knew.*

Then comes **ahlaknā** — *We destroyed.* Past tense. Plural of majesty. First person. Allah is not narrating from a distance. He is saying *I did this.* The agent of the destruction is named with full force in the very first clause.

Here's what is remarkable: in the rest of the ayah, the cities will appear in the third person — *their dwellings, after them.* They are objects of the verb. But Allah Himself is in the first person — *We destroyed, We are the inheritors.* The grammar is the lesson. The cities are spoken *about*. Allah is the one speaking. The dead do not narrate. They are narrated.

[PAUSE]

What this single opening reveals: history is not a sequence of accidents. It is a series of decisions made by a Speaker who is still speaking. The ruins on the side of the road were not killed by economics or by climate or by competing tribes. They were killed by a verb whose subject is Allah.

### Element Two: *Baṭirat Maʿīshatahā* — The Word That Names the Disease

Now we come to the heart of the ayah. The word is **baṭirat**.

This is a word so specific that classical Arabic uses it for a particular kind of animal behavior. Let me teach you the concept before we apply it.

The root **b-ṭ-r** in the lexicons — and the classical Arabic dictionaries are careful about this — describes what happens to a beast of burden, a camel or a horse, when it has been fed too well and worked too little. The animal becomes skittish. It bucks. It refuses the saddle. It does not just become disobedient — it becomes disobedient *because* it has been treated well. The very kindness that fed it has unmade its capacity to serve.

The lexicographers, ar-Rāghib among them, note that **baṭar** is not arrogance in general. It is a *specific* kind of arrogance: **the arrogance that comes from comfort.** It is the giddiness of someone whose needs have been so consistently met that they have forgotten that anyone is meeting them.

You cannot be **baṭir** when you are hungry. You cannot be **baṭir** when you are scared. **Baṭar** is a disease of safety. It is what happens to the well-fed soul that has stopped remembering it is being fed.

Stay with me.

Now look at what Allah pairs **baṭirat** with. He pairs it with **maʿīshatahā** — *her way of life.* Her livelihood. The very thing that was sustaining her.

And here is where Arabic grammar gives us a gift. In Arabic, when a verb takes a direct object without a preposition, it can carry the meaning of *acting against* or *acting with respect to* that object. The phrase **baṭirat maʿīshatahā** does not just mean "she was reckless *in* her livelihood." Several of the classical mufassirūn read it as: *she was reckless **with** her livelihood. She was ungrateful **for** her means of living. She bucked against the very thing that was carrying her.*

Notice what is being named.

It is not that the city was wicked in some general sense. It is not that the city was idolatrous, or oppressive, or cruel — though those things were also true. The Quran names a more specific charge. **The city's relationship to its own sustenance had gone wrong.** The thing that fed her became the thing she despised. The blessing became the offense.

[PAUSE]

Let me show you why this word is so devastating. Imagine someone who has been homeless for years, and finally gets a small apartment. For the first month, they wake up and look at the walls and weep with gratitude. For the first year, they keep it clean as if it were a shrine. By year five, they complain that the kitchen is too small. By year ten, they tell their friends the apartment is beneath them.

That trajectory — gratitude → entitlement → contempt — *toward the very thing that saved you* — is **baṭar**.

The Quraysh in 28:57 had just said: *"if we follow this guidance, we will lose our position."* They had named their **maʿīshah** — their way of life, their trade, their security — as the thing they could not risk. They had centered it.

Allah's response in 28:58 is, in a single word: *that centering is the disease.* Cities have done exactly this before. They held their **maʿīshah** so tightly that they began to **buck against the One who gave it.** And the One who gave it took it back.

There is something almost merciful in how precisely Allah diagnoses this. He is not condemning the Quraysh for *having* a way of life. He is not condemning them for wealth or trade or comfort. He is condemning a single specific posture toward those things: **the posture of bucking against the hand that is feeding you.**

If we are honest, most of us know exactly what this posture feels like. It is the moment we complain about a job we begged for. It is the moment we resent a spouse we prayed to marry. It is the moment we are bored by health that, six months ago, we would have traded everything to have.

**Baṭirat maʿīshatahā** is the spiritual condition of being so well-fed that you have grown to resent the meal.

### Element Three: *Lam Tuskan min Baʿdihim illā Qalīlā* — The Architecture of an Absence

Now look at what Allah says about what is left of these cities.

**fa-tilka masākinuhum lam tuskan min baʿdihim illā qalīlā** — *Those are their dwellings — never inhabited after them except a little.*

Three things to notice. Each one is a key.

First — the demonstrative pronoun **tilka.** In Arabic, **tilka** is the *far* demonstrative — "those, over there." Not **hādhihi** — "these, right here." Allah is pointing into the distance. He is showing the Quraysh ruins they can *see* from where they stand. This is not theory. The ayah works because it can be verified by anyone willing to look out the window of their caravan.

Second — the wordplay. **Masākinuhum** (their dwellings) and **tuskan** (be inhabited) and the broader root **s-k-n** — this is the root for *dwelling*, yes, but also for *stillness*, for *rest*, for *that which settles the soul.* It is the root behind **sakīnah** — the divine tranquility that descends on the heart of the believer. And it is the root behind **maskan** — the house that holds you.

Allah is saying: their *places of rest* — *did not rest* anyone after them. The houses no longer house. The things that were meant to give stillness now sit in the wind. The root has been emptied of its meaning.

Third — and this is the most important one — listen carefully to the construction: **lam tuskan min baʿdihim illā qalīlā.** *They were not inhabited after them except a little.*

The verb is in the passive — **tuskan**. *They were not inhabited.* But notice what is missing. **By whom?**

In Arabic, you would normally expect an agent here — *they were not inhabited by anyone except a few travelers,* or *by anyone except a few passing tribes.* But Allah leaves the agent unspecified. The houses simply *go uninhabited.* The land becomes inhospitable in itself. It is as if the land has lost the capacity to be lived in.

And then — **illā qalīlā.** *Except a little.*

Here we read absence as carefully as presence. Allah did not say *not at all*. He said *except a little.* Why?

Some of the classical mufassirūn read **qalīlā** as referring to passing travelers — a caravan stops for the night, a shepherd sleeps in the shade of a broken wall, someone digs a temporary well. Others read it as a brief period of time — a generation or two before the ruin spreads completely. Take both as camera angles on the same reality.

The picture that emerges is haunting: the land is not entirely empty. *People still pass through.* But no one *lives* there. No children grow up there. No marriages happen there. No new houses are built. It is a place where the *substance* of life has been withdrawn, leaving only its surface.

This is what divine destruction actually looks like in the Quran. It is rarely a sudden erasure. More often, it is a slow draining — a place that used to *mean* something to people, and now only *exists* for them. A city that becomes a shortcut on someone else's journey.

[PAUSE]

What this construction reveals — and you must let this land — is that the punishment is not just the destruction. It is the *afterlife of the destruction.* The fact that the city becomes a thing other people pass through on their way somewhere else. The fact that its name becomes a warning in someone else's mouth. The fact that what was once "home" becomes "a place where something happened once."

The ruin is not only that the city died. The ruin is that the city became a *lesson*.

### Bridge

So far the linguistic construction has named three things: the **kam** that refuses to be counted, the **baṭar** that is the disease of comfort, and the **masākin** that no longer house anyone.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About how civilizations actually die. And about what is being held over our own heads as we read.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Disease of Being Well

Begin where you already live.

There is a strange phenomenon that researchers in positive psychology have a name for. They call it *hedonic adaptation.* It works like this: when something good happens to you — a raise, a new house, a relationship — your happiness spikes. Then, over a remarkably short time, your baseline resets. Within months, the new condition becomes the floor, not the ceiling. The thing that thrilled you now bores you. You need a new thrill to feel anything at all.

Researchers describe it as morally neutral — a quirk of the brain. The Quran does not. The Quran calls it **baṭar.** And the Quran does not consider it neutral at all.

Because what hedonic adaptation describes at the level of an individual nervous system, **baṭar** describes at the level of an entire civilization's soul. It is the same disease, scaled up. A society receives blessings — security, prosperity, peace — and at first remembers Who gave them. Then, as the blessings become familiar, the remembering fades. Then, as the remembering fades, the blessings themselves become the new floor of expectation. Then, finally, the society begins to *resent* anything that might disturb the new floor.

The Quraysh's argument in 28:57 — *if we follow guidance, we will lose our security* — is the voice of a civilization at the final stage of this disease. The blessing of the sanctuary, which their grandfather Ibrāhīm had built and which had been given to them as a trust, has now become a thing they are willing to refuse guidance in order to protect.

The blessing has become the obstacle.

Stay with that. The blessing has become the obstacle.

This is what makes **baṭar** so much more dangerous than ordinary sin. Ordinary sin you can see. Ordinary sin announces itself with guilt. But **baṭar** feels like *gratitude inverted into entitlement so smoothly that the person inside it cannot detect the shift.* The well-fed soul does not know it is well-fed. The well-fed soul knows only that things should keep being this way, and feels mildly offended whenever they don't.

Let's bring this into your own life for a moment.

Think about something in your life right now that you take for granted. Not a small thing. Something large. Health. A spouse. A child. Citizenship. A bed. Functioning eyes. Pick one.

Now ask: when was the last time you thanked Allah for it *with the same intensity* with which you would beg Him for it if it were taken from you tomorrow?

[PAUSE]

If the answer is *never*, or *I don't remember*, or *not for a long time* — you have just located **baṭar** in yourself.

This is what the ayah is for. Not to make us feel guilty. To make us feel *located.* To make us understand that the disease that hollowed out ʿĀd and Thamūd is the same disease we live with as our daily emotional climate.

The Prophet ﷺ used to say, in his morning du'a: *"O Allah, I have woken up, and the blessing of You is upon me, and from You. There is no god but You. So to You is all praise and all gratitude."*

Why every morning? Because **baṭar** resets every night. The disease grows in the dark. Each new day, the well-fed soul has to be deliberately reminded that it is being fed.

The practical application is simple and severe. **Find one blessing that has gone invisible. Make it visible again.** Today. By naming it. By thanking Him out loud for it. By imagining its absence vividly enough that its presence becomes shocking again.

Because if you do not interrupt the slide into **baṭar**, the slide will not interrupt itself. That is the whole grim lesson of the ruins.

### Theme Two: *Wa Kunnā Naḥnu al-Wārithīn* — The Closing Sentence That Reframes Everything

Now we come to the closing sentence of the ayah, and I want you to read it as if you have never read it before. Because everything in the ayah has been preparing you for it.

**wa kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn** — *and it is We who are the inheritors.*

In English, "inherit" is mostly a legal word. We use it when someone dies. The will is read. The property changes hands. But in Arabic, **w-r-th** — to inherit — is a much wider word. It carries the meaning of *what remains after everything else passes.* The thing that outlasts the other things. The one who is still standing in the room when the room is empty.

There is a fairly common modern reading of this phrase that goes something like: *Allah will inherit the earth — meaning, after everyone dies, the earth will be His.*

That reading is not wrong. But it is small. Listen for what is bigger.

**Wa kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn** is in the past tense — **kunnā**. *We *were* the inheritors.* Not *We will be.* **We were.**

This is the staggering claim. **Allah is naming Himself the inheritor of cities that have not yet fallen.**

Pause on this. The grammar is doing something almost vertiginous. The cities that **baṭirat maʿīshatahā** — those that became reckless with their livelihood — those cities thought they *owned* their wealth. They thought they *owned* their security. They thought they *owned* their sanctuary. And Allah is telling us, in past-tense Arabic, *while they thought they owned it, We already were its inheritors.*

The transfer was not at the moment of destruction. The transfer was true the entire time.

[PAUSE]

What does this mean?

It means that **everything you think you own is already in escrow.** Your house. Your savings. Your body. Your children. Your reputation. Your country. Your community. They are, right now, in your hands, but the title deed has never been yours. You are the *current resident.* He is the inheritor — already, today, before anything visible has changed.

The Quraysh's terrible mistake — and ours — is to think of ownership as the default and loss as the disruption. **The ayah inverts this.** Loss is the default. Ownership is the loan. Every moment your hand is still on the thing is a moment of mercy that He has not yet recalled.

This is why the disease of **baṭar** is so deep. **Baṭar** is the lie of permanence dressed up as gratitude's opposite. It is the soul saying *this is mine, this will continue, this is owed to me.* And Allah is saying *no — even right now, even as you hold it, **I am the One who outlasts what you hold.***

The mufassirūn note something beautiful here. The same root, **w-r-th**, is used in Sūrat Maryam when Zakariyyā prays for a child: *"give me from Yourself a walī — an heir — who will inherit me and inherit from the family of Yaʿqūb"* (19:5-6). And Allah responds: *we are the inheritors.* In other words: *your inheritance, your lineage, your continuation — that is My business, not yours. I am the One who decides what survives you.*

So the closing of 28:58 is not a threat in the way we first hear it. It is a *re-frame.*

The Quraysh said: *if we follow guidance, we will lose our security.*

Allah said: *your security is already inherited. You have only ever been holding it on loan. The question is not whether you will lose it. The question is whether, when it is taken — and it will be taken — you will be remembered as one who held it with gratitude or as one who bucked against the hand that gave it.*

That is a completely different question than the one the Quraysh thought they were asking.

And it is the question this ayah, fourteen hundred years later, is asking you and me. Right now.

The practical application of this theme is harder than the first one. It is not just *be grateful.* It is something more structural. It is: **live as a tenant, not an owner.** Hold what you have been given loosely enough that, when it is recalled, you do not feel robbed — you feel returned. Because nothing was ever yours. **Wa kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn.**

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now look back at the ayah as a single piece of architecture.

It opens with **kam ahlaknā** — *how many We destroyed.* It moves through **baṭirat maʿīshatahā** — *they were reckless with their way of life.* It passes through **tilka masākinuhum** — *those are their dwellings.* It ends with **kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn** — *We are the inheritors.*

Notice the symmetry. The ayah opens and closes with **Allah in the first person.** At the beginning, He is the *destroyer.* At the end, He is the *inheritor.* And in between — sandwiched between His two acts — is the brief story of a civilization that thought it owned what it was holding.

The cities, in the middle, are in the third person. They are *spoken about.* They are surrounded — front and back — by the voice of the One who acts on them.

That is the entire theology of history in twenty-eight words. The middle of history is full of cities that think they are the subject. The opening and closing of history reveal who the subject has actually been the whole time.

---

### Bring It Together

The two themes are one insight from two directions.

**Baṭar** — the disease of being well — is what happens when a soul forgets it is a tenant. It is the inward face of the same lie.

**Al-wārithīn** — the inheritor — is the outward fact that exposes the lie. It is the truth that **baṭar** is trying not to see.

The cure for **baṭar** is therefore not just gratitude. The cure is the structural recognition that everything you have is *currently His*, not *not yet His.* The moment that flips inside you, **baṭar** has nowhere to grow. You cannot buck against a hand you remember is feeding you right now.

### Questions to Carry

1. What blessing in your life has gone invisible? Not "what should I be grateful for" — but specifically: *what is so reliably present that you no longer see it?*

2. In your relationship with what you have — your work, your home, your family, your body — where is the posture of a tenant, and where is the posture of an owner? Be specific.

3. The Quraysh said *if we follow this guidance, we will lose our security.* What is the version of that sentence in your own life? What guidance are you holding back from because the **maʿīshah** it would cost you feels too dear?

4. If you were to walk past your own life as a stranger walks past ruins — would you see a city that thanked the One who built it, or one that bucked against Him?

5. When Allah says **kunnā naḥnu al-wārithīn** — *We **were** the inheritors* — what changes in your relationship to the next thing you reach for today?

---

### The One-Sentence Distillation

The cities you drive past did not fall because they were poor — they fell because they were rich and forgot they were guests, and the One they forgot was the One who had been the owner the whole time.

---

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to give anything up.

It is asking you to *see what you are already holding* — to see it correctly. To see that the house was always His. The health was always His. The breath in your chest at this exact moment is His. And that this is not bad news. This is the news that makes the difference between the soul that loses everything in panic and the soul that returns everything in peace.

The Quraysh were terrified of losing what they thought was theirs. You and I have been given the gift of knowing, in advance, that *what we think is ours is on loan.* That knowledge, if we let it settle into us, is the thing that will protect us from becoming one of the ruins.

Drive past the ruins this week. Wherever you find them. The empty office of a colleague who was sure of his position. The marriage of someone who took it for granted. The photograph of a grandparent whose house is now someone else's. Let those small ruins teach you what the Quran is teaching you about the larger ones.

And then, gently, with no panic — *thank the One who has not yet asked for any of it back.*

### Du'a

> O Allah, You are the Inheritor of what we are still holding. Let us hold what You have given us with the gratitude of a guest, never the entitlement of an owner. Save us from the disease of being well — from the slow inversion of Your blessing into our offense. And when You ask back what was always Yours, find our hands open and our hearts at rest.
