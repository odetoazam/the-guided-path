# The Mother of Cities: Why Allah Never Destroys Without Sending

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 59

### The Arabic

> وَمَا كَانَ رَبُّكَ مُهْلِكَ ٱلْقُرَىٰ حَتَّىٰ يَبْعَثَ فِىٓ أُمِّهَا رَسُولًۭا يَتْلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ ءَايَـٰتِنَا ۚ وَمَا كُنَّا مُهْلِكِى ٱلْقُرَىٰٓ إِلَّا وَأَهْلُهَا ظَـٰلِمُونَ

### Transliteration

> Wa mā kāna Rabbuka muhlika al-qurā ḥattā yabʿatha fī ummihā rasūlan yatlū ʿalayhim āyātinā — wa mā kunnā muhlikī al-qurā illā wa ahluhā ẓālimūn.

### Translation

> Your Lord would never destroy the towns until He had sent into their mother-city a messenger reciting Our signs to them. Nor would We ever destroy the towns unless their people were wrongdoers.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World This Ayah Walked Into

To hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, you have to walk back into Makkah in the late Makkan period.

The Prophet ﷺ is being told, in the verses just before this one, that those who reject the message have grown comfortable. They have luxury. They have safety. They have a sanctuary that feeds them with the fruits of every land. And they are, despite all of that, refusing the man who walks among them.

The atmosphere of the surah up to this point has been the slow, deliberate unfolding of one long story — Musa. An infant placed in a basket. A killing in the marketplace. A flight into Madyan. A burning bush. A confrontation with Pharaoh. The drowning of Pharaoh. The destruction of Qarun, whose treasure-vaults the earth swallowed whole. Story after story of what happens to a civilization that hardens itself against the one Allah sends.

And then, just before our ayah, the Quraysh — though not named — say something stunning. They say, in effect: *if we follow this guidance with you, we will be snatched from our land.* They fear that accepting the message will cost them their security.

Allah answers their fear. He reminds them that He is the one who made their sanctuary safe. He is the one who feeds it. And then He says something that should have stopped their hearts.

[PAUSE]

He says: *My Lord does not destroy a civilization arbitrarily.* He sends. He warns. He recites. And only then, if the people meet Him with **ẓulm** — wrongdoing — does the destruction come.

But notice what is hiding inside that consolation. **Allah has just told them that He is, in fact, in the habit of sending messengers into mother-cities.** And He is sending one now. To them. In their mother-city.

The consolation is the warning.

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah that should arrest anyone who reads it carefully. Allah does not say "until We sent a messenger into the town." He says **"into its mother."** Into *ummihā*.

A town has a mother?

And there is something else. Twice in this single ayah, Allah refers to destruction. The first time, the verb structure is one way. The second time, it shifts — and shifts in a direction that reveals something tender about how Allah holds even His own justice.

Stay with me. By the end of this reflection, those two features will have re-described how you understand the relationship between mercy and accountability — not just in cities, but in your own life.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ummihā* — "Its Mother"

Let me ask you something before we open the word.

When you think of a city, what is its center? In modern terms, we might say "downtown." We might say "the capital." We might say "the financial district" or "the seat of government." Each of these is a structural answer. A geographic answer. An administrative answer.

Now hear how Allah speaks of it.

He says **fī ummihā** — *into its mother.*

In Arabic, the word **umm** (ا-م-م) is the word for mother. From the same root comes **imām** — the one who stands in front, the one who is followed. From the same root comes **ummah** — a community gathered around a common axis. The root carries the meaning of *origin*, *source*, *that from which other things proceed.* A mother is not merely first chronologically. She is the one from whom the rest derive their being.

So when Allah calls the central city *the mother of the cities*, He is not making a geographic claim. He is making an **ontological** claim. He is saying: this place is the womb from which the surrounding towns derive their character. What lives in the mother-city flows outward into the daughter-towns. What the mother-city believes, the periphery comes to believe. What the mother-city worships, the periphery comes to worship. What the mother-city deems normal, the periphery comes to deem normal.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this reveals about how Allah understands civilization.

He does not see cities as collections of buildings. He sees them as **organisms with mothers**. Every region has a heart. Every culture has a center where its values are produced and from which they radiate.

Now stay with this. Because Allah does not say *We will send a messenger to every town individually.* He says *We will send a messenger into its mother.* One messenger. Into the source. And the message will then flow through the bloodstream of that civilization into every vein, every artery, every capillary that depends on that center.

This is why Makkah was chosen. Not because it was the largest. Not because it was the wealthiest, though it had wealth. Not because it was the most powerful, militarily. It was chosen because it was a **mother**. The Kaʿbah sat at its core. Pilgrims came from every direction. What was said in Makkah did not stay in Makkah. It rode caravans into Yemen, into Sham, into Iraq, into Persia. Plant a seed in the mother-city and the daughter-towns inherit the harvest.

[PAUSE]

And here is what should slow us down.

Every age has its mother-cities. The places where the values of a generation are produced and from which they spread. In our time, those mothers are not always geographic. They are platforms. They are algorithms. They are studios where stories are made. They are universities where frameworks are built. They are feeds where consensus is shaped.

If you want to understand what a civilization is becoming, you do not study its peripheries. You study its mothers. Because what enters a mother-city is what will be born into the children.

And then ask yourself the question the ayah is quietly asking: *who is being sent into the mother-cities of our age, and what are they reciting?*

### Element Two: *Ḥattā Yabʿatha* — "Until He Had Sent"

The second feature is the structure: **mā kāna Rabbuka muhlika al-qurā ḥattā yabʿatha**.

Let me teach the structure before we use it.

In Arabic, when you say **mā kāna** followed by a noun phrase, you are not just saying "it was not." You are saying something stronger. You are saying *it is not in keeping with His nature.* It is not from His way. It is not how He does things. The construction **mā kāna li-Rabbika** or **mā kāna Rabbuka** is a way of asserting that the thing being denied is not merely absent — it is **categorically excluded from the divine character**.

So when Allah says *mā kāna Rabbuka muhlika al-qurā*, He is not saying "your Lord did not destroy the towns." He is saying **"it is not in the nature of your Lord to destroy the towns."** It is not from Him. It is not His way.

Now feel the weight of that. Allah is, in this verse, telling us about His own character. He is telling us what is and is not in keeping with who He is.

And what is excluded from His character?

Destroying *until*.

The word **ḥattā** here functions as a hinge. It is the word for "until," and it marks the threshold that must be crossed before what was denied becomes possible. *It is not in His nature to destroy the towns... until He has sent into the mother-city a messenger reciting Our signs.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with what this is saying.

It is saying that **divine destruction has a precondition**. That precondition is not the people's sin. The precondition is **divine sending**. Allah is telling us that before the question of human guilt is even on the table, He has already obligated Himself — by His own nature, not by anything we have demanded — to deliver the message clearly, into the heart of the civilization, through a messenger who **recites the signs**.

Notice He does not say "until We have sent a warner." He says *yatlū ʿalayhim āyātinā* — *reciting upon them Our signs.* The verb **talā** (ت-ل-و) means to follow, to recite, to read in succession. It implies an ordered, sustained, public unveiling of evidence. It is not a vague warning whispered once. It is a sustained recitation of clear signs.

The bar Allah sets for Himself is breathtaking. He has bound His justice to a condition that He alone fulfills.

Think about how we treat each other. We hold each other accountable on the thinnest evidence. We condemn on rumor. We cancel on a clipped sentence. We assume guilt and call it intuition. We mete out social destruction without sending anyone into the mother-city of the accused's heart to ask, *did you really mean this? have you heard the case clearly?*

And Allah — to whom every secret is open, who knows the inside of every chest, who needs no investigation — *Allah* binds Himself to send. To recite. To make the case audibly. To wait.

[PAUSE]

This is what the classical scholars call **iqāmat al-ḥujjah** — the establishment of the proof. The principle that no soul is accountable for what it could not have known, that responsibility presupposes access, that Allah's justice rests not on what we *could have* discovered in theory but on what was *placed before us* in fact.

There is a tenderness in this that we should not let slip past us.

The God who could be summary is patient. The God who could destroy on the strength of His own knowledge waits until the message has been spoken in the mother-city, in clear Arabic, by a man you can see, who eats food and walks in the markets, who recites to you the signs.

That is not the description of a Lord eager to destroy. That is the description of a Lord who has built His own justice on a foundation of mercy so deep that He will not move until the case has been fully made — and made in the place where it will be heard most clearly.

### Element Three: The Asymmetry — *Rabbuka* and *Kunnā*

Now look at the two halves of the ayah side by side.

The first half: **mā kāna Rabbuka muhlika al-qurā** — *your Lord would not destroy the towns.*

The second half: **wa mā kunnā muhlikī al-qurā** — *nor would We destroy the towns.*

The same prohibition is stated twice. But notice — Allah does not refer to Himself the same way both times.

In the first half, He says *Rabbuka* — **your Lord**. The third-person, possessive form. The Lord *of you*, O Muhammad. Singular. Tender. Addressed to the Prophet directly.

In the second half, He says *kunnā* — **We were**. The first-person plural of majesty. Royal. Authoritative.

This shift in self-reference — what the rhetoricians call **iltifāt** — is never accidental in the Quran. So why does it happen here?

[PAUSE]

Let me suggest what this shift is doing.

The first half of the ayah is about the *sending of the messenger*. It is the half that emphasizes the messenger as gift, as proof of love, as the act of a Lord who knows and cherishes His servant. So Allah speaks here as *Rabbuka* — **your** Lord. The Lord who is in relationship. The Lord who is near. The Lord who, by sending a messenger into the mother-city, is being personally tender to the very Prophet who carries this word.

The second half of the ayah is about *the wrongdoing of the people*. It is the half that emphasizes the verdict. The accountability. The fact that destruction, when it comes, comes upon *ẓālimūn* — those who have wronged. And here Allah speaks as **We** — the royal, majestic plural. The voice of sovereignty. The voice of the throne. The voice that pronounces judgment.

Do you see what this is doing?

When mercy is the subject, Allah leans close and speaks as *your Lord*. When justice is the subject, Allah pulls back and speaks as *We*. **The intimacy belongs to the sending. The majesty belongs to the verdict.**

This is not a God who delights in destruction. This is a God who keeps the warmth of His nearness for the act of warning, and reserves the formal weight of His sovereignty for the act of consequence — and even there, He prefaces the consequence with the precondition: *unless their people were wrongdoers.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment longer.

In the second half, Allah uses *illā wa ahluhā ẓālimūn* — *unless their people were wrongdoers.* The word **ẓulm** (ظ-ل-م) literally means to place a thing where it does not belong. To put darkness in the place of light. To put a false god in the place of the true God. To put injustice in the place of justice. **Ẓulm** is the displacement of the right thing by the wrong thing.

And Allah is saying — destruction does not come on towns that have merely failed to be perfect. Destruction comes only on towns that have actively, deliberately, structurally placed wrongness where rightness belonged — and held it there even after the messenger came reciting signs.

So the full picture is this: Allah, as *your Lord*, sends a messenger into the heart of the civilization. He, as *We*, only acts in consequence when the response is not mere imperfection but settled, persistent **ẓulm**. Mercy is the default. Warning is the work. Destruction is the response only to the response.

### Bridge from Linguistic to Thematic

We have seen three things now.

We have seen that Allah understands civilization as a body with a **mother** — a heart that beats meaning into every limb.

We have seen that destruction is **bound by precondition** — that Allah binds Himself to send before He acts, that *iqāmat al-ḥujjah* is not an option but His character.

We have seen that the **intimacy of "your Lord" belongs to the sending**, while the **majesty of "We" belongs to the verdict** — that Allah keeps His warmth for the work of warning and reserves His distance for the moment of consequence.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Mercy Comes With a Return Address

There is a way of imagining God that is very common, and very wrong.

It imagines that God's mercy is vague. Atmospheric. That it floats somewhere above human affairs, available in principle, but unaddressed. That mercy is the *feeling* one hopes God has, somewhere, somehow, when the time comes.

This ayah destroys that image.

[PAUSE]

Because here, mercy has a **return address**. Mercy is **bound to a specific city**. Mercy is **carried by a named person**. Mercy is **recited in a known language**. Mercy is *fī ummihā* — *into her mother.* Into a specific place, at a specific time, in a specific voice.

Think about how mercy actually reaches you in your own life.

It does not reach you as a cloud. It reaches you through a person who said the right word at the right moment. A teacher who happened to assign you the book that broke open the door. A friend who, one evening, said the sentence that made you cry and that you have never forgotten. A parent whose phrase still lives in your bones thirty years later. The mosque you walked into on the worst night. The verse that came up on your phone the morning you were about to give up.

Mercy is not vague. **Mercy is sent.**

The Quran is telling us that Allah's mercy is not a wash. It is a *bi'tha* — a deliberate sending, into a deliberate location, carrying a deliberate signal. The God of this verse is a God who **aims**. Who places. Who delivers.

[PAUSE]

And this should change how you read your own story.

The book that arrived in your hand at the moment you needed it — that was a *risāla* into the mother-city of your soul. The conversation that broke through your defenses — that was an *āya* recited upon you. The death that loosened your grip on the dunya — that was a warning into the heart of the city of your life before any consequence arrived.

We sometimes act as though we have not been warned. As though the message has not reached us. As though we were left to figure it out alone in some periphery without a messenger.

But the ayah is unmistakable. Allah does not destroy until He has sent. And He has sent. He has sent prophets into the mother-cities of the world. He has sent verses into the mother-city of every Muslim's life. And He sends, daily, smaller messengers — small mercies, small warnings, small recitations — into the mother-city of every heart.

The question is not whether He has sent.

The question is what we did when the messenger arrived.

[PAUSE]

So here is the first practical movement.

Tonight, before you sleep, ask yourself: *who was sent into the mother-city of my life this week, and did I treat them as a messenger?* That phrase that kept returning to your mind — was it noise, or was it a recitation? That person whose words bothered you — were they an annoyance, or were they bringing signs you would rather not see?

The God of this verse aims His mercy with precision. It is on us to recognize when it has arrived.

### Theme Two: The Mother-City of the Self

Now let us take this verse and turn it inward.

You are not a city, but you are like one. You have a center, and you have peripheries. You have a mother and you have her daughter-towns.

[PAUSE]

What is the mother-city of the self?

The classical scholars of the heart said it long ago. They said that within the human being there is a **qalb** — a heart — that is the seat of the self. Everything else proceeds from it. The Prophet ﷺ said that in the body there is a piece of flesh; if it is sound, the whole body is sound, and if it is corrupt, the whole body is corrupt. *Alā wa hiya al-qalb* — *behold, it is the heart.*

In other words: **the heart is the mother-city of the body.**

What enters the heart flows into the limbs. What the heart loves, the eyes seek. What the heart fears, the feet flee. What the heart believes, the tongue speaks. The hands and feet and eyes and tongues are not the source. They are the daughter-towns. They inherit their orientation from the mother.

This is why the work of religion in the Quran is so insistently aimed at the *qalb*. Why Allah does not say *I look at your prayers* but rather *I look at your hearts.* Why hypocrisy is described as a disease of the heart, not the tongue. Why hardness is described as a calcification of the heart, not the hands. The hands only do what the mother-city sends them to do.

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again with this in mind.

*Your Lord would never destroy the towns until He had sent into their mother-city a messenger reciting Our signs to them.*

If the heart is the mother-city of the self, then Allah is telling you something about your own life. He is telling you that **He will not let your life come to ruin without first sending a messenger into your heart**. He will not let the towns of your habits, your relationships, your career, your body, your reputation fall — without first sending recitation into the center.

Which means, for every soul: a messenger has been sent. A messenger is being sent. The verses are being recited. They may not be coming through a stranger's voice. They may be coming through a chronic anxiety that you cannot shake. Through a friend who keeps returning to a hard conversation. Through a parent's illness. Through a verse that arrests you for no reason on a Tuesday afternoon. Through the silence that lands on you after a sin and refuses to lift.

These are recitations into the mother-city.

[PAUSE]

And here is the harder edge of the theme.

Because if the heart is the mother-city, then *ẓulm* — the wrongdoing that becomes the precondition for destruction — is also located in the mother-city. *Ẓulm* of the limbs is downstream. *Ẓulm* of the tongue is downstream. The structural *ẓulm* — the *ẓulm* that becomes a civilization's character — is *ẓulm* of the heart.

What does *ẓulm of the heart* look like?

It looks like loving what Allah hates. It looks like fearing creation more than the Creator. It looks like preferring the approval of people to the approval of the One who made the people. It looks like placing the dunya in the seat reserved for the akhirah. It looks like making the self the imām of the heart, the thing followed, the thing obeyed — when that seat belongs to Allah alone.

*Ẓulm* in its root sense — placing a thing where it does not belong — happens first in the heart. Before the limbs ever transgress, the mother-city has already crowned the wrong king. The limbs are just the soldiers carrying out the policy of a heart that has misplaced its allegiance.

[PAUSE]

So what does this verse ask of us, in our own mother-cities?

It asks two things.

First — **welcome the messenger.** When a recitation enters your heart, do not treat it as background noise. When a sign arrives — through a verse, a person, a sudden silence, a small fear — recognize that the mercy of the Lord has just aimed itself at the center of you. Do not be the people of a mother-city who heard the messenger and laughed.

Second — **examine the seat.** Sit with your own heart and ask, honestly, what is enthroned there? Whose approval are you actually seeking when you act? Whose pleasure are you actually optimizing for? What does the *imām* of your heart command, and is that *imām* Allah, or is it a lower god wearing His clothes?

Because no town is destroyed until *ẓulm* settles into its mother. And no soul falls until something other than Allah takes the throne of the heart.

The messenger has been sent.

Do not let him recite to an empty mother-city.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Beneath the Ayah

Now look back at the verse as a whole.

> *Your Lord would never destroy the towns until He had sent into their mother-city a messenger reciting Our signs to them. Nor would We ever destroy the towns unless their people were wrongdoers.*

You see now that the ayah is built like a tent on two poles.

The first pole: **divine sending must precede divine consequence**. Mercy is the floor of justice. *Rabbuka* is its name.

The second pole: **the consequence comes only where wrongdoing has settled**. Not on imperfection. Not on weakness. On *ẓulm* — the deliberate, settled misplacement of what should have been placed rightly. *Kunnā* is its name.

Between these two poles, the ayah stretches a roof: **the mother-city**. The center. The source. The place where civilizations are formed and where souls are decided.

The mother-city receives the messenger.
The mother-city decides what to do with the recitation.
The mother-city becomes either the place where guidance takes root or the place where *ẓulm* settles.

And the daughter-towns inherit the answer.

### How the Linguistic and Thematic Converge

Notice how the two threads we followed are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic discovery — that Allah shifts from *Rabbuka* (warm, near, possessive) when speaking of the messenger to *kunnā* (royal, majestic, sovereign) when speaking of the verdict — is the same truth as our second theme. **Allah keeps His intimacy for the work of warning and His majesty for the moment of consequence.** Mercy is personal. Judgment is sovereign. The same Lord wears two faces because we ourselves stand at two different angles to Him depending on what we have done with the recitation.

And the linguistic discovery that Allah binds His own destruction to a condition He alone fulfills — that *ḥattā yabʿatha* makes sending the precondition for accountability — is the same truth as our first theme. **Mercy comes with a return address.** It is not vague. It is aimed. It is sent into the mother. It is recited in clear signs. The God of this verse does not condemn the unwarned, because the God of this verse will not leave anyone unwarned.

The whole ayah is one extended description of a God who is incapable of arbitrary destruction — because His own nature has bound Him, in advance, to the work of mercy.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the mother-city of my life right now — the place from which the rest of my days take their character? Has a messenger been sent into it, and did I recognize him?

2. When was the last time a recitation entered my heart — through a verse, a person, a sign — and I treated it as noise rather than as Allah aiming His mercy at me?

3. Is there a *ẓulm* — a misplacement, something I have put where it does not belong — that has been quietly enthroned in my heart for years?

4. When I think of Allah, do I default to *Rabbuka* — the One who leans close and sends — or have I let fear convince me He is only *kunnā*, the sovereign far away?

5. If Allah will not let a civilization fall without sending into its mother-city, what does that mean about the messengers He is sending into the mother-cities of our age — and where am I in relation to them?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

> Allah's justice is not the absence of His mercy — it is the consequence of what we did when His mercy arrived in our mother-city, recited the signs, and asked us what we would do.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not waiting to be warned. You have been warned. The Quran in your hand, the verse on your lips, the moments of arrest in your week, the conversations that keep returning — these are messengers sent into the mother-city of your life. The question of this ayah is not whether Allah will be just. He will. The question is what the mother-city of your heart will do with the messenger who has already arrived.

Open the gates. Let him recite. Let the signs reach the center.

And let the daughter-towns of your days inherit what the mother-city receives.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who never destroy without first sending — send into the mother-city of our hearts a messenger we cannot ignore. Recite to us Your signs in a voice that breaks through our distraction, and remove from the throne of our hearts every false imām that has been seated there too long. Let us be among the towns that welcomed the messenger, not among the towns that heard him and turned away. Āmīn.
