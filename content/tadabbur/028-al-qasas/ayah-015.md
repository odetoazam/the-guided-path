# The Punch That Wasn't Meant to Kill

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 15

### The Arabic

> وَدَخَلَ ٱلْمَدِينَةَ عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍ مِّنْ أَهْلِهَا فَوَجَدَ فِيهَا رَجُلَيْنِ يَقْتَتِلَانِ هَـٰذَا مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ وَهَـٰذَا مِنْ عَدُوِّهِۦ ۖ فَٱسْتَغَـٰثَهُ ٱلَّذِى مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ عَلَى ٱلَّذِى مِنْ عَدُوِّهِۦ فَوَكَزَهُۥ مُوسَىٰ فَقَضَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ۖ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِنْ عَمَلِ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ عَدُوٌّ مُّضِلٌّ مُّبِينٌ

### The Translation

> And he entered the city at a time of inattention of its people, and found two men fighting — one from his faction and one from his enemies. The one from his faction called him for help against the one from his enemies, so Mūsā struck him with his fist and finished him. He said: "This is from the work of Shayṭān. Truly he is an enemy — misleading, manifest."

---

## Introduction: The Day Before Prophethood

Let me ask you to do something before we open this ayah.

Imagine the man who, decades from now, will stand before the most powerful tyrant of the ancient world and say, *"Let my people go."* Imagine the man whose hand will turn into light, whose staff will become a serpent, who will split the sea with a strike. Imagine Mūsā ibn ʿImrān — Kalīm Allāh, the one to whom Allah spoke directly.

Now picture him as a young man. Strong. Just given, the verse before this, what Allah calls *ḥukm wa ʿilm* — judgment and knowledge. The text is telling us: he is ready. He is mature. He has been prepared.

And in the very next breath of the surah — the very next ayah — Allah tells us that this prepared man walks into a city and within a few minutes accidentally kills somebody.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. Don't move past it.

This is one of the most theologically loaded sequences in the entire Qur'an, because Allah is not embarrassed by it. He doesn't hide it. He doesn't soften it. He puts it on display in a surah whose name — *Al-Qasas* — literally means *the storytelling, the recounting of what happened*. The surah is named after the act of telling, and one of the first things it tells us about its central prophet is: *he made a mistake that cost a man his life, and he never stopped being haunted by it.*

We live in a culture that demands its heroes be uncomplicated. Politicians can't apologize. CEOs can't admit error. Even our religious figures — we want them sanitized, smooth, impossible to indict. And here is Allah, doing the exact opposite. Showing us a prophet's worst day before showing us his best one.

What is Allah doing? Why is this here? Why does the man who will speak to God need a story like this on his record?

That's the door we're going to walk through. But before we do — there are three constructions in this ayah you need to feel the weight of: **ghaflah**, **wakaza**, and the doubled **hādhā** in the architecture of the verse. Allah chose specific words. Not synonyms. Not approximations. Each one is doing structural work. And by the time we're done, you're going to see that this isn't a story about a prophet who *almost* failed.

It's a story about how Allah *makes* a prophet — through a wound that never closes.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: عَلَىٰ حِينِ غَفْلَةٍ — *On a Time of Inattention*

Let me teach you something about Arabic prepositions before we look at the phrase.

In English we say "at a time of." We have one preposition — *at* — and it's flat. It just locates something in time. Arabic has several options here. Allah could have said *fī ḥīni ghaflatin* — "in a time of inattention." That would be natural. That would be expected.

But He didn't. He said **ʿalā ḥīni ghaflatin** — "*upon* a time of inattention." *ʿAlā*. The preposition that means *on top of, over, mounted upon, riding.*

Notice something. In Arabic, when you use *ʿalā* with time, it suggests that the moment was *seized*. That the person didn't just happen to be there — they took advantage of the moment. They moved *upon* the time the way you might move upon an unguarded gate.

This is one word — one preposition — and it has already told you something the translations almost universally miss. **Mūsā did not stumble into the city accidentally. He entered when he knew nobody would see him.**

[PAUSE]

Now look at *ghaflah*. The root غ-ف-ل. The verb *ghafala* means to be inattentive, heedless, unaware — the head turned the other way. *Ghaflah* in the Qur'an is one of the most damning spiritual conditions: it's what the disbelievers are accused of, what the heart slips into when it stops remembering.

But here Allah is using it in a neutral, physical sense — *the people of the city were heedless*. Al-Ṭabarī is specific: he locates the city as Memphis (Manf) in Egypt and the hour as *al-qāʾilah* — the dead middle of the day, *niṣf al-nahār*, when the people had withdrawn for their midday rest. Other commentators picture the hour between two activities; others simply empty streets. The common thread the tafsir keeps returning to: nobody was watching.

Here's the composite picture: a young man, raised in the palace, walks into the city of his oppressed people at the moment when nobody is watching. He is being careful. He is being deliberate. He knows that as a member of Pharaoh's household visiting the Banī Isrāʾīl quarter, his movements would be noted. So he chose his moment. Al-Muyassar makes the concealment explicit in its very gloss of the verse: Mūsā entered *mustakhfiyan* — *in hiding*. The word the classical reading reaches for is not "wandered in," but "slipped in unseen."

Let's sit with this. *Why does Allah tell us this?* Why not just say "and he entered the city"?

Because Allah is preparing you for the verdict. He is telling you in advance: **Mūsā was not impulsive. Mūsā was calculating.** He thought about when to go. He thought about who might see. And *that* is what makes what happens next so devastating — because despite all the planning, despite the calculation, despite the choosing of the right hour, something he *did not plan* explodes out of him.

You can plan your circumstances. You cannot plan your reactions.

The careful one slipped on his own reflex.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The Qur'an could have made Mūsā purely a victim of circumstance — "he was just walking by, and a fight broke out, and he reacted." But Allah's choice of *ʿalā ḥīni ghaflah* refuses that easy version. It plants Mūsā as a *deliberate actor* in his own undoing. The man who scheduled his visit could not schedule his fist.

And **that** — the gap between what we control and what controls us — is what this ayah is going to unfold.

---

### Element Two: فَوَكَزَهُۥ — *He Struck Him With His Fist*

This is the word the entire ayah turns on.

Let me first teach you something about how Arabic handles killing. There is a wide vocabulary for it:

- *qatala* — to kill (the general word; intent is implied)
- *ḍaraba* — to strike
- *ṣafaʿa* — to slap with an open hand
- *ṭaʿana* — to stab
- *lakaza* / *wakaza* / *lakama* — to punch

Allah does not use *qatala* here. He does not say *fa-qatalahu Mūsā* — "so Mūsā killed him." He could have. It would have been the simplest, most direct Arabic. The whole ayah would still make sense.

Instead He uses one of the rarest verbs in the entire Qur'an: **wakaza**. This is the only place this verb appears. *Hapax legomenon.* One occurrence. In the entire revelation.

Why?

The classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib, Ibn Manẓūr — are precise on this. *Wakaza* means to strike with the closed fist or the tip of the fingers, in the chest or the side. It's the punch you throw when you're trying to *push someone away*, not when you're trying to end them. It's the verb of a brawl, not the verb of a murder. The tafsir confirms exactly this register: al-Muyassar paraphrases *fa-wakazahu* as *ḍarabahu bi-jumʿi kaffihi* — "he struck him with his gathered fist." Not a blade, not a planned blow — the bunched hand of a man breaking up a fight.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah is doing.

He is making sure that **the universe records, for all time, what Mūsā actually intended.** Mūsā did not intend to kill. He intended to *push, to strike, to break up a fight by force.* The Arabic word *wakaza* — chosen out of the entire Arabic language, used nowhere else in the Qur'an — is functioning like a forensic witness. It is the verb that proves the *manslaughter* of the act, not the *murder* of it.

And then comes the next verb: **fa-qaḍā ʿalayh**. *Qaḍā ʿalā* — literally "he finished him off, he settled the matter upon him." But the construction is striking — it's almost passive in feel. Mūsā did *wakaza* — an active, intentional verb. And then *fa-qaḍā ʿalayh* — the result fell *upon him*, on the victim, almost as if to say: *the punch was Mūsā's; the death was not.*

Stay with me here. The Arabic is doing something English cannot do easily. It's separating the **intended act** (a fist) from the **unintended outcome** (a death). The punch was a choice. The killing was a consequence.

Here is why this matters theologically. In the Sharīʿah that Mūsā will later carry to his people, there is a distinction enshrined in the law itself: *al-qatl al-ʿamd* (intentional killing) versus *al-qatl al-khaṭaʾ* (unintentional killing). The penalty differs. The moral weight differs. The path to atonement differs. **And the very vocabulary of that distinction is being laid down, in advance, in this ayah, by Allah's choice of *wakaza* over *qatala*.**

The Arabic morphology is teaching you the law before the law arrives.

[PAUSE]

But there's something more here. Notice that Mūsā's own response — the very next sentence — is to call this *ʿamal al-Shayṭān*, the work of Shayṭān. If it were truly unintended, why the self-condemnation?

This is where the depth opens. Mūsā is not condemning the *killing*. He is condemning the *fist*. He is naming the fact that **anger rose in him before discernment did**. The blow was instinctive. And what is Shayṭān's specialty? Exactly that. He whispers in the space *before you've thought*. He owns the millisecond between provocation and reaction.

This is not an imported reading — it is precisely how al-Muyassar unpacks Mūsā's words. It glosses *hādhā min ʿamal al-Shayṭān* as *hādhā min nazgh al-Shayṭān* — *this is from Shayṭān's incitement* — and then fills in the mechanism in Mūsā's own voice: *bi-an hayyaja ghaḍabī ḥattā ḍarabtu hādhā fa-halaka* — "in that he stirred up my anger until I struck this man, and he died." The classical commentary identifies Shayṭān's "work" here not as the killing itself but as the *provocation of anger* that drove the fist. The unguarded reflex is the doorway. That is the whole point.

Mūsā is essentially saying: *I did not intend to kill — but I did intend to strike, and that strike came from a place I had not yet purified. That place is where Shayṭān works. So this — all of it, the strike and the death that came of it — bears his signature.*

This is a prophet recognizing that even his *good* intention (helping the oppressed) was channelled through an *unpurified* mechanism (untrained anger). And he refuses to let himself off the hook on the technicality that he didn't mean to kill.

The translations say "he struck him." They don't say *wakaza*. They flatten the forensic precision of what Allah preserved. But now you know. **The word is doing the work of an entire moral universe.**

### Element Three: هَـٰذَا مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ وَهَـٰذَا مِنْ عَدُوِّهِۦ — *This One From His Faction, This One From His Enemies*

Look at this clause carefully. Allah doesn't say *one was an Israelite and one was a Copt.* He doesn't even name the ethnicities.

He says: **hādhā min shīʿatihi wa-hādhā min ʿaduwwihi.** *Shīʿah* — from the root ش-ي-ع — meaning a group that follows, a faction, a partisan group. *ʿAduww* — enemy.

Now here's the question. *Whose* faction? *Whose* enemy?

The pronoun *-hi* — "his" — refers to Mūsā. So Allah is calling Banī Isrāʾīl Mūsā's "faction" and the Egyptians Mūsā's "enemies." But — and this is critical — at this moment in the narrative, **Mūsā has been raised in Pharaoh's household.** He has lived in the palace. By every external marker, he is Egyptian. The Egyptian who died was, by upbringing and station, closer to him than the Israelite who survived.

So why does Allah call the Israelites *Mūsā's shīʿah*?

Because Allah is telling us something that not even Mūsā has fully articulated yet: **his loyalty has been determined before his consciousness has caught up to it.** His *shīʿah* is not who he ate dinner with. His *shīʿah* is who he is *from*. Blood, covenant, lineage — Allah is naming a deeper identity than the one Mūsā was raised inside.

[PAUSE]

But notice something subtle. Allah uses the *exact same demonstrative twice*: **hādhā... wa-hādhā**. "This one... and this one." Same word. Same grammatical weight. He doesn't say *hādhā... wa-dhālika* (this one... and *that* one), which would have created a near-far distinction. He says *hādhā... wa-hādhā* — they are presented to the reader as *equally close*, *equally present*, *equally human*.

The labels diverge — *shīʿah* versus *ʿaduww* — but the demonstratives are identical. *Two men. Both in front of you. Both made of the same dust.*

This is a quiet warning embedded in the syntax. Yes — there are factions. Yes — there is an enemy and there is a brother. But before you raise your fist: *hādhā wa-hādhā*. Look at both. They are both *this*. They are both before you. The categories of "ours" and "theirs" are real, but they do not reduce to the categories of "human" and "non-human."

Mūsā gets the categories right (he correctly identifies the wronged party) but he forgets the underlying *hādhā wa-hādhā* — that both are men. He treats the *ʿaduww* as merely *ʿaduww*. And in doing so, he kills someone Allah was simultaneously calling a *hādhā* — a *this*, a presence, a human.

The faction is real. But it never erases the *hādhā*.

[PAUSE]

Here's the bridge from word to world.

Linguistically we have seen three precision instruments in three words:

1. **ʿAlā ḥīni ghaflah** — Mūsā was deliberate before the moment, not after.
2. **Wakaza... fa-qaḍā** — the intended act and the unintended outcome were separated by Allah's vocabulary itself.
3. **Hādhā... wa-hādhā** — both men were equally *this*, equally human, despite the rival labels.

These three constructions together are not decoration. They are forming a portrait of a moral act in all its layered honesty: *I planned the timing. I did not plan the fist. I judged the faction. I forgot the humanity.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how Allah forms a prophet.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Gap Between What You Mean and What Happens

Have you ever sent a message you regretted?

Not a planned message. A *reactive* one. Maybe a text typed at 1 AM. Maybe a comment you fired off before your hand caught up to your judgment. Maybe a sentence to your spouse, your child, your sibling — that came out *wrong*, that hit harder than you intended, that did damage you didn't author.

You did not mean to hurt them. But your fingers moved before your discernment did. And by the time the message landed, the harm was real. You can apologize. You can clarify. You cannot un-land it.

That gap — the gap between *what you intended* and *what your body, your words, your fist actually did* — is one of the great hidden topographies of moral life. We almost never speak about it directly, because we don't have a name for it. We say "I didn't mean it that way." We say "it came out wrong." We say "I don't know what got into me." All of these are gestures toward the same chasm: **the place where action exceeds intention.**

This ayah is the Qur'an's map of that chasm.

[PAUSE]

Mūsā's intention was to help. Mūsā's deeper intention was to push, not to kill. But the punch landed somewhere fatal, in someone whose constitution was weaker than Mūsā's reckoning. And a man was dead.

Now watch what Mūsā does. He doesn't say *"it was an accident."* He doesn't say *"his fault for being in the fight."* He doesn't say *"justice was served — he was an oppressor."* All three would have been *available* defenses. All three are technically partly true.

He says: **hādhā min ʿamal al-Shayṭān.** *This is from the work of Shayṭān.*

This is one of the most spiritually mature self-assessments in scripture. Mūsā is naming the truth that **the gap between intention and outcome is not a morally neutral zone**. It is *exactly* the zone Shayṭān works in. Because Shayṭān cannot make you intend evil — but he can make you act before you have intended anything carefully enough.

This is what the gap is. It is the unexamined reflex. It is the punch you didn't plan. It is the text you sent without thinking. It is the tone you took with your mother that you would never have *chosen* if you had paused for one second.

And Mūsā — already given *ḥukm* and *ʿilm* — recognizes that even prophethood-in-formation is vulnerable here. He doesn't say *"Shayṭān made me do it"* (that would be excuse-making). He says *"this is from his work"* (that is naming the territory). There's a critical difference.

[PAUSE]

What changes if you live with this map?

You stop trusting your *first* response. You build a habit of the small pause. You recognize that **the most spiritually dangerous moment in your day is not when you're choosing between right and wrong — it's when you're not choosing at all, when you're just reacting**. That's where Shayṭān lives. Not in your deliberations. In your reflexes.

The practice this opens: when something provokes you — a comment, a snub, a child's defiance, a colleague's email — *count to seven before responding*. Not because seven is magical. Because in those seven seconds, you move from reflex into deliberation. You move from the zone where Shayṭān works to the zone where the soul does. **You shrink the gap.**

Mūsā, the prophet, killed a man because his gap was a fraction of a second too wide. What might yours be costing you?

### Theme Two: The Wound That Makes the Prophet

Here is something most people don't notice about Mūsā.

He is the prophet *most often discussed in the Qur'an*. More verses, more stories, more direct quotation than anyone else — including the Prophet ﷺ. And in his story, this killing comes back *again and again*. In Sūrah Ṭāhā, when Allah first speaks to him at the Burning Bush, Mūsā says — almost trembling — *"and they have a charge against me for a soul, and I fear they will kill me."* (28:33) He brings it up *himself*. To God.

In Sūrah ash-Shuʿarāʾ, when Pharaoh confronts Mūsā years later, the very first thing Pharaoh throws at him is: *"And you did the deed you did — and you are of the ungrateful."* (26:19) Pharaoh weaponizes it. *You killed a man. Who are you to lecture me?*

And Mūsā's response is one of the most extraordinary moments in scripture. He doesn't deny it. He doesn't deflect. He says: **"I did it then, when I was of those who were astray."** (26:20) *Faʿaltuhā idhan wa-anā mina-ḍ-ḍāllīn.* *I did it. When I was lost.*

Pause on that.

The greatest prophet of the pre-Muḥammadan tradition, in front of the tyrant of the age, with the eyes of his people on him, with everything at stake — *admits the wound openly*. He does not pretend it didn't happen. He does not minimize. He simply locates it: *that was me, then, in my lostness.*

[PAUSE]

This is what Allah was doing in Surah Al-Qasas, ayah 15.

He was not embarrassing Mūsā. He was *forming* him. The killing is not a stain on Mūsā's prophethood — **the killing is part of the curriculum of his prophethood.**

This is not a modern sentiment laid over the text. Ibn Kathīr says it almost in these words: Allah brought Mūsā to the rank decreed for him — prophethood, and direct speech with God — *as a consequence of* (he uses the language of cause) the killing of the Copt. The deed was the hinge: it set in motion the flight, the exile, the years in Madyan, and the road back. The classical tradition already read the wound as the pivot of the formation, not an embarrassing detour around it.

Notice, too, where the text places this. The verse *just* before — the one that gives Mūsā his *ḥukm wa ʿilm* — seals itself with *wa-kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn*, "and thus do We reward the doers of good." Ibn Kathīr quotes that clause and then, in the very next breath, narrates the killing. The reward of the *muḥsin* and the gravest act of Mūsā's young life sit shoulder to shoulder in the Qur'ān's own ordering, with no seam between them. Allah does not file the mistake somewhere far from the honor. He lays it down immediately after the words about rewarding the good — as if to say the reward and the rupture are not two stories but one.

A brief honesty is owed here on the timing, because the scholars do differ. Al-Muyassar states it plainly: *hādhā al-ʿamal min Mūsā ʿalayhi al-salām kāna qabla al-nubuwwa* — "this act of Mūsā's was before prophethood." But Ibn Kathīr preserves Mujāhid's reading that the *ḥukm wa ʿilm* Allah gave Mūsā in the previous ayah *already* denoted prophethood — which would place this killing not before the office but at its very threshold. The reflection has been leaning on the pre-prophetic reading; the more careful statement is that there is genuine *ikhtilāf*, and that on either reading the theological force is identical. Whether Mūsā was a prophet-in-waiting or a prophet still being shaped by exile, Allah recorded the wound and used it.

Think about what this wound did to Mūsā:

- It taught him *what his own fist could do*. He would never raise it carelessly again.
- It drove him into *exile* — into Madyan, where he met Shuʿayb, married, herded sheep for a decade, learned patience, learned shepherding (the apprenticeship of every prophet).
- It gave him a *visceral horror of injustice* — not as a slogan, but as something he himself had perpetrated and would never want repeated.
- It gave him *humility before God* — when the Burning Bush spoke, Mūsā was not a confident hero. He was a man with a fugitive's record asking *"who am I to be sent?"*

And here is the deep theological move: **Allah did not heal the wound. Allah used the wound.**

[PAUSE]

There is a kind of religiosity that wants its saints sanitized. *He was always perfect. He never doubted. He never erred. He never bled.* That kind of religiosity has a problem with this ayah. It wants to skip past it, or explain it away, or argue that the man who died was about to be killed anyway, so Mūsā actually didn't do anything wrong.

The Qur'an refuses that move. The Qur'an *records the wound, preserves the wound, and shows the prophet returning to the wound for the rest of his life.*

Because here is what Allah is teaching: **You do not have to be unwounded to be useful to God. You have to be honest about your wound.**

The man who carries an old shame into his work with God carries something *fuel*. The man who pretends he was always righteous carries something *weight*. The first man can speak to other broken people because he knows the inside of brokenness. The second man can only judge them.

This is why Mūsā — the man who killed unintentionally and lived in exile for ten years carrying it — is the prophet sent to the most violent regime in the ancient world to liberate the most traumatized people. He is uniquely qualified *because of the wound*, not despite it.

[PAUSE]

What does this open for you?

If you are carrying something — a thing you did, a moment you don't talk about, a wound from your own hand — Surah Al-Qasas, ayah 15 is one of the most important verses in the Qur'an for you to know exists. Because the prophet who would later split the sea, who would later see Allah's light, who would later receive the Tawrah — *that prophet was once a young man who killed someone he didn't mean to kill, and Allah did not erase it from his record.*

Allah kept it there. Forever. In a surah named *the recounting*. Because the wound, when *named honestly and brought to God*, is not your disqualification.

**It might be your formation.**

The practice this opens: take the thing you have been hiding from yourself. Sit with it the way Mūsā sat with this in front of Pharaoh — *"I did it. When I was lost."* Not minimizing. Not exaggerating. Just naming. Then ask Allah, as Mūsā does in the very next ayah, *"Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī fa-ghfir lī"* — *My Lord, I have wronged myself, so forgive me.* Al-Ṭabarī reads that next verse as the record of Mūsā's *nadam* — his remorse — and his turning back: forgive me, he says, *the soul You did not command me to kill*; cover it over me, and do not hold me to account for it. And the verse answers him at once — *fa-ghafara lah* — *so He forgave him.* The naming is met by the mercy in the very same breath.

That is the door Mūsā walked through. It opens to anyone who is willing to walk through it the same way: without alibi, without performance, without flinching.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now look at the ayah again, with everything we've seen:

- **ʿAlā ḥīni ghaflah** — the careful planning
- **Wakaza** — the unplanned reflex
- **Fa-qaḍā ʿalayh** — the consequence that exceeded the act
- **Hādhā... wa-hādhā** — the humanity the categories obscured
- **ʿAmal al-Shayṭān** — the territory of the unexamined moment
- **Mubīn** — clear, manifest — Shayṭān's enmity *was* visible. Mūsā simply hadn't been watching for it.

The architecture of the ayah is the architecture of a moral collapse: a careful man enters a careful moment, gets ambushed by his own un-careful reflex, and emerges with a body at his feet and a name for what just happened — *the work of Shayṭān*. The verse is a clinic in honest self-diagnosis.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic precision and the thematic depths are saying the same thing from two directions:

The vocabulary preserves the gap between *intention and outcome*. The story preserves the gap between *the prophet's planning and the prophet's reflex*. And both gaps are saying: **the spiritual life is not lived in the chosen moments. It is lived in the unchosen ones.** You cannot prepare for the test you scheduled. You can only train your reflexes for the test that ambushes you.

Allah preserves Mūsā's wound because every prophet is partially built from one. And every honest believer is too.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where is your *gap* — the place between your intention and your action — leaking the most? In what relationship, what setting, what trigger?

2. What is the *one moment* you keep returning to — the one you would undo if you could? Have you ever, like Mūsā, named it openly: *I did it, when I was lost*?

3. When you fail, do you reach for an alibi (it wasn't really my fault) or for *ʿamal al-Shayṭān* (this is the territory I left unguarded)? What changes when you name the territory instead of the excuse?

4. Who in your life is *hādhā* — *this* — that you have been letting yourself see only as *ʿaduww*? What would it cost you to remember they are also *hādhā*?

5. If your wound is not your disqualification — what is it asking you to do that you have been refusing because you thought you were too broken to do it?

### The One Sentence

**Allah does not erase the prophet's worst day; He uses it to build the rest of the prophet's life.**

### The Invitation

If you have been hiding something from yourself — a fist you threw, a word you said, a punch that landed harder than you meant — bring it out. Not to perform contrition. To do what Mūsā did in the very next ayah, which is to take it to Allah and say *I have wronged myself, so forgive me.*

Mūsā walked out of the city a fugitive. He walked into Madyan a shepherd. He walked back as a prophet. The wound did not stop the journey.

It started it.

Guard the millisecond where Shayṭān works. And when we have failed, give us the courage of Mūsā to say it plainly: I did it. When I was lost. And then to walk, as he walked, toward You.*
