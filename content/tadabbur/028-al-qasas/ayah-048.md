# The Mask That Falls — When "If Only" Confesses Itself

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 48

> فَلَمَّا جَآءَهُمُ ٱلْحَقُّ مِنْ عِندِنَا قَالُوا۟ لَوْلَآ أُوتِىَ مِثْلَ مَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰٓ ۚ أَوَلَمْ يَكْفُرُوا۟ بِمَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ مِن قَبْلُ ۖ قَالُوا۟ سِحْرَانِ تَظَٰهَرَا وَقَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا بِكُلٍّ كَٰفِرُونَ

*Falammā jā'ahumu l-ḥaqqu min ʿindinā qālū lawlā ūtiya mithla mā ūtiya mūsā. Awa lam yakfurū bimā ūtiya mūsā min qabl? Qālū siḥrāni taẓāharā wa qālū innā bi-kullin kāfirūn.*

> "So when the truth came to them from Us, they said, 'Why was he not given the like of what Moses was given?' Did they not disbelieve in what Moses was given before? They said, 'Two acts of magic backing each other up,' and they said, 'Indeed, we — in everything — are disbelievers.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

Surah Al-Qasas — "The Story" — has just done something the Quran rarely does. For most of the surah, Allah has been telling, in slow and intimate detail, the story of one human life: Musa, peace be upon him. From his mother placing him in the river, to his rescue in Pharaoh's house, to his exile in Madyan, to the marriage at the well, to the burning bush, to the moment he stood before Pharaoh — Allah has unspooled this life with a tenderness and density unmatched anywhere else in the Quran. Even his name, *al-qasas*, means "the narration that follows the trail."

And then, after all of that, Allah turns His face from the past to the present. He turns to the Quraysh in Makkah — the people sitting around the Prophet ﷺ, hearing this story for the first time — and He asks them, in effect: *Now that you have heard it — what is your excuse?*

This ayah is the answer to that unspoken question. It is the Quraysh's reply, recorded in their own voice, with all the texture of how a human heart actually refuses light.

### The World Behind the Words

Imagine yourself in Makkah. The Quraysh have been making demands for years. *Bring down an angel. Bring us a treasure. Make a garden of palms and grapes for yourself. Cause a spring to gush from the ground. Cause the sky to fall on us in pieces.* They have rejected every form of the Prophet's message and asked for it to be delivered differently.

And now, in this ayah, they pose what sounds like the most reasonable demand of all: *Why isn't your scripture like Musa's? Why doesn't it come down all at once, on tablets, with thunder and lightning, the way the Torah came? If your message were really from God, it would come like that.*

It sounds, on the surface, like an intellectual question. It sounds like the kind of objection a serious person might raise.

[PAUSE]

But Allah has placed this objection inside a verse that has three parts — and by the time you reach the third part, the speakers themselves have answered the question for you. They have told you, in their own words, exactly what their objection was always covering for.

### The Hook

Three times in this short ayah, the same passive verb appears: *was given*. And three times in this short ayah, the same verb appears: *they said*. Each repetition is doing something. By the time you've followed the staircase of their three statements, you will have watched a mask fall — not the mask Allah pulled off them, but the mask they pulled off themselves.

Allah let them keep talking until they confessed.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The "If Only" That Was Never an If

Before we open the word, let me show you what kind of word it is.

In Arabic, there is a particle — *lawlā* — that is sometimes translated as "why not" and sometimes translated as "if only." Both are correct. But what *lawlā* really does is express a wish or a demand whose form is interrogative but whose content is exhortative. It says: *the situation should have been otherwise.*

Think about how we use this in English, even though we don't have a single word for it. A friend says, "Why didn't you call me last night?" They are not really asking for the reason. They are saying, *you should have called.* The "why" is a costume worn by a complaint.

Now, here is what we need to understand about *lawlā* in particular. When it appears in the mouths of those who reject Allah's messengers, it almost always introduces a *condition for belief that was never going to be honored.*

> *لَوْلَآ أُوتِىَ مِثْلَ مَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ*
>
> *"If only he had been given the like of what Moses was given..."*

Read it as English: *If you had only sent him with what you sent Moses with, then we would have believed.*

Notice what this statement claims. It claims that the speaker is open to belief. It claims that the only thing standing between the speaker and faith is the wrong delivery format. It frames the speaker as a serious evaluator who has examined the evidence and found it wanting on procedural grounds.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is exactly where Allah moves in.

He answers their *lawlā* with one of the most surgical questions in the entire Quran:

> *أَوَلَمْ يَكْفُرُوا۟ بِمَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ مِن قَبْلُ*
>
> *"Did they not disbelieve in what Moses was given, before?"*

Look at what He has done. They said, "If only he had been given like Moses." Allah replies: *And what did you do with what Moses was given?*

The pretext collapses in a single sentence. Because the same people — culturally, generationally, in spirit — who are now saying *"if only it came like Moses's"* are also the people who, when it came like Moses's, rejected it then too. The Pharaoh of their own day called Musa a sorcerer. The leaders of every generation that received a prophet rejected the prophet. The "if only" was a wish for a counterfactual that already happened — and was already refused.

This is a documented grammatical use of *lawlā* — the rejection-pretext particle, used by communities at the moment they were trying to look reasonable while disbelieving. What this invites us to consider is that in our own hearts, the *lawlā* statement is often the diagnostic. *If only the evidence were stronger, I would believe. If only my husband treated me better, I would be patient. If only I had more money, I would give. If only the masjid had better khutbahs, I would pray Jumuʿah.*

The *lawlā* statement names a condition that is not the real condition. The real condition is internal. The pretext is just the costume worn by the refusal.

### Element Two: The Triple Passive — *Ūtiya... Ūtiya... Ūtiya*

Now let me direct your attention to something the listener of Arabic would feel before thinking about it.

In a single short verse, the same verb appears three times — and all three times, it is in the *passive*.

> *مَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ ... مَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ ... أُوتِىَ مِثْلَ مَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ*
>
> *what Moses was given... what Moses was given... was he not given the like of what Moses was given...*

In Arabic, the active voice is the default. To say "he gave" is *aʿṭā*. To say "he was given" is to use a special construction, *ūtiya*, where the giver is deliberately removed from view and only the receiving is named.

This is a choice. Allah could have written: *what We gave to Moses.* Or: *what God gave to Moses.* Instead, three times, He uses the form that says, *what Moses was given* — without naming the Giver in the verb itself.

[PAUSE]

Why?

Notice something. The Quraysh's complaint is being quoted. *They* are the ones using *ūtiya* the first time — *"if only he had been given the like of what Moses was given."* They cannot bring themselves to say "what Allah gave to Moses." They want the gift discussed without the Giver named. The miracle yes, the source no. The Torah yes, the Lord of Torah no.

This is a tell. When a heart is in flight from Allah, it tries to talk about His gifts as if they had no Giver. It says "the universe gave us..." instead of "Allah gave us." It says "I was lucky..." instead of "I was favored." The grammar of avoidance.

And then look what Allah does. When He answers them, He uses the *same passive form back at them.* *"Did they not disbelieve in what Moses was given?"* He does not correct their grammar. He does not say, *what I gave to Moses.* He lets the passive stand — because the rebuttal is so devastating it does not need the addition. *Even on your own grammatical terms, even refusing to name the Giver, you cannot escape what was given. The gift itself is enough to indict you.*

And then the third *ūtiya*, again the same form. The verse is built on a triple passive that mirrors back to the Quraysh their own avoidance — and then turns it on them.

What this single grammatical feature reveals is that a verse can take the listener's own language and let it become the evidence against them. They tried to talk around Allah. Allah let them — and showed that even the language of avoidance is not enough to make Him absent from the conversation.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Siḥrāni Taẓāharā* — The Two That Back Each Other Up

Now we come to the strangest phrase in the verse. After Allah has exposed the *lawlā* as a pretext, look at what they say next.

> *قَالُوا۟ سِحْرَانِ تَظَٰهَرَا*
>
> *"They said: two magics, backing each other up."*

Two things to notice before we move into meaning.

First, the grammatical form. *Siḥrāni* is the dual — not "magics," not "magic," but specifically *two acts of magic*. And *taẓāharā* is also a dual verb, but in the morphological pattern *Form VI* — the form that, in classical Arabic morphology, almost always carries the meaning of *mutual cooperation between two parties.* When two armies *taẓāharā*, they back each other up against a common enemy. When two witnesses *taẓāharā*, they corroborate. The picture is of two things deliberately leaning against each other, each making the other stronger.

Second, the classical reciters preserved two valid readings of this word, *siḥrāni* and *sāḥirāni*. One reads "two acts of magic," the other reads "two sorcerers." Don't treat these as two competing explanations. Treat them as two camera angles on the same accusation. The Quraysh are saying: *two magicians, two scriptures, two phenomena that look like miracles — and they are colluding.*

Who are the two? The classical commentators give a composite picture. Some said they meant Musa and Muhammad ﷺ — two prophets in collusion. Some said they meant the Torah and the Quran — two scriptures supposedly conspiring. Some said they meant Musa and Harun, since this was the original Pharaonic charge against the two brothers. The composite is the point: *whatever the pairing, the Quraysh are using the language of conspiracy.*

[PAUSE]

Now sit with this for a moment.

Just one sentence ago, they were saying, *"If only he had been given what Moses was given."* They were holding Musa up as the standard. Musa was, in their pretext, the genuine prophet — and the problem was that Muhammad ﷺ wasn't enough like him.

And now, in the very next breath, they say: *both of them are sorcery. Both of them. Backing each other up.*

Do you see what just happened?

The *standard they invoked* in their first statement has become *the thing they reject* in their second statement. They invoked Musa as authentic only as long as Musa could be used as a stick to beat Muhammad ﷺ with. The moment Allah pointed out that they would not have accepted Musa either, they stopped pretending Musa was their standard. They simply expanded the rejection to include him too.

This is a pattern of the heart so consistent that it deserves a name. *The standard invoked against the present prophet is never the standard the speaker actually accepts.* They will invoke the dead saint to attack the living one. They will invoke the classical scholar to attack the contemporary one. They will invoke the previous generation to attack this generation. But the moment you call their bluff and ask whether they actually live by what the dead saint or the classical scholar or the previous generation taught — the standard collapses, and you discover they accept neither.

The verse is showing us this happening in real time. *Siḥrāni taẓāharā* — the moment the standard becomes inconvenient, the standard joins the conspiracy.

What this phrase reveals is that the human heart, when it is committed to refusal, will eventually be forced to admit that its objections were never local. They were always universal — and the local objection was just the first one to surface.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Three linguistic features. The *lawlā* that was never a real "if." The triple passive *ūtiya* that voids the Giver. The *siḥrāni taẓāharā* that turns the borrowed standard into the next casualty.

Together, they are showing us something not just about words. They are showing us how a heart in refusal actually moves. Now let's ask what this verse reveals about reality itself — about us, sitting here, listening.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Diagnostic of "If Only"

Before we go to the verse, let me ask you to sit with something from your own life.

Have you ever had a conversation with someone — maybe yourself — that goes like this: *I would pray more, but the masjid is too far. I would give more, but I'm waiting for my income to stabilize. I would memorize Quran, but I don't have time. I would call my parents, but they always make me feel guilty.*

Each statement is a *lawlā* statement. It names a condition under which you would do the right thing. It positions you as someone who *would* do the right thing — *if only* the obstacle were removed.

Now ask yourself, with full honesty: in the cases where the obstacle was actually removed — the masjid that opened next door, the income that stabilized, the season of free time, the parent who softened — did you do the thing? Or did a new *lawlā* appear in the place of the old one?

[PAUSE]

This is what the verse is exposing in the Quraysh, but it is exposing the same pattern in us. The *lawlā* statement is rarely a real statement of conditions. It is, far more often, a delaying mechanism that uses the language of conditions to disguise a refusal that is already complete.

The Quraysh said: *if only he had been given like Moses.* The verse is asking: *would you have believed if he had been? You didn't believe in Moses.*

Now ask the question of yourself. *If only I had more time — would I really pray more? Or would I find a reason not to?* *If only I lived in a more spiritual environment — would I really change? Or would I find new excuses in the new environment?* *If only this teacher were more knowledgeable, this community more welcoming, this masjid less political — would I show up?*

The verse is teaching us a diagnostic tool. *When you find yourself making a lawlā statement about your own faith, treat it as suspect. Ask: have I rejected what was already given to me, in a previous form? If so, this lawlā is not about conditions. It is about my heart.*

The mercy of this teaching is that it can save you years. Years of telling yourself you would be a better Muslim *if only.* Years of waiting for the conditions to be perfect before you start. The verse is saying: the conditions will never be perfect, because the conditions were never the issue. Start now, in conditions you have, with what you've already been given. Because what you've already been given is more than you yet realize — and the *lawlā* is keeping you from seeing it.

What does this change about how I live tomorrow? It changes whether I let myself off the hook for any obligation by inventing an obstacle. The next time I hear myself say "I would, but —" I will notice the *but*, and I will ask whether that *but* would actually move if Allah moved it.

### Theme Two: The Confession at the End

Now go to the very last words of the verse.

> *وَقَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا بِكُلٍّ كَٰفِرُونَ*
>
> *"And they said: indeed we — in everything — are disbelievers."*

Read it again, slowly. Let the structure land.

*Innā* — "indeed we." This is the strongest emphatic in Arabic, the *inna* particle plus the pronoun. It is the language of confession under oath. *We hereby state...*

*Bi-kullin* — "in everything." The Arabic word *kull* means "the totality." Without exception. Across the board.

*Kāfirūn* — "we are disbelievers." Active participle, plural. The form that says: *this is what we are.*

Put it all together. *We hereby state that, in every last thing, we are refusers.*

[PAUSE]

This is the third *qālū*. The third time the verb "they said" appears in the verse. And look at the staircase the verse has walked them up.

First *qālū*: *If only he had been given like Moses.* — The mask of intellectual demand.

Second *qālū*: *Two acts of magic backing each other up.* — The mask slips. They expand the rejection to include the standard they had just invoked.

Third *qālū*: *Indeed we, in everything, are disbelievers.* — The mask is gone. The full position is named, by them, in their own voice.

The verse is letting them speak themselves into their own indictment.

This is something Allah does in many places in the Quran, but rarely so cleanly as here. He does not always need to refute the disbeliever. Sometimes He simply quotes them long enough that their own words finish the work. The opening pretext sounds reasonable. The middle position shows the inconsistency. The closing confession reveals what the pretext was always covering for.

[PAUSE]

Now turn this gently inward. Because Allah did not preserve this verse for us to use against the Quraysh, who are long dead. He preserved it as a mirror.

Have I ever found, over the course of a long argument with myself or with another person, that what started as a single specific objection ended in a much wider rejection? Have I started a conversation by saying *"I just have one question"* and ended it by saying *"actually, I don't accept any of it"?* Has my *lawlā* ever, when followed to its end, turned into *innā bi-kullin kāfirūn?*

Many of us have done this in small ways. We start with one objection to a particular teacher, and by the end of the year we are not praying. We start with a complaint about one masjid, and by the end of the decade we are not visiting any masjid. We start with one issue with one shaykh's fatwa, and by the end of the discussion we are calling all the scholars wrong.

The verse is warning us that the path from *lawlā* to *innā bi-kullin kāfirūn* is shorter than we think. The first statement is rarely the real position. The third statement, the one that arrives after enough conversation, is the real position. And by then, it is much harder to climb back up.

What does this change? It changes how I argue with my own faith. The next time I find myself walking down the staircase from *"I just have one issue"* to *"none of this works for me,"* I will recognize the pattern. I will know that I am not discovering new objections. I am unmasking an objection I had at the start, and I am letting it grow.

The mercy of the verse is that it shows us the staircase before we walk down it. We do not have to take the steps. We can stop at the first *lawlā* and ask, *what is this really about?*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now look at the whole verse with new eyes.

It has three statements from the disbelievers — the three *qālū*. And it has three uses of the word *ūtiya*, the passive *was given*. The triple structure of their speech is mirrored by the triple structure of the gift they refused. *They were given, they were given, they were given* — and *they said, they said, they said.*

In the middle of this — at the exact center of the verse — Allah inserts a single rhetorical question of His own. *Did they not disbelieve in what Moses was given before?* That question is the pivot. Before it, they sound reasonable. After it, the mask falls and falls and falls until the third *qālū* names the truth.

The architecture of the verse is the architecture of an honest conversation with a heart in refusal. Let it speak. Ask one question. Let it speak again. The truth surfaces.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries — the pretextual *lawlā*, the Giver-erasing passive *ūtiya*, the standard-collapsing *siḥrāni taẓāharā* — and the thematic depths — the diagnostic of "if only," the confession at the end — are the same insight from two directions.

A heart in refusal will use language to mask itself. It will use the conditional, the passive, the borrowed standard. But the language can only mask for so long. Eventually, given room to keep talking, the heart confesses what it always was. And Allah, in His justice, lets it confess in its own voice.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *lawlā* statement I keep making about my own faith — and would I really do the thing if the obstacle were removed?

2. Whose authority do I invoke when I want to dismiss a present obligation — and do I actually live by that authority when it inconveniences me?

3. When I find a single objection growing into a wider rejection, do I recognize the staircase, or do I keep walking down it?

4. Where in my speech do I avoid naming Allah as the Giver — and what does that avoidance protect?

5. If my objections to faith were quoted back to me three times in a row, like the Quraysh's were, what would be revealed about what I actually believe?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The objection that sounds most reasonable is often the costume worn by a refusal that, given enough air, will eventually confess itself.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to walk the staircase the Quraysh walked. You do not have to let your *lawlā* grow into your *innā bi-kullin kāfirūn*. The verse is a warning, but it is also a reprieve. It shows you the shape of the descent so you can refuse it.

The next time you hear yourself say *if only*, pause. Ask whether the obstacle is real. Ask whether you would move if Allah moved it. And if the honest answer is no, then forgive yourself for the pretext, set it down, and start with what you have already been given — because what you have already been given is the *ḥaqq* itself, *min ʿindinā*, from Him, and that is enough.

### Du'a

Yā Allah, do not let our objections become our destination. When we say *if only*, expose the pretext to us before it grows. And let our final *qālū*, when it comes, be not a confession of refusal but a confession of submission — *innā bi-kulli mā arsalta mu'minūn* — indeed we, in everything You have sent, are believers. Āmīn.
