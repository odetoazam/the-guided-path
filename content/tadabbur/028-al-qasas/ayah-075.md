# The Witness From Every Nation — When the Argument Vanishes

## Surah 28, Ayah 75

### Arabic

> [ayah:28:75] وَنَزَعْنَا مِن كُلِّ أُمَّةٍ شَهِيدًا فَقُلْنَا هَاتُوا۟ بُرْهَـٰنَكُمْ فَعَلِمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱلْحَقَّ لِلَّهِ وَضَلَّ عَنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَفْتَرُونَ

### Transliteration

*Wa nazaʿnā min kulli ummatin shahīdan, fa qulnā hātū burhānakum, fa ʿalimū anna-l-ḥaqqa lillāh, wa ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn.*

### Translation

> And We will draw forth — pull out — from every nation a witness. And We will say: "Bring your proof." Then they will know that the truth belongs to Allah. And what they used to fabricate will have wandered away from them — abandoned them — gone missing.

---

## Introduction — The World Behind the Ayah

Before we open a single word of this ayah, we have to feel the place it sits in.

Surah Al-Qasas is the surah of **Pharaoh and Musa**. It opens with a tyrant who has manufactured an entire reality. Pharaoh tells his people he is their highest lord. Pharaoh tells his court that Musa is a magician. Pharaoh stages contests. Pharaoh issues decrees. Pharaoh writes the script and casts himself as the truth-teller. And the people around him — his courtiers, his magicians, his army — live inside that fabricated reality as if it were the air they breathe.

Then Musa arrives with a sign — a staff, a hand, a sea that splits — and the script breaks.

And here, near the end of the surah, after the Pharaoh-story has been told and after Qarun has been swallowed by the earth for thinking his wealth was his own doing, Allah does something startling. He turns the camera away from those individual stories and aims it at **everyone**. Every nation. Every age. Every person who ever lived inside a fabricated certainty.

Imagine the first Muslims hearing this in Makkah. They are surrounded by a city whose entire economic and political order depends on the lie that the idols are real, that the ancestors knew best, that the Quraysh's authority is divinely sanctioned by the gods at the Kaʿbah. The Muslims are mocked daily. They are told: *bring your proof.* They are told: *what evidence do you have that there is only one God?* They are told: *we have inherited this from our fathers, that is our evidence.*

And then this ayah descends. And it tells those mocked Muslims: there is a Day coming when the question will be turned. When the demand "bring your proof" will not come from a sneering Qurayshi nobleman but from the Lord of the worlds. And on that Day, the fabricated reality — the whole atmospheric pressure of lies that everyone has been breathing — **will simply walk away from them**.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir, opening his commentary on this verse, identifies the scene with a single word: توبيخ — a public rebuke. Not a quiet judicial proceeding. Not a private accounting. A confrontation before all the witnesses of history, in which those who worshipped fabricated partners are called forward, the prophets are brought to testify, and the fabrications of a lifetime are exposed before every soul that has ever lived. The scale of the audience is part of what the ayah is doing. The humiliation is not private.

Here is what we will earn together: the verb Allah uses for how He brings the witness forward carries a weight in the language that we will sit with carefully. And the verb He uses for what happens to the lies people built their lives on is not the verb of forgetting or losing. It is the verb of **wandering** — the lies themselves get lost and cannot find their way back to the one who made them.

Two verbs. One ayah. Both reveal something about the architecture of the Last Day that nothing else in the language could have shown.

Let's walk in.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Nazaʿnā* — The Verb of Deliberate Summons

The ayah opens with one word that already does enormous work: **wa nazaʿnā** — "and We will draw forth." Most translations render this as "We will bring," "We will produce," "We will summon." All are reaching for the same action. The Arabic has its own specific weight.

Before we open this word, let's teach ourselves a small thing about Arabic. Arabic verbs come from three-letter roots, and the root carries a physical, sensory meaning that all the variations of the verb retain. The root behind *nazaʿnā* is **n-z-ʿ** (ن-ز-ع). In the classical lexicons, this root documents a semantic range running from pulling and drawing out to presenting and producing with resolve. You see it in the Quran for the angels who draw souls at death — *wa-n-nāziʿāt gharqā* — and you encounter it in the classical Arabic idiom *nazaʿa bi-ḥujja* — someone producing their proof-argument with authority, presenting their case with force.

This semantic range matters because al-Tabari, the earliest and most exhaustive of the classical commentators, renders وَنَزَعْنَا here primarily as **أحضرنا** — "We brought forth, We presented." This is a word of deliberate, authoritative summoning — purposeful, commanding. Al-Tabari himself then notes a second register that the verb carries: *nazaʿa* in Arabic includes the sense of forcefully producing a case, as in *nazaʿa bi-ḥujja* — presenting a witness or argument with resolve. Both readings — the authoritative bringing-forth, the forceful production of a witness to speak — converge on the same scene: a witness drawn from the gathered mass of assembled humanity by divine will and placed before the nations to testify.

This is not the verb of a polite bailiff calling a name from a list. It is the verb of divine authority reaching into the assembled generations and drawing out, with intention and command, the one who will now speak.

Now look at who that person is. Across all the classical sources, the identity of the shahīd is unambiguous and consistent. Mujahid's report, preserved by Ibn Kathir, states plainly: *"This means a Messenger."* Al-Tabari transmits from Qatada on an authenticated chain: *"Its witness is its prophet — who witnesses that he delivered the message of his Lord."*

[PAUSE]

Pause at Qatada's specific phrasing, because it carries more than it first appears to. The prophet does not merely testify: *I was sent.* He testifies: *I delivered.* The content of the testimony is the completion of the prophetic mission — every word conveyed, every warning sounded, every mercy offered. The path was shown in full. The hujja was established in the dunya. The message reached them.

Think about what this means for the weight of the summoning. This prophet spent his life trying to save these people. He loved them with a love the Quran elsewhere describes as consuming — *ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum* — eager for them, grieved by their hardship. He did not want them to end up here. He would rather still be pleading for them. And now Allah draws him forward with the authority of divine command and asks him to confirm what he and they both already know: *I delivered.*

This is why, even in a register of authoritative presenting, the summoning carries what it carries. The prophet would not come forward freely, because his love has not stopped. He is brought forward by something greater than his love — by the weight of divine will doing what prophetic mercy alone cannot. This is not the Day of pleading. This is the Day of weighing.

And notice: Allah does this Himself. **Nazaʿnā** is first person plural — *We* draw forth. Not angels performing a bureaucratic function. The Sovereign Himself reaches into assembled humanity and brings forward the witness. What this says about the gravity of testimony — that only divine authority can establish the conditions for true witnessing — is the whole weight of this opening verb.

[PAUSE]

Here is what's remarkable about that. We live in a world where testimony has become cheap. People testify on social media about lives they have not lived. People testify in court about events they did not see. Witnessing has become performance. And here Allah is showing us — through one verb — that **true witnessing is so heavy that only divine authority can produce it.**

The prophets — the most truthful of creation — are brought forward by Allah Himself to give testimony about their own nations. Because what they must say, even from a prophetic heart full of love, cannot be offered freely from that love alone. It must be summoned.

[PAUSE]

And then ask yourself: if the *prophets* must be brought forward by divine will to testify, what does that say about the weight of what is about to be said? And what does it say about the people who, in this dunya, gave false testimony — about each other, about Allah, about themselves — as casually as they ordered coffee?

### Element Two: *Hātū Burhānakum* — The Reversed Demand

Now look at what Allah says after the witness is drawn forth. He says — and the word order matters — **fa qulnā hātū burhānakum**. "And We will say: bring — produce — your proof."

Let's teach a small concept first. In Arabic, the word **burhān** (بُرْهَان) is not just any kind of evidence. The classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib in his Mufradāt is precise on this — define *burhān* as a decisive, clinching proof. Not a hint. Not a suggestion. Not "some reason to think." A *burhān* is the kind of proof that, once produced, settles the matter and silences the opposing argument permanently.

This is the word Allah chooses.

Now look at the verb *hātū.* In Arabic this is an imperative — a command — that means "bring it here, hand it over, produce it now." It is the kind of word a parent uses when a child is hiding something behind their back. *Hātū.* Let me see it.

So the sentence reads: *Hand over your decisive, clinching proof. Right now. In My presence.*

Notice something that nearly every reader misses on first reading. Throughout this dunya, who was demanding the *burhān?* Who was the one always asking "where is your proof? Show me your evidence! Bring it!"

It was the deniers.

Go back through the Quran. Read Surah Yusuf, Surah al-Anbiyāʾ, Surah al-Naml, Surah al-Muʾminūn. Over and over the disbelievers say to the prophets: *hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "bring your proof, if you are truthful." It is the favorite weapon of the mocker. Show me. Prove it. Where's your evidence.

And here, on the Last Day, Allah takes their own weapon — the very phrase they used to humiliate the prophets — and turns it back on them.

[PAUSE]

This is a profound ironic reversal: the challenge the deniers wielded as a weapon throughout their lives is now returned to them as the question of the Judge. The words that fell from their lips so easily in the dunya — *bring your proof* — are now the words of the One before Whom proof must actually be produced. What they used as an instrument of mockery becomes the instrument of their own reckoning.

Stay with me here, because there is something even deeper.

What were they being asked to prove?

The ayah does not specify. Allah does not say "bring your proof for *what.*" The object is left open. And the absence of the object is the point.

In Arabic, when a verb that normally takes an object appears without one, the silence is often deliberate. Allah is not naming a particular claim because **the demand encompasses everything.** Prove the partner you assigned to Me. Prove the lord you imagined. Prove the ancestor whose path you said was sacred. Prove the ideology you killed for. Prove the wealth-system you said was just. Prove the worldview you said was self-evident. Prove the version of your own life that you used to justify everything you did.

*Bring all of it. Hand it over. Right now.*

And here is what is shattering. Every human being who hears this on that Day will reach behind their back, into the great folder of certainties they carried through life — the things they were *sure* of, the things they argued about, the things they fought for — and the folder will be **empty.**

Not because Allah took the proofs away. But because they were never there.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar's commentary on this moment is precise: when they are called to produce their burhān, they realize — and the verb *faʿalimū* says this is instant knowing, not gradual conclusion — that **الحجة البالغة لله عليهم**: the conclusive, complete proof belongs to Allah *against them*. Al-Muyassar's choice of البالغة is worth pausing on. This word means fully mature, fully arrived, leaving no remainder. Not a partial proof. Not a proof that awaits supplementation or appeal. A proof so complete it closes every avenue. The hujja was established in the dunya — through the prophets, through the message delivered, through every sign that was shown and denied. It has been maturing this entire time.

Notice the architecture. The witness on one side has to be brought forward by divine will because the truth is so weighty and the testimony so consequential. The burhān on the other side does not need to be brought — there is nothing to bring. One side has so much truth it cannot produce it without divine summons. The other side has so little it cannot produce anything at all.

That asymmetry — between a truth so weighty it required divine authority to establish its testimony, and a falsehood so hollow it cannot be located — is the theological architecture of this ayah.

### Element Three: *Ḍalla ʿAnhum* — When the Lies Themselves Wander Off

And now the final phrase. **Wa ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn.** "And what they used to fabricate will have wandered away from them."

This phrase has two pieces, and both reward sitting with.

Let's teach the concept first. The root *ḍ-l-l* (ض-ل-ل) in Arabic is the root of *ḍalāl* — going astray. It is the same root used for someone who is lost in the desert, someone who has wandered off the path and cannot find their way back. It is the root used in the Fātiḥa when we ask Allah not to make us of *al-ḍāllīn* — the wandering, the lost.

Now look at what Allah does. He uses this verb — the verb of getting lost in the desert — and He makes the subject of the verb not the *people*, but **what they fabricated**. The lies themselves wander off. The fabrications become the wanderers.

Stay with this image, because the analogy is doing real work.

Imagine a man in the desert who has built an entire camp around himself — tents, banners, supplies, an identity. He believes this camp is his home, his security, the place he will live forever. Now imagine that one morning he wakes up and the camp itself has stood up on its legs and walked away from him into the dunes. Not stolen by raiders. Not burned by an enemy. The camp itself simply *left*. Got lost. Wandered into the desert and disappeared over the horizon. And the man is left standing where his life used to be, with sand where his tents stood, with empty hands where his certainties were.

That is *ḍalla ʿanhum.*

[PAUSE]

In ordinary Arabic, you would expect the opposite construction. You would expect: *and they will lose what they fabricated* — meaning the people are the active party and the lies are the passive thing that gets lost. But Allah inverts it. He makes the lies the active wanderers. The lies pack up and leave. The fabrications go missing on their own.

What this invites us to consider is profound. In this dunya, we think we are in control of our worldviews. We *hold* opinions. We *carry* beliefs. We *maintain* positions. The lies are objects we own. But on the Last Day, Allah is showing us, **the lies were never really ours.** They had a life of their own. They were always going to leave us. They were waiting for the moment when their cover would be blown and they could disappear into the dunes and pretend they had never known us.

And now look at the second piece: **mā kānū yaftarūn.**

The root behind *yaftarūn* is **f-r-y** (ف-ر-ي). In the classical lexicons, this root means to cut, to slash — and in the derived verb form, it means to cut through truth with a lie, to forge something false, to fabricate with the violence of a blade through cloth. This is not the gentle word for "imagination" or "misunderstanding." *Yaftarūn* is the word for the deliberate construction of a falsehood that cuts against reality — with intention, with will.

And the form *kānū yaftarūn* — the past habitual — says: they *used to* do this. Continuously. Repeatedly. Over the span of a life. This was not a single error. It was a whole existence of ongoing forging. Morning after morning, year after year, they maintained the cut, they refreshed the lie, they kept the fabricated world alive by tending to it.

And now that world — which took a lifetime to build, which was so habitual it felt like oxygen — simply wanders off.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar closes the scene with something the reflection in this dunya is too comfortable, perhaps, to sit with fully. The departure of the fabrications does not merely leave the fabricators standing in an empty field. Al-Muyassar is direct: *"فلم ينفعهم ذلك، بل ضرَّهم وأوردهم نار جهنم."* It does not benefit them — it harms them, and it delivers them into the fire of Jahannam.

The wandering away of the lies is not a neutral event. The fabrications leave — and in leaving, they take with them the last thing these people had. The lies were what held the edifice up. When they go, the person is left exposed: before the Judge, with الحجة البالغة fully arrayed against them, and nothing — not one argument, not one recovered certainty, not one plausible alternative — remaining in their hands. The camp walked away. The folder is empty. The only proof that exists is the one that condemns.

[PAUSE]

The authentic hadith preserved in Bukhari and Muslim offers a scene that illuminates what this looks like from the Prophet's side ﷺ. On the Day of Judgment, the Prophet ﷺ sees people he recognizes being turned away from the Ḥawḍ, and he reaches toward them — and is told: *you do not know what they did after you.* The one who delivered the message, the shahīd whose testimony established the hujja, is shown the consequence of what was fabricated after his time. The ḍalāl of fabrication is not only the ḍalāl of the one who rejected the message outright. It is the ḍalāl of anyone who built a version of the deen on something other than what was delivered — who took the clear path that was shown and quietly, habitually, continuously redirected it.

*Kānū yaftarūn.* They used to.

## PART 2 — THE THEOLOGICAL ARCHITECTURE

### What This Ayah Is Doing in the Surah

Look at where this ayah sits. Surah Al-Qasas opened with Pharaoh — the supreme fabricator of history, the man who said *ana rabbukumu-l-aʿlā*, who manufactured a theology of himself and demanded it be breathed like air by an entire civilization. And the surah closes with this: every Pharaoh of every age — every person who constructed a fabricated reality and lived inside it — will stand before the One Whom Pharaoh spent his life trying to replace.

The movement of the surah is from one fabricator (Pharaoh) to all fabricators (every nation). From one courtroom scene (Pharaoh vs. Musa, before the people of Egypt) to the only courtroom that matters (every nation before its Lord, the witnesses of all human history assembled).

And the weapon the deniers always wielded — *bring your proof* — is the very sentence that opens their own undoing.

### The Shahīd as the Hinge of Justice

Classical commentators are unified that the shahīd of each nation is its prophet — not a class of upright persons, not a category, but the specific Messenger who was sent to that people. And the content of that testimony — as Qatada specified through al-Tabari — is that the prophet *delivered the message of his Lord*. That specificity is what makes the scene theologically complete.

A shahīd in Islamic legal tradition establishes that something was witnessed: that the reality is as the shahīd attests. The shahīd is the bridge between event and verdict. When the prophets testify — *I delivered the message of my Lord* — they close the gap. There is no longer any theoretical space for the claim that the message never came, that no one told them, that the path was hidden. The shahīd seals the record. The hujja becomes البالغة — complete, mature, final.

This is why the summoning of the witness is the act that makes everything else possible. Without the witness, a theoretical gap remains. With the witness drawn forth by divine will to testify to the one thing he knows best — *I delivered* — the verdict is whole.

### *Faʿalimū* — The Knowledge That Arrives Too Late

Look at one more verb the ayah contains: **faʿalimū** — "then they will know." The *fa* here is the *fa* of immediate sequence — then, at that moment, instantly. They will know in the moment of the revelation, not after deliberation, not after reviewing arguments. The knowing happens the way sudden light happens when a door is opened in a dark room.

And what they come to know, all at once, is: *anna-l-ḥaqqa lillāh* — the truth belongs to Allah. Not "some truths." Not "the truth in the religious domain." The truth. Full stop. Everything that is true is His, attributed to Him, sourced in Him. Every fragment of reality they have ever encountered was already His. They moved through a universe that was always speaking His name, and they fabricated a different language for it.

And everything they built their certainty upon that was not from Him — *ḍalla ʿanhum.* Wandered off.

[PAUSE]

Notice: they *know* this. The Quran does not say they are told this and choose to dispute it. They know it. The fabrications have left them and in the sudden silence, the knowing arrives — with perfect clarity, with the completeness of البالغة, with no remainder of doubt remaining. There is no one left to argue with. There is no court of appeal. There is only the knowledge, arriving with the force and simplicity of sunrise, that this is the truth, and that they chose otherwise.

This may be the heaviest thing in the ayah. Not the punishment. Not the public exposure before every witness of history. The *knowing*. The moment of complete clarity that arrives at the moment when clarity can no longer change anything.

## Conclusion — What This Ayah Does to Us Now

It would be a mistake to read this ayah and place it entirely in the future. Yes, it is speaking of the Last Day. But its power lies in what it does to the present.

Every one of us carries fabrications. Not necessarily lies about religion — though some of us carry those too. But fabrications about ourselves: the story of our own righteousness. The version of events in which we were always correct. The convenient theology in which Allah wants for us exactly what we want for ourselves. The slow drift away from a truth we once knew, maintained so gradually that we do not notice until the drift has become a distance.

*Wa ḍalla ʿanhum mā kānū yaftarūn.*

The lies are already wandering. In this dunya, they are still hanging around, still serviceable, still available to be picked up and used. But they are already the kind of thing that *wanders* — rootless, homeless, without substance. When the door of the Last Day opens, they will simply — as things that were never solid always do — disappear.

The question this ayah puts before us, right now, in this dunya, is not: am I a bad person? The question is: *what in my life is a fabrication?* What have I *kānū yaftarūn* — been persistently, continuously cutting together — that I am calling truth? What is the Pharaoh inside me fabricating?

Because there is a shahīd. Not just for the nations of old. The Prophet ﷺ will testify about this ummah. And his testimony, like all prophetic testimony, will be specific: *I delivered.*

What we do with the delivery — that is the question this ayah leaves open. And leaves with us.
