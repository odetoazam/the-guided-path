# The Enemy They Raised: How Allah Plants Salvation Inside the House of Destruction

---

## Introduction

Listen to these words:

> [ayah:28:8] فَٱلْتَقَطَهُۥٓ ءَالُ فِرْعَوْنَ لِيَكُونَ لَهُمْ عَدُوًّا وَحَزَنًا ۗ إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ وَهَـٰمَـٰنَ وَجُنُودَهُمَا كَانُوا۟ خَـٰطِـِٔينَ
>
> [ayah:28:9] وَقَالَتِ ٱمْرَأَتُ فِرْعَوْنَ قُرَّتُ عَيْنٍ لِّى وَلَكَ ۖ لَا تَقْتُلُوهُ عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَنفَعَنَآ أَوْ نَتَّخِذَهُۥ وَلَدًا وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ

*Fa-ltaqaṭahu ālu Firʿawn li-yakūna lahum ʿaduwwan wa ḥazanan. Inna Firʿawna wa Hāmāna wa junūdahumā kānū khāṭiʾīn.*

*Wa qālat-imraʾatu Firʿawna qurratu ʿaynin lī wa lak. Lā taqtulūhu, ʿasā an yanfaʿanā aw nattakhidhahu waladan, wa hum lā yashʿurūn.*

"So the family of Pharaoh picked him up — that he might become to them an enemy and a cause of grief. Indeed, Pharaoh and Haman and their soldiers were in the wrong. And Pharaoh's wife said: 'A coolness of the eye for me and for you. Do not kill him. Perhaps he will benefit us, or we will adopt him as a son.' And they did not perceive."

### Rebuild the world

Before we can hear what these words are doing, we have to inhabit the moment.

Egypt under Pharaoh is not a country with a bad leader. It is an apparatus. A god-king sits at its head, claiming divinity outright. Beneath him: Hāmān, the chief architect of state ideology and construction. Beneath them: the *junūd* — soldiers, intelligence officers, executioners. And one decree runs through all of it like a current: every male child born to the Banū Isrāʾīl must die. They are slaughtered in their cribs. The mothers count days. The wombs are watched.

Into this terror, one mother — *umm Mūsā* — receives an instruction from Allah Himself. The ayah just before our passage tells us: "Nurse him, and when you fear for him, cast him into the river. Do not fear and do not grieve. We will return him to you and make him among the messengers."

Imagine that command. *Put your infant in the water.*

She does it. The basket floats. And now — at the moment our ayah begins — we have an exhale. The river is doing its work. The current is carrying him. Surely Allah will deliver him to safety. Surely some compassionate household downstream — some quiet bank, some hidden place — will be the destination.

[PAUSE]

The basket comes to rest at the palace of Pharaoh.

Of all the houses on the Nile. Of all the hands that could have reached into the water. The infant Allah has chosen to dismantle Pharaoh's empire is pulled, dripping, from the river — by Pharaoh's own household.

### The Hook

The most stunning sentence in these two ayahs is not the sentence about the rescue. It is a single particle — one letter, one sound — that the Arabic places between *they picked him up* and *that he might become an enemy*.

Allah, in describing what looks like an accident of providence, uses a grammatical construction that classical Arabic reserves for something very specific. And once you see what He has done with that single letter, the entire scene tilts. The palace is no longer where Mūsā ended up. The palace is where he was sent.

Stay with me.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The *lām* that should not be there

Let's start with one of the most arresting grammatical moments in the Qurʾān.

In Arabic — as in English — when you do something for a reason, you signal that reason with a small word. In English we say *in order to*, or *so that*. In Arabic, the equivalent is the *lām* — the letter ل — prefixed to a verb in the subjunctive. Grammarians call this **lām al-taʿlīl**, the *lām of purpose*. It tells you: this action was done *in order that* this outcome would happen.

For example: *jiʾtu li-aʿlama* — "I came *in order to* learn." The *lām* tells you why I came. My intention was to learn. The learning is the *purpose*.

Now read the ayah again:

*Fa-ltaqaṭahu ālu Firʿawna **li-yakūna** lahum ʿaduwwan wa ḥazanan.*

"So the family of Pharaoh picked him up **in order that** he would become to them an enemy and a source of grief."

[PAUSE]

Notice something extraordinary. *Whose purpose is this?*

The family of Pharaoh did not pick up a baby from the river in order to raise their own destroyer. No one does that. They pulled an infant out of the water because the queen looked into the basket and her heart melted. Their *intention*, as the next ayah will spell out, was to keep him alive, to adopt him, to make him a comfort.

So whose *li-* is this? Whose purpose is being declared?

Here is what the classical grammarians point out. When the agent stated in the sentence cannot logically have intended the outcome — when the human actor would *never* have done this thing for *that* reason — the *lām* shifts function. The classical scholars name this **lām al-ʿāqiba** or **lām al-ṣayrūra**: *the lām of consequence*, the *lām of what-it-comes-to.*

The grammar is saying: *they picked him up — and the purpose of that picking-up, though they could not see it, was that he would become their enemy and their grief.*

Whose purpose, then?

Not theirs.

**Allah's.**

The *lām* in this ayah is one of the most stunning fingerprints of divine authorship in the entire Mūsā narrative. Allah is narrating a scene of human action — *they reached down, they grasped the basket, they lifted him out* — and quietly, in a single letter, He is telling you: every motion of those hands, though they thought they were obeying their own compassion, was serving a purpose written above their heads.

Sit with this for a moment.

The grammar dissolves the appearance of accident. There is no accident in this scene. There is only one Director, and the soldiers and the queen and the river and the basket are all instruments. They believe they are choosing. They are, in some sense, choosing. But the *lām al-ʿāqiba* tells you that what they did and what was *being done through them* are two different sentences — and the sentence Allah is writing is the only one that will survive.

[PAUSE]

Now look up from the text and notice what this does to your life.

Every moment you cannot understand — every door that closes that you wanted open, every door that opens that you would never have chosen, every basket that drifts to the *wrong* shore — this *lām* sits beneath it. The agents around you are pursuing their purposes. And the *li-* of consequence is quietly threading through all of them. What looks like *to them an enemy* by accident is, in the deeper grammar, *that he might become to them an enemy* by design.

This is one word. One letter, really. And it reframes the entire scene.

### Element Two: *ʿAduwwan wa ḥazanan* — the doubled fate

Now look at *what* Allah says he will become to them.

*Li-yakūna lahum ʿaduwwan wa ḥazanan.*

"That he would become to them **an enemy** and **a [cause of] grief**."

Two words. Two nouns, indefinite, side by side: *ʿaduww* — enemy. *Ḥazan* — grief, sorrow.

Pause on the pairing. Why both?

An enemy is someone who acts against you. A source of grief is something that causes you pain. They overlap, but they are not the same. You can have enemies who do not grieve you — you simply outmaneuver them. You can have grief that comes from no enemy — illness, loss, time.

Allah is telling Pharaoh's household: this child will be *both*. He will not merely oppose you in the political sense — he will not merely return one day to demand the freedom of his people. He will *grieve* you. He will lodge himself inside your house, inside your affections. The queen will love him. Pharaoh, in some narrations, will play with him as a son. And then — when that boy becomes the man who stands before Pharaoh and says *let my people go* — the grief is not just the grief of a political enemy. It is the grief of *the one you raised*. The one you fed. The one whose first steps you watched.

This is the deeper edge of *ḥazan*. It is the grief that only comes from intimacy.

Notice what the grammar is doing. By placing *ʿaduww* and *ḥazan* together, indefinite, Allah is showing you that the very same act of compassion that pulled the basket from the river — *out of love* — is the act that plants the *grief* inside the palace. The enemy and the sorrow enter the house through the same door. The door called *we love this child*.

[PAUSE]

There is a terrible justice in this. Pharaoh built a kingdom on the killing of children. He was *the source* of *ḥazan* for every mother in Banū Isrāʾīl. Every woman who lost a son was a woman drowned in grief because of him.

And Allah's reply, written into the grammar of the universe before Pharaoh ever knew it, is this: *the grief you sowed will be raised in your own bedroom and called your son.*

You will grieve, Pharaoh. Not as the abstract pain of a tyrant who loses a war. You will grieve as a *father* grieves. The grief you handed to others will be handed back to you in the same currency, by the same hand.

This is not a punchline Allah is delivering. This is the architecture of His justice. *ʿAduwwan wa ḥazanan* is not redundancy. It is the precision of the reckoning.

### Element Three: *Wa hum lā yashʿurūn* — the closing seal

Now look at the very last words of the second ayah.

*Wa hum lā yashʿurūn.*

"And they did not perceive."

The verb is from the root *sh-ʿ-r*. In Arabic this is a particular kind of knowing. It is not the cold knowledge of facts — that's a different root, *ʿ-l-m*. *Shaʿara* is to feel something with your senses, to pick up on it, to be aware. It is the verb you use when you say *I sensed something was wrong* — that low, intuitive register beneath rational knowledge.

And it is in the present tense. Not *they did not perceive* (past). The grammar is *they are not perceiving*. Right now. In the scene. As the queen is saying *qurratu ʿaynin lī wa lak* — a comfort of the eye for me and for you — *they are not picking up* what is actually happening.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Pharaoh is not a stupid man. He is the head of the most sophisticated intelligence state in the ancient world. He has informants in every village. He has astrologers who have *already told him*, in the wider tradition, that a child from Banū Isrāʾīl will end his reign — which is *why* he ordered the slaughter in the first place.

And here is that child. In his wife's arms. In his own court.

And he does not feel it.

This is the most chilling phrase in the passage. Not because Pharaoh is uninformed. Because the verb *yashʿur* — sensing, intuition, that low animal alertness — *that very faculty* has been turned off. The man who has built a surveillance state to detect *exactly this child* is holding the child and feeling nothing.

What has Allah done to him? Allah has not blinded his eyes — he sees a baby. Allah has not stopped his ears — he hears his wife's plea. Allah has shut down the deeper register. The *shuʿūr*. The *sense*.

And notice the pronoun: *wa **hum** lā yashʿurūn.* **They** — plural. Pharaoh. Hāmān. The soldiers. The whole apparatus of perception. The entire intelligence machine of the empire is in the room with this baby, and none of them can feel what is in the room.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this single phrase is saying about reality. The most dangerous condition a human being can be in is not ignorance. It is the *combination of confidence and unawareness* — the state of being completely certain you have the situation under control, while the situation is, in fact, unfolding around you on a level your senses cannot reach.

Pharaoh, at this moment, *feels in control*. He has decreed. The decree is being carried out. His wife wants a son; she can have one. He sees no threat. The grammar tells you what he cannot see.

The pairing is exquisite. The ayah opened with the *lām al-ʿāqiba* — *Allah's purpose is being served*. The ayah closes with *lā yashʿurūn* — *and they cannot feel it*. The two halves frame the scene. Above: a hidden purpose. Below: a numbed perception. And between them, an infant in a basket, becoming what he was sent to become.

### Bridge

So look at what these three linguistic moves have done to the scene.

The *lām al-ʿāqiba* told us: this is not an accident. There is a sentence being written above what they think they are doing.

The pairing of *ʿaduwwan wa ḥazanan* told us: the very act of love is the entry point for the grief — divine justice is not bolted onto reality afterwards; it is *threaded into* the very motions of human compassion and human fear.

The closing *lā yashʿurūn* told us: those who think they are in control are precisely those who cannot feel what is happening to them.

Now let's ask what this passage reveals not just about Pharaoh — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The places you fear are sometimes the places Allah is sending you

Begin not with the ayah. Begin with a feeling you know.

There is a particular kind of distress that comes when the door you wanted closes and the door you were *afraid of* opens. You prayed for the safe outcome. You prayed for the kind shelter, the gentle landing. And instead the river of your life delivered you to the very shore you were running from. The job you didn't want. The city that scared you. The diagnosis. The conversation you dreaded. The household that holds you now — that is not the household you would have chosen.

Most people, in that moment, conclude one of two things. Either Allah did not hear them. Or the world is random and dua is theater.

Now bring the ayah into that feeling.

Umm Mūsā placed her son in a river. Whatever she prayed for, in her heart, in that moment, it was *not* "let him drift to Pharaoh's palace." That is the one outcome a mother in her position could not have prayed for. That is the worst-case scenario. That is the river delivering her son to the man who would kill her son.

And that is exactly where Allah sent him.

[PAUSE]

The mother could not have known — and this is the heart of it — that the *only place in Egypt the decree of execution could not reach* was inside Pharaoh's own palace. Every other household was under surveillance. Every other shore was watched. Every kind fisherman, every quiet refuge — all of them were inside the kill-zone of Pharaoh's order.

But Pharaoh does not search his own bedroom.

The safest place for Mūsā in all of Egypt was the lap of the man who wanted him dead.

So when we say "Allah delivered him to safety," we have to widen what we mean by safety. Allah did not deliver him to the shore the mother imagined. Allah delivered him to the *only shore the decree could not follow him onto*. And from the mother's perspective, on the bank of the river that morning, that shore would have looked like the worst possible destination.

This is one of the deepest patterns in the Qurʾān, and it is rarely named explicitly. **Sometimes the place you most fear is the only place Allah can put you that is actually safe.** Because the danger you imagine is rarely the deepest danger. And the shelter you imagine is rarely the deepest shelter. Allah knows the architecture of the threat. You only know its silhouette.

Now extend this into your life.

The door that closed on you — was it a closure, or was it a deflection away from a kill-zone you couldn't see? The path you didn't want to take — was it a punishment, or was it the only path that ran underneath the radar of a destruction you didn't know was tracking you?

This does not mean every disaster is a hidden gift. The Qurʾān does not flatten suffering into optimism. Pharaoh's household *is* hostile. The palace *is* a frightening place for a Jewish infant. The fear is not unfounded. *And yet* — Allah's wisdom is operating on a layer beneath the fear. The fear sees one floor of the building. Allah sees the foundation.

The practical application is uncomfortable. When the river of your life delivers you to a shore you did not choose, the Qurʾānic instinct is not "this is wrong, I have been abandoned." The instinct is "Let me ask why *here*. Let me ask what kill-zone this shore is actually outside of. Let me ask what the *lām al-ʿāqiba* over this scene might be." Not as a way of suppressing grief — grief is real, *ḥazan* is real, Allah names it directly in this very ayah — but as a way of widening your sense of the picture.

Pharaoh's palace looked like the death of Mūsā. It was the cradle of Mūsā.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The faculty of *shuʿūr* — and what guards it

Now turn from Mūsā to the people in the room with him.

There are *two* responses to the baby in that palace, and the Qurʾān is meticulous about distinguishing them.

The wife of Pharaoh — Āsiyah, in the prophetic tradition — looks at the child and her heart opens. She says *qurratu ʿaynin lī wa lak*: a coolness of the eye, for me and for you. Stop and feel this idiom. *Qurrat ʿayn* — "coolness of the eye." In a hot desert language, *coolness* is the opposite of the burning, restless gaze. To say someone is the *coolness* of your eye is to say: my searching is over when I look at you. The fever of looking is replaced by rest. *You are where my eyes can finally cool.*

That is what she says about an infant. From a slave caste. Floating in a basket. In a household where his existence is a crime.

And then Pharaoh — and Hāmān, and the soldiers, *junūdahumā* — they are in the same room. They see the same baby. They hear the same plea. And the ayah closes with *wa hum lā yashʿurūn*. They are not perceiving.

What is the difference?

The grammar of the verb *shaʿara* gives us a clue. *Shuʿūr* is the deepest layer of perception — the layer beneath calculation, beneath strategy, beneath self-interest. It is the layer where the heart *senses* what is actually happening. Āsiyah, looking at the child, was in that layer. Pharaoh, looking at the same child, was not.

Why?

Because the layer of *shuʿūr* is not given freely to everyone. It is guarded by what you have done with your previous moments. The ayah just before ours, in many readings, names Pharaoh and his people as *khāṭiʾīn* — habitual wrongdoers, *deliberate* sinners. That word, *khāṭiʾ*, is not the same as someone who slips. It is someone whose error has become a stance. Whose wrongdoing has become a posture. And the consequence of that posture, the Qurʾān is telling us right here, is the shutdown of *shuʿūr*. The intuitive antenna stops working.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most important insights in the Qurʾān about the inner life, and it is hidden inside a four-word phrase.

**Sin is not just an offense. Sin is sensory damage.**

Every act of *khaṭaʾ* — every deliberate, settled wrongdoing — clouds the *shuʿūr*. Not the *ʿilm*; you can still know facts. But the *shuʿūr* — the felt sense of what is actually unfolding in the room — that faculty dims. Until eventually you can be holding the very thing that will undo you, and feel only its warmth.

And conversely, *taqwā* — the watchful awareness of Allah — is not just moral cleanliness. It is what *keeps the antenna online*. The Qurʾān tells us elsewhere: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū in tattaqū-llāha yajʿal lakum furqānan* — if you have *taqwā*, He will give you a *furqān*, a faculty of discernment. That is the *shuʿūr* that Pharaoh has lost and that Āsiyah, in this moment, still has.

Now bring this home.

Every one of us has had moments where we walked into a room and *felt* something — without being able to name it. A meeting that was about to go wrong. A friendship that was beginning. A wrong path we were about to take. That feeling — that low-frequency signal — is the *shuʿūr*. It is one of the most precious instruments Allah has given to a believer.

It does not survive on its own. It is calibrated by how you live. By the small choices. By whether you let your eyes wander to what is haram. By whether your tongue protects or wounds. By whether your night has any quiet in it, or whether it is filled, every minute, with noise that drowns out the signal.

Pharaoh did not lose his *shuʿūr* in a single moment. He lost it across years of *khaṭaʾ*. By the time the basket reached his palace, his perception had been so attenuated that he could not feel his own destruction being placed in his lap.

The practical application is this. When you notice your *shuʿūr* getting quieter — when the obvious things stop landing, when warnings stop registering, when you sit in clearly wrong company and feel nothing — that is not a peace to celebrate. That is a *symptom*. Something is dimming the antenna. Allah is not punishing you by removing your *shuʿūr*; you are slowly insulating yourself from your own perception, one habit at a time.

The cure is the opposite of what Pharaoh did. The protection of *shuʿūr* is the careful, daily refusal of *khaṭaʾ*. Not perfection. Refusal. The small no, repeated. The Qurʾān, opened. The eyes, lowered. The tongue, restrained. Each of these is an act of *ṣayāna* — guardianship — of the inner antenna.

Because somewhere, eventually, a basket will float into your life. And the only question that will matter is: can you still feel what is in it?

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the architecture of these two ayahs.

The opening: *fa-ltaqaṭahu ālu Firʿawn* — Pharaoh's family picks him up.
The hinge: *li-yakūna lahum ʿaduwwan wa ḥazanan* — that he might become enemy and grief.
The verdict on the agents: *inna Firʿawna wa Hāmāna wa junūdahumā kānū khāṭiʾīn* — Pharaoh, Hāmān, and their armies were sinners.

Then a quiet shift. The voice of the queen: *qurratu ʿaynin lī wa lak* — coolness of the eye for me and for you. Her plea: *lā taqtulūhu* — don't kill him. Her hope: *ʿasā an yanfaʿanā aw nattakhidhahu waladan* — perhaps he will benefit us, or we will adopt him.

The closing: *wa hum lā yashʿurūn* — and they did not perceive.

Look at the symmetry. The passage opens with *Allah's purpose* (the *lām al-ʿāqiba*) and closes with *their absence of perception*. In between, sandwiched, are the human voices: the verdict on Pharaoh, the words of his wife. The frame is divine. The center is human. And the human center — even the *good* human voice, Āsiyah's voice — is unaware of the larger frame that holds it.

Even Āsiyah, the believer in this scene, does not know what she is doing. She thinks she is adopting a child. She is sheltering a prophet. She thinks she is acquiring a son. She is being given the *qurratu ʿayn* that will, generations later, become her ticket into Paradise — *Rabbi-bni lī ʿindaka baytan fi-l-Jannah* — when she prays to be saved from Pharaoh and his works. She does not know any of this yet. She is just looking at a baby and feeling her heart open.

This is the architecture: a divine purpose, sealed at both ends, with human beings — both the wicked and the righteous — moving through the middle with limited sight.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The *lām al-ʿāqiba* is the grammatical fingerprint of the truth that *places you fear can be places He is sending you*. Because what looks like an accidental landing is, in the deeper grammar, a placed arrival.

The pairing of *ʿaduwwan wa ḥazanan* is the linguistic shape of how *divine justice threads through human affections*. The same hands that lifted the basket out of love are the hands that will, years later, tremble at the staff that splits the sea.

And *lā yashʿurūn* is the verb-form of the theme of *shuʿūr* — the warning that the inner antenna can be dimmed by the slow accumulation of *khaṭaʾ*, until a man holds his own undoing in his lap and feels only warmth.

One ayah. Two ayahs, really. And inside them — a whole epistemology of how Allah operates inside history, and how human beings either feel it or fail to.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, in my life, did Allah deliver me to a shore I did not choose — and have I asked yet what kill-zone that shore was outside of?

2. What is dimming my *shuʿūr* right now? What small *khaṭaʾ* has become a posture in my life, slowly insulating me from what Allah is doing in the room?

3. When I look at the people closest to me — my spouse, my children, my parents — can I see them with the *qurrat ʿayn* gaze of Āsiyah? Or has the searching, hot, restless eye of Pharaoh become my default?

4. If a basket floated into my life tomorrow — a difficulty I did not order, an opportunity I did not seek — would my first instinct be to control it, or to read it?

5. What is the *li-* of consequence over this season of my life that I cannot yet see — but that I trust is there?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the river of your life delivers you to the shore you most feared, the grammar of the Qurʾān whispers that *the shore was sent*, the *purpose was not yours*, and the only question left is whether your *shuʿūr* is still alive enough to feel it.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this ayah with a theory of providence. Leave it with a discipline of perception. Tonight, before you sleep, sit with one place in your life where the river dropped you off where you did not want to be. Do not try to rewrite the story. Just say to Allah: *Yā Rabb, I do not see the lām yet. Help me trust that it is there. And keep my shuʿūr alive enough to feel You when the basket arrives.*

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who placed Mūsā in the cradle of his enemy because You knew that was the only safe place — place us in the shelters You choose for us, even when we cannot recognize them as shelter. Protect our *shuʿūr* from the slow dimming of *khaṭaʾ*, and let our eyes find rest, like Āsiyah's, in the gifts You quietly send into our lives. And on the day when every hidden *lām* is revealed, let us be among those who said *we trusted You* — not among those who did not perceive.

Āmīn.
