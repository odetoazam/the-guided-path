# The Asymmetry of the Scales
## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 84

---

## Introduction

**The Arabic:**

> [ayah:28:84] مَن جَآءَ بِٱلْحَسَنَةِ فَلَهُۥ خَيْرٌ مِّنْهَا ۖ وَمَن جَآءَ بِٱلسَّيِّئَةِ فَلَا يُجْزَى ٱلَّذِينَ عَمِلُوا۟ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ إِلَّا مَا كَانُوا۟ يَعْمَلُونَ

**Transliteration:**

*Man jāʾa bi-l-ḥasanati fa-lahu khayrun minhā, wa man jāʾa bi-s-sayyiʾati fa-lā yujzā lladhīna ʿamilū s-sayyiʾāti illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn.*

**Translation:**

"Whoever comes with a good deed will have something better than it. And whoever comes with an evil deed — those who did evil deeds will not be recompensed except for what they used to do."

---

### Where We Are in the Surah

To stand in this ayah, you need to know where you are standing.

Surah Al-Qasas — "The Stories" — has spent its first two-thirds telling you about Musa. The infant in the river. The fugitive in Madyan. The shepherd who became a prophet. The man who stood in front of Pharaoh.

And then, just when you think the surah is finished, it pivots. It introduces a new character. Not a prophet. Not a king. Just a man. **Qarun.**

Qarun was from Musa's own people. And Allah gave him treasures so vast that "the keys of his wealth would have weighed down a band of strong men." His own people warned him: don't be arrogant. Don't forget the One who gave you this. He answered with one of the most chilling sentences in the Quran:

*"I was only given this because of knowledge I possess."*

I earned this. I deserve this. This is mine.

[PAUSE]

And then the earth opened, and swallowed him, and his house, and his wealth.

The dust has barely settled on Qarun's grave when our ayah arrives. The crowd that envied him yesterday is whispering "*wayka-ʾanna llāha yabsuṭu r-rizqa li-man yashāʾu min ʿibādihī wa yaqdir*" — *as if* Allah expands provision for whomever He wills. They are realizing, in real time, that they were looking at the wrong scoreboard.

And then Allah speaks. Not about Qarun anymore. About the scoreboard itself.

### The Hook

Listen to the shape of this ayah. Two halves. Two clauses. Perfectly parallel — and then, suddenly, **not parallel at all.**

"Whoever comes with a good deed" — and the sentence completes neatly.
"Whoever comes with an evil deed" — and the sentence **breaks.**

Allah does not finish the second half the way the first half was finished. Something in the grammar refuses to balance. Something in the syntax tilts.

We are about to find out that the scales of Allah are not the scales we think they are. And that the way they *fail to balance* is the entire mercy of this religion.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to walk into this ayah through three doors. Each door is a single linguistic feature. And each feature, on its own, would be enough to sit with for an hour.

### Element One: *Jāʾa bi-* — "Comes With"

#### The concept before the word

In every language, there is a difference between *doing* something and *bringing* something.

If I tell you "I did my homework," I am describing an action that happened in the past. It exists in time. It is finished.

If I tell you "I brought my homework," I am describing something I am **carrying with me right now**. The action created an object. And the object is in my hand.

This distinction exists in Arabic with even more force. Arabic has the verb *ʿamila* — to do, to work, to perform. It also has the verb *jāʾa bi-* — literally, "to come *with*." To arrive carrying something.

These are not synonyms.

#### The word itself

Now look at what Allah says:

*Man **jāʾa** bi-l-ḥasanati...*
*Man **jāʾa** bi-s-sayyiʾati...*

He does not say *man ʿamila ḥasanatan* — "whoever did a good deed." He says *man jāʾa bi-l-ḥasanati* — "whoever **comes with** a good deed."

Stay with this. Allah is describing a scene. He is describing the Day of Judgment. And on that Day, your deeds are not memories. They are not stories you tell. They are not items on a résumé.

**They are things you carry.**

The verb *jāʾa* (root: *j-y-ʾ*, "to come, to arrive") combined with the preposition *bi-* ("with") creates the meaning: to bring, to arrive bearing. The Day of Judgment, in this grammar, is a destination — and you walk in carrying what you brought.

#### Sit with this image

Imagine a person walking toward a courthouse. In one hand, a heavy bag. In the other hand, another heavy bag. They are not arriving empty. They are not arriving with their identity, their reputation, their following. They are arriving with **what they did, made present and visible and weighable.**

The deeds you thought you "did and forgot" — Allah is telling you they were never forgotten. They became luggage. They walk to the meeting with you.

Notice something — this same construction appears in Surah An-Naml, just one surah before this one, in ayahs 89 and 90. Same verb. Same preposition. Same scene. The Quran is telling us, again and again: **deeds don't dissolve into the past. They accompany you into the future.**

[PAUSE]

#### What this single feature reveals

Before we have even examined what *kind* of deed, or what *kind* of reward, the grammar has already done something to us. It has converted abstract morality into physical weight. It has made the Day of Judgment a *scene of arrival*, not a scene of memory.

You are not what you say. You are not what you intended. **You are what you are carrying when you walk through the door.**

### Element Two: *Khayrun Minhā* — "Better Than It"

#### The concept before the word

In every transaction, there are three possible relationships between what you give and what you get.

You can get **less** than what you gave. (You were robbed.)
You can get **equal** to what you gave. (You were treated fairly.)
You can get **more** than what you gave. (You were treated generously.)

Justice — *ʿadl* — lives in the second category. Fairness, equivalence, balance. The scale should be level. What you put in is what you should get out.

That is the universal moral instinct of every human society. It is the foundation of every legal system. It is the meaning of the phrase "an eye for an eye."

Now hold that idea in your mind while we read the ayah.

#### The word itself

*Man jāʾa bi-l-ḥasanati **fa-lahu khayrun minhā**.*

"Whoever comes with a good deed — **then for him is something better than it.**"

*Khayrun* — better. *Minhā* — than it.

Notice what Allah did **not** say. He did not say *fa-lahu mithluhā* — "he will have its equivalent." He did not say *fa-lahu jazāʾuhā* — "he will have its recompense." He said *khayrun minhā*. **Something better than it.**

#### Stay with this

You showed up at the door of the courthouse carrying one good deed. A small act of kindness. A moment of patience. A sincere prayer in the dark.

You handed it in.

And what comes back across the counter is not the same thing. It is not a receipt that matches what you deposited. **It is something better than what you brought.**

The verse does not even tell you *how much* better. The word *khayrun* is left unrestricted. It could be ten times better. It could be seven hundred times better, as Surah Al-Baqarah suggests in another place. It could be beyond your imagination, as Allah hints elsewhere when He says *fa-lā taʿlamu nafsun mā ukhfiya lahum min qurrati aʿyun* — "no soul knows what has been hidden for them of comfort to the eyes."

The reward is **uncapped.**

#### The composite picture from the tradition

The classical mufassirūn (Ibn Kathīr, al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, and others, drawing on the broader Quranic corpus) all converged on this point: when Allah says *khayrun minhā*, He is describing a transaction that violates the law of equivalence. Some explained it as ten-fold multiplication, citing Surah Al-Anʿām 160. Some described it as the disproportionate generosity of a King who does not need to be fair the way humans are fair. Some pointed to the fact that the good deed itself was only possible because Allah enabled it — so even the "input" you brought to the scale was already His gift.

These are not three different opinions. They are three angles on the same reality. The King is not bound by your math. **The exchange rate is His mercy, not your effort.**

#### What this single feature reveals

Stay here for a moment, because this is one of the most important things to understand about the God you are dealing with.

He is **not fair the way you are fair.**

If He were fair the way humans are fair, the best you could ever hope for is *mithluhā* — getting back what you put in. Allah would say "you gave me a prayer, here is one prayer's worth of reward." Even-handed. Symmetric. Just.

But Allah is not just *adl*. Allah is also *karīm*. He is *al-Wahhāb*. He is the One whose generosity is not constrained by the rules of bookkeeping.

And so the first half of this ayah is telling you: **the scale is rigged in your favor.**

### Element Three: The Grammatical Asymmetry — *Illā Mā Kānū Yaʿmalūn*

This is the door I want you to walk through most carefully. Because this is where the architecture of the ayah does its most stunning work.

#### The concept before the word

Let me show you something first.

In careful prose — in Arabic or in English — a parallel sentence carries a parallel weight. If I say "whoever does good gets X, and whoever does evil gets Y," the listener expects X and Y to be the same *kind* of thing. Same grammar. Same shape. Otherwise the sentence feels off-balance.

Now, in Arabic rhetoric, an *intentional break* in parallelism is one of the most powerful signals a writer can give. When a sentence *should* be balanced but isn't — when a writer deliberately disrupts the symmetry — every classical Arab listener knows: **something is being said in the silence.**

Hold onto that. Because Allah is about to break parallelism on purpose.

#### The word itself

Look at the two halves of the ayah side by side:

> *Man jāʾa bi-l-ḥasanati fa-lahu khayrun minhā.*
> *Whoever comes with a good deed, for him is something better than it.*

> *Wa man jāʾa bi-s-sayyiʾati fa-lā yujzā lladhīna ʿamilū s-sayyiʾāti illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn.*
> *And whoever comes with an evil deed — those who did evil deeds will not be recompensed except for what they used to do.*

The first half is short. Clean. Direct. Six words.

The second half is long. Heavy. It loops back on itself. It restates. It almost stutters.

**Why?**

#### Notice the iltifāt — the shift of address

The first half starts with "*man jāʾa*" — "whoever comes" — singular. The pronoun is *lahu* — "for **him**." A single soul.

The second half starts the same way — "*wa man jāʾa*" — "whoever comes" — singular. But then, suddenly, the sentence stops talking about the singular *him* and switches to a plural: *lladhīna ʿamilū s-sayyiʾāti* — "**those who did** evil deeds."

The grammar shifts from one person to a group. The personal "him" disappears into an anonymous "they."

Stay with that for a moment. When the good deed is reckoned, the soul is named — *lahu*, **for him**, personally, intimately. When the evil deed is reckoned, the soul dissolves into a category — *those who did*. As if Allah is, even in the grammar, **declining to look the sinner in the face the way He looks at the doer of good.**

This is *iltifāt* — the rhetorical shift the classical balāghah scholars treasured. The shift in pronoun is not a stylistic accident. The personal *him* is what receives mercy. The categorical *they* is what receives accountability.

#### And then the deepest break

Look at what Allah does **not** say in the second half.

In the first half: *khayrun minhā* — "**better** than it."

In the second half, by perfect symmetry, what should come is the opposite: *aswaʾu minhā* — "**worse** than it." Or at the very least, *sharrun minhā*. Or even just *jazāʾuhā* — "its equivalent."

That is what symmetry demands. The first half multiplied upward; the second half should multiply downward. The first half gave more than was earned; the second half should give more punishment than was earned.

**But Allah refuses.**

Instead, He says: *fa-lā yujzā lladhīna ʿamilū s-sayyiʾāti illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn.* "Those who did evil deeds will not be recompensed **except for what they used to do.**"

Read that again. Slowly.

They will not be recompensed **except for what they actually did.**

The word *illā* is the great exceptive particle of Arabic — "except, only." It restricts. It caps. It draws a ceiling.

And the ceiling Allah is drawing here is this: **the evil deed gets back exactly what it gave. Nothing more.**

[PAUSE]

#### Sit here. This is the heart of the ayah.

Do you see what just happened?

Allah broke the parallelism on purpose. He set up the listener to expect symmetric multiplication: good multiplies *up*, evil multiplies *down*. That is the math of human justice. That is what the structure of the sentence was promising.

And then He pulled the floor out from under that math.

The good deed is multiplied without limit.
The evil deed is capped at exactly what it was.

**The scales are rigged in your favor on both sides.**

If you do good, Allah inflates the reward. If you do evil, Allah refuses to inflate the punishment. Your evil gets only what your evil earned — not a single grain more.

#### What the tradition says about this

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this and parallel ayahs (especially 6:160 — *man jāʾa bi-l-ḥasanati fa-lahu ʿashru amthālihā wa man jāʾa bi-s-sayyiʾati fa-lā yujzā illā mithlahā*), notes that the principle is established across the Quran: the good deed at minimum is ten-fold, the evil deed at maximum is one-for-one. The classical scholars built an entire theology of divine generosity on this asymmetry. They called it *faḍl* — the unearned excess — versus *ʿadl* — the strict balance.

Allah, in His justice, **owes** you the strict balance.
Allah, in His generosity, **gives** you the unearned excess.

He hands you both at once. He gives you the multiplication on the side of mercy. He withholds the multiplication on the side of wrath.

And He places this — *this whole architecture* — right after the story of a man who thought the math of the universe was: *I worked, therefore I deserve.* Qarun's math was *mithluhā*. Allah's math is *khayrun minhā* on one side and *illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* on the other. Qarun lived by the wrong arithmetic. The surah is teaching us a different one.

#### What this single feature reveals

The ayah is not parallel because **reality is not parallel.** Good and evil do not weigh the same on Allah's scale. They were never going to. The grammar is the theology. The asymmetric sentence is the asymmetric mercy.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have walked through three doors.

The first door — *jāʾa bi-* — taught us that deeds are luggage. You don't *remember* your deeds on the Day; you *carry* them.

The second door — *khayrun minhā* — taught us that the King does not trade by equivalence. He trades by generosity. What you hand Him He multiplies before handing back.

The third door — the broken parallelism, the capped punishment — taught us that the asymmetry of the ayah's grammar is the asymmetry of Allah's mercy. He inflates the good; He refuses to inflate the evil.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Why You Cannot Calculate Your Way Into Despair

Let me start somewhere the ayah is not, and we will arrive at the ayah from inside a feeling you already know.

There is a kind of despair that wears a religious mask. It looks pious. It sounds humble. It says: *"I have done too much wrong. I have crossed a line. I have built up a debt I cannot pay back. There is no point trying anymore."*

This despair feels honest. It feels like accurate accounting. The person doing the math is saying: "Look at the column on the left — all the years of neglect, the prayers missed, the harm caused. Look at the column on the right — a few patchy attempts at goodness. The two columns don't balance. The case is closed."

This is the math of Qarun, but inverted. Qarun's math said "I have so much credit, I don't need Allah." Despair's math says "I have so much debt, Allah won't take me."

**Both are the wrong math.**

Both treat Allah as a bookkeeper who works by *mithluhā* — strict equivalence. Both forget that Allah works by *khayrun minhā* on one side and *illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* on the other.

Now bring the ayah back in.

The ayah is telling the despairing soul: when you walk in with one good deed — *one* — it does not come back to you as one. It comes back as something better. Multiplied. Inflated. Unrecognizable from what you handed in.

And the evil you fear — all those bags of evil you have been carrying for years — when they hit the counter, they will not be multiplied against you. They will be weighed at exactly their weight. Not a grain more.

This means **the math of redemption is not what despair tells you it is.**

A single sincere act of *ḥasanah* — one prayer, one moment of patience, one tear in the dark, one act of restraint — does not enter the scale at face value. It enters multiplied. Meanwhile your sins, however many, enter at face value. The asymmetry means: you can never accumulate enough sin to outpace mercy if you are still showing up with even a few sincere goods.

The doors of return are not locked. The exchange rate at those doors is not the one despair has been telling you about.

#### The application

Tomorrow, when shayṭān whispers to you that it is too late, that you have done too much, that you cannot come back — answer him with the grammar of this ayah.

Tell him: *The first half of the ayah is short and unbounded. The second half is long and capped. The architecture of the sentence is the architecture of Allah's mercy. I am not coming back to a fair God. I am coming back to a generous one.*

Then do one good deed. Just one. Watch what Allah does with it.

### Theme Two: The Difference Between Justice and Mercy — and Why Allah Holds Both at Once

Let me tell you about something that troubles a lot of people about religion.

People wonder: how can a God be both **just** and **merciful** at the same time? Aren't these in tension? If He is just, He should give every deed its exact due. If He is merciful, He overrides the strict accounting. So which one is He?

Atheists raise this as an objection. Believers wrestle with it in quiet moments. Theologians have written volumes about it.

This ayah is, in twenty-four words, the answer.

The answer is: **Allah is just in the direction of wrath, and generous in the direction of mercy.**

He is *ʿādil* — perfectly just — when it comes to punishment. The evil deed receives *illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — only what was done. The scale is exact. No inflation, no exaggeration, no excess. A speck of injustice in the punishment would itself be injustice; so the punishment is calibrated to the deed with the precision of a jeweler's balance.

He is *faḍīl* — overflowing in generosity — when it comes to reward. The good deed receives *khayrun minhā* — something better than it. The scale is *not* exact. It is intentionally tipped. Because generosity that is exact is not generosity at all.

These are not in tension. **They are different functions performed at different counters of the same court.**

Look at the human analogy. A judge who is strict in punishment but generous in pardon is not contradicting himself. He is exhibiting two virtues that complement rather than collide. He is being *exactly fair* in what he extracts from the guilty, and *more than fair* in what he gives to the deserving.

This is the kind of King you are dealing with.

Now look back at the surah. Look at how Allah dealt with Pharaoh — the strict ʿadl of the drowning. Look at how Allah dealt with Musa — the overflowing faḍl of the prophethood, the wife, the staff, the survival. Look at how Allah dealt with Qarun — strict ʿadl, the swallowing earth, the exact return of his arrogance. Look at how Allah dealt with the believing soul standing in the crowd watching all of this — the overflowing faḍl of guidance through the surah itself.

The whole surah has been showing you what the closing ayah states explicitly: **two scales, run by the same King, calibrated to two different virtues.**

#### The paradox to sit with

Here is the paradox that should change something inside you.

If Allah were *only* just, you would be terrified — because no honest soul can hand in only good deeds. Every soul has both bags.

If Allah were *only* merciful, you would be ungoverned — because if all is forgiven regardless of action, then action ceases to matter, and the moral universe collapses into nothing.

Allah is **both at once, but assigned asymmetrically.** Justice does the floor; mercy does the ceiling. Justice ensures evil is *answered*. Mercy ensures goodness is *answered better*. The floor is *illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn*. The ceiling is *khayrun minhā* — unbounded.

This is not a compromise between two opposing forces. It is a coherent vision of how a perfect King runs a perfect court. The wrath is bounded. The mercy is not.

#### The application

Stop trying to bargain with Allah as if He were a merchant who works by strict equivalence. He is not.

Bring Him your goodness, however small, because He will multiply it past what you imagined.
Bring Him your repentance, however late, because the evil that gets weighed is only the unrepented evil — and even that is capped at its actual weight.

Walk through the door of His court with whatever you have, and **trust the architecture of the courtroom**. The room itself is built to be merciful. Allah designed it that way before you ever stepped in.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look back at the whole ayah. Look at its shape.

> *Man jāʾa bi-l-ḥasanati* — **fa-lahu khayrun minhā.**
> *Wa man jāʾa bi-s-sayyiʾati* — **fa-lā yujzā... illā mā kānū yaʿmalūn.**

Two halves. Same opening. Asymmetric closing.

The first half is six words. It opens, it gives, it ends.
The second half is fourteen words. It opens, it hesitates, it qualifies, it caps.

**The grammar of the ayah is the theology of the ayah.** Mercy is given quickly, plainly, without hedging. Punishment is given slowly, with the longer sentence, with the careful restriction, with the cap built into the syntax itself. Allah's tongue, in this ayah, is reluctant to dwell on punishment and eager to deliver mercy.

And the placement — right after the Qarun story — completes the architecture. The man who calculated by *mithluhā* lost everything. The soul that comes to Allah without calculation — that simply *arrives* with what it has — discovers that the King calculates by a different math entirely.

### Bringing It Together

Our three linguistic discoveries and our two themes are the same insight from different directions.

*Jāʾa bi-* told us deeds are carried, not remembered. The two themes built on that: the despairing soul carries less debt than they think, and the King who receives the luggage is more generous than they expect.

*Khayrun minhā* told us reward is uncapped. The themes told us why: because Allah's generosity is not a transaction, it is an attribute.

The broken parallelism told us punishment is capped. The themes told us why: because justice and mercy are different functions, and Allah refuses to multiply on the side of wrath the way He multiplies on the side of mercy.

The ayah is one statement, said three ways: **the scales of Allah are not the scales you fear.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When you imagine yourself walking toward the Day of Judgment carrying bags of deeds — what is in the bags right now? Are you packing the right things?

2. Have you been doing the math of Qarun (*I earned this, I deserve this*) or the math of despair (*I owe too much, I cannot come back*)? Both are wrong. Which version of wrong have you been living in?

3. The ayah refuses to multiply punishment. What does it ask of you, that you might refuse to multiply wrath in your own dealings — with your spouse, your children, the person who wronged you?

4. If your good deeds enter the scale multiplied, and your evil enters the scale at face value — what does that mean about the smallest good deed you have been postponing because it "wouldn't matter"?

5. What would change in your day if you genuinely believed the architecture of the courtroom was built in your favor?

You are not negotiating with a bookkeeper. You are coming home to a King who has already tilted the scales toward you before you ever picked up the first stone of a single good deed.

Bring Him what you have. However small. However late. However broken.

The grammar of this ayah is His promise: what you bring in returns multiplied, and what you fear will not be multiplied against you. Walk through the door.
