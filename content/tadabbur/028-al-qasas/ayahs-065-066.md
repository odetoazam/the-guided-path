# The Day the Answers Disappear

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayahs 65-66

---

### The Arabic

> وَيَوْمَ يُنَادِيهِمْ فَيَقُولُ مَاذَا أَجَبْتُمُ الْمُرْسَلِينَ
>
> فَعَمِيَتْ عَلَيْهِمُ الْأَنبَاءُ يَوْمَئِذٍ فَهُمْ لَا يَتَسَاءَلُونَ

### Transliteration

*Wa yawma yunādīhim fa-yaqūlu mādhā ajabtumu-l-mursalīn. Fa-ʿamiyat ʿalayhimu-l-anbāʾu yawmaʾidhin fa-hum lā yatasāʾalūn.*

### Accessible Translation

"And the Day He will call out to them, and He will say: *What did you answer the messengers?* But on that Day the accounts will be blinded to them, and they will not even ask one another."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World This Ayah Steps Into

Surah Al-Qasas is, among many things, a surah about **who is really speaking**. From the opening verses about Musa, Allah is showing us that history is not what Pharaoh thinks it is. Pharaoh stands on his throne and declares — *I am your highest lord*. He commands the killing of infant boys. He builds monuments. He gives speeches. He thinks he is the author of his own story.

And all the while, Allah is moving an infant down a river. Allah is putting that infant into the very household of the tyrant. Allah is feeding the child through his own mother's milk while Pharaoh pays her wages. The surah is showing you, slowly and patiently, that **the loud voices in this world are not the deciding voices**.

Now, in the second half of the surah, Allah turns the camera. The voice that was always speaking, but speaking through events rather than through direct address — that voice will one day speak directly. And the people who were so confident in their answers, so certain in their replies, so quick with their counter-arguments — they will be summoned.

The original listener — sitting in Makkah, hearing this — was being mocked daily. Asked dismissive, sneering questions. *What is this Quran? Who is this man? What is this resurrection you keep talking about?* They had answers for everything. They had a whole world of answers.

This ayah is telling them: there is coming a Day when the question goes the other direction. And on that Day, the people who had all the answers — won't have one.

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah that does something grammatically astonishing. It describes something happening to a person's *news*, their *story*, their *account* — and what happens to it is so strange that translators struggle.

The accounts go **blind**.

Not the people. **The accounts themselves.**

Stay with me. By the end of this reflection, you will understand why Allah chose the language of *blindness* for something that should not have eyes — and what it has to do with the most important question that will ever be asked of you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### Element One: *Yunādīhim* — The Calling Across Distance

Let's start with a small word. The verb Allah uses for what He does on that Day: **يُنَادِيهِمْ** — *yunādīhim*. "He calls to them."

In English, "calling" is generic. You call your friend. You call out a name. You call a meeting. The word does not tell you anything about distance, volume, or relationship.

In Arabic, this is not so. Arabic has many words for speech, and each one is doing different work.

There is **qāla** — he said. Plain speech. Statement.

There is **kallama** — he spoke to, with the implication of intimate, direct address. This is the word used for Allah's speech to Musa — *wa kallama-llāhu Mūsā taklīmā* — Allah spoke to Musa, a direct speaking. Face to face, as it were. Close.

There is **hamasa** — to whisper.

And there is **nādā** — from the root **ن-د-ي** — to call out, to summon **across distance**.

When Arabs used the word *nādā* in their poetry and speech, it carried the image of someone calling from a hilltop to someone in a valley. Of a shepherd calling his sheep from far away. Of someone summoning a person who is not nearby. The distance is built into the word.

Now notice something.

When Musa was at the burning bush, what does Allah say? *Nūdiya yā Mūsā* — *Musa was called.* From far away. And then Musa was brought closer. And then Allah *kallama* him — spoke to him directly, intimately.

The progression matters. **Calling across distance comes before intimate speech.**

[PAUSE]

Now look at this ayah. On the Day of Judgment, Allah's address to the deniers is **nidāʾ** — calling across distance. It is not *kallama*. It is not intimate, face-to-face speech. It is the speech of summoning. The speech of *come forward, you who have been far*.

Here's what this invites us to consider. The very *form* of the address tells you something about the relationship. These are people Allah is speaking to — but He is speaking to them **from across a gulf**. The intimacy was never built. The closeness was never cultivated. So when speech finally happens, it is the speech of distance.

Compare this with how Allah describes addressing the believers in other ayahs — *yā ʿibādī* — *O My servants* — the vocative of nearness. The pronoun of belonging. *My* servants.

Here? No possessive. No *My*. Just — **He calls them**. Like a stranger calling a stranger by description rather than name.

The word chosen for "calling" is already telling us where these people stand before any answer is given.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: *Mādhā Ajabtum* — The Question About the Response, Not the Deed

Now look at the question itself. *Mādhā ajabtumu-l-mursalīn* — **what did you answer the messengers?**

Notice what the question is **not**.

It is not "what did you do?"
It is not "what did you believe?"
It is not "what was in your heart?"
It is not "did you pray, did you fast, did you give?"

The question is about **the moment of response to the messenger**. *Ajabtum* — from the root **ج-و-ب** — to answer, to respond, to reply. The same root from which Arabic gets *jawāb* — answer. The same root from which Allah's name **al-Mujīb** — *the One who responds* — is derived.

Allah is asking: in the moment when a messenger came to you with a message — **what came back from you?**

Now here is something subtle but stunning. The Arabic does not say *mādhā qultum* — what did you say. *Qāla* is just speech, words. *Ajaba* is **answer**. And answer presumes there was first **a call**, **a question**, **a summons**.

So the structure is: *a messenger called, and Allah is asking — what did you answer back?*

[PAUSE]

This is the architecture of the human moment that Allah is putting on trial. Not the totality of your life. Not your résumé of deeds. **The moment when truth knocked on your door — what did you say through the keyhole?**

Let's sit with this for a moment, because it changes everything.

Most of us imagine the Day of Judgment as a tallying. A weighing of deeds. A scoreboard. And there are ayahs that frame it that way too. But here, in this ayah, Allah is collapsing the whole question down to **one verb**. *Ajabtum*. What did you respond?

Why?

Because **the response to the messenger is the parent of every deed**. If you answered "yes" — what followed was prayer, fasting, charity, struggle, repentance. If you answered "no" — what followed was the entire architecture of a life lived as if the message were not real.

The deed is the *child*. The answer is the *parent*.

And Allah, in His mercy and in His justice, is asking the parental question. Not "what did your life look like" but "what did your heart say when My speech arrived?"

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. The messengers, *al-mursalīn*, are plural and definite. *The* messengers. Not *a* messenger. Not *some* messengers.

This question is being asked of all humanity at once, and what it presumes is that **a messenger reached you**. In whatever form Allah's reminder reached you — through a prophet, through the inheritors of prophets, through the words of the Book carried down the centuries, through a moment of conscience that came unbidden — *something came*. And the question is: what did you do with it?

Classical interpreters are precise about what the messengers were sent with: the call to God's oneness and the turning away from everything worshipped besides Him. Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both read the question as bearing specifically on tawḥīd — how a person responded to the core claim that nothing is worthy of worship except Allah. The question sounds wide, but it has a center. And everything else in the religious life — prayer, obedience, repentance, trust — flows downstream from that single moment of response to the most fundamental thing a messenger can bring. Which means the examination on that Day, though it sounds like one question, is really a question about the entire orientation of a life.

Here is a sobering thought to carry. You are being asked this question right now. Not in a future tense. The Quran, in your ears, is the messenger's voice carried forward. *Mādhā ajabtum.* What is your answer?

---

### Element Three: *Fa-ʿAmiyat ʿAlayhimu-l-Anbāʾ* — When the News Goes Blind

Now we come to the strangest, most arresting word in the passage. **فَعَمِيَتْ** — *fa-ʿamiyat*. From the root **ع-م-ي** — blindness.

But here is the linguistic shock. Let's teach the grammar first, because the grammar is the meaning.

In Arabic, every verb has a subject — the doer of the action. The verb *ʿamiya* — to be blind — normally has a *person* as its subject. *ʿAmiya zaydun* — Zayd became blind. *ʿAmiyat ʿaynuhu* — his eye went blind.

What goes blind is the one who has the faculty of sight, or the eye itself.

But in this ayah, look at what is grammatically the subject of the verb. The subject is not *them*. The subject is **al-anbāʾ** — **the news, the accounts, the reports**.

The literal grammatical sentence is: **The accounts went blind upon them.**

Stay with this. It is not "they were blinded to the accounts." It is **the accounts themselves became blind**.

This is what classical rhetoricians call a transferred attribute — an attribute that should belong to one thing has been deliberately placed on another, and the dislocation *is* the meaning. Allah could have said *ʿamū ʿan al-anbāʾ* — they went blind to the news. But that would be a different statement.

To say "**the news went blind upon them**" is to say something so much more terrifying.

[PAUSE]

Let me build the picture.

Imagine you walk into a courtroom prepared. You have your story. You have rehearsed your defense. You have your witnesses ready. You know what you are going to say.

That is the normal experience of memory and self-narration. We are always preparing our story. Every human being walks through life building an internal account — a *nabāʾ*, a piece of news about themselves — that they will tell when asked. *I did this because.* *I was a good person because.* *I meant well because.* *I was just following.* *I didn't know.* *Everyone was doing it.*

We carry our accounts with us. They are *ours*. We have full possession of them. We can pull them out whenever we need to.

But Allah says — on that Day — **the accounts go blind**.

Not lost. Not forgotten. **Blind.**

The account is still there. It is still attached to you. It is still your story. But it has lost its capacity to *see its way out*. The narrative cannot find the path. The defense cannot locate itself. The story you rehearsed your whole life — it is standing in your throat, and it does not know which direction to face.

Classical commentators are precise about what *ʿamiyat ʿalayhim* means. Al-Tabari notes that *ʿamiya* carried in classical Arabic usage the meaning of *khafiya* — to become hidden or concealed, the way a traveler's news becomes hidden and unreachable — so the blindness here has two valences at once: the accounts do not merely fail to see, they disappear from reach. What specifically goes blind, the commentators agree, is the *ḥujja* — the argument, the proof, the excuse. They will not know what to plead, because there is nothing left that can plead for them.

Then al-Tabari adds something devastating in its precision: the accounts go blind because Allah had already *completed* His proof upon them in this world — every messenger, every calling, every avenue of excuse had been exhausted. The blindness is not arbitrary. It is the **consequence of a closed case**. The arguments that served them in this world — stalling, deflecting, postponing the question — find no footing on that Day because the case that should have answered them was already fully and patiently made. There is no escape route left, because every escape route was already addressed.

The Day of Judgment is not the Day when Allah simply **knows more than you**. It is the Day when **your own story stops working from the inside** — and it stops working because it was never built to survive the question it was always avoiding. The internal narrator goes dumb. The arguments trained for years cannot find the courtroom door.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing to notice. The word for the accounts — *al-anbāʾ* — is the plural of **nabaʾ**. And *nabaʾ* is not just any kind of news. In Quranic Arabic, *nabaʾ* is **weighty news**. Important news. News that matters. It is the word used in *ʿamma yatasāʾalūn — ʿan-in-nabāʾi-l-ʿaẓīm* — *what are they asking about? About the great news (of resurrection).*

So when Allah says the *anbāʾ* go blind, He is talking about the **weighty accounts**. The big-deal stories of your life. The defenses you considered air-tight. The moments you thought you had figured out.

Those.

Those are the ones that go blind.

---

### Element Four (briefly): *Fa-hum Lā Yatasāʾalūn* — They Cannot Even Ask Each Other

One more linguistic note before we move to the themes, because the ayah ends with a small phrase that does enormous work.

**فَهُمْ لَا يَتَسَاءَلُونَ** — *fa-hum lā yatasāʾalūn.* They do not ask one another.

The verb is in form VI — *tafāʿala* — which in Arabic morphology indicates **reciprocal action**. Not "they do not ask" but "they do not **mutually** ask." They do not turn to each other and say *what did you say? What's the answer? Help me out.*

This is a person stripped down. In this world, when we face a hard moment, the first thing we do is turn to someone else. We caucus. We strategize. We coordinate stories. Notice how, in this world, the deniers in the Quran are always shown working together — *they plot, they take counsel, they confer*.

On that Day? **The form-VI is denied to them.** The reciprocity is gone. Each person is alone with the question. No one to nudge. No one to whisper to. No one to coordinate the answer.

The conversation between humans, which is the oldest comfort we have, is severed.

Al-Muyassar sharpens this further with a precise phrase: they cannot ask each other *suʾāl intifāʿ* — asking that would yield any benefit. It is not merely that the conversation is stopped or forbidden. It is that conversation would produce nothing. Every person they might turn to is carrying the same blind account. There is no one left who can supply the answer, because the answer was the one thing no one in that community chose to build while they still could.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

What the linguistic construction has shown us is this. Allah is staging a moment of profound isolation. A summons across distance. A question that bypasses the résumé and goes straight to the heart's response. An internal narrative that goes blind. A social fabric that goes silent.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Knowledge That Has No Currency on That Day

Let me start with a scene from your own life.

Have you ever had a dream where you needed to say something important — and your voice would not work? You knew the words. You could see them in your mind. But when you opened your mouth, nothing came out. Or worse — the wrong sounds came out, and you could hear yourself failing to say what you meant, and you could not correct it.

That dream is a tiny glimpse — a five-second glimpse — of *ʿamiyat ʿalayhimu-l-anbāʾ*.

We have all had this experience in waking life too, but in a smaller form. The exam where you knew the material the night before, and you sit down and the page is white. The argument you rehearsed in the shower, and when the moment came, your tongue went thick. The eulogy you wanted to give. The apology you needed to make.

There is a knowledge we *have* and a knowledge we can *deploy*. They are not the same.

[PAUSE]

What Allah is teaching us here is something that the modern world particularly needs to hear. **We live in an age that confuses access to information with possession of wisdom.** Everyone has the Quran in their pocket. Everyone can search any hadith. Everyone has watched a hundred lectures. Everyone can quote a scholar.

This is access. This is not the same as having an answer ready when Allah asks the question.

The Companions of the Prophet ﷺ used to weep when verses about the Day of Judgment were recited — not because they did not have access to knowledge but because they understood that **the question is not what you can look up. The question is what is in you when nothing can be looked up.**

The deniers in this ayah are not described as ignorant. The Quran shows them, throughout, as people with arguments — sophisticated arguments. Pharaoh has speeches. Qarun has theories. The Makkans have counter-positions. They are *full* of accounts. They are loaded with *anbāʾ*.

And on that Day — every single one of those well-articulated, much-rehearsed accounts — goes blind.

Not because Allah erased them. Because **arguments that were built to avoid the truth cannot suddenly serve the truth on the Day the truth is everything**.

Here is the application. Sit with the questions you have rehearsed. The defenses you carry. The "but I" and "but they" and "but the situation was" that you take with you everywhere. Notice them. Notice how easily they come to mind. Notice how well-organized they are.

And ask yourself, gently — *would these stand up if the floor disappeared?*

That is not a question to torture yourself with. It is an invitation to **answer the messenger's call now**, while there is still time to give the answer in this world rather than fumble for it in the next.

The person who has answered the messenger does not need an excuse on that Day. They do not need their *anbāʾ* to function. They have already given their *jawāb*. They responded when the call came.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Loneliness That Is Built In

Now let's turn to the closing phrase. *Fa-hum lā yatasāʾalūn* — they do not ask one another.

This phrase, in some ways, is the saddest in the passage. Because it tells you about a loneliness that has been growing the whole time, and you didn't notice.

Let me build the picture.

In this world, every form of denial of guidance is **social**. Pharaoh has his ministers. Qarun has his circle. The Makkans had their gatherings. People who refuse the messenger almost never refuse alone. They refuse together. They build a community of refusal. They sit in rooms and reinforce each other. They share memes. They send each other articles. They have the same jokes about the same things. They feel, in their gathering, that they are right because they are many.

The Quran is unusually attentive to this. It notices that disbelief is **gregarious** — *innā wajadnā ābāʾanā ʿalā ummah* — *we found our forefathers on a community* — *innamā ittakhadhtum min dūni-llāhi awthānan mawaddata baynikum* — *you took besides Allah idols, the affection between you being the bond.*

The community of refusal is glued together by mutual affirmation. And the deepest pleasure of that community is that you do not have to carry the question alone. You can always turn to someone and say, *right? right? I'm right, right?* And they will say, *yes, yes, you're right.*

This is the social fabric that *fa-hum lā yatasāʾalūn* tears.

[PAUSE]

The form VI is denied. The mutual asking is severed. On that Day, every person who built their certainty on the affirmation of others is left holding the bag alone.

Modern psychology has a name for the dynamic Allah is describing. It is called **co-rumination** — the way humans use each other to maintain narratives about the world that, in isolation, would collapse. Therapists know that the fastest way to break a destructive belief is often to interrupt the social loop that keeps reinforcing it. Take the person out of the group that affirms the belief, and the belief begins to dissolve.

The Day of Judgment, in this dimension, is the **ultimate interruption of the social loop**. Every person is removed from the chorus. Every voice that used to come back saying *yes, you're right* is silent. Every face that used to nod at the joke is turned somewhere else.

And in that silence, the *anbāʾ* — the accounts that depended on the chorus to make sense — go blind.

[PAUSE]

Here is the practical movement.

Every one of us has a chorus. Every one of us has people we turn to in order to confirm the story we want to be true. This is not necessarily evil. The Prophet ﷺ told us *al-marʾu ʿalā dīni khalīlih* — *a person is on the religion of his close friend*. The chorus is real. The chorus shapes us.

The question this ayah forces is: **what is my chorus reinforcing in me?**

Is my chorus the kind that, if removed, would leave me with a *jawāb* — a real answer to the messenger? Or is my chorus the kind that, if removed, would leave my *anbāʾ* — my carefully constructed accounts — to go blind?

This is one of the most important practical applications a Muslim can take from the Quran. **Audit your chorus.** Who are the voices around you whose affirmation you depend on? What are they affirming? If on the Day of Judgment those voices were taken away — could you give your own answer? Or would you find yourself reaching for a turn that no one is willing to take with you?

Build, while there is time, a chorus of people who are also trying to answer the messenger. Tie yourself to those whose affirmation, if removed in the next world, will not leave you naked — because what they were affirming was true.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have seen.

A call from across a distance. A question that bypasses the deeds and goes to the *response*. A blindness that strikes not the eyes but the **accounts themselves**. A silence that severs the social fabric of mutual reinforcement.

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions. From one side: Allah's address on that Day reveals the structure of the relationship — distance not closeness, summons not intimacy. From the other side: the human predicament on that Day reveals the bankruptcy of an unanswered call — accounts go blind, voices fail, the chorus dissolves.

The whole passage is asking one thing.

**Have you answered?**

Not — what do you know. Not — what do you do. Not — what do you say to others. But — **when the call came, what came back from you?**

---

### Questions to Carry

1. When the Quran reaches my ears today, do I receive it as a messenger calling, or as background sound I have already organized my life around?

2. What is the *answer* I have actually given — not the answer I would write down on a survey, but the answer my time, my attention, and my decisions have actually delivered?

3. Are there *anbāʾ* — accounts, defenses, well-rehearsed justifications — that I am carrying, that I suspect would not stand if the floor were removed? Why am I still carrying them?

4. Who is in my chorus? What are they affirming in me? If their voices were stripped away tomorrow, what would I be left with?

5. If Allah called to me today and asked *mādhā ajabta* — *what did you answer?* — what is the honest reply that would rise to my tongue?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day Allah summons you across the distance and asks what you said back to the messenger, every account that was not built on a real answer will go blind in your own mouth — so answer now, while answering is still possible.

---

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not a threat. It is a **gift wrapped in fear**. Allah is telling you, in advance, that the question is coming. He is telling you the exact wording. He is telling you what will not work as an answer. He is telling you what happens to the unanswered accounts.

Allah does not show you the test paper to terrify you. He shows you the test paper to **invite you to study**.

The invitation is simple. Begin to give your answer now. Not all at once. Not perfectly. But begin. Every prayer is an answer. Every act of obedience is an answer. Every repentance is an answer. Every word of remembrance is an answer. Every moment you turn back when you were tempted to turn away is an answer.

Build your *jawāb* in pieces. Day by day. So that on the Day He calls — your answer is not something you have to scramble to construct from blind accounts. It is something you have been quietly building your whole life.

---

### Du'a

O Allah — *al-Mujīb* — the One who answers — let our answer to Your messengers be a real answer, built in this world before it is summoned in the next. Do not let our accounts go blind in our own mouths on that Day. And when You call us across the distance, draw us close enough to be among those You address as Your servants, not as strangers.

*Āmīn.*
