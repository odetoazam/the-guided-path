# The Morning After: When Yesterday's Envy Becomes Today's Terror

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَأَصْبَحَ ٱلَّذِينَ تَمَنَّوْا۟ مَكَانَهُۥ بِٱلْأَمْسِ يَقُولُونَ وَيْكَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَبْسُطُ ٱلرِّزْقَ لِمَن يَشَآءُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِۦ وَيَقْدِرُ ۖ لَوْلَآ أَن مَّنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا لَخَسَفَ بِنَا ۖ وَيْكَأَنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ

**Transliteration:**

## Introduction: The Day After You Wanted Something Dangerous

Picture the street the day before.

A long procession winds through the city. Gold harnesses. Silk-draped beasts. Servants on either side. Wealth so dense it changes the air. And lining the road — the people. Watching. Calculating. Whispering. Some of them, the Quran tells us a few verses earlier, look at this man Qarun and say from the pit of their chests: **"If only we had what he has been given. He is truly a man of immense fortune."**

That was yesterday.

Now it is morning. The sun is up. The procession is gone. The man is gone. His house is gone. The treasures are gone. The earth opened beneath him and swallowed all of it. Where the gold passed twenty-four hours ago, there is now only a tear in the ground and silence and the smell of disturbed soil.

And the same mouths that whispered *"if only we had what he has been given"* — those same mouths now open.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is what they say.

But it is not a sermon. It is not a lecture. It is the trembling, half-formed speech of people who have just realized they almost died for what they wanted. People who were saved not by their virtue but by the slowness of their wishes coming true. People who woke up and understood — perhaps for the first time in their lives — what it means to be spared.

The Qarun story runs from verse 76 to 82. This is the last verse. The closing word. And in Arabic, that closing word is built out of one of the most psychologically precise vocabularies in the entire Quran.

Stay with this. Because what's happening in these thirty-one words is not just the end of Qarun's story. It is the diagnosis of something living inside every one of us — the desires we don't yet realize would destroy us if Allah said yes.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: **Aṣbaḥa** — "They Entered the Morning"

The verse opens with one word that, in English, gets translated as a flat "began to say." But the Arabic is doing something the translation cannot easily carry.

The word is **أَصْبَحَ** — *aṣbaḥa.*

In Arabic, there is a family of verbs that don't just mean "became." They mean "became, *at a specific time of day.*" Aṣbaḥa means "entered into the morning." Amsā means "entered into the evening." Bāta means "entered into the night." These aren't decorative — they are temporal anchors. They tell you exactly *when* the change happened.

So when Arabic says *aṣbaḥa lladhīna tamannaw* — it is not just saying "those who wished began to speak." It is saying: **they entered the morning saying this.** They woke up. The night had passed. And in the first light of the new day, these were the words on their lips.

Why does this matter?

Notice something. Just one verse earlier — verse 81 — Allah describes what happened to Qarun: *fa-khasafnā bihi wa-bi-dārihi l-arḍ* — "We caused the earth to swallow him and his house." That happened, the classical commentators note, the previous day. The disaster was yesterday.

Which means: between the disaster and these words, **a night passed.**

Stay with that night. The people who had been envying Qarun went home. They went to sleep with the image of the tear in the ground still in their eyes. They lay in the dark. And somewhere in the hours between the ground closing and the sun rising — *something in them changed.*

The Quran does not show you the night. It shows you only the morning. But the word *aṣbaḥa* tells you the night happened. Tells you these words are not the reflex of the moment of disaster — they are the considered confession of people who had hours to sit with what they had wanted.

[PAUSE]

Classical commentators describe these speakers with three words that must not be collapsed into one: *mutawajjiʿīn* — grieving — *wa-muʿtabirīn* — drawing lessons — *wa-khā'ifīn* — and afraid. Not one emotion. Three, layered on top of each other. The grief is for the nearness of what they almost lost. The lesson-drawing is the theological recalibration happening in real time. The fear is for what they still might face. All three are present in the word *aṣbaḥa* — the morning of reckoning, not the afternoon of peace.

Here is what is remarkable. There is a recurring pattern in the Quran — in the stories of Madyan, of Thamud, of Lot — where after destruction Allah uses *aṣbaḥū* to describe the people: *fa-aṣbaḥū fī diyārihim jāthimīn* — "and they entered the morning in their homes, fallen on their faces, lifeless." The morning, in Quranic vocabulary, is when the verdict becomes visible. The night hides. The morning reveals.

Here, the same morning that exposes the dead exposes something else: the inner life of the survivors.

And what it exposes is a vocabulary they did not have yesterday.

### Element Two: **Tamannaw** — The Specific Word for *That* Kind of Wanting

Now look at what Allah calls these people: *alladhīna tamannaw makānahu bil-amsi* — *"those who had wished for his position the day before."*

The verb is **تَمَنَّوْا** — *tamannaw* — from the root م-ن-ي (m-n-y).

Arabic has many words for wanting. *Arāda* — to intend, to will. *Shā'a* — to wish in the sense of choosing. *Ishtahā* — to desire with appetite. *Raghiba* — to incline toward. Each one carries a different texture of wanting.

*Tamannā* is its own particular thing.

The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — define *tamannī* as the wishing for something that has not happened, the imagining of a state that is not yours. It is the wishing of "if only." It is the verb of fantasy. And critically, in Form V (*tafaʿʿala*) — which is the form used here, *tamannā* — the morphological pattern carries the meaning of *bringing the thing onto oneself,* of *internalizing the act.* Tafaʿʿul forms are reflexive — the subject is doing something to themselves.

So *tamannaw* is not just "they wished." It is "they engaged in the act of wishing — they generated the wish from within, and the wish came back to fall on them."

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The grammar itself is telling you that the act of wanting Qarun's position was something these people were *doing to themselves.* The envy was not just looking outward at him — it was reshaping their inward state.

And now look at what is in their wish: *makānahu* — "his position." Classical commentators are precise here: they specify *makānahu min al-dunyā, wa-ghinahu wa-kathrat mālihi* — his worldly standing, his wealth, and the abundance of his possessions. Not just the gold. Not just the servants. The entire edifice of the life Qarun visibly occupied. To want someone's *makān* is to want to *be them.* To trade selves.

There is a hadith — sound, in Bukhari and Muslim — where the Prophet ﷺ said: *lā ḥasada illā fī ithnatayn* — *"there is no [permissible] envy except in two [cases]"* — and then he named the man given knowledge who acts on it and the man given wealth who spends it in goodness. The classical scholars distinguish between two kinds of wanting-what-another-has: *ḥasad* — wishing the blessing would leave them — and *ghibṭah* — wishing you had the same without wanting theirs to leave.

What is happening here in verse 79 — the verse where the people first see Qarun — looks like it might be *ghibṭah.* Just a wish to have what he has. Innocent enough. Common enough. The kind of wanting most of us do every time we scroll a feed.

But here is what Allah is showing us with the morning-after word *tamannaw.*

The wish was not innocent. Because the wish, if it had been granted, would have ended them.

Look at what they say next: *law lā an manna llāhu ʿalaynā la-khasafa binā* — *"if not for Allah's favor on us, He would have caused it to swallow us."* In English we hear "us" and think it is a polite "us." It is not. It is precise. **The thing we wanted to be — the position we wished into ourselves — that is the thing that just got swallowed.** If Allah had given us what we asked for, we would now be in the hole with him.

But notice something even more acute in how classical commentators read this phrase. The mercy they acknowledge is not only for the wish they harbored inwardly — it is for **what they said.** The words *ʿalā mā qulnā* — "for what we *said*" — appear in the exegesis as the specific content of Allah's forgiveness. They are not just relieved they did not receive Qarun's position. They are relieved that their *spoken envy* — the words they put into the air in front of the procession — was not itself punished. The *tamannī* that became speech. That moved from heart to tongue.

There is something here about the weight of the spoken word in Islamic moral theology. What you utter is more than what you think. The vocalized envy is a different category from the quiet ache. And the mercy they are thanking Allah for is the mercy of not being held accountable for what their tongues said — not only for what their hearts wanted.

The verb *tamannaw* is doing structural work. It is identifying these people as the people who *wished themselves into the position that just got buried.* And the morning is when they realize what they had been asking for.

There is no Arabic word that lets you say "I had a small, harmless envy" the way English lets you. The grammar will not let you off the hook. *Tamannī* is a full act. It draws the thing onto yourself. And sometimes Allah's mercy is not in answering your *tamannī.* Sometimes Allah's mercy is in the silence that meets it.

### Element Three: **Wayka'anna** — The Sound of a Person Catching Themselves

Now the strangest word in the verse. The word that classical grammarians have been arguing about for over a thousand years. The word that — if you let it — will let you hear these people *speak.*

**وَيْكَأَنَّ** — *wayka'anna.*

It appears twice in this verse and almost nowhere else in the entire Quran. Look at it. Even visually it is unusual. It seems to be a compound of two things stuck together.

The classical grammarians give us the composite picture. Some — like al-Akhfash and many of the Basran school — say it is *way* + *ka'anna.* *Way* is an interjection in Arabic. It is the sound a person makes when they are startled into recognition. It is not a word with a meaning — it is a *noise* with a meaning. The closest English equivalents are "oh!" or "ah!" or that quick intake of breath people make when they suddenly understand something they had been getting wrong. And *ka'anna* means "as if" — the particle of resembling, of approximating.

So in this reading: *wayka'anna* = *"Oh! — it is as if..."* The breath of shock, followed by the words of someone struggling to put what they now see into language they don't yet fully have.

Others — like al-Kisā'ī and many of the Kufan school — say it is *way* + *ka* + *anna,* where *ka* carries the meaning of cause or reason. In this reading the meaning is closer to: *"Oh! — because Allah extends provision..."* — the speakers are not approximating, they are *naming the reason* for what they saw.

Some — like al-Farrā' — read it as *waylaka* + *anna,* where *waylaka* is a vocative of warning, an "alas to you!" or "woe to you!" that has been worn down by use into something softer, a sigh of self-rebuke.

Among the earliest interpreters, Qatada understood *wayka'anna* as an expression of sheer astonishment — the verbal sound of minds overturning what they had assumed to be settled. His reading sits alongside the grammarians' disagreement rather than resolving it: what he hears is the emotional register, and it matches all three grammatical readings simultaneously.

[PAUSE]

Here is where the composite sketch matters. **Do not pick one.** All these readings are the same person speaking — described from different angles. They are saying:

- *"Oh!"* — the involuntary sound of shock.
- *"It is as if..."* — the groping toward an idea they had not quite held before.
- *"Because..."* — the dawning of a *reason* they had not seen.
- *"Woe..."* — the soft self-rebuke of someone who knows they got something wrong.
- *"Astonishment..."* — the overturning of a settled assumption.

All of this is happening in one word, twice.

Listen to the sound of it. *Wayka'anna.* The *wāw* opens — *wa* — like a sigh exhaled. The *yā* tightens — *way* — like the throat catching. The *kāf* lands soft — *ka.* The *hamzah* on the *alif* breaks open — *'anna* — like the moment a sentence finally finds its grammar.

The word is a sound. The sound is the sound a person makes when yesterday's certainty collapses and today's understanding has not finished arriving.

[PAUSE]

And notice: it appears *twice* in the same verse. The first *wayka'anna* introduces a theological observation — Allah extends provision to whom He wills and restricts it. The second *wayka'anna* introduces a moral conclusion — the disbelievers do not succeed.

Why twice?

Because the realization comes in two waves. First wave: *the size of the gift was never the proof of love.* Second wave: *and the absence of the gift was never the proof of failure.* The first wave corrects how they read Qarun. The second wave corrects how they read *themselves* — because if they had been measuring success by makān, then they had been measuring their own lives by a broken ruler.

One word. Two appearances. Two collapsing certainties. And the grammar itself sounds like a person catching their breath.

### Bridge

So three linguistic things we have seen:

The morning — the verdict-revealing hour — which tells us a night has passed between the envy and the speech, and that the speakers arrive at dawn *mutawajjiʿīn wa-muʿtabirīn wa-khā'ifīn:* grieving, lesson-drawing, and afraid.

The *tamannaw* — the verb of wishing-into-oneself, which tells us the want was an *act,* not a glance — and that the mercy they receive extends even to the words those wishes became when they left their lips.

The *wayka'anna* — the breath of correction, which tells us the speakers are not lecturing us; they are *catching themselves.*

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way Allah extends and withholds provision, and about why some prayers are mercifully unanswered.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Mercy of the Wish Not Answered

Most of the way you think about Allah's mercy, you think about it as the *yes.* The prayer answered. The job given. The child healed. The marriage saved. The bill paid.

Mercy as the open door.

Now think about all the prayers in your life Allah did not answer. The job you desperately wanted and did not get. The person you wanted and were not given. The position, the city, the circumstance — the *makān* you wished into yourself and the silence that met it.

What if some of those silences were the mercy?

The people in this verse are not speaking hypothetically. They are speaking from the other side of a disaster they narrowly missed. They *know* — with the certainty of people who watched the earth close over what they wanted — that the wish they made was a wish for their own destruction. They are not guessing. The ground showed them.

But you and I do not usually get to see the ground close. We do not usually get the visible evidence of what would have happened if the wish had been granted. We just get the silence. The unanswered prayer. The door that stayed shut.

[PAUSE]

The people here thank Allah for three things at once, in the classical reading: they thank Him for not giving them what they asked for. They thank Him for not punishing them for wanting it. And they thank Him for not punishing them for *saying it* — for the words that came out of their mouths in front of the procession, for the *ʿalā mā qulnā,* the things they verbalized in their envy. Three layers of mercy nested inside the phrase *manna llāhu ʿalaynā* — "Allah conferred favor upon us."

Most people, when they give thanks for unanswered prayer, thank Allah for the first layer only: *you didn't give me the thing and I now see why.* But these speakers go further. They know that the *wanting* itself, the *speaking* of the want — those things too deserved consequence. And they were spared consequence for those as well.

This is a deeper gratitude. Not just "thank you for the closed door." But "thank you for not holding the knocking against me."

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ narrated something that the classical commentators connect directly to this punishment — the specific fate of *khasf,* of being swallowed by the earth. He ﷺ said: *baynamā rajulun yajurru izārahu idh khusifa bih, fa-huwa yatajaljalu fīhā ilā yawm al-qiyāmah* — *"While a man was dragging his lower garment out of arrogance, he was swallowed by the earth, and he is still sinking in it until the Day of Resurrection."* The narration is preserved in the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī.

Notice: the punishment of *khasf* — of the earth opening and swallowing — is the punishment for the arrogance of display. For the man who drags his garment so others see his wealth and his station. Qarun's procession — the adornments, the gold, the stagecraft of his *makān* — is not incidental to his punishment. It is connected to it. The arrogant display of provision is answered by the withdrawal of the ground that provision stood on.

Which means: the people who watched Qarun's display, who wanted to *be* Qarun, who wished themselves into his position — they were wishing themselves into the very posture that earned the earth opening beneath him.

And Allah said no.

That *no* is the mercy this verse is about.

### Theme Two: **Yabsuṭu wa-yaqdiru** — The Measure in Every Gift and Every Withholding

The first *wayka'anna* in this verse opens into a theological statement: *inna llāha yabsuṭu r-rizqa li-man yashā'u min ʿibādihi wa-yaqdiru* — "Allah extends provision to whom He wills of His servants and restricts it."

Two verbs. Both in the present tense. Both ongoing. Both from roots that carry enormous weight in Arabic.

**Yabsuṭu** — from b-s-ṭ — the root of spreading wide, of laying out flat, of extending an open hand. The same root gives us *basīṭ* — the outstretched — and *al-Bāsiṭ,* one of the divine names: the One Who spreads wide. When the Quran describes a generous hand, it uses this root. The provision Allah extends is not merely given — it is *laid out before you,* spread wide, like a cloth put down for a feast.

**Yaqdiru** — from q-d-r — the root of measurement, of decree, of power. This is the same root as *qadar* (divine decree) and *qudra* (capacity, power) and *al-Qādir* (the Able, the One Who has power over all things). To restrict provision — *yaqdiru* — is not just to withhold. It is to *measure it exactly.* To set it at precisely what it should be.

[PAUSE]

The people watching Qarun had been reading the *basṭ* — the wide laying-out of provision — as a sign of divine approval. They had been reading the *qadr* — the measured withholding — as a sign of divine neglect. This is the misreading that the disaster corrects.

The *basṭ* was not a sign Qarun was loved. The *qadr* was not a sign they were abandoned.

What these words tell us is something the Quran returns to again and again with different vocabulary: the *amount* of provision is not the *verdict* on a person. The wealthy man and the poor man are equally under the care of *al-Bāsiṭ* and *al-Qādir.* One is being given widely. One is being given at exact measure. Both are in Allah's hand.

There is a reason the second verb — *yaqdiru* — shares its root with *qadar.* When Allah restricts your provision, it is not accident. It is not neglect. It is *measurement.* It is the same divine precision that governs how water moves and how stars hold their orbits. Your provision is being *calibrated* — not by indifference, but by a wisdom that sees what you cannot.

[PAUSE]

Most of us are Qarun's observers. Most of us have stood on the street and watched the procession and felt the ache of *law annī kuntu* — *if only I were.* And most of us have, in the aftermath of not getting what we watched pass by, felt the smaller ache of unloved, of overlooked, of measured out a life smaller than what we deserved.

This verse — spoken by people who almost died for what they wanted — corrects both readings simultaneously.

The wide provision was not proof. The restricted provision was not punishment. Both are Allah's active choices. Both are ongoing — *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdiru,* present tense, continuous. And between those two ongoing actions, you are being held at exactly the measure that is right for you.

### Theme Three: **Lā yufliḥu l-kāfirūn** — What It Actually Means to Succeed

The second *wayka'anna* leads into the strangest conclusion in the verse. After all the talk of provision and restriction, of being swallowed and being spared, the people land here: *wayka'annahu lā yufliḥu l-kāfirūn* — *"Oh! — it is as if the disbelievers do not succeed."*

*Lā yufliḥ.* The root is ف-ل-ح — f-l-ḥ.

You know this root. Every adhān carries it: *ḥayya ʿala l-falāḥ* — come to success, come to falāḥ. Five times a day the call goes out. Five times a day the word hangs in the air.

But the agricultural root of *falāḥ* is rarely stopped at. The original meaning is the plowing of the earth — the *fallāḥ* is the farmer, the one who tills the ground, who breaks the hard surface so something can grow. *Falāḥ* is not the victory lap. It is the patient, sustained, unglamorous work of cultivation. It is success measured not by how things look in the procession but by what grows across seasons.

[PAUSE]

Look at what Qarun had. Gold. Servants. The adornments of the world. An entire procession of visible success. By every measure the watchers were using — the visible measure, the measure of *makān* — Qarun was the definition of *falāḥ.*

He was in the ground the next morning.

The realization the speakers reach is not only theological: they are not just saying "Qarun failed." They are saying something about *themselves.* Because if *falāḥ* is not what Qarun had — if the wide provision, the visible position, the glittering procession is not *falāḥ* — then *what is?*

The word *kāfirūn* in this context carries its full root weight. Kufr is the covering up of truth — the root k-f-r means to bury, to cover over, to conceal. The kāfir is not just the one who rejects — the kāfir is the one who, when the truth is present, conceals it. Qarun covered the truth about the source of his wealth: *innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī* — "I was only given this because of knowledge I possess." He covered the source. He concealed the dependency. He buried the gratitude.

And the earth buried him.

Classical commentators note that *lā yufliḥu l-kāfirūn* is a statement about both worlds: *lā fī l-dunyā wa-lā fī l-ākhirah* — "neither in this world nor in the next." Not a deferred judgment for after death. Qarun's failure is visible *here,* on a morning when his procession has been replaced by a wound in the ground.

[PAUSE]

And *falāḥ* — real falāḥ — is the cultivation the farmer tends across seasons. It is the knowledge-bearer who acts on what he knows. The grateful steward. The one who did not misread the provision as *proof.* The one who woke up the morning after the disaster and, *mutawajjiʿan wa-muʿtabiran wa-khā'ifan,* grieving and lesson-drawing and afraid, began to recalibrate.

The people speaking in this verse are not yet handed the certificate of success. The Quran does not declare them saved. But something has cracked open in them — the measure by which they assessed everything has broken. And in that breaking, something true has a chance to grow.

That is the beginning of *falāḥ.*

## Closing: The Morning You Have Not Yet Had

You have had mornings like this. Maybe not this dramatic. Maybe not with a literal hole in the ground. But you have woken up — sometime, somewhere — and understood that the thing you wanted the day before would have broken you if you had gotten it.

The relationship. The position. The circumstance. The version of yourself you were so certain you needed to be.

And the morning brought clarity you could not have constructed from inside the wanting.

[PAUSE]

The people in this verse are saved by two things that are not their virtue: the slowness of *tamannī* — the merciful lag between the wish and its fulfillment — and the mercy of Allah who did not hold the wish, or the speaking of it, against them.

Most of us are living inside that lag right now. Between something we want and the moment it either arrives or does not. The Quran is telling you that the lag is not neglect. The lag is the space where *aṣbaḥa* happens — the morning-after that gives you the vocabulary you did not have yesterday.

And the mercy of the morning-after is not just that you now see more clearly. It is that you are here to see it at all.

*Wayka'anna.*

*Oh. It is as if.*

That sound — the breath between certainty and understanding — is the sound of a human being becoming more human. The sound of a person who yesterday had a ruler and today realizes the ruler was wrong. The sound of *tamannī* meeting its correction. The sound of *falāḥ* beginning, quietly, in the soft light of a morning you were given.

Ask yourself: what are you wishing for right now that, if it were given, would break you?

Not as a question to answer aloud. As a question to sit with.

The people of this verse sat with a version of it for a whole night. And in the morning, they spoke words the Quran preserved — not because they were scholars or prophets, but because the speech of people who have just understood something true is itself worth preserving.

Their *wayka'anna* is your *wayka'anna.*

Their morning is available to you.
