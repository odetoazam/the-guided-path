# The Confession Before the Mission

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 33

> قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّى قَتَلْتُ مِنْهُمْ نَفْسًۭا فَأَخَافُ أَن يَقْتُلُونِ
>
> *Qāla rabbi innī qataltu minhum nafsan fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn*
>
> **"He said: My Lord, indeed I killed a soul among them, and I fear that they will kill me."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before This Ayah

Let me set the scene, because everything depends on it.

Musa is thirty-eight, maybe forty years old. He has spent the last ten years as a shepherd in Madyan. He left Egypt as a wanted man — fleeing for his life after striking down an Egyptian and being warned that the chiefs were conspiring to kill him. He crossed the desert with nothing. He arrived at a well with nothing. He married into a family of shepherds and disappeared into obscurity.

For ten years.

Ten years of sheep and silence. Ten years of being unknown. Ten years away from the palace where he was raised, away from his mother, away from his sister, away from the people he was born to. Ten years during which Pharaoh probably stopped looking. Ten years during which Musa himself may have stopped expecting anything.

And then, returning to Egypt with his wife and family, he sees a fire in the distance on a cold night. He goes to fetch a flame so his family can warm themselves.

And the fire speaks.

It says: *"O Musa, indeed I am your Lord."*

[PAUSE]

In the space of a few minutes — minutes — Musa goes from being a shepherd asking for fire to being a prophet commissioned by the Lord of the worlds. Allah identifies Himself. Allah teaches Musa the two miracles — the staff that becomes a serpent and the hand that comes out white. Allah gives him the assignment:

**Go to Pharaoh. He has transgressed.**

This is the most consequential commission ever handed to a human being up to that point in history. Confront the most powerful tyrant on earth. Demand the liberation of an entire enslaved people. Carry a message that will either crack open Egypt or get you killed.

### The Hook

Now — pay attention to what Musa says first.

He does not say "Yes, my Lord." He does not say "Thank you." He does not say "I am ready." He does not even ask for the help we know he will soon ask for — *expand my chest, untie my tongue, send my brother with me.* All of that comes later.

The first thing out of his mouth, in the way Al-Qasas narrates the moment, is a **confession**.

(A word of care here: the Quran tells this commission in more than one surah, and the surahs do not all order Musa's words the same way. In Surah Taha, the famous du'a — *rabbi-shraḥ lī ṣadrī* — comes early in his response. Classical commentators read each surah's sequencing as serving that surah's own rhetorical purpose, not as a single master transcript. So when we say "first," we mean: *the way Al-Qasas chooses to open Musa's mouth.* And the choice this surah makes is striking.)

*My Lord, I killed someone among them.*

And here is the strange thing — Allah already knew. Allah just told him to go. Allah was there in the moment Musa struck the Egyptian. Allah does not need to be informed.

So why does Musa say it?

What is happening in this confession? What does it mean that the first sentence of a prophet's mission is *not* an acceptance, *not* a prayer for strength, but the **naming of an old wound**?

Stay with me. Because what's happening here is one of the most stunning pieces of psychological honesty in the entire Quran. And once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Rabbi* — The Word Before the Confession

Let me teach you something about how Arabic addresses Allah.

In Arabic, when you call out to someone, you typically use the particle *yā* — "O so-and-so." *Yā Muhammad, yā Allāh, yā ʿabdi.* It is the standard vocative. But there is another mode of calling — a more intimate one. You can drop the *yā* entirely and instead attach a possessive pronoun to the noun, fusing yourself into the address.

So instead of *yā rabb* — "O Lord" — you say *rabbī*. **"My Lord."**

The grammatical difference is small. The emotional difference is enormous.

*Yā rabb* is calling across a room. *Rabbī* is whispering across a pillow. The *yā* version maintains the distance of the formal address. The *rabbī* version closes the distance — it does not call to a Lord out there, it names a Lord who is already *mine*.

Now look at how Musa opens.

He does not say *yā rabb*. He says *rabbī*.

**"My Lord."**

Two syllables. And in those two syllables, the entire posture of what follows is established. He is not addressing the awe-inspiring fire. He is not addressing the Voice of Sinai. He is addressing the One who is already, somehow, *his*.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because something has happened in the seconds before this word that we have to register.

Allah has just identified Himself as *rabbuka* — "your Lord" (28:30). Allah has just placed that possessive on the relationship from His own side. *I am your Lord.* And Musa, in his very first reply, reaches up and **mirrors the possessive back**.

You said *your* Lord. I am saying *my* Lord.

You claimed me. I am claiming You back.

The relationship is sealed before anything else is said. Before the confession lands, before the fear is named, before any of the heavy material of this ayah is unpacked, Musa establishes the frame: *whatever I am about to tell You, I am telling it to the One who is mine and to whom I belong.*

This is what a confession sounds like when it is happening **inside a relationship**, not in front of a tribunal.

Here is what this invites us to consider. When you bring something hard to Allah — a sin, a fear, a failure, a wound — notice how you address Him. Are you addressing a judge? A distant authority? A vending machine of forgiveness? Or are you whispering *rabbī* — naming Him as yours before you name your trouble?

The word *rabbī* changes what the confession is. It is not a defendant facing a court. It is a child running to a parent, knowing the parent already knows, and still needing to say it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Innī qataltu* — The Naming Without the Excuse

Now look at what follows.

*Innī qataltu minhum nafsan.*

"Indeed I killed a soul among them."

Three things are happening grammatically here that I want you to see.

**First**, the particle *inna*. In Arabic, *inna* is an emphasizer. It is not just "I killed." It is "**indeed** I killed." *Inna* says: *I am not minimizing this. I am not dancing around this. I am marking it.* You use *inna* when you want to underline the sentence — when you want the listener to know that what follows is being placed before them with weight.

Musa uses *inna* on his own sin.

He does not soften it. He emphasizes it.

**Second**, look at the verb. *Qataltu* — "I killed." First person singular, past tense, Form I — the bare, raw, unembellished form of the verb. Arabic has other forms — Form II *qattala* (intensive killing, mass slaughter), Form III *qātala* (mutual combat), Form VIII *iqtatala* (fought one another). Each form would shift the meaning, color it, frame it.

Musa uses Form I. The plain form. **"I killed."**

He does not say "I was forced into a confrontation." He does not say "It was self-defense" — even though the earlier ayah (28:15) makes clear it was, in a sense. He does not say "He died" — the passive construction that puts the act at a distance. He does not say "There was an incident."

He says: **"I killed."** Subject. Verb. Done.

**Third**, and this is where the linguistic honesty deepens — look at the object. *Nafsan*. "A soul."

Indefinite. Unnamed. *A soul.* Not "an Egyptian." Not "an oppressor." Not "one of the enemy." Not even "a man."

**A soul.**

Stay with me. Because the choice of *nafs* here is doing something quietly devastating.

In Arabic, *nafs* is the most weighted word for a human life that exists. It is the word the Quran uses when it says "*whoever kills a soul... it is as if he killed all of humanity*" (5:32). It is the word that strips a person of every label — ethnicity, religion, station, allegiance — and leaves only the bare fact of their being a soul. A *nafs* has no team. A *nafs* is just a life.

So when Musa names what he did, he refuses to use any framing that would make it easier on himself. He does not call the man "Egyptian" — which would invoke the oppressor identity. He does not call him "enemy" — which would invoke justification. He calls him **a soul**.

And notice: the easier framings were not just rhetorically available — they were *factually true*. The classical commentators are explicit that the man Musa struck was a Copt, *min ʿaduwwihi* — "from his enemy," one of Pharaoh's own people — and that the Israelite being beaten had cried out to Musa for rescue against him (28:15). Musa could have named him as the aggressor he genuinely was. The man had a side; the man had a record. Musa erases all of it and leaves only *nafsan* — a soul.

He counts what he did at the highest possible weight.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this is showing us.

Musa is standing before Allah in the moment of his commission, and the way he refers to his old sin is the way Allah would refer to it. He uses the heaviest word. He does not negotiate the framing. He does not pre-package the act for sympathy.

Compare this to how we typically bring our sins to Allah. *Ya Rabb, you know I was going through a hard time. Ya Rabb, you know they pushed me first. Ya Rabb, you know I didn't mean it like that.* All of those framings might even be true. But notice — Musa has the truest framing of all. His killing genuinely was unintentional in its lethal force. The Quran's own verb for the act is *wakaza* — and the commentators gloss it as a single blow of the closed fist (*ḍarabahu bi-jumʿi kaffihi*), not a strike with any weapon. One punch, and the man died (28:15). That is the very definition of a death that exceeded its intent. He could have leaned on that. He could have said "I struck, and he died" — the killing-as-accident frame.

He doesn't.

He says **I killed**.

Why? Because in the presence of *rabbī* — the Lord who is mine — the relationship is not served by the smaller, easier framings. The relationship is served by **the truth at full weight**.

And here is the remarkable thing: the full weight is exactly what allows the relationship to hold it.

When you bring Allah the minimized version, He receives the minimized version — which means the part you held back is still sitting on you. When you bring Him the full-weight version, He receives the full-weight version — which means He can now do something with it. Mercy that meets the unminimized truth is mercy that actually heals. Mercy that meets a carefully-edited version of the truth only heals the carefully-edited version.

Musa, who has just been commissioned as a prophet, knows this instinctively. **You cannot carry a mission for Allah while you are still hiding something from Him.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn* — The Present-Tense Fear

Now the third element. And this is where the ayah opens.

*Fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn.*

"And I fear that they will kill me."

Look carefully at the verbs.

The first verb of this ayah — *qataltu* — was past tense. "I killed." Done. Finished. Ten years ago.

The verb in this phrase — *akhāfu* — is **present tense**. "I fear." Not "I feared." Not "I used to fear." Not even "I will fear when I get there."

**I am, right now, afraid.**

And the second verb — *yaqtulūn* — "they will kill me" — is in the imperfect tense, the *muḍāriʿ*, which in Arabic carries both present and future. It is the verb of the not-yet-finished. The action that is still in motion. The threat that is still alive.

Do you see what is being said?

The killing — *qataltu* — is sealed in the past. The fear — *akhāfu* — is alive in the present. And the imagined retaliation — *yaqtulūn* — is hanging in a future that has not yet been written.

This is the architecture of trauma named with surgical precision.

Trauma is exactly this: a past event that has been sealed and finished, but whose echo lives in present-tense fear and imagined future re-enactment. The event is over. The fear is now. The dread is forward-looking.

Stay with me. Because Musa is not describing a logical concern. He is not saying "Calculating the probability of being recognized in Egypt, I have determined a non-trivial risk of being killed." That would be in a different grammatical mode entirely. Musa is doing something more honest.

He is saying: *the killing is in my past. The fear is in my present body, right now, as I am standing here in front of You.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the small grammatical detail that completes the picture. The verb *yaqtulūn* — "they will kill me" — has had its final letter shortened. In standard Arabic, the full form would be *yaqtulūnanī* (with the *nī* meaning "me"). The Quran's spelling here drops the final *yā* of the object pronoun.

Many of the classical reciters and grammarians note this as a kind of *takhfīf*, a lightening. Linguistically, it preserves the rhyme and tightens the verse. But there is something I want you to feel about the **shape** of this word.

*Yaqtulūn.* It ends abruptly. The "me" — the *nī* — is half-swallowed. As if Musa cannot quite get the word out fully. As if the thought of his own killing has its tongue trip at the last syllable.

You do not need to take that as a documented grammatical claim — it is what the sound of the word invites us to consider. But notice that the verse does not say *yaqtulūnī* with the full pronoun standing tall. It says *yaqtulūn* — fear with the "me" half-vanished into the noise of the imagined crowd.

There is a man inside a fear that is already partially erasing him.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So here is what these three elements have shown us.

*Rabbī* — the relationship is named first.
*Innī qataltu nafsan* — the past is named at full weight, with no minimization.
*Fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn* — the present fear is named in present tense, with the imagined future still raw.

The grammar of this ayah is the grammar of someone who is **not editing himself in front of Allah**.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about what it actually means to stand before Allah with the thing you have been carrying.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Confession That Allah Already Knew

Let me start somewhere familiar.

Imagine you are a child, and you have done something. Maybe you broke something. Maybe you took something. Maybe you said something cruel to a sibling. And the entire household knows you did it. Your parents know. The evidence is all over the room. Your face is giving you away. Your hands are still sticky with whatever you did.

And your parent looks at you and waits.

They are not waiting for information. They have the information. They are waiting for **you**.

They are waiting for you to come out from behind the lie and into the room. They are waiting for the moment you stop performing innocence and admit what is already known.

And here is the strange psychology of that moment. The confession does not change what your parent knows. It changes what *you* know. It changes what you are willing to be seen as. It changes whether the relationship gets to hold what is true, or whether the relationship has to hold a quiet fiction forever.

That is what is happening here.

Allah did not need Musa to tell Him about the killing. Allah was there in the city — the classical commentators identify it as Memphis (Manf), entered at midday when its people were resting and heedless (*ʿalā ḥīni ghaflatin min ahlihā*, 28:15). Allah was the one who, ten years earlier, watched Musa flee "fearful, watching" (*khā'ifan yataraqqab*, 28:21). Allah orchestrated the entire ten-year exile in Madyan. Allah brought Musa back to this mountain. Allah scheduled this conversation.

There is nothing for Musa to inform Allah of.

[PAUSE]

But there is everything for Musa to **become**, in front of Allah.

Because here is the thing. A prophet cannot walk to Pharaoh while still hiding something from Allah. The mission cannot ride on a relationship that has an undisclosed weight in it. If Musa walks into Egypt with the killing still un-named between him and his Lord, then somewhere inside Musa there is a small voice that says: *but what if my Lord still holds this against me? What if the moment I get close to Pharaoh, my own past comes up and disqualifies me?*

That voice will sabotage the mission.

So Musa, with prophetic instinct, **clears the channel before he walks**.

He says: I am about to go do something enormous for You. Before I take a single step, I am putting on the table the thing that has been on me for ten years. Not because You need to hear it. Because I need to know that I have said it. I need to know that the relationship between us is not built on a silence I am keeping.

And here is a detail that changes how you should hear this confession. The *forgiveness* for this killing was not pending. It had already been granted, a decade earlier, in the moment it happened. The Quran preserves Musa's words from that very day: *rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī faghfir lī* — "My Lord, I have wronged myself, so forgive me" (28:16). And the verse answers immediately, in the same breath: *faghafara lahu* — "so He forgave him." The commentators read this as Musa's remorse and turning back (*nadam* and *tawba*) the instant the man fell — he even named the act as a provocation of Shaytan that had inflamed his anger (*min nazghi-sh-shayṭān*, who stirred up his rage until he struck), and the deed itself, al-Muyassar is careful to note, came *before* his prophethood (*qabl al-nubuwwa*) — in the years when Musa was still a young man of the palace, not yet the commissioned messenger standing at the bush. The guilt was confessed, and the forgiveness was sealed, ten years before he ever stood at the fire.

So sit with what that means for our ayah. When Musa says *innī qataltu minhum nafsan* at the bush, he is **not** re-opening a case that was already closed. He is not begging again for a pardon already given. The spiritual ledger was settled long ago. What remains is not guilt — it is the *worldly* echo of the deed: *fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn*, the fear that Pharaoh's people will exact their retaliation. A forgiven sin can still leave a consequence in the world. Musa names both halves with perfect precision: the part that was already healed between him and Allah, and the part that is still alive in the streets of Egypt. This is what mature honesty before Allah sounds like — not the panicked re-confession of something He has already forgiven, but the clear-eyed naming of where the wound still touches the world.

And there is a deeper irony the classical commentators draw out. Ibn Kathir, reading the broader passage, reports Mujahid's understanding that the *ḥukm* and knowledge Allah gave Musa here was prophethood itself — and he reads the whole arc as one hidden design: the very killing Musa is now naming was the event Allah used to *carry him to this mountain*. The blow led to the warning that the chiefs were conspiring; the warning led to the flight; the flight led to Madyan; Madyan led to the ten years; and the ten years led him back — to the fire, to the voice, to the commission. The verse just before this whole episode seals it: *wa kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn* — "and thus do We reward the doers of good" (28:14). The deed he confesses is not a stain standing *outside* his prophethood. It is, in Allah's ordering, bound up with the road *toward* it. So when Musa names *qataltu nafsan*, he is naming the hinge on which his entire calling turned. The thing he carries with the most shame is woven into the very path that brought him home.

This is the model of how a soul prepares itself for service.

[PAUSE]

And notice — Allah's response to this confession is *not* in this ayah. Musa names the killing, names the fear, and Allah does not say "Yes, you killed someone, and that disqualifies you." Allah also does not say "It's fine, don't worry about it" in a dismissive way. Allah, in the verses that follow, simply continues the mission — gives him his brother, expands his support, sends him forward.

The confession was received. The mission was not withdrawn.

This is what mercy looks like when it meets honesty. It does not haggle over the weight of what you bring. It just keeps walking with you.

Here is what this changes about how I live tomorrow. There is something I am carrying. There is something you are carrying. Each of us has a *qataltu nafsan* — maybe not literally a killing, but some old weight that we have not fully named in front of Allah. We have edited it. We have explained it in our own heads. We have framed it in the most sympathetic possible way. And meanwhile the weight is still there, sitting under everything we try to do for the sake of Allah.

The work tomorrow is to find a moment — alone, on a prayer mat, in the dark — to say *rabbī* and then name the thing **with no edits**.

Not because He needs to know.

Because **you** need to have said it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Fear Is Not Disqualifying — Hidden Fear Is

Now let me come at the second theme through a different door.

Imagine someone is hiring you for a job. Maybe a serious job. Maybe a job you want very badly. And they ask: "Are you nervous?"

There are two ways to answer.

The performance answer: "No, I'm completely confident, I've got this, I'm not nervous at all." And of course, your body is shaking. Your voice is doing the thing voices do. The interviewer can see the nervousness — but you have just told them you don't have it.

The honest answer: "Yes, I'm nervous. I want this. It matters."

The first answer makes you small. It makes you small because you are now performing instead of being present, and the gap between performance and reality is a kind of theft from the room.

The second answer makes you available. The nervousness gets to be in the room without having to be hidden. The energy spent suppressing it gets returned to you. And the interviewer, in nine cases out of ten, will respect the honest answer infinitely more.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Musa does.

Allah has just commissioned him to confront Pharaoh — the most fearsome figure on earth. A perfect prophet, in the easy stereotype, would say: "Yes, my Lord, I am ready, I fear nothing because You are with me."

Musa does not say this.

Musa says: **I am afraid.**

*Fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn.* In present tense. To the Lord of the worlds. Right after being told to go to Pharaoh.

Stay with me, because this is doing something theologically enormous. Musa is a prophet — *chosen, purified, commissioned*. And his first emotional disclosure to Allah, in the moment of his commission, is **fear**.

What does this tell us?

It tells us that prophetic excellence is not the absence of fear. It is the **honesty of fear in the presence of Allah**.

A prophet does not pretend to be a different kind of human. He does not perform a fearless invulnerability that he does not actually have. He brings his fear into the conversation and says: *this is what I am working with.*

And Allah does not say "A prophet should not be afraid." Allah responds, in the verses that come right after, by **giving him what he needs**. He gives him Harun. He gives him support. He gives him the assurance *innanī maʿakumā* — "indeed I am with the two of you" (20:46). He gives him the miracles to lean on.

[PAUSE]

The fear named is the fear that gets answered. The fear hidden is the fear that compounds.

This is one of the deepest psychological truths in the Quran. Allah does not punish you for being afraid. Allah meets you exactly at the point where your fear is honestly placed. But if you hide the fear — if you perform a confidence you don't have, if you say "I'm fine, I've got this" when you don't — then the very mechanism by which Allah's help reaches you gets blocked. Because the help is calibrated to the named need, not the performed competence.

Look at the famous du'a Musa makes shortly after this in Surah Taha:
*Rabbi-shraḥ lī ṣadrī wa yassir lī amrī wa-ḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī yafqahū qawlī wa-jʿal lī wazīran min ahlī Hārūna akhī.*

"My Lord, expand my chest for me, ease my task for me, untie the knot from my tongue so they will understand what I say, and appoint for me a helper from my family — Harun, my brother." (20:25-30)

Every single one of those requests is **specific**. Musa is not asking for "general help." He is asking for the **exact accommodations a frightened, speech-impeded, lone prophet needs to face an empire**. He knows his fears. He has named them. And so he can ask, with precision, for the help that will actually meet them.

This is what the confession in our ayah unlocks. The naming of *I am afraid* is the door through which *expand my chest* can walk. Without the naming, the du'a does not have a target.

[PAUSE]

So here is what changes about how I live tomorrow.

When I am standing in front of something enormous — a confrontation I am dreading, a responsibility I doubt I can carry, a sin I cannot stop returning to, a fear I would not say out loud — I have two options.

I can perform competence in front of Allah. *Ya Rabb, I've got this, just give me a little nudge.* And that prayer will get answered at the level of the performance.

Or I can name the fear at full weight. *Rabbī, I am afraid. I have been afraid for ten years. I do not know if I can do this. Here is the specific shape of my fear.* And that prayer gets answered at the level of the truth.

The size of Allah's response is calibrated to the honesty of your asking.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now, with both halves of the ayah open, look at the shape of what Musa said.

*Rabbi* — the relationship.
*Innī qataltu minhum nafsan* — the past sin, at full weight.
*Fa-akhāfu an yaqtulūn* — the present fear, named in present tense.

Three movements. Past sealed. Present fear. Future imagined.

But notice the **connective particle** in the middle. *Fa-* in *fa-akhāfu* — "and so I fear." That *fa-* is the Arabic particle of consequence. It does not just connect. It *causes*. It says: **because of what is in the past, this is what is in the present**.

The fear is not floating. The fear is attached to a specific event. Musa, in this single sentence, traces the entire chain — *here is what happened, and here is what it left in me.*

This is the model of bringing a wound to Allah. Not "I feel anxious in general." But: *here is the specific event, and here is the specific fear that grew out of it.*

The architecture of the ayah is the architecture of an honest soul. **Name the event. Name what the event did to you. Stop there. Let Allah be the One who does something with it.**

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us that every word of Musa's confession is honest at the highest possible weight — *rabbī* establishes the relationship, *qataltu* names the sin without softening, *akhāfu* names the fear without performing.

The thematic side showed us why this matters: because mission begins with honesty, and Allah's help is calibrated to the truthfulness of the asking.

The two halves meet at one point: **Allah is not served by your editing of yourself. Allah is served by your willingness to be seen.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *qataltu nafsan* you have not yet fully named in front of Allah? Not the version edited for sympathy — the version at full weight.

2. What is the present-tense fear that grew out of a past-tense event in your life? When you bring it to Allah, do you name the link — *because this happened, this is what I now carry* — or do you only name the present anxiety?

3. When you stand in front of something enormous, what is your default? To perform confidence to Allah, or to name your specific incapacity?

4. Is there someone in your life who has confessed something to you, and you treated it the way Allah treated Musa — by simply continuing to walk with them?

5. What would change about your du'a tomorrow if you addressed Allah with *rabbī* — *my Lord* — before you said anything else?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Before Musa asks Allah for anything, he names the heaviest thing he is carrying — because a soul cannot walk on mission while it is still hiding something from the One who sent it.**

### The Closing Invitation

There is a thing on you. There is a thing on me. Tonight, before you sleep, or tomorrow before fajr, or in the next quiet moment you can find — put it down.

Not the edited version. Not the version with the explanation already attached. The version that is just: *Rabbī, this is what I did. Rabbī, this is what I am afraid of.*

And then **stop talking**, and let Him be the One who decides what happens next. He has known about it the entire time. He has been waiting, not for the information, but for you.

### Du'a

Ya Rabb — the One to whom Musa whispered *rabbī* before he said anything else — let us address You that way. Receive from us the things we have not yet named. Receive the fears that have been sitting in our chests for years. And like You did not withdraw the mission from Musa after his confession, do not withdraw Your mercy from us after ours.

Rabbī, indeed we have carried things, and we are afraid. Meet us here.

Āmīn.
