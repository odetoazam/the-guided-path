# The Direction of the Lost

## Surah Al-Qasas — Ayah 22

> وَلَمَّا تَوَجَّهَ تِلْقَآءَ مَدْيَنَ قَالَ عَسَىٰ رَبِّىٓ أَن يَهْدِيَنِى سَوَآءَ ٱلسَّبِيلِ

*Wa lammā tawajjaha tilqāʾa Madyana qāla ʿasā Rabbī an yahdiyanī sawāʾa as-sabīl.*

"And when he turned his face toward Madyan, he said: 'Perhaps my Lord will guide me to the even path.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

I want you to stand for a moment outside a city you can no longer return to.

Behind you is everything you knew. The palace where you grew up. The mother who hid you in a basket. The sister who walked beside the river watching. The Pharaoh's household that fed and clothed and educated you for forty years. The streets you knew by name. The face of the Egyptian you killed yesterday, lying in the dust, his blood on your hands. The believer who whispered to you this morning, *"They are conspiring to kill you — leave."*

You have no horse. No food. No water. No sandals worth mentioning. You don't even have a destination — just *not here*. You walked out through whatever gate was open. The sun came up. The sun is now overhead. The sun is beginning to slope toward the west. And you have been walking, and walking, and walking, away from the only home you have ever known.

You are not a prophet yet. You don't know that you will be. You are a man in his prime, raised as royalty, who in the last forty-eight hours has discovered:

— that he is not who he thought he was,
— that his birth people are slaves,
— that his adoptive household wants him dead,
— that the future he was groomed for is finished,
— and that he has killed a man with his own hands.

You are alone in a way you have never been alone before.

Now — somewhere out past the last familiar landmark — you stop walking long enough to ask yourself the question every fugitive eventually asks:

*Where am I going?*

This is the moment Allah preserves for us. Not the dramatic flight. Not the killing. Not the warning. **The quiet moment when a man with nothing left whispers a sentence into the desert air.**

### The Strand This Ayah Sits On

Surah Al-Qaṣaṣ is the surah of *displacement and divine repositioning*. The mother of Musa is told to throw her child into the river. Musa is told to flee his city. Later in the surah, Qārūn is swallowed by the earth. The Pharaoh is drowned. Whole biographies are uprooted and re-rooted by Allah's hand.

Every major figure in this surah is *moved* — sometimes by hardship, sometimes by mercy, always by Allah. The surah asks: **what do you do when the ground beneath your life gives way?**

This ayah is the first answer.

### The Hook

Eleven words. That is all this ayah is. Eleven words for one of the most psychologically extreme moments in any prophet's life.

But notice — Musa does not ask Allah to save him. He does not ask for food. He does not ask for shelter. He does not ask for the road to Madyan to be made safe. He asks for something stranger and deeper than any of those things. And the word he uses for *asking* is not even a request — it is something far more humble than a request.

Stay with me. Because hidden in this one sentence is a complete theology of what to do when you do not know what to do.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tawajjaha tilqāʾa Madyan* — "He turned his face toward Madyan"

Before we open this phrase, let me teach you something about how Arabic talks about direction.

In English, when we say "he went to Madyan," the language is flat. He went. To Madyan. End of sentence. The verb and the destination do all the work.

Arabic does something different. Arabic can say "he went to Madyan" with a simple verb like *dhahaba ilā Madyan* — and that would be neutral, factual reporting. But Arabic can also say it the way Allah says it here: **tawajjaha tilqāʾa Madyan.**

And those two are not the same sentence.

Let's open the first word.

#### *Tawajjaha* — He turned his face

The root is و-ج-ه, *w-j-h*, and the basic noun is *wajh* — the face. When Arabic wants to describe directed motion, it has many verbs available. It could use *sāra* (he traveled), *dhahaba* (he went), *intaṭlaqa* (he set out), *qaṣada* (he aimed). Each is documented and available.

Allah chose *tawajjaha.*

*Tawajjaha* is Form V — a reflexive form built on the root for *face*. Literally: **he turned his own face.** It is a verb that puts the face — the *seat of identity, the seat of intention, the seat of recognition* — at the center of the action.

Notice what this implies. The motion of Musa toward Madyan is not described primarily as motion of the *feet*. It is described as the motion of the *face*. As though Allah is telling us: *what mattered in this moment was not the walking. What mattered was the orientation.*

Let's sit with this.

Musa had been walking for hours, maybe a day. His feet were doing the obvious work. But the ayah does not say "his feet carried him toward Madyan." It says **his face turned toward Madyan.** And in Arabic, the *face* is where the *self* points. Where your face points is where your *desire* points. Where your face points is where your *hope* points.

So the ayah is showing us something subtle: at this moment, Musa stopped wandering and *chose* a direction. Not in the sense of "I know the way" — he didn't. But in the sense of *I will let this be the way my self points.*

This is the difference between fleeing and journeying. Fleeing has no face. It only has a back — the back you are turning to what you escape. **Journeying has a face.** A face that has chosen, however dimly, what it is reaching for.

The moment Musa's face turns toward Madyan, he stops being purely a fugitive. He begins to become a traveler.

[PAUSE]

#### *Tilqāʾa* — Toward, in the direction of

Now look at the next word. *Tilqāʾa.*

This word is unusual. The root is ل-ق-ي, *l-q-y* — the root of *meeting, encountering, coming face to face with*. From this root come *liqāʾ* (a meeting), *talāqī* (mutual encounter), and the famous Quranic phrase *liqāʾ Allāh* — the meeting with Allah.

*Tilqāʾa* is one of several Arabic words for *toward*. Allah could have used *naḥwa* (toward), *ilā* (to), *jihata* (in the direction of), *ṣawba* (in the direction of). All available. All documented.

He chose *tilqāʾa.*

And *tilqāʾa* is the only one of those words that carries inside its very letters the meaning of *meeting*. Of *encounter*. Of *something coming toward you as you are going toward it.*

Stay with me here. This is delicate but important.

When the ayah says Musa turned his face *tilqāʾa Madyan*, it is hinting at something the reader will only fully understand after they read the next two ayahs. Because what is Madyan to Musa right now? Madyan is a *name on the wind*. A place he has heard of. He doesn't know its streets. He doesn't know its people. He has no contact there, no kinsman, no inn waiting.

But Allah uses the word that means *toward the meeting.* As if to whisper to us — the reader, who has the benefit of knowing what happens next — *Madyan is not just a destination. Madyan is where the meetings are. Madyan is where he will meet his future wife. Madyan is where he will meet his future father-in-law, the man who will train him for prophethood for ten years. Madyan is where he will meet the well that becomes his interview, the daughters who become his entry, and ultimately — through ten years of patience — the burning bush.*

The word *tilqāʾa* does not just point Musa at a location. It points him at *all the encounters Allah has prepared for him there.*

[PAUSE]

He thinks he is walking away from a death sentence. The vocabulary tells us he is walking *toward a meeting*.

This is one of the gentlest truths in the Quran. **When the believer is forced to leave one place, Allah's vocabulary already knows what is waiting in the next.**

#### Bringing the phrase together

So the phrase *tawajjaha tilqāʾa Madyan* is not just "he went to Madyan." It is, more precisely:

*He turned his self toward the encounters that Madyan was waiting to give him.*

Eleven syllables in Arabic to do work that English cannot do in eleven sentences.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *ʿAsā Rabbī an yahdiyanī* — "Perhaps my Lord will guide me"

Now let's go to the prayer itself. And here we need to teach you something about how Arabic asks.

In Arabic, there are many ways to make a request. You can use the imperative: *ihdinī* — *Guide me!* (This is what we say in al-Fātiḥah every day: *ihdinā aṣ-ṣirāṭa al-mustaqīm*.) You can use the verb of asking: *as'aluka an tahdiyanī* — *I ask You to guide me.* You can use a wish particle: *layta Rabbī yahdīnī* — *Would that my Lord guide me.*

All of these are available. All documented. All grammatically clean.

But here, in this ayah, in this most desperate moment of Musa's young life, when his back is to a city that wants him dead and his face is toward a place he has never been — he uses none of these.

He uses *ʿasā.*

#### *ʿAsā* — The Particle of Hopeful Expectation

*ʿAsā* is one of the most beautiful and humble particles in the Quran. It is classified in classical Arabic grammar as a *verb of hopeful expectation* — *fiʿl al-rajāʾ.* It translates roughly as "perhaps" or "it may be" or "it is hoped that."

But notice what *ʿasā* is *not*.

*ʿasā* is not a command. It is not a demand. It is not a wish. It is not even a direct request. It is the language of a servant who *hopes* his master will do something — without presuming, without insisting, without claiming any right to it.

When *ʿasā* is used by a human being about Allah, it expresses a particular spiritual posture: **I do not deserve this. I am not demanding this. I am hoping. And the hoping is itself a form of worship.**

Now, the scholars of tafsīr note something stunning about *ʿasā* in the Quran. When *ʿasā* is used by Allah — *ʿasā Allāhu an yaʾtiya bil-fatḥi* (perhaps Allah will bring a victory) — the classical scholars say that *ʿasā* from Allah carries the weight of certainty. From Allah, "perhaps" is "definitely." From the slave, "perhaps" is "hopefully."

So when Musa says *ʿasā Rabbī*, he is using a word that is, in Arabic, *small enough not to presume* but, when spoken to Allah, *large enough to come true*.

#### Why not "Guide me!"?

Why doesn't Musa just say *ihdinī yā Rabb* — *Guide me, my Lord!*?

Because Musa, at this moment, is not yet a prophet. He has just killed a man. He has just fled a city. He has just been told he is wanted by the authorities. He is — in his own self-image — *a man who has done wrong*.

And the man-who-has-done-wrong does not stand before his Lord and *command*. Even when the command is for guidance. Even when the command is permissible. Even when al-Fātiḥah authorizes him to say *ihdinā.*

Musa, in this raw, exposed moment, *softens* the request to its humblest possible form. **Perhaps.** *ʿasā.*

This is the prayer of someone who is not sure they deserve to be heard, but who hopes — quietly, painfully, almost wordlessly — that they will be heard anyway.

Notice something. Notice how much *room* this leaves Allah. Musa does not specify *what kind* of guidance. He does not specify *when* it should come. He does not specify *what road* he prefers. He uses the most open, most surrendered, most "Your call, my Lord" form of request that the Arabic language possesses.

This is the prayer of total surrender. **Not "give me what I want." Not even "give me what I need." But: *I am hoping. You decide.***

[PAUSE]

#### *Rabbī* — *My* Lord

And inside that prayer, look at the word he uses for Allah. Not *Allāh*. Not *al-Khāliq*. Not *al-Qādir*. He says *Rabbī.*

The root ر-ب-ب, *r-b-b*, carries in classical Arabic the meanings of *nurturer, sustainer, owner, master, the one who raises something from one stage to the next.* A *rabb* is the one who *brings a thing from where it is to where it should be.* The mother is the *rabba* of the child in some lexical uses. The farmer is the *rabb* of the field. The master is the *rabb* of the household.

When Musa says *Rabbī* — *my Rabb* — at this moment, he is not just saying "my Lord." He is saying: **the One who has been bringing me from stage to stage my whole life. The One who pulled me from the river. The One who placed me in Pharaoh's house. The One who returned me to my mother to nurse. The One who let me grow up safe. The One who is still bringing me from stage to stage — even now, even when it looks like collapse.**

*Rabbī* is the name of Allah you say when you remember that the One in charge of your story has been in charge of your story *the whole time*.

It is the perfect name for this moment. Because what Musa is really asking is: *Rabb, you have moved me before. Move me again.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Sawāʾa as-Sabīl* — "The even path"

Now let's open the destination of his prayer. The thing he hopes to be guided *to*.

*Sawāʾa as-sabīl.*

Two words. Both worth dwelling on.

#### *Sawāʾ* — Evenness, levelness, balance

The root س-و-ي, *s-w-y*, in classical Arabic carries the meaning of *being even, being level, being equal, being balanced, being straightened out.* From this root come *istawā* (he sat upright, he settled evenly), *taswiyah* (leveling), *musāwāh* (equality), and *sawāʾ* (evenness).

When Allah describes the creation of the human being, He says *sawwā* — He *evened him out, balanced him, proportioned him.* When Allah describes the throne, He uses *istawā* — *He settled upon it.* These are all from this same root of *evenness, levelness, balance.*

*Sawāʾ as-sabīl,* then, is not just "the right path." It is **the path that is even** — meaning, the path that does not tilt, the path that does not pitch you forward or sideways, the path that holds you upright as you walk, the path on which you do not stumble.

#### *Sabīl* — Path

The root س-ب-ل, *s-b-l*, refers to a *track, a way, a road that has been used and is therefore visible.* A *sabīl* is different from a *ṭarīq* (a more general word for path) and from a *ṣirāṭ* (a wide, clearly demarcated highway, like the *ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* in al-Fātiḥah).

A *sabīl* is a *worn path* — a way that someone has walked before. It implies precedence. It implies that *someone* has gone this way and made it visible enough for you to follow.

So *sawāʾ as-sabīl* is: **the even way, the balanced track, the road that someone has walked before and that does not tilt the walker off balance.**

#### The Question Hidden in the Phrase

Now, here is where we have to pause and notice something stunning.

Musa is asking to be guided to *sawāʾ as-sabīl* — *the even path.* But he is, *physically*, walking on a desert path right now. He has a direction. He has feet. He is moving.

So what is he asking for?

[PAUSE]

He is not asking for a *physical* road. He has one. He is on it.

He is asking for an *inner* road. The *even* road. The road of the soul that does not pitch and tilt. The road of the heart that does not stumble. The road of life that does not throw him into the kinds of mistakes that produced yesterday's killing.

Look back at the ayahs just before this one. Musa, the day before, intervened in a fight on his people's behalf and *killed a man*. He did not mean to. The Quran is clear — it was a blow, not an execution. But it was still a death. And in the next ayah, Musa himself says: *"This is from the work of Shayṭān"* (28:15). He sees, with horror, that he has gone *off-balance.* He has tilted. He has stumbled.

So when he asks to be guided to *sawāʾ as-sabīl,* he is not asking for directions to Madyan. He has a direction. **He is asking to be made even again.** To be straightened. To be re-balanced. To be the kind of man who does not pitch into wrong action.

This is a prayer about character before it is a prayer about geography.

And notice — this is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* in its purest form. The physical image of a road that is level, that does not tilt, that does not throw the walker off balance — Allah uses this concrete, walkable image to point at the inner reality of a soul that is upright, balanced, not tilting into sin, not pitching into rage, not stumbling under pressure.

Musa is on a desert track asking for an inner track.

[PAUSE]

#### Bridge

So look at what these three linguistic elements together have shown us.

*Tawajjaha tilqāʾa Madyan* — he turned his face toward the encounters Madyan was waiting to give him.

*ʿAsā Rabbī an yahdiyanī* — perhaps my Sustainer, the One who has been moving me my whole life, will guide me.

*Sawāʾa as-sabīl* — to a path that does not tilt me, that does not throw me off balance again.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about what to do when your life collapses.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Prayer of the Person Who Has Lost Their Map

Let me start somewhere you know.

There is a particular kind of moment in a life. Not the dramatic moment — the moment *after* the dramatic moment. Not the breakup, but the morning after the breakup, when you wake up and realize you have a whole life to figure out and you don't know how. Not the firing, but the second week, when the severance is real but the email replies have dried up and you're staring at a ceiling at 11 a.m. on a Tuesday wondering what *exactly* you're going to do with the rest of your years. Not the diagnosis, but the third night, when the shock has worn off and the future has changed and the future has not yet arrived.

The moment *after* the collapse, when the noise has died down and the silence has come in, and you realize: **I do not know where I am going.**

This is the moment of this ayah.

Now — what do most of us do in that moment?

We *plan*. We pull out the laptop. We make a list. We strategize. We message friends. We ask for advice. We try, with everything we have, to *figure out what to do next.*

And there is nothing wrong with planning. But notice what Musa does *not* do.

Musa does not, in this moment, sit down and figure out his career pivot. He does not draft a five-year plan. He does not even, in this ayah, ask Allah for a place to sleep or food to eat or water to drink — even though he needs all three.

He asks for **orientation.**

He asks to be *pointed in the right inner direction*.

This is the move of a soul who has understood something most of us miss. When you have lost your map, the first thing to ask for is not a new map. The first thing to ask for is *the inner compass that will make the next map readable when it comes.*

Sit with this.

Musa is hours into the desert. He has no food. No water. No plan. And he understands, with a clarity that I think most of us only access in our worst moments, that *what he needs is not provision. What he needs is direction.* Because if he has direction — if he is pointed at *sawāʾ as-sabīl,* the path that does not tilt — then provision will come. Food will come. Madyan will come. The well, the wife, the father-in-law, the ten years, the burning bush, the staff, the parting of the sea — *all of it will come.* But only because he was, in this moment, pointed correctly.

Provision follows direction. Not the other way around.

#### The Inversion We Live In

Most of us, in our quiet moments, do the reverse. We ask Allah for the *job*, not for the *path that will hold us evenly* through whatever the job is. We ask Allah for the *spouse*, not for the *uprightness* that would make us a good spouse. We ask Allah for the *income*, not for the *balanced soul* that would hold whatever income with gratitude. We ask Allah for the *fix*, not for the *self* that would not have produced the problem.

Musa, in this ayah, inverts the order. He asks for the *inner* first. The outer can wait.

And what is remarkable is — *the outer comes anyway.* Read the next two ayahs. He arrives at the well of Madyan. The daughters appear. The water is given. The home is opened. The years are filled.

But the prayer that opened all of that was not "give me a home." The prayer was "*perhaps my Sustainer will guide me to the even path.*"

#### What This Changes Tomorrow

What does this mean for you, today?

When your life pitches forward into a place you didn't plan — and it will, again, eventually, in some form, because that is what life does — there is a prayer to make before you make the other prayers. Before *ya Rabb, fix this job*. Before *ya Rabb, send this spouse*. Before *ya Rabb, heal this body*.

*ʿAsā Rabbī an yahdiyanī sawāʾa as-sabīl.*

Perhaps my Sustainer will guide me to the path that does not tilt.

Because if He guides you there — then whatever else comes, you will walk it standing upright. And if He does not — then no fixed job, no answered marriage, no healed body will hold you steady, because the path itself is tilted.

This is the prayer of the person who has understood that the question is never really "*what's next?*" The question is always "*what kind of person will I be when next arrives?*"

### Theme Two: *ʿAsā* — The Theology of Hope Without Demand

Let me bring you back to that strange little word. *ʿAsā.*

I want to tell you something about the spiritual culture we live in. We have been taught — by motivational speakers, by self-help books, by a certain kind of well-meaning religious teacher — that the way to ask Allah is *with certainty*. That you should "claim it." That you should "visualize it." That you should "ask boldly." That weak prayer is unbelief and strong prayer is faith.

And there is *some* truth in that. The Prophet ﷺ taught us to ask Allah with confidence in His ability and generosity. We are told not to say *if You will, forgive me* — we are told to ask decisively.

But look at this ayah.

Musa, in his most desperate moment, in his most needful hour, **asks with the most tentative possible word in the Arabic language.**

*Perhaps.*

Why?

[PAUSE]

Because there is a difference between *certainty about Allah's ability* and *presumption about Allah's decision.*

A believer is, at every moment, certain that Allah *can* do whatever He wills. A believer is, at every moment, *not presuming* about what Allah *will* do. Allah's *can* is total. Allah's *will* is His sovereignty alone.

And *ʿasā* is the precise vocabulary of a soul that has internalized both. It says: *I know You can. I do not presume what You will. I am hoping. Your call.*

This is not weak prayer. This is *educated* prayer. This is the prayer of the slave who has understood his slavery and his Master's sovereignty so deeply that the very word he uses for "ask" has surrender built into it.

#### Where this lives in your life

Think of the people you love. Have you ever wanted, *desperately*, to ask someone you love for something — and you have been so aware of how much they have already given you, so aware of how little right you have to ask for more, that the request comes out of you not as a demand but as a whisper? *Maybe... if it isn't too much... if it's possible... I was wondering...*

That whisper is *ʿasā.*

It is not the whisper of someone who doubts the beloved's capacity to give. It is the whisper of someone who is so flooded with awareness of the beloved's already-existing generosity that to ask boldly would feel like an insult.

This is how Musa speaks to his Rabb in the desert.

He has already been carried in a basket on a river. He has already been pulled from the water. He has already been returned to his mother to nurse. He has already been raised in safety in the enemy's house. He has already, even today, been warned in time to escape. He has already received, *already*, more than he could count.

To stand in that desert and *demand* would be a kind of forgetting.

So he whispers. *Perhaps.* Because *perhaps* is the word of someone who remembers.

#### What This Changes Tomorrow

There is a prayer you have been afraid to make.

Maybe because you feel you don't deserve it. Maybe because you have made it before and it wasn't answered the way you wanted. Maybe because you feel the asking itself is presumptuous, given how much you've already been given.

*ʿasā* is for you.

You don't have to demand. You don't have to "claim it." You don't have to perform certainty you don't feel. You can just whisper *perhaps* — and that whisper, in the vocabulary of the Quran, is the most accepted form of prayer the desperate possess.

*ʿasā Rabbī an yarḥamanī.* Perhaps my Sustainer will have mercy on me.
*ʿasā Rabbī an yaʿfuwa ʿannī.* Perhaps my Sustainer will pardon me.
*ʿasā Rabbī an yajʿala lī mukhrajan.* Perhaps my Sustainer will make for me a way out.

This is not a smaller prayer. This is the prayer Musa made in the desert. And the desert opened.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what eleven words have shown us.

*Tawajjaha* taught us that journeying is not about feet — it is about where the *face* of the self is pointed.

*Tilqāʾa* taught us that Allah's vocabulary already knows the meetings He has prepared in the place we are reluctantly walking toward.

*ʿAsā* taught us that the most accepted prayer is sometimes the most tentative one — that *perhaps* in the mouth of the slave is enough.

*Rabbī* taught us that the One we ask is not a distant deity but the Sustainer who has been moving us from stage to stage since the first breath we took.

*Yahdiyanī sawāʾa as-sabīl* taught us that the first thing to ask for, when life pitches forward into the unknown, is not a new map but an inner path that does not tilt.

And all of these are *the same insight from different angles*. The insight is this:

**When your life collapses, the work is not to figure out what to do. The work is to turn your face toward Allah, whisper *perhaps*, and ask for the inner steadiness that will let you walk whatever comes next without falling over.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When the ground of your life last gave way, what did you ask for first — a fix, or a *self that could hold the fix*?

2. Is there a prayer you have been afraid to make boldly that you could make as *ʿasā* — as *perhaps* — tonight?

3. If you had to describe, honestly, the direction your *face* is pointed right now — not your feet, not your calendar, but your *face*, the seat of your hope and intention — where would you say it is pointed?

4. What would it mean, in your specific life, to ask Allah for *sawāʾ as-sabīl* — the path that does not tilt you?

5. Has there been a Madyan in your life — a place you were forced to walk toward, that you only later realized was the place of all the meetings Allah had prepared for you?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**When you cannot see your way, do not ask for the way — ask for the *self* that can walk any way Allah opens.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, you do not need to plan your life. You do not need to have answers. You do not need to know what is next.

You only need to do what Musa did in the desert.

Turn your face — not your feet, your *face* — toward Allah. And whisper *ʿasā.* Perhaps. And then ask, very simply, very humbly, for what Musa asked for. Not money. Not relief. Not even rescue.

The even path.

The road that does not tilt.

The inner steadiness that will let you walk whatever ground Allah lays beneath your feet tomorrow.

And then sleep. Because the One you have whispered to has been moving you from stage to stage your whole life, and the next stage — wherever it is — is already prepared.

### Du'ā

*Yā Rabb, our Sustainer who has carried us from stage to stage when we did not know we were being carried — when the ground gives way beneath our lives, turn our faces toward You before our feet move. Accept from us the smallest, most tentative *perhaps* as You accepted it from Your slave Mūsā in the desert. And guide us — not to the road we have planned, but to the path that does not tilt us, until we meet You upright. Āmīn.*
