# What They Feared Most: How the Oppressor Builds the Ladder for the Oppressed

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 6

### The Arabic

> [ayah:28:6] وَنُمَكِّنَ لَهُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَنُرِىَ فِرْعَوْنَ وَهَـٰمَـٰنَ وَجُنُودَهُمَا مِنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَحْذَرُونَ

### Transliteration

*Wa numakkina lahum fī al-arḍi wa nuriya Firʿawna wa Hāmāna wa junūdahumā minhum mā kānū yaḥḏharūn.*

### Translation

*And to establish them firmly in the land, and to show Pharaoh and Haman and their soldiers — from among those very people — the thing they had been guarding against.*

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before this ayah can land, we need to understand what kind of moment it speaks into.

Imagine standing in a courtroom where the verdict has just been announced — except the trial hasn't happened yet. Allah opens Surah Al-Qasas not by narrating history but by *announcing destiny*. He lays out, in five verses, what He intends. Then — only then — He begins the story of Musa.

This is deliberate. It is one of the most daring narrative choices in the Quran. The reader knows the ending before a single scene has played out. And this means the question driving the entire surah is not: *Will the oppressed be saved?* That's already settled. The question is: *How does Allah accomplish what seems impossible?*

The world the surah enters is specific and terrible. Verse 4 has just described a civilization built on a single logic: the systematic destruction of a people. Pharaoh divided his society into factions. He found one group and *weakened* them — deliberately, systematically — killing their sons, sparing their women. In the ancient world, a people without sons is a people without a future. The infanticide isn't just cruelty. It's mathematics. One generation of killing, and the problem disappears by itself.

This is Pharaoh's plan. It is the most sophisticated oppression imaginable: not random violence but targeted, generational erasure.

And into this world, Allah says something that should have been inconceivable: *We intend to favor those people. We intend to make them leaders. We intend to make them inheritors.* And then, as if that weren't enough, He adds this ayah — the final line of the opening declaration:

We will *establish* them. We will *show* Pharaoh and Haman and all their armies, from among those very people, the exact thing they spent their entire lives trying to prevent.

Here is what's stunning about that sentence. Pharaoh didn't just oppress randomly. He built a *system* specifically designed to prevent one outcome. And Allah says: He will cause Pharaoh to *see* that outcome — to be made to witness it — and it will come from within the very people he was oppressing.

Let us sit inside that for a moment before we go any further.

The most powerful empire in the ancient world built its entire apparatus of terror around a single fear. And Allah says: I will show them that fear, made real, delivered from among those they oppressed.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *Numakkina*: The Difference Between Rescue and Roots

Let's start with what seems like the simpler word — the first verb in this ayah — and discover it isn't simple at all.

In Arabic grammar, there is a concept called *mazid fīh* — the "augmented" verb form. Arabic verbs begin in a basic, unaugmented form (called Form I), and then grow in complexity through a series of patterns, each carrying its own shade of meaning. One of the most important patterns is what we call Form II: you double the middle root letter, and something changes. The basic meaning intensifies, deepens, or becomes applied to an object in a new way.

Think of it like the difference between lighting a candle and *illuminating* a room. The action is related — both involve light — but the second verb carries a sense of thoroughness, of something actively being done to an object from the outside.

Now listen to the root at the heart of this word: **م-ك-ن** (*m-k-n*).

The root means *place* — to have a place, to be settled somewhere, to belong. The noun derived from it — *makān* — is the Arabic word for "location." To *kana* (the basic Form I verb) is to be in a place. To have ground beneath your feet. To belong somewhere.

Now Allah chooses Form II: *numakkina*.

[PAUSE]

He doesn't just promise the oppressed a *place*. He promises to *actively establish* them — to deeply root them, to make them belong to the land in a way that no power on earth can displace. The doubled *kāf* — that intensification in the middle of the verb — carries the weight of permanence. This is not liberation followed by fragility. This is *tamkīn*: thoroughgoing, embedded, authoritative establishment.

Notice what this implies about the people who are being established. The preceding ayah called them *alladhīna ustuḍʿifū fī al-arḍ* — "those who were deliberately weakened in the land." The very same earth — *al-arḍ* — appears in both verse 5 and verse 6. The same land in which they were weakened is the land in which they will be *established*. The geography doesn't change. The power within it does.

There is something here worth staying with. Many people who escape oppression carry a kind of inner homelessness with them long after the oppressor is gone. They are free, but not established. They are safe, but not rooted. They are no longer enslaved, but they do not yet know how to *belong* — to the earth, to history, to their own future. This is the wound that outlasts the chains.

Allah does not promise the oppressed merely *relief*. He promises *tamkīn*. He promises that the same ground that witnessed their humiliation will witness their authority.

That is not a small promise.

---

### Element Two — *Nuriya*: Allah Makes Pharaoh See

Now we come to the word that carries the real devastation of this ayah. And to hear it properly, we need to understand another Arabic verb pattern.

In Arabic, Form IV — the *af'ala* pattern — is called the causative form. It doesn't just describe an action. It describes someone *causing* that action to happen in someone else. In English we approximate this with "make" or "cause." But Arabic bakes it into the verb's very structure.

The basic root here is **ر-أ-ي** (*r-ʾ-y*): *to see*. The basic Form I verb (*raʾā*) simply means "to see." But Form IV — *arā* — means *to make see*, to *cause seeing*, to *show*.

Allah says: *wa nuriya Firʿawna wa Hāmāna* — "and We *cause Pharaoh and Haman to see*" — "and We *show* Pharaoh and Haman."

[PAUSE]

Stay with that.

Pharaoh's entire life — every policy, every edict, every act of violence — was about *control*. Control over the Nile. Control over the calendar. Control over who lived and who died. And when reports reached him that a liberator would arise from Banu Israel, his response was absolute: control the birth-rate. Control the next generation. Control the future.

And here, in this single word — *nuriya*, Form IV, Allah as subject — we see the final accounting of all that control. **Allah is the one who causes Pharaoh to see.** Not Musa. Not an army. Allah Himself. The verb's subject is the divine "We." The object is Pharaoh.

Pharaoh could control his soldiers. He could not control his own eyes — or what would be placed before them.

There is something almost unbearable about this formulation. At the end of everything, the one thing Pharaoh could not govern was his own perception of his defeat. He would *be made* to see it. The Arabic doesn't leave him the dignity of looking away. The causative form removes his agency entirely in the moment of witnessing.

And what exactly does Allah cause him to see?

*Minhum mā kānū yaḥḏharūn.*

From among those very people — the thing they were guarding against.

We'll come to that phrase. But first — the name that stands beside Pharaoh.

---

### Element Three — *Yaḥḏharūna*: The Fear That Builds Systems

Why does the ayah say *yaḥḏharūna* and not simply *yakhāfūna* — they feared?

Arabic has a rich vocabulary for fear. *Khawf* is general fear — the fear you feel when a storm comes, when danger appears. *Khashyah* is awe-mingled fear — the fear of a greatness that dwarfs you, often used in the Quran for the fear of Allah. *Rahbah* is terror — panic, dread.

But *ḥidhr* — the root of *yaḥḏharūna* — is something specific. Classical Arabic dictionaries define it as *precautionary* fear: the vigilant, watchful anxiety that makes you take action to prevent what you dread. You don't just *feel* this fear. You *build around it*. You create systems because of it. You establish policies. You organize your life in response to it.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means about Pharaoh and Haman.

*Kānū yaḥḏharūn* — they *were* (persistently, continuously) guarding against something. The combination of the past-tense *kānū* with the ongoing-action *yaḥḏharūn* creates a picture of habitual, architectural fear. This wasn't a passing dread. It was the animating logic of their entire governance. Every time a son was born to a family of Banu Israel, there was a calculation. Every policy — the infanticide, the forced labor, the systematic weakening — emerged from this *ḥidhr*: this strategic, precautionary anxiety about what might come from among those people.

And then Allah's sentence closes with three Arabic words that carry everything:

**مِنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا يَحْذَرُونَ** — *from among them, the very thing they were guarding against.*

The "them" (*minhum*) refers back to *alladhīna ustuḍʿifū* — the deliberately weakened ones. The very people Pharaoh had been systematically destroying. And Allah says: what they feared came *from within* those people. Not from an external power. Not from a foreign army. From inside the community that was being oppressed.

Here is where the linguistic structure does something extraordinary. Pharaoh's entire system of *ḥidhr* — his precautionary apparatus — was directed outward and downward. Kill the sons. Control the birth-rate. Manage the population. But Allah's promise moved *within* the oppressed community, beneath the reach of every precaution.

The thing Pharaoh feared most was already there. It had been there all along.

---

### Bridge to the Thematic Depths

What the linguistic structure has shown us is this: there are two divine actions in this ayah, precisely calibrated to their recipients.

For the oppressed: *tamkīn* — deep, intensive, thorough establishment (Form II, intensified).

For the oppressor: *nuriya* — a causing of seeing, a forced witnessing of what was feared (Form IV, causative).

Allah does not just reverse the situation. He designs the reversal. The oppressed are not merely freed — they are established with an intensity that matches the intensity of the oppression. And the oppressor is not merely defeated — he is *made to see* his defeat coming from within the very people he crushed.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The System That Builds Its Own Undoing

There is a concept in social science called the *self-fulfilling prophecy*: when you believe something enough, you act in ways that make it come true. The student who believes they'll fail stops trying. The investor who believes the market will crash withdraws, contributing to the crash.

But there is a lesser-discussed shadow version of this: the *self-defeating precaution*. When the thing you fear is so vivid, and your response to it so total, that your very efforts to prevent it create the conditions for it to happen.

Pharaoh had heard a prophecy — or some version of it. A liberator would arise from Banu Israel. His response was architectural: he would make sure it couldn't happen. Kill every male child born to them. Drain the next generation before it could produce anyone dangerous.

But now follow what his precaution actually produced.

A mother, terrified for her newborn son, places him in a basket on the Nile. The basket travels downstream. It enters — of all places — Pharaoh's own palace. His wife Asiya sees it and is moved. She appeals to Pharaoh: "This child will be a comfort for me and for you — do not kill him, perhaps he will be of benefit to us, or we may adopt him as a son." And Pharaoh — the man who ordered the infanticide of an entire generation — agrees.

[PAUSE]

Stay with the irony. Pharaoh's own wife, softened by the very orphaned vulnerability that the infanticide campaign had created — because no mother would send her child into the Nile unless she was desperate — uses that vulnerability to bring the liberator into the palace.

And then: the infant refuses every wet-nurse in the palace. No one can feed him. Allah says in verse 12: *wa ḥarramnā ʿalayhi al-marāḍiʿ min qabl* — "We had made unlawful for him all wet-nurses before." So they search. And the sister of Musa, who has been watching from a distance, steps forward: "Shall I direct you to a household that will nurse him for you and take care of him?"

She takes them back to the mother.

The very woman whose son was supposed to be killed — by Pharaoh's decree — ends up nursing her own child *inside Pharaoh's palace*, paid by Pharaoh's treasury.

The oppressor's machinery of control delivered the liberator to the safest house in Egypt. Fed him. Educated him. Gave him royal knowledge. The palace — the seat of the oppression — became the incubator for the revolution.

*Minhum mā kānū yaḥḏharūn.* From among them — the exact thing they feared.

This is not irony for its own sake. This is a window into something true about how power operates when it is arrayed against divine will. The instruments of control are never absolute. They always have a seam. They are always, somewhere, working against themselves.

Because control driven by fear — *ḥidhr* — is inherently anxious. And anxious systems make exceptions. Pharaoh's wife made an exception. And through that exception, Musa grew up.

There is a profound message here for every person sitting under a structure that seems immovable. The very extremity of an oppressive system — its totalizing quality, its reach into every corner — generates the pressure that eventually finds the seam. It cannot help it. Because it is driven by the fear of the very people it is trying to suppress, and that fear means it can never fully look away from them. And in always watching them, it is always, somewhere, inadvertently serving them.

[PAUSE]

This is not a counsel of passivity. Musa's mother did something — she placed the basket. His sister did something — she followed. These are acts of audacious, trusting courage. But the point is: they were working with a current that was already running. The current of divine intention had been declared before the story began.

### Theme Two — Tamkīn: The Difference Between Being Saved and Being Rooted

Let's go back to that first word.

Think about what it means to be *established*. Not just free. Not just safe. But established.

Most of us have experienced some version of rescue — a moment where a situation that was crushing us ended. A job loss that resolved. A medical scare that passed. A relationship that mended. And we know, from the inside, that the end of the crisis is not the end of the wound. Because relief is not the same as establishment. You can be out of danger and still be unrooted. You can be free of the oppressor and still carry him inside you — still make decisions as if his gaze were still there, still flinch at shadows, still measure your safety by whether *he* is satisfied.

The Arabic word the Quran uses for what Allah promises is not *najāt* — salvation, escape. It's *tamkīn* — from the root of *place*, of belonging, of being anchored.

*Numakkina lahum fī al-arḍ.* We will establish them — thoroughly, intensively — *in the earth*.

The same earth that had been the theater of their humiliation.

There is something here about the nature of healing that the Quran is pointing toward. The healing of oppression does not happen by leaving the earth behind and going somewhere else. It happens by being *established in the very earth* where the wound occurred. Not escape into an abstraction. Roots, going down into the same soil.

This is why the language of inheritors (*wārithīn*) in the preceding ayah is so significant. Inheritors don't abandon the estate. They receive it. They take what was held over them and find themselves, by divine design, *holding* it. Not as conquerors drunk on revenge — but as those to whom it was always destined to belong.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the full weight of this against the Meccan backdrop.

This surah was revealed to a community that knew exactly what it meant to be *mustadʿafīn* — deliberately weakened. The early Muslims in Mecca were mocked, tortured, boycotted, killed. They were not an abstract oppressed community. They were specific people with specific names, who had lost family members, who had been driven out of their homes, who had been told by the most powerful people around them: *you are nothing, you will be nothing.*

And Allah reveals a surah that begins with this declaration: *We intend to favor the deliberately weakened. We intend to make them leaders. We intend to make them inheritors. We will establish them in the earth. And We will cause the oppressors to see the very thing they feared — coming from among those people.*

This is not just comfort. This is a reordering of reality. It is the invisible counter-decree running beneath the visible tyranny.

And then Allah tells the story of Musa — the full, detailed story — as evidence. As proof that the decree has already been executed before. That this is not wishful thinking. It is the pattern of how divine will moves through human history.

The Meccan Muslims who heard this surah didn't just receive a story. They received a mirror. And in it, they saw themselves — and they saw the ending.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Now that the linguistic and thematic work is complete, look at the structure of the divine declaration in verses 5 and 6 as a whole:

*We intend* —

→ to bestow favor on the deliberately weakened *(the gift of divine attention)*

→ to make them leaders *(authority)*

→ to make them inheritors *(possession)*

→ to establish them in the earth *(tamkīn — depth, roots, permanence)*

→ to show Pharaoh and Haman and their armies, from among those people, what they feared *(nuriya — forced witnessing)*

Notice what comes last. The oppressor's defeat is the *final* item on the list. The entire focus of the declaration is on what Allah will do *for* the oppressed — the cascade of favor, authority, inheritance, and establishment. Pharaoh's collapse is almost an afterthought, mentioned last, as the inevitable conclusion of the empowerment that precedes it.

This is not a story about defeating Pharaoh. It is a story about establishing the oppressed. Pharaoh's defeat is just what happens when that establishment is complete.

### The Two Verbs, Two Directions

The two verbs we examined — *numakkina* (Form II, intensified) and *nuriya* (Form IV, causative) — move in opposite directions, toward their respective recipients.

Toward the oppressed: an action of *deepening*. Going down into the earth. Making firm. Creating roots.

Toward the oppressor: an action of *forcing outward*. Making perception happen. Removing the ability to look away.

Allah designs both. The establishment of the oppressed and the witnessing of the oppressor are not two separate events. They are one event seen from two directions. And in the word *minhum* — *from among them* — the two are bound together: the liberation of the oppressed is itself what the oppressor is forced to see.

### Questions to Carry

What in my life am I building systems of *ḥidhr* around — anxious, precautionary structures designed to prevent an outcome I fear? And is there any possibility that those very structures are accelerating what I dread?

When I pray for deliverance, do I find myself hoping for *najāt* (escape) or *tamkīn* (establishment)? What would it mean to be not just free of a difficulty but *rooted* after it?

In the moment when Pharaoh agreed to keep the infant — the moment his system broke — was he making a choice, or was he moved? Where in my own life has the counter-current of divine will found a seam in something I thought was solid?

What does it mean that Allah says He will *cause Pharaoh to see* — not that Pharaoh happened to see? If witnessing is something Allah administers, what am I being caused to witness right now?

If the Meccan Muslims were the implied audience of this surah, and they had their Pharaoh, their Haman, their deliberate weakening — where are those structures in my own world? And what does it mean that Allah addressed this surah to people who seemed, in every material sense, to be losing?

### The One Sentence

*The systems the powerful build to prevent their own downfall are the very instruments through which Allah fulfills His promise to the oppressed — and He causes the oppressor to witness it with their own eyes.*

### The Closing Invitation

Let this ayah do what it was designed to do.

If you are in a season of weakness — deliberate or otherwise — let the grammar of *numakkina* settle into you. Not: *you will eventually escape.* But: *you will be established in the very earth where you are standing now*. The roots go down here. The establishment happens here. You don't have to find another earth.

And if you have been watching, from a distance, a situation that seems immovable — let the word *nuriya* do its work. Allah is the subject of that verb. He is the one who causes things to be seen. He is the one who opens eyes at the moment of reckoning. Your task is not to force that moment. Your task is to work with the current that is already running — the invisible counter-decree that was announced before the story began.
