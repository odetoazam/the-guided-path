# He Chooses — And There Is No Choice Beside His

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
[ayah:28:68] وَرَبُّكَ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ وَيَخْتَارُ ۗ مَا كَانَ لَهُمُ ٱلْخِيَرَةُ ۚ سُبْحَـٰنَ ٱللَّهِ وَتَعَـٰلَىٰ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa rabbuka yakhluqu mā yashā'u wa yakhtār. Mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah. Subḥāna-llāhi wa taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn.*

**Translation:**
"And your Lord creates what He wills and chooses. Not for them was the choice. Exalted is Allah and high above what they associate with Him."

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me first place you somewhere.

You are reading the twenty-eighth surah of the Quran. Surah Al-Qasas. The name means *The Stories* — and the longest, most sustained narrative in the surah is the story of Musa, peace be upon him. The surah opens with a baby in Egypt. A mother who has just been told that her son is going to be hunted by Pharaoh's regime. She is given an instruction that, on its face, sounds like the most insane thing you could ever say to a mother: *put him in the river.*

She does it.

And as the surah unfolds, you watch a single life get assembled by a Hand that no one in the story can see. The basket floats. It floats toward the palace of the man who wants him dead. The man's own wife pulls him out and falls in love with him. His own mother is hired to nurse him — paid by the regime that is supposed to be killing him. He grows up. He kills a man. He flees. He arrives at a well. He marries. He stays for ten years in the desert. And then, in the middle of an ordinary night, he sees a fire on a mountain — and the entire arc of his life suddenly makes sense, because Allah is speaking to him from that fire and sending him back to confront the very man who once tried to kill him.

If you ask Musa's mother, at any point in this story, "What do you want?" — she does not want any of this. She wants her baby in her arms. She wants safety. She wants normalcy.

If you ask Musa, when he is fleeing Egypt with blood on his hands, "What do you want?" — he does not want to be a prophet. He wants to disappear.

[PAUSE]

And yet every choice that was *not* theirs to make turned out to be the choice that made them.

Now we arrive at ayah 68. The surah is nearly over. The storytelling stops. The screen pulls back. And Allah, in fourteen words, names the principle that was operating the entire time. He names the Hand you have been watching move.

Here is the hook I want you to hold:

The ayah does not say *Allah chooses for them.* It does not say *Allah chooses well for them.* It says something that, when you read it carefully in Arabic, is almost shocking in its bluntness — and then it ends with a word that most translations completely flatten. **There is a sentence in the middle of this ayah where Allah strips human beings of something — and once you see what it is He has stripped from us, the surah you have just read will open like a flower.**

Let's get inside the words.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Two Verbs — *Yakhluqu* and *Yakhtār*

Before we open the words, let me teach you something about Arabic verbs.

In Arabic, when two verbs are joined by the connector *wa* — "and" — the listener's ear immediately asks a question: *are these two verbs the same act, or two different acts?* Sometimes Arabic stacks verbs that mean almost the same thing to create emphasis, like a hammer hitting a nail twice. Other times, the second verb is doing something the first verb is not doing. And the listener is supposed to feel the difference.

Here Allah says: **يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَاءُ وَيَخْتَارُ** — *He creates what He wills, and He chooses.*

Two verbs. *Yakhluqu* — He creates. *Yakhtār* — He chooses.

Notice something. These are not the same act.

To **create** is to bring something from non-existence into existence. The root خ-ل-ق, *kh-l-q*, in classical Arabic carries the sense of shaping, measuring out, proportioning. A potter *khalaqa* the pot when he gives it its dimensions. Allah creates — meaning, He brings into being and gives shape to.

To **choose** — *yakhtār*, from the root خ-ي-ر, *kh-y-r* — is something different. This root is the root of the word *khayr*, which means **good**. To choose, in Arabic, is not just to pick. It is to *select the good.* When you say *ikhtartu* in Arabic, you are not saying "I picked one at random." You are saying "I selected what was best."

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the first hinge.

Allah is saying: *I create what I will — and from what I create, I select what is best.*

Creation is the wide act. Choice is the narrow act inside it. He brings into being a universe of possibilities, and then from that universe He selects — *He picks out the good.* Not for Himself. He is beyond need. He picks out the good *for the unfolding of His will.*

This is why classical scholars stopped here for a long time. Some said *yakhtār* refers to His choosing of prophets out of mankind. Some said it refers to His choosing of certain places — Makkah, Madinah, Al-Quds. Some said it refers to His choosing of times — Laylat al-Qadr, the months of Hajj. Some said it refers to His choosing of certain souls for Himself.

Don't pick one. Hold all of them. Each is a camera angle on the same reality. **Allah does not just create. He selects. Out of all the babies born in Egypt that year, He selected one to put in a basket. Out of all the mountains in the world, He selected one to speak from. Out of all the times in human history, He selected one to send the final messenger. He creates — and then He chooses.**

Here is what this single grammatical pairing reveals: the world is not a flat field of equal possibilities through which Allah neutrally allows things to happen. The world is a curated act. Every prophet, every revelation, every guided heart, every Laylat al-Qadr — Allah did not just allow these. **He selected them.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Sentence at the Heart — *Mā Kāna Lahumu-l-Khiyarah*

Now let's open the second sentence. This is where the ayah turns.

**مَا كَانَ لَهُمُ الْخِيَرَةُ** — *Mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah.*

Before we translate it, I want to teach you the grammatical shape, because the shape is doing almost all of the work.

In Arabic, you can negate something in many ways. You can use *lā* (no). You can use *laysa* (it is not). You can use *lam* (it was not). But there is one construction that is heavier than all of these. It is **مَا كَانَ** — *mā kāna.* Literally: *it was not the case that...* / *it never was that...*

This construction does something specific in Arabic. It does not just negate the present. It negates the past. It negates the structural reality of something. When Allah says *mā kāna lahum* — *it was never theirs* — He is not saying "they don't have it right now." He is saying **"it was never the case that this belonged to them. Not now. Not before. Not as a structural matter. Ever."**

[PAUSE]

Now the noun: **الْخِيَرَة** — *al-khiyarah.*

This is from the same root we just met — خ-ي-ر, *kh-y-r* — the root of *khayr*, good. But notice the form. It is not *ikhtiyār* (the action of choosing). It is *al-khiyarah* with the definite article *al-*. The grammarians tell us this form names *the very faculty of choice itself,* or *the right of selection.* And the *al-* makes it definite — not just *a* choice, but *the* choice. The *al-khiyarah* is the prerogative of selection. The seat from which choices are made.

So now read the sentence again:

**Mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah.**

*Not for them was the prerogative of selection. Not now. Not ever. It was never theirs.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment.

Allah is not saying *human beings cannot make small choices.* He is not saying you cannot pick what you want for breakfast or which job to apply to or who to marry. He is saying something far deeper: **the throne of selection — the seat from which the architecture of reality is chosen — was never yours.** It was never yours which family you would be born into. It was never yours which century. It was never yours whether your messenger would be Musa or Muhammad. It was never yours whether revelation would come through the Quraysh or through some other tribe. It was never yours which mountain Allah would speak from or which night the Quran would descend on. *Mā kāna lahum.* That seat was never theirs.

Here is what I want you to notice — and this is the absence that this ayah is built on.

In Arabic, when you negate possession, you usually need to say *for whom* it is not. *Mā kāna lahum* — *it was not for them.* Fine. But there is no specification of **what kind** of choice. The word *al-khiyarah* is left wide open. *The* choice. *The* selection. The definite article makes it cosmic. Allah did not say "the choice of prophethood was not theirs," even though that is part of what is meant. He said *the choice* — leaving the scope of what is being denied to expand to fill the listener's imagination.

This is the gap. This is the absence. **Allah does not specify which choice He is taking from you, because He is taking back the architecture itself.** The right to design reality from the top down was never yours.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with me, because there is one more layer.

The classical scholars asked a beautiful question about this sentence. Some read *mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah* as a statement: *they did not have the choice.* Others read it almost as a corrective, as if Allah is dismissing a presumption — *as if to say: 'they thought they had it; they did not.'* The form of the sentence allows both. **What it does not allow is for you to walk away thinking that the seat of cosmic choice is yours and Allah's together.** That option the grammar simply does not leave open.

### Element Three: The Ending — *Subḥāna-llāhi wa Taʿālā ʿAmmā Yushrikūn*

The ayah ends with something that, on the surface, sounds like a familiar Quranic formula. *Subḥāna-llāhi wa taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn.* "Glory be to Allah, and exalted is He far above what they associate."

We have all heard sentences like this many times. They are easy to skim. Don't skim.

Two verbs again: *subḥāna* — exalted, declared free from defect — and *taʿālā* — high above.

But here is what most translations miss. The preposition is **عَن** — *ʿan.* "Far above **from**." In Arabic, *ʿan* with words of exaltation carries the sense of *high above — and away from.* It is not just vertical altitude. It is *distance.* Allah is high above *and at a remove from* what they associate.

And what are "they" associating?

[PAUSE]

This is the second great absence in the ayah. Allah does not name the shirk. He does not say "what they associate of partners in worship" or "what they associate of idols." He just says *ʿammā yushrikūn* — "from what they associate."

When you read this ayah in isolation, you might assume the shirk being referred to is the standard shirk of the mushrikūn of Makkah — their idols, their statues, their Lat and ʿUzza. And that is part of what is meant.

But look where this sentence is placed. It is placed *immediately after* a declaration that the *khiyarah* — the cosmic prerogative of selection — was never theirs. The *yushrikūn* is grammatically linked to what just came before. **The shirk that is being denounced here is, among other things, the shirk of imagining that the prerogative of cosmic choice belongs to anyone besides Allah.**

This is breathtaking when you see it.

The classical mufassirūn caught this. They noted that the Makkans had a particular complaint that runs through several surahs of the Quran: *Why was the Quran not sent down on a great man from one of the two cities?* Why this orphan from the Banu Hashim? Why not someone with more wealth, more lineage, more political weight? *We* would have chosen differently.

And Allah's answer in this ayah is *mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah.* **The choice was never yours. To imagine that the choice should be yours, to imagine that the prerogative of cosmic selection sits with you and your preferences and your council of elders — that is itself a form of shirk.** Not the shirk of stone idols. The shirk of imagining that the throne of *al-khiyarah* has more than one seat.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Look at what we have now.

We have a Lord who creates, and who from creation selects the good. We have a sentence that withdraws from human beings the cosmic prerogative of choice — *not just now, but ever.* And we have a final declaration that exalts Allah and distances Him from the assumption that this prerogative could be shared.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how to live.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Suffering of the Person Who Will Not Surrender the Chair

Let me start somewhere very human.

There is a particular kind of suffering that you can spot in people once you know what to look for. It is the suffering of the person who is constantly editing reality in their head. They are running a different version of their life on a parallel screen — the life where they got into that school, the life where their parents stayed together, the life where they did not get sick, the life where they married the other person. They live in this country, but their head lives in another. They have this body, but their head lives in another. They have these parents, this race, this century, this set of constraints — and their head is *always* in a different room.

This person is exhausted. They cannot tell you why. They sleep eight hours and wake up tired. They achieve things and feel nothing. They check off goals and find no peace.

Here is the diagnosis the ayah gives us: **this person is sitting in a chair that was never theirs.**

The seat of *al-khiyarah* — the prerogative of designing reality — they are trying to occupy it. They are running parallel versions of their life because, on some level, they believe that they should have been the one to choose. They should have been the one to select which family, which body, which century. And because Allah will not give them that seat, they have made themselves a fake throne in their head and they sit on it and grieve.

The ayah does not scold this person. It liberates them. **Mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah.** *It was never yours.* Not as a punishment. As a fact. The chair you have been trying to sit in has never had your name on it. It has never had *any* human being's name on it. It belongs to your Lord.

Now think about Musa's mother for a moment. Surah Al-Qasas has just spent verses on her. She did not choose to live under Pharaoh. She did not choose the year her son was born — the year of the male-infant killings. She did not choose to put him in the river. She was told to. And the ayah we are reflecting on is, in many ways, Allah pulling back the curtain on her story and saying *this was Me. The selecting was Mine.*

Here is the strange thing. The moment she let go of the seat — the moment she placed the basket in the water — was the moment her son came back to her. The mufassirūn note this beautiful detail: Allah *returned him to her* (*fa-radadnāhu ilā ummihi*) precisely because she did the thing that looked like surrender. **The cosmic choice she could not make was made on her behalf, more beautifully than she could have made it herself.**

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes one thing very concretely. When you find yourself in the grip of *what if* — what if I had done that, what if they had chosen me, what if my life were different — pause. Notice that you are sitting in the chair. Notice the chair was never yours. And get up.

Not because *what if* is forbidden. Because the seat from which *what if* feels like a real option is a seat that does not exist.

### Theme Two: The Quiet Shirk of "I Would Have Chosen Differently"

Now let's go to a place that is harder.

The ayah ends with shirk. *ʿAmmā yushrikūn.* And we said that the shirk being denounced is not just stone idols — it is, in part, the assumption that the throne of cosmic choice could belong to someone other than Allah.

Let me name a form of this shirk that lives inside almost every believer's heart, and that almost no one calls by its real name.

It is the quiet sentence: **"I would have chosen differently."**

I would have chosen a different family for myself. I would have chosen a different test. I would have chosen a different decade to be born in. I would have chosen, if I had been Allah, to give guidance to that loved one of mine who refused it. I would have chosen to make this religion easier. I would have chosen to make this verse clearer. I would have chosen — if it had been up to me — to design the world differently.

[PAUSE]

We don't say these sentences out loud, because we know they would sound bad. But they live in us. They are the soundtrack of our resentment. And the ayah is telling us, very gently and very precisely, that the impulse behind these sentences is a quiet form of *ishrāk*. Not because we are bowing to idols. Because we have, in the privacy of our hearts, put a second seat next to the throne of *al-khiyarah* and seated ourselves on it.

Here is the test. When you encounter something in your life that you did not choose — a constraint, a loss, a body you did not pick, a family you did not pick — what happens next in your heart? Do you assume that the right way the universe should be arranged is *the way you would have arranged it*? And that anything that deviates from your design is a defect?

If yes — then somewhere in you, the throne is contested.

The ayah is an invitation to dethrone yourself. Not violently. Not with shame. **With relief.**

Because here is what the ayah is actually offering: *you do not have to be Allah.* You never had to be. The seat you have been trying to sit in is heavy, and unbearable, and not yours. You can get up. You can let the seat be empty of you. And in the moment you do, you will discover that the seat was never empty — it has always been occupied by the One whose choosing is *yakhtār,* whose every selection is selection of *khayr*, of the good.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you receive what comes. When something arrives in your life that you would not have chosen, the first sentence in your heart can shift. Not *why me*, not *if only*, not *I would have chosen differently.* Instead: **He chose. And His choosing is from the root of khayr. I do not yet see the khayr. But the seat from which this was chosen is the only seat that ever had the authority to choose.**

That sentence does not make pain go away. It does something quieter and more durable. It dissolves the second throne in your heart. And when the second throne is dissolved, the pain that remains is just pain — not pain plus the unbearable weight of imagining that you should have been able to prevent it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Look at the shape of the ayah now.

It opens with affirmation: *your Lord creates what He wills, and chooses.* The full positive declaration of divine prerogative.

It moves to negation: *not for them was the choice.* The corresponding human limit.

It closes with exaltation and distancing: *exalted is Allah, and high above — and at a remove from — what they associate.*

Affirmation. Negation. Exaltation.

The shape is the message. Allah affirms His prerogative, denies that it is shared, and then exalts Himself away from the assumption that it could be. The ayah is *tawhid* in three movements. The opening and the closing are the two pillars; the middle sentence — *mā kāna lahumu-l-khiyarah* — is the floor between them.

### Bringing It Together

What the linguistic journey showed us: Allah creates broadly and selects narrowly; the seat of selection has never been a human seat; the assumption that it could be is itself a kind of shirk.

What the themes showed us: the person who refuses to give up that seat suffers in a particular and recognizable way; the believer's quiet sentence *"I would have chosen differently"* is the place where this shirk lives, and dethroning it is not a defeat — it is a relief.

These are the same insight. One says it about words. The other says it about hearts. **The relief that comes when you stop trying to be Allah is the felt experience of the ayah's grammar.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, specifically, in my life right now am I sitting in a chair labeled *al-khiyarah* — running parallel versions of how things should have gone?

2. What is the sentence I say to myself, in private, that begins with *"I would have chosen..."*? What does that sentence reveal about where my heart has placed a second throne?

3. If I really believed that the seat of cosmic selection has never been mine and has never been any human being's, what would change about how I receive what tomorrow brings?

4. Looking back at the long story of Musa in this surah — where in *my own* life can I see, in retrospect, a basket being placed in a river that I would never have placed myself, and a current carrying me toward something I could never have planned?

5. What would it feel like, just for one day, to interpret every unchosen thing in my life as a *selection* — as something that fell from *yakhtār*, the verb whose root is *khayr*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The seat from which the architecture of your life is chosen has never been yours — and the moment you stop trying to sit in it is the moment you discover Who has been sitting in it all along, and that His choosing is, by the very root of the word, the selection of good.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to leave this reflection with a new resolution. Resolutions are a way of climbing back into the chair.

Leave instead with a noticing. For the next few days, when something arrives in your life that you did not choose — a small inconvenience, a piece of news, a memory that visits you — notice the first sentence that rises in your heart. If that sentence is *I would have chosen differently,* let it pass. Don't fight it. Don't argue with it. Just let it pass. And underneath it, let a quieter sentence rise: *He chose. And the verb is yakhtār.*

That is all. The grammar will do the rest.

### Du'a

O Allah, You create what You will, and You choose — and Your choosing is the very root of the good. Lift from our hearts the weight of the second throne we have built beside Yours. Let us receive what You select for us as the selection of *khayr*, even when our eyes have not yet learned to see it. And exalt Yourself in our hearts above every quiet sentence that imagines we could have chosen better than You. *Subḥānaka.* *Taʿālayta.* Glory be to You. You are high above. Āmīn.
