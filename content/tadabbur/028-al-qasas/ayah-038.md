# The Brick and the Ladder: When Power Builds Its Own Theology

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Imagine standing in the throne room of the most powerful empire on earth.

The pillars are obsidian. The walls are gold leaf hammered so thin that the torchlight makes them ripple like water. Around the king stand the **malaʾ** — the council, the inner circle, the men whose nods can collapse a province. They have come because there is a problem. Not a military problem. Not a financial problem. A different kind of problem.

A man named Moses has walked into this throne room, weeks ago, and has not left the air the same. He came with a staff and a hand and a sentence that has been bouncing around the marble like a fly that won't die: *"Your Lord and the Lord of your fathers."*

The chiefs have been waiting for Pharaoh to settle the matter. To put it back to sleep. Because they can feel something — something the empire has not had to feel in generations — they can feel **a rival ontology** loose in the room.

This is the moment we are dropping into.

This ayah is not Pharaoh discovering doubt. Pharaoh discovered nothing. This ayah is Pharaoh **performing certainty for an audience that is starting to wobble**. Watch carefully. Because what he says next is one of the most exposed sentences in the entire Quran — every word is a window into how power, when cornered by truth, manufactures its own theology rather than submit.

There is a hook here that we need to hang onto for the next hour. Pharaoh, the man who has declared *"I am your lord most high,"* the man who could simply *say* he is god and be done with it — for some reason, in this ayah, **decides to build something**. A construction project. With a builder. With fire. With clay.

Why?

Why does the man who claims to be god need a ladder?

Stay with that question. Because the answer is going to unfold something about power, about ego, about the architecture of our own internal Pharaohs that we did not come into this room expecting.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Mā ʿalimtu lakum min ilāhin ghayrī* — "I do not know of any god for you other than me"

Let's begin with the sentence so familiar that we read past it without hearing what is actually being said.

Notice he does not say *"there is no god other than me."* He could have. Arabic gives him that option, and his grammar is good enough to use it. He says something subtler. He says:

**"I do not *know* of any god for you other than me."**

Before we open the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic handles knowledge claims.

When you want to assert that something **is not real**, Arabic gives you a clean tool — a flat negation of being. *Lā ilāha ghayrī* would have said *"there is no god other than me."* Direct. Ontological. A claim about reality itself.

But when you negate your **knowledge** of something, you are doing something different. You are not saying it isn't real. You are saying you have not personally encountered it. You are pulling the claim back from the world and tucking it inside your own head. This is what *mā ʿalimtu* does. The verb is from the root **ʿ-l-m** — to know, to be aware of. He is saying: *as far as my knowledge extends, no such being exists.*

Now let's sit with this.

Pharaoh has just made the most arrogant claim a tongue can make — *I am the only god for you* — and yet, grammatically, he has hidden behind **the limits of his own awareness**. He has, without meaning to, **confessed that he is bounded by knowledge**. He has admitted that there could be something outside what he knows.

This is the first crack. And he does not see it. But Allah, in choosing this exact phrasing for him, is letting us see it.

Notice something more. The phrase **"lakum"** — "*for you*" — addressed to the chiefs. He is not saying *I do not know of any god*. He is saying: *I do not know of any god **for you**.* As if godhood is something distributed, assigned, allocated to populations. As if his ownership of these men is so total that even the question of whom they may worship is **his administrative decision**.

Here is what is remarkable. The man who claims absolute divinity has, in the same breath, **made godhood a function of his governance**. He has demoted the very category he is trying to claim. He thinks he is elevating himself. He is actually flattening God into a bureaucratic role he has assigned to himself.

[PAUSE]

A real god does not have to issue memos about being god.

The classical commentators picked up on this immediately. Some read *mā ʿalimtu* as plain arrogance dressed in false humility. Others heard in it the rhetorical hedge of a politician who knows the room is unsure. Still others heard the slip of a man whose theology has never been examined and who, the moment he tries to articulate it, exposes that it was never really there. These are not competing readings. They are **the same moment seen from three angles** — the inner emptiness, the outer performance, and the audience's wavering trust. All three are present in that single phrase.

What does this single phrase reveal? **That the loudest claims of certainty are almost always camouflage for an unexamined interior.** Pharaoh is not stating a creed. He is filling silence.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Fa-awqid lī yā Hāmānu ʿalā-ṭ-ṭīn* — "So kindle for me, O Haman, upon the clay"

Now here is where the ayah does something almost cinematic.

He has just claimed to be the only god. The expected next move would be a decree. A punishment. A miracle. Instead — and this is so strange that you have to slow down to notice it — Pharaoh **turns to a contractor**.

*"Fa-awqid lī yā Hāmānu ʿalā-ṭ-ṭīn."*

*Kindle for me, O Haman, upon the clay.*

Before we open the words, teach yourself this: when a sentence shifts from a metaphysical claim to a construction order in the span of a comma, the writer is telling you something. The juxtaposition is the meaning.

The verb **awqid** is from **w-q-d** — to kindle, to ignite, to set a fire blazing. It is the same root used for the fire of Hell in other ayahs. It is the language of forges, of kilns, of heat applied to material in order to transform it.

And what is being kindled upon? **Ṭīn.** Clay. The root **ṭ-y-n** is the substance of the earth itself. The very same word used elsewhere in the Quran for the material from which the human being was originally fashioned.

Stay with this image for a moment. Pharaoh, claiming divinity, calls for fire to be lit upon clay. The same clay from which his own body was kneaded. He is going to take **the substance of his own createdness** — the dust he is made of — and bake it into a structure that he hopes will carry him **above** the God of Moses.

There is a phrase scholars use when an action exposes its actor: **a self-witnessing sentence**. This is one. Every brick in his tower is going to be a piece of him admitting that he is made of earth. The very building material he chooses to ascend on **proves that he cannot ascend**.

Notice also: he says *"awqid lī"* — *kindle **for me**.* And then *"ij'al lī"* — *make **for me**.* The "for me" repeats. Lī. Lī. He cannot construct his own tower. He needs Haman. He needs labor. He needs fire he did not light. He needs clay he did not form. The god of the empire is, by his own admission, **a project manager**.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's stunning. The very fact that he has to **outsource the construction of his cosmology** is the refutation of his claim to be its center. A real ilāh does not say *"build me a way to look at God."* A real ilāh does not contract out his theology.

Let me sit with the choice of Haman's name here. Haman, in the Quranic narrative, is the architect-vizier — the man of stone and structure. Allah does not say *"Pharaoh said to his vizier."* He names him. *Yā Hāmānu.* And the naming is exposure — a record. Centuries later, when this conversation is over, the man who lit the fire is going to be remembered by name. He thought he was carrying out an order. He was actually signing a confession.

Read the absence here, too. Allah does *not* tell us whether the tower was ever built. The ayah ends Pharaoh's speech, and the tower hangs in the air — half-imagined, half-issued. Was it constructed? Did it rise? The Quran withholds the answer because **the tower is not the point**. The *commissioning* of the tower is the point. The moment a man calls for a ladder to God is the moment he has already lost the argument.

What does this phrase reveal? **That every project of self-deification is, secretly, an act of dependence.** The taller the tower, the more help you needed to build it. The grander the throne, the more hands carried the wood.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Laʿallī aṭṭaliʿu ilā ilāhi Mūsā* — "That I might look up at the God of Moses"

This is the phrase the entire ayah pivots on. And it is the one most easily missed.

Let me teach the form first. *Laʿalla* in Arabic is a particle of **hope or expectation**. It carries uncertainty inside it — *perhaps, maybe, that I might.* It is the language of a person reaching for something they are not sure they will grasp. Compare it to the certainty of *inna* — *indeed, surely.* Pharaoh uses both in this same ayah, and the contrast is everything.

When he speaks about Moses he says: *"wa innī la-aẓunnuhū mina-l-kādhibīn"* — *indeed, I do think he is among the liars.* Certainty wrapped around an opinion.

But when he speaks about looking at the God of Moses he says: *"laʿallī aṭṭaliʿ"* — *that I might be able to look up.* Hesitation wrapped around an act.

Stop and feel what just happened. The man who has declared himself the only god has, in the same breath, **expressed hope that he might be able to look up at another God**. The Arabic gives away what the speech is trying to hide. He is not confident there is no God of Moses. He is **hoping** he can see Him to disprove Him.

You only hope to see what you already half-suspect is there.

And look at the verb. **Aṭṭaliʿu** — from the root **ṭ-l-ʿ**, the same root used for the rising of the sun. *Maṭlaʿ al-fajr* — the rising of dawn. The verb in this Form V (*iṭṭalaʿa*) carries the sense of *climbing up to peer at*, *cresting a ridge to glimpse*, *raising one's gaze with effort*. It is the verb of someone *trying to catch sight of something elevated*.

This is the man who claims to *be* God. And the Arabic he chooses — without realizing — places him **below**, climbing, peering, hoping. He has, with his own tongue, **mapped the geography of reality**: God is above, he is beneath, and between them there is a tower he has not yet built.

[PAUSE]

Notice the address shift here too. The first half of the ayah is spoken to the *malaʾ* — the chiefs. *Yā ayyuhal-malaʾ.* The middle is spoken to Haman. *Yā Hāmānu.* But the ending — the part about *the God of Moses* and *I think he is a liar* — has no addressee. It just hangs. Who is he talking to in that last clause?

Himself, perhaps. Or perhaps Allah is letting us hear the part of the speech that was never meant to be heard — **the part where the performance leaks into private uncertainty**.

What does this phrase reveal? **That the tyrant is the first person who does not believe his own theology.** The interior of arrogance is not confidence. It is a man on a ladder, hoping.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

We have looked at three sentences. We have watched a man who claims to be god confess, in three different ways, that he is not.

- He hid behind the limits of his own knowledge.
- He outsourced the construction of his cosmology.
- He used the grammar of hope and the verb of climbing toward what is above him.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about Pharaoh's words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Architecture of Manufactured Certainty

Here is something most of us have lived through and never named.

You are in an argument. You are losing. You can feel the ground giving way beneath your position — not because the other person is louder, but because *something they said is true*, and a part of you that does not get a vote in your speech already knows it. And so what do you do?

You **escalate**. You restate your position more confidently. You bring in evidence you have not vetted. You raise your voice not because the room cannot hear you but because **you cannot hear yourself doubting**.

Every one of us has done this. In conversations with parents, with spouses, with strangers on the internet. The pattern is so old that Allah is showing us its archetype four thousand years ago in a throne room in Egypt.

This is what Pharaoh is doing in this ayah. He is not stating his theology. He is **building a fortress around a position he cannot defend**, brick by linguistic brick.

Notice what he calls for. Not an argument. Not evidence. Not a debate with Moses. **A tower.** Physical infrastructure. Because when your epistemic position is collapsing, the move is always to **convert ideas into objects**. If you cannot win the argument, **build something that looks like winning**.

This is why dictators build monuments. This is why insecure power builds palaces. This is why entire industries exist to produce the *visual atmosphere of certainty* — the wood-paneled office, the leather chair, the framed diploma — because the human nervous system reads architecture as argument.

The ayah is teaching us to **read the building backwards**. When you see grand construction in service of an idea, ask: what is the idea afraid of? When you see a man build a tower to look at God, you know — with certainty — **he is no longer sure there is no God**.

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars said something striking about this verse. They said the *ṣarḥ* — the tower — was not for seeing. It was for *being seen*. The chiefs needed to watch Pharaoh do something dramatic, because his speech was no longer enough. **He was losing the room.** A king who needs theater is a king who has already lost the throne in the only place that matters — inside the heads of his subjects.

Now bring this inside.

Where in your own life are you building towers? Where are you constructing elaborate justifications for a position your own heart has already conceded? Where are you escalating volume because you cannot bear the quiet of doubt? Where are you outsourcing — to friends, to scholars, to news anchors, to algorithms — the construction of a worldview you secretly suspect cannot hold?

The ayah is not just about Pharaoh. The ayah is about **the moment any of us discovers we have built our identity around something we no longer believe** — and our first instinct is to build something bigger to bury that discovery in.

The practical application: when you notice yourself escalating — getting louder, more elaborate, more performative — pause. That escalation is a signal. It is telling you something you need to hear. **The tower you are about to build is going to be made of the same clay you are made of. It will not lift you above what you are running from.**

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The God Who is Sought is the God Who is Real

Now here is the deepest thing in this ayah, and it is hidden in plain sight.

Pharaoh wants to look at *the God of Moses*.

Read that again. Slowly.

The man who has just claimed to be the only god — **in the same sentence, names another God.** And not abstractly. Not as a hypothesis. He names Him as the God *of Moses*. He gives the rival deity a possessive, a relationship, a person who is His.

Stop and feel what just happened. He has **acknowledged a covenant** in the act of trying to deny it. He has confessed, without meaning to, that **God is the kind of being who has people**. That He is known *through* the ones who carry His message. That you cannot describe the true God without naming the one He sent.

This is what makes the ayah so quietly devastating. Pharaoh's blasphemy is not the absence of theology. It is the **presence of accurate theology**, malformed by ego. He sees correctly. He just refuses correctly.

[PAUSE]

Here is the universal struggle hidden in this.

There is a reality outside of us. It does not depend on our knowledge of it. The sun does not need our awareness to rise. The stars do not check whether we are looking before they burn. And the One who made both does not consult our ego before being God.

The mistake every Pharaoh makes — the great Pharaoh in Egypt and the small Pharaoh inside us — is to treat **the world as the projection of our consciousness** rather than our consciousness as **a small light inside the world**. We start to act as if our awareness is the ceiling of reality. As if what we do not know does not exist. As if what we have not accepted is not real.

And so Allah does something exquisite in this ayah. He lets Pharaoh — the very embodiment of this mistake — articulate it perfectly: *mā ʿalimtu lakum min ilāhin ghayrī.* *I do not know of any god for you other than me.*

The whole error of the human ego, in seven words.

Because the question is never *what do I know?* The question is *what is real?* And reality does not require your permission to be real. The God of Moses was God before Pharaoh heard His name. He will be God after the tower has fallen and the kingdom is sand. The clay will outlast the king who tried to bake it into a ladder.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the turn.

The same instinct that destroyed Pharaoh — the instinct to want to *see* God — is the instinct that, properly oriented, **saves us**. Moses himself, on Mount Sinai, asked: *Rabbi arinī anẓur ilayk* — *My Lord, show me, that I may look at You.* The same verb root. The same yearning. The same human reaching upward.

What separates Moses from Pharaoh?

Not the desire. **The posture.**

Moses asked from below, knowing he was below. Pharaoh built a ladder, pretending he was above. Moses said *Rabbi* — *my Lord*. Pharaoh said *aṭṭaliʿu* — *that I might climb up to look*. Both wanted to see. One knelt. One built.

**The same yearning becomes either prayer or tower, depending on the heart.**

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this is the most personal thing in the reflection.

There is a Pharaoh inside every one of us. He is the part of you that, when challenged, wants to build a tower. He is the voice that says *as far as I know, there is no God who would ask this of me*. He is the calculation that turns covenant into a tower to peer over, rather than a ground to kneel on.

And there is a Moses inside every one of us. He is the part of you that, when faced with the same reality, **kneels**. Who says *I am made of clay, and that clay is not the problem — pretending it isn't is the problem*.

You do not need to *defeat* the Pharaoh inside you. You need to **stop letting him commission towers**. The next time you feel the impulse to build something elaborate to defend a position your heart has already begun to question — kneel instead. Drop the brick. Put down the blueprint. Say what Moses said: *Rabbi*.

That single word is taller than any tower Haman could build.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Look back at the ayah now and notice how it is built.

It opens with **a claim** — *I am the only god for you.*
It moves to **a construction** — *kindle the clay, build the tower.*
It rises to **a purpose** — *that I might look up at the God of Moses.*
It closes with **a verdict** — *I think he is among the liars.*

Four moves. Claim → Construction → Purpose → Verdict.

Now read it backwards. The verdict is the foundation. The purpose is the framing. The construction is the building. The claim is the spire. **The ayah is itself a tower.** Allah has built, in the very structure of the sentence, the thing Pharaoh is asking Haman to build. And the tower of the ayah, like the tower of Pharaoh, **cannot reach what it claims to reach** — because at its base is *I think* — *aẓunnu* — the language of suspicion, not knowledge.

The whole speech rests on a guess. Every word above is held up by a *maybe*.

This is the architecture of every false certainty in history. The spire is loud. The foundation is fog.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight from two directions.

From the language: the man who claims to be god uses verbs of hoping, climbing, ignorance, and dependence. From the themes: the man who claims to be god builds, escalates, outsources, and points — without meaning to — at the God he is denying.

It is **the same exposure**. Pharaoh's tongue and Pharaoh's tower both confess the same thing: **that he is below, and reaching, and afraid.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life are you building a tower instead of having a conversation?
2. When you feel the impulse to escalate — to get louder, longer, more elaborate — what is the quiet underneath that you are trying to drown out?
3. Whose name do you accidentally invoke when you try to deny the truth? What does your denial accidentally confess?
4. Have you confused *I do not know of any* with *there is none*? What lives outside the perimeter of your knowing?
5. Is the yearning you carry to see God — and most of us carry one — being built into a prayer, or being built into a tower?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**Every Pharaoh, in his loudest moment, confesses with his grammar what he denies with his throne.**

### The Closing Invitation

The next time you catch yourself escalating, building, performing certainty — pause. Listen for the *laʿallī* under your *innī*. The hope under the claim. The reaching under the throne. That is your heart telling you the truth your mouth has not yet learned to say.

Drop the brick. The tower will not hold you. The clay is not the way up.

The way up is down.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who are above the towers of every Pharaoh that has ever lived and every Pharaoh that lives inside me — let me not be the one who builds when I should kneel. Let my grammar match my reality. Let me not say "I do not know" when I should say "show me." And when I am reaching, let it be in prayer, not in construction. Āmīn.*
