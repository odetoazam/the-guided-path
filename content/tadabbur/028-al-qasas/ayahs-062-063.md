# The Question With No Answer: When the Idols Are Called to Speak

## Introduction

Listen to these two ayahs:

**وَيَوْمَ يُنَادِيهِمْ فَيَقُولُ أَيْنَ شُرَكَائِيَ الَّذِينَ كُنتُمْ تَزْعُمُونَ ۝ قَالَ الَّذِينَ حَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ الْقَوْلُ رَبَّنَا هَـٰؤُلَاءِ الَّذِينَ أَغْوَيْنَا أَغْوَيْنَاهُمْ كَمَا غَوَيْنَا ۖ تَبَرَّأْنَا إِلَيْكَ ۖ مَا كَانُوا إِيَّانَا يَعْبُدُونَ**

*Wa yawma yunādīhim fa-yaqūlu ayna shurakāʾiya alladhīna kuntum tazʿumūn. Qāla alladhīna ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-qawlu rabbanā hāʾulāʾi alladhīna aghwaynā — aghwaynāhum kamā ghawaynā — tabarraʾnā ilayka. Mā kānū iyyānā yaʿbudūn.*

*And on the Day He will call to them and say: "Where are My partners whom you used to claim?" Those upon whom the word has come true will say: "Our Lord, these are the ones we led astray. We led them astray just as we ourselves had gone astray. We declare ourselves free before You — they were not really worshipping us."*

[PAUSE]

### Rebuild the World

Before we open this ayah, sit for a moment with where it lives.

Surah Al-Qasas is a surah about collapse. Not abstract collapse — specific, named, vivid collapse. Pharaoh, who said *"I am your highest lord,"* is drowning. Qarun, who said *"I was given this because of knowledge I possess,"* is being swallowed by the earth. Musa, who once lived in a palace, is hiding in the desert with nothing.

The surah keeps showing you what looks permanent, then showing you it isn't.

And now, after walking you through these collapses across history, the surah turns toward something more intimate. Not the collapse of an empire. The collapse of every false partner the human heart has ever held onto. Every name we whispered for help. Every face we feared more than Allah's. Every dependency we treated as ultimate.

Imagine a courtroom. The judge speaks. The judge asks a question. And then — silence. The people who were asked cannot answer. So the judge turns and asks the *defendants the people had been pointing to* — and *those defendants speak instead.*

That is the scene about to unfold.

### The Hook

Notice something strange even before we go in. Allah asks a question. The people who were idolaters are the ones being addressed. But in the next ayah, **they don't answer.** Someone else answers for them. Someone else is given the microphone.

Why?

And there's a stranger thing. The ones who finally speak — the so-called partners — say something almost impossible: *"They were not really worshipping us."* But they *were.* The Quran has told us a hundred times that people worshipped these things. So what does this denial mean?

Stay with these two questions. We're going to walk into them.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We're going to walk through three linguistic features in this ayah. Each one, on its own, is a window. Together they form a single architecture.

### Element One: *Yunādīhim* — The Verb of Calling Across Distance

The ayah begins: *Wa yawma yunādīhim* — "And on the Day He will **call out** to them."

Let me first teach the concept. In Arabic, there are several words for "to say" or "to speak." There is *qāla* — to say. There is *kallama* — to speak with, intimately. There is *hamasa* — to whisper. And there is *nādā* — from the root *n-d-y* — which carries something the others don't.

*Nādā* is **calling across distance.** It's the verb you use when the person you're addressing is far from you, when you need to raise your voice, when there's space between speaker and listener that the words have to travel across.

In everyday life: you don't *nādā* someone sitting next to you at dinner. You *nādā* your child across a field. You *nādā* a ship from the shore. The very choice of this verb tells you that **distance is part of the meaning.**

Now look at the ayah again. *Wa yawma yunādīhim* — "And on the Day He will call out to them."

[PAUSE]

Here is what's remarkable. These are people who lived their entire lives near Allah. He was, as the Quran says elsewhere, *closer to them than their jugular vein.* He was never far. So why is the verb *nādā* — calling across distance — used now?

The distance is **theirs**, not His. The verb is exposing something. All those years they spent treating Allah as far away — far from their decisions, far from their attention, far from their hearts — that distance has now become real. **They built the distance with their lives, and on that Day the distance shows up as a fact.** Allah has to *call out* to them because they are, by their own making, far.

Stay with this. The grammar is doing theology. The verb form *yunādī* (Form III, the *fāʿala* pattern) often carries the meaning of reciprocity or directionality between two parties — calling **toward** someone. The geometry of the word assumes a gap.

Ibn Kathir names the character of this divine address precisely: it is a *tawbīkh wa tahdīd* — a rebuke and a warning. Not a neutral summons. A calling-out. And calling-out, by its very nature, names a distance: between the life that was lived and the reality that was always there, between the claims that were made and the truth they obscured.

We feel this in life already. Have you ever spoken to a person who is sitting right in front of you, but is so far inside their own world that you have to raise your voice — not in volume, but in urgency — just to get them to hear? That's a *nidāʾ*. The body is close. The person is far.

That is what the human being has done with Allah their whole life. And on that Day, the configuration they chose becomes audible. Allah does not speak to them. He **calls** to them.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Kuntum Tazʿumūn* — The Verb You Use For What You Half-Believe

Now look at what Allah is calling out to them about. *Ayna shurakāʾiya alladhīna kuntum tazʿumūn* — "Where are My partners whom you **used to claim**?"

The verb here is *tazʿumūn,* from the root *z-ʿ-m*. And again, let me first teach the concept, because this word does specific work in Arabic.

There are several verbs for "to say" or "to claim." There is *qāla* — to say. There is *adʿā* — to assert. There is *shahida* — to bear witness with certainty. And there is *zaʿama.*

*Zaʿama* is, in classical Arabic lexicons, **a claim made without solid proof — often a claim the speaker himself is not fully sure of.** Lexicographers describe it as "the saddle-blanket of lies" — meaning, when you find this word in a sentence, what follows is almost always suspect. You use *qāla* when reporting something neutrally. You use *zaʿama* when there's already a wink in your voice, when even the speaker has doubts.

So in English we might translate it as: "to claim" — but with a tone. Like saying, *he alleges, he professes, he supposes.* Not *he says.*

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again. *Ayna shurakāʾiya alladhīna kuntum tazʿumūn.* "Where are my partners whom you **were-merely-claiming**?"

Notice something. Allah does not say: *"Where are My partners whom you worshipped?"* He does not say: *"Where are My partners whom you loved?"* He says — *whom you **claimed**.* Whom you **professed.** Whom you **alleged.**

And there is something devastating in this word choice. Because *zaʿama* is the word for a claim you weren't fully sure of in the first place.

Think about this. The person who builds their whole life around a partner-with-Allah — money, status, a job, a person, an ideology, an image of themselves — somewhere deep inside, **they knew it wasn't really God.** They knew. The certainty was performed. The claim was *zaʿm* — alleged, professed, half-believed.

Allah is exposing not just their idolatry. He is exposing **the quality of belief they had in their own idolatry.** It wasn't certainty. It was a story they told themselves.

And here's where this lands in our lives, in this room, right now. Every time we treat something as ultimate that we know in our quiet moments is not ultimate — that is *zaʿm.* The bank balance that we know cannot save us, but we behave as if it can. The reputation that we know is air, but we feed it with our attention. The relationship we know is created and fragile, but we treat as the foundation of our existence.

We *know.* In the quiet moments before sleep, in the moment of fear, in the moment of grief — we know. But we keep performing the claim.

And on that Day, Allah will name our knowing for what it was. **You weren't even fully convinced.** You were *claiming.*

### Element Three: *Mā Kānū Iyyānā Yaʿbudūn* — The Word That Should Not Be There

Now we come to the strangest sentence in the passage. The so-called partners — when they finally speak — say: *Tabarraʾnā ilayka — mā kānū iyyānā yaʿbudūn.* "We declare ourselves free before You — **they were not really worshipping us.**"

This is impossible. They *were* worshipping them. The Quran has told us this. Surah after surah describes idolaters bowing to idols, sacrificing to idols, praying to idols. So how can the idols themselves say *"they were not worshipping us"?*

Let's slow down. Let me teach a piece of Arabic syntax first.

In Arabic, you can negate a sentence in different ways depending on **where you put the emphasis.** Consider these two sentences:

1. *Mā kānū yaʿbudūnanā* — "They were not worshipping us."
2. *Mā kānū iyyānā yaʿbudūn* — "It was not *us* they were worshipping."

In English they sound similar. In Arabic, they are profoundly different. The second one — which is what the ayah actually uses — **fronts the object pronoun.** *Iyyānā* — "us" — comes *before* the verb. And in Arabic, fronting like this is one of the strongest forms of emphasis and restriction.

The meaning is not just *"they weren't worshipping us."* It is: ***"It was not us — specifically not us — that they were worshipping."***

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference?

Now look what this opens. The partners are not saying *"there was no worship happening."* There was worship happening. They saw it. People bowed. People sacrificed. People built temples.

What the partners are saying is: **"That worship — it was not really directed at us. We were just the surface. There was something underneath that they were actually serving."**

The classical commentators press this point from a specific angle. Al-Muyassar, reading this very declaration, states plainly: they were not worshipping the partners — they were worshipping the shayatins. What appeared to be idol-worship was, at its operational center, submission to the whispered voice that told them *this is enough, this will protect you, this deserves your ultimate concern.* The idol occupied the visible position. The seduction ran deeper.

And the Quran names what that deeper seduction aimed at: *"They follow nothing but conjecture and what their own souls desire"* (53:23). *"Have you seen the one who takes his own desire as his god?"* (45:23). The shayatins do not receive worship directly — they redirect it. And what they redirect worship toward is the ego's own hunger: the desire to be sufficient without Allah, to be protected by something controllable, to be at the center of one's own world.

The statue was the screen. The self was the projector.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most exposing sentences in the Quran. It tells you that **idolatry is never really about the idol.** The idol is a costume. Underneath every false partner is a desire that did not want to surrender to Allah, a self that needed the idol to keep its story intact. The shayatins are the architects of that redirection — they did not create the desire, they found it and gave it an address.

This is why the next ayah, immediately after, says — *Wa qīla idʿū shurakāʾakum fa-daʿawhum fa-lam yastajībū lahum* — "And it will be said: 'Call your partners.' So they call them, and they do not respond."

The partners don't respond because they were never alive in the relationship. The relationship was always between the self and the self. The idol was the mirror.

### Bridge

So look what we have. *Yunādīhim* — He calls them across the distance they built. *Tazʿumūn* — claims they were never fully sure of. *Mā kānū iyyānā yaʿbudūn* — and the things they were claiming weren't even the real object.

Three layers of exposure. Distance, half-belief, and misdirection. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Self as the Real Idol

Begin here, before we re-enter the ayah. Think about the last thing you were afraid of losing. Not abstractly — specifically. Picture it.

It might be a job. A relationship. A reputation. A particular future you've been building toward. A body that is still working. A mind that is still sharp. The respect of a particular person.

Now ask yourself: **what is it about losing that thing that scares me?**

Stay with the question. Don't move past it.

If you sit with it long enough, you'll notice something. The fear is almost never really about the thing itself. The fear is about **who you would have to be without it.** The story you've been telling about yourself — that you are successful, that you are loved, that you are competent, that you are safe — depends on that thing being there.

The thing is the costume. The self is what's underneath.

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah is showing. *Mā kānū iyyānā yaʿbudūn* — "It wasn't really us they were serving." Idolatry is rarely the worship of stone. It is the worship of **a self that needs the stone to keep its story intact.**

And this is why the ayah is so terrifying. Because if the partners aren't really the partners — if the idol underneath the idol is the self — then **the real work of tawḥīd is not the elimination of false gods outside.** It is the dethronement of the false god inside. The dismantling of the story the self tells about what it cannot live without.

This is harder than removing a statue. You can walk away from a building. You cannot walk away from yourself.

And yet this is exactly what the Quran keeps calling us to. *"Whoever is freed from the stinginess of his own soul — those are the ones who succeed"* (64:16). The word *shuḥḥ* — that stinginess — is not about money alone. It is about the self's grip on itself. Its refusal to let go. Its insistence on being the center.

The idol outside is just where the idol inside sends its proxies.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: *Tabarraʾnā Ilayka* — The Disavowal That Comes Too Late

The most precise word in the second ayah is one we can glide past: *tabarraʾnā ilayka.* "We declare ourselves free before You."

*Tabarraʾa* comes from the root *b-r-ʾ* — to be clear, to be free of something, to declare innocence. You use it when you are formally, publicly, irreversibly dissolving a bond that people assumed existed. This is not "I changed my mind." This is a legal-theological declaration.

And notice: the partners do not simply say *tabarraʾnā* — "we declare ourselves free." They say *tabarraʾnā **ilayka*** — "we declare ourselves free **toward You, before You, in Your direction.**" Al-Tabari, reading this closely, specifies what exactly is being dissolved: *walāyatuhum wa nuṣratuhum* — "their guardianship and their support." The *tabarruʾ* is from the entire relational structure — the network of patronage, protection, and allegiance that existed between the worshippers and their false partners.

This is the language of *walāya* — alliance, loyalty, protection — being formally annulled.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what this means. The human worshipped the idol assuming the idol was their *walī* — their guardian, their protector. And the idol accepted that position — silently, by not objecting, by being there, by being built into the center of the city and the center of the life. The *walāya* was never explicit. It was enacted through daily choices. Through what you ran toward when afraid. Through what you sacrificed for. Through what your voice softened when you spoke about it.

But on the Day, the idol speaks and says: *There was never a walāya between us. We were never your guardian. We dissolve every claim you might have on us. We dissolve it publicly, before Allah Himself.*

Can you feel what this does to the worshipper? Everything they organized their life around dissolves not with a bang but with a sentence. A declaration. A legal formula. Their protection evaporates not through catastrophe but through testimony.

And the formula is addressed *toward Allah* — not toward the worshippers. The partners aren't even speaking to the people who worshipped them. They are speaking **to Allah.** The worshippers are being spoken about, not spoken to. They are already beyond address.

[PAUSE]

There is a deep mercy underneath the terror of this scene, though it is easy to miss it. The *tabarruʾ* is a mirror held up before this life. If the partners will dissolve their relationship publicly on that Day — then the relationship was always dissolvable. It was never ultimate. It never had the weight that was placed on it. And this means: we can dissolve it now, voluntarily, while there is still time to choose what to return to.

The ayah is not only a scene from Judgment Day. It is a window into the nature of every attachment we are building today. Every false partner you hold onto is holding a *tabarruʾ* in its hand that it will deliver on that Day. The question the ayah asks is whether you will hear that declaration now, in this life, when there is still something to be done about it.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Three: *Ḥaqqa ʿAlayhimu Al-Qawl* — The Sealed Word and Its Speakers

There is a phrase in the ayah that sits before all the confessions begin: *qāla alladhīna ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-qawl.* "Those upon whom the word has come true."

*Ḥaqqa* — from the root *h-q-q* — meaning: to become real, to become due, to settle into finality. *Al-qawl* — the word. Whose word? The divine word. The decree.

This phrase is quietly devastating. Before the confession even begins, we are told that these speakers are those on whom the divine word **has already been finalized.** Their state is not being determined on that Day. It is being disclosed. The *qawl* — the word — became due a long time ago.

[PAUSE]

And who are these speakers exactly? The classical scholars named this carefully, and their positions differ in an instructive way. Al-Tabari, citing the Companion-era exegete Qatada, identifies them as the **shayatins** — those on whom Allah's wrath and curse was sealed precisely because they actively seduced humanity, drawing generation after generation away from Allah. "They are the shayatins," Qatada says simply. Al-Muyassar describes them more broadly as *duʿāt al-kufr* — the callers to disbelief — a phrase wide enough to include both demonic and human leaders who marshaled others into rejection of Allah.

Both readings are looking at the same reality from slightly different angles. Shayatins are the *duʿāt al-kufr* par excellence — the original callers to misguidance. And human leaders who organized others into kufr share in that same function. What both readings share is the key point: these are not passive bystanders, not idols that simply stood in a corner and were worshipped without their involvement. These are **architects of misguidance** — those who actively invited, seduced, organized. They are now standing before the consequence of what they built.

[PAUSE]

Now read what they say: *Aghwaynāhum kamā ghawaynā.* "We led them astray **just as** we ourselves had gone astray."

The word *ghawā* — from root *gh-w-y* — is not the word for making a minor mistake. It carries the meaning of going deeply, comprehensively wrong — straying in a way that is total, not merely a step off-path. And notice the syntax: *kamā ghawaynā*. "Just as we ourselves had gone astray." The comparison is exact. There is no superiority in their voice. No false dignity. Only an accounting.

They are saying: we did not have something they lacked. We were not standing on solid ground handing out shortcuts to the lost. We were already in the condition they ended up in. We gave them what we had: our own lostness.

[PAUSE]

This is the most precise description of how misguidance spreads in the Quran. The blind leading the blind is not merely a metaphor — it is the mechanics of *ghawāya*. It spreads from person to person not through conscious deception alone but through **shared lostness.** The person who tells you that money is ultimate, that image is ultimate, that power is ultimate — they are not always lying. They believe it. They have gone astray themselves. And they spread it because you cannot give what you do not have, and you cannot hold back what you already carry.

This is why the ayah does not end with blame directed outward. It ends with a parallel: *aghwaynāhum kamā ghawaynā.* The same way. The same condition. The leaders of misguidance — whether demonic or human — are disclosed not as masterminds but as people who were lost and led from their lostness.

[PAUSE — long]

### Theme Four: The Silence of the Worshippers — Returning to the Hook

Return now to the question we opened with. Allah asks a question. The idolaters — the people being addressed — **do not answer.** Someone else answers.

Why don't they answer?

In a courtroom, when a witness goes silent, it can mean several things. They don't know the answer. They know the answer and it condemns them. Or the question has undone them so completely that the category of "answer" no longer applies.

Here, I think it is all three at once.

*Ayna shurakāʾiya?* — "Where are My partners?" The idolaters cannot answer because the answer is: *nowhere.* The things they treated as ultimate are simply gone. They cannot point to them. They cannot summon them. The very next ayah tells us: they call them, and they do not respond. The partners who were so present in every decision, every fear, every sacrifice of this life — have become unreachable.

But there is something deeper in the silence. When you have organized your entire existence around something, and that something reveals itself on the Day of Days to be a *zaʿm* — a claim, an allegation you were never fully sure of — what do you say? The question undoes not just the relationship with the idol. It undoes the whole **architecture of a life.**

[PAUSE]

The silence is one of the most powerful pedagogical moments in Surah Al-Qasas. The surah began with Pharaoh's voice — loud, commanding, performing certainty. *"I am your highest lord."* And it arrives here, near its end, in silence. The people whose lives were organized around loudly-performed certainties stand mute before a single question.

Because the question is not really about the location of the idols. The question is: **why?** Why did you choose this instead of Me?

And there is no answer to that question that does not unravel the one being asked.

Ibn Kathir calls this divine questioning a *tawbīkh* — a rebuke. But a rebuke is not only a punishment. It is a naming. Allah names, on that Day, exactly what was done. And the person who organized their entire life around a false certainty is left with no language, because the language they would have reached for was *zaʿm*, and *zaʿm* has nothing left to say.

## Closing: What To Do With This

There is a reason these two ayahs sit in Surah Al-Qasas and not elsewhere. The surah has already shown you three named collapses: Pharaoh, Qarun, and the exile of Musa. By the time you arrive at these ayahs, you have seen what happens when people build their identities on external power, external wealth, and externally-conferred status. The surah has done its work.

Now it asks you the intimate question: **and what have you built your identity on?**

Not in abstraction. In specifics. The specific thing you would describe as "I can't lose this." The specific person whose opinion of you you have placed above the opinion of Allah. The specific habit, the specific comfort, the specific story about yourself that you have treated as more reliable than *lā ilāha illa Allāh.*

These ayahs are not primarily about stone idols in seventh-century Arabia. They are about the live idols we carry inside us, right now, in lives that look nothing like the ancient desert and contain all of the same worship.

[PAUSE]

The *tabarruʾ* is coming. The disavowal is going to happen. The walāya you assumed with every false partner will be dissolved publicly, before Allah, by the very things you trusted with your ultimate concern. The question these ayahs ask — and they are asking it now, in this moment, while there is time — is not whether the dissolution will come. It will. The question is: **do you want to perform your own *tabarruʾ* now, while the choice is still yours?**

To say: I name this thing for what it is. I know it cannot save me. I know the story I've been telling was *zaʿm* — a claim, not certainty. I know the distance between me and Allah has been mine to build. And so it is mine to close.

And then: to close it.

[PAUSE]

The distance is not permanent. *Nādā* implies a gap — but a gap can be crossed. The verb does not say the person is unreachable. It says they are far. And far is a condition that can change.

This is the mercy underneath the terror of these two ayahs. They show you the full architecture of self-deception in bright light — the distance you built, the half-belief you performed, the misdirection underneath the misdirection, the disavowal waiting at the end — so that you can see it now, before it is disclosed, before the question has no possible answer left.

*Ayna shurakāʾiya?* — "Where are My partners?"

The question is being asked of us too. Right now. In the stillness after this ayah.

And we still have time to answer honestly.

[PAUSE]
