# If Allah Made the Night Permanent

*Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 71*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

> قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِن جَعَلَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلَّيْلَ سَرْمَدًا إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ مَنْ إِلَـٰهٌ غَيْرُ ٱللَّهِ يَأْتِيكُم بِضِيَآءٍ ۖ أَفَلَا تَسْمَعُونَ

*Qul ara'aytum in jaʿala-llāhu ʿalaykumu-l-layla sarmadan ilā yawmi-l-qiyāmati man ilāhun ghayru-llāhi ya'tīkum bi-ḍiyā'in — afalā tasmaʿūn.*

> **Say: "Have you considered — if Allah were to make the night permanent over you, until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than Allah could bring you light? Will you not then listen?"**

---

### Where We Are

We are deep in Surah Al-Qasas — the surah of *stories*. The whole surah has been one long demonstration. A baby on a river. A mother whose chest empties of fear because Allah promised. A young man fleeing into the desert with nothing. A weak woman walking shyly with a message that changes a prophet's life. Mountains of sand, a flock of sheep, a stick that turns into a snake, a tyrant who drowns.

For seventy ayahs we have watched Allah arrange the world. Watched Him pick up the helpless and place them where He wants. Watched Him bring down the mighty until the sea closes over them.

And now — almost as if Allah pauses the storytelling and turns to face us — He says: *Qul.* **Say.**

The audience shifts. The Prophet ﷺ is told to speak. Not to a community of believers. To the very people watching all of this and still bowing to gods that cannot do any of it. Quraysh. The merchants of Makkah who slept under stars they did not make and woke to a sun they did not light.

Allah doesn't argue with them. He doesn't list proofs. He runs a thought-experiment.

### The Hook

He is about to take one of the most ordinary things in your life — something so background you have not thought about it in weeks — and use it to dismantle every false god you carry.

Stay with me. The proof is not where you think it is. And the question He ends with is not the question you would expect Him to ask.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ara'aytum* — "Have you considered?"

Before we touch the ayah, let me show you something about how questions work.

There is a difference between asking someone *"What do you think?"* and asking them *"Picture this — what would you do?"* The first question stays in the head. The second one drags you into a scene. You have to *see* something before you can answer.

In Arabic, there is a special construction for this second kind of question. It is built from the root *r-ʾ-y* — to see. But when you stack it into the form *ara'ayta* (or, here, the plural *ara'aytum*) it stops meaning literal seeing. It means: **"Have you seen — have you considered — what would you say if…?"** Classical grammarians call this construction a request for an *opinion grounded in an imagined scene*. You cannot answer it without first picturing what is being described.

It is the Arabic equivalent of grabbing someone by the shoulders and saying: *"Look. Just look at this for a second."*

That is the word Allah opens the ayah with.

Notice something. He is not asking Quraysh to debate theology. He is not asking them to weigh evidence. He is asking them to **enter a room in their imagination and look around.**

And He says it in the plural. *Ara'aytum* — all of you. Not just the leaders. Not just the priests of Hubal. Every single person sleeping in Makkah that night. Every poet, every trader, every slave, every child. **All of you — picture this.**

Stay with that. Allah does not bully Quraysh with a verdict. He invites them into a scene and waits for them to draw their own conclusion. He treats them as people capable of seeing — if only they will look.

This is one of the kindest opening words in the Quran's polemic. It is the word of a teacher who refuses to give up on the student. *Picture it with me. Then you tell me what you find.*

### Element Two: *Sarmadan* — A Word That Sounds Like What It Means

Now we step into the imagined scene itself. And the first thing Allah does inside it is hand you a single, strange word.

*Sarmadan.*

This word does not appear often in the Quran. In fact, it appears only here and in the next ayah — twice, back to back, and then never again. It is a rare word. It is meant to feel rare.

What does it mean? The lexicographers describe *sarmad* as something that is *continuous, unbroken, with no gap, no end in sight.* It is permanence — but permanence felt as *weight,* not as comfort. It is the kind of forever that presses down on you.

Listen to the word itself. *Sar-mad.* The sound drags. The *mad* at the end stretches. In Arabic recitation, the *alif* at the end pulls the syllable into a long, heavy exhale. You cannot say *sarmadan* quickly. The mouth has to slow down.

This is not decoration. The sound of the word is enacting what the word *means.* A night that goes on, and on, and on. A darkness with no horizon.

Now — here is something easy to miss. Allah did not say *"if Allah made the night long"* or *"if Allah extended the night."* He used a word that means **night without end until the Day of Resurrection.** *Ilā yawmi-l-qiyāmah.* All the way to the end of the world.

Why so extreme? Why not just say "for a year" or "for a long time"?

Because the point is not the *duration.* The point is the *helplessness.* Allah is constructing a scenario in which **no human plan can save you.** You cannot wait it out. You cannot build a fire big enough. You cannot migrate to a sunnier country. The sun is gone. Forever. Until everything ends.

Notice what Allah is doing. He is taking your usual escape routes — patience, planning, movement, technology — and He is closing every one of them. He is sealing the room. And only then, with the doors shut, does He ask His question.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me on one more thing. Why night? Why not heat, or hunger, or fear?

Because night is the most ordinary mercy you have. You did not earn it. You did not negotiate it. You did not even *notice* it this morning when light returned to your room. The sun rose because the sun rises. That is the deepest level of forgetting — when a mercy becomes so reliable you stop seeing it as a mercy at all.

Allah is reaching for the gift you have most taken for granted. Because the gift you take for granted is the one that will most expose your false gods when it is withdrawn.

### Element Three: The Asymmetric Word — *Ḍiyā'* and What is Missing

This is where the ayah opens completely. And to see it, we need to look very carefully at one word — and then at one word that **is not in the ayah at all.**

Allah asks: *man ilāhun ghayru-llāhi ya'tīkum bi-ḍiyā'in?* — **"What god other than Allah could bring you light?"**

The word for light here is *ḍiyā'.*

Now, in Arabic, there are several words for light. *Nūr* is one. *Ḍiyā'* is another. They are not synonyms. The classical lexicographers and the mufassirūn — Al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt,* and others after him — draw a careful distinction. *Nūr* is a softer, derivative light. It is borrowed light. The light of a lamp. The light of the moon, which has no fire of its own. *Ḍiyā'*, by contrast, is light that **originates from a source that burns.** It is the light of the sun. Light that comes with heat, with energy, with the power to sustain life.

That distinction is not theoretical. The Quran itself uses these two words with deliberate precision elsewhere — *ḍiyā'* for the sun, *nūr* for the moon (Surah Yunus 10:5).

So when Allah asks *"who could bring you ḍiyā'?"* — He is not asking *"who could turn on a lamp for you?"* He is asking: **"Who could bring you back the sun?"**

Stay with this. The challenge is not whether your false god can light a candle in the dark. The challenge is whether your false god can hang a star in the sky that burns for a billion years and warms a planet from ninety-three million miles away.

That is what *ḍiyā'* names.

And the answer, of course, is no. No idol can do that. No ancestor can do that. No king, no priest, no money, no career, no ideology can do that. Quraysh knew this. They had no theology that claimed Hubal made the sun.

[PAUSE]

But now — here is the deeper thing. **Look at what is missing from the ayah.**

Allah says *"bring you light"* — *ya'tīkum bi-ḍiyā'.* He does not say *"bring you back the sun."* He does not say *"bring you back the sun and the moon and the stars."* He just says: **light.**

Why?

Because the *source* doesn't matter for the argument. He is not saying "no one else can build a sun." He is saying — *bring light. By any means. Through any method you like. Use whatever god, whatever device, whatever ritual, whatever power. Bring some light into a world I have darkened.*

And still — nothing.

This is a deeper kind of helplessness than we usually think about. It is not just that your false god cannot do what Allah does *the way Allah does it.* It is that your false god cannot do what Allah does **at all.** By any means. Through any pathway. Period.

The ayah is not measuring your false god against Allah's method. It is measuring your false god against Allah's *result.*

And there is one more absence. Notice: Allah does not say *"who could bring you light *when I have taken it away.*"* He does not include that qualifier. He just imagines the darkness and asks: who, other than Me, could bring light? The implication is sharper than it first appears. **In this scenario, the rival god is not being asked to undo what Allah did.** The rival god is being asked to do anything productive at all in a world Allah has stopped sustaining.

What this is revealing — quietly, without ever saying it directly — is that **everything your false god seems to do for you, your false god is doing inside a world that Allah is holding up.** Take that scaffolding away, and your false god has nothing left to stand on.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

So look at what these three words have built.

*Ara'aytum* — Allah invites us into the scene as people capable of seeing. *Sarmadan* — He closes every escape route by extending the night beyond any human plan. *Ḍiyā'* — He asks not for a lamp but for the sun, and not even for the sun specifically, but for any working substitute by any means.

These are not three separate observations. They are one argument in three movements. **Allah is showing us that what we worship instead of Him cannot survive five seconds in a world He stops carrying.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we actually live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Mercy You Stopped Noticing

Think about the last time you said *Alhamdulillah* for the sun.

Probably never. Not specifically. Not really.

We thank Allah for the dramatic things. The job offer after the long unemployment. The pregnancy after the long wait. The healing after the long illness. The escape after the long fear. These mercies announce themselves. They arrive with adrenaline. They make us *feel* gratitude because the relief is so sharp against the prior pain.

But the deepest mercies in your life do not announce themselves. They show up every morning, on time, without fanfare. The sun rises. Your heart beats. Your eyes open. Your lungs take in air. A ceiling you did not build holds up over your head. A planet you did not stabilize spins under your feet at the perfect speed.

These mercies are so reliable that gratitude has nowhere to *grab onto.* There is no contrast. The sun rose yesterday and will rise tomorrow, so why would today's sunrise feel like a gift?

This is what makes the ayah's design so devastating. **Allah is not punishing you with the thought-experiment. He is *teaching you to see.*** He is briefly removing the most ordinary mercy in your life so that the next time you walk outside in the morning, you might — even for a second — look up and *know* who is doing this.

The Prophet ﷺ taught us to say, when we wake: *Al-ḥamdu lillāhi-lladhī aḥyānā baʿda mā amātanā wa-ilayhi-n-nushūr.* "Praise belongs to Allah who gave us life after He had given us death, and to Him is the resurrection." It is the smallest, most ordinary moment — opening your eyes in the morning — and it is being treated as a resurrection. As something extraordinary. As a mercy worth naming aloud.

This ayah trains the same muscle. It is gratitude pedagogy. It is teaching us to *see* the gift before the gift is taken away.

And there is a hard mirror inside it. Ask yourself: what else are you doing this with? What other ordinary mercy in your life have you stopped noticing because it has been there too long? A parent. A spouse. A working body. A safe neighborhood. A roof. The fact that you can read these words.

Allah is not threatening to take any of it away. He is showing you what it would mean if He did — so you can love it, and Him, while you still have it.

**The practical application:** Tomorrow morning, before you reach for your phone, before you check the time, before you stand up — open your eyes and *see* the light. Just for ten seconds. *Ḍiyā'.* Sun-light. Burning, sustaining, free. And remember the One who, every single morning of your life, has never once failed to bring it.

### Theme Two: The God You Cannot Replace

Now let's get to the harder question. The one underneath the ayah.

Because Quraysh did not actually believe Hubal made the sun. Ask any of them and they would have said *Allah* made the sun. Their idols were not creators. The idols were intercessors, intermediaries, helpers. The idols handled the *small* things — fertility, victory, trade, fortune. Allah handled the cosmic things.

This is how almost every false god works. It is not usually a rival creator. It is a *substitute caretaker* for the parts of life you cannot bear to leave in Allah's hands.

So the ayah is doing something subtle. It is not just exposing Hubal. It is exposing the **structure of false worship itself.** The structure goes: *I will trust Allah for the things that are too big for anyone else, and I will trust [the thing] for the things I can't quite trust Allah with.*

Substitute anything for [the thing]. Your career. Your savings. Your network. Your strategy. Your image. Your relationship. Your control. Your plan.

The ayah's challenge is this: **the thing you are trusting alongside Allah for the small things cannot survive five seconds without the big things Allah is doing for free.**

Your career strategy presupposes a functioning economy that you did not build. Your savings presuppose a currency that you did not create and a stability you cannot guarantee. Your relationship presupposes two beating hearts you do not control. Your image presupposes social structures that could vanish in a week.

Every "small" thing you have substituted for Allah is sitting on top of the "big" things Allah is doing — silently, freely, every second, without your noticing.

When Allah removes the night for one night, the entire substitute economy collapses. Not because Allah is destroying it. Because **it was never standing on its own to begin with.**

This is what *tawḥīd* really means at its working level. Not the verbal recognition that Allah is one. Quraysh had that. *Tawḥīd* is the inner recognition that **there is no second floor.** No backup god. No secondary caretaker holding up the parts of your life Allah is not handling. It is *all* Him. The sun and the salary. The lungs and the lover. The mountains and the mortgage. There is no division of labor in the unseen.

[PAUSE]

And then — listen to this — Allah ends the ayah with a question that is not the question we expect.

We expect: *"Will you not then **see**?"* He started with *ara'aytum* — have you *seen?* It would be symmetric to end with *"will you not see?"*

But He doesn't. He ends with: **afalā tasmaʿūn — will you not then listen?**

Not see. *Listen.*

Why?

Because the proof has already been seen. The sun rises every morning. They have already *seen* it. The world has been displaying this argument since the day they were born. Seeing is not their problem.

Their problem is that they have *heard* the message about who is behind the sun — and have not *listened.* They have received the words. They have not received the meaning.

*Samʿ* in the Quran is almost never about ears. It is about the moral capacity to hear and *yield.* To let the message land. To let it change you. To stop arguing back inside your head while a truth tries to enter.

So the ayah's final move is to shift the burden. *You have seen. The question is whether you will listen.* The proof is everywhere — sun, sky, breath, mercy. The question is not whether the evidence is sufficient. **The question is whether you are.**

**The practical application:** Identify one thing in your life right now that you are quietly trusting *alongside* Allah for. One backup god. One second floor. And ask yourself the ayah's question: *if Allah withdrew the ordinary mercies that this thing depends on, what would my backup actually be worth?* Then take whatever piece of trust you have placed in that thing and place it where it always belonged.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now, after all this, look at what the ayah is shaped like.

It opens with a command — *Qul.* **Say.** Allah does not say it Himself; He gives the words to His messenger ﷺ. The argument is delegated, person-to-person, human-to-human.

Then it pivots into a thought-experiment — *ara'aytum.* **Picture this.**

Then it builds the impossible scenario — *night, made permanent, by Allah, until the Day of Resurrection.* Four heavy phrases stacked on top of each other.

Then it issues the challenge — *man ilāhun ghayru-llāhi ya'tīkum bi-ḍiyā'?* **What god other than Allah could bring you light?**

And then it lands the punch — not on the eye, but on the ear. *Afalā tasmaʿūn.* **Will you not then listen?**

The architecture moves from a human voice (*Say*) to a human imagination (*picture*) to a cosmic scenario (*the long night*) to a stripped question (*who else?*) to a moral demand (*listen.*) It is the shape of an invitation that closes in on you gently and ends, finally, at the place no argument can reach for you — your own willingness to receive.

And the next ayah — 28:72 — repeats the entire structure, but with the *day* made permanent instead of the night, and ends with *"will you not then **see**?"* The pair is one argument. Night and day. Listening and seeing. The full apparatus of human perception. Allah is using everything you have — eyes, ears, imagination, memory of mornings, fear of darkness — to bring you home.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes have been the same insight from two directions.

*Ara'aytum* opens the door for a person who has stopped noticing. *Sarmadan* is the weight of a mercy never seen as a mercy. *Ḍiyā'* is the substance no false god can produce. And the closing *afalā tasmaʿūn* tells you that the failure was never of evidence — it was of *reception.*

Every theme has said the same thing in a different language. **You are surrounded by proofs. The proofs are the ordinary things. The ordinary things rest on Allah and nothing else. And what is being asked of you is not more evidence — it is the willingness to let the evidence land.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the most ordinary mercy in my life that I have stopped seeing as a mercy?

2. What in my life am I quietly trusting *alongside* Allah for — what is my backup god, my second floor?

3. If Allah withdrew the silent, free things He is doing for me every second, what would the things I have built on top survive for — five seconds? Five minutes?

4. The ayah ends with *will you not listen* — not *will you not look.* Where in my life is the issue not lack of evidence, but lack of yielding?

5. When was the last time I said *Alhamdulillah* — and meant it — for the sun?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The proof of Allah's singularity is not in the dramatic exceptions of life but in the ordinary mercies you stopped noticing — and the question is no longer whether you can see them, but whether you will finally let them in.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow morning, when light returns to your room without being asked — pause. Not for an hour. For one breath. And let what Allah has been quietly doing for you, every morning of your life, finally be *heard.*

### Du'a

O Allah, You who never let the night sit on us forever, who never withheld the morning from us though we never earned it — open our ears the way You have opened our eyes. Teach us to *listen* to the proof You have been writing across every dawn of our lives. And forgive us for every backup god we have leaned on inside a world that has only ever been held up by You.

*Āmīn.*
