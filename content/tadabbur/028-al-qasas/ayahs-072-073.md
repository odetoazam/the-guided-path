# The Question No One Can Answer

## Surah Al-Qasas — Ayahs 72-73

---

### The Words Themselves

**Arabic (Ayah 72):**

قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ إِن جَعَلَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمُ ٱلنَّهَارَ سَرْمَدًا إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ مَنْ إِلَـٰهٌ غَيْرُ ٱللَّهِ يَأْتِيكُم بِلَيْلٍ تَسْكُنُونَ فِيهِ ۖ أَفَلَا تُبْصِرُونَ

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

Surah Al-Qasas is the surah of two collapses.

The first collapse is Pharaoh. The man who said *"I am your lord, most high."* The man who turned a river into a graveyard for infant boys. The man whose throne convinced him he was a god. And the surah opens by telling us the story of how that throne ended — drowned, with everyone watching.

The second collapse is Qarun. He didn't claim divinity. He just had so much money the keys to his treasure required a team of strong men to lift. He said the famous line: *"I was given this because of knowledge I possess."* I earned this. I built this. This is mine. And the surah, just a few ayahs before the one we're reading tonight, swallows him whole. Earth opens. Treasure gone. Crowd gasps.

So by the time we arrive at our ayah, the surah has already shown us two of the most powerful things a human being can possess — **political power** and **economic power** — and it has reduced both of them to dust.

Now Allah turns to the listener directly and asks a question.

Not about kings. Not about treasure. About the **sky**.

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine you are sitting in Makkah, fourteen hundred years ago. There is no electricity. There are no streetlights. When the sun sets, it is *gone*. The dark that descends on a desert at night is not the soft glow of a modern city — it is a dark so complete you can almost feel it press against your skin.

And then dawn arrives. Slowly. The horizon turns gray, then pink, then gold. The world returns.

You have lived this rhythm every single day of your life. So has your father. So did your grandfather. So did everyone who ever lived. The day comes. The night comes. The day comes. The night comes.

It is so reliable that you have stopped noticing it.

And Allah is about to make you notice.

### The Hook

There is a question buried in this ayah that no rival deity — no idol in the Kaʿba, no emperor in Persia, no philosopher in Athens, no algorithm in Silicon Valley — has ever been able to answer.

The question is not "Does God exist?"

The question is something much stranger. Much more intimate.

It is a question about whether you could survive what you constantly take for granted.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ara'aytum* — "Have You Considered?"

Before we open the word, let's teach the structure.

In English, when we want someone to think carefully about a hypothetical, we say: *"Imagine if..."* or *"What if..."* or *"Have you ever thought about..."* These are invitation phrases. They prepare the listener's mind to entertain a scenario that isn't actually happening — and to think about what it would mean if it did.

Arabic has a very particular version of this. The verb *ra'ā* means "to see." But in the form *ara'ayta* — literally "have you seen?" — it does not mean physical seeing. It means: **"have you formed a considered judgment about this?"** It is a phrase that asks the listener to bring their full intellectual attention to a thought experiment.

So when Allah says *ara'aytum* — "have you all considered" — He is not asking whether you saw something with your eyes. He is asking: *have you sat down, brought your full mind to bear, and thought about this?*

Now notice the word.

**قُلْ أَرَأَيْتُمْ — "Say: Have you considered..."**

Allah is commanding the Prophet ﷺ to put this question to people. Not to lecture them. Not to inform them. Not to assert a doctrine. To *ask*.

Let's sit with this for a moment.

Allah, who could open this ayah with any rhetorical mode in the universe — command, declaration, threat, promise — chooses the gentlest possible mode: **a question.** And not just any question, but the kind of question that asks the listener to do the thinking themselves.

Here's what's remarkable. The Quran is not begging you to believe. It is not arguing with you. It is *handing you the tools to arrive on your own.* The phrasing assumes you have intellect. It assumes you can follow a hypothetical. It assumes that if you genuinely *consider* — bring your mind to bear, sit with the scenario — you will reach the conclusion yourself.

Notice something. We live in an era of arguments. Political arguments. Theological arguments. Argument upon argument upon argument. And almost all of them have the same posture: *let me prove to you that I am right.*

This ayah's posture is the opposite. *Let me invite you to see what is already in front of you.*

[PAUSE]

There is a deep dignity in being asked rather than told. To be asked is to be treated as someone capable of thought. To be told is to be treated as someone in need of correction. Allah, in the very opening word of His argument, dignifies the listener.

What this single word reveals: **the Quran's primary mode is not coercion but invitation. It does not break you down to build you up. It asks you to look — really look — and trusts that looking will be enough.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Sarmad* — The Word That Sounds Like What It Means

Now we move to a word that almost no Arab in pre-Islamic Arabia would have spoken in casual conversation. A word that lands in this ayah like a stone dropping into still water.

**سَرْمَد — sarmad.**

Let me teach the concept first.

In English, when we want to describe something that goes on without end, we have several options. "Permanent." "Endless." "Eternal." "Continuous." Each of these words has a different texture. *Permanent* is dry and bureaucratic — a permanent address. *Endless* is exhausted — an endless meeting. *Eternal* is grand and lofty — eternal love. *Continuous* is mechanical — continuous service.

Arabic has its own family of words for unending duration: *abad* (forever, going forward), *azal* (forever, going backward), *dā'im* (continuous), *khulūd* (abiding). Each carries its own weight.

But *sarmad* is something else.

Al-Tabari glosses *sarmad* in this very passage as *dā'iman lā layla maʿahu abadan* — "continuously, with no night accompanying it, ever." Note the triple insistence: continuous, no night, ever. The word does not just mean long duration. It means duration with no joint, no seam, no rest stop — something that continues so absolutely that it offers no respite at all.

Now hear the word again, slowly:

**سَرْمَد.** *Sar-mad.*

Two thick consonants. *Sīn* like a hiss that does not stop. *Mīm* and *dāl* hammered together at the end. The word does not have a soft landing. It does not breathe. It just *continues.*

[PAUSE]

This is the principle the linguists call *al-lafẓ yushbihu al-maʿnā* — the word resembles its meaning. The shape of the word in your mouth enacts what the word describes. *Sarmad* is a word that refuses to release you, just like the reality it describes.

And what is that reality? **Day without end.** Sun stuck at noon. Heat with no horizon. Light with nowhere to hide.

Stay with this for a moment. Let it sit in your mind. Imagine — really imagine — that the next time the sun rose, it just... stayed. It did not move. There would be no evening. There would be no shadow that lengthens. There would be no relief. The temperature would climb and never fall. Crops would burn. Bodies would dehydrate. Sleep — which your body chemistry literally requires the dark to produce — would become impossible.

Within days, civilization would collapse. Within weeks, every living thing on the surface of the earth would be dead.

And here is what is staggering. Allah does not need to *do* anything to bring this about. He simply has to *stop doing* what He is already doing — the silent, unceasing, unnoticed work of rotating the earth on its axis at exactly the right speed, tilted at exactly the right angle, in exactly the right orbit, so that day and night arrive and leave in rhythm.

[PAUSE]

Here's what is remarkable about Allah's word choice. He could have said *dā'iman* — "continuously." That would have made grammatical sense. He could have said *abadan* — "forever." That would have worked too. But He chose *sarmad* — a word so harsh and seamless that the very sound of it presses against you.

Because the threat is not just "the day would never end." The threat is: **the day would never end, and there would be no joint, no seam, no merciful pause where you could catch your breath.**

What this word reveals: **mercy is not just what Allah gives you. Mercy is also the seam He places between things. Mercy lives in the joints of creation — in the pauses, the transitions, the alternations that keep any single condition from becoming unbearable.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Taskunūna Fīh* — "You Find Rest In It"

Now we come to the word that, when you understand it, transforms everything.

The ayah says: *man ilāhun ghayru Allāhi ya'tīkum bi-laylin **taskunūna fīh*** — "what god other than Allah could bring you a night **in which you find rest**?"

Let me teach the root first.

The Arabic root **س-ك-ن** is one of the great roots of the Quran. From it comes:
- **sakan** — a dwelling, a home
- **sakīna** — tranquility, deep stillness
- **maskan** — a place of residence
- **askana** — to settle someone, to give them somewhere to be
- And here: **taskunūna** — "you find rest, you settle, you become still."

Across the Quran, *sukūn* — stillness — is one of Allah's signature gifts. He sends *sakīna* down into the hearts of the believers at moments of fear. He makes the spouse a *sakan* for the other. He says of the womb that the soul *yaskunu ilayhā* — settles into her.

*Sukūn* is not just the absence of motion. It is the presence of peace.

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah.

He does not say "a night in which you can sleep." He does not say "a night in which you can rest your bodies." He says: *taskunūna fīh* — a night **into which you settle, in which you find your home, in which your soul stops its restless motion and comes to rest.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something. We tend to think of the night as the absence of the day. Just the off-switch. The dark hours we have to endure until the productive hours begin again. Especially in our culture — where productivity is the measure of worth — night is just downtime. Wasted time. The hours we begrudge our body for needing.

But the Quran flips this completely. Night is not the absence of day. **Night is its own gift.** Night is its own *sakan* — its own place of rest, its own dwelling, its own home.

Stay with this image. Think about the literal fact of nightfall.

When the sun sets, the temperature drops. Cortisol drops. Melatonin rises. The eyes, which have been processing visual information for sixteen hours, finally close. The body, which has been pumping blood through skeletal muscles, finally relaxes. The neurons, which have been firing constantly, finally consolidate the day into memory.

Night is when the body literally repairs itself. The cells regenerate. The brain clears its waste. The immune system mobilizes. The bones add density. Hormones reset. Hunger pangs are dampened. Trauma is metabolized.

None of this happens during the day. **You die a little every day. And you are resurrected every night.**

[PAUSE]

And the most remarkable thing — the thing that should leave us speechless — is that we do not *do* any of this. We do not choose for cortisol to drop. We do not engineer melatonin. We do not direct cellular repair. We do not negotiate with our hormones. We just... lie down. And our Lord runs the entire machinery of restoration on us while we are unconscious.

We *find rest in it* — *taskunūna fīh.* The verb is in the second person. **You** rest. But everything that the resting actually does — every healing, every consolidation, every restoration — is done **to you** by Allah while you sleep.

Now hear the grammatical observation al-Tabari surfaces about this very phrase — because it opens a door.

The pronoun *fīhi* — "in it" — is grammatically ambiguous in a theologically significant way. Al-Tabari notes two defensible readings: the first is that *fīhi* refers specifically to the night (*you find rest in the night*). The second is that *fīhi* encompasses the full rhythm — both the night *and* the day together, the entire alternating pattern. In that second reading, you do not find *sukūn* only in the night. **You find it in the pattern itself.** The reliable coming and going is where the soul settles.

[PAUSE]

Think about this. We do not just need the night. We need to *know that the night will come.* A person who cannot predict when they will next sleep cannot rest even during sleep — the mind stays vigilant, the nervous system stays aroused. Real rest requires not just darkness but *trust in the rhythm.* The soul settles because the pattern is trustworthy.

Al-Muyassar adds another layer to what the night provides, using two verbs together: *tastaqirrūna wa tahdaū'ūna fīhi* — "you settle and you become quiet in it." Not just rest. Quiet. The Arabic *hudū'* is a ceasing of agitation — the quieting of what was stirred. Night does not merely pause the day; it actively stills what the day has disturbed.

What this word reveals: **Allah did not just give us the night. He gave us a rhythm we can trust. And it is in that trustworthiness — the night that never fails to come, the day that never fails to follow — that sukūn, real stillness, becomes possible. The rest is in the pattern, not only in the dark.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Four: *Wa min raḥmatihi* — The Mercy That Alternates

Now we are in Ayah 73, and its opening is stunning in its compression.

**وَمِن رَّحْمَتِهِۦ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلَّيْلَ وَٱلنَّهَارَ**

*Wa min raḥmatihi jaʿala lakumu'l-layla wa'n-nahāra* — "And from His mercy He made for you the night and the day."

Notice the grammar. The ayah does not say: "from His mercy, He gave you rest." It does not say: "from His mercy, He gave you the day for work." It says: "from His mercy, He made for you **the night and the day**" — both together, as a single gift.

Al-Tabari notes something precise here that rewards careful attention. The act of mercy, he writes, is not simply *giving* the night or *giving* the day. The mercy is that Allah *distinguished between them* — *fa-khālafa baynahumā* — He made one dark and one light, one for settling and one for seeking. **The contrast itself is the mercy.** Al-Muyassar uses the same phrase: *fa-khālafa baynahumā, fa-jaʿala hādhā al-layla ẓalāman* — He alternated between them, and He made the night genuinely *dark.*

Not dim. Not slightly cooler. *Ẓalāman* — dark.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete.

Imagine a world where there was no meaningful distinction between day and night. The sun moved, but the temperature barely changed. The light was always dim. The dark was never deep. The body never quite knew when to produce melatonin, when to drop cortisol, when to begin its restoration. Without clear contrast — without the firm edge of alternation — the biological machinery of rest cannot fully engage. The rhythms of life require *definition*: clear transitions, genuine opposition between the two conditions.

The darkness of night is not a design flaw or a concession to physics. **The darkness is the mercy.** Allah made the night *ẓalāman* — deliberately, as an act of care — because genuine darkness is what body and soul require to truly rest.

And this is why *sarmad* — that relentless, seamless perpetuity — is not just a hypothetical inconvenience. It is the elimination of the very contrast that makes life possible. To take away the alternation is to take away the mercy, because the mercy was always *in* the alternation.

What this element reveals: **Mercy does not always look like softness. Sometimes mercy is a firm edge. The night is dark enough to actually rest in. The day is bright enough to actually work in. The definiteness of the contrast — the clarity of the alternation — is itself the gift.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Five: *Li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi* — The Purpose of the Day

The divine design is now complete.

**لِتَسْكُنُوا۟ فِيهِ وَلِتَبْتَغُوا۟ مِن فَضْلِهِۦ**

*Li-taskunū fīhi wa li-tabtaghū min faḍlih* — "that you may rest in it and that you may seek of His bounty."

Night: rest. Day: seeking. And notice the order. **Rest is listed first.**

The root of *tabtaghū* is **ب-غ-ي** — *ibtighā'*, the reaching toward something desired. The Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*) carries intentionality — this is not passive receiving but deliberate, effortful pursuit. You go out. You move. You search.

And from what? From *faḍlih* — His bounty, His grace, His surplus. Not from your own store. Not from your own invention. The day is for going out and seeking what Allah has already placed in the world for you to find. Al-Muyassar is direct: *jaʿala lakumu'n-nahāra ḍiyā'an li-tabtaghū fīhi maʿāyishakum* — He made the day as light so that you may seek in it your livelihoods.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the entire framework of work.

In our culture, work is typically defined by what *we* produce. The value of the day is measured by what we make, what we close, what we accomplish. The human being is the agent; the world is the material; productivity is the measure.

The Quran shifts the frame entirely. The day is not for *producing* out of yourself — it is for *seeking from His faḍl*. The bounty is already there. It has already been placed. Your task is to go and find your portion. You are not a manufacturer. You are a seeker. And there is a vast difference in what that does to the human soul.

*Ibtighā'* is still a strong, active verb. It commands effort. But it locates the *source* of what you find outside yourself, in the generosity of Allah. The effort is real. The appointment of what the effort will find — that is His.

What this element reveals: **The day is for ibtighā' — active seeking — but the object of that seeking is already provided. Human effort is the journey, not the source. This reframes every working day from self-assertion to participation: I went out and found what my Lord had already prepared for me.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Six: *Laʿallakum tashkurūn* — The Possibility of Gratitude

And then the ayah ends with a word that should stop you cold.

**وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ**

*Wa laʿallakum tashkurūn* — "and perhaps you will give thanks."

*Laʿalla.* Perhaps.

Not *li-tashkurūn* — "so that you will give thanks." Not *yajib ʿalaykum al-shukr* — "gratitude is required of you." Not a command. Not a demand. **Perhaps.**

[PAUSE]

In Arabic rhetoric, *laʿalla* expresses a hoped-for outcome that is not guaranteed. It is the word of a teacher who has given everything and is now hoping the student will understand. It is the word of a parent who has provided everything and is now hoping — not commanding — that the child will notice.

Allah has subjugated the night. He has subjugated the day. He has built your rest into the fabric of creation. He has placed your provision in the world and activated your capacity to seek it. He has been doing this every single day of your life since before you drew your first breath.

And His expression at the close of this ayah is not a demand. It is a hope.

*Perhaps you will give thanks.*

[PAUSE]

Gratitude in the Quran is not a transaction. It is not: you receive X, therefore you owe X in return. The word *shukr* is from a root that means to recognize and to acknowledge — to keep the giver present in your awareness. *Shukr* is a form of seeing. It is what happens when you finally, genuinely, notice.

And Allah, who has created the night and the day, who has made rest available to you every single evening of your life, who has written your provision into the world — Allah is hoping that one day, you will notice.

*Perhaps.*

What this word reveals: **Gratitude in the Quran is not obligatory payment — it is invited recognition. Allah does not need your thanks. He hopes you arrive at noticing, because noticing is what is good for you. The person who sees is the person who lives fully in a world designed by Love.**

## PART 2: THE DEEPER READING

### The Diptych You May Have Missed

To understand these two ayahs fully, you have to take one step back — to the ayah immediately before them.

In 28:71, Allah poses the mirror question: *"Have you considered — if Allah were to make the night continuous over you until the Day of Resurrection, what god other than Allah could bring you light by which you may see?"*

And then in 28:72, He poses the question we are reading: *"Have you considered — if He were to make the day continuous?"*

Two ayahs. Two hypotheticals. One about perpetual darkness. One about perpetual light. Placed back to back.

This is a deliberate rhetorical structure — two parallel invitations that frame a single truth from both sides. In 28:71, the hypothetical terror is being trapped in darkness, unable to see, unable to find your way. In 28:72, the hypothetical terror is being trapped in relentless light, unable to rest, unable to stop. Both are unbearable. The human being requires neither permanent darkness nor permanent light — but the *alternation* between them. And only Allah controls that alternation.

[PAUSE]

Now notice what the two questions close with. The first — about perpetual night — ends with *afalā taʿqilūn*: will you not *reason*? The second — about perpetual day — ends with *afalā tubṣirūn*: will you not *see*?

Al-Muyassar reads *tubṣirūn* concretely: *"do you not see with your very eyes the alternation of night and day?"* The sign is not in a text. It is not at the end of a philosophical argument. It is in the sky, *right now*, visible to anyone who looks up.

This matters. *Taʿqilūn* is internal — the mind reasoning through a hypothetical. *Tubṣirūn* is external — the eyes observing what is plainly there. The first question asks you to *think* about what perpetual darkness would mean. The second asks something simpler and more humbling: you don't need to reason your way to it. **You just need to open your eyes.** The alternation is happening right now. You can see it. Look.

[PAUSE]

### The Question That Dismantles Everything

Now let's return to where the surah has placed us.

The surah has just collapsed two empires. Pharaoh — political power. Qarun — economic power. And now Allah turns from those dramatic collapses to ask about something so quiet, so basic, so perpetually present that no one thinks to question it.

**Can your gods bring you the night?**

Not: can they split a sea? Not: can they raise the dead? Not: can they send a revelation? Can they bring you the night?

This is the precision of the argument. The Quran is not asking the people of Makkah to contemplate something extraordinary. Al-Tabari is clear that these words are directed at the mushrikīn of the Prophet's ﷺ community — the very people who maintained idols in the Kaʿba and assigned to those idols the power to intercede, to protect, to provide. The Quran's challenge to them is not about miracles. It is about the absolutely ordinary.

Anyone can claim power over the spectacular. No one can claim power over the absolutely reliable. The alternation of night and day is so consistent, so complete, so beyond human interference that its very reliability is the signature of the One who maintains it. Ibn Kathir frames these two ayahs precisely as *Signs of Tawhid* — because the argument operates on what we take for granted. The idols in the Kaʿba had never been challenged with this question. They could not be challenged with it — because the answer was not in doubt.

The question has never been answered. Not by any idol in the Kaʿba. Not by any emperor in any age. Not by any technology in any century. **No one can bring you the night. No one can make the sun set. No one can force the dawn.**

What remains, once you have genuinely sat with this question, is not just an intellectual acknowledgment. It is a confrontation with who you have been directing your devotion toward — and whether they were, in any meaningful sense, there.

*Who, then, have you been serving?*

## PART 3: THE DEEPEST LAYER

### Mercy Hidden in Plain Sight

Let me bring everything together.

Ibn Kathir names these two ayahs with a title that is itself a theological statement: *Night and Day are among the Blessings of Allah and are Signs of Tawhid.* Blessings. Signs. Both at once.

A blessing is something given to you for your benefit. A sign is something placed in the world to point you toward something beyond itself. Night and day are both simultaneously. The alternation provides rest, enables labor, sustains life — that is the blessing. And the alternation points, inescapably, to the One who maintains it — that is the sign.

Classical commentators note that if the night continued without end, it would cause not just physical suffering but *ghamm wa humūm* — distress, heaviness, anxious sorrow. And if the day continued without end, the same. The human soul requires both the productive brightness of day *and* the restorative darkness of night to remain whole. The alternation is not merely physiological provision — it is provision for the *rūḥ*, the spirit. Ibn Kathir's phrase is worth sitting in: continuous day or continuous night would cause *ghamm* — a pressing weight on the heart. The rhythm of alternation is not just keeping your body alive. It is keeping your soul from being crushed.

[PAUSE]

There is a theology embedded in the night that our modern era has almost entirely forgotten.

We have streetlights. We have screens. We have alarm clocks that impose our schedules on our biology. We have productivity culture that treats sleep as a concession to weakness, a theft from the hours we could be earning. We have, for the first time in human history, made it possible to almost entirely ignore the alternation of night and day — to live as if there were no rhythm, no design, no gift encoded in the turning of the earth.

And what we have discovered in this freedom from the natural rhythm is not liberation. We have discovered epidemic insomnia. Chronic anxiety. Burnout that does not recover with a weekend. Depression that seems to have no object.

Perhaps what we have discovered is the texture of *sarmad*. Not the literal kind — the sun is still setting. But a functional kind: the blurring of the boundary between work-time and rest-time, between seeking-time and still-time, until both conditions lose their meaning and neither provides what it was designed to provide. When night is no longer dark enough to rest in, and day is no longer purposeful enough to seek in, the gift of the alternation dissolves even as the alternation continues.

The Quran is not just describing the night and day as facts. It is prescribing a relationship to the rhythm. **Rest in the night. Seek in the day. Notice the mercy in the alternation. Give thanks.**

[PAUSE]

### The Practice of Noticing

The ayah ends, as we said, with *laʿallakum tashkurūn* — perhaps you will give thanks.

That "perhaps" is both the most gentle and the most searching word in the passage. Gentle, because it does not demand. Searching, because it implies that many people — perhaps most people — receive the night and the day every single day of their lives and never notice at all.

You have rested in ten thousand nights. The sun has set on you ten thousand times. Ten thousand dawns have arrived, and the world has returned, and your body has been restored, and your portion for the day has been placed in the world for you to find.

*Perhaps you will give thanks.*

The practice the ayah invites is not complicated. It is simply this: *notice.* The next time the sun sets, notice it. Not as data. Not as the background of your commute. As a gift. As the One who is always doing the thing you never notice — running the machinery of your restoration, turning the earth, making the dark that your soul requires — doing it again, quietly, without fanfare, without asking anything in return.

Al-Muyassar closes its commentary on these ayahs with a phrase of compressed beauty: *wa li-tashkurū lahu ʿalā inʿāmihi ʿalaykum bi-dhālik* — "and that you give thanks to Him for His blessing upon you in this." The *dhālik* — "in this" — points to the night and the day together, the entire alternating gift. Not thanks for some extraordinary intervention. Thanks for *this.* For the sunset. For the dawn. For the thing that happened yesterday and will happen again tomorrow.

*Perhaps*, watching the sun go down tonight, something in you will finally see what it is.

And that seeing — that is *shukr.*
