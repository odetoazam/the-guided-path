# When the Term is Fulfilled

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 29

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

> فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ مُوسَى ٱلْأَجَلَ وَسَارَ بِأَهْلِهِۦ ءَانَسَ مِن جَانِبِ ٱلطُّورِ نَارًا قَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ ٱمْكُثُوٓا۟ إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًا لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ جَذْوَةٍ مِّنَ ٱلنَّارِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

**Transliteration:**

*Falammā qaḍā Mūsā al-ajala wa sāra bi-ahlihī ānasa min jānibi aṭ-Ṭūri nāran. Qāla li-ahlihi-mkuthū innī ānastu nāran la'allī ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw jadhwatin mina an-nāri la'allakum taṣṭalūn.*

**Translation:**

> So when Moses had completed the term and was traveling with his family, he perceived — from the side of the mountain — a fire. He said to his family: "Stay here. I have perceived a fire. Perhaps I will bring you from it some information, or a burning brand from the fire, that you may warm yourselves."

## Introduction: The World Before This Ayah

Before we can hear this verse, we have to feel the weight of what comes before it.

Ten years.

That is the term Moses has just completed. Ten years tending sheep for his father-in-law Shu'ayb, in the deserts of Madyan — a man who once walked the marble corridors of Pharaoh's palace, raised among gold, now sleeping under stars, his hands rough with the work of an unpaid servant. He had fled Egypt as a fugitive after striking down an Egyptian. He had crossed the wilderness alone, thirsty, with nothing. He had arrived at the well of Madyan a stranger — and now, ten years later, he is leaving Madyan again.

Stay with this for a moment. Moses is forty years old. He has been on the run, then in exile, for most of his adult life. He has a wife now. He has — the Arabic says *ahlihi*, his family — possibly children. He has nothing else. No mission. No revelation. No certainty about why his life unfolded this way. He is leading his family through the cold wilderness, at night, probably lost — the classical commentators tell us he had veered off the main path — and there is a child crying, the wind is biting, and he cannot make a fire.

Then he sees something.

A glow, in the distance, on the side of a mountain.

[PAUSE]

This is the moment the entire Surah of Al-Qasas has been building toward. The surah opened with Pharaoh's tyranny. It showed us Moses' miraculous infancy. It tracked him through his accidental killing, his flight, his marriage. Everything — *everything* — was preparing this single instant when a tired father sees a fire and walks toward it.

He does not know that the fire is not a fire.
He does not know that the mountain is not just a mountain.
He does not know that in walking toward it, he is walking into prophethood, into the conversation with Allah that will define monotheism for three thousand years.

**He just thinks he sees something that might warm his family.**

That gap — between what Moses thinks is happening and what is actually happening — is where this ayah lives. And it is where some of the most stunning theology in the Quran is encoded, in words so quiet you could miss them.

So let's listen carefully.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Qaḍā al-ajal* — "He completed the term"

Let me start with something everyone has experienced.

You agree to do something for a fixed period. A lease. A job contract. A two-year commitment somewhere. And while you're inside it, the days seem endless — but they are also somehow shapeless, because there is no event marking them, just the slow accumulation of duty. Then, one day, the term ends. You sign the last form, you hand in the keys, you finish the final shift. And suddenly the entire stretch of time that felt like nothing reveals itself as having been *something* — a finished thing, with a shape, with edges.

Arabic has a verb for this exact experience: *qaḍā*.

The root **ق-ض-ي** carries a beautiful cluster of meanings. It means to decide, to decree, to fulfill, to complete, to bring to its appointed conclusion. It is the same root behind *qaḍī* — a judge — because a judge's job is to bring a matter to its rightful end. It is also the root behind *qadar* — divine destiny — because Allah brings every matter to its appointed conclusion. And it is the verb used here for what Moses did with his ten years.

Notice — Allah does not say *Moses worked the term*. He does not say *Moses endured the term*. He says *Moses qaḍā the term*.

He **brought it to its rightful conclusion**.

Stay with that word.

There is a way of doing time, where you survive it — and there is a way of doing time, where you fulfill it. Moses' ten years were not endurance. They were *qaḍā* — a complete, finished, honored discharge of what was agreed.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is remarkable.

The next word is *al-ajal* — **the term**. Specifically, *the* term. Definite. Specified.

Earlier in the surah, ayah 28:27, Shu'ayb had offered Moses a choice: eight years, *or* ten if he wished. The contract was technically flexible. Moses could have left at eight. The minimum was satisfied. But the ayah does not say "Moses completed *an* term" — *ajalan*, indefinite. It says *al-ajal* — **the term**. The definite article *al-* tells us Moses completed the **full, maximum, generous term**. He did not give Shu'ayb the minimum. He gave him everything.

This is a single grammatical particle — one Arabic letter, *alif-lām* — and it encodes Moses' entire character. He did not bargain his way out early. He fulfilled the full measure.

[PAUSE — let this land]

And now ask the question I want you to carry through this entire reflection:

**Why did Allah make Moses wait ten years before calling him?**

Allah is *al-Qādir* — the All-Powerful. He could have called Moses at the burning bush the moment Moses crossed the border out of Egypt. He could have called him at the well of Madyan, when he watered the sheep of Shu'ayb's daughters. He could have called him on his wedding night, or in the first year of shepherding, or the second, or the fifth.

Why the tenth?

The Quran will not answer this question directly. But it embeds the answer in the verb. *Qaḍā al-ajal* — *he completed the appointed term*. Allah was teaching Moses, before He spoke to him, how to *finish things*. How to honor a contract to the end. How to do ten years of unseen, undocumented, unrewarded labor — tending sheep, no audience, no recognition, just the quiet faithfulness of showing up.

Because in three months, Moses is going to be told to walk into Pharaoh's throne room. And the man who can walk into Pharaoh's throne room and not flinch is not the man who fled. It is the man who learned, over ten years of obscurity, that *Allah sees what no one else sees*.

The decade in Madyan was not waiting *for* prophethood. The decade in Madyan was the *training ground* for prophethood. Every quiet morning with the sheep was a tutorial in patience. Every honored handshake with Shu'ayb was a tutorial in trust. Every winter night was a tutorial in dependence.

The lesson of *qaḍā al-ajal* is this:

**The hidden years are not delay. The hidden years are the curriculum.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Ānasa* — "He perceived"

Now we come to a word that, once you see it, you will never read this passage the same way again.

The Arabic does not say Moses *saw* (*ra'ā*) the fire. It does not say he *noticed* it (*laḥaẓa*). It uses a very specific verb: *ānasa*.

The root **أ-ن-س** is one of the most beautiful clusters in the Arabic language. From this root comes *insān* — human being — because humans are creatures who seek companionship. From this root comes *uns* — intimate familiarity, the warm comfort of being with someone you love. From this root comes *anīs* — a beloved companion. The root carries the meaning of *seeing-with-warmth*, *perceiving-with-affection*, *encountering-as-friend*.

*Ānasa* does not just mean "he saw." It means **he saw something that felt like home**. He saw something that drew him with intimacy. He saw something his heart leaned toward.

[PAUSE]

Let me put this in modern language.

You know the feeling, late at night when you've been driving in the dark for hours, lost, your phone dying — and you crest a hill, and you see, in the distance, the warm yellow glow of a gas station? That feeling — *that lift in the chest*, that immediate emotional pull toward the light — that is *uns*. That is *anasa*. It is not just perception. It is *recognition*. It is *welcome*.

Moses did not just *see* a fire on the mountain. He **felt drawn to it**.

And here is what is staggering.

Allah is telling us, in a single verb, that even *before Moses knew this fire was the call to prophethood*, his heart already recognized it. There was something in him that leaned toward it. The verb is doing the theological work of saying: *the prophetic call did not come to Moses as a stranger. It came as something his soul already knew.*

[PAUSE]

And then — this is the part I want you to sit with — Allah repeats the verb.

Moses says to his family: *innī ānastu nāran* — "I have *ānasa-ed* a fire." He uses the same verb when describing it to them. Why? Because he wants to communicate not just that he saw a fire, but that he felt drawn to it. He is, without realizing it, *bearing witness to his own pull*.

This is one of those moments where the Quran lets us see a prophet's inner life. Moses cannot articulate yet *why* this fire feels different. He has no theological vocabulary for what is happening to him. He just knows: *something about this fire feels like coming home*.

Compare this to how he describes it to his family: *la'allī ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin* — "perhaps I will bring you from it some **information** (*khabar*)."

Watch what is happening. Moses tells his family he is going to investigate the fire for two reasons: news/information, and warmth. But the verb he uses for *seeing* the fire — *ānasa* — has already given the game away. **He is not going for information. He is going because his heart is being pulled there.**

The rational explanation he gives his family is the explanation his conscious mind produced. But the verb *ānasa* tells us what his deeper self already knew: *that fire is for me*.

[PAUSE]

There is a tafsir tradition — recorded by al-Rāzī and others — that suggests the very word *ānasa* hints that the fire was not a normal fire at all, because a normal fire seen in the dark by a cold man would have provoked *yearning* (*ishtiyāq*) more than *uns*. *Uns* implies a fire that already felt *familiar*. As though Moses' soul recognized its own appointment before his mind could understand it.

Let's translate this into something every listener can feel:

**There are moments in your life when something happens — a verse you hear, a person you meet, a path you stumble into — and your conscious mind says, *this is interesting*, but something deeper in you says, *this is mine*. Your heart leans before your reason has weighed.**

That is *anasa*.

And the Quran is whispering to us: *that pull, that lean, that quiet recognition — pay attention to it. It is sometimes the most reliable signal Allah has given you that a particular fire on a particular mountain is, in fact, your appointment.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *La'allī* and *La'allakum* — The Doubled Hope

The third element is so subtle that most translations completely flatten it. But once you see it, the entire texture of the ayah changes.

Moses says two things to his family:

> *la'allī ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw jadhwatin mina an-nār* — **perhaps** I will bring you information or a burning brand
>
> *la'allakum taṣṭalūn* — **perhaps** you may warm yourselves

Twice in one sentence: *la'alla*. *Perhaps*. *Maybe*.

In Arabic, *la'alla* is the particle of *raja'* — hope. It is the language of someone who does not know the outcome but is reaching toward it. It is not the language of certainty. It is the language of *possibility under Allah*.

Now stop and ask: **why is Moses speaking in the language of "perhaps"?**

He is a grown man. He is the head of his family. They are cold. He is going to investigate a fire that is *right there* on the side of the mountain. Why does he not say, *"I'll go get fire — wait here"*? Why does he not speak with certainty about a perfectly mundane errand?

Because Moses, the prophet-to-be, the man who has spent ten years tending sheep under the open sky, *has internalized a particular relationship with reality*. He has learned, in the deserts of Madyan, that **nothing happens except by Allah's will**. Even fetching a stick of fire is not guaranteed. The fire might be out by the time he arrives. He might fall and break his leg. A wolf might come. The cold might worsen. He cannot promise his family even the smallest comfort, because *he knows he is not the giver*.

So he says *la'alla*. *Perhaps*. *In sha' Allah*, before that phrase had even been canonized.

[PAUSE]

But there is more.

Notice the structure of the two *la'allas*. The first one is about *Moses' action*: *perhaps I will bring you*. The second is about *their experience*: *perhaps you will warm yourselves*.

He is doubling the uncertainty. Even *if* he brings the fire, *perhaps* they will be warmed. Why? Because even the warming is not in his hands. Allah is the one who allows fire to give heat, who allows skin to receive warmth, who allows shivering bodies to be comforted.

Moses is, in this single sentence, performing the most exquisite *tawḥīd*. He is breaking the chain of causation everywhere it would normally be assumed. He is not saying: *I will get fire, and we will be warm*. He is saying: *I will perhaps get fire — and perhaps then we will be warm*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the load-bearing analogy.

Imagine two parents, both standing in a cold parking lot, with a child shivering between them.

Parent A says: "Wait here. I'll go get the car and crank the heat. You'll be warm in two minutes."

Parent B says: "Wait here. I'll try to get the car started. If Allah wills, the heat will work, and if Allah wills, you'll warm up."

The first parent is not lying. The car will probably start. The heat will probably work. The child will probably warm up. But the second parent is *living in a different reality*. The second parent has noticed that *every link in that causal chain is held up by something*. And the second parent is not willing to speak as though those links hold themselves up.

Moses is Parent B.

This is what ten years of *qaḍā al-ajal* — ten years of completing the term — has done to his speech. He cannot promise his cold family fire, *because he knows fire does not belong to him*.

[PAUSE]

And the most stunning thing is: **the very thing he says "perhaps" about, Allah is about to give him infinitely more of**.

He says *perhaps I will bring you some information (khabar)*.

Allah is about to give him *the entire Torah*.

He says *perhaps a burning brand from the fire*.

Allah is about to make his *staff itself* a kind of fire-tool, the staff that will swallow Pharaoh's serpents.

He says *perhaps you may warm yourselves*.

Allah is about to *warm an entire people* — Bani Isra'il — out of the cold humiliation of slavery into the warmth of liberation and revelation.

**Every "perhaps" Moses utters in this ayah, Allah is about to convert into a "certainly."**

This is the divine pattern: the servant who speaks in the language of *perhaps* — out of respect for Allah's authorship — is the servant Allah elevates to *certainly*. The one who refuses to claim more than is in his hand is the one Allah fills the hand of.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Three small features.

*Qaḍā al-ajal* — the discipline of finishing.
*Ānasa* — the heart's recognition of its own appointment.
*La'alla* — the speech of a soul that knows Allah holds the chain.

Three quiet words, before the burning bush even speaks.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Architecture of Divine Timing

I want to start with something that almost everyone listening has lived through.

You have been waiting for something. A job. A spouse. A diagnosis. A door to open. A door to close. A clarity. A baby. A way out of where you are. And the waiting has been long, and during the waiting you have asked, more than once, *why is this taking so long? What is Allah doing? Why is He not answering me yet?*

And then — sometimes — the thing comes. And when it comes, you look back and realize: *it could not have come earlier*. You see, with sudden clarity, that the version of you who would have received it three years ago was not ready for it. The job would have crushed you. The marriage would have failed. The clarity would have been wasted.

That looking-back, that *retrospective sense-making of the wait* — that is one of the most universal human experiences. And the Quran has a word for the structure underneath it.

That word is **ajal**.

[PAUSE]

*Ajal* in Arabic means an *appointed term* — a length of time with a fixed, predetermined endpoint, known to Allah. The Quran uses it for the lifespan of nations. It uses it for the lifespan of human beings. It uses it for the duration of pregnancy. It uses it for the term of a debt. And here, in this ayah, it uses it for the ten years Moses spent in Madyan.

Every meaningful stretch of time in the Quranic worldview has an *ajal*. Not a vague duration — a *measured* duration, set by Allah, known to Allah, calibrated by Allah to do specific work in the soul of the one living through it.

Stay with that.

When Moses was in year three of shepherding, sleeping on stones, his hands cracked from the cold, *Allah already knew the exact moment He would call him*. Allah was not improvising. Allah was not waiting to see how Moses would do. **The clock was already set.** Year ten, on a specific night, on a specific road, near a specific mountain, walking with a specific family — that appointment was already on Allah's calendar.

Which means: every moment Moses thought he was waiting, *he was actually arriving*.

[PAUSE]

This is the architecture of divine timing. It is not that Allah is slow. It is not that Allah forgets. It is that *the destination requires a journey, and the journey requires duration, and the duration is the architect*.

You cannot arrive at the burning bush as the man who fled Egypt. You arrive at the burning bush as the man who has finished a decade of patient, hidden labor. Those two men are not the same man. The ten years are not the *delay* of the burning bush. The ten years are *what makes the man capable of standing before the burning bush*.

Apply this to yourself.

The wait you are inside of right now — whatever it is — is not a vacancy. It is a *forge*. The version of you that exists on the other side of this wait is being constructed *by* the wait. The capacity, the patience, the dependence on Allah, the slow loosening of your need to control — these are being built into you in real time, even on the days when nothing seems to be happening.

You are not killing time in Madyan. **Madyan is killing the part of you that could not have stood at the mountain.**

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is simple but transformative:

**Re-narrate your wait.**

Whatever you are in the middle of — the unresolved, the deferred, the not-yet — stop calling it *delay*. Call it *ajal*. Call it *the term*. Believe, the way Moses had to believe, that Allah has set the duration with care, and that the duration is doing work in you that you cannot see and could not have asked for.

And then do what Moses did. *Qaḍā al-ajal*. Finish the term. Don't bargain. Don't shortcut. Don't try to leave at year eight when ten was offered. Give Allah the full measure of your patience, because **the version of you Allah is making is being made by exactly this length of waiting**.

### Theme Two: The Ordinary Becoming Sacred — and the Eye That Can See It

I want to ask you a strange question.

How many times in your life have you walked past a burning bush?

Not literally. I mean: how many moments has Allah laid in your path — moments charged with His presence, moments that, if you had stopped and turned aside, would have transformed you — and you walked past, because you were tired, because you were busy, because you did not have the eye that *anasa* gives?

How many fires on how many mountains have you missed?

[PAUSE]

Here is what is staggering about this ayah.

The fire that Moses sees is, in fact, *the most consequential fire in the history of monotheism*. From this fire, Allah will speak directly to Moses. From this fire will come the staff that becomes the serpent. From this fire will issue the call: *innī anā Allāh* — *Indeed, I am Allah*. From this single point of light on a dark mountain, the entire arc of Mosaic prophethood will unfold — Pharaoh's defeat, the parting of the sea, the giving of the Torah on this same mountain, the founding of the Israelite covenant.

And yet — *Moses thinks it is just a fire*.

He thinks he is going to grab a stick of flame to warm his cold family.

The most sacred event of his life is presenting itself to him as an utterly ordinary moment.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the deepest patterns in the Quran, and it is everywhere once you start looking for it. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ received the first revelation while doing what he had done many times before — going to the cave of Hira for solitude. Mary received the angel while at her quiet station of worship. Zakariya received the news of John while praying as he had prayed for years. Yusuf met his brothers' destiny while doing the ordinary work of administering grain.

**The sacred almost always arrives wearing the clothes of the ordinary.**

It does not announce itself. It does not bring a brass band. It looks, at the moment of its arrival, exactly like the moment before it. A fire on a mountain. A cave on a hilltop. A morning of prayer. A line of customers asking for grain.

And the test — the real test — is *whether you are the kind of person who notices*.

[PAUSE]

This is why the verb *ānasa* is so important. It is the verb of *the noticing heart*. The heart that has been formed, over ten years of hidden labor, into a sensitive instrument — capable of recognizing, even before the mind catches up, *this is mine; this is for me; I am being addressed here*.

What forms that heart? The same thing that formed Moses' heart.

*Qaḍā al-ajal*. Faithful, unwitnessed, ordinary work. Sheep tended. Promises kept. Years finished without bargaining. The slow erosion of the ego that needs immediate reward. The gradual building of the *uns* with Allah that comes from countless small acts of obedience in obscurity.

The eye that can see a burning bush is the eye that has spent ten years looking at sheep, and seeing — *underneath the sheep* — the kindness of the Lord who made them.

[PAUSE]

Let me put this in modern terms.

There is a person who works at a school. Year in, year out, she teaches twelve-year-olds. Most days she comes home tired. Most days nothing dramatic happens. But because she has built — through sheer faithfulness in obscurity — the eye that can see, there comes a day when a child in her classroom says one sentence at lunch, and she *recognizes* that this child is in trouble. The mother who was too busy missed it. The father who was too tired missed it. But this teacher, who has tended her sheep for years, *ānasat* it. She perceived it with the warmth of recognition. And because she perceived it, she acted. And because she acted, a life was saved.

That is a burning bush.

Most of them never get reported. They happen in classrooms and hospital corridors and quiet conversations after Maghrib prayer. They happen to people who tended their sheep for so long that, when their fire came, they did not walk past it.

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is this:

**Build the eye.**

You build the eye through *qaḍā al-ajal* — through finishing the ordinary terms life sets before you with full faithfulness. Through showing up to the unwitnessed work. Through the *dhikr* no one sees you do. Through the kindness extended to people who cannot repay you. Through the prayers prayed in empty rooms.

This is how the heart becomes capable of *ānasa* — of recognition. So that when *your* fire appears on the side of *your* mountain — and it will, in some form, at some moment Allah has already appointed — you do not walk past it.

You turn toward it.

And you say, as Moses said, the most courageous and most humble words a human can say at the foot of a holy mountain:

*La'allī*. *Perhaps*.

*Perhaps Allah has something for me here.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what this ayah has shown us.

The linguistic discoveries — *qaḍā al-ajal*, *ānasa*, *la'alla* — and the themes — divine timing, the ordinary becoming sacred — are the same insight reaching us from two directions.

From the linguistic side: Allah uses three small features to encode a complete spiritual posture — *complete the term* (finishing), *perceive with intimacy* (noticing), *speak in perhaps* (acknowledging Allah's authorship). Three quiet acts that together compose the soul of a prophet-in-formation.

From the thematic side: divine timing is the architect, and the ordinary is the disguise the sacred wears. The hidden years build the eye. The eye sees the fire. The fire is the call.

These are not two ideas. They are one truth, told twice — once through grammar, once through narrative.

**The decade of obscurity is not the waiting room. The decade of obscurity is the school. And the moment of revelation does not arrive as revelation. It arrives as a fire on a mountain that a cold man might be tempted to walk past.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What "ajal" am I currently inside of — what term, what season, what stretch — and have I been treating it as delay, or as curriculum?

2. Where in my recent life has my heart leaned toward something — *ānasa-ed* something — that my mind dismissed as ordinary? Did I turn toward it, or walk past it?

3. How often do I speak in the language of *la'alla* — of "perhaps, by Allah's permission" — versus the language of confident self-promise? What would change in my speech if I gave Allah back authorship of the small chains?

4. If I imagine the version of myself that will exist five years from now, having faithfully completed the term I am inside of — *qaḍā al-ajal* — what capacity, what stillness, what depth would that version have, that this version does not?

5. What "burning bush" might Allah be placing in my path right now, disguised as something ordinary? What am I being asked to *notice*?

Just notice that this ayah — twenty-eight Arabic words, spoken about a cold night on a desert road — has placed a small question in your chest:

*What term am I in the middle of completing?*

You do not need to answer the question aloud. You do not need to make a plan. Simply walk with the question for the next few days. Let it sit beside you when you pray. Let it sit beside you when you wake up tomorrow morning and resume the ordinary work of your ordinary life — the work that Allah, who has set every *ajal*, is using to build you into the person who will not walk past *your* fire when it appears.

That is the invitation. Nothing more. Nothing less.

---

### A Closing Du'a

Yā Allah — You who set every term, who fulfill every appointment, who placed the fire on the mountain for Moses on the night You had already chosen — let us complete with faithfulness the terms You have set for us, let our hearts learn the *uns* that recognizes Your call when it comes disguised as the ordinary, and grant us the humility to speak — as Your servant Moses spoke — in the language of *perhaps*, knowing that every fire and every warmth and every step toward the mountain is held entirely in Your hand.

Āmīn.
