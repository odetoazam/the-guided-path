# The House That Belongs to Those Who Stopped Wanting It

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
تِلْكَ الدَّارُ الْآخِرَةُ نَجْعَلُهَا لِلَّذِينَ لَا يُرِيدُونَ عُلُوًّا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فَسَادًا ۚ وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ

**Transliteration:**
*Tilka ad-dāru al-ākhiratu najʿaluhā lilladhīna lā yurīdūna ʿuluwwan fī al-arḍi wa lā fasādan. Wal-ʿāqibatu lil-muttaqīn.*

**Translation:**
That Home of the Hereafter — We assign it to those who seek neither self-elevation on the earth, nor corruption. And the final outcome belongs to the God-conscious.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To enter this ayah, you have to step backward — only a few verses — into the dust of what just happened.

A man named Qarun had walked out among his people. His procession was so dazzling that the ayah before this one tells us his keys alone — *just the keys to his treasure vaults* — required a band of strong men to carry. Not the treasure. The keys.

The crowd watched him pass. And the ayah captures their internal monologue with surgical precision: "Oh, if only we had what Qarun has been given. Truly, he is a man of immense fortune." [PAUSE]

That is the spiritual climate. People standing on the side of a road, watching a procession of gold and silver and servants and silk, and the longing rising in their chests — *if only.*

And then the earth opened.

The ayah just two verses before ours says: فَخَسَفْنَا بِهِ وَبِدَارِهِ الْأَرْضَ — "So We caused the earth to swallow him and his home." The procession ended. The keys vanished. The strong men carrying them vanished. The silk vanished. And the same crowd that had whispered *if only* the day before now whispered something else: "Were it not that Allah had been gracious to us, He would have caused the earth to swallow us too."

The wish became a warning. The longing became a near-miss.

Now imagine you are one of those people. You went to sleep wanting his life. You woke up grateful you didn't get it. You are standing at the edge of the crater where his mansion used to be. The dust is still settling. The keys are gone. The crowd is silent.

And in that silence, Allah speaks.

### The Hook

He does not say, "Look at what happened to him." He does not say, "Be warned." He says something stranger. He lifts the camera from the swallowed ground and points it at a **Home** — a Home you cannot see — and He tells you exactly who gets the keys to *that* one.

And the description He gives of those people is not what you would expect. He doesn't say "those who pray." He doesn't say "those who give." He says something about what they *don't want*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The Owner of the unseen Home, after showing us a man swallowed by his seen one, identifies its inheritors not by what they did — but by what they had stopped desiring.

What is it that they had stopped wanting? And why does Allah frame the inheritance of eternity around an *absence* of desire rather than a presence of action?

That is where we are going.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tilka* — The Word That Points Far Away

The ayah opens with a single small word: **تِلْكَ** (*tilka*).

Before we ask what it does here, let's teach the concept.

In Arabic, when you point at something, you choose between two families of words. There are demonstratives for things that are **near** — *hādhā*, *hādhihi*, "this" — and demonstratives for things that are **far** — *dhālika*, *tilka*, "that." English flattens this distinction. Arabic does not. When an Arabic speaker says *tilka* rather than *hādhihi*, they are doing something physical with their hand. They are pointing away from themselves. They are gesturing across distance.

Think of how you'd describe two houses to a friend. *This* one — the one we're standing in front of — versus *that* one — the one across the valley, far enough that we have to squint. The choice of word locates the listener spatially. It tells you where you are and where the thing is.

Now open the ayah.

> تِلْكَ الدَّارُ الْآخِرَةُ
> *That Home of the Hereafter*

Notice something. Allah is the speaker. He could have said *hādhihi* — "this Home." Especially given that He is the One who built it, owns it, and gives it. From His vantage, nothing is far. From His knowledge, the Hereafter is more present than the world we touch.

But He doesn't say *hādhihi*. He says *tilka*. **He points at it from where we are standing, not from where He is.** [PAUSE]

Sit with that.

Allah is not describing the Hereafter from His own vantage. He is reaching down into the perspective of a person standing in a crater of dust where Qarun's house used to be, and He is pointing — from *that* person's eyes — at something far. He is meeting us in our distance. He is naming, with one word, the gap between where we are and what He is showing us.

Here's what is remarkable. The same Allah who can say *kun fa-yakūn* — "Be, and it is" — collapsing the distance between possibility and existence in one syllable, **chooses to preserve our experience of distance** when He speaks to us about the Home that awaits us. He doesn't drag the Hereafter into our laps. He lets it remain *there*, far, requiring us to look up and across.

Why?

Because the moment the Hereafter feels *here* — close, immediate, automatic — we stop walking toward it. *Tilka* is not a word of remoteness for the sake of remoteness. It is a word that keeps the journey alive. It keeps us oriented. It tells us: the Home is real, the Home is yours, and the Home is far enough that your steps still matter.

There is also a second register inside *tilka*. In classical Arabic rhetoric, the far demonstrative is used not only for spatial distance but for **status**. When you want to elevate something — to indicate that it is exalted, dignified, towering above ordinary things — you point at it with *tilka* even if it is right next to you. The way a host might gesture at a chair across the room and say "*that* is the seat of honor," not because it is far but because it is high.

So *tilka* here does double work. It honors the distance, and it honors the **stature**. The Home is far in time and high in rank. Both at once. With one word.

[PAUSE — major]

What this single word reveals: Allah, in addressing us about the eternal Home, deliberately preserves our angle of looking up at it. He will not pretend, for our comfort, that we have arrived.

### Element Two: *Najʿaluhā* — A Verb of Assignment, Not Reward

The next words: **نَجْعَلُهَا** (*najʿaluhā*) — "We assign it" or "We make it."

This deserves slow attention because the verb Allah chose here is doing something most translations cannot carry.

Let's teach the concept first.

In Arabic, there are many verbs that mean "to give." There is *aʿṭā* — to hand over. There is *manaḥa* — to grant. There is *razaqa* — to provide. There is *waraththa* — to bequeath. Each of these has a slightly different shape. Each carries a different relationship between the giver, the gift, and the receiver.

The verb here is **jaʿala** — from the root ج-ع-ل — which in classical usage carries the meaning of *placing*, *appointing*, *designating*, *making something into something else*. It is the verb of allocation. The verb a king uses when he assigns a province to a governor. The verb a craftsman uses when he transforms raw material into a finished object. It is not the verb of casual handing-over. It is the verb of **deliberate placement**.

Now open the ayah.

> نَجْعَلُهَا لِلَّذِينَ لَا يُرِيدُونَ عُلُوًّا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فَسَادًا
> *We assign it to those who seek neither self-elevation on the earth, nor corruption.*

Notice. Allah did not say "We *give* it to" — as if it were a gift handed over the counter. He did not say "We *reward* them with it" — as if it were earned by transaction. He said **We assign it**. We *place* it. We *designate* it for them.

The verb is sovereign. It says: this Home has an owner, and the owner is making allocation decisions. Like a will being written. Like a deed being signed. Like a king pointing at land and saying, "This will go to that one."

Stay with the implication. **The Hereafter is not won. It is assigned.** [PAUSE]

This is destabilizing in the best way. We tend to imagine the Afterlife as a kind of cosmic vending machine. You put in deeds, you get out paradise. The verb *jaʿala* refuses that picture. The Home is not vended. It is placed. By a Sovereign. According to His criteria. And He is telling us, in this very ayah, what those criteria are.

Notice also the tense. *Najʿaluhā* is **present-tense, ongoing**. Not *jaʿalnāhā* — "We assigned it" — past, finished. Not *sa-najʿaluhā* — "We will assign it" — future, deferred. *Najʿaluhā* — "We are assigning it, We assign it, We keep assigning it." The allocation is happening now. As you breathe. As you read this. The deed is being written even as the human walks through the world that will not last.

What this invites us to consider: the allocation of eternity is not a future judgment we are waiting for. It is a present designation that is being shaped, in real time, by who we are becoming. Every act of internal turning — toward humility, away from grandiosity — is the pen of *najʿaluhā* moving on the deed.

And one more thing. There is a *nūn* of majesty at the front of the verb. **We** assign it — not "I" assign it. In Arabic, this royal plural (*nūn al-ʿaẓamah*) is reserved for moments where Allah wants to underline His sovereignty, His vastness, the weight of the decree. He is not saying "I" — soft, intimate, conversational. He is saying "We" — the voice of the Throne. The voice that places mountains. The voice that opens earths.

The decree of who inherits the Home is being made in *that* voice.

[PAUSE]

What this single verb reveals: the Hereafter is not earned by transaction; it is assigned by sovereignty — and the assignment is happening *now*, according to who you are *becoming*.

### Element Three: *Lā Yurīdūna* — The Absence That Inherits

Now we come to the strangest and most beautiful turn in the ayah. The description of the inheritors.

> لِلَّذِينَ لَا يُرِيدُونَ عُلُوًّا فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فَسَادًا
> *for those who do not seek (lā yurīdūna) self-elevation on the earth, nor corruption.*

Let's teach the concept first.

In Arabic, **irādah** — from the root ر-و-د — is not just "wanting" in the casual sense. It is *willing*. It is the inner orientation, the directionality of the soul. When you say *urīdu*, you are saying more than "I'd like." You are saying "I am pointed toward this. My will is turned this way. This is what I am reaching for."

It is the verb of internal compass.

So *lā yurīdūna* is not "they do not get" or "they do not achieve." It is **they do not *will* it. They do not *aim* at it. Their internal compass is not pointed there.** [PAUSE]

Notice what Allah did not say.

He did not say "those who do not *commit* corruption." He did not say "those who do not *achieve* elevation." He went one level deeper than action. He went to the level of **the wanting itself**. He is describing people whose *desire* has been rearranged.

This is not behavioral modification. This is interior surgery.

Many people refrain from corruption because they fear getting caught. Many refrain from arrogance because the moment hasn't come. Many do not stand above others not because they don't want to — but because they haven't had the chance. Allah is not describing those people. He is describing people who, if you opened their chest and looked at the compass of their *irādah*, the needle is not pointed at *ʿuluww* or *fasād* at all. The wanting itself has died.

Now let's open the two things they no longer want.

**ʿUluwwan fī al-arḍ** — "self-elevation on the earth." The root ع-ل-و carries the meaning of *being high*, *rising*. Allah's own name *al-ʿAlī* — the Most High — comes from this root. So *ʿuluww* is not a neutral word for "tall" or "elevated." It is a word that, when used of a creature, carries the **theft of an attribute that belongs to Allah alone**.

When a human seeks *ʿuluww*, they are not just being ambitious in a flat sense. They are reaching for an altitude that is not theirs. They are saying — by their reaching — "I want to be above. Above others. Above accountability. Above the ground."

And recall who we just watched. *Qarun*. The Arabic of his story uses this very root. Pharaoh, who appeared earlier in this same surah, is described with *istakbara* — but his brother in this surah, Qarun, is the one whose entire procession was an exercise in *ʿuluww*. The keys, the gold, the followers, the height of his self-display. The ground swallowed exactly the man who was reaching upward.

And then **fasād**. The root ف-س-د means *to corrupt, to spoil, to break what was working*. Classical lexicons use it for milk that has gone off, for fruit that has rotted, for a body that has decayed. It is the language of *something that was once whole being unwoven*. When applied to the earth, *fasād* means the unweaving of the order Allah placed in His creation — whether through bloodshed, injustice, oppression, deceit, the breaking of trusts, the unjust taking of wealth.

Notice the pairing. *ʿUluww* and *fasād*. Self-elevation and the unweaving of the world. **They almost always travel together.** [PAUSE]

This is one of the quiet psychological insights of the ayah. Allah is not listing two random sins. He is naming a *sequence*. The reaching upward is what generates the unweaving downward. The one who wants to stand above others will, eventually, in order to maintain that altitude, have to break something — a person, a trust, a system, a truth. *ʿUluww* is the inner posture. *Fasād* is what that posture leaves in its wake.

Pharaoh, earlier in the surah: *innahu kāna min al-mufsidīn* — "indeed, he was among the corrupters." His *ʿuluww* over Bani Isra'il *was* his *fasād*. The two are not separable.

So when Allah says the Home is assigned to those who do not *will* either, He is describing people who have been delivered from a single underlying disease that expresses itself in two symptoms. They have not just stopped *doing* arrogance and corruption. They have stopped *aiming* at the altitude that produces both.

Now — and this is what most readers miss — notice an asymmetry that the linguistic structure reveals.

Allah did not say "those who do not seek elevation **over people**." He did not specify the object. He just said *ʿuluwwan fī al-arḍ* — "elevation on the earth," in the earth, anywhere on it. The object is left wide open. **Any** elevation. **Any** standing-above.

This unspecified object is doing real work. It refuses to let us narrow the meaning to political tyranny only — though that is included. It includes the small *ʿuluww* of wanting to be admired more than your brother. The *ʿuluww* of wanting your name above someone else's on a list. The *ʿuluww* of wanting to be the one who is *seen* praying, while others are not. The *ʿuluww* of subtle religious self-display.

What is left unspecified is left wide. That is the gift of Arabic. That is also the warning.

[PAUSE — major]

What this single phrase reveals: the inheritors of eternity are not identified by what they did, but by what they no longer *want*. The Hereafter is allocated according to the orientation of desire itself.

---

### Bridge

The linguistic construction has shown us three things. *Tilka* — that Home, far and high, kept distant on purpose so we keep walking. *Najʿaluhā* — assigned, not earned, allocated in real time by a Sovereign. *Lā yurīdūna* — described by an absence in their wanting, not a presence in their doing.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what kind of person inherits the Home that cannot be swallowed.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Disease That Hides Inside Ambition

Let me start somewhere familiar.

You know the feeling of scrolling through a feed and seeing someone — someone close enough to you that the comparison stings — receive a promotion, a baby announcement, a viral post, a public honor. There is the first feeling: a flicker of happiness for them. And then, half a second later, the second feeling: a small thinning of the air in your chest. A subtle *should that have been me?* A subtle *I work harder than them, why are they being seen?*

You don't act on it. You don't sabotage anyone. You like the post. You leave a kind comment. But that thinning of the air — that is the *seed* of *ʿuluww*. That is the compass-needle of the soul flickering, for one second, toward the wrong direction. The reaching for an altitude.

Most of us live with that flicker all day long. We have made peace with it. We tell ourselves it's just ambition, just drive, just being a person in a competitive world.

Allah, in this ayah, is telling us something difficult. **He is naming the flicker.**

He is not waiting for it to grow into open arrogance. He is not waiting for it to express itself as corruption. He is showing us that the Home of the Hereafter is allocated to people whose flicker has been quieted. Whose compass no longer points there at all.

This is the inversion the ayah performs on our understanding of ambition. In the world, ambition is a virtue. The one who reaches highest wins. The one who outclimbs others is praised. The whole vocabulary of modern life — career trajectory, personal brand, scaling up, leveling up — is the vocabulary of *ʿuluww*. We have made a religion out of the rising arc.

The Quran does not say ambition is wrong. It says the *target* matters. There is *himmah* — high aspiration — that is praised throughout the Quran, when the aspiration is toward Allah, toward beneficial knowledge, toward serving others, toward eternity. And there is *ʿuluww* — elevation over others on the earth — which is the disease.

The difference is the direction of the arrow. *Himmah* aims upward at Allah. *ʿUluww* aims sideways at people.

[PAUSE]

Now connect this to Qarun.

When the people on the side of the road said "if only we had what Qarun has been given," they were not envying his comfort. Comfort doesn't draw that kind of longing. They were envying his **altitude**. The way he was *above* them as he passed. The way they had to look up to see his procession. That altitude was the wealth. The gold was just its currency.

And when the earth swallowed him, what it swallowed was not the money. It was the altitude. The ground rose up to the level of his ankles, his knees, his waist, his neck — and pulled him down to the level of everyone else. And then below.

The Quran is, in some sense, the long, patient correction of the human reach for altitude. From Iblis's first refusal — *I am better than him, You created me from fire and him from clay* — through Pharaoh, through Qarun, through Hāmān — every named villain in the Quran has the same disease. *ʿUluww*. The reaching for an altitude that is not theirs.

So the test of whether you are an inheritor of *that* Home is not whether you pray more than your neighbor. It is whether, when your neighbor is honored and you are not, the air in your chest stays full.

Let me name one practical application before moving on.

A small daily check. Three questions you can ask yourself at the end of any day in which you have done a good deed — a charity, a service, a moment of patience, a public act of worship: 

*Was I, even slightly, hoping someone would notice? Was I, even slightly, comparing my deed to someone else's? Did I, even for a second, want to be seen above another?*

If yes — and there will be many days when the honest answer is yes — that is not a verdict. It is a diagnostic. It is the compass-needle flickering. Allah, in this ayah, is not saying *those who never flickered*. He is saying *those who do not will it*. The flicker can come and you can refuse to follow it. You can notice the air thinning and breathe the wanting back out. That is the practice. That is the *jaʿala* being written in real time on the deed of the Home.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Strangeness of Being Defined by Absence

Now I want to ask a question that may have been forming in your mind throughout this reflection.

Why did Allah describe the inheritors of the Hereafter by what they *don't want* — instead of by what they *do*?

He could have said "We assign it to those who pray." He could have said "those who fast." He could have said "those who give." He could have said "those who believe." There are dozens of ayahs throughout the Quran that describe the inheritors of paradise by what they do, what they affirm, what they practice. Why, in this particular ayah, after this particular event, does Allah turn to absence?

Sit with that.

Here is what I believe the ayah is teaching us. **There is a level of the soul at which the cleaning has to happen before the building can begin.** And the cleaning is described by what has been removed.

Think of it this way. If you want to plant a garden in a field that has been overgrown with weeds for twenty years, you cannot start by planting. You have to start by clearing. The first work is *subtraction*. Pulling out roots. Removing what doesn't belong. And only after the field has been emptied — only after the *negative* work is done — can the *positive* work of planting begin.

The soul is the field. The weeds are *ʿuluww* and *fasād* — the reaching upward and the breaking outward. And what Allah is describing in this ayah is not a beautiful garden of deeds. He is describing the **cleared field**. The soul that has had its weeds removed.

This is why the ayah uses the language of absence. Because at this layer — the layer of the soul's orientation — the work is described by what has been *removed*, not by what has been *added*. A person who is still secretly reaching for altitude can do all the rituals, all the charity, all the worship, and the field underneath is still tangled. The weeds are still pulling at the roots of every good deed.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what is harder. Allah is implying that the *removal* of these two wantings — *ʿuluww* and *fasād* — is itself the qualification for the Home. Not the trophy that comes from the qualification. The qualification *is* the cleared field.

This reframes the entire spiritual life.

We tend to think: I will do deeds, and then Allah will make my soul good, and then I will inherit paradise. The order is action → transformation → reward.

The ayah suggests: Allah will see who has had their *wanting* rearranged — who has been cleared of the reach for altitude — and *those* are the ones to whom the Home is assigned. The transformation is the qualification. The deeds are the evidence of the transformation, not the cause of the inheritance.

This is humbling in a particular way. Because you cannot fake the absence of *ʿuluww*. You can fake a prayer. You can fake charity. You can fake patience for an afternoon. You cannot fake the genuine quieting of the desire to be seen above others. Allah, who sees the inside, is allocating the eternal Home on the basis of what cannot be performed.

And then comes the closing phrase of the ayah, which we have not yet opened.

> وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ
> *And the final outcome (al-ʿāqibah) belongs to the God-conscious (al-muttaqīn).*

**Al-ʿāqibah** — from the root ع-ق-ب — means the *end*, the *outcome*, the *consequence*, the *what-comes-after*. The same root gives us *ʿaqib* — the heel — because the heel is what comes last in the foot. *Al-ʿāqibah* is the *heel* of every story. The final word. What is left standing after everything else has been pulled away.

And it belongs to **al-muttaqīn** — from the root و-ق-ي, meaning *to guard*, *to shield*. The *muttaqī* is one who places a shield between themselves and what would harm them — specifically, who places a shield between themselves and the displeasure of Allah.

Notice what just happened. The ayah opened with a description by absence — *those who do not will*. And it closes with a description by quality — *the muttaqīn*. These are the same people described twice. From outside, they look like people who don't reach. From inside, they look like people who have placed a *shield* between themselves and the reach.

Their non-wanting is not emptiness. It is *protection*. Their lack of *irādah* for *ʿuluww* and *fasād* is because they have placed *taqwa* — guarded awareness of Allah — between themselves and those wantings.

[PAUSE]

That is the practical application of this theme. *Taqwa* is not just fear. It is not just rule-following. It is the *shield you place between your nafs and its instinct to climb over others.* It is the thing that catches the flicker before it becomes a step. The *muttaqī* is the gardener who keeps the field cleared. Not by force of will alone — but by living so close to the awareness of Allah that the weeds find no soil to take root in.

The connection to Qarun is now complete. Qarun had every shield removed. He looked at his wealth and said, "I was given this because of knowledge I have." (28:78) The shield between his nafs and *ʿuluww* was down. The reach was unguarded. The earth swallowed him.

The inheritors of *that* Home, the one we cannot see, are simply the opposite of Qarun. Where his shield was down, theirs is up. Where his reach was unrestrained, theirs has been quieted. Where his outcome was the crater, theirs is *al-ʿāqibah*. The heel of the story. The final word.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Look now at the ayah whole. Notice its shape.

> تِلْكَ الدَّارُ الْآخِرَةُ — *That Home of the Hereafter*
> نَجْعَلُهَا لِلَّذِينَ — *We assign it to those*
> لَا يُرِيدُونَ عُلُوًّا فِي الْأَرْضِ — *who do not seek elevation on the earth*
> وَلَا فَسَادًا — *nor corruption*
> وَالْعَاقِبَةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ — *and the outcome belongs to the God-conscious*

The structure is a mirror. It opens with **the Home** (the destination) and closes with **the outcome** (the destination, named again). It opens with **those who do not will** (a description of interior absence) and closes with **the muttaqīn** (a description of interior shielding). The center is the two diseases — *ʿuluww* and *fasād* — the things being guarded against.

The center of the ayah is the disease. The edges of the ayah are the cure and the reward. *The architecture itself is the diagnosis: at the heart of every human life is the question of whether you reach upward or guard the reach.* What you do with that center determines whether you stand at the edge of the crater — or walk toward the Home.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us: *tilka* (the far Home), *najʿaluhā* (assigned by sovereignty, in present time), *lā yurīdūna* (described by absence of will).

The thematic side showed us: the disease that hides inside ambition, and the strangeness of being defined by absence — by a field that has been cleared.

Both sides converge on a single truth. **The Hereafter is not a transaction. It is an assignment. And the criterion for assignment is not what you have built, but what has been removed from you. Not what you reach for, but what you have stopped reaching for. Not your altitude, but the quieting of the desire for altitude.**

The Home that cannot be swallowed belongs to the person whose interior reach for altitude has itself been swallowed — voluntarily, by the shield of *taqwa*.

### Questions to Carry

1. When someone close to me is honored and I am not, does the air in my chest stay full — or does it thin?

2. The things I am ambitious about right now — are they aimed *upward at Allah*, or *sideways at being above others*? How would I know the difference?

3. If I imagine my soul as a field, what weeds — what small reaches for altitude, what small habits of breaking what is whole — am I aware of but have not yet pulled?

4. Allah is, according to this ayah, allocating the eternal Home in the present tense, right now, based on who I am becoming. What about my interior life today is shaping that allocation?

5. *Taqwa* as a *shield* between me and my nafs's instinct to climb — what would it look like, this week, to thicken that shield by one degree?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The Home that cannot be taken is assigned, in real time, to those whose desire to rise above others has itself been quieted.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to become someone new before you can begin. You only need to begin noticing the flicker. The small thinning of air when another is praised. The small wish to be seen above. The small pull toward unweaving what is whole.

Each time you notice it and do not follow it — each time you let the wanting pass through without becoming a step — the pen of *najʿaluhā* moves on the deed of *tilka ad-dār*. The Sovereign is writing. You are not invisible.

That is the invitation. Not to become perfect. To become *aware*. To begin placing the shield.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who are *al-ʿAlī* — the One whose altitude is truly Yours — quiet in our chests the reach for an altitude that does not belong to us. Place between our souls and the wanting of *ʿuluww* and *fasād* the shield of Your awareness, so that when our Home is assigned, we are among those who did not, in the secret of our hearts, will what You did not will for us. And let the *ʿāqibah* — the heel of our story, the final word over our lives — be one You have already prepared for those who learned to stop reaching.

Āmīn.
