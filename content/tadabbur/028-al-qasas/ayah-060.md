# The Borrower Who Forgot the Loan

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 60

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> وَمَا أُوتِيتُم مِّن شَيْءٍ فَمَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَزِينَتُهَا ۚ وَمَا عِندَ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ ۚ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Wa mā ūtītum min shay'in fa-matāʿu l-ḥayāti d-dunyā wa-zīnatuhā — wa mā ʿinda Llāhi khayrun wa-abqā — a-fa-lā taʿqilūn.

**Translation:**
> And whatever you have been given — of anything at all — is but the enjoyment of the life of this world and its adornment. And what is with Allah is better and more lasting. So will you not use reason?

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To feel this ayah land the way it landed on its first listeners, we need to step back into the room where it was first heard.

Surah Al-Qasas — "The Stories" — has been building to a confrontation for dozens of ayahs. It has told you the story of Musa: an infant placed in a river, a fugitive fleeing a palace, an exile in a foreign land, a man returned to face the most powerful ruler of his age. The surah is, beneath all its narrative texture, **a question about what you trust when everything is stripped away.**

By the time we reach ayah 60, something very specific has happened. The Quraysh — the people who are listening to the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah — have stated, out loud, their reason for not following the guidance. You can find it just a few verses earlier:

> *"They say: 'If we follow the guidance with you, we will be snatched away from our land.'"* (28:57)

This is not a theological objection. It is not a philosophical doubt. It is a calculation. They are saying, in the language of rational self-interest: *I have done the math. Following you will cost me too much. I will lose my trade, my position, my security. I have what I have, and I am not willing to risk it.*

Allah responds by showing them the cities before them that made the same calculation — and lost everything (28:58-59). Then He says, in the ayah just before ours: *I am the One who made this sanctuary safe for you. I provide you. You are not providing yourselves.*

And then comes this ayah.

[PAUSE]

It is one sentence, really. Clean. Final. Twenty-two Arabic words that take every argument the Quraysh have made — every practical concern, every worldly attachment, every calculation about security and status — and hold them up to the light.

What you have been given. All of it. Of any kind, of any amount. Is temporary use of a world named after its own smallness. What is with Allah is better. And more lasting. So — will you not reason?

The argument is complete in under a minute of speech. And yet it takes a lifetime to fully inhabit.

### The Context That Makes the Closing Word Land

This ayah is unusual in the way it closes. Most Quranic ayahs close with an attribute of Allah — *He is the Forgiving, the Knowing; He is Powerful over all things.* This ayah closes with a question aimed at the listener: **أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ** — "So will you not use reason?"

That closing word — *reason* — is not accidental. Because the people being addressed consider themselves reasonable. The Quraysh are the merchants of Arabia. They are the negotiators, the caravan leaders, the men who read trade routes and calculate profit margins. They take pride in their practical intelligence. They have just made what they think is a *rational* argument: accepting the message will cost us too much.

Allah closes with: *taʿqilūn.* Use reason.

He is not dismissing their intelligence. He is calling it back to itself. He is saying: the very faculty you are so proud of — reason — has reached the wrong conclusion. Not because it failed, but because it stopped too soon. It saw the present hand and forgot to ask who dealt it.

That is what we are here to explore.

### The Hook

There are three features of this ayah that, once seen, cannot be unseen.

The first is a single word — **مَتَاع** — that has encoded within it the entire Quranic diagnosis of the human attachment to this world. Not in its translation. In its root. In what the word means at its deepest linguistic level.

The second is the name of this world. The word **الدُّنْيَا** — which we translate as "the worldly life" — is actually a description. And that description, when you read it in the original Arabic, names this world by telling you exactly what rank it holds. The word itself is a verdict.

The third is an asymmetry in the grammar that most people read straight past. There are two **مَا** constructions in this ayah — *whatever you were given* and *what is with Allah*. They look parallel. But the verbs are not the same. And in that difference lives one of the deepest truths about what it means to hold something, versus what it means to lose it.

Stay with me. By the end of this reflection, the word "possession" may never feel quite the same again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: What *Matāʿ* Actually Means

Let me teach a concept before we open the word.

Imagine that I hand you a car. I say: "This is yours — for now." You drive it home. You park it in your driveway. You take it to work every morning. You wash it on weekends. You show it to people. It feels like yours. You say, without thinking, *my car.*

But there is a day — already set — when I will knock on your door and ask for the keys. The car was never transferred. The title was never signed. The enjoyment was real. But the ownership was always mine.

Now: what would you call what you had?

You had *use*. You had *access*. You had *enjoyment* of a thing. But you did not have the thing.

This is, exactly and precisely, the meaning of the word **مَتَاع** (*matāʿ*).

[PAUSE]

The root is م-ت-ع. From this root in classical Arabic comes: what is enjoyed temporarily. What provides benefit for a period and then is taken back or used up. In Lisān al-ʿArab, the entry makes it explicit: *al-matāʿ mā yuntafaʿu bihi ilā ḥīn* — "al-matāʿ is what one benefits from until a time." Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in *Mufradāt*, the definitive Quranic lexicon, defines it as *al-intifāʿ bi-l-shayʾ muddatan* — "the benefiting from a thing for a duration." The Arabic dictionaries consistently note that *matāʿ* is the provision of the traveler — what you pack for a journey, knowing it will be consumed before you arrive.

This is the word Allah uses for *everything you have been given in this life.*

Not just your money. Not just your property. Your health. Your beauty. Your intelligence. Your relationships. Your years. All of it: **مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا** — temporary use of the worldly life.

[PAUSE]

Notice something carefully. The ayah does not say your possessions are worthless. It does not say you should not enjoy them. *Matāʿ* is not deprivation — it is *enjoyment*. The word includes the pleasure. The food you eat, the family that warms you, the health that lets you move freely through the world — this is real enjoyment. Allah is not telling you it was nothing.

He is telling you what category it belongs to.

Not ownership. **Use.**

Here's what is remarkable about this framing. The Quran is not asking you to despise what you have. It is asking you to accurately categorize what you have. The man who drives a rental car joyfully and takes care of it and enjoys the journey is doing everything right. The man who begins to believe the rental is his and plans his life around never returning it — that man is heading for a reckoning with reality.

Most of us do not despise the dunya. We categorize it wrong. We file it under *ownership* when it belongs under *temporary access.*

[PAUSE]

And then Allah pairs *matāʿ* with something even more specific: **وَزِينَتُهَا** — "and its adornment."

The root is ز-ي-ن — *zayn* — to adorn, to beautify, to decorate. *Zīna* is ornamentation. External beauty. The thing added to a surface to make it attractive, to make it catch the eye.

The distinction matters. *Matāʿ* speaks to the function — temporary enjoyment, use. *Zīna* speaks to the appearance — what draws you in. Together they form a complete picture: the worldly life offers you both its *use* and its *beauty*. Both are real. Neither is yours to keep.

But *zīna* adds one more layer. Adornment is, by definition, external. It is applied to a surface. It is not the thing itself. The mirror that reflects the light is not the light. The painting on the wall of the borrowed house is not the wall. When you fall in love with the *zīna* of the dunya — its beauty, its shimmer, its status markers, its aesthetic pleasure — you are falling in love with something *applied to the surface of something borrowed.*

The ayah is not puritanical. It is precise.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The World Named After Its Own Rank

Let me now turn to what may be the most stunning silence in this ayah — a word so familiar we have stopped hearing what it actually says.

**الدُّنْيَا** — *al-dunyā.* "The worldly life." "The lower life." We translate it, we say it a hundred times, we move on.

But let us stop.

The word *al-dunyā* is not a neutral noun. It is a **superlative adjective**. Its root is د-ن-و — *danā yadn ū* — meaning to be near, to approach, but also: to be low, to be inferior in rank. From this root come: *danā'ah* (lowliness, baseness), *danīy* (low, ignoble), *dunūw* (nearness, lowness).

The word *al-dunyā* is the feminine superlative of this root. It means: **the nearer one. The lower one. The lesser of the two.**

[PAUSE]

The other half of the pair — the word Allah does not use here but whose absence you should feel — is *al-ākhira*: the other, the later, the further one.

So in Arabic, the phrase *al-ḥayāt al-dunyā* is not "the worldly life" in a neutral sense. It is *the lower life. The lesser life. The nearer life.* And by extension, the *ākhira* is the higher life, the greater life, the life on the other side.

The Quran has named the two realms of human existence using a single coordinate: *near/far, low/high, lesser/greater.* And this world — the one you are in right now, with all its beauty and all its provision and all its matāʿ — has been given a name that is, in Arabic, the description of its rank in relation to what comes after.

This is not the name we would have given it. We would have named it something grand. We would have called it the Primary Life, the Foundational Life, the Real Life. And Allah says: it is the Nearer Life. The Lower Life. You are here first not because here is where the heights are, but because you must begin somewhere before you can arrive.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Allah does not say the dunya is evil. He does not say it is worthless. He says something far more disorienting: He says its name already tells you what it is. The Arabic-speaking listener needed no argument. The word itself is the argument. Every time you say *al-dunyā*, you are, in the grammar of the Arabic language, saying *the lesser one.*

We have been calling this life "the worldly life" in English for so long that we have lost the judgment embedded in the Arabic word. The word is not a noun. It is a verdict. And we are living in it.

Let that land.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Grammar of Permanence — *ʿInda Allāhi* vs. *Ūtītum*

Now we come to the asymmetry I promised at the beginning — the feature that, once you see it, will change the way you read the second half of this ayah forever.

Let me teach the concept first.

In Arabic, the difference between giving and keeping is not just semantic. It is grammatical. When something has been dispatched — transferred out, given away, sent — the language marks it with a past tense verb: *ūtī* (it was given), *aʿṭā* (he gave), *arsala* (he sent). The event is complete. The thing has left one hand and entered another.

But when something *remains* — when it is still present, still held, still in its original place — Arabic does not use a past tense verb. It uses the preposition **عِند** — *ʿinda* — meaning "with, near, in the presence of, in the possession of." *ʿInda* is the word of ongoing presence. It does not describe an event. It describes a *state that continues.*

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again.

First half: **وَمَا أُوتِيتُم مِّن شَيْءٍ** — "And whatever *you were given* — of anything at all."

The verb is **أُوتِيتُم** — *ūtītum.* This is the passive, past-tense form of the verb "to give." It has already happened. The giving is done. The thing is in your hand — and the grammar of how it got there tells you everything: **it was dispatched.** Something was in a divine hand and it was released. It entered your world. And what has been dispatched can be recalled.

Second half: **وَمَا عِندَ اللَّهِ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ** — "And what *is with Allah* is better and more lasting."

No past tense. No "was given." No dispatch. Allah does not say "what Allah *will give you*." He says "what *is with Allah.*" Present state. Ongoing presence. The thing has not been sent. It has not been dispatched. It has not entered the realm of transfer and retrieval. **It is still there.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see the asymmetry?

What you have: the grammar of dispatching. A past-tense passive verb. Something that was released, entered your hand, and can — because all dispatched things can — return to the one who sent them.

What is with Allah: the grammar of remaining. A present-state preposition. *ʿInda.* Not "was given" but "is with." It has not left. It has not entered the flow of giving and returning. It sits in the realm of divine permanence, stable, undisturbed, waiting with the patience of what has never been transferred.

This is not a small distinction. This is the Quran using grammar — the deepest level of language — to describe ontology. The grammar of what you have tells you what kind of thing it is: a dispatched loan. The grammar of what Allah has tells you what kind of thing that is: a permanent possession that has never left its owner.

[PAUSE]

And now notice: the thing that "is with Allah" — that *ʿinda Allāhi* — is described with two words, both indefinite.

**خَيْرٌ** — not *al-khayr*, not "the good," but *khayrun*: "better, good" — without limit, without specification. And **أَبْقَىٰ** — not *al-abqā*, not "the most lasting," but *abqā*: "more lasting, more enduring" — again, without specification.

Why indefinite? In Arabic, indefiniteness can carry the meaning of vastness, of incomparability, of something too large to be placed within the definite article's frame. When the Quran uses an indefinite comparative, it is often because the comparison points to something so far beyond what we know that pinning it to a specific frame would shrink it.

*Khayrun* — better, in ways you do not yet have categories to specify.
*Abqā* — more lasting, in a duration you have no units to measure.

The Quran is not giving you a precise comparison. It is telling you that on the other side of this temporary, dispatched, adornment-covered use-loan, there is something better and more lasting — and the only honest thing to say about it is: *infinitely so.*

### The Bridge

Let us name what the language has shown us before we walk into the depths.

*Matāʿ* tells us: you are a user of what you think you own. Everything you have been given is enjoyment-for-a-duration. *Zīna* tells us: the beauty you have been drawn in by is external, applied to a surface that is not yours.

*Al-dunyā* tells us: the world's name is already its verdict. It is "the lower one." The Arabic word does not describe a place. It describes a rank.

And the grammatical asymmetry tells us: what you have arrived in your hand through a dispatch — a past event, an act of divine release that can be reversed. What is with Allah has not moved. It is still there, undispersed, richer and more enduring than any unit of human duration can measure.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about how a human being lives.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Borrower Who Forgot the Loan

I want to start somewhere most of us have been.

You have borrowed something from a friend — a book, a piece of equipment, maybe a significant amount of money. At first, you are acutely aware that it is borrowed. You hold it carefully. You think about returning it. You are conscious, every time you use it, that it belongs to someone else.

And then time passes. The object sits on your shelf. The debt fades from your daily awareness. The book blends into your bookshelf. The money blends into your account. You stop thinking about it. And — here is the dangerous part — you begin, without ever making a conscious decision, to *act as though it is yours.*

You make plans that assume you'll always have it. You build your sense of security around it. You define yourself in relation to it. And then, one day, the friend asks for it back. And something in you — not rationally, you would never claim it was yours — but something in your *gut* feels that a loss has occurred. Something that was *yours* has been taken.

But it was never yours.

**The problem was not the borrowing. The problem was that somewhere along the way, you forgot you were a borrower.**

[PAUSE]

This is the psychological state this ayah is diagnosing.

And what makes it a diagnosis rather than a condemnation is the precision of the word *matāʿ.* Allah does not say you were given false goods. He does not say you were deceived. He says you were given *matāʿ* — real enjoyment, genuine benefit, actual provision. The food nourished you. The health served you. The relationships gave you real love and real companionship. The beauty was real beauty. None of it was a lie.

But it was *matāʿ.* Loan-enjoyment. Use-access. Temporary transfer.

The Quran is not asking you to deny the reality of what you received. It is asking you to **accurately hold the relationship you stand in to what you received.**

You are the borrower.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what happens when a person genuinely inhabits their role as borrower — not theoretically, but in their bones.

Their grief is different. When something is taken, they experience loss — loss is real — but not violation. A borrower who loses the borrowed thing feels sad. They don't feel robbed. The distinction is enormous. Most of our suffering around loss is not about the loss itself but about the felt violation: *this was mine and it was taken.* When you actually, deeply know yourself as a borrower, the violation dissolves, and what remains is just the grief of absence — which is human, and which passes.

Their gratitude is different. A borrower who is given extended use of something excellent is *grateful.* They know they are receiving something they have no claim to. Every morning with health, every year with the person they love, every day with the faculties they use so casually — these become gifts rather than entitlements. The gratitude is not performance. It is the natural response of someone who knows they are receiving what they have no right to demand.

Their relationship to losing is different. When the loan ends — when health fails, when money depletes, when the person they love returns to Allah — the borrower is not shattered in the same way. They are grieving, yes. But they are not in a crisis of identity, because their identity was never built on what they held. It was built on the relationship with the One who lent.

[PAUSE]

The Quran's phrase is *matāʿ al-ḥayāt al-dunyā* — temporary use of the lower life. And then immediately: *wa zīnatuhā* — and its adornment.

I want to sit with *zīna* for a moment longer in this thematic context. Because *zīna* — adornment — is the thing that makes forgetting easy.

When what you have is beautiful, when it shines, when it turns heads, when it generates admiration — it is very difficult to remember that it is borrowed. Adornment does something to perception. It makes the thing *feel* substantial, permanent, essential. A person dressed in magnificent clothes seems more solid, more real, more themselves, than the same person in a plain robe. The adornment of the dunya does the same thing: it makes what is temporary feel permanent. It makes what is borrowed feel owned. It makes the loan feel like property.

This is why *zīna* and *matāʿ* appear together. The enjoyment draws you in; the adornment makes you forget to leave.

[PAUSE]

**The practical shift:** Find one thing in your life that you have been holding as ownership when it is actually loan-access. Not abstractly — specifically. It might be a relationship. A position. A capacity. A level of comfort. And practice saying — out loud, if you can — not "I have this" but "I have been given use of this." Notice what that shift does to your body. Notice where the resistance lives.

That resistance is the forgetting.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Betrayal of Reason

Now we arrive at the most surprising element of this ayah, and the one that closes it.

After presenting the comparison — *matāʿ* versus what is with Allah, the lower life versus what is better and more lasting — Allah does not close with a threat. He does not close with a description of punishment. He closes with a question:

**أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ** — "So will you not use reason?"

I said at the beginning that this word was aimed at people who prided themselves on being practical, intelligent, self-interested reasoners. And I want to press into what that means.

Because there is a common assumption about the Quran and reason. The assumption is that faith and reason are in tension — that you believe *despite* what reason tells you, or that reason stops at the door of the mosque and the heart takes over inside. Faith is feeling; reason is calculation. And the Quran asks you to suspend the latter for the former.

This ayah destroys that assumption.

[PAUSE]

The closing word is not *āminūn* — "will you not believe?" It is not *tashkurūn* — "will you not be grateful?" It is not *tataqūn* — "will you not be mindful?" All of those would make sense as closes to a comparison between dunya and akhirah.

Allah says *taʿqilūn.* From the root **ع-ق-ل** — *ʿaqala.*

Let me teach this root before we go further.

*ʿAqala* — the original, physical meaning of this root is to **tie. To tether. To bind.** The *ʿiqāl* is the rope used to hobble a camel — to bind its legs so it does not wander off. The *ʿaql* — reason, the human faculty of understanding — is named from this physical root. **Reason, in Arabic, is the tethering rope of the soul.**

It is the faculty that *binds you to reality* when every other force in you wants to wander. It tethers the self to what is actually true, the way the rope tethers the camel to the ground when the wind wants to pull it elsewhere.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says *a-fa-lā taʿqilūn*, He is not asking "do you not think?" He is asking: **"Will you not bind yourselves to reality?"** Will you not tether your soul to what is actually true about the nature of this world and what is with Allah?

The people He is addressing *do* think. That is the whole problem. They have thought their way to a conclusion: *we have what we have; following this guidance risks what we have; therefore we will not follow.* That is a complete rational argument. Every premise holds. The logic is valid.

But it failed because it operated within too small a frame. It calculated the present gain without accounting for the permanent. It weighed the cost of accepting against the cost of what is held in the hand, but never weighed what is held in the hand against *what is with Allah.* Reason stopped at the first horizon and never looked further.

What Allah calls *taʿqilūn* is reason that refuses to stop at the first horizon. Reason that is willing to carry the calculation all the way through.

[PAUSE]

Here is what that full calculation looks like.

*What I have: matāʿ.* Temporary use. Real enjoyment. Actual benefit. Duration: one lifetime, or less.

*What is with Allah: khayrun wa-abqā.* Better, in quality. More lasting, in duration. The comparative is indefinite — which means the degree of "better" and the degree of "more lasting" are both open-ended.

What does reason, properly tethered to this comparison, conclude?

It concludes what anyone concludes when they compare a finite good to an infinite one: **the finite is not nothing, but it is not worth sacrificing the infinite for.**

A traveler who sells their home country's citizenship for a temporary tourist visa has made a transaction that even the most rudimentary reason would reject. The visa gives you access. The citizenship gives you belonging. The visa runs out. The citizenship does not. This is not a hard calculation. It is the easiest calculation in the world — *if you are willing to extend the time horizon far enough to include what happens when the visa expires.*

[PAUSE]

This is what the Quraysh were refusing to do. Not because they were stupid. Because looking beyond the visa horizon is emotionally costly. It requires confronting the fact that the visa *is* expiring. It requires making plans for what comes after, rather than plans for how to maximize the current access. It requires a form of self-examination that the comfort of *zīna* — of dazzling adornment — makes it very easy to avoid.

*A-fa-lā taʿqilūn* is not a rebuke of intelligence. It is an invitation to *finish the calculation.* To run the numbers all the way to the end. To include in the balance sheet not just what you have now, but what you will have when the *matāʿ* is recalled and the visa expires.

Reason, properly tethered, does not lead away from faith. It leads directly to it.

[PAUSE]

But now — and I want to sit here a moment before we close — there is something tender in this question.

Allah could have said *a-fa-lā taʿqilūna* — addressed to the third person, to "those people." He does not. He says *taʿqilūn* — addressed to the second person. To *you.* To the person hearing these words right now.

The question is personal.

And it is not a threat. It is a *fa* — "so." As in: given everything I have just shown you — given the matāʿ of everything you hold, given the name of this world, given what is still with Me, undispatched, waiting — *so*: will you not tether yourself to this? Will you not let your reason run to the full length of its rope?

It is the most generous question imaginable. Allah has laid out the comparison with perfect clarity. And then He steps back and asks: will you look at it?

[PAUSE]

**The practical shift:** Identify one place in your life where your reason has been operating on a shortened time horizon — where you have been calculating costs and benefits in a frame that stops at *this life.* Not an abstract place. A specific decision, habit, or attachment. And then, for one minute, extend the frame. Run the same calculation with the full accounting: what is the cost in the permanent, not just the temporary? What does reason, when tethered to the longer horizon, actually conclude?

Then sit with what it concludes.

That sitting is *ʿaql.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Stand back now and look at the shape of this ayah.

It is built in two halves, separated by two pauses — two places where the recitation breathes. And the two halves are constructed as a perfect opposition:

**First half:** *Whatever you were given — of anything — is enjoyment of the lower life and its adornment.*

This half is about: the passive past (you *were given*), the temporary (*matāʿ*), the named-lesser (*al-dunyā*), the external (*zīna*).

**Second half:** *What is with Allah is better and more lasting.*

This half is about: the perpetual present (*ʿinda Allāhi* — it is still there), the open-ended (*khayrun wa-abqā* — incomparably so), the permanent.

Between these two halves: **no verb of transfer.** What is with Allah is not described as "what Allah will give you" or "what Allah has prepared." It is simply *what is with Allah.* It has not been dispatched. It has not entered the grammar of giving. It waits in the grammar of presence.

And the whole structure ends with a question that is also an invitation: **will you not tether your reason to this comparison?**

The ayah is architecturally complete. It gives you the diagnosis (what you have is loan-access), the alternative (what is permanently with Allah), and the tool (your own reason, properly extended). Everything needed for the listener to arrive at the conclusion themselves.

### How the Linguistic and Thematic Are One

What the linguistic journey found and what the themes found are not two separate things. They are the same insight from two directions.

The word *matāʿ* — which encodes temporality into the very label of what you possess — is the same truth as the psychology of the borrower who forgot the loan. The language is describing the relationship; the theme is describing what happens when we misread the relationship.

The etymology of *al-dunyā* — the word meaning "the lower one" — is the same truth as the second theme's claim about reason. When reason stops at this horizon, it has stopped at the lower one. It has refused to look at what is higher. The word's etymology is, in effect, a diagnosis of shortened reason.

And the grammatical asymmetry — past-tense passive for what you were given, perpetual present for what is with Allah — is the bridge between both themes. The borrower who forgets the loan forgets precisely this grammatical truth: that what is in your hand came through an act of giving that can be reversed, while what is with Allah has never left.

**One sentence:** Everything you have been given is temporary use dressed in borrowed beauty, and what is permanently with Allah — better beyond comparison — waits for the soul that was willing to tether its reason all the way to the final accounting.

### Questions to Carry

1. If I held everything I currently possess as *matāʿ* — temporary use, not ownership — what in my daily emotional life would change most noticeably?

2. The dunya is named "the lower one" in Arabic. When I say this word, do I *feel* what the word means? Or have I been living in the lower world while treating it as though it were the higher one?

3. Where in my life is my reason stopping at a shortened horizon — calculating costs and benefits without including the permanent side of the ledger?

4. What is one thing I am currently treating as ownership that, if I am honest, I received through *ūtītum* — a dispatch that can be recalled?

5. The ayah says what is with Allah is *khayrun* — better — indefinitely. Not better in one specific way, but open-endedly better. Have I ever let myself dwell on what that actually means?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Everything you hold is a temporary loan wearing beautiful clothes, and the One who made the loan is waiting with something incomparably better — if only your reason will agree to be tethered long enough to see it.

### The Closing Invitation

Allah did not give you this ayah to make you miserable in the dunya. He gave it to you so that you could hold the dunya correctly — with open hands, with genuine enjoyment, with real gratitude, and without the weight of false ownership crushing you when the loan is recalled.

The borrower who knows they are borrowing is free in a way the false owner is not. They enjoy what they have without terror of losing it. They give generously because they know they are giving from what was already given. They grieve losses with grief that heals, not grief that shatters. They hold lightly because holding lightly is what the actual relationship requires.

*A-fa-lā taʿqilūn.* So — will you not tether yourself to this?

Not to the grief of it. To the freedom of it.

Because the moment you genuinely know yourself as a borrower — *that* is the moment the loan becomes most beautiful. You are no longer defending what you cannot keep. You are simply using what you have been given, gratefully, wisely, and with one hand always reaching toward what is with Allah, undispatched, waiting, incomparably better than anything the lower world ever held.

### Du'a

> O Allah — You who gave us *matāʿ* and then showed us what is *ʿindak* — let us not be fooled by the zīna into forgetting the loan. Tether our reason to the full accounting, the one that includes the permanent alongside the temporary. And when the day comes that our *matāʿ* is recalled, let us find that we spent our borrowing time building toward what is with You. Āmīn.
