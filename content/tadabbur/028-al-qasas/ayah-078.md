# The Sentence That Buried Qārūn

## When "I Earned This" Becomes the Lie

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> قَالَ إِنَّمَا أُوتِيتُهُۥ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ عِندِىٓ ۚ أَوَلَمْ يَعْلَمْ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ أَهْلَكَ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ مِنَ ٱلْقُرُونِ مَنْ هُوَ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُ قُوَّةً وَأَكْثَرُ جَمْعًا ۚ وَلَا يُسْـَٔلُ عَن ذُنُوبِهِمُ ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Qāla innamā ūtītuhū ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī. Awa lam yaʿlam anna-llāha qad ahlaka min qablihī mina-l-qurūni man huwa ashaddu minhu quwwatan wa aktharu jamʿā. Wa lā yusʾalu ʿan dhunūbihimu-l-mujrimūn.*

**Translation:**
> He said: "I was only given this because of knowledge I have." Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed before him, from generations, those who were stronger than him in power and greater in accumulation? And the criminals will not be asked about their sins.

---

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, you have to feel the moment.

Surah Al-Qasas is a surah of two tyrannies. The first half is Pharaoh — political tyranny, the man who owned the throne. The second half is Qārūn — economic tyranny, the man who owned the vault. And in the middle stands Mūsā, the same prophet, confronting both.

Qārūn is no foreigner. He is, the early scholars tell us, *min qawm Mūsā* — **from Mūsā's own people**. He spoke the same language. He prayed in the same direction. He shared the same lineage of slavery and deliverance. And then Allah opened the earth's treasuries to him.

The community around him watched. Some envied him. Some warned him. The believers came to him — and this is in the ayah just before our ayah — and they said something extraordinary. They said: *do not exult, do not be arrogant, do not corrupt, seek with what Allah has given you the abode of the next life and do not forget your portion of this one.*

They didn't tell him to give it all away. They didn't tell him wealth was evil. They gave him **balanced, generous, almost gentle counsel**. They handed him a map.

And then Qārūn opens his mouth.

This ayah — ayah 78 — is what he says back. One sentence. Five words in Arabic doing the entire work of his defense.

### The Hook

Here is what should stop you before we even begin.

The believers gave Qārūn an entire ethical framework. He answered with one short phrase. And Allah preserved that phrase, exactly as he said it, in revelation that will be recited until the Day of Judgment.

Why?

Because in those five words is the **exact self-deception** that destroys every wealthy civilization, every successful career, every gifted human being who forgets where the gift came from.

This is not a story about a rich man from long ago. This is the diagnostic for the most common spiritual disease of our era.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three linguistic elements to open. Each one carries something the translation cannot carry.

---

### Element One: *Innamā* — The Word That Locks the Door

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you something about Arabic that English does not have a clean way of doing.

In English, when we want to say "this and nothing else," we have to build a sentence. "I got it ONLY because…" "It was JUST because…" We have to stretch and emphasize and italicize.

Arabic has a single word that does all of that work at once. It's called a **particle of restriction** — *adāt al-ḥaṣr*. The most famous of them is the word *innamā*.

Think of *innamā* like a door slamming shut on every other possibility. When you say *innamā X*, you are not just saying "X is the reason." You are saying "**X is the reason, and I am closing every other door**."

It is the linguistic equivalent of putting both hands up and saying: "Don't even bring up anything else. This is the explanation. End of conversation."

Now look at what Qārūn says.

> **إِنَّمَا** أُوتِيتُهُۥ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ عِندِىٓ
> ***Innamā* ūtītuhū ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī**

"I was given this **only** because of knowledge I have."

Notice what he is locking out.

He is locking out his parents. He is locking out his teachers. He is locking out the believers who just spoke to him. He is locking out the soil of Egypt that gave the harvest. He is locking out the chance that came along when he was eighteen. He is locking out every prophet in his lineage. He is locking out his health, his hands, his eyes, his breath.

He is locking out **Allah**.

The word *innamā* in his mouth is the door of his heart slamming shut on every contributor to his life — every contributor except one. **Himself.**

This is what makes the sentence devastating. He didn't simply say "my knowledge helped." He said *innamā* — **only that, nothing else**.

Here's what we learn from one particle: arrogance is not loud. Arrogance is a closing motion. It is the moment we shut the door on every other explanation for who we are.

---

### Element Two: *Ūtītuhū* — The Verb That Betrays Him

Now stay with me, because this is the most stunning piece of the entire ayah, and it lives in a single verb conjugation.

Let me set it up first.

In Arabic — as in English — verbs have what's called **voice**. Active voice: "I gave him the book." Passive voice: "He was given the book." Active voice puts the doer in the spotlight. Passive voice points away from the doer; it places attention on what happened *to* the receiver.

The Arabic verb here is *ūtītu* — past tense, first person, **passive**. Built on the root *ʾ-T-Y* (to bring, to give). The literal force of it is: "**I was given.**" Not "I earned." Not "I obtained." Not "I made." **I was given.**

Read that again, slowly.

Qārūn says: *innamā **ūtītu**-hū ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī*. **"I was given it — only because of knowledge I have."**

The verb itself — the verb he chose — admits that he is on the receiving end of an action someone else performed. The grammar of his own sentence betrays his theology. He cannot say "I made it." He has to say "I was given it." Even in the heart of his arrogance, his Arabic forces him to confess that **wealth comes**.

And then — and this is the move that destroys him — he immediately turns around and steals the credit for what was given.

It's as if a man walks into a banquet hall, gets handed a plate of food by the host, and says: "I was served this meal **only because of my appetite**."

The verb itself says "I was served." His next breath claims he is the reason.

The passive voice in Quranic Arabic — when the agent is deliberately hidden — frequently signals the Divine. When the Quran says "it was said," "it was given," "it was decreed," the unnamed agent is often Allah; it is a recognized feature of Quranic style, a way of naming the gift while implying the Giver, leaving the one who receives to connect the two. So when Qārūn says *ūtītu* — "I was given" — the linguistic shape of his sentence has already gestured toward the real Giver. He just refuses to look.

This is the human condition in one verb. We use the passive — we breathe air we did not make, drink water we did not invent, wake up with a heartbeat we did not start — and then we say "**I earned this**."

The verb knows. He doesn't.

---

### Element Three: *ʿAlā ʿIlmin ʿIndī* — The Knowledge He Claims

Now we come to the phrase the classical commentators spent the most ink on. **ʿAlā ʿilmin ʿindī** — "upon knowledge with me" — "because of knowledge I have."

Three small words. Massive interpretive depth.

The preposition *ʿalā* here carries the sense of **causation** or **basis** — "on the grounds of," "because of," "by virtue of." Then *ʿilmin* — "a knowledge" — left **indefinite**, no "the." Then *ʿindī* — "with me," "in my possession," "at my side."

So: "on the basis of a knowledge that is with me."

What knowledge?

The classical commentators converge on a primary reading that is theologically bolder than the translation suggests. Al-Tabari records the position that dominated among the early scholars of tafsir: Qārūn was not simply saying "I worked hard and my skills paid off." He was saying something far more presumptuous — that Allah gave him these treasuries because Allah *recognized his superiority*, because Allah knew his inner merit and rewarded it accordingly. The full weight of his claim, in al-Tabari's rendering, was: "I was given these treasuries by the excellence of a knowledge that is in me, which Allah Himself knows I have, and so He was pleased with me for that, and distinguished me with this wealth above you, because of His knowledge of my excellence above you." Ibn Kathir draws out the same logic: "Allah has only given me this wealth because He knows that I deserve it and because He loves me — He has given it to me because He knows I am fit for this."

Read that carefully.

This is not secular self-confidence. This is **theological self-appointment**: the claim that wealth is divine endorsement. That the balance of the account is proof of proximity to God. That *having* is evidence of *being chosen*. Qārūn was not rejecting religion — he was weaponizing it, turning the language of divine favor into a crown he placed on his own head.

Alongside this primary reading, the classical sources also record related dimensions: some referred to commercial acumen and expertise in trade; some pointed to traditions about esoteric or alchemical knowledge Qārūn believed had secured his fortune. These are not three incompatible scholarly positions so much as three surfaces of the same instinct — and it is worth noting as one reader's synthesis, not as a resolution the scholars themselves offered, that all three share a common logic: whatever I have came from something *inside me*, something *mine*, something that makes me *worthy*. The costume changes; the fist pointing at the chest does not.

And the final word of his sentence — *ʿindī* — seals it. "**With me.**"

The whole sentence is a vector pointing inward. *Innamā* — locking the door. *Ūtītuhū* — admitting the gift, then ignoring the Giver. *ʿAlā ʿilmin* — claiming a basis. *ʿIndī* — and locating that basis inside himself.

The grammar makes a fist. The fist points at his own chest.

---

### Bridge

We have done the linguistic work. Three elements:

- *Innamā* — the door-slamming particle of restriction.
- *Ūtītu* — the passive verb that confesses what his theology denies.
- *ʿAlā ʿilmin ʿindī* — the inward-pointing claim whose primary content, the classical scholars tell us, was not mere self-confidence but theological self-coronation: "Allah gave me this because He knows I deserve it."

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about a man from long ago — but about how the human soul deceives itself about its own success. About reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

We have two themes to walk through. Each one begins where you already live.

---

### Theme One: The Meritocracy Myth — and Why the Successful Are Always the Most Vulnerable

Let me start somewhere far from the ayah.

In modern psychology there is a well-documented phenomenon called **the self-serving attribution bias**. It works like this: when something good happens to us, we explain it through internal causes — "I worked hard, I'm talented, I planned well." When something bad happens, we explain it through external causes — "the market was bad, the boss was unfair, the situation was impossible."

Good outcomes? **I did it.** Bad outcomes? **It happened to me.**

Every human mind does this. Studies have replicated it across cultures, ages, and contexts. It is not a personality flaw of greedy people. It is the default operating system of the unwatched self.

Now sit with what this means.

The more **successful** a person becomes, the more good outcomes they have to attribute. And the more they attribute to themselves, the more they build a story in their head: *I am the cause of my own life.*

The wealthy CEO didn't start out a narcissist. He started out a graduate. Every quarter of growth, every promotion, every deal closed, every "we couldn't have done it without you" — each became a tiny brick in the wall of *innamā ūtītuhū ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī*. **"I was only given this because of what I have."**

By the time he is at the top, his entire architecture of self has been built out of a thousand small attributions, each one feeling perfectly reasonable at the time.

This is why the Quran does not give us Qārūn as a cartoon villain. He is **not** stupid. He is not even, as far as the surface of the story goes, hateful. He is something far more dangerous and far more common.

**He is a man who has been successful long enough to forget that he was given.**

Ibn Kathir, in his commentary on this passage, draws our attention to two parallel moments elsewhere in the Quran — 39:49 and 41:50 — where Allah describes the same human pattern in general terms: when harm touches a person, he cries out to his Lord; but when We grant him good, he says, "this was given to me only because of knowledge." Qārūn, in other words, is not a unique villain. He is the named, concrete archetype of a pattern the Quran returns to across multiple surahs. The man is the illustration. The disease is universal.

The believers in the ayah before — they were not arguing with his wealth. They were warning him about the **attribution**. They knew the disease. They said, in essence: *the wealth is fine. The amnesia is what will kill you.*

And then comes the diagnostic question — Allah's question, after Qārūn speaks:

> أَوَلَمْ يَعْلَمْ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ أَهْلَكَ مِن قَبْلِهِۦ مِنَ ٱلْقُرُونِ مَنْ هُوَ **أَشَدُّ مِنْهُ قُوَّةً وَأَكْثَرُ جَمْعًا**
>
> "Did he not know that Allah had already destroyed before him, from generations, those who were **stronger than him in power and greater in accumulation**?"

Read this slowly.

Allah's response to "I was given this because of my knowledge" is not "no, you weren't." It is not a theological argument. It is a **historical observation**.

He says: *people stronger than you, with bigger piles than you, have been buried before you came along.*

Why is this the answer?

Because Qārūn's self-attribution rests on a hidden assumption: that **success is evidence of merit**. That **what you have proves what you are**. And Allah collapses that assumption in one move — by pointing to the graveyard of people who had more than Qārūn and got destroyed anyway.

If success were evidence of merit, those people would still be standing.

Notice, too, the cutting irony of *awa lam yaʿlam* — "did he not know?" Qārūn's entire defense rested on the word *ʿilm*, knowledge. He claimed to have it. He claimed it was the source of everything. And the ayah immediately turns that same word against him: the most elementary *ʿilm* — awareness of what has come before, the memory of ruined civilizations, the lesson written across every age — is precisely what he lacks. The crown he placed on his own head is the word the Quran uses to expose him.

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow?

Every time something good happens, notice where your mind reaches for the cause. If the hand moves toward yourself — *I planned this, I worked for this, I earned this* — that is the first motion of *innamā*. It is not the statement of a villain. It is the ordinary breath of a forgetful human being. The practice is not self-laceration. It is a gentle, daily redirection: *yes — and who made the plan possible? Who opened the door when I knocked?*

The opposite of *innamā* is not passivity. It is **gratitude with a full accounting of contributors**. It is the recognition that even the knowledge I claim as mine — the education, the insight, the skill — arrived through teachers, through books, through a mind I was given, through time I was granted. The *ʿilm* itself was a gift.

This is not a comfortable discipline for the successful. It requires work proportional to prosperity: the more you have, the longer the list of acknowledgments. The more you are given, the more doors you have to hold open rather than slam shut.

Qārūn slammed every door at once.

---

### Theme Two: The Asymmetric Absence — When the Judge Does Not Ask

We come now to the final clause. The one that seems almost like a legal footnote but lands like a sentence.

> وَلَا يُسْـَٔلُ عَن ذُنُوبِهِمُ ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ
> *Wa lā yusʾalu ʿan dhunūbihimu-l-mujrimūn*
> "And the criminals will not be asked about their sins."

First: notice the structure. The ayah began with Qārūn's one-sentence defense. Then Allah rebutted it with a historical observation. And now — silence from the accused. Not because they were given time to speak. Because there is nothing left to ask.

What does it mean that the criminals will not be asked?

Classical commentators approach this with care, because the surface reading creates a real tension. Elsewhere in the Quran — in 37:24 — we read *waqifūhum innahum masʾulūn*: "stop them, for they will be questioned." How can they both be questioned and not questioned?

Al-Muyassar resolves this by distinguishing between two modes of questioning. The criminals will not be asked *for information* — Allah already knows their sins in their entirety and requires no testimony to complete His knowledge. But they will be questioned in the mode of rebuke and confirmation, *suʾāl tawbīkh wa taqrīr*: the question that strips away the last pretense, that makes the transgressor hear his own guilt spoken aloud before creation as witness. One asking seeks knowledge the questioner lacks. The other announces a verdict the Judge has always held.

This is the asymmetric absence: there is no inquiry because there is nothing to discover. The file is complete. The record is closed. What looks like silence before judgment is not neutrality — it is the full weight of a case decided by a Knower who needed no investigation.

Now return to Qārūn and his sentence.

He said *innamā ūtītuhū ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī* — "I was given this only because of knowledge I have." The word *ʿilm* is his shield, his proof, his crown. And the final clause of the ayah takes that exact word — knowledge — and turns it inside out.

Qārūn said: I have knowledge.
The ayah ends: Allah has knowledge. Complete, prior, requiring no questions.

The criminal will not be asked, not because his case is weak, but because the One who judges already knows everything the criminal tried to hide, tried to reframe, tried to crown as merit. There is no courtroom where the self-serving attribution holds up. The *innamā* that locked every other door on earth — that door will not survive in the presence of the One who saw every room, who knew every motion of the heart before the sentence was even spoken.

This is not a threat. It is a structure. The same divine knowledge that Qārūn tried to claim as his alibi — "Allah knows I deserve this" — is the divine knowledge that will leave him with nothing to say. The logic of his own defense collapses against itself.

## PART 3: THE LIVING APPLICATION

### The Two Moves

By now we have turned this ayah over several times. Let me bring it down to the ground.

There are two moves embedded in Qārūn's sentence, and both of them live in us.

**Move one** is the *innamā* move — the closing motion. Every time we receive something good and explain it with ourselves as the sole cause, we make that move. Every degree certificate on the wall that doesn't mention the teacher. Every business success story that starts "I decided to..." and ends before thanking the person who took the first meeting. Every personal achievement narrated without the mentors, the health, the timing, the soil.

**Move two** is the theological version — what the classical scholars identified as the deeper content of Qārūn's claim. This is not merely self-confidence; it is the belief that your outcomes are evidence of God's special favor toward you, that your success signals your spiritual status. This is more dangerous than simple arrogance, because it feels like faith. It sounds like gratitude. "God blessed me." But when "God blessed me" is used to mean "God has evaluated me and found me superior" — when it becomes a ranking rather than a receipt — the grammar has flipped. The gift has become a verdict. And the believer has appointed himself the beneficiary of a divine preference he does not actually know.

The Quran's answer to both moves is the same: **look at the *qurūn***. The generations. The ones who had more than you and were buried anyway. History is not a trophy case for the successful. It is a graveyard of people who also said *innamā*.

### What This Ayah Asks of Us

It asks for something that requires daily practice, not a one-time decision.

It asks us to **hold our good fortune loosely** — to describe it accurately, which means describing it with the full accounting of contributors. The parents who sacrificed. The teachers who opened doors. The health that held. The timing that cooperated. The prayers of people who loved us. The mercy of a God who did not owe us any of it.

It asks us to **resist the theological version** — to refuse the move that makes prosperity into evidence of divine favoritism, and to remember that the ones Allah destroyed before us were, by every visible measure, more impressive than we are.

And it asks us to sit with the final clause — *lā yusʾalu* — not as a distant threat but as a present mirror. There is One who knows. Everything we have privately attributed to ourselves, every *innamā* whispered under the breath, every door quietly closed — it is all visible, all already known. The case will not require our testimony.

That awareness, held gently, is what keeps the heart from making the fist.

*May Allah protect us from the arrogance of attribution, and grant us the clarity to see every gift as a gift — and every moment of strength as a loan.*
