# The God Who Owns Both Ends
## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 70

---

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَهُوَ ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ لَهُ ٱلْحَمْدُ فِى ٱلْأُولَىٰ وَٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ ۖ وَلَهُ ٱلْحُكْمُ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa huwa Allāhu lā ilāha illā huw, lahu-l-ḥamdu fī-l-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah, wa lahu-l-ḥukmu wa ilayhi turjaʿūn.*

**Translation:**
And He is Allah — there is no god but He. To Him belongs all praise in the first life and in the last; and to Him belongs the decision; and to Him you will be returned.

---

## Introduction — The Declaration Before the Parable

Surah Al-Qasas is a surah of storytelling. The very name *al-qaṣaṣ* means "the narratives." Allah walks us through Musa's life — the basket on the Nile, the fugitive years in Madyan, the cool well at the edge of town, the staff that became a serpent before Fir'awn. The surah builds across these stories, and then — just before it introduces one of the Quran's most dramatic characters — something unusual happens.

Allah pauses the stories.

He does not pause with another narrative. He pauses with a declaration. At 28:68–69, the Quran shifts into concentrated theological statement: Allah creates and chooses, and creation has no say. He knows what hearts conceal and what lips announce. And then — our ayah:

> *And He is Allah — there is no god but He. To Him belongs all praise in the first life and in the last; and to Him belongs the decision; and to Him you will be returned.*

[PAUSE]

After this declaration, the surah continues — parables about night and day, about the dependence of all creation on its Creator (28:71–75). And then, six verses after our ayah, Allah introduces a man named Qarun.

Qarun was wealthy. So wealthy his keys needed teams of strong men to lift. His people told him: *be grateful, don't be arrogant, don't seek corruption.* And he answered with one of the most famous sentences in the Quran:

> *"I was only given this because of knowledge I have."* (28:78)

*I earned this. I deserve this. I am the source.*

A few verses later, the earth opens beneath him and his palace. And the people who had envied him stand at the rim of that hole and say: *"How strange — Allah expands provision for whom He wills, and constricts it. Were it not for Allah's favor, He would have swallowed us too."*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the architecture? Allah first declares the truth — *to Him belongs all praise, all judgment, all return* — and then, almost immediately, He gives us a living case study of what it looks like when a man builds his life against that truth. This ayah is not a conclusion drawn from Qarun's story. It is the premise that Qarun's story then tests.

The surah is operating at full power here. It does not explain Qarun's error after the fact. It establishes the truth *first*, lets it settle in your hearing, and then walks you through someone who tried to contradict it — so you can see the contradiction with your own eyes.

**Here is what we want to discover together:** Allah does not just say "praise belongs to Me." He specifies *where* the praise belongs. He locates it. He gives it an address — and the address is **everywhere**. There is no inch of existence, no chapter of time, no breath of yours, that lies outside the territory of His praise.

By the end, we'll see that this short ayah is doing something stunning: it is stating the truth that Qarun will soon deny — and inviting every reader to choose which side of that truth they stand on before they watch it dramatized.

# PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three elements to open. Each of them, on its own, is small. Together, they form an architecture.

---

## Element One: *Lahu al-ḥamd* — "To Him belongs *the* praise"

### Teaching the grammar before opening the ayah

In Arabic, when you put a prepositional phrase *before* what we'd normally call the subject — when you say "*to Him* (belongs) *the praise*" instead of "the praise (belongs) to Him" — you are not just rearranging words for style. The fronted phrase carries the weight of **restriction**. The technical name for this in classical rhetoric is *taqdīm mā ḥaqquhu al-taʾkhīr yufīd al-ḥaṣr* — "fronting what would normally come later produces exclusivity."

An everyday example. If a friend tells you, "I helped you," that is information. But if the same friend says, "*It was I* who helped you" — putting the "I" first — they are no longer just informing you. They are claiming the credit *exclusively*. They are pushing every other possible helper out of the frame.

That is the grammatical move Allah is making here.

### Opening the words

Allah does not say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — "praise (is) to Allah." He says **لَهُ ٱلْحَمْدُ** — *lahu al-ḥamd*. **To Him, the praise.**

The *lahu* comes first.

And the *ḥamd* — praise — carries the definite article *al-*. Not *some* praise. Not *a kind of* praise. **The** praise. The category itself. Every instance of it that has ever existed or will exist.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Stack the two grammatical forces together. The fronting says: *to Him exclusively.* The definite article says: *the entire category.* So the sentence is saying:

> *The whole category of praise — wherever it appears, whoever speaks it, whatever it is directed toward — belongs, in its reality, to Him alone.*

The classical commentators understood *al-ḥamd* here as *al-thanāʾ al-jamīl* — beautiful praise, laudatory tribute of the finest kind. This is not the routine acknowledgment of a benefit received; it is the recognition of intrinsic excellence, of a worth that would exist even if no one experienced any blessing from it at all. Allah is not just saying: *thank Me for what I've given you.* He is saying: *the beauty in all things, the excellence behind all things, the worth that makes anything worth praising — that belongs to Me.*

Notice what this does to us. When you compliment a beautiful painting — that praise, in its essence, belongs to Allah, because He is the one who gave the painter eyes and pigment and the capacity to see beauty. When a child praises a parent — that praise, in its essence, belongs to Allah, because He is the one who made parents capable of love. When the cardiologist praises a healthy heart — that praise belongs to Allah, because *He* made the muscle that beats a hundred thousand times a day without asking your permission.

Every "wow," every "thank you," every "that was incredible" — Allah is saying: *that praise had a real address all along. It was being mailed to Me.*

This is the door Qarun will try to keep open, six verses from now. He will want some of the praise re-routed to himself: *I earned this, because of knowledge I have.* This ayah closes that door before he even knocks. There is no praise that legitimately stops at a human being and ends there. It is always passing through, on its way home.

## Element Two: *fī al-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah* — "in the first and the last"

### Teaching the concept before opening the ayah

In Arabic, *al-ūlā* literally means "the first (one, feminine)" and *al-ākhirah* means "the last (one, feminine)." These are not abstract nouns like "beginning" and "end." They are *adjectives that imply a missing noun*. Imagine someone said in English: "in the early one and the later one." Your mind would immediately ask: *the early what?* Early house? Early chapter? Early life?

This is exactly what the classical commentators noticed. When Allah uses *al-ūlā* and *al-ākhirah* this way, the implied noun is most commonly understood as *al-dār* — "the abode" — or *al-ḥayāh* — "the life." So the phrase reads: *in the first abode and in the last abode. In the first life and in the last life.*

### Opening the words

**لَهُ ٱلْحَمْدُ فِى ٱلْأُولَىٰ وَٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ**

To Him belongs the praise — *in the first* and *in the last*.

Now here is what is remarkable. Allah did not say *fī al-dunyā wa-l-ākhirah* — "in this world and the next" — even though that's the more common pairing in the Quran. He chose *al-ūlā*. The "first one."

Why does this matter? Because *al-dunyā* and *al-ākhirah* are *opposites*. "The near" versus "the far." "The low" versus "the high." When Allah says *dunyā/ākhirah*, He is contrasting two different realms.

But *al-ūlā / al-ākhirah* is not a contrast. It is a **sequence**. The first one and the last one *of the same thing*. The first chapter and the last chapter of one continuous book. The opening movement and the closing movement of one symphony.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The ayah is telling us that this life and the next are not two separate worlds with a wall between them. They are **two installments of one reality**. The praise that belongs to Allah is the same praise across both. The Allah who is praised in the maternity ward is the Allah who is praised at the resurrection. He does not change. The praise does not change. Only *we* move — from the first room of His house to the last room of His house.

This is doing surgical work against a very specific human disease: the disease of compartmentalization. We think *now* is one game, and *later* is another. We try to run two ledgers — one for this life ("how am I doing socially, financially, romantically?") and one for the next ("am I praying enough?"). The ayah refuses that split. There is one ledger. One praise-economy. One ongoing relationship.

### The asymmetry — what Allah did *not* say

Look at the structure carefully. The ayah says *lahu al-ḥamdu fī al-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah* — His praise is in *both* the first and the last. But then it says *wa lahu al-ḥukmu* — and to Him belongs the *judgment / decision* — **without locating it.**

He did not say "His judgment is in the first and the last." Just: His judgment.

Al-Tabari reads *al-ḥukm* here as *al-qaḍāʾ bayna khalqihi* — the decree between His creation. The judgment is not an abstract cosmic force; it is a settlement between persons, a reckoning of every claim and counter-claim, every reallocation of credit that was ever made across the first abode. Qarun will say, six verses from now: *this wealth is mine.* The *ḥukm* is the moment that claim is finally adjudicated — not across two stages, but in one decisive verdict.

Why, then, does Allah locate *praise* across the two lives but not *judgment*? Because praise is something that **manifests across time** — sometimes loudly, sometimes silently. In *al-ūlā*, the praise of Allah is partial, contested, claimed by others. Qarun will hear it being recited around him and re-route it. So Allah locates the praise across time: *don't worry — it belongs to Me here too, not just there.*

But *ḥukm* — the decision, the final word — Allah does not need to locate it, because *ḥukm* in its absolute form has only one address. The judgment isn't divided across two stages. It's one verdict that lands.

[PAUSE]

Notice how this rewards careful reading. The grammar itself is teaching us the difference between **praise** (which is always being given, sometimes correctly, sometimes incorrectly) and **judgment** (which arrives, decisively, once). The first needs the geography of "first and last" because it is contested across that geography. The second does not.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: *ilayhi turjaʿūn* — "to Him you will be returned"

### Teaching the grammar before opening the ayah

In Arabic, the verb *rajaʿa* means "to return." But Allah does not use it in its active form here. He uses the *passive* — *turjaʿūn* — "you **will be returned**."

This is the heart of the matter. In English, "return" is something you can do yourself. *I returned home. I returned the book.* You are the actor.

But *turjaʿūn* is passive. **You will be returned.** Someone else is doing the returning. You are the object, not the subject.

A simple example. If I say "the package arrived," you picture a package that traveled on its own. If I say "the package was delivered," your mind immediately asks: *by whom?* The passive demands an agent. And in this ayah, the agent is implied with overwhelming force — *to Him* you will be returned. He is the destination *and* the one doing the returning.

### Opening the word

**وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ**

*And to Him you will be returned.*

[PAUSE]

This is one of those small grammatical choices that, once you see it, you cannot un-see. Allah does not say *tarjiʿūn* — "you will return" (active). He says *turjaʿūn* — "you will be returned" (passive).

Here's why this is shattering. We are used to thinking of death and the afterlife as something *we* do. *I* will face Allah. *I* will be raised. *I* will give my account. The ayah says: no. The verb is being done *to* you. You are not the active traveler in this final journey. You are the one being brought.

Think of a child being carried, asleep, from the back seat of a car into the house. The child does not *come* into the house. The child *is brought* into the house. The child is too small, too tired, too unconscious of the geography to do it themselves.

That is us. We will not stride confidently into the next abode. We will be carried. Brought. Returned.

### Why "returned" and not "brought"?

And notice — Allah did not say "you will be *brought*" (which would be *tujlabūn* or *tuʾtawn*). He said you will be **returned** — *turjaʿūn*. From the root *r-j-ʿ*, which lexically means *to come back to a place one was before.*

You are not being introduced to Allah for the first time. You are being **brought back**. There is a covenant in the background of this verb — the moment in 7:172 when Allah took every soul before its body and asked *alastu bi-rabbikum?* — "Am I not your Lord?" And every soul answered *balā* — "yes."

The passive verb plus the root of "returning" creates a single, dense theological statement: you came from Him; you forgot; He is bringing you back. You will not arrive somewhere new. You will arrive somewhere your soul has been before.

The classical commentators are precise about why this return matters. Al-Tabari reads *turjaʿūn* as being returned *min baʿdi mamātikum* — after your deaths — for a specific purpose: *fa-yaqḍī baynakum bi-l-ḥaqq*, "and He will judge between you with truth." Al-Muyassar pairs the return with *al-ḥisāb wa-l-jazāʾ* — the accounting and the recompense. The return is not to a holding room. It is to a court where the books have been kept, where every re-routing of praise has been recorded, where the difference between what a person claimed and what was true will be settled — *bi-l-ḥaqq*, in truth, without remainder.

[PAUSE]

This is the answer to what Qarun is about to do. Qarun will build a tower of self-authored identity. *I have knowledge. I built this. I earned this.* The ayah strips that to the studs. You are not the author of your beginning — you were created. You are not the author of your end — you will be returned. The middle, in which you imagined yourself the protagonist, is the only sliver in which your illusion of authorship even seemed plausible. And even that sliver is rented from Him.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Bridge

We've opened three doors:

- *Lahu al-ḥamd* — praise belongs exclusively, entirely, to Him.
- *Fī al-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah* — across both installments of one continuous reality.
- *Ilayhi turjaʿūn* — and the destination is not somewhere new; it is the place you came from.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

## Theme One: The Quiet Disease of Re-Routing Praise

Let's start somewhere very human.

You finish a project at work. It was hard. You stayed late. You solved the problem no one else could. And then someone on your team — someone who barely showed up — takes credit for it in the meeting.

How does that feel?

For most of us, it feels like a kind of theft. We didn't just want the praise — we wanted the *true address of the praise to be honored*. The wrongness isn't that praise was given. The wrongness is that it was *delivered to the wrong mailbox*.

Hold onto that feeling. Because that is exactly the disease Allah is naming with this ayah: the re-routing of praise.

We notice it instantly when someone else does it. When the colleague takes our credit. When the leader claims the team's work. When the student plagiarizes. Our instinct is reliable: *praise was misdirected.* The credit landed somewhere it didn't belong.

But Allah is pointing at a subtler, far more universal version of this. Not the credit-stealing of others — the credit-claiming of the self.

[PAUSE]

The Quran's most vivid case study of this disease arrives just six verses after our ayah. Qarun. A man from among Musa's own people, elevated with such wealth that his storehouses required teams of strong men just to carry the keys. His people came to him and said: *seek the abode of the hereafter through what Allah has given you. Do not forget your portion of this world. Do good as Allah has done good to you.* They were telling him: this belongs to Allah, not to you. Use it the way someone uses a trust, not the way an owner uses property.

And Qarun answered with eight words that became one of the Quran's most terrible sentences:

> *"I was only given this because of knowledge I have."* (28:78)

*Because of knowledge I have.* Not "because of what Allah gave me." Not "because of Providence." Because of *me*.

He took praise that was passing through him on its way to its real address — and he held it. He closed his fist around it. He built an identity out of it: *I am the kind of person who earns this. I am the explanation for my own success.*

The earth opened.

[PAUSE]

But here is what the ayah will not let us do: walk away thinking Qarun was simply an unusual monster and we are nothing like him. The disease he exemplified to its terminal degree operates at low levels in virtually every human heart. The artist who begins to believe the gift is *from* them and not *through* them. The parent who accepts the gratitude of their children as if they personally authored the love they feel. The scholar who mistakes the light of knowledge for a light he generated himself, rather than one he was shown.

None of us would use Qarun's exact words. But the underlying claim is made quietly, constantly, in how we speak, how we take credit, how we explain our own good fortune.

Allah's declaration — *lahu al-ḥamd fī al-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah* — is the standing correction. Every instance of praise that has ever existed has a true address. You can hold it, briefly, on its way through — like a relay runner holds a baton. But you are not its origin and you are not its destination. You are the carrier.

## Theme Two: One Book, Not Two

Here is a question that the ayah's grammar will not let us avoid.

Why does Allah locate praise across *both* the first abode and the last — *fī al-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah* — as if it needed locating? Doesn't He already own everything? What would it mean for His praise to be *contested*?

It would mean exactly what we see around us.

In *al-ūlā*, the first abode, the praise is partial and embattled. Some people direct it correctly — they say *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, they acknowledge the source. Others redirect it — to their own cleverness, their own effort, their own deservingness, their own nation, their own ideology. Qarun redirected it to his knowledge. Fir'awn redirected it to his claimed divinity. Every human system that treats itself as the explanation for its own goodness is redirecting praise.

So when Allah says *His* praise belongs to Him *in the first abode too* — not just in the clear light of the resurrection, where every account will be obvious — He is making a claim that cuts against the ambient confusion of this life. *Even here. Even in the fog of this world where the accounts aren't settled yet. Even here, it was Mine.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold the word *al-ūlā* next to *al-ākhirah* one more time. Not *al-dunyā* and *al-ākhirah* — the near and the far, the low and the high. But *the first* and *the last* — two chapters of one thing. Two rooms in one house.

This life is not practice for the real one. It is chapter one of a book whose final chapter you haven't read. The character you're building now — the habits of praise, the habits of attribution, the habits of gratitude or ingratitude — those are the character of the person who arrives in chapter two.

The person who, in this life, learned to say *lahu al-ḥamd* — to Him, not to me, not to chance, not to my effort alone — that person arrives at the resurrection speaking a language they already know. The character they have been practicing is the one they will be.

The person who, in this life, learned to close their fist around praise that was just passing through — they, too, will arrive having practiced for what comes next. Al-Muyassar renders the purpose of the return with quiet precision: *al-ḥisāb wa-l-jazāʾ* — the accounting and the recompense. Not a fresh start. An accounting of everything that happened in chapter one, before chapter two can begin.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Three: You Are Not the Traveler

The final word of the ayah is the most intimate.

*Turjaʿūn.* You will be returned.

Not: you will travel. Not: you will arrive. Not: you will come. *You will be returned.* Passive. Done to you. By Him.

We live in a culture that prizes agency above almost everything else. Take control. Be the author of your own story. Your life is what you make it. Self-determination is the highest virtue. And within the narrow province where this advice is true — work, habit, character, effort — it is valid and it matters.

But the ayah is describing a different province. The province of the ultimate. And there, the grammar switches. The active verb — *you go* — becomes passive: *you are brought.*

[PAUSE]

Think about what it means to be returned to a place rather than arriving at one for the first time. When you arrive somewhere for the first time, you are a stranger. You don't know the protocols. You don't know the language. You have to learn everything. The place is alien and you are foreign to it.

But when you are *returned* — the word carries memory. Something in the root *r-j-ʿ* implies: *this is where you were before.* You came from here. The place knows you, because you were made there.

The covenant of 7:172 hums in the background of this single verb. Allah gathered every soul before it was placed in a body and asked: *alastu bi-rabbikum?* Am I not your Lord? And every soul — yours, mine, Qarun's — answered *balā*. Yes.

The soul that arrives at the resurrection is not arriving at a foreign court. It is arriving at a conversation it once had, in a place it once knew, before the forgetting of embodied life. The return is a remembering.

[PAUSE]

This changes the entire emotional register of the final journey. We often speak of death as the threshold into the unknown. And in one sense it is — we cannot know the details of what awaits. But the destination is not a stranger. The destination is the One who asked your soul *alastu bi-rabbikum* and heard it say yes. You are being returned to a relationship, not introduced to one.

And notice: Allah does not say the *righteous* will be returned. He says *turjaʿūn* — second person plural, addressed to everyone within earshot of the ayah. All of you will be returned. Not only those who kept their praise correctly directed. All.

This is not a comfort meant to eliminate accountability; the *ḥukm* — al-Tabari's *al-qaḍāʾ bayna khalqihi*, the decree between His creation — still awaits, settling every claim in truth. But it is a statement about the structure of the universe. Everyone ends up back at the origin. The only question is what you did with the praise while you were the carrier.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## The Question the Ayah Leaves You With

We have traveled through this short ayah — eighteen Arabic words — and found an entire architecture.

*Lahu al-ḥamd*: to Him belongs the entire category of praise, exclusively, without remainder. Not some of it. Not the part directed at Him consciously. All of it — al-thanāʾ al-jamīl, the beautiful praise, the recognition of intrinsic excellence — wherever it arises, whatever it lands on — in its deep reality, it belongs to Him.

*Fī al-ūlā wa-l-ākhirah*: across both chapters of the one continuous story of existence. In the contested fog of this life, where accounts aren't settled yet, and in the clear light of the next, where they will be. Same praise. Same Owner. Same truth — whether acknowledged or not.

*Wa lahu al-ḥukm*: and to Him belongs the judgment. Not as an afterthought. The decree between His creation — between every claim and counter-claim made in the first abode — is His. Every Qarun who ever said *"I earned this"* and every person who ever had something taken from them will stand inside this verdict.

*Wa ilayhi turjaʿūn*: and to Him you will be returned. Passive. Inevitable. Not a threat — a description of the shape of reality. You came from Him. You are being held, briefly, in the first abode. And you will be brought home.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question the surah was building toward when it placed this ayah before the Qarun story.

Qarun will hear these truths and choose not to see them. He will look at his keys, his storehouses, his social standing, and say: *this is mine, and I made it.* He is the test case. He is what it looks like to live inside this ayah's claims and refuse them.

And the surah is saying to you, six verses before you meet him: *before you watch what Qarun did with these truths — what will you do with them?*

Not what will you say. Not what theological positions will you adopt. What will you *do* with them. When the praise comes to you — as it will, in some form, in some measure — will you hold it or let it pass through? When you explain your own success, which vocabulary will you use? When you stand at the rim of someone else's collapse and feel the relief of being spared — will you say, as Qarun's onlookers said, *"were it not for Allah's favor"*? Or will you quietly take a little credit for your own survival?

[PAUSE]

The ayah is not asking you to be self-effacing in a performative way. It is not asking you to deny effort or competence or the real work you put in. It is asking you to keep the mail routing correct. To let the praise pass through you to its true address. To hold it only as a carrier, never as a destination.

*To Him belongs all praise — in the first life and in the last.*

That sentence is waiting for you at both ends.

You will hear it in the maternity ward and at the deathbed. You will hear it at the beginning of every gift and at the accounting of every claim. You will hear it, eventually, when the earth settles over everything you thought you owned — and in the moment your soul, passive and brought, is returned to the One it once knew.

It is not a threat.

It is a description of the shape of everything.

And it is the most liberating thing you could possibly know.

---

*End of reflection — Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 70*
