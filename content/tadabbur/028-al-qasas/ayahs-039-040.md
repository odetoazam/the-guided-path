# The Arrogance That Forgot It Would Be Returned

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayahs 39–40

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To hear these two ayahs the way the first listeners heard them, you have to step out of our world — where Pharaohs are mummies in museums and the Exodus is a children's story — and back into a world where Pharaoh was the *richest, most stable, most ancient, most architecturally permanent* civilization any of those listeners had ever heard of.

The Arabs of Makkah did not know much about much. But they knew about Egypt. The caravan routes ran north. The stories ran south. Pharaoh, in the cultural imagination of seventh-century Arabia, was not a villain. He was a *system*. A system so vast, so old, so beyond questioning, that the question wasn't *whether* Pharaoh ruled — the question was *who is Pharaoh this generation*.

And in Makkah, those first listeners were — what?

A handful. Mocked. Tortured in the sun on hot stones. Watching their leader Muhammad ﷺ — the most truthful man among them — being called a liar by men who controlled the trade, the politics, the religion, the wells. Men who were, in their own small way, miniature Pharaohs.

Surah Al-Qasas is descending into that world. And it is doing something very specific: it is telling the story of Musa — not as a children's tale, not as ancient history — but as a *map of how power actually falls*.

The whole surah is built around displacement and return. A baby is displaced to a river. A man is displaced from his city. A people are displaced from their homeland. And then — at the climax — a tyrant is displaced from his throne, his army, his palace, his certainty.

Right before our two ayahs, Pharaoh has just made the most extreme claim a human being can make. He has stood before his people and said:

> *"I do not know of any god for you other than me."*

Then he has ordered Haman to build a tower so he can "climb up and look at the God of Musa."

That is the setup. The text has shown you a man at the top of the ladder of arrogance — staring up, climbing higher, telling everyone he has reached the top.

And now, in just twenty-eight words, Allah is going to take him down.

### The Hook

There is a moment in these two ayahs where Allah does not describe Pharaoh's death.

He describes the *handling* of his body.

And the verb He chooses — one single Arabic verb — is the same verb you would use for tossing out trash. Stay with that. We are going to ask why the King of Egypt, the man who claimed to be God, gets a verb that is normally reserved for what you throw away.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Istakbara* — The Verb That Swells

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work.

Arabic has a system called *awzān* — patterns. The same three-letter root can be poured into different molds, and each mold *does something* to the meaning. Imagine the root letters as water and the patterns as differently-shaped vessels. The water is the same; the *shape* tells you what's happening.

The root here is **k-b-r** (ك-ب-ر) — the root of "big." From it you get *kabīr* (great), *Allāhu akbar* (Allah is greater), *takbīr* (declaring greatness).

But the verb Allah uses is not the simple form *kabura* (he was big). It is the **tenth form**: *istakbara* (اسْتَكْبَرَ).

The tenth form in Arabic has a very specific flavor. It often means *to seek for oneself* or *to claim for oneself* what the root denotes. *Istaghfara* — to seek forgiveness. *Istaʿāna* — to seek help. *Istakbara* — to seek bigness for oneself. To *claim* greatness. To *take* greatness as if it were yours to take.

Notice what this does. Allah is not saying Pharaoh *was* great. He is saying Pharaoh *grabbed at* greatness. He reached for a size that was not his — and tried to wear it.

Listen to something else. The verb is just one word — *wastakbara* — but it carries a sonic swelling. The mouth has to *expand* to say it. *Is — tak — ba — ra*. Four syllables that grow louder. The word itself puffs up.

Then Allah does something remarkable with the grammar.

*Wastakbara huwa wa junūduhu.*

In Arabic, the verb usually carries the subject inside it. "He became arrogant" is one word: *istakbara*. The "he" is built in.

But Allah does not stop at the built-in *he*. He adds an *external* pronoun — *huwa* — "he." Then He adds *and his soldiers.*

*Wastakbara — huwa — wa junūduhu.*

Why repeat the *he*?

Because in Arabic, when you add the separate pronoun on top of the verb that already contains it, you are doing something called *taʾkīd* — emphasis. You are putting your finger on the chest. **He** became arrogant. *Him specifically. As a person. As a self.*

And then *and his soldiers.* Because arrogance, when it sits on a throne, is *contagious*. It does not stay in one heart. It seeps down into every person who carries a spear in your name. Every soldier who beats a slave because *you* told him to feel big. Every administrator who signed an order because *you* said the order was right.

Allah is showing us a sociology of arrogance. It is never just one man. A tyrant's pride colonizes every person who serves him.

Stay with this. **You have soldiers too.**

Not literal ones. But anyone who serves your ego is your soldier. The friend who validates every bad decision. The follower who never pushes back. The child you raised to never question you. The colleague who laughs at your unkind jokes.

A small Pharaoh in any of us recruits *junūd.* And when Allah tallies the arrogance, He does not count only the head. He counts the army.

Then comes the qualifier — and this is the part of the ayah that should make us tremble.

*Bighayri al-ḥaqq.*

"Without right."

[PAUSE]

Stop and read that again. *They became arrogant — without right.*

What is Allah implying by saying *without right*?

Some scholars — and this is one of the most uncomfortable observations in the classical tafsir — read this as a quiet admission that *arrogance with right* is at least linguistically conceivable. That if any being could claim absolute greatness, it would be a being to whom greatness actually belonged.

That being is not Pharaoh. That being is Allah.

In the hadith qudsi, Allah says: *Pride is My cloak and greatness is My garment.* It is *His* size. He is the only One who can wear that cloak without it being a costume.

So when Pharaoh swells himself up — *istakbara* — he is not just being unpleasant. He is *putting on a robe that does not belong to him.* He is wearing Allah's clothing in public.

And the ayah's verdict is sharp: *bighayri al-ḥaqq.* You took a robe that wasn't yours.

What this invites us to consider — and I'll signal that this is an interpretive bridge, not just grammar — is that every act of *istikbar* in our own lives is a costume change. Every time we expand ourselves over another person, we are taking down a garment from a shelf that has Allah's name on it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Ẓannū* — The Presumption That Built the Throne

The second ayah of the pair gives us the *engine* of the arrogance.

*Wa ẓannū annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn.*

"And they presumed that to Us they would not be returned."

The verb is *ẓannū* — from the root **ẓ-n-n**, which in Arabic has a fascinating dual quality. *Ẓann* can mean a strong presumption that approaches certainty, or it can mean a doubtful conjecture. Context decides which.

Here, the context is clear: this is the *ẓann* of someone who has built their entire life on an assumption — and never tested it. Not knowledge. Not certainty earned through investigation. A *working assumption* so old, so comfortable, that it has hardened into something they treat like fact.

What was the assumption?

*Annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn.*

"That to Us they would not be returned."

I want you to notice three things about the way this phrase is constructed, because each one is doing work.

**First — the word order.** In normal Arabic, you would expect: *they would not be returned to Us.* Verb, then prepositional phrase. But Allah puts *ilaynā* — "to Us" — *first.* He fronts it.

In Arabic rhetoric, fronting a phrase puts a spotlight on it. The emphasis is not "they would not be returned" — the emphasis is **"to *Us* — they would not be returned."**

Pharaoh's denial was not abstract. It was personal. The thing he could not bring himself to imagine was specifically *being returned to Allah.* Standing in front of Him. Having to answer.

**Second — the passive voice.** *Lā yurjaʿūn.* "They would not be returned." Not "they would not return." *Returned* — by someone else.

Arabic could have used the active form. It didn't. It used the passive — because the act of being summoned back to Allah is something that *happens to you.* You don't choose it. You don't initiate it. Someone *brings you back.*

And Pharaoh's *ẓann* — his deep working assumption — was that *no one would ever bring him back.* He thought he was free. Free of accountability. Free of an audience he had not chosen. He thought his career would simply *end* — with the throne, the palace, the army, the river, the sun.

He did not think there was a hand that could reach across death and *pull him back.*

**Third — and this is the most haunting part.** The verb *yurjaʿūn* is in the present-future tense. Allah is not saying "they thought they had not been returned." He is saying "they thought they *will not* be returned."

This is the tense of a *future they had cancelled.* They had taken the day of return off their calendar. They had crossed it out. They lived as if that page didn't exist.

And here is the *tadabbur* point. Pause for it.

[PAUSE]

Every act of arrogance — every small Pharaoh moment in any of us — is built on this same hidden *ẓann.* On the quiet, never-spoken assumption that *I will not be returned and asked about this.*

The man who screams at his wife is operating on the *ẓann* that no one will replay that scene for him. The boss who humiliates the intern is operating on the *ẓann* that the intern's tears will not be evidence one day. The teenager who mocks the poor kid is operating on the *ẓann* that the mockery is *just gone* — vanished into air.

*Wa ẓannū annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn.*

The Quran is naming the engine. *This* is what runs the human tendency to step on other humans. Not malice. Not even cruelty. **The quiet, comfortable, never-examined assumption that nothing will be replayed.**

Remove that assumption — really remove it — and arrogance has no fuel.

### Element Three: *Nabadhnāhum* — The Verb Reserved for Refuse

Now we arrive at the verb I teased at the beginning.

*Fa-akhadhnāhu wa junūdahu fa-nabadhnāhum fī al-yamm.*

"So We seized him and his soldiers, and We flung them into the sea."

Read it slowly. The structure is a chain — *fa, fa, fa* — *so, then, then.* Three quick consecutive actions. Allah is showing you how fast the collapse is.

But focus on the second verb. *Nabadhnāhum.*

The root is **n-b-dh** (ن-ب-ذ). In classical Arabic lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib — this root carries a very specific meaning. *Nabdh* is not generic "throwing." It is *the throwing away of something you have no further use for.*

You *nabadh* the date pit after you've eaten the date. You *nabadh* a worn-out sandal. You *nabadh* the bone after the meat is gone.

It is the verb of *discarding.*

Think about what Allah is doing here.

Pharaoh's body — and we know from elsewhere in the Quran that the body itself was preserved as a sign — Pharaoh's body, the body of the man who built temples to house his corpse forever, the body of the man whose entire civilization was organized around the permanence of *his* death — that body, in the Quran's verdict, is *nabdh.* Refuse. Castoff.

The whole apparatus of Egyptian mummification was a refusal of *nabdh.* They built a society dedicated to making sure Pharaoh's body was *preserved, honored, eternal.* And the Quran's word for what actually happened to him is the word for *taking out the trash.*

This is *istidlal al-maʿqūl bi-l-maḥsūs.* Allah is taking a physical image — a body floating in the sea — and using it to point at an unseen reality. The unseen reality is this: *the bigness Pharaoh constructed was never bigness.* It was an inflated sack. And when Allah punctured it, all that came out was the air. The body left behind — the heaviest, most monumentalized body in human history — was, in the divine accounting, *something to throw out.*

Now look at *where* he was thrown.

*Fī al-yamm.*

Most translations say "the sea." But the Arabic word *al-yamm* is interesting. The classical lexicons note that *al-yamm* is a large body of water — sometimes a sea, sometimes a great river. And in the Quran, *al-yamm* is the same word used in this very surah, just earlier, for the river that *carried Musa's basket.*

[PAUSE]

Did you hear that?

The same word.

The *yamm* that, at the beginning of the surah, *carried the baby Musa to safety* is the *yamm* that, at the climax, *carries Pharaoh's discarded body away.*

The same water.

The same word.

One Arabic noun does the entire architectural work of the surah: the water that saves the prophet is the water that disposes of the tyrant.

Surah Al-Qasas is built on this. The opening: a mother *throws* her baby into the *yamm* in trust. The verb used in 28:7 is *fa-alqīhi fī al-yamm* — "cast him into the yamm." The ending: Allah *throws* a tyrant into the *yamm* in judgment. The same elemental motion — *casting into water* — appears at the beginning and the end of the same surah. But one casting is mercy. The other is *nabdh.*

This is not coincidence. This is composition.

The same *yamm* that Pharaoh thought he commanded — the water that ran through Egypt, the Nile, the sea around his kingdom — was the water that, when Allah commanded it, carried away the prophet Pharaoh was hunting *and* later carried away Pharaoh himself.

The element you think you own is on loan.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

We have walked through three things.

We watched a man inflate himself into a costume that was not his — *istakbara.*

We watched the engine inside that inflation — the never-tested assumption that he would not be returned — *ẓannū annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn.*

We watched the verb of his disposal — *nabadhnāhum* — refuse.

These three are not separate observations. They are the same insight from three angles. Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Assumption Underneath All Cruelty

Here is something psychologists have noticed in the last fifty years, but the Quran is naming in twenty-eight words.

When researchers study why ordinary people commit cruel acts, they keep arriving at the same finding: it is almost never about *believing the cruelty is good.* It is about *believing there will be no replay.*

The Stanford Prison Experiment, Milgram's obedience studies, the studies on dehumanization in war — what they keep finding is that cruel acts proliferate in environments where the actor *implicitly assumes no one is watching, no one will remember, no one will hold them to account.*

This is the modern psychological frame. And the Quran has handed us the deeper version of it.

Because secular psychology can only address the *human* witness. They can install body cameras. They can put HR in the room. They can record the meeting. But all of these are only the visible audience.

The Quran's claim is harder and gentler at the same time. The Quran's claim is: *there is always a witness, and there will always be a replay.*

*Wa ẓannū annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn.*

The presumption is the problem.

Now bring this home. Forget Pharaoh. Think about your last week.

Was there a moment when you spoke to someone in a tone you would not have used if a camera was on? A moment when you cut someone off, dismissed them, used your *position* — as parent, as boss, as older sibling, as senior in some hierarchy — to make them small?

If you can find that moment, ask yourself, very honestly: *what did I assume in that moment?*

You did not assume cruelty was good. You did not even *think.* You operated on an autopilot whose underlying instruction was: *this will not be replayed for me.*

The Quran is offering us a kind of cognitive intervention. It is saying: change the underlying instruction. Walk through your day with the assumption that **everything will be returned to Him.** Every tone, every glance, every text, every silence when you should have spoken.

Do not catastrophize this. The point is not to make you scared. The point is to make you *present.*

A person who lives with *raj'a* awake — the awareness of return — is not a paranoid person. They are a *responsive* person. They are the person who, in the middle of a sentence, hears themselves and adjusts. They are the person who texts the apology before the sun goes down. They are the person who, when they have power over another, becomes gentler instead of bigger.

This is what dissolves the small Pharaoh inside us. Not willpower. Not therapy. Not even kindness as a value. The slow internalization of one sentence:

*Ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn — annahum yurjaʿūn.*

*They thought they would not be returned. They will be.*

[PAUSE]

**The practical application:** Pick one relationship this week where you have *positional power* — over an employee, a child, a younger sibling, a service worker, a customer service rep on the phone. Before that next interaction, say to yourself, silently: *this conversation will be played back to me.* Watch what happens to your voice.

### Theme Two: The Yamm Is Always On Loan

Here is the second theme, and it is perhaps the most consoling thing in these two ayahs — though it might not sound consoling at first.

Pharaoh thought he owned the Nile.

I mean that literally. In the Egyptian religious system, Pharaoh was identified with Hapi, the god of the inundation. The river belonged to him. The water was, in some theological sense, *part of his body.* His coronation involved rituals tied to the flood. His name appeared on the river. Egypt without the Nile was nothing — and Pharaoh *was* the Nile.

So when Allah dropped him into the *yamm*, He did not just kill him. He *demonstrated something to him.*

He demonstrated: *the water you thought was yours was always Mine.*

Now zoom out. Because every one of us has a yamm.

Your yamm is whatever element of life you have quietly come to assume *belongs to you.* It might be your health. It might be your intelligence. It might be your beauty, your career, your marriage, your bank account, your reputation, your ability to think clearly, your children's safety. Whatever it is — there is something in your life that you have stopped *receiving* and started *owning.*

You do not say it out loud. You would never say "this is mine." But your behavior, your anxiety, your gratitude levels — they reveal what you have begun to treat as native rather than gifted.

The terrifying mercy of these two ayahs is that Allah is showing you, in advance, what happens at the end of that road.

You can find out the hard way that the yamm was on loan — like Pharaoh.

Or you can find out the easy way — by saying it now. By saying it every morning. By unclenching your fingers around the thing you thought was yours, before the wave comes to take it.

This is the secret architecture of Surah Al-Qasas. The surah opens with a mother who *let go* of her baby into the yamm — and got him back. The surah closes with a man who *clenched* against the yamm — and was disposed by it.

The difference between the two is not religion or ritual. It is *grip.*

The mother of Musa held the baby in an open hand. Pharaoh held Egypt in a closed fist.

And the yamm — the same yamm — responded to those two grips in opposite ways.

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest invitation in this passage. Not to be afraid of the yamm. To *relax your grip on it.*

In a society that teaches you to *get* and *hold* and *keep* and *expand* — Allah is showing you that the people who *hold* are the people who get *thrown*, and the people who *let go* are the people who get *carried.*

**The practical application:** Identify one thing in your life that you have been gripping tightly — something you secretly believe is *yours.* Tonight, before you sleep, name it out loud to Allah. Say: *Ya Allah, I have been treating this as mine. It is Yours. I am only borrowing it. Let me hold it with an open hand.* Do this for thirty days and see what happens to your anxiety about it.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Look at what we have seen.

These two ayahs are a single sentence in the architecture of the surah. The surah opens with a *casting into the yamm* — that is mercy. The surah closes with a *casting into the yamm* — that is judgment.

The same verb of casting. The same body of water. Two opposite outcomes.

The hinge between them is not the water. The hinge is the *grip.*

And inside the two ayahs themselves, the hinge of the indictment is one phrase: *ẓannū annahum ilaynā lā yurjaʿūn.* "They presumed they would not be returned." Take away that presumption — that one quiet, comfortable, never-examined assumption — and the whole arrogance collapses on its own.

So the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions:

- *Istakbara* names what arrogance *looks like* — a costume of bigness.
- *Ẓannū lā yurjaʿūn* names what arrogance *runs on* — the cancellation of return.
- *Nabadhnāhum fī al-yamm* names what arrogance *ends as* — refuse in the same water that once carried mercy.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *yamm* in my life right now — the element I have stopped receiving and started owning?

2. Who are my *junūd*? Whose presence reinforces my smallness of self by validating my bigness of ego?

3. If a recording of my last twenty-four hours were played back to me — every tone, every text, every silence — what would I want to edit?

4. Where in my life have I quietly cancelled the day of return — assumed that something will not be replayed for me?

5. Am I holding the people and gifts in my life with an open hand, or a closed fist?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The end of every arrogance is the moment Allah shows the arrogant that the size they wore was a costume, the soldiers they commanded were borrowed, and the water they thought was theirs was always His.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not be Pharaoh. You will not declare yourself a god. You will not order a tower to be built to climb to the sky.

But the small Pharaoh inside any human being — the one that swells in traffic, in arguments, in moments of unchecked power over someone weaker — that Pharaoh is here. And he runs on the same fuel as the original. He runs on the *ẓann* that nothing will be replayed.

The invitation of this passage is not to be afraid. The invitation is to *remember.* To walk through your week with the screen of *raj'a* — return — switched back on in the back of your mind. Not as a threat. As a *companion.*

You will find, slowly, that the man who *swelled* — *wastakbara* — begins to deflate. Not into nothing. Into the right size. The size Allah made you. Which is, it turns out, exactly the size that fits in His hand without being thrown.

### Du'a

Ya Allah, You are the One who clothes Yourself in greatness — and there is no greatness but Yours. Strip from us the costume of bigness we have stolen from Your wardrobe.

Awaken in us the certainty of return — so that every word we speak today is spoken to an audience that includes You. And let the yamm of our lives — every gift, every breath, every relationship — be held in our hands the way the mother of Musa held her baby: open, trusting, letting go.

Do not let us be among those who are *thrown.* Let us be among those who are *carried.*

Āmīn.
