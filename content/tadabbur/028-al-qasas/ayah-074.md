# The Day He Will Call Them: When the Question Repeats

## Introduction

Listen to these words first, slowly:

**وَيَوْمَ يُنَادِيهِمْ فَيَقُولُ أَيْنَ شُرَكَائِيَ الَّذِينَ كُنتُمْ تَزْعُمُونَ**

*Wa yawma yunādīhim fa-yaqūlu ayna shurakā'iya alladhīna kuntum tazʿumūn.*

*"And on the Day He will call to them and say: Where are My so-called partners — the ones you used to claim?"*

Nine words. One question. And on the Day this question is asked, there will be no escape from it — because the One asking is the One who already knows.

But before we step into this ayah, I want you to feel where it sits. We are deep in Surah Al-Qasas. The surah began with a Pharaoh who thought he owned Egypt — his rivers, his armies, his sky-touching cities. The surah moved through Musa's exile, his return, his confrontation, his vindication. And then it pivoted to Qarun — the man from Musa's own people who believed his wealth was his own production. Qarun, who said: *"I was only given this because of knowledge I possess."* The earth swallowed him.

And now, near the end of the surah, Allah is no longer telling stories. He is showing us a courtroom. He is showing us a Day. And He is asking a question.

Here is what I want you to notice before we even open the ayah:

**Allah asked this exact question twelve verses earlier.**

In ayah 62 of this same surah, He said: *"And on the Day He will call them and say: Where are My partners whom you used to claim?"* Then in ayah 65, He asks them another question: *"What answer did you give the messengers?"* And now in ayah 74 — He asks the first question **again**. The same words. The same question. [PAUSE]

Why? Why would the Most Eloquent repeat Himself in the space of twelve verses?

Stay with that question. Because the answer to it — the *reason* this question is asked twice — is the entire ayah. And once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word *Yunādīhim* — A Public Calling of Rebuke

Let's begin with the verb. Allah did not say *yaqūlu lahum* — "He will say to them." He said **yunādīhim** — "He will call out to them."

The classical commentators are precise about what kind of call this is. Ibn Kathir describes it as a call "by way of rebuke" — **tawbīkh** — for those who worshipped other gods besides Allah. Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar confirm: Allah is calling the mushrikeen and asking them after the partners they claimed *fī al-dunyā* — in this world. This is not a private inquiry. This is a **public summoning**. Ibn Kathir adds a detail that sharpens everything: this call comes *before all the witnesses* — the gathered nations, the communities of every age, the messengers who were sent to each of them. On that Day, Allah calls these people out not in a side room but at the center of the assembly of all creation.

In Arabic, *nādā* carries this quality — it is the word of calling into open space, the word a herald uses before a crowd. And here it is the word Allah uses to address those who gave His throne to others. The *nidaa* is the rebuke made manifest before witnesses.

Now here is something to hold — not as a grammatical derivation, but as a matter for the heart's contemplation. The Quran uses *nādā* elsewhere for intimate, close encounters: the call to Musa at the tree in this very surah (28:30), the cries of the prophets lifting their voices to their Lord. *Nādā* is not exclusively the verb of distance. What defines this *nidaa* is what the classical sources describe: its character as **tawbīkh**, and its audience — all witnesses.

And in contemplation, consider what that public exposure means. In this life, Allah is closer to us than our jugular vein. He says: *"And when My servants ask you concerning Me — indeed I am near."* Near. *Qarīb.* So near that a whisper reaches Him. But these people had arranged their days around other centers — an idol, a reputation, a habitual reaching toward something beside Him. What was arranged in private is now, on this Day, named before all of creation. The *nidaa* does not create distance. It **reveals** what the life had already arranged, in the presence of every witness.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. *Yunādīhim* is in the imperfect tense — *al-fiʿl al-muḍāriʿ* — which in Arabic conveys ongoing, vivid, present action. Allah did not say "He called them" in the past tense — He said **He will call them**, but in a form that makes it feel like it is happening now. The grammar collapses the future into a permanent present. This Day is not far. It is closer than you think. It is, in a sense, already pressing on every moment — and only the body's continued breathing keeps us from hearing the call.

Here is what this word reveals: **the *nidaa* of that Day is a public rebuke before all witnesses — a naming, before all of creation, of what was privately arranged. On that Day, every claim made about who shares Allah's throne is called out at the center of the assembly.**

### Element Two: The Word *Shurakā'ī* — Possessive Pronoun on a Lie

Now look at the next word. **Shurakā'iya** — "My partners."

Stop here. Read it again. *My* partners.

Allah is asking them about *His* partners. The possessive pronoun *-ī* at the end means *mine.* He is calling these false gods His own partners.

But Allah has no partners. So what is this grammar doing?

In classical Arabic balāghah, this is a rhetorical device the scholars called **al-tahakkum** — sacred irony. Allah is repeating back to them, in His own voice, the lie *they* told about Him. It is as if a king were to say to a traitor: *"Where is my co-ruler — the one you appointed for me?"* The king has no co-ruler. The traitor invented one. And by saying "my co-ruler," the king is not affirming the partnership — he is forcing the traitor to hear his own fabrication coming back at him in the voice of the One he wronged.

This is a courtroom technique. The accusation is built into the question.

But there is something even more devastating beneath this grammar. Notice: Allah does not say *al-āliha* (the gods) or *al-aṣnām* (the idols). He says **shurakā'ī** — partners. The word *sharīk* in Arabic does not mean an alternative. A *sharīk* is someone who **shares**. A business partner shares the books. A marital partner shares the home. A *sharīk* in worship would have to be someone who shares the throne.

Do you see what this exposes? *Shirk* is not the worship of a different god. *Shirk* is the **insertion** of something else into the space that belongs to Allah alone. The mushrik does not deny Allah. The mushrik says: *Yes, Allah is real — but so is this other thing. So is my wealth. So is my reputation. So is my fear of what people will think.* The mushrik makes room.

And Allah's question on that Day is not *"Where are the gods you invented?"* — it is sharper than that. It is: *"Where is the one you let share with Me?"*

[PAUSE]

Notice one more thing — and this is subtle. The word *shurakā'ī* is plural. *Partners*, not *partner*. Because the heart that makes room for one rival never stops at one. The man who sells his integrity for status will also sell it for wealth. The woman who fears her father's disapproval will also fear her husband's, her child's, her neighbor's. *Shirk* multiplies. It cannot stay contained. And on that Day, every single one of them will be summoned by name: *Where are My partners — plural — the ones you kept inserting beside Me?*

This is what this word reveals: **the false god you served was never an alternative to Allah. It was an insertion next to Him. And on that Day, you will be asked to produce what you inserted — and produce it before its true Owner.**

### Element Three: *Kuntum Tazʿumūn* — The Verb of Empty Claim

Now the final phrase. **Alladhīna kuntum tazʿumūn** — "the ones you used to claim."

The verb here is *zaʿama.* And in Arabic, *zaʿama* is not a neutral word for "to say" or "to believe." *Zaʿama* is the verb of **empty claim** — assertion without evidence, conviction without grounding.

The classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in *Mufradāt* — note that *zaʿm* is most often used in Arabic for a claim that is either false, doubtful, or unverified. There is even an Arabic proverb: *"Za'amū" maṭiyyatu al-kadhib* — *"They claim" is the mount of lies.* When you want to introduce a statement you do not yourself stand behind, you put *zaʿama* in front of it.

So when Allah uses this verb in His question, He is not asking them about something they *believed*. He is asking them about something they **claimed without ground**.

Now here is where it gets uncomfortable.

The mushrik in this ayah is not someone who carefully studied alternative theologies and concluded that Allah has partners. The mushrik is someone who *claimed* — who asserted, who repeated what they were told, who built their life around something they never actually examined. Allah is exposing the **groundlessness** of their attachment.

Notice the construction: **kuntum tazʿumūn.** *Kuntum* is past tense, *tazʿumūn* is imperfect/ongoing. Together they form a continuous past: "you used to keep claiming." The Arabs called this construction the *kāna + muḍāriʿ* — and it means **habitual action**. They did not just claim once. They claimed and claimed and claimed. The claim was the rhythm of their life. It was their default.

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself this question — and let it cut: **what do I claim about reality without ever having examined the claim?**

The man who lives for his career claims, every time he wakes at 5am, that this work is what makes him valuable. He has not examined the claim. He just keeps making it.

The woman who lives in fear of her family's opinion claims, every time she silences herself, that her safety lies in their approval. She has not examined the claim. She just keeps making it.

The young person who lives for the next dopamine hit claims, with every scroll, that this stimulation is worth the time it consumes. They have not examined the claim. They just keep making it.

*Kuntum tazʿumūn.* You **used to keep claiming**.

And here is what is terrifying about this verb. *Zaʿm* is not active belief — it is something weaker. It is the kind of assertion you can disown the moment you are pressed on it. You can say "I never really believed it" and walk away. **Except on this Day.** On this Day, Allah holds you to the claim. He does not let you slip out of it by saying "I was just going along." He freezes the claim, names it, and asks you to produce its referent.

This is what this verb reveals: **the things you let share Allah's throne in your life were not even things you carefully believed in. They were claims you never examined. And on the Day of Standing, Allah will ask you to produce, before His face, the thing you spent your life serving without ever testing whether it was real.**

[PAUSE]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

So we have three words, and three revelations.

*Yunādīhim* — a public call of rebuke before all witnesses: what was privately arranged is named before all of creation on that Day.

*Shurakā'ī* — what you served was not an alternative to Allah; it was an insertion next to Him. And He is the One asking after it.

*Kuntum tazʿumūn* — what you inserted was not a thing you had carefully verified; it was a claim you kept making. And on that Day, the claim is forced to produce its evidence.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Repeated Question

Let me return to where we started. **Allah asks this question twice in this same surah.** Ayah 62. Ayah 74. Same question. Twelve verses apart.

In human life, when someone asks you a question and you do not answer, they ask it again. The repetition is a sign that the first answer did not land — or was not given.

Imagine a parent who comes home to find a window broken. They look at the child and say: *"Who did this?"* The child shrugs. The parent goes and inspects the damage, asks the other children, gathers evidence. Then they return to the first child and ask again, gently but with the weight of what they now know: *"Who did this?"*

The second question is not a repetition. It is a reckoning. The first question was an opening — *come clean.* The second question is the closing — *now there is nowhere to go.*

This is what Allah is doing in Surah Al-Qasas. The first question in ayah 62 is structured as an opening. The mushrikūn are given the question. Then in ayah 63, those who were followed begin to disown their followers. In ayah 64 they are told *"Call your partners"* — and the partners do not respond. In ayah 65 they are asked the second question: *"What answer did you give the messengers?"* And then in ayahs 66 through 73, Allah shows them His own signs — the day, the night, the mercy, the provision. He is **building the case**. He is laying down the evidence. He is showing them that everything they ever needed was already given.

And then — in ayah 74 — He asks the **first question again.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight of that? The first time, the question was a doorway. The second time, the doorway is closed behind them and the question becomes a verdict. Between the two askings, Allah has shown them everything. The sun. The night. The seas. The provision. And He has shown them His messengers were sent. And He has shown them their alleged partners cannot even speak.

So when the question comes the second time, it is not asking for information. It is asking for a confession that is no longer possible to give.

This is the structure of accountability in the Quran. **Allah never punishes the unprepared.** He warns. He sends. He provides signs. He gives time. He repeats. And only after the case is complete — only after the question has been asked, and the evidence laid out, and the question asked again — only then does the silence settle.

And the silence of that Day is the loudest thing in the ayah. Because notice: **Allah asks. The ayah ends. There is no answer.**

Allah's question goes out before all witnesses. *Yunādīhim.* He calls them. And the ayah closes. The shuraka' do not appear. They cannot appear. Because Allah Himself established — between the first question and the second — that when the mushrikeen were commanded to call their partners, the partners gave no response. The idol stood mute. The reputation could not speak. The career could not step forward and say: *"Yes, I was worth it. Here is the return on what you invested in me."*

[PAUSE]

There is only the silence. And in that silence, the whole life is visible.

Ibn Kathir draws our attention to something that sharpens the silence further. He notes that between these two questions, from every nation a witness is brought forward — and Mujahid, the great student of Ibn Abbas, explains that these witnesses are the **messengers**. Not neutral observers. The very people who were sent to each community with the word of Allah, who stood before them and said: *This is the truth. This is the path. Turn.* Those witnesses are present at the assembly. And the question is asked again.

Which means: the reckoning on that Day is conducted in the presence of everyone who tried to help. The mercy arrived before the verdict. The messenger stood before the mushrik before the mushrik ever stood before the question. On that Day, when Allah asks *"Where are My partners?"* — the messenger who came to that person is standing there too. The evidence of care is present in the same assembly as the evidence of neglect.

[PAUSE]

This is what the repeated question reveals about the character of the One asking it: **He asks twice because He is the kind of God who gives time. He builds the case before closing it. He sends messengers before summoning witnesses. And only when everything that could have been done has been done — only then does the silence of the second question settle.**

### Theme Two: The Proof That Cannot Be Produced

Follow this ayah past its boundary. Immediately after — in the verses that complete the passage — Allah says: *"And We shall take out from every nation a witness, and We shall say: Bring your proof."*

**Bring your proof.**

Here is the sequel to *kuntum tazʿumūn*. The verb *zaʿama* — claiming without grounding — was the rhythm of the life. The claim was the mount of the lie, as the Arabs said. And now, on the Day when all mounts are stilled, proof is demanded.

[PAUSE]

Think about what proof would even look like here. A man who spent his working life serving his wealth — claiming with every early morning and every late night that his security lived in the account balance — what proof does he bring on that Day that the account balance was worth the investment? Does he produce the number? The number is gone. Does he produce the comfort it bought? The comfort ended with his last breath. Does he produce the status it earned? Status dissolves in the grave.

A woman who silenced her own voice her entire life out of fear of what people would think — claiming with every self-erasure that approval was the thing worth protecting — what proof does she bring? Does she produce the approval? The people who gave it cannot remember. Does she produce the peace it brought? There was no peace. Only more silencing.

This is not cruelty. This is **clarity**. On that Day, reality is simply allowed to be what it is without the fog of *zaʿm*. The claim has to show itself. And the things that were always only claims — that were always asserted without ground, like the proverb says, on the mount of lies — dissolve in the light of that Day the way fog dissolves when the sun rises fully.

Notice that the verse does not say: *"Bring your gods."* It says: *"Bring your proof."* The focus is not on the object of worship — it is on the **warrant**. Did you have a reason? Did you examine what you were building your life around? Did you ever hold the thing you served up to the light and ask honestly: *Is this real? Is this worth it? Is this what I was made for?*

*Kuntum tazʿumūn.* You used to keep claiming. And on that Day, the claim is asked to show its evidence — and the thing that was always only a claim cannot show it.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Three: What the Surah's Arc Was Building Toward

Step back now and see the whole surah that contains this ayah.

Surah Al-Qasas begins with Pharaoh — a man who claimed that Egypt was his. *"Do I not possess the dominion of Egypt, and these rivers flowing beneath me?"* He put himself where only Allah belongs. He said: *"I am your highest lord."* And the surah opens by showing an infant in a river — the one who would dismantle Pharaoh's claim. The infant's mother was commanded to do the most impossible thing: trust, let go, release her child into the water. And the word of Allah came before Pharaoh's word ever formed: *"We will return him to you."*

What is the surah's first movement teaching? That the world runs on ownership that is not yours and never was. Egypt is not Pharaoh's. The river is not Pharaoh's. The infant is not even the mother's in the way fear tells her he is — he belongs to what Allah is doing, and what Allah is doing cannot be blocked by the mightiest empire that has ever stood.

Then the surah pivots to Qarun — from political ownership to economic ownership. *"I was only given this wealth because of knowledge I possess."* Mine. My production. My earning. My genius. The earth opened and received him.

And now, at the close of the surah, Allah asks: *"Where are My partners?"* It is the summation of everything the surah has been showing. Pharaoh claimed a partnership with power. Qarun claimed a partnership with provision. And every mushrik in this ayah claimed a partnership with something — belonging to Allah alone — that they placed beside Him without right.

The arc of Al-Qasas is a single, complete revelation: **everything you think you own is already His. Everything you have elevated as a partner already belongs to Him. And the Day that truth becomes undeniable is the Day He asks the question a second time — before all witnesses, without escape.**

### Closing: What This Ayah Is Doing to You Right Now

I want to end where the ayah ends: in the silence.

**Ayna shurakā'ī.** Where are My partners?

The question is not for information. Allah already knows. He knew when the choice was made, when the habit formed, when the claim quietly became the scaffolding of a life. He knew.

The question is for **you**. For the one hearing it. For the one reading this now, before the Day comes.

What is sitting in the space that belongs to Allah alone?

Not dramatically. Not the theological idols carved in stone. I mean the small, daily insertions. The need for a particular person's approval that has quietly been given the weight of divine sanction. The career that has slowly been trusted to provide what only provision can provide. The fear of an outcome — so constant, so reliable in its grip — that it has become a kind of prayer directed somewhere other than Him.

*Kuntum tazʿumūn.* You used to keep claiming.

Now, while there is still time — while the *muḍāriʿ* is still running, while the verb is still imperfect, while the Day has not arrived — this ayah is giving you the question early. Not as a verdict. As a mercy.

**Where are My partners — the ones you kept claiming?**

Ask this now, in the quiet. Look at what you reach for when you are afraid. Look at what fills the silence that could be for Him. Look at what you have been building your days around without ever testing whether it was real.

That is where the question is pointing. Not to condemn. To free.

[PAUSE]

Because here is what the surah has already shown: the One who asks this question on that Day is the same One who sent the messenger before asking it. The same One who provided the day and the night. The same One whose mercy, as Allah says in ayah 73, was built into the very structure of creation — the night for rest, the day for seeking, the provision distributed before you ever reached for it — so that you might give thanks.

The question is asked twice in this surah because mercy asks twice. *Come back,* it says the first time. Then it waits while the witnesses are gathered and the evidence is laid. Then it asks again.

This is not a God who closes the account without warning. This is a God who calls — *yunādī* — and calls again, in this life and on that Day, until the calling is answered or the time has run out.

Answer it now, while you still can.
