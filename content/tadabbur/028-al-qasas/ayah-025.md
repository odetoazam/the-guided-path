# The Walk of Shyness: How Dignity Moves

## Surah Al-Qasas, Ayah 25

### Arabic

> [ayah:28:25] فَجَآءَتْهُ إِحْدَىٰهُمَا تَمْشِى عَلَى ٱسْتِحْيَآءٍ قَالَتْ إِنَّ أَبِى يَدْعُوكَ لِيَجْزِيَكَ أَجْرَ مَا سَقَيْتَ لَنَا ۚ فَلَمَّا جَآءَهُۥ وَقَصَّ عَلَيْهِ ٱلْقَصَصَ قَالَ لَا تَخَفْ ۖ نَجَوْتَ مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

### Translation

> Then one of the two women came to him, walking with shyness. She said, "Indeed, my father is calling you so that he may reward you for having watered for us." So when he came to him and recounted to him the story, he said, "Have no fear. You have escaped from the wrongdoing people."

---

## Introduction: The Moment Before This Moment

Before we open this ayah, we need to feel the world Musa is standing in.

Two ayahs ago, he was a fugitive. He had killed a man — not maliciously, but a blow had landed harder than he meant. Word had spread. The palace he was raised in now wanted him dead. Ibn Kathīr pauses on a contrast easy to miss here: Musa had been raised in Pharaoh's household in luxury and ease, in a position of leadership — he was not a man accustomed to being alone, and now he was utterly alone. A man had run to him in the marketplace and warned him: *they are taking counsel about you to kill you, so leave.* And he did. He fled into the desert — *khā'ifan yataraqqab*, the Quran says, which Ibn Kathīr glosses as moving along while *turning to look over his shoulder* — with no provisions, no map, no destination — just the prayer **رَبِّ نَجِّنِى مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ** — *My Lord, save me from the wrongdoing people.*

Days pass. He arrives at Madyan. He sees a crowd of men watering their animals at a well, and beyond them, two women holding their flock back, waiting. He asks them what's wrong. They answer with a sentence that tells us everything about their lives: *we don't water until the shepherds clear out, and our father is a very old man.* al-Muyassar fills in what they leave unsaid: they hung back because they could not push their way in among the men, and so they waited for the crowd to thin. And when Musa saw them, the same source notes, his heart softened toward them — *raqqa lahumā* — a hunted man with nothing of his own still moved to mercy by two strangers' need. So Musa — exhausted, hunted, a stranger in a strange land — waters their animals for them. Then he walks to the shade of a tree — al-Ṭabarī relates, on the authority of al-Suddī, that it was a *samura*, a thorny desert acacia — and collapses.

And there, in the dust, he prays the most devastating du'a in the Quran:

**رَبِّ إِنِّى لِمَآ أَنزَلْتَ إِلَىَّ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَقِيرٌ** — *My Lord, I am, for whatever good You send down to me, in desperate need.*

He doesn't ask for anything specific. He doesn't list his wants. The classical commentators are clear about how concrete the need underneath was — al-Muyassar glosses the *khayr*, the "good," he is pleading for as *food*, for his hunger had by now grown severe. And yet notice what Musa actually says: he never names it. He leaves the word wide open. He just says: *I am poor. I am in need. Whatever You send — I need it.*

[PAUSE]

Now imagine you are him. You have just finished praying. Your eyes are closed. You don't know if anyone heard. You don't know if you'll eat today. You don't know where you'll sleep. You are a wanted man in a foreign town. You have helped two strangers, and the most you expect from the world is to be left alone in this shade until you can stand again.

And then — footsteps.

**Here is the hook:** The Quran could have said *she came to him.* That would have been enough. But Allah does not let us miss this footstep. He tells us *how* she walked. He spends an entire phrase on the texture of a single woman's gait approaching an exhausted man under a tree. Why? Why does this — of all the details available to omnipotent narration — get this much room?

That is what we are going to sit with today.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: تَمْشِى عَلَىٰ ٱسْتِحْيَآءٍ — *Walking upon shyness*

#### Teaching the construction first

In Arabic, the preposition عَلَىٰ — *'alā* — most basically means *upon*. You sit *upon* a chair. You stand *upon* the ground. It is a preposition of resting, of being supported by something underneath.

Now, Arabic uses this preposition in a particular idiomatic way. When you do something **'alā** a certain quality, it means that quality is the *ground* you are walking on. The quality is not a side feature of the action. It is the floor beneath the action. The action is *supported* by it.

If I told you in everyday English *she walked with shyness,* you might picture a slight hesitation, a downward glance — shyness as a flavor added to the walk. But the Arabic does something different. It does not say *bi-istihyā'* (with shyness, accompanying shyness). It says **'alā istihyā'in** — *upon shyness.* As if shyness were the ground itself. As if every step pressed into it. As if she could not walk except by walking on top of this quality.

[PAUSE]

#### Opening the word

Now let's look at the word she is walking upon.

**ٱسْتِحْيَآء** — *istihyā'*.

The root is ح-ي-ي — *ḥ-y-y* — and this is one of the strangest, most beautiful roots in the entire Arabic language. Because the very same root gives us two words that, on the surface, seem to have nothing to do with each other:

- **ḥayāh** — *life*
- **ḥayā'** — *modesty, shyness, a sense of shame in the noble sense*

The same root. *Life* and *modesty.* The classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* — note this and do not treat it as an accident. They say something like this: *ḥayā'* is the quality by which a soul *remains alive* to its own dignity. To lose *ḥayā'* is to begin to die inwardly. The moment a human being no longer feels the small inner recoil at exposure, at violation, at shamelessness — something has died.

So when this woman walks *upon istihyā'in* — she is not walking upon a weakness. She is walking upon the very thing that keeps her soul *alive.*

#### Going deeper: the form of the word

Stay with me, because the *form* of this word is doing work too.

The basic form of the root would give us *ḥayā'.* But Allah does not say *tamshī 'alā ḥayā'in.* He says *tamshī 'alā istihyā'in.* That added *isti-* prefix puts the word into what Arabic grammarians call **Form X** (*istafʿala*).

Form X most characteristically carries the meaning of *seeking, requesting, drawing forth from within.* *Istaghfara* — to seek forgiveness. *Istaʿāna* — to seek help. *Istahdā* — to seek guidance.

So *istihyā'* is not just *ḥayā'.* It is *ḥayā' that is being drawn upon, actively, in the moment.* It is shyness *summoned.* Dignity *being deployed* in the present tense of her walking.

[PAUSE]

Notice something extraordinary. She is not walking *with* a quality she happens to possess. She is walking *upon* a quality she is *actively drawing forth from within herself* at every step. Each footfall is a small act of dignity, deliberately enacted, because she is — for the first time in her life that the Quran tells us about — approaching a young, strong, unrelated man, alone, with a message from her father.

She is not embarrassed. The Arabic is very precise. *Istihyā'* is not embarrassment. Embarrassment is something done *to* you by circumstance. *Istihyā'* is something done *by* you, *from within* you, in protection of your own dignity and the dignity of the encounter you are walking into.

#### The verb: tamshī — *she walks*

One more thing in this phrase before we leave it. The verb is **تَمْشِى** — *tamshī* — *she walks.* Present tense, continuous. Not *jā'at* (she came, perfective, completed). The previous verb in the ayah is *jā'at* — *she came.* But then immediately Allah switches the camera. He shifts into the present continuous: *tamshī.*

In Arabic narration, this is called a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial clause. It freezes time. The previous verb gave us the arrival. This verb makes us watch the approach. We are not being told she came. We are being made to *watch her coming.* Step by step. Upon shyness. The camera is in slow motion.

[PAUSE]

What does this single phrase reveal? That Allah, in narrating an answered prayer, gives an entire breath to *how dignity moves through the world.* Not how it is announced. How it walks.

### Element Two: إِحْدَىٰهُمَا — *One of the two of them*

This word looks small. It is not.

The ayah does not say *fa-jā'athu al-mar'atu* — *the woman came.* It does not say *fa-jā'athu Ṣafūrā* — naming her. It does not even say *one of the women.* It says **إِحْدَىٰهُمَا** — *one of the two of them.*

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, when you want to specify *one out of a pair,* you do not use the general word for "one" (*wāḥidah*). You use **iḥdā** — a feminine form reserved precisely for *one out of a known, specified two.* It carries the dual within it. You cannot use *iḥdā* unless the listener already knows there are exactly two.

So this word *iḥdāhumā* is a grammatical hinge. It assumes you remember the two women at the well two ayahs ago. It assumes you know there is a pair. It selects *one* out of that pair — without telling you which one.

#### Read the absence

Here is what is remarkable. **Allah does not say which one.**

The classical mufassirūn discuss this. Some say it was the older. Some say the younger. Some say it was the one Musa would later marry. Some say it was the other. The Quran is *deliberately* unspecified.

Why?

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is where the silence is louder than the speech.

If Allah had named her, we would remember her by name. We would build her identity around being *the one who fetched Musa.* But Allah does not let her be reduced to her function. She is *one of the two* — she retains her place in the pair, her sisterhood, her belonging. She is not extracted from her family and turned into a character. She remains, even in this moment of agency, *iḥdāhumā* — a daughter, a sister, embedded.

And there is something else. By leaving her unnamed and merely *one of the two,* Allah preserves the *istihyā'* even at the level of the verse itself. The text walks toward us the same way she walks toward Musa. It does not over-disclose. It does not point a finger and say *this one, look at her.* The Quran itself, in narrating her, lowers its voice.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does. The form of the verse mirrors the content of the verse. She walks upon *istihyā'.* The Quran narrates her upon *istihyā'.* The verse practices what it describes.

#### One more layer: the asymmetry

Watch this asymmetry. Two ayahs earlier, when the women spoke at the well, the verb was **dual**: *qālatā* — *the two of them said.* They spoke together. They were a single social unit, defended by being two.

Now, in this ayah, only **one** approaches. The dual has dissolved. Why? Because the situation has changed. Their father has been told. The household has accepted the stranger. There is now a structure of protection (a father, a message, a purpose). And so one can come where two were needed before.

Even the grammatical *number* in this ayah is telling us about how dignity moves through changing circumstances. When two were needed, two went. When one is appropriate, one goes. And when one goes, she walks *'alā istihyā'in.*

What this phrase reveals: dignity is not isolation. Dignity is calibrated belonging. She comes alone, but she does not come *as* a lone woman — she comes as *one of two*, with a father behind her and a sister at the well, and Allah's narration honors that whole structure even as it sends her forward.

### Element Three: لَا تَخَفْ ۖ نَجَوْتَ — *Do not fear. You have escaped.*

#### The word the father uses

When Musa finally reaches the old man's house and tells him everything — the killing, the flight, the fear — the old man says four words that change the temperature of Musa's life:

**لَا تَخَفْ ۖ نَجَوْتَ مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ**

*Do not fear. You have escaped from the wrongdoing people.*

Listen to that second word. **نَجَوْتَ** — *najawta* — *you have escaped.* The root is ن-ج-و, and it appears in classical lexicons with a remarkable image: *najā* originally referred to high ground that a person climbed onto to escape a flood. *Najāh,* salvation, is being lifted onto ground the water cannot reach.

[PAUSE]

Now go back two ayahs. Remember Musa's prayer as he was fleeing Egypt?

**رَبِّ نَجِّنِى مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ** — *My Lord, save me (najjinī) from the wrongdoing people.*

Same root. *N-j-w.* He had prayed *najjinī* — *save me.*

And now an old man, who has never met him, in a land he has never been to, says to him: *najawta — you have been saved — from the wrongdoing people.*

The exact phrase. *Min al-qawmi al-ẓālimīn.* The wrongdoing people. The same five-word phrase Musa had used in his desperate prayer is now being spoken back to him as completed fact.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Sit with this.

Musa prayed *save me from the wrongdoing people.* He did not know if anyone heard. He prayed it alone, dust on his clothes, no one within miles who knew his name. And the answer comes — not as a thunderclap, not as a vision, not as an angel descending — but as a sentence spoken by an elderly father in a Madyanite household, using the *same words back to him.* As if Allah were saying: *I heard you. Here is your prayer, returned to you, completed.*

#### The grammar of the answer

Notice the tense. The father does not say *you will escape.* He says **najawta** — perfective, completed. *You have escaped.* Past tense. Done. The matter is closed.

In Arabic, the perfective on the lips of someone speaking truth is a guarantee. It is the tense of fait accompli. The father is not predicting safety. He is announcing an accomplished fact, because for the people of Madyan, beyond the reach of Egypt's hand, Musa is *already* safe. He just doesn't know it yet. The father knows it before Musa knows it. And the verse lets us hear safety announced as past tense while Musa is still in the body of a fugitive.

#### And the first word: lā takhaf

**لَا تَخَفْ** — *do not fear.* Two words.

In Arabic, *lā* + jussive (the form *takhaf*) is the negative command. It is the form Allah uses repeatedly to His prophets in the Quran. *Lā takhaf.* *Lā taḥzan.* *Lā tawjal.* The first thing spoken to a believer in trouble. The grammar of consolation.

But notice the speaker. It is not Allah here. It is an old man. A father. A human being. Yet the words he uses are Allah's own register of consolation. As if Allah were speaking *through* this old man's mouth — not by inspiration in a technical sense, but by orchestration. By placing into the father's mouth precisely the phrase Musa's heart needed.

#### Bridge

So look at the architecture. The verse opens with a woman walking upon shyness, carrying a message of reward. The verse closes with an old man speaking the language of divine consolation. Between them — Musa, the receiver. The whole ayah is one motion of provision *meeting* a soul that had just prayed *for whatever good You send down to me, I am in desperate need.*

Now let's ask what this is showing us not just about words — but about how Allah answers prayers, and how dignity moves in the world.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Texture of an Answered Du'a

Let me start somewhere very human.

Most of us, when we imagine an answered prayer, imagine something dramatic. A door opens. A check arrives. A diagnosis reverses. A relationship is restored. We picture the answer as an *event* — a single, visible transaction between heaven and the world. We pray. Heaven hears. Heaven sends. We receive.

But ask anyone whose prayer has actually been answered in a serious way, and they will rarely describe it that way. They will describe a *sequence.* A coincidence that turned into a meeting that turned into a job. A wrong turn that turned into a conversation that turned into a marriage. A delay that turned into an avoidance that turned into a rescue.

Answered prayers, in real life, almost never look like a *bolt.* They look like a *braided rope.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at how Allah braids the answer to Musa's du'a.

Musa prayed for two things. He prayed *save me from the wrongdoing people.* And then later, under the tree, he prayed *I am, for whatever good You send down to me, in desperate need.*

How is this prayer answered?

It is answered by a girl's footsteps.

It is answered by the way she walks — not *with* shyness but *upon* it.

It is answered by the message she carries — *my father is calling you so that he may reward you.*

It is answered by the word *yajziyaka* — *that he may reward you* — a small word with the root *j-z-y,* which is the same root Allah uses for the cosmic reward of the righteous. Musa watered animals for two strangers; the reward begins to come back through them, in the same vocabulary the Quran uses for the reward of the next life.

It is answered by an old man who, having never met him, calls him to dinner.

It is answered by a sentence — *najawta* — that uses the exact root Musa himself used in his prayer.

This is not one event. This is a *braid.* Allah has been weaving the answer to Musa's prayer through other people's lives for who knows how long. The women's father is old, so they need to water late, so they wait while the men finish, so a hungry, exiled stranger sees them and, his heart softening, helps, so he collapses under a tree, so he prays, so they go home and tell their father, so the father sends one of them back, so she walks upon shyness, so the message lands, so Musa rises, so he reaches the household, so he tells his story, so the old man says *do not fear.*

[PAUSE]

Every step of that chain involved another person's decision, another person's mercy, another person's circumstance. The answer to your prayer is almost always running through other people's lives before it reaches yours. And by the time you realize you have been answered, you have already been carried by hands you did not see.

This is why du'a is not a vending machine. This is why answered prayers feel like *recognitions* more than *deliveries.* You don't open the package. You look up one day and realize: *I was being carried.*

What does this change about tomorrow? It means when you make du'a, you are not only asking for a thing. You are giving Allah permission to weave that thing through the lives of strangers you will only meet at the moment of its arrival. The girl walking toward Musa was Allah answering his prayer with her feet. Someone is, right now, somewhere, becoming the answer to a prayer you made.

And someone else's prayer is, right now, walking through *your* day, looking for the footstep where you become its answer.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Hayā' as the Architecture of a Soul

Let me say something that will sound strange in the modern ear, and then let's earn it.

In the modern moral vocabulary, *shame* is almost always a bad word. We talk about *shame culture* as a thing to escape. We talk about *body shame, sexual shame, religious shame* — all things therapists try to dismantle. And rightly, often. Because so much of what we have called *shame* in the modern world is actually *humiliation* — the violence of being made small by another person's gaze.

But the Arabic *ḥayā'* is not that. *Ḥayā'* is not shame imposed *from outside.* *Ḥayā'* is dignity *radiated from inside.*

Think of it this way. Humiliation is what happens when someone else's eyes diminish you. *Ḥayā'* is what happens when *your own awareness of who you are* causes you to move carefully through the world — not because you are afraid, but because you know your worth and you refuse to spend it cheaply.

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ said: *al-ḥayā'u khayrun kulluh* — *ḥayā' is good, all of it.* And in another narration: *lā ya'tī illā bi-khayr* — *it brings nothing but good.* He also said: *if you have no ḥayā', do whatever you want.* Meaning: ḥayā' is the *inner regulator* of a soul. When it is intact, the soul governs itself. When it dies, no external rule can replace it.

This is why the same root gives us *life* and *modesty.* Because the inner regulator that makes a soul careful about its own dignity is the same regulator that keeps it *alive* — alive to itself, alive to others, alive to Allah.

#### Now watch what this ayah is showing us

When this young woman walks toward Musa, *upon istihyā',* she is not displaying timidity. She is not displaying fear. She is displaying the *most powerful inner architecture a soul can have.* She is moving through a moment that the modern world would call *awkward* — a young woman approaching an unrelated young man alone with a message — and she is moving through it *governed from within.* Not by anxiety. Not by performance. By an active drawing-forth of dignity at every step.

This is why the verb is *istihyā'* (Form X — *summoning*). She is not passively shy. She is *actively deploying* her dignity. She is *climbing onto* her own moral high ground at every footfall.

And here is the thing — she is *unhesitant.* She delivers the message. She speaks. She names her father. She names the purpose. She is not silenced by her *ḥayā'.* She is *enabled* by it. Her dignity does not stop her from acting. It shapes *how* she acts.

[PAUSE]

This is the part the modern reader needs to hear. *Ḥayā'* is not the opposite of agency. *Ḥayā'* is *agency exercised with dignity intact.* It is not what stops you from speaking. It is what shapes the way you speak. It is not what stops you from walking forward. It is the ground you walk *upon* as you walk forward.

She acts. She speaks. She negotiates. Two ayahs later she will recommend Musa to her father, with extraordinary insight — *yā abati ista'jirhu, inna khayra mani-sta'jarta-l-qawiyyu-l-amīn* — *Father, hire him, the best of those you can hire is the strong and the trustworthy.* She is articulate. She is decisive. She is, the next ayah will show, a woman of remarkable judgment.

But the *way* she moves through every one of these acts is *upon istihyā'in.*

That is what dignity looks like when it is integrated with action. Not silence. Not retreat. *Action carried out on the ground of inner regulation.*

#### What does this change?

It changes how we read every act of social courage in our own lives.

When you have to speak in a meeting and you feel that small inner pull toward carefulness — that is not weakness. That is *ḥayā'* asking to be the ground you walk on. The question is not *can I overcome it to be bold?* The question is *can I act boldly while standing on it?*

When you have to deliver hard news, or approach a stranger, or speak truth to someone older than you — there is a kind of dignity that says *I will do this with my soul fully present, my worth fully intact, no part of me thrown away for the sake of doing it.*

That is the walk this verse is teaching.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have seen.

The linguistic discoveries — the present-continuous verb that puts us *inside* her walking, the preposition *'alā* that makes shyness the ground beneath her, the Form X *istihyā'* that turns dignity into something *actively summoned,* the *iḥdāhumā* that keeps her embedded in her sisterhood even as she steps forward, the *najawta* that returns Musa's own prayer-vocabulary back to him as completed fact —

— and the themes — the texture of answered prayer as a braided rope rather than a bolt, and *ḥayā'* as the inner architecture of a soul rather than its silencing —

— these are the same insight from two directions.

The same insight is this: **Allah's answers travel through the dignity of human beings.** Musa's prayer is answered by a girl who walks upon shyness, carrying a message from a father who speaks the language of divine consolation. The medium of the miracle is human dignity itself — a particular kind of dignity that does not shrink and does not strut, that comes forward but comes forward *upon* something.

The Quran is showing us that when Allah answers a prayer, He often does so *through* the moral architecture of someone else's soul. The cleaner that architecture, the cleaner the channel. The young woman of Madyan is a channel because she *walks upon* something. The old father is a channel because he speaks in the register of *lā takhaf.* Musa receives the answer because he prayed it honestly. Three moral architectures meet in a single afternoon, and a prophet's life is saved.

### Questions to Carry

1. What did I last pray for that I now suspect has been answered through someone else's footsteps — and did I notice them as the answer when they arrived?

2. Where in my own life am I being asked to walk *upon* dignity into a moment I would prefer to avoid? What would change if I stopped trying to summon courage and started trying to summon *ḥayā'*?

3. Am I, today, a clean channel for someone else's answered prayer? Is the architecture of my soul intact enough for Allah to send His mercy *through* me to someone else?

4. When I make du'a, do I trust Allah enough to weave the answer through people I will only meet at the moment of its arrival?

5. What is the *lā takhaf* sentence I most need to hear — and to whom am I supposed to be speaking it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When Allah answers your prayer, He often sends the answer walking — through the dignity of a stranger whose name you may never know.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow, when you walk into a difficult conversation, a hard meeting, a moment where you must speak when you would rather not — try this. Do not try to be bold. Do not try to be small. Try to walk *'alā istihyā'in* — upon the ground of an inner dignity that you actively summon at every step. Notice that the walk itself is the testimony. Notice that the texture of how you move is half of what you are saying.

And when something good arrives in your life this week — pause before you call it luck. Trace it back. Find the footsteps. Find the strangers in the chain. Thank the One who wove it. Then ask: *whose answered prayer am I walking toward right now?*

### Du'a

O Allah, You who answered Musa under the tree with a footstep he did not expect — make our prayers honest enough to be answered, and our souls clean enough to be the answer to someone else's prayer.

O Allah, teach us to walk *upon istihyā'in* — not in fear, not in performance, but in the dignity of knowing whose servants we are.

And when the wrongdoing people surround us, let us hear, even in the voice of a stranger, Your *lā takhaf — najawta.* Āmīn.
