# The Dead Brother's Flesh — The Ayah That Made the Invisible Sin Visible

## The Ayah

[ayah:49:12] يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱجْتَنِبُوا۟ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ ٱلظَّنِّ إِنَّ بَعْضَ ٱلظَّنِّ إِثْمٌ ۖ وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا۟ وَلَا يَغْتَب بَّعْضُكُم بَعْضًا ۚ أَيُحِبُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَن يَأْكُلَ لَحْمَ أَخِيهِ مَيْتًا فَكَرِهْتُمُوهُ ۚ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ تَوَّابٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū — ijtanibū kathīran min aẓ-ẓanni, inna baʿḍa aẓ-ẓanni ithm. Wa lā tajassasū wa lā yaghtab baʿḍukum baʿḍā. A-yuḥibbu aḥadukum an yaʾkula laḥma akhīhi maytan fa-karihtumūh. Wa-ttaqū Allāh. Inna Allāha tawwābun raḥīm.*

**O you who have believed — step wide around much of assumption. Truly, some assumption is sin. And do not spy, and do not let some of you backbite others. Would one of you love to eat the flesh of his brother, dead? You would detest it. So fear Allah — truly, Allah is ever-returning, ever-merciful.**

## Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word, let's stand in the room this ayah was revealed into.

Surah Al-Ḥujurāt is one of the Quran's most carefully designed surahs on the ethics of a community. It was revealed in Madinah, in the years after the Muslim community had grown large enough that it was no longer a small circle of intimates. It was now a society. And a society has a specific set of diseases that a small circle does not have — the diseases of *report.*

In a village, everyone knows everyone directly. In a society, most of what you know about other people arrives through third parties. You hear something *about* someone. You form an impression *from a distance.* You judge without having stood in front of the person you are judging. This is the territory Surah Al-Ḥujurāt addresses — and this ayah, near its center, is its sharpest surgical instrument.

[PAUSE]

Now hold this in your chest for a moment. The companions of the Prophet ﷺ — the people whose daily life this ayah first addressed — were not strangers to one another. They prayed together five times a day. They ate together. They fought together. They married into one another's families. If there was ever a community where gossip and assumption *should* have been rare, it was this one.

And yet Allah revealed this ayah *to them.* To that community. With all that closeness.

The ayah is telling you something the community learned about itself: **the diseases of assumption, spying, and backbiting are not symptoms of a bad community. They are the background radiation of every community, even the best one ever assembled.** If it needed to be addressed there, in that company, with that Prophet present among them — how much more in ours?

**The Hook:**

This ayah contains what may be the most visceral image in the Quran. Allah will not argue with you about the ethics of backbiting. He will not cite consequences. He will not threaten punishment. Instead, He will put a single image in your mouth — literally, in your mouth — that will make you unable to backbite the same way again, if you let the image land.

But the image comes third, not first. Before it lands, the ayah walks you through three stages that you have to understand in order. By the time the image arrives, you will already know what it is doing. The ayah is a surgery, and the steel is laid out in sequence.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Ijtanibū* — Step Wide

Before we open the first command, I want to show you something about Arabic verb forms.

In Arabic, a simple verb can be intensified or redirected by being placed in one of ten patterns. Form I is the basic verb. Form VIII is a specific pattern — *iftaʿala* — which often carries the sense of *doing the action to oneself* or *taking the action upon oneself deliberately.* It is a reflexive intensifier. When you move a verb from Form I to Form VIII, the action becomes a chosen, self-involving motion — not just something you do but something you *step into.*

The root of our first command is **ج-ن-ب** — which means *side.* A *jānib* is a side, a flank. The Form I verb *janaba* means "he set aside." Simple.

Now watch what Allah does. He uses Form VIII: *ijtanibū.*

[PAUSE]

*Ijtanibū* does not mean "don't assume." It does not even mean "avoid assumption." In the shape Allah chose, it means something closer to: **take yourself, by your own effort, to the side of this.** Go around. Step wide. Put distance between yourself and this thing, actively, deliberately.

If the command had been *lā taẓunnū* — "do not assume" — it would have been simple prohibition. But Allah is not saying "do not assume." Allah is saying *step wide around assumption.* Which implies that you cannot simply not-assume. You have to physically re-route your attention.

This is a grammatical acknowledgment of what you already know about your own mind. You cannot, at will, simply stop having assumptions about people. Assumptions rise in you unbidden. What you can do is refuse to walk toward them. Step wide. Take the longer path around. Do not feed them with attention.

[PAUSE]

And notice the object — *kathīran min aẓ-ẓanni.* "Much of assumption."

Classical scholars saw something crucial here. Allah does not say *ijtanibū aẓ-ẓanna kullahu* — "avoid all assumption." Because assumption, in some forms, is unavoidable and even necessary. You must form some working hypotheses to function — you must assume the person selling you bread is selling bread, not poison. Some *ẓann* is unavoidable. Some is even required.

What Allah commands is *kathīran* — "much of it." *Indefinite much.* The amount left unspecified deliberately. Which means the burden is placed on the believer to judge, in each case, whether this particular assumption is the *kathīr* to avoid or the necessary minimum.

And the next clause gives the reason: *inna baʿḍa aẓ-ẓanni ithm.* "Truly, some of assumption is sin."

[PAUSE]

Look at that phrasing. Not "all assumption is sin." Not "assumption leads to sin." *Some of assumption* — *is* — *sin.* The is-ness is direct. The assumption itself, in certain forms, is already the sin. Not its consequences. Not its utterance. The inner motion of assuming, when it rises against a Muslim without evidence, is itself what you are accountable for.

This reframes the entire psychological stance. You are not being asked to police your words. You are being asked to police your inner life.

One clear statement: **The first command is not a prohibition on a behavior. It is a command to put distance between yourself and a category of internal states. Allah is saying *go around this territory entirely* — because some of the territory is already sin before a single word is spoken.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Lā Tajassasū* — Do Not Dig

The second command sits between the first and the third like a door. If you do not understand what this door is doing, the third command does not land the way the ayah means it to.

*Wa lā tajassasū.* "And do not spy."

Root: **ج-س-س**. Form V — *tafaʿʿala.* Another intensified pattern. The Form I verb *jassa* means "he probed, he felt for something by touch." *Tajassus* is the intensified version: *persistent, deliberate investigation of what is hidden.*

[PAUSE]

Classical linguists distinguished this root from its near-relative *taḥassasa* (**ح-س-س**, to seek knowledge for a good purpose, to check on the welfare of someone). The difference is the intention. *Taḥassus* is watching to protect — a parent watching a child, a leader checking on his people. *Tajassus* is watching to *find.* It is the digging of the investigator who has decided there is something hidden and is determined to uncover it.

Now watch where Allah places this command. Between *ẓann* (assumption) and *ghībah* (backbiting).

This is not accidental. It is a diagnostic sequence. These three sins are stages of the same disease:

1. **Ẓann** — you form a negative assumption about someone in your mind.
2. **Tajassus** — the assumption hardens into curiosity; now you start *digging* — quietly, covertly, trying to find the evidence that will confirm what you already suspect.
3. **Ghībah** — once you have either found or fabricated enough, you *speak* what you have assembled to a third party.

The progression is from the private to the active to the public. Allah is not listing three unrelated sins. He is naming a single process in three phases. And He is placing the command against each phase in reverse order of visibility — because the inner sin is the hardest to catch, and the outer sin is the last step you take before the damage becomes permanent.

[PAUSE]

*Lā tajassasū.* Do not dig. When the assumption rises, do not go looking for its confirmation. This is the decisive point at which the process can still be reversed. Once you start *searching* for evidence, you will find it — whether it is there or not — because the eye trained to look for cracks will see them in any surface.

You know this from your own life. If you have decided someone is being dishonest, every gesture they make begins to look suspicious. Their hesitation is a tell. Their eye contact is too intense. Their absence from the meeting confirms their intent. You are not observing — you are *selecting.*

The ayah says: refuse the selection. Do not dig. Step wide from the assumption *and* from the search.

One clear statement: **The middle command is the one that interrupts the chain. If you do not spy, the assumption starves and dies. The whole disease collapses if this step is refused.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Dead Brother's Flesh

Now we come to the image.

Allah does not argue against backbiting. He does not lay out consequences. He does not warn of hellfire. He asks a question.

*A-yuḥibbu aḥadukum an yaʾkula laḥma akhīhi maytan?*

"Would one of you love to eat the flesh of his brother — dead?"

Let me first walk you around what this question is *not.* It is not *"would you agree to eat…"* — it is not about consent. It is not *"would you tolerate eating…"* — it is not about endurance. The verb is *yuḥibb.* Root: **ح-ب-ب**. **Love.** Would one of you *love* to do this? Would it *please* you? Would it be attractive to you?

[PAUSE]

The question is so carefully framed that you cannot escape it easily. The verb bypasses your rational defenses and asks about your appetite. Your desire. Because Allah knows that backbiting, when you are doing it, *is* attractive. It is pleasurable. The talk flows. There is a release. There is a bond with the person you are talking to. You are not, in that moment, holding your nose and unwillingly gossiping. You are enjoying it.

So Allah asks: you like this. But would you like *this*?

And then He builds the image.

*Laḥma akhīhi.* The flesh — of his brother.

Classical linguists note the progression in word choice. Not "the body of a stranger." Not "the flesh of any human." *Laḥma akhīhi* — the *flesh,* the meaty muscle, of *his brother.* The one who shares blood with him. The one whose protection was his responsibility. The one for whom he would, in any other context, have drawn a weapon to defend.

And then the word that makes the image unbearable.

*Maytan.* Dead.

[PAUSE]

Stay with *maytan* for a moment. The word is a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial accusative — describing the state of the brother at the moment of the eating. Not "your brother, whom you plan to kill." Not "your brother, who died in battle and whose flesh was offered as food." Simply: *maytan* — "while dead." The brother is dead. You did not kill him. But he is lying there, and you — what? You bite into him.

Every element of the image is calibrated. Consider:

- **Dead** — he cannot defend himself. He cannot speak back. He cannot explain. He is defenseless in a way he never was in life. This is *precisely* the condition of the person being backbitten. They are not in the room. They cannot respond. They do not even know they are being consumed.

- **Your brother** — not a stranger. Not an enemy. Someone with whom you share belonging. Someone whose harm is, in the logic of community, also your harm.

- **His flesh** — not his property, not his reputation, not his name. His physical substance. The thing that is most intimately *him.* Because when you gossip about a person, you are not merely talking about their behavior — you are consuming what makes them *them.*

- **Eating** — not stealing. Not even destroying. Eating. The act of incorporation. You take in what was them and make it into yourself. The gossip is not just said — it is *absorbed.* It becomes part of your fabric.

Classical tafsirists, from Ibn Kathīr to al-Qurṭubī, noted the precision of this image. Each of them arrived at the same layered reading from slightly different angles. Ibn Kathīr emphasized the *defenselessness* of the dead. Al-Qurṭubī emphasized the *brotherhood* — that what you would never do to someone in your own family, you easily do to someone who is, in the community of believers, exactly that family. Al-Rāzī emphasized the *appetite* implied by the verb *yuḥibb* — that the ayah is naming something about the pleasure we take in gossip that we would never admit in any other context.

Build the composite image. It is not optional material. It is where the ayah lives.

[PAUSE]

And then the closing clause: *fa-karihtumūh.* "You would detest it."

The grammar here is unusual. The particle *fa-* often means "so" or "then." But the verb *karihtum* is in the *perfect tense* — "you detested." Not "you would detest" (conditional). **"You detested it."** Past.

Classical grammarians noted this. Allah is not asking you a hypothetical question. He is reporting a verdict you have already rendered. *You already detest it.* The reaction is not being requested — it is being reported back to you as something your fiṭrah, your innate human nature, has already confirmed.

And this is the ayah's final move. It does not argue with you. It holds up your own reaction, places it next to your gossip, and says: **you already know.** Your detesting is not in doubt. The inconsistency between how your body revolts at the image and how your tongue participates in the act is not something Allah needs to resolve — you must resolve it.

One clear statement: **The ayah does not forbid backbiting. It makes backbiting impossible to perform without inner contradiction. The image and your own detestation, placed side by side, do the work that a thousand prohibitions could not.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

We have seen three things. The Form VIII command *ijtanibū* that asks for a physical re-routing of attention. The middle command *lā tajassasū* that interrupts the chain of the disease. And the image — the brother's flesh, dead, eaten — that converts prohibition into visceral reality.

Now let us ask what this ayah means not just for what we avoid, but for how we live among other human beings.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Why Backbiting Feels Good — And What the Ayah Is Really Asking

Let me start outside the ayah.

If you have ever been in a conversation where, at some point, someone brings up a mutual acquaintance — and the conversation leans into that acquaintance's flaws, or failures, or embarrassing moments — you know what happens next. A warmth enters the room. The speakers get closer to each other. There is laughter, but a specific kind of laughter — knowing, shared. The energy rises. You leave the conversation having bonded.

And hours later, if you are the kind of person who pays attention, you notice something has happened inside you. A faint residue. You are less happy than you expected to be, given how fun the conversation was. There is a small weight you are carrying that you cannot name.

[PAUSE]

This residue is not a coincidence. It is the specific, predictable after-effect of a specific activity. Let's name it: **backbiting creates false intimacy.**

Here is the mechanism. Two people bonding over a shared opinion about an absent third party do not need to share anything actually vulnerable with one another. They don't need to be brave. They don't need to expose weakness in themselves. They can achieve the feeling of closeness — *quickly* — by directing judgment outward at someone who is not present.

The target is the price of admission. You pay the third person's reputation to buy intimacy with the second person. And because the payment is not deducted from you — it is deducted from someone who will never know — the transaction feels free.

It is not free. The ayah knows it is not free.

[PAUSE]

Now watch what Allah has done with this image. *A-yuḥibbu aḥadukum…* Allah is not asking you to be ashamed of your desire to backbite. He is asking you to examine what the desire actually *is.*

You do not love to harm people. You do not, in the honest center of yourself, want to eat your brother's flesh. What you love is the feeling of closeness that the backbiting produces. The target is incidental — the connection is what you are after.

So the ayah is pointing you toward a diagnosis. **If you have a persistent appetite for backbiting, it is because you have not built enough real intimacy anywhere else.** Because real intimacy — the vulnerable kind, where you share a fear with your spouse or admit a mistake to a friend — is hard. It asks something of you. Backbiting intimacy asks nothing, delivers a dilute version, and the shortage in your life makes you return to it.

[PAUSE]

Classical scholars, when discussing *ghībah*, often emphasized that the sin is not fully cured by simply stopping the behavior. It is cured by replacing what the behavior was doing. If the tongue has been accustomed to one activity and you remove the activity, the tongue will seek it back — unless something takes its place.

This is why, in the tradition, the replacement is prescribed concretely. Dhikr. Remembering Allah. The tongue that remembers Him cannot, in the same moment, consume a brother's flesh. Reciting Quran. Asking for forgiveness. Making duʿāʾ for the very people you were tempted to backbite about. These are the activities that fill the opening so that the old behavior does not flow back in.

And here is the part most miss. When you replace backbiting with *duʿāʾ* for the person — asking Allah to bless them, to guide them, to give them what you wish for yourself — *you generate the real intimacy the backbiting was failing to produce.* Not with the other gossiper. With Allah. And eventually, often, with the person themselves. Because something softens in you when you pray for someone you had been tempted to tear down.

[PAUSE]

**The core insight:** The ayah is not only forbidding a behavior. It is revealing a hunger. The hunger for closeness is real and legitimate. What is being forbidden is the cheap way of feeding it at someone else's cost. And the remedy is not suppression — it is the relocation of the hunger toward a source that actually fills it.

**Practical application:** The next time you notice yourself drifting into backbiting-shaped conversation, do not try to stop yourself. Redirect. Make one specific, private duʿāʾ for the person whose name just came up. Not out loud. Silently, within yourself — *O Allah, bless them, forgive them what is hidden, guide them in what they need, give them from Yourself what I would want for myself.* The conversation will either change direction or will lose its hold on you. You will walk out of it lighter.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Suspicion as a Daily Discipline

Let us go back to the first command. *Ijtanibū kathīran min aẓ-ẓanni.* Step wide around much of assumption.

I want to stay here because this is the hardest part of the ayah to live, and it is the one most people skip over.

[PAUSE]

Modern life is a river of suspicion. You read a headline — your mind generates a judgment about a person you have never met. You see a social media post — your mind forms an opinion about the poster's character. You walk past a stranger with a tattoo, a scarf, a hairstyle — your mind produces a whole biography in the half-second your eye passes over them. And this is just the public sphere.

In your private sphere, the judgments are even more active. Your coworker didn't reply to your email — they must be annoyed with you. Your friend cancelled last-minute — they must not actually value the friendship. Your spouse was quiet at dinner — they must be disappointed in you. The mind is manufacturing these stories constantly, as a background process.

Allah is not saying your mind should stop doing this. He knows it will not stop. He is saying: **step wide around it.** Do not walk toward these stories. Do not feed them. Do not turn them over and examine them. Do not test them for plausibility. Simply — move around.

[PAUSE]

Classical scholars developed what they called *ḥusn al-ẓann* — *holding the good assumption.* Not absence of ẓann. *Good* ẓann. The deliberate choice to frame an ambiguous situation in the most generous terms available.

Your coworker did not reply — maybe their inbox is overwhelmed, maybe they are sick, maybe the email did not arrive. Your friend cancelled — maybe they are protecting you from their own hard day, maybe they are sparing you poor company. Your spouse was quiet — maybe they were thinking, maybe they were tired, maybe they were processing something from work.

None of these generous interpretations are *proven.* They are simply the *chosen* interpretation. And the choice is yours. You have to choose one interpretation or another, because the mind will not tolerate ambiguity for long. The only question is whether you choose the one that protects your brother or the one that accuses him.

[PAUSE]

Here is the spiritual claim buried in the ayah. *The believer's default, in ambiguous situations involving other believers, should be the interpretation most favorable to the other person.* Not as a naive posture. Not as denial of reality. As a *discipline* — a daily exercise of the same muscle that prays, fasts, and gives.

And here is why it matters beyond kindness. Every time you choose *ḥusn al-ẓann* in an ambiguous situation, you build a small piece of spiritual architecture in yourself. You become a person whose first response to another person is protection rather than suspicion. Over years, this adds up. You become, genuinely, a different kind of human being — the kind around whom others feel safe, the kind who can hold confidences, the kind whose presence in a community is a blessing rather than a risk.

And the reverse is also true. Every time you choose *sūʾ al-ẓann* — the ugly assumption — you build a small piece of the opposite architecture. Over years, you become a person whose mind is full of other people's supposed failures. A bitter interior. A ledger of slights. Your community begins to sense the energy you carry. Eventually, the ẓann you directed at others begins to be returned — because you have taught those around you that you are not a safe place to land.

[PAUSE]

The ayah ends with a promise most readers do not register. *Inna Allāha tawwābun raḥīm.* "Truly, Allah is ever-returning, ever-merciful."

Classical grammarians noted the intensive form *tawwāb* — a hyperbolic pattern that means not just "the one who accepts repentance" but the one whose *turning-back* toward the servant is abundant, repeated, continuous. The servant turns. Allah turns. Over and over. The ayah ends on this divine name because *Allah knows you will fail.* You will backbite. You will assume. You will dig. And when you notice it, and turn, He will turn to you — not once, but every time.

The threat of the image was severe. The consolation of the closing is proportional. This is not a verse that condemns you — it is a verse that invites you to become someone the image no longer applies to, with full permission to come back to Allah each time you slip on the way.

[PAUSE]

**The core insight:** Ẓann is not an event. It is a habit. And habits can only be changed by the reverse habit, repeated. You cannot stop making assumptions about people — but you can make a discipline, over years, of choosing the generous interpretation each time you catch yourself making one. The ayah is commanding this discipline, and promising that Allah will turn toward you each time you turn.

**Practical application:** For the next week, try this one thing. Whenever you catch yourself forming a negative assumption about someone — *no matter how small* — pause and manually construct one plausible generous interpretation. Say it to yourself in words. You do not have to believe it is definitely true. You just have to generate it and hold it alongside the ugly one. Over a week of this, your mind will begin to produce the generous interpretation on its own, faster. The muscle grows.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at what we have been walking through.

The ayah is built like a surgical theater. It has a preparation phase (the first command), a cutting phase (the middle command), and a visceral demonstration (the image), followed by a closing (the divine names).

The preparation: *step wide around much of assumption.* The inner life is cleaned first.
The cut: *do not spy.* The chain is broken at its decisive link.
The demonstration: *would you love to eat your brother's flesh, dead?* The outer behavior is placed against a physical horror so precise your body refuses on its own.
The closing: *Allah is ever-returning, ever-merciful.* The one who fails — as you will — is already held.

And listen to the order of divine names at the end. *Tawwāb* comes before *Raḥīm.* The Turning-One before the Merciful-One. Classical scholars noticed this pairing. The *tawwāb* comes first because the mercy enters through the turning. Until you turn, the mercy is available but not applied. The ayah's final mercy is a door — but you have to walk through it with your return.

The architecture is this: **the ayah is the whole drama of sin in miniature. Inner state → private action → public act → horror → return → mercy. Every step is named. Every step has a door out. The door at the end is the one Allah has not allowed to close.**

### The Emotional Arc

We started with a question: why does an ayah forbidding gossip end with a corpse? We walked through *ijtanibū* as the physical-attention command. We walked through *lā tajassasū* as the interrupter of the chain. We walked through the image as an inner surgery, an appeal to your own reaction rather than to punishment. We walked through backbiting's false intimacy and its true replacement. We walked through ẓann as a daily discipline. And we arrived at the divine names that hold the whole drama in mercy.

What we discovered is this: **Allah did not want to merely forbid these sins. He wanted to make them impossible to commit honestly.** If you have understood the image, you cannot backbite the same way again. If you have internalized *ijtanibū,* you cannot wander toward assumption the same way again. If you have absorbed *lā tajassasū,* you cannot dig with a clean conscience. The ayah is a kind of vaccine — once it enters your system, the behavior becomes harder to perform without noticing what you are doing.

### Questions to Carry

1. Think of the last conversation in which you participated in gossip or backbiting, even mildly. What *need* were you actually trying to meet in that conversation — closeness, validation, entertainment? And what would it take to meet that same need without the backbiting?

2. Where in your life, right now, are you doing *tajassus* — quietly, privately digging for confirmation of a suspicion you already hold? About a family member? A coworker? A public figure? What would happen if you stopped digging — simply stopped — for a week?

3. Name one person about whom you have a persistent *sūʾ al-ẓann* — an ugly assumption you keep returning to. What is the most generous plausible interpretation of their behavior? Not the most likely — the most generous. Can you hold it beside the ugly one long enough for it to loosen its grip?

4. The ayah's image is viscerally extreme. Why do you think Allah did not use a gentler image? What does the severity of the image reveal about how He sees this sin — and how do you reconcile that with the gentleness of the closing *tawwāb raḥīm?*

5. If Allah revealed this ayah to *the Prophet's community* — a community of genuine closeness and faith — what does that tell you about the prevalence of these sins in any Muslim gathering today, including those you are part of? And what is the one small thing you could do, in your own circle, to reduce the background radiation of ẓann and ghībah this week?

### One Thing to Remember

> **Allah did not make backbiting illegal — He made it impossible to perform honestly. Once you see your brother's dead flesh on your own lips, you cannot unseeit, and the mercy at the end of the ayah is the door that stays open every time you return.**

### The Closing Invitation

If this ayah wants one thing from you tonight, it is this.

Before you sleep, bring to mind one person you have spoken ill of recently. You do not need to contact them. You do not need to confess to anyone. Simply — silently, to Allah — ask Him to bless them in the way you would want to be blessed. *Allāhumma bārik lahum, wa-ghfir lahum, wa-ʿāfihim, wa-ʿfu ʿanhum.* Bless them, forgive them, keep them well, pardon them.

If you do this once, a crack opens. If you make it a habit, the crack becomes a door, and the person you were tempted to consume becomes the person you begin to protect in prayer — which is the opposite of *ghībah,* the opposite of *tajassus,* the opposite of *ẓann.* You have walked through the whole ayah in reverse, and you have arrived at *tawwāb raḥīm* without needing the ayah to drag you there.

### Du'a

Allāhumma, You who named this sin not with words but with an image, place that image before us every time our tongues drift toward our brothers and sisters who are not in the room. Let us *step wide* around assumption, let us refuse to *dig,* let us become people in whose company others feel safe.

Take from us the appetite for cheap intimacy, and replace it with the slower intimacy of Your dhikr and the intimacy of sincere duʿāʾ for those we were tempted to tear down. And when we fail — as we will — turn to us as *Tawwāb,* hold us in Your *Raḥmah,* and return us again to the door this ayah left open.

*Āmīn.*
