# Until It Returns: The Fight Whose Only Goal Is Coming Home

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **وَإِن طَآئِفَتَانِ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ ٱقْتَتَلُوا۟ فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ فَإِنۢ بَغَتْ إِحْدَىٰهُمَا عَلَى ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ فَقَـٰتِلُوا۟ ٱلَّتِى تَبْغِى حَتَّىٰ تَفِىٓءَ إِلَىٰٓ أَمْرِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ فَإِن فَآءَتْ فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمَا بِٱلْعَدْلِ وَأَقْسِطُوٓا۟ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُقْسِطِينَ**

In transliteration:

> *Wa in ṭāʾifatāni mina-l-muʾminīna-qtatalū fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā. Fa-in baghat iḥdāhumā ʿala-l-ukhrā fa-qātilu-llatī tabghī ḥattā tafīʾa ilā amri-llāh. Fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bil-ʿadli wa-aqsiṭū. Inna-llāha yuḥibbu-l-muqsiṭīn.*

And in plain English:

> "And if two parties among the believers fight, then make peace between the two of them. But if one of them transgresses against the other, then fight the one that transgresses until it returns to the command of Allah. Then if it returns, make peace between them with justice, and act with equity. Indeed, Allah loves those who act with equity."

Now let me rebuild the room this verse first landed in.

Picture Madinah. Not the serene Madinah of our imaginations — the real one. A city of tribes that, a few years earlier, had been soaked in generations of blood feud. The Aws and the Khazraj had fought wars so long that Islam arriving among them was, the Companions said, like the desert itself blooming. But old wounds don't vanish on a calendar. The temperature could still spike. Tempers still flared. And the worst thing imaginable in this new fragile brotherhood — the nightmare scenario — was that believers, *muʾminūn*, people who prayed behind the same Prophet ﷺ, would one day raise hands against each other.

Among the narrations preserved for this verse — and one of them is reported in al-Bukhārī through Anas ibn Mālik — there was an incident involving ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy. The Prophet ﷺ was riding, the report says, and a quarrel of words escalated between two groups until it came to blows — hands, sandals, palm branches. Other early commentators preserve a tradition about a dispute over a piece of land between two clans. The scholars differ on exactly which occasion brought the verse down, and I want to be honest about that — there is more than one tradition here, and the Quran's command is bigger than any single one of them. **What they agree on is the shape of the moment: believers, in the same community, fighting.**

And here is the thing I want you to hold as a question for the next hour.

When a community fractures — when the people you'd call "us" split into two and start swinging — what is the first thing a wise heart wants? Usually: *find out who's right.* Pick the side. Win. And if force is needed, crush the wrong one.

Allah is about to give an instruction that does something stranger and far more beautiful than that. He is going to refuse to let go of either side's faith. He is going to put the duty of peace not on the fighters but on *you* — the one watching. And He is going to bound even the legitimate use of force by a single word — a word that doesn't mean *defeat* and doesn't mean *destroy*.

It means **come home**.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Two — and Yet Many. The Grammar That Refuses to Erase Anyone

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we touch the verse, because Arabic is going to do something here that English literally cannot do.

English has two numbers: singular and plural. One book, many books. That's it. Arabic has **three**: singular, *dual*, and plural. There is a special form — a whole separate grammatical shape — reserved for exactly two of something. Two parties, two hands, two eyes. The dual.

Why does that matter? Because it means that in Arabic, every time you speak, you are constantly making a choice the English speaker never even faces. Are these *two*, or are these *many*? And when a speaker who controls that choice perfectly suddenly slides from one to the other inside a single sentence — that slide is never an accident. It's a brushstroke.

Now look.

The verse opens: **طَآئِفَتَانِ** — *ṭāʾifatān.* "Two parties." That ending, the *-ān*, is the dual. Two. Clean. Two blocs.

Then immediately the verb: **ٱقْتَتَلُوا۟** — *iqtatalū.* "They fought." But that *-ū* ending is not the dual. It's the **plural** — the form you use for three, ten, a hundred.

So in the space of two words Allah says, literally: *"Two parties... the many fought."*

This is not loose grammar. The classical commentators caught it instantly. Al-Jalālayn pauses right here and explains it precisely: the verb came in the plural *naẓaran ilā-l-maʿnā* — "with regard to the meaning" — because each ṭāʾifa is itself a crowd of individuals. Two camps, yes — but made of many, many people.

And then watch what happens next. The command to reconcile: **فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمَا** — *fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā.* "Make peace between **the two of them**." And *baynahumā* — that *-humā* — snaps straight back to the **dual**.

[PAUSE]

Let that settle, because it is breathtaking.

**When they are fighting, Allah counts them as many — *iqtatalū*. When it is time to make peace, He counts them as two — *baynahumā*.**

Why? Sit with it. In the fight, what is real is the multitude — the individual fists, the individual rage, every single person who chose to swing. The plural exposes the fight for what it is: not an abstraction, not "two sides," but a hundred separate human beings each personally committing the act. The plural *iqtatalū* won't let anyone hide inside the crowd. *You* fought. Each of you.

But the moment we turn to healing, the lens pulls back. Now they are *two*. Two units that can be brought to a table. Because you cannot reconcile a mob — you can only reconcile *parties*. Peace needs the dual. Peace needs you to see two things that can face each other and be made whole.

The grammar is doing the diagnosis and the cure in four words. **It magnifies personal responsibility in the conflict and restores wholeness for the resolution.** No English translation you will ever read can carry that, because English doesn't own the dual. You needed the Arabic to even see it was there.

And here is what this single feature reveals before we go any further:

These people are fighting. Hands, sandals, blood. And Allah's very first word for them — *before* the fight is even mentioned — is **مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ.** *Among the believers.* He does not call them rebels. He does not call them hypocrites. He does not strip the label *muʾminūn* from either side. Even mid-sin, even with blood on the ground, **they keep their faith on the record.**

[PAUSE]

Hold that. We will come back to it, because it changes everything about what justice is allowed to do next.

### Element Two: *Baghā* and *Tafīʾ* — The Sound of Overflowing, and the Sound of Coming Home

Now the verse turns. *Fa-in baghat iḥdāhumā* — "But if one of them transgresses."

I want to teach you to listen with your ears, not just your mind. Because Arabic, more than almost any language, sounds like what it means. And these next two words are a study in opposites — one ugly, one soft — and the ugliness and the softness are the entire point.

First: **بَغَتْ** — *baghat.* And its sister a few words later, **تَبْغِى** — *tabghī.* Same root: *b-gh-y.*

Say it. *Baghā.* That **غ** — the *ghayn* — is a guttural sound, made at the very back of the throat, thick and swelling. The root itself, in the lexicons, carries the idea of *overflowing one's bounds.* A wound that *yabghī* is one that festers and swells past its edges. Water that *baghā* is water that bursts its banks. **Baghy is not just "wrongdoing." It is excess. It is a thing exceeding its proper limit, swelling past where it should stop.** The sound swells in your throat the way the act swells past its boundary. The phonetics carry the sin.

Now notice a tense shift the verse makes — small, surgical, and it decides who you are allowed to fight.

First it says: *fa-in **baghat**.* **Baghat** is the perfect tense — a completed act. *If one of them transgressed.* But when the command to confront comes, the verse does not repeat the perfect. It says: *fa-qātilu-llatī **tabghī**.* "Fight the one that **transgresses**" — and *tabghī* is the **imperfect**, the tense of ongoing, continuous, present action.

[PAUSE]

Feel the precision of that. You do not go to war over a transgression that *happened* and is over — *baghat*, past, done. You confront the party that is *tabghī* — that is **still transgressing, persisting, refusing to stop.** The grammar itself draws the line between punishing a closed offense and stopping an active one. Force is licensed only against the ongoing aggression, not the finished one. The tense is the rule.

And now — the opposite sound. The goal of all this force:

**حَتَّىٰ تَفِىٓءَ** — *ḥattā tafīʾa.* "Until it **returns**."

Say *that.* *Tafīʾ.* After the harsh swelling throat of *baghā*, this word is soft — it lands on a gentle hamza, a quiet catch of breath, and then it opens. The sound itself relaxes. And the meaning is exquisite.

The root is *f-y-ʾ*, and in the lexicons it does not just mean "to return." It means a *particular* kind of returning. *Al-fayʾ* is the **afternoon shade** — the shadow that *comes back* across the ground after the sun has passed its peak and begun to descend. In the morning the shade retreats; in the afternoon it *returns*. That returning shade is called *fayʾ.* The same root gives us *fayʾ* meaning spoils that come back to the community without battle. The whole root is about **something coming back to where it belongs, returning to its rightful place after having turned away.**

So Allah does not say *fight until it is defeated.* He does not say *until it surrenders to you*, or *until it is crushed*, or *until it submits to your authority.* He says: **fight until it returns** — like the shade returns. The aggressor is not an enemy to be destroyed. The aggressor is a believer who has turned away and must be brought **back**.

And back to *what*? The verse is exact: **إِلَىٰٓ أَمْرِ ٱللَّهِ** — *ilā amri-llāh.* "To the **command of Allah**." Not back to *you.* Not back to your tribe's terms. Not back to the winning side's conditions. The destination of the return is *Allah's command* — the Book and the Sunnah, as al-Muyassar and al-Qurṭubī both explain it. The whole purpose of the force was never your victory. It was its reorientation toward God.

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened to the meaning of the fight. In a normal war, the goal is *over there* — defeat the enemy, take the ground. In this verse the goal is *back here* — return the brother to the fold. The force points not outward at a target but homeward at a destination. **You are not fighting to win. You are fighting to bring someone home.**

What this single word — *tafīʾ* — reveals: that even sanctioned force in Islam is not punitive. It is restorative. Its success is not measured by how thoroughly the other is broken, but by whether they *returned*. And the instant they do — *fa-in fāʾat*, "then if it returns" — the licence to fight evaporates in the same breath. The next word is *aṣliḥū.* Make peace. The shade has come back. Lower your hand.

### Element Three: The Two Justices — and the Hidden Hamza That Erases a Wrong

Now look at how the verse ends, because it ends twice — and the doubling is the lesson.

After the aggressor returns, Allah says: **فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمَا بِٱلْعَدْلِ** — *fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bil-ʿadl.* "Make peace between them **with justice**." That's *ʿadl* — the root *ʿ-d-l*, which means balance, evenness, putting things in equilibrium. A scale that hangs level is *ʿādil.*

You'd think that's the conclusion. Reconcile fairly. Done. But it isn't done. The verse adds a *second*, fresh command: **وَأَقْسِطُوٓا۟** — *wa-aqsiṭū.* "And act with **equity**." A different word for justice — *qisṭ*, root *q-s-ṭ.* And then it seals the whole verse with: **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُقْسِطِينَ** — "Indeed, Allah loves the **muqsiṭīn**" — those who do *qisṭ.*

So why two words for justice? Why end on *qisṭ* and not *ʿadl*? They overlap, but classically they aren't identical. *ʿAdl* is justice as inner *balance* — the correct, even state of things; it can be abstract, internal, a fairness you hold in the heart. *Qisṭ* is justice made **visible and distributed** — *qisṭ* is the tangible *share*, the portion you actually hand over so that each party manifestly receives their due. *ʿAdl* is the scale being level. *Qisṭ* is each person walking away holding what is rightfully theirs, where everyone can see it. The verse moves from the inner principle to the public act. *Be balanced — and then make that balance real and visible in what each party receives.*

But now I have to show you something in the word *aqsiṭū* that is one of the quiet marvels of Quranic Arabic.

Here is a fact about this root that will sound impossible. The verb **قَسَطَ** — *qasaṭa*, the plain Form I — does **not** mean "to be just." It means the *opposite.* *Qasaṭa* means **to deviate, to act unjustly, to wrong.** The Quran itself uses it this way: *the deviators* (*al-qāsiṭūn*) in Sūrat al-Jinn are *fuel for Hell.* So the bare root is a word for *injustice.*

How, then, does *aqsiṭū* — built on that same root — mean "**act justly**"?

Because of a single added letter. The hamza at the front — the *a-* of *aqsaṭa* — is what Arabic grammarians call the **hamza of removal** (*hamzat al-salb*, the privative). It is a prefix that *strips the root of its meaning and inverts it.* The way English does with "to dust" — which can mean to *add* dust or to *remove* it. *Aqsaṭa* literally means **to remove the *qasṭ*** — to take away the injustice. To de-injustice. To strip the wrong out.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that does to the verse.

The final command in this entire scene of fighting and blood is not merely *"be fair."* Built into the very morphology of the word is: **remove the injustice. Strip the wrong out of the situation. De-deviate it.** And the people Allah says He *loves* — the *muqsiṭīn* — are, at the level of the word's own bones, **the people who go in and take the injustice out.** Not the people who avoided injustice. The people who actively *removed* it.

And mark the last astonishment. This verse has just walked us through betrayal, transgression, organized violence between believers. After all of that, the very last word is not *fear* and not *punishment*. It is **يُحِبُّ** — *yuḥibbu.* "Allah **loves**." Out of a verse about civil war, Allah extracts a statement of His *love* — and He attaches that love specifically to the ones who stepped in and pulled the injustice out by its roots.

[PAUSE]

What these closing words reveal: that the point of the whole exercise — the peacemaking, the bounded force, the return — was never just to *stop* the bad. It was to *restore the good so completely* that the wrong is actually *removed*, visibly, with each party holding their due. And that act of removal is something Allah does not merely permit. He **loves** it.

### The Bridge

Step back and see what the words have shown us.

The grammar refused to erase anyone — *believers* even mid-fight, *many* in the sin and *two* in the healing. The sounds told the story — the swelling throat of *baghā*, the soft homecoming of *tafīʾ.* And the ending turned a scene of violence into a statement of divine *love* for those who actively strip injustice out.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how we are meant to *live* inside a community of imperfect, fighting, beloved human beings.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Peace Is Not Neutrality — The Verse Is Addressed to *You*

Let me start where you already live.

You've seen it. Maybe a thread blows up between two people you both know. Maybe two relatives stop speaking and the whole family quietly chooses sides — or quietly chooses *no* side, which feels safer. Maybe two friends fracture and you tell yourself the wise, mature thing: *it's not my business. I'm staying out of it. I don't want to get involved.*

We have built an entire modern ethic around that instinct and we call it maturity. *Stay in your lane. Don't get involved. Not my circus.* Psychologists have a name for the cousin of this instinct: the **bystander effect** — the well-documented finding that the *more* people witness someone in trouble, the *less* likely any single one of them is to act, because everyone assumes someone else will. The crowd diffuses the responsibility until it evaporates. Everyone watches. No one moves.

Now go back to the verse and ask the question we usually skip: **who is being commanded?**

*Fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā* — "**make peace** between the two of them." That's a command in the plural. *You all, do this.* But to *whom* is it spoken? Read it carefully. It is **not** addressed to the two fighting parties. They are referred to in the third person — *them*, *between the two of them*, *one of them*, *the other.* They are spoken *about.* The ones spoken *to* — the ones carrying the imperative *aṣliḥū* — are someone else entirely.

Al-Muyassar makes it explicit, inserting the addressee right into the meaning: *"make peace — **O believers** — between them."* The command falls on **the rest of the community. The witnesses. The bystanders.** You.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight shift?

We instinctively read a verse about a fight as being *about the fighters.* It isn't. It's about *us* — the third party, the ones who could so easily say *not my business.* Allah looks past the two who are swinging and lays the duty squarely on the shoulders of everyone watching. **In the economy of this verse, the neutral bystander does not exist.** There is no morally clean place to stand outside the conflict. Either you are a peacemaker, or you are someone who watched believers bleed and chose comfort.

And notice — this is not optional advice. *Aṣliḥū* is a command, an imperative, Form IV — *to actively bring about* reconciliation. Not "wish them well." Not "hope it works out." *Go and make the peace.* The reconciliation does not generate itself; someone has to *cause* it. The grammar puts that someone in the second person and points at you.

Now feel the tension this creates, because it's real. Getting involved is *dangerous.* You might get it wrong. You might get hurt. You might be accused of taking sides. Staying out is *safe.* This is exactly why the bystander effect is so powerful — non-involvement always feels like the responsible choice. And Allah, who knows the human heart completely, *overrides* that instinct directly. He does not say *get involved if it's safe* or *if you're qualified* or *if they ask you to.* He simply says: *aṣliḥū.* Make the peace. The default is engagement. The burden of proof is on walking away.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the specific application. The next time two people you know — two friends, two relatives, two people in your masjid — fracture, notice the voice that says *stay out of it, it's not your business.* And recognize that voice for what this verse says it is: not wisdom, but the bystander's exit. Then ask the harder question this verse demands: *What is the one small thing I could do to bring them back toward each other?* A call. A meal. A carried message. A refusal to repeat the gossip that's feeding the fire. You don't have to fix it all. But the verse forbids you the luxury of doing nothing.

[PAUSE]

There is a reason the very next verse, 49:10, says: *"The believers are but brothers, so make peace between your two brothers."* The peacemaker isn't a meddler. The peacemaker is doing the work of *brotherhood itself.*

### Theme Two: The Point of the Fight Is the Return — Justice That Heals Instead of Devours

Let me start, again, with something you know in your bones.

Someone wrongs you. Badly. Clearly — there's no ambiguity, you are in the right and they are in the wrong. And something rises up in you. What does it want? Be honest. It doesn't just want the wrong to *stop.* It wants them to *pay.* It wants them diminished. It wants the satisfaction of their defeat. We have a whole modern machinery for this now — we *cancel*, we *expose*, we *ratio*, we pile on. The goal is rarely "bring them back." The goal is to *win*, and winning means *they lose, visibly, permanently.*

Now I want you to watch what this verse does with a situation where there genuinely *is* a wrongdoer. Because this is not a verse about a misunderstanding. *Baghat iḥdāhumā* — one of them has genuinely transgressed. There is a real aggressor. Allah *names* the wrong. He does not do the cheap thing of saying "both sides are equally at fault." One party is *tabghī* — actively, persistently in the wrong — and the verse licenses *force* against them: *fa-qātilū.* So this is the hard case. The case where you'd most expect the licence to crush.

And what does Allah do with that licence?

He bounds it on *every single side.*

First, look at the word for the fighting itself. When the verse describes the believers' sin, it used *iqtatalū* and *qātilū* — but notice the command against the aggressor is **فَقَـٰتِلُوا۟**, *qātilū* — Form III. Form III in Arabic is the form of *mutual engagement* — *to fight against, to engage in combat with.* It is **not** *uqtulū* — *qatala*, the Form I that simply means *to kill.* Allah does not say "kill the transgressors." He says "engage them in combat" — a fight aimed at *subduing the aggression*, not at *ending the lives.* The word itself withholds the licence to slaughter. Even here, the violence is measured.

Second — and we saw it already, but now feel its full force — the *terminus.* *Ḥattā tafīʾ* — "**until** it returns." The fight has a built-in expiry, and the expiry is not the enemy's destruction. It is their *return.* The shade coming home. The very instant the aggressor turns back toward the command of Allah, the war is *over* — *fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū.* "Then if it returns, **make peace**." You don't get to finish them off. You don't get to extract the last ounce of payment. The moment they come back, your hand drops and you are commanded — *commanded* — to reconcile with them.

[PAUSE]

Think about how violently that cuts against the grain of the wronged heart. The aggressor — the one who was clearly, namedly in the wrong — *returns*, and your instruction is not "make them grovel," not "remember what they did," but *aṣliḥū bil-ʿadl.* Make peace. With justice. As though they had never strayed.

And third — the bracketing. Look at the architecture of the whole verse. It *opens* with *aṣliḥū* — make peace. It *closes* with *aṣliḥū* — make peace. The force, the fighting, the *qātilū* — all of it sits in the *middle*, completely enclosed on both sides by the command to reconcile. **Peace is the frame. Violence is only ever the regrettable interior, bounded above and below by the command to heal.** Force is never the destination in this verse. It is a corridor you pass through, as briefly as possible, to get from one peace to another.

This is what we so rarely manage. We think justice and mercy are opposites — that to be *just* about a wrong is to *withhold* mercy, and to be *merciful* is to go soft on justice. This verse fuses them. The justice here *is* the mercy. The *qisṭ* — remember, the active *removal* of injustice — is itself the means of restoring the relationship. You don't reconcile by *ignoring* the wrong. You reconcile by *removing* it, cleanly, so that what's left between the two parties is no longer poisoned. **Real reconciliation requires real justice — and real justice aims at restoration, not revenge.**

And then the final seal, which we should never stop being amazed by: *inna-llāha yuḥibbu-l-muqsiṭīn.* "Allah loves those who do *qisṭ.*" Allah does not say He loves those who *win.* He does not say He loves the *victorious* or the *strong* or even the *patient.* He says He loves the ones who went in and *pulled the injustice out* — who refused both to ignore the wrong *and* to feast on the wrongdoer — who held the scale level until each party walked away holding their due.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the specific application. The next time you are genuinely wronged — and you *know* you're in the right — notice the part of you that wants more than the wrong to *stop.* The part that wants them to *pay.* And measure that part against this verse. Ask: *Am I trying to win, or am I trying to bring this back to rightness?* Because the verse tells you precisely when your "fight" is supposed to end — not when you feel avenged, but the moment the other turns back toward what is right. *Ḥattā tafīʾ.* Until they come home. And the instant they do, your job changes from *resistance* to *aṣliḥū.* The hardest, holiest discipline in this verse is knowing when to lower your hand.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we've done the work, look at the shape of the whole verse — because it is built as a perfect arc, and the arc *is* the argument.

It opens with **peace** — *aṣliḥū.*
It moves to **diagnosis** — *baghat*, one has transgressed.
It permits **bounded force** — *qātilū*, fight, but only the one persisting, only as engagement, not slaughter.
It sets the **single goal** — *ḥattā tafīʾ*, until it returns home to Allah's command.
And then it descends the other side of the arc, symmetrically, back to **peace** — *fa-aṣliḥū* — now deepened into **justice**, *bil-ʿadl*, and then **active equity**, *aqsiṭū.*
And it lands on **love** — *yuḥibbu-l-muqsiṭīn.*

Peace → conflict → return → peace → love. The violence is buried in the exact center, walled in on both sides. The verse begins by commanding reconciliation and ends by telling you that reconciliation is something *God Himself loves.* **The structure tells you that in the divine vision, conflict is always the middle of the story — never the beginning, never the end.**

### Bringing It Together

See how the two journeys — the words and the themes — were the same insight from two directions.

The **grammar** that kept calling them *believers* mid-fight, and counted them as *two* the moment peace was on the table — that was the same truth as the **theme** that the goal of all force is *the return*: Allah never writes anyone off, so neither may you.

And the **word** *aqsiṭū* — with its hidden hamza that means *remove the injustice* — was the same truth as the **theme** that real reconciliation isn't pretending the wrong didn't happen, but actively pulling it out by the root. The morphology and the meaning were teaching the identical lesson: *justice that heals.*

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life right now is there a fracture between two people I know — and what is the voice that's telling me to stay out of it actually protecting?
2. When I am wronged, can I tell the difference between wanting the wrong to *stop* and wanting the wrongdoer to *suffer*?
3. Have I ever mistaken *staying neutral* for *staying clean* — when the verse says there is no clean ground outside the work of peace?
4. When someone who wronged me *turns back* — do I let my hand drop, or do I keep extracting payment after the fight is already won?
5. Am I a person who merely *avoids* injustice, or one who *goes in and removes* it — the kind Allah says He loves?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When believers come to blows, Allah refuses to revoke their faith, lays the duty of peace on everyone watching, bounds every act of force by the single aim of bringing the wrongdoer *home* rather than defeating him — and crowns the whole scene not with victory but with His love for those who actively strip the injustice out.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what this verse invites — not demands, invites. That you stop seeing yourself as a spectator to the conflicts around you. That you let go of the comfortable lie that staying out of it is the mature thing. That when you must confront a real wrong, you hold in your heart the word *tafīʾ* — and remember that your aim was never to win, but to bring someone home. And that you become, in whatever small theater of life you've been given, one of the *muqsiṭīn* — one of the ones who walks toward the broken thing and gently, justly, pulls the injustice out.

Because that is the person Allah says He loves.

And having told us that You love the muqsiṭīn — O Allah, make us among them.*
