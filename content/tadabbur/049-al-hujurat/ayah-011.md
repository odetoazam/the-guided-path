# The Hidden Scale: Why Mockery Is a Wound You Inflict on Yourself

## Introduction: The Room You Already Know

> يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا يَسْخَرْ قَوْمٌ مِّن قَوْمٍ عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَكُونُوا۟ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ
>
> *Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā yaskhar qawmun min qawmin ʿasā an yakūnū khayran minhum...*
>
> **"O you who believe! Let no people ridicule another people — perhaps they are better than them..."**

Before we open a single word, I want you to walk into a room.

It's a gathering — a majlis, a classroom, a workplace, a family WhatsApp group, a comment section. People are together. And somewhere in that room, something small happens. A glance. A smirk. A clever little phrase at someone's expense, and the laugh that follows. A nickname that started as a joke and never went away. Someone walks in, and a quiet ripple moves through the room — *that* one. The one we've all silently agreed to find slightly ridiculous.

You know this room. Every human being who has ever lived knows this room. Because the instinct to climb by stepping on someone else is one of the oldest movements of the human soul.

Now here is what I want you to hold onto as we begin. This ayah was revealed into a community that was, by every measure we have, the best generation of believers who ever lived. The companions of the Prophet ﷺ. People who had given up everything — homes, wealth, tribes, safety — for the sake of Allah. And *even there*, in that room, Allah saw this happening. People mocking people. Tongues finding the soft target. Nicknames that stung.

[PAUSE]

So this is not a verse for "bad people." There's no comfort here of saying *that's not me*. This was revealed for the believers — `yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū` — and it was revealed because the believers needed it.

And here is the hook, the thing I want you hungry to understand before we go in:

When Allah forbids this mockery, He does not say *be nice*. He does not appeal to manners. He does something far stranger and far deeper. **He tells the mocker a secret about reality itself** — a secret hidden inside one tiny word — and then He tells the mocker something about *who they are actually wounding* that should stop the tongue cold.

The person you are looking down on may be standing higher than you on a scale you cannot see. And the insult you throw at them — grammatically, in the very structure of the sentence — lands on *you*.

Let's go find out how.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word That Demolishes Certainty — *ʿasā* (عَسَىٰ)

#### First, let me teach you the tool

In any language, there are words that *open* a sentence and words that *close* it. A word like "definitely" closes a door — it ends the conversation. A word like "perhaps" leaves the door swinging.

In Arabic there is a small word, `عَسَىٰ` (*ʿasā*), and it belongs to a family of words the grammarians call verbs of *hope* and *expectation* — `أفعال الرجاء`. It means something like *perhaps, it may well be, there is reason to expect that...* It introduces a possibility that is alive — not idle speculation, but a genuine, weighted maybe.

Now hold that everyday tool in your hand, and let's watch what Allah does with it.

#### Open the word

Look at the shape of the sentence:

> لَا يَسْخَرْ قَوْمٌ مِّن قَوْمٍ — **عَسَىٰٓ** أَن يَكُونُوا۟ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ
>
> "Let no people ridicule another people — **perhaps** they are better than them."

The prohibition comes first: *don't mock*. And then, immediately, before you can even ask *why not?* — Allah hands you the reason in a single word. `ʿasā`. **Perhaps.**

Let's sit with this. Because the placement is everything.

#### Why "perhaps" and not "they are"

Notice something. Allah does *not* say: "Do not mock them, because they are better than you." That would have been a different verse — a verse about *those specific people* being superior.

He says: *Do not mock them — because perhaps they are better than you.*

Why the *perhaps*?

Here is what's remarkable. The word `ʿasā` does not describe *them*. It describes **the limit of your knowledge.** It is not a statement about the person you're mocking. It is a statement about *you* — about the wall at the edge of your vision.

When you mock someone, what are you actually doing? You are making a judgment. You are placing them *below* you on a scale. And to do that, you have to believe — at some level, in that instant — that you *know* where they stand. That you can see the scale. That your eyes have measured them and found them lacking.

And Allah drops one word into that certainty and shatters it. `ʿasā`. *Perhaps.* You don't know. You cannot see the scale.

[PAUSE]

#### The hidden scale

Stay with me, because this is the heart of it.

There are two scales in this world. There is the scale *we* can see — the visible one. Wealth. Beauty. Eloquence. Status. Strength. The accent, the clothes, the body, the job, the family name. This is the scale on which mockery operates. The mocker looks at the visible scale, sees someone "lower," and laughs.

But there is another scale. `خَيْرًا` — *better*. And the classical commentators are unanimous on what *better* means here: better **with Allah** (*khayran ʿindallāh*). Al-Jalalayn says it plainly: `عسى أن يكونوا خيرا منهم عند الله` — perhaps they are better than them *in the sight of Allah*. Al-Muyassar makes it concrete: perhaps the one mocked is better than the one mocking.

And the scale of `ʿindallāh` — what's *on* it? Sincerity. A heart that breaks before its Lord at night. A patience no one applauds. A quiet act of mercy with no witness. A taqwā buried so deep that even the person carrying it doesn't fully see it.

That scale is **invisible**. Completely. You cannot read it off a person's face or their clothes or their grammar. The poorest, plainest, most-mocked person in that room may be carrying a weight of light on the hidden scale that would make the mocker's whole life feel weightless by comparison.

So when Allah says `ʿasā` — *perhaps* — He is not being vague. He is being *precise*. He is telling you: the only scale that matters is one you are structurally incapable of reading. Your mockery is a measurement taken with a broken instrument, of a quantity you cannot detect.

[PAUSE]

The mocker laughs because he thinks he knows. `ʿasā` is Allah saying: *you have no idea.*

This single word should be enough to silence the tongue forever. To mock another human being, you have to be certain you're above them. And you can never, ever be certain. The door is always swinging.

> **What this one word reveals:** Mockery is built on a certainty no human being is allowed to have. `ʿasā` removes the floor from underneath contempt.

### Element Two: The Knife That Turns in Your Own Hand — *talmizū anfusakum* (تَلْمِزُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَكُمْ)

#### First, the tool

Every language has a way of pointing a verb back at the one who does it. *I cut the bread* points outward. *I cut myself* — the action curves back and lands on the doer. Grammarians call the object in that second sentence a **reflexive object**. The arrow leaves and returns to the archer.

Hold that. Now watch.

#### Open the words

The verse moves from mockery to a second sin:

> وَلَا تَلْمِزُوٓا۟ **أَنفُسَكُمْ**
>
> "And do not *lamz* — **yourselves**."

The verb `تَلْمِزُوٓا۟` (*talmizū*), from the root `ل-م-ز` (lamz), means to defame, to find fault, to stab with a look or a word — a sharper, often more face-to-face cruelty than backbiting. It's the cutting remark, the pointed criticism designed to wound. (It's the same root behind `لُمَزَة` — *lumazah* — the habitual fault-finder that Allah curses in Sūrat al-Humazah, 104:1.)

So far, expected. But now look at the object. *Who* are you forbidden to defame?

`أَنفُسَكُمْ`. **Yourselves.**

[PAUSE]

#### Read what's there — and what's missing

Stop here. Something is strange.

The natural sentence — the one we *expect* — would be *"do not defame one another."* Do not defame each other. That's the social instruction. That's what the verse is obviously *about*.

But that is not what Allah said. Allah said: *do not defame **yourselves**.* `anfusakum` — your own selves.

And this is no accident of phrasing. The classical commentators stop here too. Al-Jalalayn glosses it directly: `لا تعيبوا فتعابوا، أي لا يعب بعضكم بعضا` — "do not find fault, lest you be found at fault; that is, let none of you defame another." Al-Muyassar: `ولا يَعِبْ بعضكم بعضًا` — let none of you defame any other.

So the *meaning* is "do not defame one another." Everyone agrees. **But Allah chose to express that meaning using the word "yourselves."**

Why?

#### The community as one body

Here is the door this opens.

Allah is telling you a truth about who you *are* — about what the believers, together, actually constitute. When you defame your brother, the grammar insists: **you are defaming yourself.** Not metaphorically. The verse treats the believing community as a single self — `nafs` — one body, one soul. The arrow you fire at him leaves your bow and lands in your own chest, because, on the level Allah is describing, *he is you.*

The Prophet ﷺ taught this same reality in another image: the believers, in their mutual love and mercy and compassion, are like **one body** — when one limb suffers, the whole body responds with fever and sleeplessness. Al-Hujurat's grammar *is* that hadith, compressed into a single pronoun. `anfusakum`. The hand does not mock the foot. The eye does not despise the knee. They are one self.

[PAUSE]

Let's go deeper still. There is a second layer, and the commentators name it too.

`لا تعيبوا فتعابوا` — *do not defame, lest you be defamed.* The arrow that leaves your mouth has a way of coming back. The one you mock today mocks you tomorrow. The culture of contempt you build is the culture you will live inside. You are sharpening the very knife that will find you. When you make it normal to cut people down, you have made yourself a target in a room full of newly sharpened tongues. *You are defaming yourself* — your own future, your own standing, your own peace.

And there is a third, deepest layer. The one who lives by *lamz* — by fault-finding, by the cutting word — *disfigures his own soul.* Every act of contempt is a stroke that hardens the heart, that feeds the arrogance, that corrodes the very self that commits it. The mocker thinks he is doing damage to the mocked. The truth Allah embeds in the grammar is that the deepest wound is always self-inflicted. `talmizū anfusakum` — you are stabbing *yourself.*

> **What this one pronoun reveals:** There is no such thing as "putting someone else down." On the scale of the *ummah* as one body, on the scale of consequence, and on the scale of the soul — every blow you aim outward lands on you. Allah didn't say "each other." He said "yourselves." Believe Him.

### Element Three: The Verb That Needs a Partner — *tanābazū* (تَنَابَزُوا۟)

#### First, the tool

Here is one of the quiet wonders of Arabic. The language can take a root and reshape it to mean *people doing something back and forth to each other.* This is **Form VI** — the pattern `تَفَاعَلَ` (*tafāʿala*) — the form of **mutual, reciprocal action**.

`كَتَبَ` (*kataba*) — he wrote. `تَكَاتَبَ` (*takātaba*) — they corresponded *with each other.* `قَاتَلَ` — he fought. `تَقَاتَلَ` — they fought *one another.* The very *shape* of the word — that long *alif* after the first letter, that opening `ta-* prefix — encodes a back-and-forth. It takes two. It builds a loop.

Hold the shape of that loop in your mind. Now look at the verse.

#### Open the word

> وَلَا **تَنَابَزُوا۟** بِٱلْأَلْقَـٰبِ
>
> "And do not **call each other** by [offensive] nicknames."

`تَنَابَزُوا۟` (*tanābazū*), from the root `ن-ب-ز` (nabz) — to label with a degrading name, an insulting tag. And it is — confirmed in the morphology — **Form VI.** The reciprocal form. The *back-and-forth* form.

#### Why the form is the meaning

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not use Form I — *do not name someone with a bad nickname* — a single act, one person tagging another. He chose Form VI: `tanābazū`. *Do not nickname **one another** back and forth.*

Let's sit with why this is so precise.

A nickname, the cruel kind, is almost never a single event. It's a **system.** You tag me, so I tag you. Your circle has a name for that family; their circle has a name right back. It escalates. It becomes the air everyone breathes. It builds a *loop* — and Form VI is the grammar of the loop. The word's very shape diagnoses the disease: this is not one person's sin, it is a *machinery* that two or more people build together and keep running.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's beautiful about the prohibition. To break a Form VI verb, you don't need everyone to stop. **You need one person to step out of the loop.** A reciprocal action *requires* a partner. The moment one side refuses to return the tag — refuses to fire back, refuses to laugh, refuses to pass it on — the loop has no second half. It collapses. `tanābazū` cannot continue if you simply will not play your part in the `tafāʿul`.

So the grammar doesn't just forbid the sin. It shows you the exit. *Be the one who breaks the loop.*

#### And then — the name that replaces the name

Look at how Allah closes this:

> بِئْسَ ٱلِٱسْمُ ٱلْفُسُوقُ بَعْدَ ٱلْإِيمَـٰنِ
>
> "Wretched is the *name* — *corruption* — after faith."

Do you see the turn? They were busy handing out names — `الْأَلْقَاب`, nicknames, labels for each other. And Allah says: there is only one name worth worrying about. While you were branding your brother with a tag, *you* were earning a name. And the name you earn through this — `الْفُسُوقُ`, *fusūq*, rebellion, corruption — is `بِئْسَ`, **wretched, vile.** What a wretched name to wear *after* you had been given the name "believer."

The labeler gets labeled. They reached for `الْأَلْقَاب` to define someone below them, and the only `اسم` — the only *name* — that actually got assigned in the exchange was the one Allah pinned on *the mocker*: `فُسُوق`. The arrow returns again. (Notice — this is `talmizū anfusakum` all over, from a new direction.)

> **What this one verb-form reveals:** Cruelty is rarely solitary — it's a loop two people build and feed. Form VI both names the machinery and reveals its weakness: refuse to be the second half, and the whole thing dies. And the only name that sticks at the end of it is the one you wrote on yourself.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Step back and look at what these three words have shown us.

`ʿasā` told us we are **blind to the only scale that counts.**

`anfusakum` told us every blow aimed outward **lands on ourselves.**

`tanābazū` told us cruelty is a **loop** — and that one refusal breaks it.

Three words. Three dismantlings of the contempt reflex, from three different angles. But these are not just facts about Arabic grammar. They are facts about *reality* — about the unseen architecture of how souls relate, how consequences travel, how Allah actually weighs a human being.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way we live, every single day.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Contempt Reflex — Why We Build Ourselves Up by Tearing Others Down

#### Start where you live

Let me start somewhere uncomfortable, because it's true for all of us.

Have you ever noticed how *good* it feels — in a small, sharp, slightly shameful way — to be part of the group that's laughing *at* someone? There's a warmth to it. A belonging. When the room finds someone ridiculous together, the people doing the mocking feel suddenly, momentarily, *closer* to each other and *higher* than before.

Psychologists have a clinical name for this. They call it **downward social comparison.** The human ego, when it feels unsure of its own worth, has a shortcut available: instead of building yourself *up* — which is slow, and hard, and humbling — you can simply find someone to place *below* you. And in that instant, by pure contrast, you rise. You don't have to climb. You just have to push someone down and stand on the difference.

This is the engine under mockery. It is almost never really about the person being mocked. It is about the mocker's own hunger to feel elevated, to feel safe, to feel *in.* The mocked person is just the raw material.

[PAUSE]

#### Now let the ayah illuminate it

And into that exact mechanism, Allah inserts `ʿasā`.

Because watch what `ʿasā` does to downward comparison. The entire trick depends on one assumption: *that I am, in fact, above them.* That the scale reads the way I think it reads. The whole warm rush of superiority is borrowed against a measurement I have not actually taken and *cannot* take.

`ʿasā` calls in the loan. *Perhaps they are better than you.* And suddenly the ground you were standing on — the imagined "higher" position — was never solid. You built a feeling of elevation on a scale you can't read. You were standing on air.

Think about who the companions actually mocked, according to the narrations. Al-Jalalayn reports the verse came down regarding people who looked down on poor Muslims — companions like `عَمَّار` and `صُهَيْب`. The *poor* ones. The ones low on the *visible* scale. And these were among the most beloved to Allah and His Messenger — souls of staggering weight on the *hidden* scale. The mockers had the scale **exactly upside down.** They were laughing *up* at people they thought they were laughing *down* at.

[PAUSE]

That is the terror and the mercy of `ʿasā`. Every time you feel that little rush of superiority, the honest response is: *I might have the scale upside down right now.* The person I find slightly ridiculous might be the reason this gathering is not punished. The one I'd never sit next to might be carried to the highest place while I'm left wondering where my deeds went.

#### Go deeper — the modern accelerant

Now bring this into our actual world, because something has changed.

Downward comparison used to be limited by the size of the room. You could only mock the people physically near you, and they could see your face, and there was a cost. But we have built machines — feeds, comment sections, group chats, quote-tweets — whose *entire business model* is the contempt reflex at planetary scale. The pile-on. The viral humiliation. The screenshot passed around to be laughed at. The nickname that becomes a meme and follows a human being for years.

We have industrialized `tanābuz`. We have made the loop frictionless and infinite. And the rush of being on the *right* side of the mockery — part of the laughing majority — is now available to you twenty times before breakfast.

And to every single instance of it, this ancient verse says the same three things. *Perhaps they are better than you.* That blow lands on **your** soul. Step out of the loop.

#### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical edge — and let me make it specific, not vague.

**The next time you feel the pull to join the laughter — at someone in the room, someone in a video, someone in a thread — let `ʿasā` interrupt you.** Train one sentence into your reflex: *I cannot see the scale.* Before the smirk, before the share, before the comment: *perhaps, with Allah, this person I'm about to step on is standing higher than me.*

You will be wrong about people's true rank far more often than you imagine. `ʿasā` is Allah's mercy to you — a chance to not be caught laughing at the people He loves.

### Theme Two: The Name You Wear — Identity After Faith

#### Start where you live

We are obsessed with names. With labels. With identity.

Think about how much of modern life is the assigning and defending of labels. *He's a this. She's one of those. That whole group is —.* We compress entire human beings into a tag, and once the tag is on, we stop seeing the person. The nickname, the category, the reputation — it becomes a cage we build around someone, and they live inside our label of them whether it fits or not.

And we know, from the inside, how it feels to be labeled. To have someone reduce the whole ocean of who you are to one cruel word. To be known in a room by a name you never chose and would give anything to escape. There are people walking around right now carrying a nickname from when they were fourteen years old that still makes them flinch.

This verse is *intimately* about that wound. Among the narrations al-Baghawi preserves, the prohibition on nicknames came down in connection with `ثَابِت بْن قَيْس` — a companion who was hard of hearing — and a moment of names and labels causing real hurt among the believers. (The early commentators preserve more than one occasion here, and we should hold them as a cluster of light on the same verse rather than a single settled story.) The point is constant across all of them: Allah saw a *name* wound a believer, and He legislated against it.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now watch where Allah takes the conversation about names.

> بِئْسَ ٱلِٱسْمُ ٱلْفُسُوقُ بَعْدَ ٱلْإِيمَـٰنِ
>
> "Wretched is the name — *corruption* — after faith."

The whole human game is *assigning* names to others. Allah flips the camera around. He says: stop worrying about the names you're handing out, and worry about **the name you're earning.**

Because here is the unseen reality. While you were busy labeling your brother, a name was being written over *you*. Not by people — by Allah. And the question that should terrify and orient us is not *what do they call me?* It is *what does Allah call me?*

[PAUSE]

And look at the heartbreak compressed into `بَعْدَ ٱلْإِيمَـٰنِ` — **after faith.**

You were given the most beautiful name a creature can wear. `مُؤْمِن`. *Believer.* Allah Himself called you that — `yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū`, the verse *opens* by addressing you with your noble name. And then, through the cheap pleasure of a mocking word, you trade it. You set down "believer" and pick up `فُسُوق` — corruption, rebellion. `بِئْسَ` — what a wretched, what a vile exchange.

It's the image of a person handed a royal title who insists on being known by an insult instead. *After* you had `īmān`. *After* you'd been named by the King. You chose the gutter-name for the sake of a laugh.

#### Go deeper — the door He leaves open

But do not miss the mercy at the very end, because the verse does not close the door:

> وَمَن لَّمْ يَتُبْ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ
>
> "And whoever does not repent — it is *they* who are the wrongdoers."

Read the condition carefully. It is not *whoever does this is a wrongdoer.* It is *whoever does this **and does not turn back** (`lam yatub`).* The verb `تَابَ` — to repent, to turn around and come back — is held open as the escape from `ظَالِم`, the wrongdoer's name.

Do you see the symmetry? The whole ayah is about *names* and *labels* — the ones we slap on each other. And it ends by saying: there is exactly one label that is permanent, `الظَّالِمُونَ`, the wrongdoers — and even *that* one is escapable, right up until you refuse to repent. The only nickname that ever truly sticks to a soul is the one it refuses to wash off.

[PAUSE]

So Allah, in a verse condemning the casual assigning of names, reserves the heaviest name — *wrongdoer* — and then, with `wa man lam yatub`, hands you the eraser before He's even finished the sentence. Mockery says: *you are this, forever, and I get to decide.* Allah says: *no name is final but the one you cling to. Turn back, and the wretched name lifts off.*

#### What this changes tomorrow

Here is the specific, practical shift.

**Tonight, think of one name you have assigned to a person or a group — a tag you carry them in.** A label that lets you dismiss them without seeing them. And ask: *am I sure? `ʿasā` — perhaps they are better than me. Have I caged a believer in my own cruelty?* Loosen it. Let the person back out of the box.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what the linguistic discoveries and the themes turned out to be — the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammar taught us three things:
- `ʿasā` — **you cannot read the scale.**
- `anfusakum` — **the blow lands on you.**
- `tanābazū` — **it's a loop, and you can break it.**

The themes took those same three truths and walked them into our lives:
- The contempt reflex feeds on a superiority we cannot actually verify — `ʿasā` is the verse that pulls the floor out from under it.
- We are obsessed with the names we *give*; Allah redirects us to the name we *earn* — and `anfusakum` already warned us that what we do to others is done to ourselves.
- And the whole machinery of mockery — `tanābuz`, the loop — has a single exit: repentance, `tawba`, the refusal to keep playing, the turning back before the name becomes permanent.

There is an architecture here. The verse opens by calling you `الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا` — *the believers*, your noble name. It closes by warning that the unrepentant become `الظَّالِمُونَ` — *the wrongdoers*, the wretched name. And the entire space between those two names is filled with the small cruelties of the tongue — *sukhriyya, lamz, tanābuz* — the exact behaviors that carry a soul from the first name to the second. The verse *is* the journey between two names, and it shows you, in its own structure, both how the fall happens and exactly where the door back is.

### One-Sentence Distillation

You cannot see the scale that weighs souls, the contempt you fling at others lands on your own self, and the only name that ever truly sticks is the one you refuse to repent from — so guard your tongue, because the one you are mocking may be the beloved of Allah.

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not asking you to be polite. Politeness manages the surface. This is asking you to undergo a quiet revolution in how you *see* — to walk into every room carrying `ʿasā` like a lamp, suspecting hidden greatness in exactly the people the world has agreed to overlook. To feel the unity of `anfusakum` so deeply that mocking a brother becomes as unthinkable as biting your own hand. To be the one who breaks the loop.

Not because you owe anyone manners. Because Allah has shown you, in eleven gentle and devastating words, that contempt is built on a lie about reality — and the believer is someone who refuses to live by lies.
