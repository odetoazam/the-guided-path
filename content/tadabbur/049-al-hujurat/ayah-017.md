# The Favor You Cannot Do

### Surah Al-Hujurat, Ayah 17

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with the sound of them before we touch the meaning.

> **يَمُنُّونَ عَلَيْكَ أَنْ أَسْلَمُوا ۖ قُل لَّا تَمُنُّوا عَلَيَّ إِسْلَامَكُم ۖ بَلِ اللَّهُ يَمُنُّ عَلَيْكُمْ أَنْ هَدَاكُمْ لِلْإِيمَانِ إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ**

*Yamunnūna ʿalayka an aslamū. Qul lā tamunnū ʿalayya islāmakum. Bal Allāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum an hadākum lil-īmān, in kuntum ṣādiqīn.*

> "They consider it a favor to you that they have submitted. Say: Do not count your submission to me as a favor. Rather, it is Allah who has done *you* the favor, in that He guided you to faith — if you are truthful."

### The world this came into

To feel this ayah, you have to stand in a particular moment in Madinah.

Islam is no longer a persecuted whisper. It has become the rising power of the peninsula. And when power rises, something changes in the kind of people who come toward it. In the early Makkan years, people accepted Islam and lost everything — their families, their safety, their standing. Coming to faith was a way to *bleed*.

But now, in the later Madinan period, tribes from the surrounding desert — the *aʿrāb*, the Bedouin Arabs — begin arriving in numbers. And some of them come with a different posture entirely. They come, and they *let the Prophet know* what they have done. The classical commentators paint the scene vividly: they came reminding him, *we came to you with our households and our heavy burdens; we did not fight you the way the other tribes fought you; we handed ourselves over without you having to lift a sword against us.* As al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi preserve it — they came counting it up.

Picture it. Someone stands before the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and the unspoken message is: *You owe us. We gave you our submission. Notice it. Be grateful.*

[PAUSE]

Now — the surah they are standing inside of is **Surah Al-Hujurat**, the surah of *adab*, of refined conduct, of how a soul carries itself before Allah and before His Messenger. The whole surah has been training the believers in the inner manners of faith: lower your voices, verify before you act, don't mock, don't backbite, don't be suspicious. And just three verses earlier, in verse 14, Allah had already drawn a quiet, surgical line through these same desert Arabs: *they said "we believe" — tell them, you have not believed; rather say "we have submitted," for faith has not yet entered your hearts.*

So when we arrive at verse 17, the ground is already prepared. The question of the surah has become: **what does the heart actually look like when it stands before God? And what does it look like when it only thinks it does?**

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hold as we begin.

In this one short ayah, Allah is going to take an arrow — an arrow of obligation that these people have aimed *outward*, from themselves toward God and His Messenger — and He is going to turn it completely around. The same word will be used three times. The first two times it will be in *their* mouths. The third time it will be in *His*.

And by the time that word has been spoken for the third time, the entire universe of who-owes-whom will have been silently, devastatingly inverted.

Let's go find out how.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that humming root won't stop saying — *mann*

#### Teaching the concept first

Before we open the ayah, I want to teach you something about a particular human gesture — because Arabic has a single word for it, and that word is the engine of this entire verse.

Think of a kind of giving that *poisons itself*.

Imagine someone helps you. They lend you money in a hard month. Genuine help. But then — a week later, a year later — they bring it up. "Remember when I helped you?" And then again. And again. Every time you slightly disappoint them, the favor resurfaces. "After everything I did for you." The gift is never allowed to die into the past and become pure. It is kept alive on a ledger, and the ledger is used as leverage.

We all know this gesture. We have all received it. Some of us, if we're honest, have *done* it.

There is a word in Arabic that means exactly this: to do someone a kindness and then *hold it over them* — to remind them of it in a way that wounds, to convert a gift into a debt, to make the receiver feel small so that the giver can feel large.

That word is **مَنّ — *mann***, from the root **م-ن-ن (mīm-nūn-nūn)**.

#### Opening the word

Now listen to the ayah again, and count.

> **يَمُنُّونَ** عَلَيْكَ أَنْ أَسْلَمُوا ۖ قُل لَّا **تَمُنُّوا** عَلَيَّ إِسْلَامَكُم ۖ بَلِ اللَّهُ **يَمُنُّ** عَلَيْكُمْ

*Yamunnūna… lā tamunnū… Allāhu yamunnu.*

Three times. The same root, hammered three times into nineteen words. *Yamunnūna* — they do *mann* upon you. *Lā tamunnū* — do not do *mann* upon me. *Allāhu yamunnu* — Allah does *mann* upon you.

[PAUSE]

Notice, first, the *sound* of it. That doubled **nūn** — *mun-nūn, ta-mun-nū, ya-mun-nu*. It hums. It drones. Say it out loud and feel how the tongue gets stuck on that nasal hum and has to push through it. There is something in the very acoustics of this word that resembles the thing it describes — the low, insistent *murmur* of someone reminding you, reminding you, reminding you of what they did for you. The word grumbles. It mutters. The phonetics are doing the work of the meaning.

#### Deepening

Now let's sit with what this root actually holds, because it is one of the most extraordinary words in the Quran — and here is why.

The root *mann* does **not** only mean the poisoned reminder. At its origin, in the lexicons — in Lisān al-ʿArab, in al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt* — *mann* means simply **to bestow a great good, to confer a benefit**. A *minnah* is a genuine, weighty favor. This is the same root from which comes one of the names of Allah: **al-Mannān** — the One who bestows abundantly, who gives and gives without being asked. It is the same root as **al-mann**, the heavenly food, the sweet provision that Allah rained down on the Children of Israel in the wilderness — *al-mann wa-al-salwā* — pure gift, falling from the sky, that no one earned and no one could repay.

So here is the heart of this word. *Mann* is a single act seen from two completely opposite directions.

When the **giver is truly the source** — when the one bestowing actually *owns* what He gives, and needs nothing back — *mann* is the most beautiful thing in existence. It is grace. It is *al-Mannān* raining down provision.

But when the **creature** does it — when one who himself was given everything turns around and acts as though *he* is the source, *he* is the benefactor, *he* has placed someone in his debt — the very same word curdles into something ugly. It becomes the wound. It becomes "after all I've done for you."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done by choosing this one word and repeating it?

He has placed the Bedouins' *mann* and His own *mann* into the **same grammatical frame** — *yamunnūna ʿalayka* / *Allāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum* — so that we are forced to compare them directly. And the comparison annihilates them. When *they* do *mann*, they are creatures pretending to be the source of a gift they did not own. When *Allah* does *mann*, He is the actual Source, the one to whom *al-Mannān* belongs as a name. Their *mann* is a counterfeit of His. They have taken the divine gesture and worn it like a costume — and the ayah quietly strips it off.

#### Concluding the element

So this single word, said three times, reveals the entire crime and its correction at once: **they claimed the posture that belongs to God alone.** To do *mann* — real *mann*, the kind that genuinely places another in your debt — is something only the true Source can do. And the only true Source is Allah.

[PAUSE — let this land.]

### Element Two: The arrow that turns around — *ʿalayka… ʿalayya… ʿalaykum*

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, the little preposition **عَلَى (ʿalā)** — "upon," "over" — carries weight. It is the preposition of something pressing *down* on you, resting *on* your shoulders. When a duty is *ʿalā* you, it is a burden upon you. When someone does something *ʿalayka*, they are placing it *on* you — and with a word like *mann*, what they place on you is obligation. Indebtedness. A weight you now owe.

So watch the direction of this preposition as it moves through the ayah. The direction *is* the message.

#### Opening the feature

Three times the verb *mann* appears, and each time it is bolted to this preposition *ʿalā*, but the **target keeps changing**:

1. **يَمُنُّونَ عَلَيْكَ** — *yamunnūna ʿalayka* — "they do *mann* **upon you** [O Prophet]." The arrow points from the Bedouins toward the Messenger ﷺ.

2. **لَّا تَمُنُّوا عَلَيَّ** — *lā tamunnū ʿalayya* — "do not do *mann* **upon me**." The arrow is *intercepted*, refused at the door. *Don't point that at me.*

3. **بَلِ اللَّهُ يَمُنُّ عَلَيْكُمْ** — *bal Allāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum* — "rather, Allah does *mann* **upon you**." The arrow is spun a full one hundred and eighty degrees. It now points *back at them*.

#### Deepening

Stay with me, because this is one of the most elegant reversals in the Quran, and it happens almost entirely through that one tiny word changing its object.

The Bedouins fire the arrow: *ʿalayka* — onto you, Messenger. They believe they are the benefactors and he is the beneficiary. They have constructed a whole little economy in their minds: *we gave, he received, therefore he owes.*

Then comes **بَل — *bal***. This is one of the most powerful particles in Arabic. *Bal* is the particle of **retraction and reversal** — it means "no, on the contrary; strike that, here is the truth instead." It is a hinge. Everything before it is being overturned by everything after it. The whole sentence pivots on this single syllable.

And after the pivot: *Allāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum.* The arrow they aimed *outward* now lands *on them*. The favor was never flowing the direction they thought. It was always flowing *toward* them — from the only One who can actually be a source.

[PAUSE]

But notice the middle step. Notice what the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to say in between: **قُل لَّا تَمُنُّوا عَلَيَّ — Say: do not do *mann* upon me.**

He doesn't argue. He doesn't say "actually, *I* did *you* the favor by accepting you, by giving you a community, by securing your tribe." He could have. In purely worldly terms, he could have made that case. Instead he is commanded simply to *step out of the way*. To decline the arrow entirely. *Don't aim it at me — and don't think the answer is to aim it back at yourselves the other way. Take it off the human axis altogether.*

This is staggering when you feel it. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ refuses to occupy the position of "the one you owe." He will not let himself be made a creditor. Why? Because he knows — better than anyone who has ever lived — that *he too* is a recipient. That nothing flowing toward him from these people originated with them, and nothing he is, originated with him. He clears the space so that the only Name that can rightly stand in the position of Benefactor can stand there alone: **Allāh.**

#### Concluding the element

So the grammar tells the theology. Watch the preposition and you watch the truth being corrected in real time: *ʿalayka* (their illusion) → *lā… ʿalayya* (the refusal) → *bal… ʿalaykum* (the reality). The favor never went the way they thought. **It was always coming toward them, never going out from them.**

### Element Three: The two words they confused — *islām* and *īmān*

#### Teaching the concept first

There are two words in this ayah that English flattens into near-synonyms, but Arabic — and the Quran — keep carefully apart. Understanding the gap between them is the whole hinge of this verse.

The first is **إِسْلَام — *islām***, from the root **س-ل-م (sīn-lām-mīm)**, the root of *peace, safety, surrender, handing-over*. To do *islām* (the verb *aslama*) is to **submit, to hand yourself over, to enter into the security of surrender**. Crucially — and the scholars are careful here — *islām* in its base sense describes an **outward** reality. It is the surrender you can *see*: the declaration, the prayer, the joining of the community, the body that has come into the fold.

The second is **إِيمَان — *īmān***, from the root **أ-م-ن (hamza-mīm-nūn)**, the root of *security, trust, deep inner safety* — the same root from which we get *amān* (sanctuary) and *amānah* (trust). *Īmān* is **faith that has settled into the heart**. It is inward. It is the conviction that has put down roots in the chest, beneath the surface, where no one but Allah can see.

The Quran itself draws this exact line, in this exact surah, just three verses earlier. In **49:14**: the desert Arabs said *"we have believed [āmannā],"* and Allah corrects them: *"Say: you have not believed; rather say 'we have submitted [aslamnā]', for faith [īmān] has not yet entered your hearts."* Islam at the door; iman in the heart. Outer and inner.

Hold that distinction. Now watch what the Bedouins do with it in verse 17.

#### Opening the words

> يَمُنُّونَ عَلَيْكَ أَنْ **أَسْلَمُوا** … قُل لَّا تَمُنُّوا عَلَيَّ **إِسْلَامَكُم** … بَلِ اللَّهُ يَمُنُّ عَلَيْكُمْ أَنْ هَدَاكُمْ **لِلْإِيمَانِ**

Three things to see. They boast of their **islām** — *aslamū*, *islāmakum*. Twice. That is the word *they* keep reaching for. But when Allah reverses the arrow and names the *real* favor, He does **not** say He guided them to *islām*. He says: *hadākum **lil-īmān*** — He guided you to **faith**.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepening

Feel the precision of this. It is almost unbearably gentle and unbearably exact at the same time.

The Bedouins are bragging about the **lesser** thing — the outward surrender, the thing that is, frankly, partly *their* doing, an act they performed, a movement of the body and the tongue that they can point to and say *we did that*. And precisely *because* it is the thing they performed, they think they can claim it as their gift, their favor, their *mann*.

And Allah, in correcting them, does not even contest the *islām*. He lets it stand. *Fine — you submitted.* What He does instead is point past it, to the **greater** thing, the thing they have *not yet* attained and the thing that, unlike their *islām*, was never theirs to perform: **the guidance toward *īmān*.**

Do you see the trap closing?

They think the transaction is: *we gave You our islām.* Allah reframes the entire scene: *the only thing worth speaking about here is the īmān I am offering to guide you toward — and that, you could never have given yourselves. That is Mine to give.* The thing they're proud of is the small outer act. The thing that actually matters is the inner reality, and the inner reality is not something a creature can manufacture. **You cannot will faith into your own heart.** You can submit — you can come to the door, you can perform the outward act. But the light entering the chest, the conviction taking root, the *īmān* — that descends. That is *hidāyah*. That is gift.

And so the very structure of the words exposes the foolishness of the boast. **They were doing *mann* over the cheaper thing while ignoring that the precious thing was being handed to them for free.** It is like a man who knocks on the door of a palace, and when the King opens it and invites him into rooms of treasure, the man stands in the doorway demanding to be thanked for *knocking*.

#### A note on the verb *hadākum*

And the verb Allah chooses for what *He* did: **هَدَاكُمْ — *hadākum***, "He guided you," from **ه-د-ي (hadā)**. Not "He accepted you." Not "He rewarded you." *He guided you.* Guidance is the act of one who already knows the way taking the hand of one who is lost and was never going to find it alone. It assumes their helplessness. It is the most tender possible word — and the most decisive correction. *You did not arrive. You were brought.*

#### Concluding the element

So the two roots, *slm* and *Amn*, are not decoration. They are the whole argument in miniature. **They boasted of *islām* — the outward act they performed. Allah named *īmān* — the inward gift only He can guide a heart toward.** The thing they counted as their favor to God was the very thing that proved how much they still needed Him.

[PAUSE — major.]

### Bridge out of the language

Look at what these three words — *mann*, *ʿalā*, *islām/īmān* — have built together.

A people came doing *mann*, pointing the arrow of obligation outward. The Messenger ﷺ was told to step out of its path. And then Allah turned it around and revealed the truth that the arrow was always traveling toward them, carrying not their gift to Him but His gift to them — and not even the small gift of their submission, but the immense gift of being *guided toward faith itself*.

The grammar has shown us a reversal. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of reality itself, and about the quiet sickness that lives in every one of our hearts.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The resentful giver, and the ledger we keep with God

#### Beginning where we live

Let me start far away from the ayah, in a place you already know intimately.

There is a particular kind of pain that comes from being *reminded* of a favor. Someone helped you once, and now you can never be fully at ease around them, because the help is always hovering, ready to be deployed. The gift became a leash. And here is the strange thing psychologists and ordinary human experience both confirm: **the reminder destroys the gift retroactively.** Once someone says "after all I did for you," you go back and you doubt whether it was ever really *for* you at all — or whether it was always an investment, a down payment on future control.

The Quran knows this gesture so precisely that it forbids it explicitly. In Surah al-Baqarah, **2:264**, Allah commands the believers: *"Do not nullify your charity with *mann* and injury" — lā tubṭilū ṣadaqātikum bil-manni wal-adhā.* The same root. He is telling us: when you give to the poor and then hold it over them, you don't just *damage* the reward — you **annihilate** it. The charity is rendered void. *Battala* — wiped out as though it never happened. The reminder doesn't reduce the gift's value; it deletes the gift.

Now hold that next to verse 17. In 2:264 Allah forbids us from doing *mann* over our charity **to other human beings**. In 49:17, these people are doing *mann* over their "charity"...

[PAUSE]

...**to God Himself.**

The very gesture Allah condemns as poison when aimed at the poor, they have aimed at the Lord of the poor. They are reminding *Allah* of the favor of their conversion. They have opened a ledger with the One who owns the ledger, the pen, the hand, and the man holding it.

#### Letting it turn on us

And before we shake our heads at the Bedouins — stop. Because this is not a Bedouin disease. This is a *human* disease, and it lives in our prayer.

How often does our worship carry a quiet undertone of *mann*? *I prayed today, so where is my ease? I fasted, I gave, I stayed away from what was forbidden — and look at how my life is still hard. After everything I've done for You.* We don't say it out loud. But the ledger is open. We are doing *mann* upon Allah, reminding Him — as if He needs reminding — of our submission, expecting that our *islām* has placed Him in our debt.

This is the transactional heart. And the transactional heart cannot rest, because it is always calculating, always owed, always slightly resentful that the payout hasn't come. It turns the most beautiful relationship in existence — the slave before his Lord — into an accounts dispute.

[PAUSE]

#### The reversal as medicine

And the cure is the single word *bal*. *Rather.* No — turn the ledger around. You think the entries on your side are credits? Every single one of them was first a gift to you. The faith that made you want to pray — gift. The health that let you fast — gift. The very desire to obey, the softness in your heart that pulled you toward the prayer mat at all — *that itself* was the favor of *al-Mannān* before it was ever an act of yours.

You cannot put God in your debt, because the currency you would pay Him with — your faith, your worship, your willingness — He minted and placed in your hand before you spent it.

#### What changes tomorrow

So here is the practical turn. Tonight, or tomorrow, when you stand to pray — catch the ledger. Notice the small voice that says *look what I'm doing for You.* And reverse it deliberately. Before the prayer, say in your heart: *This standing is itself Your gift. That You woke me, that You turned my heart here, that I want this at all — al-minnatu lillāh, the favor is Yours.* Pray not as a creditor presenting an invoice, but as a debtor who has just been handed, free of charge, the very ability to say thank you.

That single inversion — from creditor to grateful debtor — changes the entire emotional texture of a life of worship. The creditor is always tense, always owed. The grateful debtor is at peace, always receiving.

### Theme Two: You were brought — the gift hidden inside your own movement toward God

#### Beginning where we live

Here is one of the most subtle truths in the whole of the spiritual life, and this ayah holds it like a jewel.

Think about a moment you turned back to Allah. Maybe after a long absence. Maybe a night you finally broke down and prayed, or finally let go of something you'd been gripping, or finally *meant* the words you'd been saying emptily for years. And remember how that felt — it felt like *you* did it. It felt like a movement *you* made. *I came back. I chose this. I turned toward Him.*

And in one sense, you did. The choice was real. The effort was yours.

But this ayah is whispering something underneath that, something that doesn't cancel your effort but *contextualizes* it utterly: **what felt like your movement toward God was His movement toward you first.** *Bal Allāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum an hadākum lil-īmān.* The guidance came first. The hand that you thought reached *out* was a hand that had already been *taken*.

#### Letting the ayah illuminate it

This is why Allah says *hadākum* — "He guided you" — and not "you found Me." The hidden architecture of every return to God is that the *return itself was enabled by the One returned to*. You did not generate the impulse from nothing. The softening of your heart, the dissatisfaction with your distance, the strange pull on a random Tuesday night — those were not your inventions. Those were *hidāyah* already in motion. By the time you "chose" Him, He had already chosen to make you able to choose.

In Surah al-Aʿrāf the people of Paradise say it perfectly: *"al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī hadānā li-hādhā, wa-mā kunnā li-nahtadiya law-lā an hadāna-llāh"* — "Praise to Allah who guided us to this; we would never have been guided had Allah not guided us." We would never have found the way. Not "it would have been harder." **Never.**

[PAUSE]

#### The paradox, left unresolved

Now — do not rush past the tension here, because the tension is where the truth lives. The ayah does not say "you did nothing." These people *did* submit; their *islām* is acknowledged. Your effort *is* real. Your choice *is* counted. And *yet* the very capacity for that effort was a gift.

Both are true. You row the boat — and the river was placed beneath you, the current was set in your favor, the strength in your arms was loaned to you, and the far shore was put there by Another. You really row. And you owe every inch of the crossing to the One who made the river.

The mature heart does not collapse this paradox. It holds it. It works as if everything depends on its effort, and it thanks God as if its effort were nothing — because in the scale of the actual gift, it nearly is.

#### The sting in the tail — *in kuntum ṣādiqīn*

And then the ayah ends with three words that refuse to let us relax: **إِن كُنتُمْ صَادِقِينَ — *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "if you are truthful."**

Notice what Allah does *not* do here. He does not close the verse with reassurance. He leaves it on a **condition** — and a condition that hangs open. The commentators (al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī) explain: *truthful in your claim that you believe.* The whole reversal — *Allah did you the favor of guiding you to faith* — is suspended on whether their claim of faith is even *real*. *If* you are truthful. The verse withholds the verdict. It hands them a mirror instead of a certificate.

[PAUSE]

And it hands *us* the same mirror. Because the antidote to the *mann* of the resentful worshipper is precisely *ṣidq* — truthfulness, the heart actually matching its claim. The Bedouin's problem was a gap between the loud outer *islām* and the absent inner *īmān*. *Ṣidq* is the closing of that gap. It is when the surface and the depths finally tell the same story.

#### What changes tomorrow

So the practical fruit of this theme is a question you carry, not an action you perform. The next time you feel that warm sense of *I did this, I came to Him, I earned this nearness* — pause and let the ayah re-narrate it: *No. I was brought. He moved toward me first. Even this gratitude I feel is something He is enabling right now.* And then ask the closing question honestly: *Am I ṣādiq? Does my inner reality match my outer claim — or am I, like them, boasting of a submission at the door while the rooms of the heart are still empty?*

That question, asked gently and often, is itself a form of being guided.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of our journey were the same insight approached from two directions.

The **language** showed us a reversal: one root, *mann*, said three times, with the arrow of *ʿalā* turning from *ʿalayka* (their illusion) to *bal… ʿalaykum* (the truth) — and the careful swap of *islām* (what they performed) for *īmān* (what only Allah could give them).

The **themes** showed us the same reversal as it lives in the human heart: the resentful giver who keeps a ledger with God, and the deeper truth that our very movement toward Him was His movement toward us first.

They are one insight. **You cannot do God a favor.** Not because your effort is nothing, but because the very things you would offer Him — your submission, your faith, your gratitude, your capacity to turn at all — were placed in your hands by Him before you ever offered them back. The arrow of *mann* can only ever, truly, point one direction: from *al-Mannān* toward the creature. Every time we try to reverse it, we are like the man demanding thanks for knocking on the door of the treasury.

### The architecture

And see now how perfectly the surah built to this. Verse 14 separated *islām* from *īmān* — outer from inner. Verse 17 takes that distinction and weaponizes it with mercy: *you boasted of the outer; here is the inner you were given for free.* The surah of *adab*, of refined conduct, ends its address to these desert Arabs not with a rule about how to behave — but with the root of all good conduct: **knowing who the Benefactor actually is.** Because every bad manner in the whole surah — the raised voice, the mockery, the suspicion, the backbiting — grows from the same soil: a self that thinks it is a source. And the cure for all of it is the single word *bal*.

### Questions to carry

1. When I worship, am I presenting an invoice or receiving a gift?
2. Where in my life am I doing *mann* — holding a kindness over someone — and calling it generosity?
3. The faith in my heart — did I build it, or was I guided to it? And does the way I carry myself reflect which of those is true?
4. *In kuntum ṣādiqīn* — if I am truthful. Where is the gap between my outer *islām* and my inner *īmān*, and am I willing to look at it?
5. If even my gratitude is a gift He is enabling right now — what is left for me to be proud of?

### One-sentence distillation

*You can never place God in your debt, because the very faith you would offer Him was His gift to you before it was ever your offering to Him.*

### The closing invitation

So let something shift. Not a new rule — a new posture. Walk into your next prayer as one who has already received everything, including the desire to pray. Let the ledger close. Let the arrow turn. Let *al-minnatu lillāh* — the favor belongs to Allah — become the quiet ground beneath every act of worship, so that you serve Him not to make Him owe you, but out of wonder that He let you serve Him at all.

### A du'a from these words

*O Allah, al-Mannān, the One whose favor cannot be repaid — we do not stand before You as creditors but as those who owe You even the strength to stand. You guided us when we could never have found the way; do not let us boast of a submission at the door while You wait to bring us into the rooms of faith. Make our inner truth match our outer claim, and let us be, before You, among the ṣādiqīn. Āmīn.*
