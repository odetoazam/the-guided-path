# Written Before You Were: The Cure for Despair and Pride

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first to the sound of it, before any meaning at all:

> [ayah:57:22] مَآ أَصَابَ مِن مُّصِيبَةٍ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِىٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ إِلَّا فِى كِتَـٰبٍ مِّن قَبْلِ أَن نَّبْرَأَهَآ ۚ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ يَسِيرٌ
>
> *Mā aṣāba min muṣībatin fil-arḍi wa lā fī anfusikum illā fī kitābin min qabli an nabra'ahā; inna dhālika ʿalā-llāhi yasīr.*
>
> **"No calamity strikes upon the earth or within your own selves except that it is in a Register before We bring it into being — indeed that, for Allah, is easy."**

And then the sentence that follows, joined to it like a hand to a wrist:

> لِّكَيْلَا تَأْسَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ مَا فَاتَكُمْ وَلَا تَفْرَحُوا۟ بِمَآ ءَاتَىٰكُمْ
>
> *Likaylā ta'saw ʿalā mā fātakum wa lā tafraḥū bimā ātākum.*
>
> **"So that you do not grieve over what has slipped away from you, nor exult over what He has given you."**

### Rebuild the world

To feel why these words were sent, put yourself in Madinah.

This is a community that has buried its dead. Some of the people hearing this surah carried the bodies of their brothers down from the slope of Uḥud. They have left homes in Makkah they will never see again — gardens, wells, the rooms their children were born in — gone, simply gone, and not coming back. And at the very same time, this is a community on the *rise*. Madinah is growing. There is property now, there is harvest, there are spoils, there is a state. Wealth is arriving in hands that, a few years earlier, held nothing.

So picture a single heart in that city. In the morning it aches over a grave or a lost home in Makkah. By the afternoon it swells with the arrival of new provision. Grief and elation, grief and elation — the heart swinging like a door in the wind, slammed open and shut by whatever the day delivers.

Surah Al-Ḥadīd has just spent several ayahs describing exactly that world — *laʿibun wa lahwun*, play and distraction, adornment and boasting and the piling up of wealth and children — the dunya as a crop that grows green, dazzles the farmer, then withers to yellow straw. The whole surah is one long invitation: *spend it, give it, hold it loosely, it is not yours.* But you cannot simply *tell* a grieving, rising heart to hold the world loosely. The heart will say: *how?*

These two ayahs are the *how*.

### The hook

Here is what should stop us before we even begin. Allah is about to tell us something staggering about the architecture of reality — that every loss we will ever suffer was written down *before we existed*. That is the kind of statement you would expect to be the climax. The destination. The thing the whole passage is building toward.

But it is not the destination. Watch what happens. Allah states this cosmic fact in ayah 22 — and then in ayah 23 He opens with a single small word, **لِّكَيْلَا**, *likaylā*, "**so that** you do not..." — and the whole weight of the universe's pre-written Register turns out to be *in service* of something happening inside your chest.

He did not tell us about the Register to satisfy our curiosity about the unseen.

He told us so that a particular thing would stop happening to our hearts.

Let us find out what.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that means *to hit the target*

Before we open the ayah, let me give you an everyday picture.

Imagine an archer. She draws the bow, exhales, releases — and the arrow lands dead center in the target. In English we have a word for that exact event: a *hit*. Not a stray shot, not a wild miss that happened to graze something. A *hit* implies a target, an aim, an intention. You cannot "hit" by accident in the full sense of the word — to hit is to *arrive where you were sent.*

Hold that picture.

Now look at the word Allah chose for *calamity*:

> مُّصِيبَة — *muṣībah*

We translate it "calamity," "disaster," "affliction." And so we imagine something chaotic, random, senseless — the very opposite of order. But the Arabic root tells a completely different story.

**مُّصِيبَة comes from the root ص-و-ب (ṣ-w-b)** — and the core meaning of this root is *to hit the mark. To strike the target. To land precisely where aimed.* From this very same root comes the word **صَوَاب** — *ṣawāb* — which means *correctness*, *being right*, *hitting upon the truth.* When you give the right answer in Arabic, you have produced *ṣawāb* — you have hit the mark.

Let that land for a moment.

The word for the worst thing that will ever happen to you — the diagnosis, the death, the loss, the door that closes forever — is built from the same root as the word for *getting it exactly right.*

[PAUSE]

And notice the verb that carries it: **أَصَابَ** — *aṣāba* — the same root again, in a form that means *to strike, to hit upon, to befall by reaching.* So the ayah does not say "if bad things randomly occur." Read literally, it says: *Nothing lands a hit — no arrow finds its target in the earth or in your own selves — except...*

A muṣībah, in the deepest texture of the Arabic, is **not a stray bullet. It is an aimed arrow.** It was *sent*. It *arrived*. It landed exactly where it was meant to land — in this earth, or in this self, *yours* — and not one inch beside it.

Now — be careful here, because the heart can hear this two ways. One way is cold: *everything is fixed, nothing matters, why try.* But that is not what the word says. The word says something warm and terrible at once: the thing that struck you was **not an accident of a careless universe.** It was *placed.* It was *aimed.* And an aim implies an Aimer — One who knows the target, knows you, and released the arrow on purpose.

This is the first stone beneath our feet. The calamity is not chaos. It is *ṣawāb* — it is, from the side of the One who sent it, exactly *right.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: "Within your own selves" — the arrow that finds the inside

Now notice something about *where* these arrows land. Allah names two fields:

> فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِىٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ
>
> "...in **the earth**, nor in **your own selves**."

Sit with the asymmetry here. The first field is **ٱلْأَرْض** — *al-arḍ*, "**the** earth" — definite, vast, impersonal. Drought, famine, the failed harvest, the flood, the lost home, the lost wealth. The classical commentators — al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī — list these: *qaḥṭ al-maṭar*, the withholding of rain; the shortfall of crops; the loss of property out there in the world.

But the second field is not "the bodies" or "the people." It is **أَنفُسِكُمْ** — *anfusikum* — "**your own selves**," with the pronoun *-kum*, **you**, fastened right onto it. The illness in your chest. The child you buried. The grief, the fear, the thing that strikes not the field but the *soul itself*.

Do you feel the movement? The ayah travels from the widest possible outside — *the whole earth* — and drives the arrow all the way *in*, to the most private interior you possess: your own self. From the famine in the distant province to the ache behind your own ribs. **Nothing is too far out, and nothing is too far in,** to be outside the aim.

The commentators even differ on the precise scope of *anfusikum* — al-Qurṭubī reports it as illness and affliction (*qatādah*), and reports others who included even the trials of the soul's own discipline. Take them not as competing answers but as camera angles on one reality: **every kind of strike, from the body's sickness to the soul's sorrow, is named.** There is no loss so intimate that it falls outside the verse. The arrow reaches the inside.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: Written *before We bring it into being*

Now we come to the center. Watch this clause closely, because it is doing something the mind almost cannot hold:

> إِلَّا فِى كِتَـٰبٍ مِّن قَبْلِ أَن نَّبْرَأَهَآ
>
> "...except it is in a **Register**, **before** We **bring it into being**."

First, a small but real thing. The word for the record is **كِتَاب** — *kitāb* — but it is **indefinite**. Not "*the* Book," but "*a* Register," *a* writing. The tafsīr tradition — al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar — identifies it as *al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ*, the Preserved Tablet. But the bare, indefinite form of the word lands on the ear differently: *it is written down — somewhere it is written — it exists in a record* — before the identification of *which* record even arrives. The first thing impressed on us is not the filing cabinet. It is the simple, staggering fact: **it is written.**

Now the verb. **نَّبْرَأَهَآ** — *nabra'ahā* — "before We bring **it** into being."

This verb comes from the root **ب-ر-أ (b-r-')**, and this is one of the great roots of the Qur'an. It is the root of one of Allah's own names: **ٱلْبَارِئ** — *al-Bāri'* — **the Maker, the Originator**, the One who brings a thing out of nothing into distinct existence. *Bara'a* is not just "to make"; it carries the sense of *originating something fresh, separating it out, giving it its own distinct being.*

And here is a thread worth holding gently: this same root, *b-r-'*, is the root of the word for *recovering from illness* — *bara'a min al-maraḍ*, to be made well, restored, set free of the sickness — and the root of *barī'*, to be *free and clear* of something. The root that means *to be brought into being* is woven from the same fiber as *to be set free and made whole.* Keep that near; we will need it.

So what does the clause actually say? **Before We originate it — before the soul is brought out of nothingness, before the earth is laid, before the calamity itself is created — it is already written.** The commentators split only on what the "it" (*-hā*) refers to: al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn read it as *before We create the earth and the souls*; Ibn ʿAbbās is reported as reading *before We create the calamity itself*; others, the souls. Notice that whichever way you read it, the conclusion is the same and it is total: **the writing precedes the existing.**

[PAUSE — let this sit]

Let me make this concrete with the only analogy that does it justice.

Think of a screenplay. Every scene is written down — the joyful ones and the devastating ones — and the script is *complete and bound* before the actors are ever cast. Before the actor playing the grieving father is even *born*, his scene of grief already exists, fully written, on page 84.

Now imagine that actor, decades later, stepping onto the stage, and when the grieving scene arrives he is *destroyed* by it — not as an actor, but personally — crying out, "*Why has this been taken from me? This was mine and now it's gone!*"

And you want to gently take his shoulder and say: *it was never taken from you. It was written before you existed. You did not lose a thing you owned — you arrived into a scene already authored. You cannot be robbed of what was never, for one instant, yours to begin with.*

That is the hinge of this entire passage. **If the loss was written before you were brought into being, then the loss was never your possession that got stolen. It was a scene you were always going to walk through.** And you cannot mourn a robbery that never happened.

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three things, and they stack:

The calamity is **aimed**, not random — *muṣībah*, the arrow that hits its mark.
It reaches **everywhere** — from the whole earth to the inside of your own self.
And it was **written before you existed** — *min qabli an nabra'ahā* — so it was never a possession that could be stolen from you.

And then Allah seals ayah 22 with: **إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ يَسِيرٌ** — "indeed *that* is **easy** for Allah." That word, *yasīr*, "easy" — al-Baghawī notes it means the *recording* of all this, in all its uncountable detail, every arrow and every target across all of time, is *hayyin*, light, effortless for Him. What would crush a universe to compute, He holds without weight.

Now let us ask what all of this is *for*. Because Allah does not leave it as cosmology. He tells us, in the very next breath, why He told us.

> "Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the two things quietly stealing the peace out of your chest."

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The two thieves of the present moment

Let me start where you live, not in the seventh century.

There is a well-documented finding in behavioral psychology called **loss aversion.** Put simply: human beings feel the pain of losing something roughly *twice* as intensely as they feel the pleasure of gaining the same thing. Lose a hundred dollars and the sting is sharp and lingering. Find a hundred dollars and the lift is real but fades by lunchtime. We are not balanced creatures. We are tilted — tilted toward grief.

And there is a second finding, the **hedonic treadmill.** Whatever we gain — the promotion, the house, the new phone — lifts our mood briefly and then becomes the new normal, the new baseline we now feel *entitled* to and now fear losing. So the gain doesn't make us content; it just resets the floor and adds one more thing to be anxious about losing.

Put those two together and you have a precise description of the human heart as most of us actually experience it: **dragged down hard by every loss, lifted only briefly by every gain, and never, ever at rest.** A door in the wind.

Now hear ayah 23 again, and hear that it is naming *exactly these two thieves*:

> لِّكَيْلَا **تَأْسَوْا۟** عَلَىٰ مَا فَاتَكُمْ وَلَا **تَفْرَحُوا۟** بِمَآ ءَاتَىٰكُمْ
>
> "**So that** you do not **grieve** over what has slipped away from you, nor **exult** over what He has given you."

Thief number one: **تَأْسَوْا** — *ta'saw* — grief, sorrow, the despairing ache over **مَا فَاتَكُمْ**, "what *fāta*-ed you" — what *escaped*, *slipped past*, *got away.* That is loss aversion, named fourteen centuries early.

Thief number two: **تَفْرَحُوا** — *tafraḥū* — exultation, the giddy swelling of the self over **مَا ءَاتَىٰكُمْ**, "what He has *given* you." That is the hedonic high of the gain.

And notice the exquisite balance of the two verbs of *receiving*. What you lost, *fātakum* — it "escaped you," it slipped out of your open hand. What you have, *ātākum* — He "gave it to you," it was placed in your hand by Another. Look at what that pairing quietly establishes: **in neither case were you the owner.** The thing that left did not belong to you — it was passing through, and it left. The thing you hold does not belong to you — it was *given*, on loan, by the Giver. Your hand is not a vault. It is a road that things travel down.

[PAUSE]

And here is where ayah 22 and ayah 23 lock together into one mechanism — this is the whole device:

*Why* should you not be wrecked by the loss? **Because it was written before you existed** — it was never your possession to lose. The screenplay was bound before you were born.

*Why* should you not be intoxicated by the gain? **For the very same reason** — it too was written, it too is a scene, it too is *given*, not earned and owned. If your suffering wasn't a robbery, then your prosperity isn't a trophy. The same truth that pulls you up off the floor of despair is the truth that pulls you down off the ceiling of pride.

One fact. Both thieves disarmed at once. *That* is the elegance of putting ayah 22 before ayah 23. He gives you the cosmology, then says: *here is what it is for — so that your heart can finally stop slamming in the wind.*

What does this change tomorrow? Here is the practice. When the loss comes — and it will — train the tongue and the heart to say not "*why was this taken from me*" but "*this was written; it was never mine; it has returned to its Owner.*" And when the gain comes, train the heart to say not "*look what I built*" but "*this was given; it is on loan; I am a road, not a vault.*" Run that for a week and watch the door stop slamming.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The man who lives in his own imagination

But the ayah is not finished, and its ending is unexpected. After this gentle, healing logic about grief and gain, watch the final clause:

> وَٱللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ
>
> "And Allah does not love every **self-deluded boaster**."

Why end a verse about emotional peace with a statement about *who Allah does not love*? Stay with me — the connection is the whole point.

Look at the first word: **مُخْتَال** — *mukhtāl*. We translate it "arrogant" or "self-deluded." But its root is **خ-ي-ل (kh-y-l)** — and this is the root of **خَيَال** — *khayāl* — which means **imagination, fantasy, an image in the mind, a phantom, a thing that is not actually real.** (It is the same root, the Arabs noted, from which the proud, strutting gait of a horse is named — the horse that prances as though it were more than it is.)

So a *mukhtāl* is not just a proud person. A *mukhtāl* is, at root, **a person living inside a fantasy** — someone who has mistaken the *khayāl*, the imagined image of themselves, for reality. He struts through a story he has authored in his own head, in which he is the owner, the achiever, the source of his own good fortune.

And do you see now why this is the *perfect* word to end on? Go back to the two thieves. **What is the engine underneath both the crushing grief and the intoxicating pride? It is the fantasy of ownership.** The despair says, "*mine was taken!*" The pride says, "*mine was earned!*" Both are spoken from inside the *khayāl* — the imagined world in which I am the owner and the source.

The second word seals it: **فَخُور** — *fakhūr* — from *f-kh-r*, to boast — and the form is *intensive*: not one who occasionally boasts, but one whose very *posture* is boasting, a self that is structurally turned toward "look at what is *mine*."

So the ayah's logic completes its circle: *grieve and exult less* — *because nothing was ever yours* — *because the one who thinks things are his is a* mukhtāl, *a man lost in a fantasy, and that fantasy is precisely what Allah does not love.* The verse heals you by waking you up out of the *khayāl.*

[PAUSE]

Now, return to that thread we set aside — the root of *nabra'ahā*, the root *b-r-'*, the root of *al-Bāri'* the Originator, and also the root of *bara'a*, **to recover, to be made well, to be set free.**

Here is the quiet gift hidden in the verb. The same word that tells you the calamity was *written before you were originated* is built from the root of *healing and being set free.* The very grammar whispers it: to truly receive this ayah — to know that the loss was written, that you are not the owner, that you are a road and not a vault — is not a cold resignation. **It is a recovery.** It is the soul being *bara'a*-ed, made well, set free, from the exhausting fantasy of ownership that had it swinging in the wind.

What does this change tomorrow? Watch for the *mukhtāl* in your own self-talk — the sentence that begins "*I built, I earned, I deserve, I made myself.*" That sentence is the fantasy. When you catch it, replace it with the true sentence: "*I was given. It was written. I am being carried.*" That is not a smaller life. It is a freer one.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architectural reveal

Now stand back and see the whole structure, because it is built with breathtaking precision.

**Ayah 22 is the foundation: the metaphysical fact.** Every arrow is aimed. Every strike, from the whole earth to your inmost self, is *written before you were brought into being* — and recording it all is *easy* for the One who wrote it.

**Ayah 23 is the purpose: لِّكَيْلَا — "so that."** And the entire towering fact of the pre-written Register turns out to bend down and serve one humble, human end — *that your heart would stop swinging between despair over loss and intoxication over gain.*

And then the final clause, **مُخْتَال فَخُور**, reaches all the way back and names the disease the whole passage was curing: the **fantasy of ownership** — the imagined self that thinks the loss was a robbery and the gain a trophy.

So the passage is a perfect arc: *Here is how reality is actually built (it is all written) → here is what that knowledge is for (so your heart can rest) → here is the sickness it heals (the fantasy that you are the owner).* Cosmology in service of the heart. The unseen Register, deployed entirely to set one human chest free.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The words told us: the calamity is an *aimed arrow* (ṣawāb), it reaches the *inside* of you (anfusikum), it was *written before you existed* (nabra'ahā), and recording it is *effortless* for Him (yasīr).

The themes told us: therefore *grieve less and exult less* (ta'saw / tafraḥū), because *nothing was ever yours* — what left "escaped," what you hold was "given" — and the one who forgets this is a *mukhtāl*, a man lost in a fantasy.

Both roads arrive at the same door, and the root *b-r-'* is the name carved over it: to receive this is not to be resigned. **It is to be set free.**

### Questions to carry

- Think of the loss you are still grieving most. Can you say of it, honestly — *this was written before I existed; it was never my possession*? What in you resists saying that, and why?
- Where in your life right now are you speaking from inside the *khayāl* — the sentence that begins "*I earned, I built, I deserve*"?
- Loss aversion makes you feel every loss twice as hard as every gain. What would your week feel like if the two were weighted *equally* — because both were merely written scenes?
- Is your hand a *vault* or a *road*? Which one have you been living as?
- The verb of healing and the verb of being-originated share a root. What in you needs to *recover* from the fantasy of ownership?

### One-sentence distillation

Every loss you will ever suffer was written before you were brought into being — so it was never yours to lose, and never yours to be robbed of, and knowing this is meant to lift you off the floor of despair and off the ceiling of pride at once, and set your heart free.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to carry the exhausting fantasy any longer. You were never the owner — of the wealth, the health, the people, the years. You are a road they travel down, and an Author wrote every scene before you arrived on the stage. The invitation of these two ayahs is not to feel *nothing.* It is to feel everything *while standing on solid ground* — to grieve without being destroyed, to receive without being intoxicated, because beneath both is the same quiet, unshakable knowledge: *it is written, it is His, and that — for Him — is easy.*

### A brief du'ā

O Bāri', O Originator who wrote every scene before You brought us into being — set our hearts free of the fantasy that we are the owners. Let us not be destroyed by what escapes our hands, nor drunk on what You place in them. And make us recover, by Your mercy, from the swinging and the strutting, into the stillness of trusting that every arrow You aim is *ṣawāb* — exactly right. Āmīn.
