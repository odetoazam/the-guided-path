# The One I Created Alone

## Introduction: A Man Who Had Everything

Let me read these words to you first. Sit with the sound of them before we touch the meaning.

> **ذَرْنِي وَمَنْ خَلَقْتُ وَحِيدًا**
> *Dharnī wa man khalaqtu waḥīdā*
> **Leave Me — and the one I created alone.**

> **وَجَعَلْتُ لَهُ مَالًا مَّمْدُودًا ۝ وَبَنِينَ شُهُودًا ۝ وَمَهَّدتُّ لَهُ تَمْهِيدًا**
> *Wa jaʿaltu lahu mālan mamdūdā · wa banīna shuhūdā · wa mahhadtu lahu tamhīdā*
> **And gave him wealth, stretched out long. And sons, present at his side. And I smoothed the whole of his life into a level, easy road.**

> **ثُمَّ يَطْمَعُ أَنْ أَزِيدَ ۝ كَلَّا ۖ إِنَّهُ كَانَ لِآيَاتِنَا عَنِيدًا ۝ سَأُرْهِقُهُ صَعُودًا**
> *Thumma yaṭmaʿu an azīd · kallā · innahu kāna li-āyātinā ʿanīdā · sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdā*
> **Then — he longs that I give him still more. No. He set himself against Our signs, hard and stubborn. I will load onto him a steep, exhausting climb.**

[PAUSE]

### The world behind these words

To feel what is happening here, you have to stand for a moment in Mecca, in the early years of revelation, and look at one particular man.

His name was al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra. The mufassirūn are agreed — al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Jalālayn all name him — that these verses came down about him. And to understand the weight of what Allah says, you have to understand what this man *was* in his society.

He was not a man on the margins. He was the center. He was so wealthy that his caravans of trade ran the length of the year. He had ten sons — some say more — and they did not have to travel for their livelihood the way poorer men's sons did; they stayed in Mecca, at his side, around his table. He was a chief among the chiefs of Quraysh. He was a judge of poetry, a man whose verdict on a poem could make or break a poet's name. When the Arabs needed someone to assess the strange, overwhelming speech that Muḥammad ﷺ was reciting — was it poetry? magic? madness? — it was al-Walīd they turned to.

And here is the thing the early biographers preserve: **he was honest about it, for a moment.** He heard the Qur'an. And it moved him. He is reported to have said that this speech had a sweetness to it, a richness, that its lowest branch hung heavy with fruit and its highest reached up beyond grasp — that it was not the speech of a man. For one moment, the most discerning literary judge in Arabia stood at the edge of the truth and *saw* it.

And then his people came to him. They said: the others are turning to you. If you praise this, we are finished. And so he went back, and he thought, and he calculated — the Qur'an even narrates his internal deliberation a few verses later: *he reflected and he plotted… then he frowned and he scowled… then he turned away in arrogance and said, this is nothing but magic passed down, nothing but the word of a human being.*

[PAUSE]

So this is the man. Not a fool. Not a brute. A man who had seen everything, been given everything, and *understood* the truth when it came to him — and turned from it anyway, to protect a position that Allah Himself had handed to him.

### The hook

Now — here is what I want you to notice before we go a single step further, and then we will earn it slowly, word by word.

In these seven verses, Allah lists everything He gave this man. The wealth. The sons. The smoothed-out life. It is an inventory of blessing. And the inventory **opens** and **closes** with the same hidden idea — an idea so quiet you can read the passage ten times and miss it.

The man's whole world was built on the word **alone**. And the word **alone** is exactly where Allah begins. And the place He says He will send him — at the very end — is the precise, deliberate *opposite* of the gift He describes in the middle.

This passage is built like a trap closing. Let me show you the architecture from the inside out.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Dharnī* — "Leave Me to him"

Let me begin with the very first word, because it is doing something we tend to read straight past.

**ذَرْنِي — *dharnī*.** It is a command — an imperative — and its root (*w-dh-r*) means *to leave a thing be, to let it go, to set it aside.* The verb is addressed to one person. *Leave.* And attached to the end is the pronoun *-nī*: **Me.** "Leave Me." Or, fully: *Leave Me and the one I created.*

Now, before we ask who is being spoken to, let me give you the everyday shape of this expression, because every language has it.

Imagine a parent watching their child being wronged by someone — and the child is upset, agitated, ready to act. And the parent puts a hand on the child's shoulder and says, very quietly: **"Leave him to me."** Just that. *Leave him to me.*

What does that sentence carry? It carries no anger in its *sound*. It is almost gentle. But everyone in the room goes cold when they hear it. Because "leave him to me" is not a request for help. It is the most total threat a person can make. It says: *step back. You don't need to lift a finger. This is now between him and me, and he has no idea what he has walked into.*

[PAUSE]

That is exactly the register of **ذَرْنِي**. Al-Qurṭubī says it plainly: *wa hiya kalimatu waʿīdin wa tahdīd* — **"this is a word of warning and threat."** It is not information. It is a sentence Allah speaks the way a sovereign speaks when the matter has left every other court and arrived at the throne.

And the grammar holds a second blade. Al-Jalālayn notes that the phrase that follows — *wa man khalaqtu,* "and the one I created" — can be parsed two ways. Read one way, it is simply joined to the object: *leave Me, and leave him* — hand the whole matter over. But read the other way, it is what the grammarians call a *mafʿūl maʿahu,* an object of *accompaniment,* and the sentence shifts to: *leave Me — alone — with him.* Sit with that second reading. It is the sound of a door closing on a room with only two people left inside it. *Step out. Shut it behind you. He and I have something to settle.*

Now feel who is being told this. The grammatical addressee is the Prophet ﷺ. And think about what the Prophet was enduring in these years from exactly men like al-Walīd — the mockery, the organized campaign to discredit the revelation, the wounding of being called a madman by the most respected voice in the city. The Prophet ﷺ carried that. And Allah says to him: **ذَرْنِي. Leave him to Me.**

Stay with how much mercy is folded inside that threat. Allah is not only threatening al-Walīd. He is *unburdening His Messenger.* He is saying: you don't have to win this argument. You don't have to balance these scales. Put him down. He is My concern now.

There is a closeness in that word that should undo us a little. **ذَرْنِي** — *leave Me* — is the language of someone taking a weight out of your hands. The same divine breath that crushes the arrogant is the breath that lifts the load off the one who is tired of carrying it.

And here is what this single word reveals — let me state it cleanly:

**When Allah says "leave him to Me," the matter is already finished. The verb is gentle. The verdict is total.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Waḥīdā* — the title of honor that became the verdict

Now the second word — and this is where the passage turns from threatening to something almost unbearable in its precision.

**وَحِيدًا — *waḥīdā*.** Its root is *w-ḥ-d*, the root of *one*, of *alone*, of *sole and single*. In the verse it sits as a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial description — telling us the *state* in which this man was created. *I created him… waḥīdā.* Alone. Single. With nothing and no one.

The mufassirūn read this directly. Al-Baghawī: *khalaqtuhu fī baṭni ummihi waḥīdan farīdan, lā māla lahu wa lā walad* — **"I created him in his mother's belly, single and solitary, with no wealth and no child."** That is the surface, and it is true, and it is already enough to humble anyone: every emperor, every billionaire, every chief of Quraysh entered this world the identical way — naked, owning nothing, knowing no one, unable to lift his own head. Created *alone.*

But now I have to tell you something the classical commentators preserve, and once you hear it you will never read this word the same way.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record it: **al-Walīd was called, by his own people, *al-Waḥīd.*** "The Unique One." "The One-and-Only." It was his title. It was the name his clan gave him in pride — *there is no one like him among us.* He may even have boasted it of himself.

[PAUSE — let this land]

Do you feel what has happened?

Allah takes the man's proudest title — the very word he wore as a crown, *the Unique, the One-and-Only* — and He uses that exact word to describe the day the man had **nothing.**

*You call yourself al-Waḥīd? Yes. You were waḥīd. The day I made you — alone. No wealth that men envy. No sons at your table. No tribe at your back. No tongue in your mouth. One soul, naked, in the dark of a womb, owning the sum total of nothing. That is the "uniqueness" you came into the world with. Everything you built the title on — I gave you afterward.*

The same syllables that meant *peerless eminence* in the mouth of his clan mean *utter dependence* in the mouth of his Creator. Not one letter changed. Only the speaker changed. And when the speaker is Allah, the word tells the truth.

This is the genius of *reading absence.* What does a man like al-Walīd build his whole identity on? On being *self-made.* On being *one of a kind.* And Allah reaches into the very word he uses for that, and empties it out, and shows him the moment when "alone" meant "had nothing, was nothing, could do nothing — until I acted."

What this word reveals:

**Every title we wear to say "I am exceptional" is on loan. Strip the gifts away and you find the state you were really created in — alone, and owning nothing but your need for Allah.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Mahhadtu… tamhīdā* against *ṣaʿūdā* — the level road and the steep climb

Now hold those two words — *leave him to Me*, and *I made him alone* — and watch what Allah does in the middle and at the end. Because the architecture is about to close.

In the middle of the passage comes the inventory of gifts, and it builds:

- **مَالًا مَّمْدُودًا — *mālan mamdūdā*** — wealth that is *stretched out, extended, drawn long.* The root carries the image of something pulled out to its full length, unbroken, continuous. Al-Muyassar glosses it: *mālan mabsūṭan wāsiʿan* — wealth laid out wide and open. Not a sum he counted — a horizon of it.
- **بَنِينَ شُهُودًا — *banīna shuhūdā*** — sons who are *present, witnessing, at hand.* Al-Muyassar notes the nuance: sons who did not have to leave Mecca, who stayed *with him, never absent.* The blessing isn't only that he had sons — it's that they were *there,* around him, every day. Presence itself as a gift.
- And then the keystone: **وَمَهَّدتُّ لَهُ تَمْهِيدًا — *mahhadtu lahu tamhīdā*.**

Let me teach you this word carefully, because it is the hinge of the whole passage.

The verb *mahhada* comes from *mahd.* And *mahd* — do you know what it is? It is the **cradle.** It is the bed you prepare for an infant, smoothed flat, soft, every lump taken out, every danger removed, so the child can lie in it without a single care. From there *mahhada* widens to mean: *to make a surface level and smooth; to flatten the road; to prepare a way so that nothing on it will trip you, nothing will resist you.*

So when Allah says *mahhadtu lahu* — **"I smoothed everything out for him"** — the picture is: I took his life and I *flattened the road.* I removed the friction. I leveled the ground so his every step landed easy.

And then Allah adds a word after it: **تَمْهِيدًا — *tamhīdā*.** Now, in Arabic there is a construction where you follow a verb with a noun built from the *same root,* purely to intensify it. It's like saying in English, "I prepared it — *and I mean truly prepared it.*" The Arabs call it the absolute object — *al-mafʿūl al-muṭlaq* — and its job is to slam the door on any doubt about how thoroughly the action was done. *Mahhadtu… tamhīdā* — "I smoothed it for him — *a complete, total, leave-nothing-out smoothing.*"

And notice how al-Muyassar carries that very doubling into his own gloss: *yassartu lahu subula al-ʿaysh taysīran* — **"I made the roads of his livelihood easy for him, an utter easing."** He answers the intensified verb with an intensified verb of his own — *yassartu… taysīran,* the same slam-the-door construction — because the only honest way to paraphrase *mahhadtu… tamhīdā* is to repeat the move. And note what the smoothing *concretely* was, in his reading: not abstract comfort, but the *means of living* — the paths by which a man eats, trades, provides, survives — every one of them cleared and leveled in advance, so that the work of staying alive, which costs most of humanity its sweat and its years, cost this man nothing at all.

So sit in the full picture now. Here is a man whose life Allah describes as a **cradle.** Wealth stretched to the horizon. Sons always present. And the whole road of his days flattened, softened, leveled — *thoroughly* — so that he lived, in the most literal sense the word allows, *like an infant rocked in a bed with every discomfort engineered away.*

[PAUSE]

And now — the trap closes. Read the final verse:

**سَأُرْهِقُهُ صَعُودًا — *sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdā*.**

*Sa-urhiquhu* — "I will burden him, I will overwhelm him, I will press onto him." And what will Allah press onto him? **صَعُودًا — *ṣaʿūdā*.** The root is *ṣ-ʿ-d* — *to climb, to ascend, to go up a steep incline.* A *ṣaʿūd* is a **steep, exhausting uphill** — a slope so severe that to climb it is agony, the kind of ascent where you claw upward and never crest the top. Al-Muyassar renders the meaning: *mashaqqatan min al-ʿadhābi wa-l-irhāqi lā rāḥata lahu minhā* — **"a hardship of torment and exhaustion with no rest in it."**

Now put the two words side by side. Feel them against each other:

> **مَهَّدتُّ — *mahhadtu*** — I laid your life out *flat.* The cradle. The level road. Every step easy.
> **صَعُودًا — *ṣaʿūdā*** — I will put you on a *climb.* The endless uphill. Every step agony. No rest.

[PAUSE]

This is not a random punishment. This is the **exact inversion** of the gift. The man who was given the most *level* road in Mecca is sentenced to the most *vertical* one in the next life. The cradle is answered by the climb. The smoothed ground is answered by the slope with no summit. The "no effort required" of his dunyā becomes the "no rest permitted" of his ākhira.

What was given as *down and flat and soft* comes back as *up and steep and without end.*

This is the architecture I promised you. Watch the whole shape now:

**He entered the world *waḥīdan* — alone, with nothing. Allah *stretched out* his wealth, *gathered* his sons, *flattened* his road. The man answered by wanting *more* and spurning the One who gave. So the flattened road is folded back into a climb with no top, and he ascends it the way he arrived — *alone.*** Given everything, he returns to nothing — but now the nothing is a mountain he must climb forever.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Let me name what these three words together have shown us, before we turn from language to life. Allah does not threaten this man with a punishment picked at random from a list. He threatens him with the *photographic negative of his own blessings.* The threat is custom-built from the gift. And that should make every one of us look very carefully at what we have been given — because the shape of the gift may be the shape of the test.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The man who wanted *more*

Let me start where you already live.

There is a phenomenon that psychologists have a clinical name for. They call it the **hedonic treadmill,** or *hedonic adaptation.* Here is what it means, stripped of the jargon.

You want a thing. A raise. A house. A phone. A relationship. You are certain that *when I get this, I will be content.* And you get it. And for a brief window — days, maybe weeks — you are lifted. And then something quiet and almost invisible happens: **the new thing becomes the floor.** It stops being a gift you received and becomes the baseline you stand on. And from that new, higher floor, you look up — and there is a new thing to want, exactly as far above you as the last one was. You have not arrived. You have only moved the starting line. The treadmill turns, and you are running, and the scenery never changes.

Researchers have tracked this in people who win lotteries, who get the promotion, who buy the dream home. The spike comes. The spike fades. The baseline resets. And the wanting — the wanting is immortal.

[PAUSE]

Now read this verse again, and watch fourteen centuries collapse into a single word.

Allah lists it all — the stretched-out wealth, the present sons, the cradle-smooth life — and then He says:

**ثُمَّ يَطْمَعُ أَنْ أَزِيدَ — *thumma yaṭmaʿu an azīd.***

**"Then — he *longs* that I give him *more.*"**

*Yaṭmaʿu.* The root *ṭ-m-ʿ* is not neutral wanting. It is *coveting,* greedy reaching, the appetite that strains toward a thing. And notice the word that hinges the verse: **ثُمَّ — *thumma.*** "Then." "After all that." It is not a soft "and." It is a "then" with a raised eyebrow. *I gave, and I gave, and I gave, and I leveled his whole road — and **then?** Then he reaches for more.*

And al-Muyassar catches a detail that makes the ingratitude sharper still. What was it he longed to be given *more* of? Not some new horizon, some unmet need. Al-Muyassar spells it out: *an azīda lahu fī mālihi wa waladih* — that I increase him *in his wealth and his children* — the very two gifts that had just been laid before him. He was not even reaching past the blessings he had. He was standing on top of them, ignoring the Hand that gave them, and asking that same Hand for a larger pile of the identical thing. The gift had become invisible; only the *quantity* was still in view.

This is the Qur'an naming the hedonic treadmill in the seventh century, in one verb and one particle. The wealth became his floor. The sons became his floor. The smoothed life became his floor. And from that floor — a floor most of humanity will never stand on — he looked up and *wanted.*

And then comes the single word that answers the wanting: **كَلَّا — *kallā.*** "No." We usually hear it as a general rebuke, and it is that. But al-Muyassar reads it first as a flat, specific refusal *of the very thing he was reaching for:* *laysa al-amru kamā yazʿumu hādhā al-fājir al-athīm, lā azīduhu ʿalā dhālik* — **"It is not as this brazen sinner imagines; I will not increase him beyond what he has."** Sit with how exact that is. The man longed for *more* — *more* wealth, *more* sons, the same gifts in a larger pile — and the divine answer is not silence and not a vague warning. It is the door of the treadmill slammed shut: *the increase you crave, you will not get. The pile stops here.* The appetite that was sure it only needed a little more to be content is told, in one syllable, that the "more" it built its life around will never arrive.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing I most need you to see. The Qur'an does not present this as al-Walīd's private disease. It presents it as the *human* disease. Listen to how the same diagnosis appears elsewhere:

> *Kallā inna l-insāna la-yaṭghā · an raʾāhu staghnā.*
> **"No — indeed, the human being transgresses, the moment he sees himself self-sufficient."** (96:6–7)

The trigger for rebellion, the Qur'an says, is not *lack.* It is *fullness.* It is the feeling — *I have enough, I have arrived, I am self-made, I am al-Waḥīd, the one-and-only.* That feeling is the carbon monoxide of the soul: odorless, invisible, and it puts you to sleep while it kills you.

So let me ask you, gently, to do something uncomfortable. Don't think about al-Walīd. Think about the last upgrade in your own life. The thing you were sure would satisfy you. How long did the gratitude last before it became the new normal — before you stopped *seeing* it as a gift at all, and started seeing it as the baseline you were owed, and started looking past it to the next thing?

That gap — between how long the *gift* lasted and how short the *gratitude* lasted — that gap is the treadmill. That gap is *thumma yaṭmaʿu.* And it is in every one of us.

[PAUSE]

Here is the way out, and it is not complicated, though it is hard. The opposite of the treadmill is not getting less. It is *seeing more* — seeing what is already in your hands as what it actually is: a gift, undeserved, that could be withdrawn, given by Someone. The man in this verse had the cradle and saw a launching pad. The believer is the one who has far less and *sees the cradle* — and weeps with gratitude that he was placed in it.

So the practical change for tomorrow is small and specific: **before you let yourself want the next thing, name three things already in your hands as gifts — out loud, to Allah, by name.** Not "thank You for everything." That is the floor talking. Name them. *This roof. This child's breathing. This cup of water I did not have to dig a well for.* Naming is how you step off the treadmill. Because the moment a thing is *named as a gift,* it stops being a floor — and becomes, again, a wonder.

### Theme Two: The smoothed road as the deepest test

Now let me take you somewhere harder.

We have a deep, almost unkillable assumption — and most of us carry it without ever saying it out loud. The assumption is this: **comfort is approval.** If my life is smooth, God must be pleased with me. If the road is level, I must be on the right road. We read ease as a verdict in our favor.

The Qur'an puts that assumption on the table and breaks it in two.

Look again at the man in these verses. By every external measure, his life was *blessed.* Wealth to the horizon. Sons at his side. A road Allah Himself describes as *thoroughly smoothed* — *mahhadtu lahu tamhīdā.* If comfort were approval, this man was the most approved-of soul in Mecca.

And what does Allah say of him in the same breath? **إِنَّهُ كَانَ لِآيَاتِنَا عَنِيدًا — *innahu kāna li-āyātinā ʿanīdā*** — "he set himself, stubbornly, hard, *against* Our signs." The root *ʿ-n-d* is the obstinacy of someone who *knows* and digs in anyway. Remember — this is the man who *heard* the Qur'an and was *moved* by it and called it sweet, and then chose his position over the truth he had already tasted.

And notice what "Our signs" *means* here. Al-Muyassar widens it: *kāna lil-Qurʾāni wa ḥujaji Allāhi ʿalā khalqihi muʿānidan mukadhdhiban* — he was obstinate toward **"the Qur'an, and the proofs of Allah upon His creation."** The *āyāt* he dug in against were not only the recited verses; they were *every* standing evidence of his Lord — the wealth, the sons, the smoothed road themselves were *āyāt,* signs pointing back to a Giver, and he set himself against the message they carried. And in that same gloss the obstinacy does not stand alone: al-Muyassar pairs *ʿanīd* with **مُكَذِّب — *mukadhdhib,* one who cries lies.** Obstinacy is the posture; belying is the act. He did not merely refuse to bow — he stood over the truth he had privately tasted and called it, in public, a lie.

And al-Muyassar will not let us soften it into mere stubbornness. He names al-Walīd *al-fājir al-athīm* — the brazen sinner — and describes him as one who *bāraza* Allah and His Messenger *bi-l-muḥāraba:* a man who stepped out onto open ground to *wage war.* Picture the image hiding in that word. Not someone who quietly looked away, but a champion striding out for single combat — squaring up, in the open, against his own Creator. That is the temperature of *ʿanīd* here. *Obstinacy* is the cold word for it. The hot word is *war.* And he picked that fight from the most comfortable position in Mecca.

[PAUSE]

So here is the thing we have to face. His smooth road was not God's approval. His smooth road was the *test he was failing.*

The Qur'an has a word for this, and it should make us tremble: **istidrāj.** It is the principle that Allah sometimes responds to a person's turning-away not with hardship — but with *more ease.* More wealth. More smoothness. A longer, flatter, more comfortable road. Not as a reward. As a rope. *We will draw them in by degrees from where they do not know* (7:182). The comfort itself becomes the test, and the more comfortable it gets, the deeper the sleep, and the harder it becomes to ever wake.

And the mufassirūn refuse to quarantine any of this in the past. Al-Muyassar, having named al-Walīd, immediately widens the lens in the same breath: *wa hādhā jazāʾu kulli man* — "and **this is the recompense of everyone who**" does the same. The verse came down about one man in Mecca. It was never meant to stay about him.

This is the terrifying flip side of *mahhadtu.* A smoothed road is a mercy if it *bends your knee in gratitude.* The exact same smoothed road is a trap if it makes you forget Who flattened it. The road doesn't tell you which one it is. **Your heart's response to the road tells you.**

[PAUSE]

And this should completely rewire how we read our own ease.

When life is smooth — when the wealth is stretched out and the people you love are present and the road is level under your feet — the question is no longer "*am I being rewarded?*" The question becomes much sharper and much more useful: **"What is this comfort doing to my heart? Is it bending me toward Allah in gratitude — or is it quietly convincing me I don't need Him?"**

Because the cradle and the trap look *identical* from the outside. Both are soft. Both are level. The only difference is invisible — it is whether the one lying in it remembers the One who made the bed.

And now you understand why the punishment in this passage takes the shape it does. The *ṣaʿūd,* the steep endless climb — it is not only the inversion of his comfort. It is the *correction* of it. The man spent a lifetime on a flat road that let him forget he was utterly dependent — that he came into the world *waḥīdan,* with nothing. So he is given a road that makes dependence *impossible to forget* — a climb so steep that every single step screams *I cannot do this on my own.* The flat road let him pretend he was self-sufficient. The steep road strips the pretense away forever. It is, in the most severe possible sense, the truth he refused — handed back to him as terrain.

So the practical change here is a habit of suspicion toward your own ease — not anxiety, *suspicion.* The next time life is smooth, don't only enjoy it. Interrogate it. Ask: *is this cradle making me softer toward Allah, or harder? Am I praying more from this comfort, or less? Am I more aware of the Giver, or have I started to feel I earned the gift?* The smooth road is not the answer. It is the *exam.* And most of us have been reading the exam as if it were the grade.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, now that you can see it

Let me show you the whole shape one more time, because now every piece is in your hands.

The passage is a perfect, closing ring:

- It **opens** with **وَحِيدًا — alone.** He came into the world owning nothing, *needing* everything. The state of total dependence.
- It **fills the middle** with the gifts that *answered* that dependence — wealth *stretched out,* sons *present,* a road *thoroughly smoothed.* Allah meeting the man's nothingness with everything.
- It **pivots** on **ثُمَّ يَطْمَعُ — then he wants more** — the gifts forgotten the instant they were received, the floor mistaken for the ceiling.
- And it **closes** with **صَعُودًا — the steep climb** — the smoothed road folded back into a vertical one, the cradle inverted into a mountain, the man sent back to the *aloneness* he started in — except now the aloneness is a slope he climbs forever.

He began *alone with nothing, and was given everything.* He ends *alone with nothing, climbing forever.* The ring closes on the exact word it opened with — except the second "alone" is the one he built himself, by refusing to thank the One who filled the first.

### The two journeys were one journey

Notice that the linguistic discoveries and the themes were never two separate things — they were the same truth seen from two sides.

The word **waḥīdā** *is* the theme of the treadmill: he forgot he was created with nothing, so nothing was ever enough.

The word **mahhadtu** *is* the theme of comfort-as-test: the smoothed road was never the verdict — it was the trial he slept through.

And the word **ṣaʿūdā** *is* both themes resolving at once: the endpoint of wanting-more, and the unmasking of the smoothed road for what it always was.

The language was never decoration on top of the meaning. The language *was* the meaning. Allah said it all in the choice of words — we only walked slowly enough to see it.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. Don't answer them quickly. Let them follow you out of this sitting.

1. What is the *waḥīd* in your life — the title, the achievement, the thing that makes you feel exceptional and self-made? And can you trace, honestly, where it actually came from?
2. Measure the gap in yourself: how *long* did the last gift last, and how *short* did your gratitude for it last? What lives in that gap?
3. Where is your road smooth right now — and is that smoothness bending you toward Allah, or quietly teaching you that you don't need Him?
4. If Allah were to describe *your* life the way He described al-Walīd's — listing what He gave, and then a single word for how you responded — what would that word be?

### One sentence

**Everything you have was given to a soul that arrived owning nothing — and the only question that matters is whether the gift makes you remember the Giver, or makes you forget that you were ever *alone.***

### The closing invitation

You do not have to be al-Walīd to learn from al-Walīd. You only have to be honest about the treadmill turning under your own feet, and the cradle you may be lying in without thanking the One who smoothed it.

So here is the invitation — not an obligation, an invitation. Tonight, before you sleep, look at the road you are on. If it is smooth, do not assume you have passed. Bend your knee on it. Name what is in your hands as gifts, by name, to the One who placed them there. Step off the treadmill for one honest minute. And let the comfort you have been given become — at last — the thing that brings you *back* to Him, instead of the thing that lets you forget Him.

### A closing du'ā

> O Allah — You created me *alone,* owning nothing, and then You stretched out gifts before me that I did not earn. Do not let Your blessings become the rope that draws me away from You. Let every ease bend my knee in gratitude instead of hardening my heart in forgetting. And when my road is smooth, keep me awake — so that I never mistake the cradle of Your mercy for proof that I no longer need You.
>
> Āmīn.
