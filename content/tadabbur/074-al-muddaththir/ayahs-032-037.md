# The Night That Turns Its Back, The Dawn That Unveils

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to these words first. Just the sound of them, before any meaning.

> **كَلَّا وَالْقَمَرِ • وَاللَّيْلِ إِذْ أَدْبَرَ • وَالصُّبْحِ إِذَا أَسْفَرَ • إِنَّهَا لَإِحْدَى الْكُبَرِ • نَذِيرًا لِّلْبَشَرِ • لِمَن شَاءَ مِنكُمْ أَن يَتَقَدَّمَ أَوْ يَتَأَخَّرَ**

> *Kallā wal-qamar • wal-layli idh adbar • waṣ-ṣubḥi idhā asfar • innahā la-iḥdā al-kubar • nadhīran lil-bashar • liman shā'a minkum an yataqaddama aw yata'akhkhar.*

> "Nay! By the moon, and by the night as it turns its back to depart, and by the morning as it unveils itself — indeed, it is one of the greatest [calamities], a warning to mankind — to whoever among you wills to step forward or to hold back."

Did you hear it? Almost every line lands on the same sound. *Qamar. Adbar. Asfar. Kubar. Bashar. Ta'akhkhar.* Six lines, one closing beat, struck again and again like a hand against a door at night. *-ar. -ar. -ar.* We will come back to that drumbeat, because the sound itself is doing work.

### Where we are standing

To feel these verses, you have to stand where the first listeners stood.

This is one of the earliest passages of the Quran, revealed in Makkah, in the opening years, when the message was new and raw and dangerous. Just before these verses, Allah had mentioned a fire called **Saqar**, and He said over it are **nineteen** guardians. And the proud men of Makkah laughed. Classical commentary relates that one of their leaders scoffed — *only nineteen?* — boasting that a handful of his men could overpower the keepers of Hell. The number became a joke to them. A thing to flex against.

But that exact number was never arbitrary. Ibn Kathir relays the divine purpose stated just before our passage (74:31): Allah fixed the count of the guardians *as a trial* — so that the People of Scripture would arrive at certainty, the believers would increase in faith, and those with disease in their hearts and the deniers would stumble and say, "What does Allah intend by this?" In other words, the very same number sorts the room. One figure — nineteen — settles some hearts and scandalizes others. What the mockers turned into a punchline was, all along, a sieve.

So the passage opens by turning to face that laughter. And the first word is a word that shuts a mouth.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Here is what I want you to sit with for the next hour.

Allah is about to swear an oath. And when Allah swears, He swears by things you can *see* — the moon, the night, the morning. But He never swears by something merely because it is beautiful. Every object of His oath is a finger pointing somewhere else, toward something you *cannot* see.

So the question is this: **why these three?** Why the moon, and then specifically the night *as it leaves*, and the morning *as it arrives*? Out of the entire sky, why choose the two moments of motion — the dark pulling away, the light coming forward?

And then — having sworn by a sky that *retreats and advances* — why does He end by telling you that you are free to *step forward or hold back*?

Hold that question. The whole sky is about to describe your soul back to you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Kallā* — the word that closes a door so another can open

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse.

There is a small word, **كَلَّا** (*kallā*), that is almost impossible to translate cleanly. In English we reach for "Nay," or "No," or "By no means." But *kallā* is sharper than any of those. It is the sound you make when someone says something so wrong that you don't argue with it — you stop it. It is a hand raised flat. It is "Enough. That's done."

Now, the scholars looked closely at this *kallā* at the opening of our passage, and something beautiful emerged from their differences.

**Al-Qurtubi** reports the view of **al-Farrā'**, who said this *kallā* is a *ṣilat al-qasam* — a connector that simply leads into the oath, so the meaning is essentially "...by the moon." On this reading you don't even pause on it; it leans forward into the swearing. **Al-Baghawi** says plainly: *"this is an oath; it means ḥaqqan* — truly." And **al-Jalālayn** glosses it as *istiftāḥ bi-maʿnā alā* — an opening particle meaning "Lo!" or "Truly."

But al-Qurtubi also relays that **al-Ṭabari permitted stopping on it** — pausing fully — and reading it as a rebuttal hurled back at the ones who claimed they could overpower the guardians of the Fire. On *that* reading, *kallā* means: **"No. You cannot. Never."**

And **al-Muyassar** widens the target by one notch. He reads the rebuttal as aimed not merely at the one boast about the guardians, but at the whole posture of denial behind it: *laysa al-amr kamā dhakarū min al-takdhīb lil-rasūl fīmā jā'a bihi* — "the matter is not as they claimed in their rejection of the Messenger and what he brought." So the door this little word slams is not only one man's bravado about nineteen angels; it is the entire stance of disbelief that produced the bravado. *Kallā* — none of it stands.

[PAUSE]

Now notice — we are not forced to choose. This is a composite, and both readings are true at once. *Kallā* does two things in a single breath. It **slams a door**: *No — your boast about overpowering nineteen angels is empty air.* And in the same motion it **opens a door**: *Truly — by the moon, listen now.*

It clears the room. The laughter is swept out. And into that sudden silence, the oath begins.

Think about how a teacher silences a noisy room. Not by speaking louder than the noise — by one sharp word that drops everything to stillness, and *then* speaking softly into the quiet. *Kallā* is that sharp word. Everything you were so sure of — set it down. Now look up at the sky.

**What this single word reveals:** Before Allah shows you the truth, He first removes the noise of your certainty. The bravado has to die before the oath can be heard.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Adbara* and *Asfara* — the night that turns its back, the dawn that unveils its face

This is the heart of the passage. Stay very close to me here.

Allah swears by three things: the moon, the night, the morning. But watch *how* He describes the night and the morning — because He does not describe them at rest. He describes them in motion, and He chooses His verbs with terrifying precision.

First, a small lesson in how Arabic builds words. Arabic takes a three-letter root and bends it into shapes called *forms*. One of these, **Form IV** (*afʿala*), most often makes something *happen to* an object — it is the causative shape, the form that pushes an action outward onto a thing. But a small family of verbs lives inside Form IV *intransitively* — naming a motion the subject makes entirely on its own, with no object to receive it. Both of our verbs here are exactly that kind: the night and the morning are not acted upon; they *move themselves.* Keep that in mind.

**The night:** *wal-layli idh **adbar*** — "by the night as it ***adbara***."

The root here is **د-ب-ر** (*d-b-r*), and at its core it means **the back** — *dubur* is literally the back, the rear of a thing. *Adbara*, in Form IV, does not mean the night "ended" or "got dark." It means the night **turned its back on you and walked away.** The night, in Arabic, is given a spine and a shoulder. It rotates. It shows you its back. It *departs.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that image. The night is not switched off like a lamp. The night is a presence that *leaves* — it turns, and goes, the way a guest gathers their coat and steps out into the dark, the way someone you've been arguing with finally turns and walks down the hall. *Adbara.* It showed you its back.

This departure-reading is not a flourish; it is where the great commentators land. **Al-Ṭabari** glosses *idh adbara* as *wallā dhāhiban* — "when it turned away, departing." **Al-Muyassar** says the same in two words, *wallā wa-dhahaba* — "it turned away and went." (There is a small *ikhtilāf* worth knowing among the reciters and exegetes: the word is read both as *adbara* and, in another reading, *dabara*, and **al-Jalālayn** even leans toward the night that *comes on after the day* — *jā'a baʿd al-nahār*. The image of arrival and the image of departure both have a home in the tradition. But the two running commentaries that walk through the verse word by word, Ṭabari and al-Muyassar, both hear the night *leaving* — and that is the reading these verses are built on here.)

**The morning:** *waṣ-ṣubḥi idhā **asfar*** — "by the morning as it ***asfara***."

Now this verb. The root is **س-ف-ر** (*s-f-r*), and you must feel where else this root lives in Arabic. *Asfara* in Form IV means to **shine, to brighten** — yes. But the deepest sense of *s-f-r* is **to uncover, to unveil, to remove a covering from a face.** When a woman lifts the veil from her face, the Arabs said *safarat al-mar'ah* — she *unveiled.* The traveler is a *musāfir* because he *uncovers* distance, strips away the ground between here and there.

So the dawn does not merely "come." **The dawn removes a veil.** *Asfara aṣ-ṣubḥ* — the morning **unveiled its face.** The light is not added to the world; a covering is *taken off* the world. What was hidden in the dark is suddenly *shown.*

And this is not a meaning read *into* the word from the outside. **Al-Muyassar** glosses *asfara* here with two verbs joined together — *aḍā'a wa-nkashafa* — "it gave light *and was uncovered.*" Not just brightness, but *uncovering* — *inkashafa*, the very verb you use when a veil is lifted. **Al-Jalālayn** strips it to a single word: *ẓahara* — "it became apparent," it was *made to appear.* The classical lexicon and the classical tafsir agree: the dawn does not manufacture the day; it *discloses* it.

[PAUSE]

Now put the two verbs side by side and feel what Allah has built.

- The night — *adbara* — **turns its back and retreats.**
- The dawn — *asfara* — **turns its face and unveils.**

One motion *away.* One motion *toward.* One hides; one shows. One pulls back into darkness; one comes forward into light.

Here is what is remarkable. **These are the two directions of the entire passage.** Hold that thought — we are going to find them again at the very end, hidden inside your own freedom. Allah is sketching, in the sky, with the dark and the light, the *shape* of a choice He is about to lay at your feet.

And notice the spiritual physics of *asfara* especially. The Fire that this oath is about to name — Saqar — is precisely something **uncovered.** A warning *unveils* what was hidden. You were walking in the dark, unable to see the edge of the cliff; the dawn *asfara* — it pulled the veil off the landscape and now you can see exactly where the drop is. **The warning is the dawn.** It does not create the danger. It *uncovers* it, so you can see to step.

**What these two verbs reveal:** The cosmos itself is built out of retreat and arrival, hiding and unveiling — and Allah swears by exactly those two motions because they are about to become the two motions of your soul.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Three: *Nadhīran lil-bashar* — and the word that names both the victim and the audience

[ayah:74:35] إِنَّهَا لَإِحْدَى ٱلْكُبَرِ

*It* — *innahā*, feminine — refers back to Saqar, the Fire; **al-Jalālayn** says it plainly: *innahā — ay Saqar.* And *al-kubar* is a plural of intensity: not just "big," but **the gravest of grave things.** Al-Jalālayn fills the word out — *al-balāyā al-ʿiẓām,* "the colossal calamities" — and al-Muyassar matches him: *iḥdā al-ʿaẓā'im,* one of the supreme catastrophes of existence. The deniers had shrunk the Fire into a joke they could overpower. Allah restores its true size: *it is one of the very greatest.*

[ayah:74:36] نَذِيرًا لِّلْبَشَرِ

Before we reach *bashar,* notice one fine thing the grammarians caught in *nadhīran* itself. It arrives in the **masculine** — yet it is describing the Fire, which all along has been spoken of as feminine (*innahā… iḥdā al-kubar,* all feminine). Why the sudden shift? **Al-Jalālayn** explains: *nadhīran* is a circumstantial state (*ḥāl*) drawn from "*iḥdā*," and *it was made masculine because by now the Fire is being spoken of in the sense of al-ʿadhāb* — the punishment, the torment. Feel what that means. At the exact instant the verse turns to *warn* you, the Fire stops being a distant, abstract "it" and hardens into *the torment itself* — *al-ʿadhāb* — and even the grammar bends to register the change. The word for "warning" takes the gender of the word for "punishment," because a warning is only ever a warning *about a real and coming pain.*

Now, Arabic has more than one word for "human being." There is *insān*, there is *nās*, and there is **بَشَر** (*bashar*). And *bashar* is not neutral. The root **ب-ش-ر** is tied to the **skin, the outer surface, the complexion** — *basharah* is literally the skin of a person. *Bashar* names the human being precisely as a **creature of flesh and skin.**

Here is what should stop your breath.

Go back just a few verses, to 74:29, where Allah first described this same Fire. He said it is **لَوَّاحَةٌ لِّلْبَشَرِ** — *lawwāḥatun lil-bashar* — "**scorching to the bashar.**" The classical commentators noted the double sense: it scorches *mankind*, and it scorches *the skin* — blackening it, searing the surface.

So look. **The same word — *bashar* — names the thing the Fire burns, and names the audience the Fire warns.**

- 74:29 — the Fire is *scorching to the bashar* (the skin / the human).
- 74:36 — the Fire is *a warning to the bashar* (the human / the skin).

The very flesh that the Fire would scorch is the flesh that is being warned. Allah does not say "a warning to *the people*" in some abstract civic sense. He says: a warning **to the skin** — to the part of you that would feel it. He aims the warning at the exact surface that would burn.

[PAUSE]

Feel the mercy and the terror folded together in one word. It is as if a doctor, describing a disease that attacks the heart, looks at you and says: *and this is a warning — to your heart.* Not "to the public." To *the organ that is at risk.* The warning is addressed to precisely what is vulnerable.

**What this word reveals:** The warning is not a general announcement floating above humanity. It is aimed at the flesh that would feel the flame. The audience and the victim are named by the same word — because they are meant to be the same person, *unless that person hears.*

### Bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three things. A word — *kallā* — that clears the room of noise. Two verbs — *adbara* and *asfara* — that paint retreat and arrival, hiding and unveiling, across the whole sky. And one word — *bashar* — that points the warning at the exact skin that would burn.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about you.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: A warning is the dawn breaking over your life

Let me start where you actually live.

We have a complicated relationship with being warned. Think about the smoke detector that shrieks at 3 a.m. because of a little steam from the shower. Think about the friend who says, carefully, "I'm worried about how much you've been drinking." Think about the doctor who calls you back into the office and asks you to sit down. Think about the dashboard light that comes on while you're already late.

What is our first reaction? Almost always: **irritation.** We resent the warning. We feel accused. We want to silence the alarm, change the subject, look away from the light on the dashboard. There is a part of the human heart that experiences a warning as an *attack.*

Now hold that resentment up against this verse.

Allah calls the Fire *nadhīran* — **a warning.** And the entire architecture of these verses tells you that this warning is the *dawn* — *asfara*, the unveiling. Remember what we found: the morning does not create the danger. It *uncovers* the landscape so you can see the edge before you walk off it.

[PAUSE]

So here is the reframe. **To be warned is to be shown the cliff while you still have feet to step back with.**

Think about who does *not* get warned. The person walking toward the cliff in total darkness, with no one calling out, no light breaking — that person is not being spared. That person is being abandoned. The cruelty would be *silence.* The mercy is the alarm.

The deniers in Makkah heard "Saqar — nineteen guardians" and laughed, as though Allah were threatening them. But a threat and a warning are opposite things. A threat says: *I want to harm you.* A warning says: *I do not want you harmed — so look.* The whole reason the Fire is *described in advance,* in detail, with its name and its keepers and its scorching of the skin, is so that not one human being arrives at it *surprised.*

And notice — the night is *adbara.* It is *still leaving.* It has turned its back and is walking away, which means the dawn is not yet fully here, which means **there is still time.** The oath is sworn in the gray hour, the in-between, the moment when the dark is departing and the light is arriving. That is exactly the hour you are living in right now. Not the full noon of the reckoning. The dawn, breaking. The veil, lifting. *While you can still see to choose.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time something warns you — a verse, a fear in the chest at 3 a.m., a friend's careful word, the sudden awareness of how you've been living — try, just once, to receive it not as an accusation but as *dawn.* As a veil being lifted off your own life by Someone who does not want you to walk off the edge. The discomfort of the warning is the discomfort of light hitting eyes that had gotten used to the dark. Don't reach to switch it off. Let it *asfara* — let it unveil.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: You move yourself — the reflexive freedom of *yataqaddama aw yata'akhkhara*

Now the passage does something that I find almost unbearable in its gentleness. After all the gravity — the greatest calamity, the warning, the scorching — it ends not with a command and not with a threat, but with a sentence about *your will.*

[ayah:74:37] لِمَن شَآءَ مِنكُمْ أَن يَتَقَدَّمَ أَوْ يَتَأَخَّرَ
*liman shā'a minkum an yataqaddama aw yata'akhkhar* —
"to **whoever among you wills** to step forward or to hold back."

Let me start, again, where you live.

There is a concept in psychology called *locus of control.* People with an external locus believe life happens *to* them — their choices are pushed by circumstances, other people, luck, their childhood, their brain chemistry. People with an internal locus believe that, whatever the pressures, the move is finally *theirs.* And almost all of us, when we are afraid, slide toward the external. *I couldn't help it. They made me. I had no choice. It's just how I am.* We outsource the steering wheel.

Now feel what Allah does to that instinct with two verbs.

The two verbs at the end — *yataqaddama* (to step forward) and *yata'akhkhara* (to hold back) — are both in **Form V** of Arabic, the form built on the pattern *tafaʿʿala.* And Form V carries a specific flavor: it is **reflexive.** The action turns back on the doer. It is not "to be advanced" or "to be pushed back." It is *to advance one's own self.* *To hold one's own self back.* The doer and the done-to are the same person.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight of that? Allah did not say "for whoever We drive forward or drag behind." He did not even say "whoever goes forward or back." He chose the *reflexive* forms, the forms that put the hand firmly on **your** shoulder and say: *you move you.* No one pushes you toward the Fire. No one drags you to safety against your will. *Yataqaddama* — **you step yourself forward.** *Yata'akhkhara* — **you hold yourself back.**

And it is sealed at the front by *liman shā'a minkum* — "for **whoever wills** among you." *Shā'a* — wills, wishes, chooses. The door of the whole sentence is *will.*

And here the grammar does something quietly devastating. **Al-Jalālayn** notes that *liman shā'a minkum* — "for whoever wills among you" — is a *badal,* a grammatical **substitution**, for *al-bashar* in the line before it. Stop and feel that. Two verses ago, *bashar* was the flesh the Fire would *scorch* (74:29) and the flesh the warning is *aimed at* (74:36) — the vulnerable skin, the part of you that would burn. And now that exact word is *replaced* in the sentence by **"whoever wills."** The skin that would feel the flame and the will that chooses its direction are filled into the *same slot.* The one being warned *is* the one who gets to decide. Allah does not warn a passive object and then steer it; He warns you and then, in the very next breath, hands you the wheel.

And the commentators do not leave "forward" and "back" as empty directions. They tell you exactly what the two motions *are.* **Al-Muyassar** reads *yataqaddama* as *yataqarrab ilā rabbihi bi-fiʿl al-ṭāʿāt* — "to draw nearer to his Lord through acts of obedience" — and *yata'akhkhar* as falling behind *bi-fiʿl al-maʿāṣī,* "through committing sins." **Al-Jalālayn** makes it starker still: to step forward is to move *ilā al-khayr… ilā al-jannah bil-īmān* — "toward the good, toward the Garden, through faith" — and to hold back is to slide *ilā al-sharr… ilā al-nār bil-kufr,* "toward evil, toward the Fire, through disbelief." So this is not abstract motion in a void. *Taqaddum* is obedience, is faith, is the Garden. *Ta'akhkhur* is sin, is denial, is the Fire. The two directions in the sky have become, in the classical reading, the two destinations of a life.

Now — remember the question I asked you at the very beginning? Why did Allah swear by the moon, the night, the dawn?

Here is the answer, and it lands now.

[PAUSE]

**The moon has no choice.** It runs its measured course because it was commanded to; it cannot decide to rise late. **The night has no choice** — when it is time, it *adbara,* it turns its back and goes, whether it would stay or not. **The dawn has no choice** — when the command comes, it *asfara,* it unveils, on time, every time, without consultation.

The entire sky that Allah swears by is a sky of **perfect, will-less obedience.** Sun, moon, night, dawn — they move by decree. They cannot step forward or hold back. They have no *shā'a.*

And then, at the end of the oath, Allah turns from that obedient, determined cosmos to *you* — and gives you the one thing He withheld from all of it: **will.** *Liman shā'a minkum.* The moon cannot choose. *You can.*

[PAUSE]

This is the staggering thing. Allah swears **by** the things that have no freedom — **to** the one creature that has it. He calls the witness of a cosmos that cannot disobey, in order to address the creature that can. The oath itself is the argument: *Look at this entire sky moving in flawless submission, none of it free — and I have given freedom to you. So what will you do with it?*

And now — the architecture closes. Go back to *adbara* and *asfara,* the night that retreats and the dawn that advances. Those two cosmic motions — **away** and **toward** — were never just about the sky. They were a rehearsal. Because the verse ends on the two motions of the human soul: *yata'akhkhara* — to **hold back, to retreat** (the way the night turned its back) — and *yataqaddama* — to **step forward, to advance** (the way the dawn came forward into the light).

The night models *ta'akhkhur.* The dawn models *taqaddum.* Allah showed you the two directions written across the sky in light and dark — *and then handed you the choice of which one to walk.* The sky retreats and advances *by command.* You retreat or advance *by will.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Stop saying "I had no choice." On the things that matter to your soul, that sentence is almost never true, and these verses know it. Every prayer you stand for, every gaze you lower, every word you swallow, every act of worship you *step toward* and every sin you *hold yourself back from* — these are *Form V* movements. They are reflexive. They are *yours.* The dignity Allah gave you is the same as the terror: no one will do it for you, and no one is doing it to you. You move you. So tonight, pick one thing — one — and *yataqaddam.* Step yourself one pace toward Him, on purpose, by will, the way the dawn comes forward into the light.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape, because it is breathtaking in its symmetry.

The passage is built in two mirrored halves around a single hinge:

1. **The clearing** — *Kallā.* The noise is swept out.
2. **The oath of two motions** — the night *adbara* (retreat, away, into dark) and the dawn *asfara* (advance, toward, into light).
3. **The hinge** — *it is one of the greatest; a warning to the bashar.* The Fire, named at its true size, aimed at the very skin that would feel it.
4. **The choice of two motions** — you *yata'akhkhara* (hold back, retreat) or *yataqaddama* (step forward, advance).

Do you see it? The two motions of the *sky* in the first half — away and toward — become the two motions of the *soul* in the second half — away and toward. The oath and its answer are the same shape. Allah swore by the retreat and the advance of the heavens **in order to** put the retreat and the advance of your heart into your own hands.

And the whole thing is hammered together by sound — that relentless rhyme we heard at the start: *qamar, adbar, asfar, kubar, bashar, ta'akhkhar.* Six beats on the same syllable, like a fist on a door, like a heartbeat speeding up, like an alarm that will not let you sleep through the dawn. The *sound* is the warning. The form is the meaning.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were one truth seen from two sides.

*Asfara* — the dawn that *unveils* — taught us that **a warning is mercy**: it does not make the danger, it uncovers it while you can still see to step. And the reflexive *yataqaddama / yata'akhkhara* taught us that **you are free** — that the stepping is genuinely yours. Put them together and you have the entire spiritual situation of a human being: **the light has been turned on, the cliff has been shown, the time is the gray hour of a departing night — and the next step belongs to you alone.**

### Questions to carry

- When something in my life warns me, do I receive it as an attack to be silenced — or as a dawn lifting the veil off a danger I could not see?
- The moon cannot choose; I can. What have I been doing with the one gift Allah withheld from the entire obedient sky?
- Where in my life have I been saying "I had no choice," when the verb was really reflexive all along — *I held myself back*?
- It is still the gray hour; the night has turned its back but is still walking away. What one step forward can I take *before the dawn is full*?
- If the warning is aimed at the very part of me that would feel the Fire, then who is the One sending it — and what does it mean that He does not want me there?

### The whole passage in one sentence

Allah swears by a sky that retreats into night and advances into dawn — a sky with no will of its own — to tell you that the Fire is a danger *unveiled to you in mercy while there is still time,* and that you, alone among His creation, are free to step toward it or away from it.

### The closing invitation

You are living in the *adbara* hour — the dark has turned its back, the dawn is *asfara*-ing, the veil is lifting off your life. Nothing in these verses pushes you. Allah ends not with "you must" but with *liman shā'a* — "for whoever wills." The door is held open, not shoved. So the invitation is simply this: do not stand still and call it fate. Take the gift the moon was never given. *Step yourself forward.*

### A brief du'ā

O Allah, You who turn the night to retreat and unveil the morning by Your command — unveil the truth to my heart the way You unveil the dawn, and do not let me sleep through Your warning. You gave me a will the very heavens do not possess; let me spend it stepping toward You and not holding back. Make me of those who, when warned, draw nearer — *liman shā'a an yataqaddam.* Āmīn.
