# The Taste of Knowing

## The Two Ayahs

## Introduction: The World These Words Fell Into

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

You are in Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ has been preaching for years now, and something strange is happening in the city. It is not that people don't believe him. Many of them believe him precisely *because* they know him. This is the man they nicknamed *al-Amīn* — the Trustworthy — before a single word of revelation came down. They handed him their valuables for safekeeping. They asked him to arbitrate their disputes. When they wanted to slander the Qur'an, the sharpest minds among them couldn't call it poetry, couldn't call it the speech of a soothsayer, couldn't fit it into any category they had.

There is a report that has come down about this very surah. Fussilat opens, and by the thirteenth ayah it delivers a warning so stark that when the Prophet ﷺ recited it to ʿUtba ibn Rabīʿa — one of the great chiefs of Quraysh, sent to negotiate, to *bribe* the Prophet into silence — ʿUtba put his hand over the Prophet's mouth and begged him to stop, for the sake of their kinship. He went back to his people shaken, saying he had heard something that was not the speech of any man. And then — [PAUSE] — and then they rejected it anyway.

That is the world. That is the emotional climate. These are not people in the dark being punished for not finding the light. These are people standing in full daylight, feeling the warmth of the sun on their faces, insisting the sky is black.

And notice the *name* of this surah. **Fussilat.** From a root that means to be separated out, made distinct, laid out in detail, explained until nothing is left blurry. Allah is naming the surah after His own clarity. As if to say: *the signs were not obscure. I made them distinct. I separated truth from falsehood so cleanly that a child could tell them apart.* This is the strand running through the whole surah — clarity offered, clarity refused. The skin and hearing and eyesight of these very people will testify against them later in this surah (41:20–22), and they will ask their own bodies, *why did you speak against us?*

So when we arrive at ayahs 27 and 28, we are arriving at a verdict. And here is what should keep you awake tonight: this passage about the severest punishment is not, at its heart, about heat. It is about *knowing*. Somewhere inside these two ayahs, Allah names the actual crime — and it is not the crime you would expect. It is a crime that lives, right now, in the most modern corners of your own heart.

Let me show you.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### The word that should not fit — *nudhīq*, "to make taste"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the ayah.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns, and the patterns *do things*. There is a base pattern — a plain verb, "he did." And then there is a heightened pattern, what the grammarians call Form IV, where you take that action and you make *someone else* undergo it. English does this with clumsy helper-words: I *eat*, but I *feed* you. I *know*, but I *make* you *know*. Arabic does it inside the shape of the word itself. The word becomes causative — it reaches out and puts the action *into* another person.

Now here is the base verb we're dealing with: *dhāqa* — to taste. It's the everyday word for what your tongue does with a morsel of food. It is intimate, small, sensory. You don't *taste* an ocean. You taste a drop. Tasting is the verb of the tiniest, most personal sampling.

Watch what Allah does with it.

He does not say *nuʿadhdhib* — "We will punish." He does not say *nuḥriq* — "We will burn." He reaches for the causative of the smallest sensory verb in the language: *lanudhīqanna* — **"We will most certainly make them taste."**

Sit with this. Why *taste*?

Notice the collision inside the phrase. On one side, *nudhīq* — a word built for a drop on the tongue. On the other side, its object: *ʿadhāban shadīdan* — a *severe* punishment, and in the next ayah, an *eternal home*. The vocabulary of the infinitely small, wrapped around the infinitely vast. That is not an accident of style. **That is the point.**

Here is one way to sit with what that collision opens. When you *taste* something, no one can taste it for you. I can describe honey to you for an hour — its color, its weight, the way it coats the spoon — and you will know *about* honey. But the instant it touches your tongue, all my description collapses into a single fact you now own and cannot un-own. Tasting is the end of secondhand knowledge. It is the moment a thing stops being an idea and becomes *yours*.

And these were people whose entire crime — we're about to see — was living in the world of *description*. They knew *about* the truth. They discussed it, negotiated over it, put their hands over the Prophet's mouth to stop hearing it. They kept it, deliberately, at the safe distance of an idea.

So the punishment answers the crime in its own grammar. You kept the truth at arm's length as a concept? [PAUSE] Then you will *taste*. The thing you refused to let touch you will now touch you in the most direct, most personal, most un-outsource-able way a being can experience anything. You will no longer know *about* it. You will *own* it.

The physical image — a tongue, a taste — is the stepping stone. What it points to is a reality no tongue can reach: the end of every comfortable distance you ever kept between yourself and a truth you didn't want to swallow.

That is what one verb does. And it does it quietly, before you've even noticed.

### The certainty that cannot be exceeded — the doubled oath

Now look at the *shell* around that verb. In Arabic, there are ways to say a thing, and there are ways to say a thing so that no doubt can survive the sentence.

Let me teach you the tool. Arabic has two devices of emphasis that can be layered onto a verb. There is a small prefix — the *lām* of emphasis — that clamps onto the front, like a fist grabbing the word. And there is a heavy syllable — the "energetic *nūn*," *nūn al-tawkīd* — that clamps onto the back. Each one, alone, raises a statement to a solemn assertion. It's the difference between "I'll come" and "I *will absolutely* come."

Now — you almost never need both. One is emphatic enough for an oath. So what does it mean when Allah puts *both* on the same verb, and then does it *again* on the very next verb?

Look:

*la-nudhīqanna* — *la* in front, *-nna* behind. The fist on both ends.

*wa-la-najziyannahum* — again. *la* in front, *-nna* behind.

Twice. Back to back. This is the maximum. There is no third level of certainty in the Arabic language. Allah has taken the strongest form of assertion available and used it, and then repeated the strongest form of assertion available.

Here's what's worth pausing over. We are used to Allah using this register for *mercy* — "*la-najziyannahum ajrahum*," We will *most certainly* give them their reward. We lean into that. We want that certainty. It comforts us.

But the certainty runs in both directions. **The same grammar that guarantees the reward guarantees the reckoning.** You cannot have a God whose promises are ironclad and whose warnings are negotiable. The strength of your hope in His mercy is measured by the seriousness with which you take His warning — because they are spoken in the *identical* grammatical breath. To soften one is to soften both.

### The asymmetry hidden in one word — *aswaʾ*, "the worst"

And now the word I most want you to see. Because this one is doing something you will miss if you read too fast.

*wa-lanajziyannahum* — "We will recompense them" — for what? *aswaʾa alladhī kānū yaʿmalūn* — for **the worst** of what they used to do.

*Aswaʾ.* It's a superlative — the elative form, "the worst," "the most evil." Not "what they did." Not "the sum of what they did." The **worst** of what they did.

Now here is the tool for reading this. In the Qur'an, the deepest meanings often live not in what a verse says, but in what a *parallel* verse says differently. And there is a phrase that recurs, almost word-for-word, elsewhere in the Book — spoken about the believers. Allah says of the righteous, in more than one place: *wa-lanajziyannahum ajrahum bi-aḥsani alladhī kānū yaʿmalūn* — "We will most certainly reward them according to **the best** of what they used to do."

Stop. Put the two side by side.

For the believer: recompensed by *aḥsan* — **the best** of what they did.
For the one who knowingly rejected: recompensed by *aswaʾ* — **the worst** of what they did.

Feel the asymmetry. Because this is where the *raḥma* is hiding, in a verse about the Fire.

Think about what it means to be judged by your *best*. The believer did beautiful things and ugly things, like every human being. He had mornings of sincerity and afternoons of pettiness. He gave charity and he gossiped. And Allah says: I will reach into the whole tangled record of your life, and I will take *the best of it*, and I will build your reward on that. Your finest moment becomes the measure. The worst is quietly set aside.

Now hold that generosity in your hand, and look at what is *withheld* from the one who knowingly rejected. That same reaching-in happens — but Allah takes *the worst*. And here is the thing the parallel teaches that the verse alone does not: **the withholding is the point.** The rejecter is not being invented as a monster. He too, presumably, had moments that looked decent from the outside. The verse does not say those didn't happen. It says they will not be the measure. The generosity of *"I'll judge you by your best"* — the door was open. It is the same door. It was open to everyone.

So what closed it?

Hold that question. Because the ayah is about to answer it — but it makes you wait until the very last word.

### The last word — *yajḥadūn*, and the crime finally named

We arrive at the final word of the passage, and everything has been building toward it.

*jazāʾan bimā kānū bi-āyātinā **yajḥadūn*** — "a recompense for what they used to do to Our signs: *yajḥadūn*."

Let me teach you this word, because it does not mean what the translations make it mean. Every English rendering says "reject" or "deny," and that's not wrong — but it's a fraction of it.

Arabic has several words for refusing the truth. There is *kufr* — from a root meaning to *cover*, to bury a seed under soil; the disbeliever covers over a truth. There is *takdhīb* — to call something a lie, from the root for lying. And then there is *juḥūd*, from the root *jaḥada* — and *jaḥd* has a very precise, very chilling meaning that the others don't carry.

*Jaḥd* is to deny something **while knowing, inside yourself, that it is true.**

This is not the denial of ignorance. It is the denial of the eyewitness who says "I saw nothing." It is the denial of the person who has the evidence in their hand and drops it behind their back and says "what evidence?" The Qur'an itself defines the word for us, in Surat an-Naml, describing Pharaoh's people: *wa jaḥadū bihā wa-stayqanat-hā anfusuhum* — "they rejected the signs *while their own souls were certain of them*." That is *jaḥd*. Certainty on the inside, denial on the outside. The soul says *yes* and the tongue says *no*, and the person chooses the tongue.

[PAUSE]

Now — do you feel the whole passage snap into place?

Go back to the door that closed. The believer's door stayed open — judged by his best — because whatever his sins, he did not stand in the light and call it darkness. The rejecter's door closed on one specific act: not ignorance, not confusion, not even wrongdoing in the ordinary sense. It closed on *jaḥd* — **knowing, and denying anyway.**

And now the surah's name comes back to you like a returning wave. *Fussilat.* The signs were *made distinct.* Made so clear that denying them could no longer be honest. This is why the crime named at the end is not "they didn't understand." It is not even "they sinned." It is: **they knew, and they said no.**

One clarification the grammar keeps honest, so I don't overstate it: the verse names *jaḥd* as the ground of *this* recompense for *these* people whom the surah has already shown to be knowing rejecters. It is not a private license to declare who among people today is a "knowing" denier — that judgment belongs to the One who reads the soul. What the word gives us is not a weapon to point outward. It is a mirror to turn inward. [PAUSE]

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not about words, but about the machinery of the human heart itself.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### The truth you already know

Let me start somewhere completely ordinary.

There is a cigarette in a smoker's hand, and printed on the box, in letters he chose to buy anyway, are the words *this will kill you*. He has read them ten thousand times. He is not confused about lung cancer. He does not need a lecture on the science. If you sat him down and asked him, "Is smoking bad for you?" he would say "obviously" before you finished the sentence.

He *knows.* And he lights it anyway.

Now — that's an easy example, so let's make it uncomfortable. Think of the thing in *your* life right now that you already know. Not the thing you're confused about. The thing you have complete clarity on and are not doing. The prayer you know you're delaying. The relationship you know is poisoning you. The apology you know you owe. The habit you know is eating your years. You don't need more information. You have never needed more information. If knowing were enough, you'd have changed already.

We have built an entire modern vocabulary to protect ourselves from this gap. We say *"that's just not my truth right now."* We say *"I'm doing my own research"* about a thing we have already researched and simply didn't like the answer to. Psychologists have a clinical name for the discomfort of holding a belief and acting against it — *cognitive dissonance* — and they discovered something that should terrify us: when the discomfort gets bad enough, we usually don't change the behavior. **We change the belief.** We quietly edit what we "know" so it stops accusing us. We don't rise to the truth. We renovate it downward until it fits the life we've already decided to live.

That renovation — that is where *jaḥd* is born. It doesn't start as a bold, cinematic "I reject God." It starts as a small, comfortable dimming of a light you'd rather not see by. You don't extinguish the truth. You just stop looking directly at it. And you do it so gradually that you can always tell yourself you never really *knew*.

And here is what these two ayahs do to that entire strategy. [PAUSE] They say: **Allah kept the receipt.**

The renovation doesn't fool the One who reads the soul. The souls of Pharaoh's people were "certain of them" *while* the tongues denied — and Allah recorded the certainty, not the tongue. Which means the most private thing about you — the flicker of *yes* you feel and immediately suppress, the moment of recognition you talk yourself out of by the time you've finished your coffee — *that* flicker is not private. It is the most seen thing about you. The verse is not addressed to the ignorant. **It is addressed to the ones who know.** It is addressed, if we're honest, to us.

So what does this actually change about tomorrow? Only this, but it's everything: it moves the spiritual question off the wrong shelf. Most of us treat our failures as a *knowledge* problem — "if I just understood more, read more, listened to one more lecture, I'd finally do it." The verse gently takes that excuse away. Your problem was never that you didn't know. Tomorrow, when you catch yourself reaching for "I need to learn more" about the thing you already understand completely — recognize that reach for what it is. It's the smoker reading the box. The next honest step isn't more knowing. It's the one small act of *saying yes with your body* to the thing your soul already said yes to.

### Judged by your best

Now let me take you somewhere we all live, and almost never admit.

There is a well-documented crack in the human mind. When *you* are late, you know it's because traffic was insane and your kid wouldn't put his shoes on and the world conspired against you. When *someone else* is late, they're just the kind of person who's late — inconsiderate, disorganized, doesn't value your time. Psychologists call it the fundamental attribution error, but you don't need the term. You've felt it from both sides. **We judge ourselves by our intentions and our best moments. We judge everyone else by their worst.**

We do it constantly. A friend snaps at you once and you file it under "he's toxic." You snap at someone once and you file it under "I was having a hard week." You reach into your own messy record and — automatically, mercifully — you pull out your best and call *that* the real you. And you reach into your enemy's record and pull out his worst and call *that* the real him.

Now hold that instinct up against the asymmetry we found in the words.

To the believer, Allah says: *bi-aḥsani alladhī kānū yaʿmalūn* — I will reach into your record and pull out **your best** and build your eternity on it. [PAUSE] Do you understand what that is? **It is Allah choosing to treat you the way you naturally treat only yourself.** The generosity you reflexively extend to your own worst days — "that wasn't really me, I was better than that" — Allah extends it to you *for real*, and makes it binding, and seals it with the strongest oath in the language. Your finest hour, your most sincere sujūd, the one time you gave and told no one, the moment you forgave when you had every right not to — He finds it in the pile, and He says: *this. This is the measure. Build his home on this.*

And to the one who knowingly turned away, the withholding: judged by *aswaʾ*, the worst. Not because Allah is stingy — the same door, remember, stood open. But because *jaḥd* is precisely the act of refusing the relationship in which mercy operates. To knowingly deny the sign is to refuse to stand where the light of "judged by your best" falls. You cannot receive the generosity of a covenant you have looked at, understood, and walked away from.

Here's what this asks of you tomorrow, and it's harder than it sounds. That person you've already judged — the family member you've written off, the one whose worst moment you've made into their whole identity — Allah is, this very moment, sifting *their* record for *their* best, with a mercy you have refused to extend. Who are you to hold someone to a standard the Lord of the Worlds declined to hold His own servants to? [PAUSE] The next time you catch yourself reaching into someone's pile and pulling out their worst to define them — stop. Reach again. Pull out their best. Not because they've earned it. Because that is exactly, *precisely*, the mercy you are begging Allah to show you on the Day your own pile is opened.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — an ayah built backwards

Now that we've done the work, let me show you something about how these two ayahs are *built*, because the design will prove the whole thing wasn't accidental.

Follow the single root *j-z-y*, "to recompense," through the passage. It appears three times, and where it appears is not random:

*wa-la-najziyannahum* — "We will recompense them" (27)
*dhālika **jazāʾu** aʿdāʾillāh* — "that is the **recompense** of the enemies of Allah" (28)
*jazāʾan* bimā kānū… yajḥadūn — "a **recompense** for their knowing rejection" (28)

The word "recompense" opens, recurs, and closes. And notice what sits *enclosed* between them: *an-nār… lahum fīhā dāru l-khuld* — "the Fire… for them within it, a home of eternity." The word for eternal dwelling sits *surrounded* on every side by the word for recompense. The grammar does what the meaning describes: something *enclosed*, with no gap in the wall, hemmed in by justice on every side. The sentence encloses "eternity" the way the Fire will enclose its people. [PAUSE]

And then feel the *direction* of the whole passage. It does not begin with the crime and end with the punishment, the way a logical argument would. It begins with the **sensation** — *taste* — and it makes you feel the severity, the eternity, the enclosing walls, *before* it tells you why. The reason is held back to the final word: *yajḥadūn*, they knew and denied. The ayah is built **backwards.** You experience the verdict first, in your body, on your tongue — and only at the end do you understand the cause. Which is exactly how it will be. No one tastes the consequence and *then* gets to relitigate the choice. The consequence comes first as a fact; the reason was settled long ago, in a thousand quiet moments of looking at a light and choosing the dark.

### The two paths were always one insight

And here is where the linguistic journey and the two themes turn out to be the same discovery seen from two sides.

The words showed us a God whose certainty is doubled, whose mercy reaches for our best, and who reserves His severest reckoning not for the ignorant but for the *knowing*. The themes showed us a heart that already knows what it will not do, that already extends to itself the mercy it denies to others.

Put them together and you get one sentence of self-knowledge: **the distance between me and my Lord was never a distance of knowledge. It was always a distance of the will.** I know. I have always known. The signs were *fussilat* — made distinct — in my own chest. The only question the ayah leaves on the table is whether I will let what I know reach my hands and my days — or whether I will keep it, comfortably, at the safe cool distance of an idea, until the day it is no longer an idea, and I *taste* it.

### Questions to carry

- What is the one truth in your life right now that you *fully know* and are not living — the thing that needs no further study? [PAUSE] What would it cost you to say yes to it with your body this week, and why does that cost feel larger than it is?
- When you feel the flicker of recognition — that quiet inner *yes* to something you'd rather not accept — what do you usually do in the next sixty seconds to make it go away?
- If Allah is reaching into your record to find your *best*, what would He find there? And if that pile feels thin — what is stopping you from adding to it today?
- Who have you defined by their worst moment? What would change if you handed them the same mercy — "judged by their best" — that you are asking Allah to hand you?

### One sentence to remember

**The severest thing about the Fire is not its heat but its honesty: it is only ever the taste of a truth you already knew and chose, again and again, to push away.**

### An invitation

So let this not become one more thing you *know about* and file away — because that would be the very move the ayah is warning against. The mercy in these verses is enormous and it is *available*: a door that judges you by your best, held open by the strongest promise Allah ever makes, closed only by the one thing you actually control — whether you'll keep denying what you already see. You don't need to become a scholar tonight. You need to stop renovating the truth downward. Let one thing you know become one thing you do. That is the whole of it.

### A closing du'ā

*O Allah, You who made Your signs distinct so that no honest heart could miss them — do not let us be among those who know and deny. Let what our souls recognize reach our hands and our days. And when our record is opened, You who reach for the best of what Your servants did — reach into ours, find whatever good is buried there, and be merciful to us by it, not by the worst we have done. Āmīn.*
