# Only a Human, Only One God

## Introduction: The Man They Could Not Get Past

Let me set the scene before we open a single word.

The people of Makkah have just said something extraordinary. Look at the verse right before ours — verse five. They said: *"Our hearts are within coverings from that to which you invite us, and in our ears is deafness, and between us and you is a veil. So work; indeed, we are working."*

Sit with the strangeness of that. They are not saying *we disagree with your argument.* They are saying *we have sealed ourselves.* There is a curtain. There is a wall. They have made themselves unreachable on purpose, and then — almost mockingly — they tell him: go ahead, do your thing, we'll do ours.

Now. How do you answer a person who has declared themselves a closed door?

You might expect a thunderbolt. You might expect the Prophet ﷺ to be told: *threaten them, overwhelm them, prove yourself.* Because notice what their resistance is really fixed on. It is fixed on **him**. *Between us and you* is a veil. *You* invite us. The barrier they describe is a barrier against a person. They have made the messenger the obstacle.

[PAUSE]

And here is the first stunning move of the surah. Allah does not tell him to make himself bigger. He tells him to make himself **smaller**. To remove himself from the equation almost entirely. The very first word He puts in the Prophet's mouth is a command to step out of the way.

*Qul* — Say.

And what is he commanded to say? Not "I am the Messenger of the Lord of the worlds, fear me." He is commanded to say: *I am only a human being like you.*

Here is the hook I want you to carry through everything that follows. In this one verse, the Prophet ﷺ is told to use the **exact same word**, the exact same restricting particle, to describe **two** things — and they could not seem more opposite. He uses it to shrink himself down to nothing: *only* a human. And then he uses it again to point to the only thing in existence that is everything: *only* One God. The same small word frames the smallest claim and the largest claim a person can make.

Why would the smallness of the messenger and the oneness of God be spoken in the identical grammar? What is the connection between *I am only a human* and *your God is only One*?

That question is the door. Let's walk through it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Closes Every Other Door — *Innamā*

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you a tool that Arabic has and English barely does.

In English, when we want to say "only," we drop the word "only" somewhere in the sentence and hope it lands in the right place. "I only saw him." "I saw only him." We shuffle it around. It's clumsy, and it's often ambiguous.

Arabic has a precision instrument for this. It's called *ḥaṣr* — restriction. It's the act of taking a statement and sealing it shut, so that the thing you affirm is affirmed and **everything else is denied**. It doesn't just say "this is true." It says "this is true, *and nothing outside of this is.*" It is affirmation plus exclusion, fused into one move.

One of the most powerful ways to do this is the particle **إِنَّمَا** — *innamā*. When *innamā* opens a sentence, it acts like a spotlight in a dark room. Everything the light touches: that, and only that. Everything else: pushed into darkness, denied.

Now let's open the verse.

**إِنَّمَآ أَنَا۠ بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُكُمْ** — *innamā anā basharun mithlukum.*

Word by word: *innamā* — the restriction. *Anā* — I. *Basharun* — a human being, and notice it is **indefinite**, *a* human, one of a category, nothing singled out. *Mithlukum* — like you.

Hear what *innamā* does here. It does not merely say "I am a human." It says: **I am only a human — and that is the entire truth of my person; there is nothing else to fixate on.** Every door they wanted to open about him — *is he a sorcerer, is he a poet, is he possessed, is he after power* — *innamā* slams every one of them shut. The light falls on one thing: *basharun mithlukum.* A human like you. And everything else they imagined about him is denied in a single particle.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with the word *bashar* itself. The Quran has another word for the human — *insān* — which leans toward the human as a being of intellect, of intimacy, of forgetfulness. But *bashar* is the human as **flesh and skin**. The root carries the sense of the outer surface of the body, the skin you can touch. It is the most physical, most ordinary, most un-mystical word available. He is told to describe himself by the word that means *I am the one whose skin you can see, who eats and sleeps and grieves as you do.*

Hasan al-Baṣrī, the great early master, reading this very verse, said something that has echoed for thirteen centuries: **"Allah taught him humility."** Both al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī preserve this from him. *ʿAllamahu Allāhu at-tawāḍuʿ.* The most honored human being who ever lived is taught, by his Lord, to introduce himself with the plainest possible word, restricted by *innamā* so that nothing inflates it.

And al-Baghawī adds a line that opens the whole logic of the verse. He glosses it: *"like one of you — and were it not for the revelation, I would not be calling you."* Do you see what that does? The *innamā* shrinks the man precisely so that the **only** thing left standing tall is the thing coming *through* him. Empty the vessel of any claim of its own, and what remains visible is the water it carries.

So we hold the first half: *I am only a human.* The messenger reduced, on purpose, by a single restricting word.

Now watch what happens when that same word returns.

### Element Two: The Mirror — *Annamā*

The verse continues: **يُوحَىٰٓ إِلَىَّ أَنَّمَآ إِلَٰهُكُمْ إِلَٰهٌ وَٰحِدٌ** — *yūḥā ilayya annamā ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid.*

*Yūḥā* — it is revealed. This is a Form IV verb from the root of *waḥy*, revelation, and it is **passive** and it is **present-tense**. Not "it was revealed once." *It is revealed* — ongoing, continuous. Something is being poured into me, the verb says, and I am not its author; notice the passive — *it is revealed to me*, by One unnamed in the verb itself, because the One revealing is about to be named as the whole point.

And what is revealed? *Annamā* — and there it is again. The restriction particle, returned. *Innamā* shrank the man. **Annamā** now expands to fill the universe: *ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid* — your God is only One God.

Stay with me, because this is the architecture the whole verse is built on, and most of us read straight past it.

The verse contains **two restrictions, back to back**, in mirror image:

> **innamā** — I am *only* a human...
> **annamā** — your God is *only* One.

The same sealing, the same shutting of every other door — applied first to the messenger and then to the Message. And now the question from the introduction answers itself. Why the identical grammar for the smallest claim and the largest? **Because they are one motion.** The hand that pushes the messenger down out of the way is the same hand that lifts your gaze up to the One. To restrict the man to *merely human* is to clear the entire field so that the *only One God* can stand alone in it, with nothing — not even His own Prophet — sharing the light.

[PAUSE]

This is *tawḥīd* spoken in syntax before it is spoken in doctrine. Look at the phrase itself: *ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid* — "your God is one God." The word *ilāh* — the one worthy of worship — appears **twice**, and is then sealed by *wāḥid*, one. It is almost insistent, almost repetitive: God, God, One. As if to say: whatever else you have been calling a god — your three hundred and sixty idols around the Kaʿba, your intercessors, your inherited names — *innamā*, *annamā*, the door is shut on every one of them. *Your* God — the One who actually holds your existence — is one, and the category has room for no other.

So now we have the diagnosis and the spotlight. He is *only* a man. He is *only* One. Everything false has been excluded. The field is clear.

And on a cleared field, Allah gives a command. Two commands, in fact. And they are the most beautiful part of the verse.

### Element Three: The Two-Fold Turn — *Fa-staqīmū* and *Wa-staghfirūhu*

Let me teach one more piece of grammar, because the whole emotional weight of the verse lives in it.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — *forms* — and each form bends the basic meaning in a particular direction. There is a form, **Form X**, built on the shell *ista-fʿala*, and its signature flavor is **seeking, striving toward, asking for**. Take a root and put it in Form X and you very often get: *to seek that thing, to demand it, to work toward it.* From *ʿilm*, knowledge, you don't just *know* — *istaʿlama*, you *seek* to know, you inquire. The *ista-* prefix is the sound of a hand reaching out for something it does not yet fully hold.

Now hear the command — and hear that it comes after a *fā*, the "so," the "therefore":

**فَٱسْتَقِيمُوٓاْ إِلَيْهِ** — *fa-staqīmū ilayhi.* **So** — go straight toward Him.

*Istaqīmū* is Form X from the root *q-w-m*, to stand, to rise, to be upright. In Form X it becomes *istiqāma* — the famous word — to seek straightness, to hold yourself upright on a course, to keep going true. And the *fā* before it is load-bearing: *because* your God is One — **therefore** straighten yourselves toward Him. The oneness is the reason; the straightening is the response. If there is only One, then there is only one direction worth facing, and a divided, wandering heart is simply a lie about reality.

But notice something the grammar does that should stop us. *Istaqāma*, to go straight, normally pairs with *ʿalā* — go straight *upon* a path. Here Allah does not say *istaqīmū ʿalā ṭarīqihi*, go straight upon His path. He says *istaqīmū* **ilayhi** — go straight **toward Him**. Not toward a path. Toward a Person.

Al-Qurṭubī catches this exactly. He says it means: *wajjihū wujūhakum bid-duʿāʾi lahu wal-masʾalati ilayh* — **direct your faces** toward Him in supplication and in asking of Him. And then he gives the most ordinary, human illustration. He says it's like when a man tells another: *istaqim ilā manzilik* — "go straight home." Meaning: *don't turn aside for anything; don't get diverted; head for the house and nothing else.* 

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what that does? *Istiqāma* is not a posture you adopt once. It is a *direction of travel* you refuse to be diverted from. Go straight home — don't stop at this distraction, don't detour for that desire, don't get pulled down this alley. Keep your face pointed at the door of the One the whole way. That is the first command: **a life that does not get diverted from its destination.**

And here is where the verse becomes merciful in a way that should bring tears.

Because Allah knows what *bashar* — that fleshly, ordinary human — actually does on the road home. He gets diverted. He stops. He wanders into the alley. **No human walks a perfectly straight line.** And so the very next word, joined by *wa*, "and," is a second Form X command:

**وَٱسْتَغْفِرُوهُ** — *wa-staghfirūhu.* And **seek His forgiveness.**

*Istaghfirū* — Form X again, the same reaching-out shell — from *gh-f-r*, to cover, to conceal, to pardon. Seek to be covered. Seek to have it pardoned.

Look at the pairing. Two Form X imperatives, side by side, both built on *ista-*, the hand reaching out:

> *fa-**sta**qīmū* — keep seeking to go straight
> *wa-**sta**ghfirūhu* — and keep seeking to be forgiven

[PAUSE]

This is the entire spiritual life in two words. Allah does not command *be perfectly straight.* He commands *seek straightness* — *and seek forgiveness for the times you weren't.* The two are bolted together by a single *wa* precisely because they are inseparable. The one who is genuinely walking toward Him is the one who keeps having to ask to be covered for the steps that veered. And al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both gloss *wa-staghfirūhu* simply: ask forgiveness **for your sins** — Qurṭubī adds, *for your shirk*, your association of partners. The seeking-straight and the seeking-pardon are the two legs you actually walk on. Remove either and you fall: pure striving without forgiveness becomes despair; pure forgiveness-seeking without striving becomes presumption. Together, they are a *walk.*

And then — the warning. **وَوَيْلٌ لِّلْمُشْرِكِينَ** — *wa-waylun lil-mushrikīn.* And woe to those who associate partners.

*Wayl.* Al-Jalālayn defines it with stark economy: *kalimatu ʿadhāb* — **it is a word of punishment.** A single word that is itself a sentence of torment. And it falls on the *mushrikīn* — the doers of *shirk*, from the root that means to share, to make a partner. The exact opposite of *istaqīmū ilayhi*. The straight-goer faces the One and refuses every diversion. The *mushrik* faces many, divides his heart, hands pieces of his devotion to partners who hold nothing of his existence.

Now we have walked the whole grammar. *Only* a man. *Only* One. So go straight toward Him — and seek to be covered when you don't. Let's now ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Person Who Loves God and Is Cruel to People

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You have met — maybe you have *been* — the person who is intensely "spiritual" and quietly miserable to be around. The one who talks constantly about their connection to God, their dreams, their states, their devotion — and who is stingy with money, harsh with the waiter, cold to their family, contemptuous of the poor. There is a modern phrase for a cousin of this: *spiritual bypassing.* Using the language of the vertical — God, transcendence, surrender — to avoid the demands of the horizontal: actual kindness to actual people.

We sense, instinctively, that something is broken in that person. That their "connection upward" is somehow fraudulent if it produces nothing sideways. But we struggle to say *why.* Isn't worship between a person and God? What does my charity to a stranger have to do with my *tawḥīd*?

Watch what the Quran does. Because verse seven answers this with a precision that should take your breath away.

The verse has just warned the *mushrik*. Now it defines him. And here is the astonishment: **of all the ways to define a person who has the wrong belief about God, look at how Allah chooses to.**

**ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْتُونَ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَهُم بِٱلْءَاخِرَةِ هُمْ كَٰفِرُونَ** — *"those who do not give the zakah, and who, in the Hereafter — they are disbelievers."*

Stop. *Shirk* is a sin of **belief** — it is about *whom you worship.* You would expect it defined by a belief: *those who worship idols, those who set up partners.* Instead, the first thing named is a **failure of the hand**: *they do not give the zakah.* They do not give. The charity that flows from a believer to the poor, the weak, the indebted — they withhold it.

[PAUSE]

Why would the Quran identify the person with the broken *vertical* relationship — wrong belief about God — primarily by his broken *horizontal* one — closed hand to people?

Because they are the same wound.

Look back at what the One God commanded just one verse earlier: *istaqīmū ilayhi* — turn your *whole self* toward Him. *Tawḥīd* is the gathering of a scattered heart into a single direction. And a heart truly gathered toward the One who is *al-Razzāq*, the Provider, the Owner of everything — a heart that genuinely knows its wealth is His and only passing through its hands — **that heart cannot clench.** It gives, because it has stopped believing the lie that what it holds is its own. The open hand is the *fingerprint* of real *tawḥīd*. And the clenched fist is the fingerprint of *shirk* — because to withhold from the poor is to secretly worship the wealth, to make *it* the god you will not let go of.

Al-Muyassar draws the line out with breathtaking clarity. The *mushrikīn*, it says, are those who *"did not purify themselves through the oneness of their Lord and sincerity to Him, and they did not pray and did not give zakah — so there is no sincerity in them toward the Creator, and no benefit in them for the creation."* 

Read that last phrase again. **No sincerity toward the Creator, and no benefit for the creation.** That is the complete portrait of the severed person. Cut off above. Cut off beside. The two cuts are one cut. You cannot be truly joined to the One and severed from His creatures, because everything sideways flows from what is true vertically. The river that is genuinely connected to its source *waters the valley.* A river that waters nothing was never really connected to the source — it was a puddle pretending.

And then the verse names the deepest root: *wa-hum bil-ākhirati hum kāfirūn* — *and in the Hereafter, they are disbelievers.* There it is. Why does the hand clench? Because deep down he does not believe there is a Day when the account is settled, when the giver is repaid infinitely and the hoarder finds his hoard worthless. Strip the Hereafter from a heart and watch the hand close. Why give, if this world is all there is? **Disbelief in the Hereafter is the quiet engine of every closed fist.**

So here is what changes about how you live tomorrow.

Stop measuring your relationship with God only by what happens on the prayer mat. Measure it also by what happens at your wallet, at your dinner table, in your tone with the person who can do nothing for you. The Quran has just told you these are not two separate ledgers. Your *tawḥīd* is *audited by your generosity.* This week, find the gap between what you profess upward and what you give sideways — and close it by an inch. Not because charity earns God. Because a hand that will not open is telling you something true about a heart that has not yet fully turned.

### Theme Two: You Were Never Commanded to Be Perfect

Now let me take you to a different ache — one many sincere believers carry like a stone.

It is the ache of the person who is genuinely trying, and genuinely failing, and slowly concluding that they are a fraud. They pray, and their mind wanders. They repent, and they fall again. They resolve, and they break the resolve by Tuesday. And a voice inside says: *a real believer wouldn't keep stumbling like this. Your repeated falling is proof your faith isn't real.* It is the spiritual face of perfectionism — and like all perfectionism, it does not produce excellence. It produces despair, and then it produces quitting. *Why keep walking a straight line I clearly cannot walk?*

If that voice lives in you, this verse was sent for you. Let me show you how tenderly.

Go back to the two commands, and watch their *order.* Allah says: *fa-staqīmū ilayhi* — go straight toward Him. **And then immediately**, in the same breath, joined by *wa*: *wa-staghfirūhu* — and seek forgiveness.

Ask yourself the obvious question we usually skip. **If you went perfectly straight, why would you need forgiveness?**

The two commands sit side by side because Allah, who created the *bashar* — the fleshly, forgetful, easily-diverted human — **built the falling into the instruction.** He did not say "go straight, and *if* you slip, seek forgiveness," as an afterthought, an emergency exit. He commanded the seeking of forgiveness *in the same imperative breath* as the seeking of straightness. He commanded both as permanent, ongoing practices. The straight path, in the Quran's own design, is **not** a line you walk without deviation. It is a line you walk while *continuously correcting back onto it.*

[PAUSE]

Think about what it actually means to drive a car down a straight road. You do not set the wheel once and remove your hands. If you did, you'd be in the ditch within seconds. To go *straight* is to make thousands of tiny corrections — a little left, a little right — constantly. **The straightness is not the absence of deviation. It is the presence of correction.** A driver who never touched the wheel because the road "should" be straight would crash. The one who arrives is the one who never stopped adjusting.

*Istaqīmū wa-staghfirū* is that wheel. The *istighfār* — the seeking of forgiveness — is not the sign that your *istiqāma* failed. **It is the mechanism by which your *istiqāma* succeeds.** Every "I turned aside — forgive me — I turn back" is a hand correcting the wheel. The person who never seeks forgiveness is not the one walking straightest. He is the one who has taken his hands off the wheel — the presumptuous, the heedless — and he is already in the ditch and doesn't know it.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ, who was the *most* straight, sought forgiveness most — by his own report, more than seventy times a day. Not because he deviated most. Because *istighfār* is not the failure state. It is the very texture of a life pointed at God. The closer you walk to the One, the more sensitive the wheel, the more constant the small corrections. The seeking of forgiveness *increases* with nearness, it does not decrease.

[PAUSE]

And notice, finally, the mercy in the grammar one more time. Both verbs are Form X — *seek.* He did not command *istaqīmū* as *qūmū*, the bare "stand up straight!" of a drill sergeant. He commanded the *seeking* of straightness. He honored, inside the very word, that this is a thing you *reach toward,* not a state you snap into. The whole life is permission to be *en route.* You are not commanded to have arrived. You are commanded to keep your face pointed home and your hand on the wheel.

So what changes tomorrow? When you fall — and you will — refuse the voice that says the fall disproves your faith. Reach for the wheel: *astaghfirullāh,* and turn the face back toward Him. That turning-back *is* the worship. Do not wait until you are "worthy" of facing Him to face Him. The facing, with all your imperfection, *seeking* both straightness and pardon, is the entire thing He asked of you. Make *istighfār* not the alarm you pull in emergencies, but the steady breath of your day.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Beneath It

Now that we've walked every piece, stand back and see how the verse was built. It is a single, descending-then-ascending motion:

- **First, the messenger is emptied** — *innamā anā basharun mithlukum.* Restriction one. He is shrunk to nothing-but-human, so nothing about *him* can be the obstacle.
- **Then the field, now cleared, is filled by the One** — *annamā ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid.* Restriction two. The identical grammar that emptied the man now declares the fullness of God. *The same word that made the messenger small made the message everything.*
- **Then, because there is only One, the command to turn** — *fa-staqīmū ilayhi.* One God, one direction, one undivided face.
- **And because you are still only *bashar*, the command to be covered as you turn** — *wa-staghfirūhu.* The wheel for the straight road.
- **And the warning to those who turned the wheel toward many** — *waylun lil-mushrikīn* — defined, devastatingly, not by a creed they recited but by *a hand that would not open and a Day they would not believe in.*

Two restrictions, two commands, one warning. The man made small so the One could be seen whole; the One seen whole so the self could be turned toward Him; the self turned toward Him while being mercifully forgiven for every veer; and the whole thing *proven real* by what the hand gives to the least of His creatures.

### The Two Discoveries Are One

The linguistic journey found two Form X verbs reaching toward God — *istaqīmū*, *istaghfirū* — seeking straightness and seeking pardon. The thematic journey found that a heart truly turned toward the One *opens its hand* to creation and *keeps correcting* its own course. **These are the same finding.** The reaching-toward-God of the grammar and the giving-toward-people of the theme are one current: a heart genuinely turned to the One God cannot stay clenched and cannot stay still. It gives, and it corrects, and it keeps its face pointed home.

### Questions to Carry

1. The Quran defined the *mushrik* by a closed hand before a wrong creed. If someone audited *your tawḥīd* by your generosity alone, what would the verdict be?
2. Do you treat *istighfār* as an emergency brake — pulled only after disaster — or as the steady hand on the wheel that keeps you straight all day long?
3. Hasan al-Baṣrī said Allah taught His most beloved *humility* — to say *I am only a human.* Where is your "only a human" — the place you keep inflating yourself instead of getting out of the way so the truth can be seen?
4. *Istaqim ilā manzilik* — go straight home, don't turn aside. What is the alley you keep getting diverted into, between you and the door of the One?
5. The closed fist, the verse says, grows from quiet disbelief in the Hereafter. Where does *this world is all there is* still secretly run your decisions about money, time, and giving?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Because your God is *only* One, empty yourself, turn your whole undivided face toward Him in a straightness you keep correcting with forgiveness — and let the open hand to His creation be the proof that the turning was real.

### The Closing Invitation

This verse does not ask you to become flawless before you may face God. It asks the opposite: that you face Him *as* a *bashar*, only human, with a hand on the wheel — seeking to go straight and seeking to be covered, both at once, for the rest of your life. And it quietly tells you where to look to know if the facing is genuine: not only at your forehead on the ground, but at your open or closed hand toward the people who can give you nothing back. Let one inch of that hand open this week. Let one *astaghfirullāh* become a steady breath rather than an alarm. That is not a burden laid on you. It is the door home, held open.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who are *only* One, gather my scattered heart into a single direction and let my face go straight toward You and turn aside for nothing. When I veer — and I am only *bashar*, and I will — let my hand find the wheel of *istighfār* before I find the ditch. And make my hand, which You see, open toward Your creation, that the turning toward You may be true and not a thing my tongue claims while my fist denies. *Āmīn.*
