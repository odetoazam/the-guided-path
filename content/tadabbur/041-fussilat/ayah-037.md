# Created Them: The Pronoun That Demotes a God

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the words in front of us — first in their own language, then in ours.

> وَمِنْ ءَايَـٰتِهِ ٱلَّيْلُ وَٱلنَّهَارُ وَٱلشَّمْسُ وَٱلْقَمَرُ ۚ لَا تَسْجُدُوا۟ لِلشَّمْسِ وَلَا لِلْقَمَرِ وَٱسْجُدُوا۟ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَهُنَّ إِن كُنتُمْ إِيَّاهُ تَعْبُدُونَ

*Wa min āyātihi-l-laylu wa-n-nahāru wa-sh-shamsu wa-l-qamar. Lā tasjudū li-sh-shamsi wa lā li-l-qamari wa-sjudū li-llāhi-lladhī khalaqahunna in kuntum iyyāhu taʿbudūn.*

"And among His signs are the night and the day, the sun and the moon. Do not prostrate to the sun, nor to the moon — but prostrate to Allah, the One who created them, if it is truly Him you worship."

### Let us rebuild the world this verse walked into.

I want you to forget, for a moment, everything you know about astronomy. Forget that the sun is a ball of fusing hydrogen ninety-three million miles away. Forget that the moon is a dead rock reflecting borrowed light.

Stand instead in the world of the first human beings who heard these words. In that world, the sun is not an object. **The sun is the single most powerful presence anyone has ever encountered.** It is the giver of warmth, the killer of darkness, the ripener of crops, the one thing that has never once failed to return. When it rises, the whole earth obeys it. When it sets, the whole world goes still. To a human standing in an open field with no electric light, no science, no skyscraper between them and the heavens — the sun is the closest thing to a visible God that exists.

And the moon. The moon governs the tides, marks the months, lights the traveler's road at night, and waxes and wanes with a rhythm so perfect that entire calendars and harvests and pilgrimages are built on its motion.

Now understand: across the ancient world — in Babylon, in Egypt, in Arabia, among the people of Saba whom this very Qur'an mentions — these two were not metaphors for the divine. **They were the divine.** People built temples to the sun. They timed their lives to the moon. They prostrated — literally lowered their faces to the ground — before the two brightest things in the sky.

This was not stupidity. This was the most *reasonable* idolatry imaginable. If you were going to worship anything you could see, the sun and the moon are the obvious candidates. They are magnificent. They are reliable. They are life-giving. They are beautiful.

### The Hook

So here is the tension I want you to carry with you. Allah is about to address the most defensible form of worship the human heart has ever invented — the worship of the genuinely magnificent. And He is going to dismantle it. Not with a thunderbolt. Not with a long argument.

He is going to dismantle it with **a single pronoun.** One suffix, three letters, attached to the end of one word — and the sun-god and the moon-god collapse into rubble.

Stay with me, and watch how He does it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "And *among* His signs" — the word that shrinks the universe

Let's begin where the ayah begins: **وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ** — *wa min āyātih* — "and **among** His signs."

I want to teach you one small word before we go further, because everything turns on it. The word is **مِنْ** — *min*. In Arabic, *min* is what grammarians call a partitive particle. It means "from" or "some of." It takes a whole and points to a *piece* of it.

Here is an everyday example. If I say, "I gave you my money," I have given you all of it. But if I say, "I gave you *some of* my money" — *min* my money — I have told you, in the very grammar, that there is more. Much more. What you hold in your hand is a fragment of a far larger store.

Now look at what Allah does. He does not say, "These four — night, day, sun, moon — are His signs," as if to gather them up and present them as the headline. He says these are *min āyātih* — **among** His signs. *Some* of them.

Notice what this does. [PAUSE]

The sun — the thing an entire civilization called the supreme deity — is filed by Allah under the category "some of My signs." Not "the greatest of My signs." Not even "an important sign." Just... one of many. One item on a list so long it cannot be counted.

Al-Qurtubi, commenting here, calls these *ʿalāmātih ad-dāllah ʿalā waḥdāniyyatihi wa qudratih* — "His marks, the ones that point to His oneness and His power." Hold onto that word: *marks*. A mark is not the thing itself. A mark points *away* from itself, toward the one who made the mark. A footprint is not a foot. A signature is not the person. **A sign is, by definition, something whose entire job is to direct your attention past itself to something else.**

So before Allah has even mentioned the prohibition, the grammar has already done quiet, devastating work. He has taken the two greatest gods of the ancient world and, with one preposition, reclassified them: you are not destinations. You are signposts. And a signpost that you stop and worship is a signpost that has failed at the one thing it exists to do.

Let that settle, and let's go to the word that finishes the job.

### Element Two: خَلَقَهُنَّ — the pronoun that performs the theology

Here is the center of the whole verse. After the prohibition — "do not prostrate to the sun, nor to the moon" — Allah commands: **وَاسْجُدُوا لِلَّهِ الَّذِي خَلَقَهُنَّ** — "but prostrate to Allah, the One who created **them**."

I need to teach you something about Arabic pronouns, because English is about to hide a miracle from you. In English, "them" is just "them." It does not tell you the gender. It does not tell you whether the things referred to are people or objects. Arabic is far more precise. Arabic pronouns carry gender and number and even a distinction between the rational and the non-rational.

The word is خَلَقَهُنَّ — *khalaqahunna*. *Khalaqa* — "He created." And then that ending: **ـهُنَّ** — *hunna*.

Now, here is what you need to know. The pronoun *hunna* is a **feminine plural** ending. And in Arabic grammar, the feminine plural *hunna* is the form used for plurals of **non-rational, non-human things** — objects, items, *stuff*. When Arabic refers to a group of rational beings — people, angels, intelligent agents who could be worshipped — it uses different pronouns and tends toward the masculine. But when it gathers up a pile of mere *objects*, it files them under this feminine plural, the grammar of things.

The classical grammarians caught this immediately. Al-Baghawi pauses right here and explains: *innamā qāla "khalaqahunna" bi-t-ta'nīth li-annahu ajrāhā ʿalā ṭarīqi jamʿi-t-taksīr, wa lam yujrihā ʿalā ṭarīqi-t-taghlīb li-l-mudhakkari ʿalā-l-mu'annath* — "He said *khalaqahunna*, in the feminine, because He ran them along the path of a **broken plural** [the plural of objects], and did not run them along the path of letting the masculine dominate the feminine."

Stay with me, because this is extraordinary. [PAUSE]

Look at the four things on the list: the night (al-layl), the day (al-nahar), the moon (al-qamar) — these are **masculine** nouns in Arabic. The sun (al-shams) is **feminine**. Now, the normal rule of Arabic — the rule called *taghlīb* — is that when you have a mixed group of masculine and feminine, **the masculine wins.** One man in a room of a hundred women, and the pronoun goes masculine. That is the default. That is what the language *expects* here.

But Allah does not do the expected thing. He does not let the masculine dominate. He overrides the rule of *taghlīb* and instead sweeps all four — sun, moon, night, day — into the **feminine plural of objects**. *Hunna.* The grammar you would use for a pile of stones. The grammar you would use for a set of tools.

Do you see what just happened? [PAUSE]

The people prostrated to the sun because it was *grand*. They prostrated to the moon because it was *majestic*. And Allah, in the act of telling them to stop, does not even argue about the grandeur. **He just demotes them grammatically.** He reaches into the sentence and strips the sun-god and the moon-god of every shred of personhood, of every claim to majesty, and files them away under the pronoun you would use for *things*. *Khalaqahunna.* "He created **them**" — them, the objects, the items, the made-things.

The verb does it too. خَلَقَ — *khalaqa* — "He created." The root خ-ل-ق is the root of fashioning, of bringing-into-being from nothing, of shaping clay. To say something was *khalaqa*-ed is to say it was **manufactured.** And nothing that was manufactured can be the manufacturer. Nothing that was shaped can be the shaper. The very word that describes the sun's origin is the word that disqualifies it from divinity.

So here is the whole argument of the verse, compressed into one word and its ending: *You bow to the sun because it is great? Let Me show you what the sun actually is. It is one of "them." It is an object I made. Watch — I can refer to it with the same little pronoun I use for a heap of pebbles.* [PAUSE]

This is what I mean when I say the grammar **performs** the theology. Allah does not merely *tell* you the sun is created and therefore unworthy of worship. The pronoun *enacts* it. By the time you reach the end of the word *khalaqahunna*, the demotion has already happened inside your own mouth, in the very act of pronouncing it.

### Element Three: إِيَّاهُ تَعْبُدُونَ — putting "Him" first

Now to the closing words, and one last piece of grammar, because Allah is not finished tightening the screw.

The verse ends: **إِن كُنتُمْ إِيَّاهُ تَعْبُدُونَ** — *in kuntum iyyāhu taʿbudūn* — "if it is truly **Him** you worship."

Let me teach you the structure. In normal Arabic word order, the verb tends to come first, then its object. "You worship Him" would naturally be *taʿbudūnahu* — the verb with the object stuck on the end. That is the default. That is the unmarked, ordinary way to say it.

But that is not what Allah says. He takes the object — إِيَّاهُ, *iyyāhu*, "Him" — and He **pulls it out and puts it in front of the verb.** *Iyyāhu taʿbudūn.* "Him — you worship." Literally, "It-is-Him you worship."

In Arabic rhetoric, this fronting of the object has a precise and powerful effect. The grammarians call it *ḥaṣr* or *qaṣr* — **restriction, exclusivity.** When you take the object and throw it to the front of the sentence, you are no longer saying "you worship Him." You are saying "**Him and Him alone** you worship — Him and no other."

The everyday version: there is a difference between saying "I called you" and saying "It was *you* I called." The second one carries an exclusion inside it. You, and not anyone else. You, specifically, to the exclusion of all others. That fronting is the sound of a door closing on every alternative.

So when Allah ends with *iyyāhu taʿbudūn*, He is not casually saying "if you happen to worship Him." He is saying: **"if it is Him — Him exclusively, Him to the exclusion of every sun and every moon and every magnificent created thing — that you worship."** The fronted pronoun has walled off every other option in advance.

And notice the condition wrapped around it: **إِن كُنتُمْ** — *in kuntum* — "**if** you were / if you are." There is a quiet challenge in that *if*. It is as though Allah says: you *claim* to be worshippers. You bow, you prostrate, you perform devotion — fine. But devotion aimed at a created thing is not worship of God at all. So *if* — if it is genuinely Him you mean by all your bowing — then here is where the bowing must go. The condition exposes the claim. It asks: is your worship actually arriving at its true address, or is it being delivered to a signpost?

### Bridging out of the language

Let me gather what these three pieces of grammar have shown us, because they are not three points. They are one blade, struck three times into the same place.

*Min* — these are only *some* of His signs; the sun is one item on an uncountable list.
*Khalaqahunna* — and that item is a made-thing, demoted by its very pronoun to the grammar of objects.
*Iyyāhu* — so redirect your worship, exclusively and by name, to the One who made it.

Together they take the most defensible idolatry in human history and dissolve it — not by denying that the sun is magnificent, but by showing that its magnificence is **borrowed, manufactured, and pointing somewhere else.**

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The idolatry of the magnificent — worshipping the gift instead of the Giver

I want to start somewhere very close to home, far away from sun-temples and ancient Babylon.

Think about the last time something truly took your breath away. Maybe it was a person — someone so beautiful, so brilliant, so charismatic that being near them felt like being near the source of all good things. Maybe it was a career, a calling, a craft you loved so much that it quietly became the thing you organized your whole life around. Maybe it was your own child, your own success, your own reputation. Maybe it was money — not as paper, but as the warm, reliable, life-giving sun that you were sure would never fail to rise.

Here is the uncomfortable truth this verse exposes: **the human heart does not usually fall for ugly idols.** Nobody builds a temple to a thing they find repulsive. We fall for the magnificent. We fall for the genuinely good, the genuinely beautiful, the genuinely powerful gift — and somewhere in the falling, our attention stops traveling *through* the gift toward the Giver and gets *stuck* on the gift itself.

That is precisely the trap of the sun-worshipper. The sun *is* magnificent. It *is* life-giving. It *is* reliable. The error was never in admiring it. The error was in letting the admiration **terminate** there — in treating the signpost as the destination.

Let the ayah illuminate this. Allah does not say "the sun is worthless, why would you look at it?" He affirms it as a *sign* — one of His signs, a thing worth contemplating, a thing of real splendor. The contemplation is not the problem. The *prostration* is. The moment your face goes down to the gift, you have insulted the Giver — not because the gift was bad, but because you mistook the letter for the one who wrote it.

And here is the movement that should reorder something inside us. [PAUSE] The very feature that makes a thing seductive as an idol — its magnificence — is *itself* a created feature. The sun's brightness was *given* to it. Al-Qurtubi makes exactly this point: *wa law shā'a la-aʿdamahā aw ṭamasa nūrahā* — "and had He willed, He would have made [the sun] vanish, or blotted out its light." The brightness you are bowing to is on loan. It can be switched off. **You are not even worshipping the sun's own light; you are worshipping a glow that Someone Else is choosing, this very second, to keep switched on.**

So when you find yourself bending your life around a person, a passion, a possession — pause and ask the question this verse is really asking: *Where did that thing get its glory?* The beauty you love in the beloved — who fashioned it? The brilliance you admire — who deposited it? The reliability you lean on — who guarantees it, sunrise after sunrise? Every magnificent thing in your life is the sun: a borrowed light, a *sign*, whose splendor is a finger pointing past itself.

The practical shift is small and enormous at once. It is not "stop loving the beautiful things." It is: **let your love pass through them.** Train the heart so that when it is struck by something magnificent, the very next motion — almost a reflex — is to look up, past the gift, and say: *the One who made this is greater.* Admire the sun all you like. Just never let your forehead touch the ground for it. Save that for the One who created *them*.

### Theme Two: Your worship has an address — and the condition that asks if it's arriving

Here is the second human door. I want you to think about effort that is sincere but **misdelivered.**

Imagine someone who writes letters every single day — heartfelt, faithful, devoted letters — and posts them with total sincerity to the wrong address. The devotion is real. The discipline is real. The love is real. And none of it ever arrives. Year after year of faithful letters landing in an empty house, because the address on the envelope was wrong.

This is the quiet terror inside the verse's closing condition: **إِن كُنتُمْ إِيَّاهُ تَعْبُدُونَ** — "*if* it is truly Him you worship."

The sun-worshippers were not lazy. They were not irreligious. They prostrated. They had rituals, temples, devotions, sincerity. By every external measure, they were *worshippers.* And Allah's response is not to praise the sincerity — it is to ask, with that piercing *if*, whether all that devotion was ever actually arriving anywhere. **Worship aimed at a created thing is not worship of God arriving at the wrong address. It is mail that never gets delivered at all.**

Let the grammar we uncovered come back here. Remember *iyyāhu* — "Him alone," the fronted pronoun that walls off every alternative. The verse is supplying the correct address with extraordinary precision: not the sun, not the moon, not the magnificent thing, but *Him* — the One identified by a single, unmistakable description: **الَّذِي خَلَقَهُنَّ**, "the One who created them." That is the address. Not a name you might confuse with another. A *relationship*: the Maker of the very things you were tempted to bow to.

Now bring this into your own life of worship, because the danger did not die with the sun-temples. We are perfectly capable of performing the forms of worship while the address quietly drifts. You can pray — and find your prayer is really aimed at being *seen* praying. You can give charity — and find the charity is really posted to your own reputation. You can pursue knowledge of the religion — and find the devotion is really directed at being *known* as knowledgeable. The prostration is happening. But to which sun? [PAUSE]

This is why the verse does not end with the command and stop. It ends with a *condition that turns into a question.* "*If* it is truly Him you worship." It hands the examination back to you. It refuses to let you assume that because the motions are religious, the address is correct.

And there is a mercy folded inside this severity. The same God who diagnoses the misdelivery gives you the corrected envelope in the same breath. He does not leave you guessing. *Wa-sjudū li-llāhi-lladhī khalaqahunna* — here, this way, bow *here.* And remember: this is a verse of recitational prostration. (The schools differ on the precise point of the sajdah — some place it at the close of this ayah, others at the verse that follows; the *ikhtilāf* is well known and the act itself is what matters.) Which means the verse does not merely *instruct* you to correct the address. It invites you, in the moment of reciting it, to **physically perform the correction** — to put your forehead down, right now, in the right direction. The body does what the words command. The letter, at last, is posted to the right house.

The practical shift: build the habit of *checking the address* before, during, and after every act of devotion. A single sentence, whispered at the start of the prayer, the charity, the fast: *This is for You — You alone — the One who made everything I am tempted to do this for.* Re-aim the envelope. Then bow.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with deliberate symmetry.

It opens with **four created things** named in a row — night, day, sun, moon. It then issues a **prohibition** aimed at exactly two of them — "do not prostrate to the sun, nor to the moon." Then it pivots on the hinge-word **خَلَقَهُنَّ**, "created them" — and that single word reaches back and gathers up all four, naming them, in one stroke, as *made*. Then it issues the **command** — "prostrate to Allah" — and closes with the **condition** that hands the whole thing back to you: "if it is truly Him you worship."

Notice the absence we have not yet named. Allah lists *four* signs but forbids prostration to only *two* — the sun and the moon. He says nothing about prostrating to the night or the day. Why? Because no one ever did. The night and the day are included to make the *category* — "these are all just signs, all just made-things" — but the prohibition lands only where the human heart actually goes wrong: on the two bright, magnificent, *visible* objects. The verse is surgical. It builds the full theological category out of four, then aims the correction precisely at the two that history shows we cannot resist. **The structure itself knows the human heart.**

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The grammar said: *min* — only some of His signs; *khalaqahunna* — demoted to the pronoun of objects; *iyyāhu* — redirect, exclusively, to Him.

The themes said: do not let your forehead stop at the magnificent gift; and make sure your lifetime of worship is actually arriving at the right address.

They are one truth: **the most reasonable idolatry — the worship of the genuinely magnificent — is dismantled not by denying the magnificence, but by remembering who manufactured it.** The sun is real. Its glory is real. And its glory is *borrowed, created, and pointing past itself* — which is exactly what the little pronoun *hunna* was telling you all along.

### Questions to carry

1. What is the "sun" in my life right now — the magnificent, reliable, life-giving thing my heart has quietly started bowing to?
2. When something takes my breath away, does my attention pass *through* it to the Giver, or does it get stuck on the gift?
3. If I traced the address on my acts of worship — my prayer, my giving, my striving — where would the envelope actually be delivered?
4. Do I admire the brightness in things while forgetting that Someone is choosing, this very second, to keep that light switched on?
5. The verse says "*if* it is truly Him you worship." If I sat with that *if* honestly — what would it expose?

### One-sentence distillation

The sun and moon you are tempted to bow to are only *some* of His signs — made-things demoted by a single pronoun — so take your prostration off the magnificent gift and deliver it, exclusively, to the One who created them.

### The closing invitation

I am not asking you to admire the world less. Admire it more — admire the sun, the moon, the breathtaking gifts in your life with everything you have. The invitation is only this: when the admiration rises, let it keep traveling. Do not let it stop at the signpost. Let the most beautiful things you encounter become the *fastest route* to remembering the One who made them — so that your wonder at creation and your worship of the Creator stop competing, and become the same motion. And when the chance comes, as it does in this very verse, put your forehead to the ground in the right direction.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who created the night and the day, the sun and the moon, and made them all only *some* of Your countless signs — let our love for every beautiful thing You have given travel through it and land at Your feet. Keep our prostration from stopping at any gift, and let it arrive, always, at the right address: You alone, the One who created them all. *Iyyāka naʿbud* — it is You alone we worship; do not let us mistake the signpost for the destination. Āmīn.
