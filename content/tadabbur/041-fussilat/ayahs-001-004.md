# A Revelation From Mercy That Most Refuse to Hear

*Ha-Meem. A revelation from the Most Merciful, the Bestower of Mercy — a Book whose verses have been distinctly detailed, an Arabic Qur'an for a people who know: a bearer of glad tidings and a warner. Yet most of them turned away, so they do not hear.*

## Introduction: The Room Where These Words First Landed

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand in the place where these verses first arrived.

Mecca, in the middle years of the Prophet's ﷺ mission. The message has been in the air for a while now. The novelty has worn off. The leaders of Quraysh are no longer merely curious or dismissive — they have organized. They have strategies. And one of their strategies, recorded for us in this very surah just a few verses later, was a strategy about *sound*. They said to one another: **"Do not listen to this Qur'an, and make noise over it, so that you might overwhelm it."** *La tasma'u lihadha-l-Qur'an walghaw feehi.*

Sit with what that tells you. They were not afraid the Qur'an would fail to make sense. They were afraid it would make *too much* sense. Their tactic was not to argue with the words — it was to make sure the words never landed. Drown them. Talk over them. Clap, shout, recite poetry, do anything so that this speech never reaches a quiet human heart, because they had learned from experience that if it reached a quiet heart, that heart tended to surrender.

So the danger was never the message's weakness. The danger was its clarity meeting an open ear.

Now hold that in mind, because this passage — the very opening of the surah — is Allah describing His own Book *before* recording how that Book was received. It is as if Allah lays the Book down on the table in front of you, turns it slowly so the light catches every facet, tells you exactly where it came from and exactly what it is for — and only *then*, at the very end, tells you what most people did with it.

And there is a second thing you need to feel. This surah, Fussilat, does not stand alone. It sits in a family — the seven surahs that all begin with those same two letters, *Ha-Meem*, the ones the scholars call the *Hawamim*. Turn back one page, to the end of the previous surah, Ghafir, and you find it opened the *exact same way*: **Ha-Meem. A sending-down of the Book from Allah, the Mighty, the Knowing** — *min Allahi-l-'Azizi-l-'Aleem*. Same two letters. Same word for revelation. But a different pair of names. There, the Book comes from *Might and Knowledge*. Here, one page later, the very same Book is introduced again — and this time it comes from *Ar-Rahman, Ar-Raheem*. Mercy upon mercy.

It is the same jewel, turned. And the strand running through these surahs is a single sustained meditation on this one reality: *the Book, and what a human being does when it arrives.*

So here is the question I want to leave hanging over everything that follows. **How is it possible** for the most merciful Sender to send the most clearly articulated message in the most understandable language for the express benefit of the receiver — and for that message to be met, by most, with turning away? What exactly breaks down in that transaction? Where, precisely, does it fail?

Stay with that. The passage is going to answer it — but not where you expect.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### First: A Descent, and the Two Names It Falls From

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the word, because if you don't have this tool in your hand, the word will just slide past you.

In Arabic, a huge amount of meaning is carried not by *which* letters a word has, but by the *shape* those letters are poured into. Take a simple root and you can build a plain verb from it — "he sent down," one action, done. But there is a second shape, what the grammarians call the second form, and pouring the same root into that shape does something specific: it *intensifies* the action, or it spreads the action out — makes it happen repeatedly, gradually, in stages, over time. Think of the difference in English between "he dropped it" and "he lowered it down bit by bit." Same basic event. Completely different texture. One is a single instant. The other is a process you can watch unfold.

Now look at the first real word of this surah: *tanzīl*.

Allah did not say *inzāl* — the plain sending-down, the whole thing delivered at once. He said *tanzīl* — the word built on that second, intensive, gradual shape. And this is not a poet's flourish. This is a documented fact about the Qur'an that these very letters are announcing about themselves. This Book did not arrive as a single dropped package. It came down across twenty-three years, verse by verse, situation by situation, wound by wound and question by question, always arriving at the moment it was needed and never before.

Notice what that *is*, before we call it anything. It is pacing. It is a Sender who does not overwhelm. It is speech metered out to match exactly what a human being can carry at one time.

**And now watch where the descent falls from.**

*Tanzīlun mina-r-Rahmani-r-Raheem.* A sending-down *from Ar-Rahman, Ar-Raheem.*

Stop here. This is the hinge of the entire opening. Allah has ninety-nine names. He is *Al-'Aziz*, the Mighty. *Al-Jabbar*, the Overwhelming. *Al-Malik*, the Sovereign. *Al-'Aleem*, the All-Knowing — and He used *that* name for this Book just one page earlier. He had every name available to Him. And to introduce the origin of His speech *in this surah*, He selected the two that are built from a single root — the root of the womb, the root of mercy — and placed them back to back.

Before you are told a single command. Before a single law, a single warning, a single threshold you must not cross. Before any of it, you are told the return address on the letter. And the return address is *mercy, and then mercy again.*

Let's sit with those two names, because they are not a repetition — they are two different angles on the same tenderness. The first, *Ar-Rahman*, is built on a shape in Arabic that denotes fullness, vastness, a mercy so overflowing at a given moment that it saturates everything. The second, *Ar-Raheem*, is built on a shape that denotes constancy, permanence, a mercy that *keeps* coming, that renews and renews and does not run out. So the classical scholars read them together like this: *Ar-Rahman* is the ocean; *Ar-Raheem* is that the ocean never dries. One is the immensity. The other is the endurance.

Now put the two halves of this verse together and feel what has just been claimed. The gradual, patient, never-overwhelming *manner* of the revelation — the *tanzīl* — is not a technical detail about delivery schedules. **The pacing is the mercy made visible.** A God who is *Ar-Rahman, Ar-Raheem* does not dump an ocean on a person who can hold a cup. He pours. He waits. He pours again. The very shape of that first word — the gradual shape — is the fingerprint of the two names that follow it.

The physical thing here is *descent* — something coming down, which we can picture. But descent is the stepping-stone. What it points to is unseen: a Source bending toward you, lowering itself to your level, metering out light in doses your eyes can bear. You have felt this, even if you've never named it. You have felt it from anyone who ever loved you enough to not tell you everything at once. Who let you grow into a hard truth instead of crushing you with it on day one.

That is *tanzīl min Ar-Rahman Ar-Raheem*.

### Second: The Book That Was Cut Into Clarity

Here is the word the whole surah is named after. This surah does not have a nickname; it has *this word* for a name. *Fussilat.* When a surah is named after a single verb, that verb is carrying the weight of everything.

*Kitabun fussilat ayatuhu* — "a Book whose verses have been *fussilat*."

Let me first give you the root, because it is one of the most physical, hands-on roots in the language. The root behind *fussilat* is the root of *separating*. It is the word for the joint between two bones — the place where one thing becomes distinctly two. It is the word a judge's ruling comes from — the *fasl*, the decisive cut that separates the true claim from the false one. It is the seam, the division, the clean line where confusion ends and distinctness begins. When a tailor cuts cloth into named, separated pieces, when a butcher separates the joints, when a judge separates right from wrong — that is this root.

And once again the word is poured into that intensive, second shape — so it does not mean merely "separated," it means *thoroughly* separated, meticulously articulated, distinguished part from part with total precision. Nothing left in a lump. Nothing blurred together. Every verse given its own edge.

Now — do you hear what has happened across just three verses?

The surah *opened* with *Ha-Meem*. Two bare letters. A sound that is almost pre-verbal — the *Haa* is just breath from the chest, the *Meem* is just lips closing on a hum. It is sound before it has been shaped into meaning; raw material; the undifferentiated alphabet itself, sitting there unassembled. And then, two verses later, that same undifferentiated stuff is declared to be *fussilat* — cut, separated, articulated into fully distinct meanings. **The Book travels, in front of your eyes, from the un-shaped hum of *Ha-Meem* to the fully-articulated distinctness of *fussilat*.** From raw sound to perfect clarity. And the whole journey between those two points is the act of mercy we just named — because the verse doesn't even tell you *who* did the separating.

Notice that. *Fussilat* is passive. "Its verses *were* detailed." By whom? The grammar leaves the doer unnamed — but it doesn't need to name Him, because the verse *before* already did: *from Ar-Rahman, Ar-Raheem.* The One who cut this Book into clarity is the same Mercy the descent fell from. So the clarity itself is an act of mercy. To make something clear *for* someone is to serve them. A God who wanted only to test you could have sent something dense, ambiguous, cruel to decode. Instead He sent something *fussilat* — carved into distinct, graspable pieces — precisely so you could hold it.

And I want you to *hear* the word, because the sound is doing the work of the meaning. *Fus-si-lat.* That *ص* in the middle is one of the emphatic letters — a heavy, whistling, forceful sound made by pressing the tongue and cutting the airflow. The word for "cutting into clear separation" is a word your own mouth has to cut to pronounce. You cannot say *fussilat* softly. The tongue itself performs a separation. The sound *is* the meaning.

Then the verse tells you the form this clarity took: *Qur'anan 'arabiyyan* — "an Arabic Qur'an." Not a riddle. Not a foreign tongue that would need a priesthood to decode. A recitation in the clearest, most expressive language available to the people it first reached — the word *'arabiyy* itself carrying the sense of what is *clear, plain, unambiguous* in expression. So the theme holds all the way through: descended gently, cut into clarity, delivered in a plain tongue. Every choice bends toward the receiver.

And then — the first crack of light on the receiver's own responsibility: *liqawmin ya'lamūn.* "For a people who know."

Not "for all people." Not "for everyone." *For a people who know.* The Book is offered to everyone, but it *lands* — it becomes benefit, it becomes light — in a particular kind of heart: one that already carries some knowing, some willingness, some ear turned toward truth. And *ya'lamūn* is present-tense, ongoing — not "who once learned a fact" but "who *keep* knowing," who live in a posture of wanting to understand.

Feel the tension this sets up. The Sender has done *everything* on His side. Gentle descent. Perfect articulation. Clear language. The gift is flawless. And yet the last three words of the verse quietly turn the spotlight around and shine it on you: *this reaches — a people who know.* Which means it is now possible, for the first time in the passage, to be a person to whom it does *not* reach. Not because the Book failed. Because of what the receiver brought, or refused to bring.

Which is exactly the wound the next verse presses on.

### Third: They Did Not Disbelieve — They Did Not Hear

The last verse gives the Book two final descriptions, and then it turns, and the turn is where your breath should catch.

First the descriptions: *bashīran wa nadhīra.* "A bringer of glad tidings, and a warner."

Two words, both built on the same intensive shape that means this is not something the Book *occasionally does* — it is something the Book *is*, by its nature, permanently. It doesn't merely give some good news; it is, in its essence, *a bearer of good news*. It doesn't merely issue an occasional warning; it is, in its essence, *a warner*.

And listen to the two words themselves, because again the sound is carrying freight. *Bashīr* — the root is the root of *good news*, and it's the same root as the word for *skin*, because in the Arab imagination good news is the thing that changes your *face*, that rushes the blood to your skin, that you *see* on someone before they speak. *Bashīr* even sounds like it opens — the *sh* spreads, spills outward, like news too good to keep. And then *nadhīr* — heavier, the *dh* sitting low and grave in the mouth, a word that presses down rather than spreads out. Say them one after the other and your own mouth rises and falls: the lift of good news, the weight of the warning. The Book holds both. It is the friend who celebrates with you *and* the friend who grabs your arm before you step off the curb.

And then — *fa-a'rada aktharuhum.* "But most of them turned away."

The verb *a'rada* is beautiful and terrible. Its root is the root of the *side*, the flank of a thing. And the shape it's poured into means, quite literally, *to give someone your side* — to turn your flank to them, to show them your shoulder instead of your face. Picture it physically, because the Qur'an means it physically. Someone is speaking to you, offering you something in their open hands — and you *rotate*. You turn your body. Now they are addressing your shoulder. You have not argued. You have not refuted a word they said. You have simply *turned your side*, and by turning your side you have ended the conversation without ever having it.

That is what most did. Not *fa-kadhabū*, "they called it a lie." Not *fa-jādalū*, "they argued against it." **They turned their side.** They declined to even face it.

And here is the phrase I need you to slow all the way down for. The verse does not conclude, "so they did not *believe*." It does not say "so they *rejected* it." It says: *fahum lā yasma'ūn.* **"So they do not hear."**

*They do not hear.*

Now — these were Arabs. The Book had just been called *'arabiyy*, in their own clear tongue. The sounds physically entered their ears; of course they *heard* it in the sense your ear registers vibration. So this "not hearing" cannot be about the ear. This is the physical word — hearing — being used as a stepping-stone to point at something unseen: the *reception* of the heart. There is a hearing that is just the eardrum, and there is a hearing where the meaning actually arrives, unlocks a door, and is let inside. It is the difference between a sound entering a room and a guest entering a home. They had the first. They shut the door on the second. And notice the tense has shifted — *turned away* is past, a completed act, but *do not hear* is present and ongoing, a permanent condition they now live inside. The turning-away was a moment. The deafness became a home address.

And do you see the terrible symmetry with how the surah began? It opened with *Ha-Meem* — a sound you cannot "hear" as meaning until you lean in and let it be arranged for you. And it closes this opening with people who *will not lean in* — who have meaning offered to them, fully arranged, fully clear, and refuse to let it in. The Book begins with an invitation to listen closely and ends, in these four verses, with the portrait of a people who decided, in advance, not to.

Now hold the whole verse in one hand, because there is an asymmetry hidden inside it that I do not want you to miss. The Book was given as *both* — glad tidings *and* warning. And what does turning your side away actually accomplish? You might think: at least I've dodged the warning. At least I don't have to hear the hard part. But *lā yasma'ūn* — "they do not hear" — is total. It doesn't say they missed the warning. It says they do not *hear*. Which means the very same turn that dodged the warning **also forfeited the good news.**

Read that again, slowly. The one who turns his side to avoid the *nadhīr* — the warning — walks away having also lost the *bashīr* — the glad tidings. He thinks he has escaped a threat. He has actually declined a gift. Both were in the same voice. You cannot deafen yourself to one without deafening yourself to the other. The warning and the good news arrive *through the same ear* — and he closed it.

So look at what these three words — *tanzīl*, *fussilat*, *lā yasma'ūn* — have shown us together. A Book that descended gently from mercy. A Book cut into perfect clarity by that same mercy. And a people who, facing a flawless gift, simply turned their side and stopped hearing.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about seventh-century Mecca — but about the human heart in any century, including this one, including yours and mine.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### The Message Was Never the Problem

Let me put you in a very ordinary moment.

Your phone lights up. It's a message from someone who loves you — a parent, an old friend, a mentor — and you can see from the first line that it's *long*. It's one of those messages. It's going to say something real. Maybe something you need. And you do the thing we all do now, a hundred times a day: you glance, you register that it exists, you feel the small weight of it, and you swipe it away. *Later.* You'll read it later. The little "Read" receipt fires off. They can see you saw it. And later never comes.

We have built an entire civilization around this gesture. We are the most message-saturated human beings who have ever lived. Notifications, feeds, alerts, updates — thousands of pieces of information reaching for us every single day. And to survive it, we have had to develop a defensive skill, and we've become masters of it: the skill of *receiving without receiving*. Of registering that something arrived without ever letting it land. We have trained our hearts to do to almost everything what those Meccans did to the Qur'an — to keep the eardrum open and the door of the heart firmly shut. "Seen" but not heard. Present but not received.

We tell ourselves this is about volume — there's just too much, we *have* to filter. And that's true, partly. But be honest with me for a second. It isn't only the trivial things we swipe away. The message we most reliably leave unopened is the one that would ask something of us. The one that might change something. The clearer it is about what it wants from us, the faster our thumb moves.

*Now* hear this verse.

The Meccans' problem was never that the Qur'an was unclear. Allah has spent three verses hammering exactly that point home: it descended gently, it was *fussilat* — carved into distinctness — it came in their own plain tongue, it was *bashīr* and *nadhīr*, unmistakable in both its joy and its urgency. Every possible barrier on the *sender's* side was removed. And still — *fa-a'rada aktharuhum, fahum lā yasma'ūn.* They turned their side, and they do not hear.

Do you understand what that does to our favorite excuse? We love to believe that our distance from the Qur'an is an *understanding* problem. "I don't get much from it." "It's hard for me to connect." "It doesn't speak to me." And this passage looks at that entire category of excuse and quietly sets it aside — because the people to whom it was *most* clear, in *their own language*, at the *height* of its eloquence, were the ones who turned away hardest. **Clarity was never the bottleneck. Reception was.** The message was never the problem. The ear was.

And here is where it stops being a comfortable story about *them* and becomes uncomfortable about *us*. Because the danger the verse exposes is not the danger of rejecting truth after wrestling with it. It's subtler and far more common than that. It is the danger of *turning your side* — of never facing it at all. Most of us are not in an argument with the Qur'an. We have simply arranged our lives so that it never gets a quiet, undefended moment with our heart. We keep the noise up — Quraysh's own strategy, *make noise over it* — not by shouting over a reciter, but by keeping the feed scrolling, the podcast playing, the schedule full, so that the one Book that descended from mercy to be *heard* is the one voice in our lives that never gets an open ear.

So what actually changes tomorrow? Something small and brutal in its simplicity. It is not "understand the Qur'an better." It is *hear* it once — genuinely. Give it what those Meccans refused to give: one undefended moment. Not with a scholar, not with ten tabs of commentary open, not as a study project. Just you, a few verses in a language you *can* be helped to understand, and a decision — made in advance, the way *they* made the opposite decision in advance — that this time, you will not turn your side. You will let the meaning past the eardrum and into the room. Five minutes. One passage. Door open. That is the entire practice, and this verse says it is the only thing that was ever missing.

### What You Forfeit When You Look Away

There's a letter sitting in a drawer somewhere, or an email marked unread, and you know the one I mean. Maybe it's from the doctor's office. Maybe it's a bank statement you're afraid to open, or a message from someone you had a falling-out with. And you haven't opened it. And every day you don't open it, a little cloud sits over that drawer. You tell yourself you're protecting yourself — that as long as it stays sealed, whatever bad news is inside can't reach you. Avoidance *feels* like safety. It feels like you're at least holding the bad thing at arm's length.

But here is the cruel mechanics of the sealed envelope, and every one of us has learned it the hard way. The thing you were avoiding was almost never *only* bad. The doctor's letter might say *you're fine.* The bank statement might say *you have more room than you feared.* The message from the estranged friend might say *I miss you; can we fix this?* By refusing to open the envelope to escape the worst-case warning, **you also sealed away the good news that was sitting right beside it in the same envelope.** Avoidance doesn't just delay the pain. It cancels the relief you didn't know was coming. It costs you the *bashīr* along with the *nadhīr*.

You already knew this in your bones. This verse just named it as a spiritual law.

Because look again at exactly what the Book was called, in the breath right before the people turned away: *bashīran wa nadhīra* — glad tidings *and* warning. The two things came bound together, in one voice, through one ear. And when *aktharuhum* — most of them — turned their side, the verse does not say they escaped the warning. It says *lā yasma'ūn*: they do not hear. Full stop. The deafness they chose in order to dodge the hard part *also* deafened them to the good part.

And what a *bashīr* it was that they sealed away. This is the good news that the God who made you is *Ar-Rahman, Ar-Raheem*. That there is a return for the one who returns. That the wound you carry has a Healer. That the very Book you're turning from *descended gently* precisely because its Author did not want to break you. That the door you've been dreading is a door of mercy. All of that — the entire content of the good news — was riding in on the same voice as the warning. And the person who turns his side to avoid being told he's off course simultaneously refuses to be told there's a way home.

This is the great inversion the verse exposes about avoidance: **we turn away thinking we're refusing a demand, and we're actually declining a gift.** The Meccan hears "warner" and braces and rotates his shoulder — and in that same motion, without meaning to, he shrugs off "bringer of glad tidings." He thinks he's protecting himself from a threat. He's actually starving himself of the one relief he most needs.

I want to bring this all the way home, into your own chest, because it is easy to keep this at the level of theology and let it float there. Think of the specific corner of your religious life you most avoid. The topic you skip. The reminder you scroll past. The lecture you close because you can feel it's about to ask something of you — the one about the prayer you keep delaying, or the wealth you're not sure about, or the relationship you know isn't right. You turn your side to it *to escape the warning.* And this verse walks up beside you at that exact moment and says, gently: *do you realize what else is in that voice? Do you realize the mercy you are refusing along with the correction? The peace that was bundled with the hard word? You are not just dodging a "no." You are sealing the envelope on a "there is still a way for you."*

So the change this asks of you is not to go seeking out warnings — nobody needs to manufacture more fear. The change is to *stop sealing the envelope.* The next time you feel that reflex — the flinch, the reach for the phone, the "I'll deal with this later" as some reminder or verse or honest thought turns toward the part of your life you've been guarding — catch your own shoulder mid-rotation. And instead of turning your side, turn your face. Because the warning you're bracing for arrived holding the good news you've been aching for, and they will not come through any door but the one you keep shutting.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Beneath the Four Verses

Now that we've done the work, stand back and look at the shape of what Allah built here, because it could not be an accident.

Watch how the passage is weighted. On Allah's side of this transaction — the Sender's side — there is a *cascade* of giving. Count them. First: *tanzīl* — the gentle, gradual descent. Second: *min Ar-Rahman Ar-Raheem* — and its Source is named as mercy, twice. Third: *fussilat* — its verses were cut into perfect clarity. Fourth: *Qur'anan 'arabiyya* — delivered in a plain, understandable tongue. Fifth: *bashīran wa nadhīra* — carrying both the joy and the urgency a soul needs. **Five distinct acts of giving.** Five movements of a Sender bending down toward a receiver, removing every barrier, one after another after another.

And then the human side of the transaction. How many movements does it get? *Two.* *Fa-a'rada* — they turned their side. *Fahum lā yasma'ūn* — so they do not hear. That's it. Five acts of divine descent, met by two acts of human refusal. The imbalance is the message. The architecture is *saying* something: look how far the Mercy came, and look how little it took to turn away from all of it. A whole cascade of light, and a single shrug of the shoulder to shut it out.

And then the bracket — the detail that proves the whole thing was designed. The passage opens with *Ha-Meem*: a sound you cannot receive as meaning unless you lean in and let it be arranged for you. And it closes with *lā yasma'ūn*: people who have the meaning arranged perfectly for them and *refuse to lean in.* The opening is meaning waiting for a listener. The ending is a listener refusing the meaning. The entire revelation, the whole Book that pours out of this surah, sits inside that bracket — held between a divine invitation to *hear* and a human decision *not to.* The letters that need a listener at the start; the listeners who deny their hearing at the end.

### The Two Roads Are One Road

And do you see now that our two themes were never two? They were one insight approached from two sides.

The linguistic journey showed us that the message was flawless — descended from mercy, cut into clarity, spoken plainly, offered as both good news and warning. The first theme showed us that when such a message fails, it is never the message that failed; it is the ear that closed. And the second theme showed us what that closed ear actually *costs* — that turning your side to dodge the warning forfeits the good news riding in beside it. Put them together and you have the whole of these four verses in a single breath: **the most merciful, most clearly articulated message ever sent arrives as pure gift — and the only thing on earth that can defeat it is a heart that decides, in advance, not to hear.**

We began in a room in Mecca where people were literally strategizing to keep this Book from landing. We end realizing we have our own strategies — quieter, more sophisticated, running all day — for keeping every merciful, clarifying voice at the level of the eardrum and never letting it into the room. The distance between us and those Meccans is not as wide as we'd like it to be.

### Questions to Carry

- When was the last time you gave the Qur'an one undefended moment — no second screen, no study agenda, just an open ear — and where in your day could that moment actually live?
- Which reminder, verse, or honest thought do you most reliably turn your side to — and if you're honest, is it because it's *unclear*, or because it's *too* clear about what it's asking?
- What good news might be bundled into the very voice you've been avoiding — and would you even recognize it if you let it in?
- If turning away is a decision you can make in advance, so is turning toward. What would it look like to decide, before tomorrow morning even begins, that this time you will not shrug your shoulder?
- Whose voice in your life is a *bashīr wa nadhīr* — carrying both love and hard truth — and have you been sealing that envelope too?

### One Sentence to Keep

The message was never the problem — the ear was; and the same turning-away that dodges the warning quietly forfeits the mercy that came with it.

### The Invitation

You do not have to become a scholar tonight. You do not have to solve your whole religious life. This passage is not asking for that. It is asking for something almost embarrassingly small, and almost impossibly hard: *one moment of not turning your side.* One passage, heard the way a guest is welcomed into a home rather than a sound registered at a door. Because the Sender has already done everything on His side — the descent, the clarity, the language, the good news held out in open hands. The only thing that was ever missing, in Mecca and in your life, is a face that turns toward instead of away. Turn it. And let what descended from mercy finally land.

### A Du'a From These Verses

O Ar-Rahman, Ar-Raheem, from whom this Book descended gently so as not to break us — do not let us be among those who turn their side. Open in us the hearing beneath the hearing, so that Your clear speech does not merely reach our ears but is welcomed into our hearts. And let us never, in fleeing Your warning, forfeit the glad tidings You bound to it. Make us, by Your mercy, a people who know — and who hear. Ameen.
