# Under Our Feet: When the Deceived Want a Victim Instead of a Way Out

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the words in front of you, three ways.

**The Arabic:**

> وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ رَبَّنَآ أَرِنَا ٱلَّذَيْنِ أَضَلَّانَا مِنَ ٱلْجِنِّ وَٱلْإِنسِ نَجْعَلْهُمَا تَحْتَ أَقْدَامِنَا لِيَكُونَا مِنَ ٱلْأَسْفَلِينَ

**The sound of it:**

*Wa qāla 'lladhīna kafarū rabbanā arinā 'lladhayni aḍallānā mina 'l-jinni wa'l-insi najʿalhumā taḥta aqdāminā li-yakūnā mina 'l-asfalīn.*

**The meaning, in plain English:**

"And those who disbelieved will say: *Our Lord, show us the two who misled us — from the jinn and mankind — that we may place them beneath our feet, so they will be among the lowest.*"

[PAUSE]

Now let me rebuild the room this verse was spoken into.

We are deep inside Sūrah Fussilat — a Makkan surah whose name means *"explained in detail," "made distinct."* And the surah has just done something terrifying in its detail. A few verses back, Allah described the enemies of His message being herded toward the Fire. And at the edge of it, something happens that no human court has ever seen: their own bodies turn state's witness. Their ears speak. Their eyes speak. Their very skin opens its mouth and testifies against them (41:20-21). They turn to their own skin and cry, *"Why did you testify against us?"* — and the skin answers, *"Allah, who made everything speak, made us speak."*

So picture the state of the person in this verse. He is not standing at the beginning of the reckoning, full of excuses. He is **past** all of that. The evidence is in. The verdict is delivered. His own flesh has already betrayed him. He is now inside the consequence.

And just before this verse, Allah told us *how* he got there. Verse 25: *"And We assigned to them companions, who made attractive to them what was before them and what was behind them."* There were voices. There were friends. There was a whole atmosphere of people and unseen whisperers who made the wrong road look like the only sensible road. The person in our verse is now remembering those voices.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Here is what should make you lean in.

This man is, for the first time, completely without illusion. He sees reality exactly as it is. He is standing in the presence of his Lord. His mouth opens. And out of all the things a person could say in that moment — out of *"forgive me,"* out of *"give me one more chance,"* out of *"I was wrong"* — what does he ask for?

He asks for **a name and an address.** He wants Allah to *point out* the two who deceived him. Not so he can understand. So he can **stamp on them.**

What does it tell us about the human heart, that even after every veil is torn away — even standing before God Himself — its first instinct is not to repent, but to find someone to crush?

Let's find out. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: A Verb in the Past Tense for an Event That Hasn't Happened Yet

Let me teach you something about how language carries time before we touch the word.

In every language you speak, the past tense does one job: it tells you something already happened. *"He walked."* Done. Finished. Behind us. The future tense does the opposite job: *"He will walk."* Not yet. Out ahead. Uncertain, in a sense — because the future is the realm of what *might* be.

Now imagine a friend telling you about a contract. If he says, *"They're going to pay me,"* you hear hope. If he says, *"They paid me — the money's in the account,"* you hear certainty. Same event, two tenses, two completely different levels of confidence. The past tense, when used for something not yet done, is the grammar of a guarantee. *"Consider it done."* We do this in English too: a chef says *"that's plated"* before the plate leaves the kitchen. A done deal.

Hold that.

Now look at the first word of our verse:

> **وَقَالَ** — *wa qāla* — "And [he] said."

This is a **perfect-tense verb.** Root *q-w-l*. Grammatically, completed action. *Said.* Past.

But notice — this conversation has not happened yet. This is a scene from the Day of Judgment. It is, from our position in time, entirely in the future. The Fire has not been entered. The words have not been spoken. By every rule of ordinary speech, Allah should have said *"they will say"* — *sa-yaqūlu.*

He said *"they said."*

Let's sit with this.

The classical commentator al-Qurtubī pauses on exactly this point. He writes that the verb is given *"in the form of the past, while what is meant is the future"* — *bi-lafẓ al-māḍī wa'l-murād al-mustaqbal.* The form is past. The meaning is yet to come.

Why would Allah do that?

Here is what's remarkable. In the Arabic of the Qur'an, when Allah describes a future event of the Hereafter using the completed past tense, He is doing something the human tongue cannot honestly do. **He is speaking about the future from a place outside of time.** For us, the future is the uncertain. For the One who created time itself, the Day of Judgment is not a *prediction.* It is not a forecast that *might* come true. It is as fixed, as accomplished, as settled as anything that already lies behind us in history.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that does to the verse. You are not overhearing a *warning* about what *could* happen to a person who refuses the truth. You are overhearing a transcript. A recording of words already spoken — from God's vantage point — by a person who, right now, may still be walking around, eating, laughing, certain he has plenty of time.

The grammar is the proof of the certainty. Allah did not say *"this might be your conversation."* He filed it under *done.*

And that should land on us not as information, but as a chill. Somewhere in the unseen ledger, the words we will say on that Day are written in the past tense. The only thing still open — the only thing still in the *future* — is whose mouth ends up saying them.

### Element Two: "The Two Who Misled Us" — and What the Dual Number Reveals

I need to teach you a feature of Arabic that English simply does not have, because the entire weight of this verse turns on it.

English has two numbers: singular and plural. *One foot, many feet.* *One person, many people.* That's it. The moment you have more than one of something, English lumps it all into "plural."

Arabic has **three** numbers. Singular. Plural. And in between them, a number all its own — the **dual.** A specific grammatical form that means *exactly two.* Not one, not three, not "some." Two. Arabic has special verb endings, special pronouns, special noun forms that exist *only* to say "a pair."

This matters enormously, because when Arabic uses the dual, it is making a deliberate, narrow claim. It is pointing at **two specific entities** and no others.

Now watch how many times this verse reaches for the dual. It is almost insistent.

> أَرِنَا **ٱلَّذَيْنِ** — *arinā 'lladhayni* — "Show us **the two who**…"

That word, *alladhayni*, is the dual relative pronoun. It does not mean "those who" in general. It means *"the two who."*

> **أَضَلَّانَا** — *aḍallānā* — "**the two of them** misled us."

The verb ends in a dual ending — *the two of them* did the misleading.

> نَجْعَلْ**هُمَا** — *najʿal-humā* — "that we may place **the two of them**…"

Dual object pronoun. *Them — the two.*

> لِيَكُونَا — *li-yakūnā* — "so that **the two of them** will be…"

Dual again.

Four times in a single short verse, the grammar narrows its eyes onto **a pair.** Not a crowd of tempters. Not "everyone who ever led me astray." Two.

[PAUSE]

So who are the two?

Here the classical commentators converge into a single, striking picture. Al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, and al-Qurtubī — citing Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd — all read the dual the same way. The two are:

**Iblīs** — the first of the jinn to establish disbelief and rebellion. And **the son of Ādam** — the one who killed his brother — the first human being to establish murder.

One from the jinn. One from mankind. *Min al-jinni wa'l-ins.* The verse names the two realms explicitly, and the dual binds one figure to each.

Now — why *these* two? Listen closely, because there's a precise logic in the tradition, and it's tied to a saying the Prophet ﷺ gave us. Al-Qurtubī cites the *marfūʿ* hadith — a narration traced to the Prophet ﷺ himself, reported by al-Tirmidhī: *"No soul is killed wrongfully except that there is a share of the sin upon the first son of Ādam — because he was the first to establish (sanna) killing."*

There's the key word: **sanna.** *To establish a practice. To open a road that others will walk down.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what the dual is really teaching us. These two are not named because they are the two who personally whispered in this man's ear. They are named because they are the **two origin points** of the world's evil — the two who first *opened the road.* Iblīs opened the road of rebellion against Allah. The son of Ādam opened the road of shedding blood. And every soul that has walked either road since is, in a sense, walking in their footprints.

So when this man in the Fire says *"show me the two who misled us,"* the tradition is telling us he is, on some level, reaching past every direct tempter in his own life all the way back to the **source** — to the two who first made these sins thinkable.

But — and here is where the dual turns from a grammar lesson into a mirror — notice what he is doing with that knowledge.

He has, in a sense, correctly diagnosed the disease. He understands that misguidance has an origin, a structure, a chain. He has a complete theory of how evil entered the world. **And he is using all of it to avoid the one person he could still do something about: himself.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

The dual lets him point with surgical precision at *two.* And the entire point is the one he is refusing to count. The man making this speech is the *third* party in every transaction of his own ruin. Iblīs whispered. A human tradition of sin made it normal. And *he* said yes. The grammar can isolate the two who opened the road. It cannot make a person walk it. That part was always, only, him.

### Element Three: "Beneath Our Feet" and "The Lowest" — the Vocabulary of Descent

Let's turn to the request itself — what he actually wants done to these two. And I want you to listen to this part with your *ears,* because the sound is carrying the meaning.

> نَجْعَلْهُمَا **تَحْتَ أَقْدَامِنَا** — *najʿalhumā taḥta aqdāminā* — "that we may place them **beneath our feet.**"

> لِيَكُونَا مِنَ **ٱلْأَسْفَلِينَ** — *li-yakūnā mina 'l-asfalīn* — "so that they may be **among the lowest.**"

First, the verb *najʿal* — from the root *j-ʿ-l* — "to make, to place, to render." It is a verb of active arrangement. He does not say *"so they will fall beneath our feet"* — as if by accident. He says *"so we may put them"* there. He wants to be the one doing the placing. He wants agency in their humiliation.

And where does he want to put them? *Taḥta aqdāminā* — *beneath our feet.* This is the universal human image of total contempt. Across every culture, to be *under someone's foot,* to be *trampled,* to be *stepped on,* is the lowest position of degradation a body can occupy. He is not asking for justice in the abstract. He is asking for the satisfaction of standing **on** them.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the final word: *al-asfalīn.* From the root *s-f-l* — lowness, the bottom, the depths. Say it slowly. *As-fa-līn.* It is a heavy, sinking, downward-falling word. The *ʿqāf* and *fā* and *lām* drag the sound downward like something settling to the floor of a deep well. The very phonetics of the word *descend.* This is the Qur'an's sonic genius — the word for "the lowest ones" *sounds* like a thing sinking.

And al-Baghawī, citing Ibn ʿAbbās, unpacks what *al-asfalīn* means here: *"that the two of them may be in the lowest depth of the Fire"* — *fī'l-dark al-asfal min al-nār* — and, in Ibn ʿAbbās's words, *"so that the two of them suffer a punishment more severe than ours."*

[PAUSE]

Now — stay with me, because here is the thing the whole verse has been building toward, and it is devastating.

Where is the man making this request?

**He is in the Fire.**

He is not standing in a courtroom petitioning a judge from a position of safety. He is not above, looking down. He is *inside the punishment* asking for two others to be placed *beneath* him, in a layer *lower* than his own.

And here is the quiet, terrible logic of it: **putting someone beneath your feet does not lift you up.** If he gets exactly what he is asking for — if Allah grants this request to the letter — his own situation does not improve by a single degree. The Fire around him does not cool. His skin, which already testified against him, does not heal. The only thing that changes is that there is now someone *lower* than him to look down at.

He has reached the absolute bottom of existence — and his strategy for coping with it is to find someone he can be *less-bottom* than.

[PAUSE — major pause]

That is the vocabulary of descent doing its full work. *Taḥta* — beneath. *Al-asfalīn* — the lowest. Two words pointing relentlessly *downward.* And the man using them does not realize that pointing downward, in the place he now stands, is not a victory. It is just a description of where he already is.

### Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us, three words at a time.

The **perfect tense** told us this conversation is not a possibility — it is a settled fact, written in the past tense from outside of time. The **dual** told us this man has a complete, precise theory of who opened the roads of evil — and is using it to look everywhere except at himself. And the **vocabulary of descent** told us that his entire strategy, at the bottom of everything, is to find someone he can place beneath his feet.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About what lives in *your* heart and mine, long before any Fire.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Reflex of Blame — When We Want a Culprit More Than a Cure

Let me start where you actually live.

Think about the last time something in your life genuinely fell apart. A relationship ended. A project failed. A habit you swore you'd quit pulled you back under. Sit with the *very first* movement of your mind in that moment — not the second thought, the polished one, but the very first reflex.

For most of us, the first reflex is not *"What did I do?"* The first reflex is *"Whose fault is this?"*

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it the **self-serving bias** — the deeply human tendency to credit our successes to ourselves and to blame our failures on other people, on circumstances, on bad luck. *I passed the exam because I'm smart. I failed because the questions were unfair.* It is one of the most reliable, most universal patterns in the entire human mind. We are, every one of us, brilliant defense attorneys for ourselves and ruthless prosecutors of everyone else.

And it makes a kind of sense, doesn't it? Blame is *comfortable.* If the fault is out there — in *them,* in the situation, in the people who misled me — then I don't have to do the hard, painful work of changing *me.* Blame lets the wound stay exactly where it is, as long as I can keep pointing at who caused it.

[PAUSE]

Now bring that reflex into our verse.

This man has the clearest possible vision. There are no more illusions to hide behind. He stands before his Lord with the full truth visible. And in that moment of perfect clarity, his self-serving bias does not die. **It gives its final, fullest performance.** *Show me the two who did this to me. Not me — them. Let me put them under my feet.*

Here is what should stop us cold. We tend to imagine that on the Day of Judgment, the truth will *force* honesty out of us. That seeing reality clearly will automatically produce repentance. **This verse says no.** This man sees *perfectly* — and still reaches for a culprit instead of a cure. The clarity did not soften him. It just gave his blame a sharper target.

Which means the reflex to blame is not a fog that the truth burns away. It is a *posture of the heart* — and a posture you carry through death unless you put it down *now.*

Let the ayah illuminate one more layer. Notice what he calls Allah: *Rabbanā.* **"Our Lord."** Even here, at the bottom, he knows exactly Who he is speaking to. He has not lost his theology. He still addresses Allah by His Lordship. The tragedy is not that he forgot God. The tragedy is that he finally turned to God — and used the conversation to ask for *vengeance* instead of *mercy.* He had the right address and sent the wrong letter.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question this theme leaves on your chest, and the practical thing to do with it.

The next time something falls apart and you feel that first hot reflex — *whose fault is this* — catch it. Name it. And ask the harder, quieter question underneath it: *What was my part?* Not because the others had no part. The verse itself confirms there *were* two who opened the road. But because **their part is not the part you can fix.** The blame-reflex is so seductive precisely because it points you at the one variable in the equation you have no power over. Repentance points you at the only one you do.

Tomorrow, when you fail at something, try saying the sentence this man could not: *"Yā Rabb, my part in this was ___. Forgive me, and help me change it."* That single sentence is the entire difference between his speech and a saved one.

### Theme Two: The Misery That Seeks Someone Below It

Let me open this one somewhere ordinary and a little uncomfortable.

Have you ever noticed how, when people are suffering, some of them go looking for someone *worse off* — not to help them, but to feel better by comparison? The struggling worker who finds relief in mocking someone poorer. The unhappy person in a group chat who feels lighter the moment someone else's life is exposed as more of a mess. There's an old, grim folk wisdom about it: *misery loves company.* But the truer, darker version is this: **misery loves company that's beneath it.** It is not enough, sometimes, to have a companion in pain. The wounded ego wants a companion it can look *down* on.

Why? Because being at the bottom is unbearable to the ego. And the cheapest possible relief from being at the bottom is not climbing up — that's hard — but finding someone *lower.* You don't rise an inch. But the view changes. Now there's a floor beneath you with a face on it.

Hold that, and return to our man in the Fire.

[PAUSE]

This is *precisely* what he is asking for. He is at the bottom of existence. And he does not ask to come *up.* He does not say *"Our Lord, raise me out of this."* He asks for two people to be placed *down* — *beneath his feet,* among *the lowest* — so that he is no longer, technically, the very bottom.

This is *misery loves company that's beneath it,* written at the scale of eternity. It is the group-chat reflex, the comparison-shopping of suffering, the whole pathetic economy of *"at least I'm not as bad as them"* — carried into the Fire and offered to God as a prayer.

And the ayah exposes its total futility with a single fact we already touched: **it changes nothing about him.** He could have the two of them ground beneath his heel for all eternity, and his own punishment would not lessen by an atom. The downward comparison gives the ego a momentary perch, but the Fire is still the Fire.

Let me draw the line all the way back to your life, because this is not only about the Hereafter.

[PAUSE]

Every time *you* reach for someone beneath you to feel better — every time you handle your own sense of failure by quietly cataloguing how others are worse — you are practicing, in miniature, the exact move this man makes in the Fire. You are choosing the cheap relief of a downward glance over the real work of climbing. And here is the spiritual danger: it *works,* a little, every time. It gives just enough comfort that you never deal with why you were at the bottom to begin with. It is a painkiller that lets the disease grow.

The way out — the *only* way out — is the opposite motion. It is to stop measuring yourself against those below and start reaching for the One above. This man's neighbor in scripture, the believer, is the one who in this very life said *Rabbanā* and asked to be *raised* — *"Our Lord, forgive us"* — instead of asking for someone to be lowered.

So here is the practical change. The next time you feel the pull to feel better by looking at someone worse off — pause, and flip the direction of your gaze. Instead of *"at least I'm above them,"* try *"Yā Allah, raise me."* One look goes down and keeps you exactly where you are. The other looks up — and is the first inch of actually moving.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back and see how the two halves of this reflection are the same truth, approached from opposite sides.

The **language** showed us a man whose grammar is flawless. His tense is certain. His dual is precise — he can name the exact two who opened the roads of evil. His vocabulary of descent is vivid and deliberate. Every word is *correct.* And the **themes** showed us a heart that is using all of that correctness to do two ruinous things: to blame instead of repent, and to seek someone below instead of reaching for the One above.

That is the haunting unity of this verse. **This is not a stupid man.** He is not confused. He sees reality perfectly, diagnoses the structure of his own ruin accurately, and addresses God by the right name. His problem was never a lack of understanding. His problem was the *posture of his heart* — and the verse catches that posture frozen, in the past tense, already filed under *done.*

Which means the warning of this ayah is not *"be smarter."* It is *"watch where your clarity points you."* Because you can understand everything and still, in the moment that matters most, ask for a victim instead of a way out.

### Questions to Carry

1. When something in my life falls apart, what is the genuine *first* movement of my mind — toward *"whose fault?"* or toward *"what was my part?"*
2. The man in this verse addressed Allah correctly as *"our Lord"* — and asked for the wrong thing. How often do I turn to Allah in difficulty and ask Him to deal with *others* rather than to change *me*?
3. Where in my life do I quietly feel better by finding someone beneath me — and how is that the same downward glance this verse condemns?
4. If a transcript of what I will say on that Day already exists, written in the past tense — what posture of heart, carried *now*, is writing it?
5. There were two who opened the road. But I am the one who walked it. Have I made peace with the part that was only ever mine?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the deceived finally see with perfect clarity, their first instinct is not to repent but to find someone to crush beneath their feet — yet placing another lower never lifts you out of the Fire.

### The Closing Invitation

You are reading this on the right side of the verse. The conversation it records has not yet reached your mouth. The future — the one genuinely open thing — is whose lips end up saying these words. So let this be an invitation, not a verdict: the move that saves a person is not made in the Fire, where this man made his. It is made *here,* in every small failure of every ordinary day, when you choose to say *"my part was ___, forgive me"* instead of *"show me who did this to me."* Practice the saved man's sentence now, while it still changes the ending.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, when our lives fall apart, turn our first instinct away from blame and toward Your forgiveness. Do not let us be among those who finally call upon You only to ask for vengeance. Raise us by Your mercy, so that we never need someone beneath our feet to bear our weight. Make our clarity lead us to repentance, and write our names among those who were lifted up — not among the lowest. Āmīn.*
