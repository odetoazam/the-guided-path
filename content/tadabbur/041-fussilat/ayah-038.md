# The Worship That Never Tires

## Surah Fussilat, Ayah 38

> فَإِنِ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ فَٱلَّذِينَ عِندَ رَبِّكَ يُسَبِّحُونَ لَهُۥ بِٱلَّيْلِ وَٱلنَّهَارِ وَهُمْ لَا يَسْـَٔمُونَ ۩

*Fa-ini-stakbarū fa-lladhīna ʿinda rabbika yusabbiḥūna lahu bi-l-layli wa-n-nahāri wa-hum lā yasʾamūn.*

**"But if they are too arrogant — then those who are near your Lord glorify Him by night and day, and they never grow weary."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

Imagine you are the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah. For years you have stood before your own people — your uncles, your cousins, the men you grew up trading with — and you have told them one thing: *the sun you bow toward at dawn, the moon you track across the night, these are not gods. They are lamps. Bow instead to the One who lit them.*

And they have refused. Not because they lacked the argument. The ayah just before this one, 41:37, lays the logic out with almost unbearable clarity: **"Do not prostrate to the sun or to the moon, but prostrate to Allah, who created them."** The case is closed. The reasoning is airtight. The sun is a created thing; you do not worship the painting, you honor the painter.

And still — they turn away.

Now put yourself fully inside that moment. You have delivered the truth perfectly. And it has landed on deaf ears, on folded arms, on raised chins. What does that *do* to a person? There is a particular ache in being right and being rejected. A particular loneliness in carrying light to people who would rather stay in the dark. If you have ever told someone something true and watched them refuse it — a parent, a friend, a whole room — you know a small drop of what this felt like.

So here is the question this ayah answers. After the perfect argument fails — **what now?**

You might expect a threat. You might expect: *if they refuse, warn them of the Fire.* You might expect God to turn His attention to the deniers and crush their excuse.

[PAUSE]

He does not.

Instead, He does something so unexpected, so quietly enormous, that once you see it you will never recite this ayah the same way again. He lifts the Prophet's gaze — and ours — off the people who refused, and points it upward, to a reality already in motion above our heads.

Here is the hook. Hold onto it: **What if the response to human arrogance is not anger — but the simple, crushing fact that the universe never stopped worshipping for even a second?**

Let's go and find it in the words themselves.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Istakbarū* — The Arrogance You Manufacture Yourself

### Teaching the tool first

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs are built, because the entire weight of this opening rests on it.

In Arabic, a root — three consonants — is like a seed. You can plant that seed in different *forms*, different patterns, and each pattern grows the meaning in a different direction. Think of the English root "large." From it you can grow: *to enlarge* (to make something bigger), *to be large* (a simple state), or imagine a special form that means *to make yourself out to be large* — to puff yourself up, to claim a bigness you don't actually have.

Arabic has exactly that last form. It's called **Form X**, and it carries the sense of *seeking* something, *claiming* it, *deeming yourself* to have it. You take a quality and you reach for it, demand it, drape it over yourself.

Hold that idea: a form that means *claiming a quality for yourself*.

### Opening the word

The root here is **ك-ب-ر (k-b-r)** — the root of *greatness, bigness, majesty*. It is one of the most weighted roots in the entire Quran, because from it comes one of Allah's own names: **al-Mutakabbir** — the Supremely Great, the One who is rightfully and absolutely Magnificent.

And the verb in our ayah is **ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ — *istakbarū*.**

Look at its shape. The Leeds morphological corpus confirms it: this is **Form X** of the root *k-b-r*. The *ist-* at the front is that "seeking, claiming, deeming-yourself" prefix.

So the word does not simply mean "they were great." It means: **they made themselves out to be great. They claimed a bigness. They reached for a majesty and wrapped it around themselves.**

### Sitting with it

Now notice something, and stay with me here, because this is where the morphology stops being grammar and becomes theology.

*Kibr* — true greatness — genuinely exists. It is real. It is just not theirs. It belongs to One Being, and He even named Himself by it: **al-Mutakabbir.** Greatness is a divine attribute. It is God's coat.

So what is a human being doing in *istikbār*?

He is putting on a coat that is not his. He is claiming, in the very structure of the word, an attribute that belongs to Allah alone. The Form X morphology exposes the lie *built into the act itself.* Arrogance, the Quran is telling us at the level of grammar, is not just a bad attitude — **it is theft.** It is a creature reaching up and trying to pull the cloak of majesty off of the Creator and onto himself.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's remarkable. The grammar tells you it can never succeed. Because the *ist-* form means *to claim* — not *to possess*. You can claim greatness all day long. The word never lets you actually *have* it. The arrogant person, even at the height of his arrogance, is only ever *pretending*. He has manufactured a bigness inside his own head. It exists nowhere else in the universe.

The classical commentators are unanimous and plain about *what* they refused. Al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Muyassar all gloss it the same way: *istakbarū ʿan al-sujūd* — **they were too arrogant to prostrate.** Their greatness was too precious, in their own minds, to be put on the ground.

Think about what that means physically. The arrogant man cannot bring his forehead to the floor. The very posture of *sujūd* — the highest part of you, your face, laid in the dust — is the bodily opposite of *istikbār*. Arrogance is a stiffness in the spine. Worship is the willingness to bend.

So when the ayah opens with *fa-ini-stakbarū* — **"but if they are too arrogant"** — we are not hearing about a personality flaw. We are hearing about a soul that has stolen a coat it cannot wear and stiffened its own back so it can never bow.

That's the first word. Let it settle. [PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Sentence That Refuses to Finish

### Teaching the tool first

Here is a second feature, and it lives not in a word but in a *silence*.

In Arabic — and in English too — a conditional sentence has two halves. "If it rains, *then I'll bring an umbrella.*" The first half sets the condition. The second half — the *apodosis*, scholars call it — tells you the result. "If it rains..." leaves you hanging. Your mind *needs* the second half. You lean forward waiting for it.

Now imagine someone says to you: "If you're going to be like that —" and then just... stops. Turns. Walks away. And starts talking calmly to someone else entirely.

What does that silence communicate? It communicates that you are *not even worth the second half of the sentence.* The dropped ending is more cutting than any completed threat could have been.

Keep that feeling in your body.

### Opening the silence

The ayah begins: **فَإِنِ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ — "But if they are too arrogant..."**

Your ear, trained on Arabic, now waits. *If they are too arrogant — then WHAT?* Then they are doomed? Then warn them? Then leave them? The grammar has opened a door and you are standing in it, waiting for the consequence to walk through.

And what comes through the door instead?

**فَٱلَّذِينَ عِندَ رَبِّكَ يُسَبِّحُونَ لَهُۥ — "then those who are near your Lord glorify Him."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. The expected consequence — the threat, the warning, the *you* of it all — **never comes.** The grammatical "then" doesn't point back at the arrogant. It pivots completely away from them, lifts up to the heavens, and reports on something else entirely: *the angels are glorifying Him.*

Do you feel what just happened? The arrogant deniers set up a confrontation. They puffed themselves up, expecting — in some corner of their pride — that their refusal *mattered*. That God would have to deal with them. That their "no" would land like a blow.

And the divine response is to **not even complete the sentence about them.** It is the conversational equivalent of God turning away mid-thought, unbothered, and simply noting: *the worship continues elsewhere, perfectly, without you.*

The unspoken consequence is the whole point. The dropped apodosis says, more loudly than any spoken threat: **Your refusal changes nothing. You were never load-bearing. The cathedral of worship does not have a single stone resting on your shoulders.**

This is what scholars of *balāghah* call the suppressed apodosis — and here the suppression is itself the message. What is left unsaid is the deniers' irrelevance.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: *Lā Yasʾamūn* — The Word That Sounds Like a Sigh

### Teaching the tool first

One more word, and this one I want you to *hear*, not just understand.

Sometimes a word in the Quran sounds like the very thing it describes. The harsh words are harsh on the tongue; the gentle words flow. This is not decoration — it is meaning carried in sound.

### Opening the word

The ayah ends: **وَهُمْ لَا يَسْـَٔمُونَ — "and they never grow weary."**

The root is **س-أ-م (s-a-m)**, and the verb is *yasʾamūn*. Say it slowly. *Ya — sʾa — mūn.* Notice the little catch in the middle — that hamza, that glottal stop, a tiny hitch in the throat. The word almost makes you sigh to pronounce it. *Saʾima.* It sounds like exactly what it means: **to be sick of something. To be bored. To be worn down by the sheer tedium of repetition.**

This isn't ordinary tiredness — not the *taʿab* of muscles after labor. The lexicon and the commentators are precise here. Al-Qurṭubī glosses it: *lā yamallūn ʿibādatahu* — **they do not get bored of His worship.** Al-Muyassar adds: *lā yafturūn* — they do not slacken, do not lose momentum. Al-Baghawī combines both: *lā yamallūn wa-lā yafturūn.*

So *saʾam* is the weariness of *monotony*. It is the feeling of having done a thing so many times that the soul goes numb to it. It is the yawn in the heart.

And al-Qurṭubī does something beautiful — he reaches for poetry to make sure we feel it. He quotes the pre-Islamic poet Zuhayr:

> *"I have grown weary (saʾimtu) of the burdens of life — and whoever lives eighty years, by your father, grows weary."*

Do you hear what Zuhayr is confessing? That *saʾam* is the human condition. That to be a creature in time is, eventually, to get tired of even living. Eighty years and the soul sags. The human being is a being who *wears out.*

### Sitting with it

Now hold Zuhayr's sigh in one hand. And in the other, hold the ayah's negation: **لَا يَسْـَٔمُونَ — they do *not* wear out.**

The contrast is the entire universe in a single line. On one side: the human being, who can grow weary of *life itself* given enough decades. On the other side: beings *ʿinda rabbika*, near your Lord, who glorify Him **by night and by day** — *bi-l-layli wa-n-nahār*, the full unbroken cycle, dawn after dawn after dawn for the entire age of creation — and the boredom *never comes.* The yawn in the heart never arrives. The hundred-millionth tasbīḥ is offered with the same freshness as the first.

[PAUSE]

And notice the verb of their worship: **يُسَبِّحُونَ — *yusabbiḥūn*.** The Leeds corpus marks it Form II — *sabbaḥa*, with the doubled, stressed middle letter. Form II in Arabic intensifies and multiplies. You can even *hear* the repetition in the shadda, the doubled *b*, the sound knocking twice. *Tasbīḥ* is not a single statement; it is the endless, abundant declaration that **Allah is free of every flaw, every deficiency, every limitation.** It pours out of them, doubled and redoubled, night and day, and it never once curdles into routine.

That is the third word. And now we have our three: a stolen greatness (*istakbarū*), a sentence too dismissive to finish, and a worship that never sighs (*lā yasʾamūn*).

## The Bridge

Look at what the language has shown us. It has placed two beings side by side without ever naming the comparison out loud.

The arrogant creature: stiff-backed, unable to bow, claiming a greatness that isn't his, and — though the ayah never says it directly — *exactly the kind of being who gets bored, who wears out, who is too proud to repeat an act of humility even once.*

The beings near the Lord: endlessly bowing, claiming nothing for themselves, pouring out *tasbīḥ* by night and day, and never, ever weary.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Your Refusal Is Lighter Than You Think

### Begin where you live

Let me start with something very human, very modern.

Have you ever quit something dramatically — slammed a door, walked off a team, deleted an account, sworn you were done — and then discovered, days later, that the thing kept running perfectly well without you? The project shipped. The group chat kept laughing. The world, it turned out, was not holding its breath for your return.

There is a specific deflation in that. We carry, most of us, a quiet conviction that we are *load-bearing*. That our participation is part of what holds the structure up. That if we withdraw, something will crack.

And usually — let's be honest — it doesn't crack. Life closes over the space where we were like water closing over a dropped stone.

Now. Take that very human experience and turn it toward God.

### Let the ayah illuminate it

The deniers of Makkah believed their refusal *mattered.* That is the hidden vanity inside all arrogance: the assumption that your "no" has weight. That by withholding your worship, you are somehow making a dent. *I will not bow* — said as though the not-bowing were an event in the cosmos.

And this ayah, with its sentence that won't even finish, replies: **the worship was never depending on you.**

*Fa-lladhīna ʿinda rabbika yusabbiḥūn* — those near your Lord glorify Him — *by night and by day, and they never tire.* While you were busy refusing, the heavens did not pause. Not for a second. The angels did not look down, notice your folded arms, and falter in their tasbīḥ. The cosmic liturgy rolled on, seamless, full, *uninterrupted by your absence.*

Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing to soften it: **this is simultaneously the most humbling and the most liberating thing you could possibly hear.**

Humbling — because it means your grand refusal is not a thunderclap. It is a stone dropped in an ocean. The water closes over it. God is *al-Ghaniyy*, the Self-Sufficient; as another ayah says, *if you disbelieve, surely Allah is free of need of you* (39:7). Your defiance does not wound Him. It cannot. He was complete before you, and He is complete without you.

But now feel the *other* side of the same fact. [PAUSE]

If your *refusal* changes nothing about His majesty — then your *worship* adds nothing to it either. He does not need your prayer the way an idol needs its offerings, the way a king needs his subjects' taxes. The angels' endless tasbīḥ does not *fund* God. He is not running on it.

So why do they do it? Why does the universe glorify a God who needs none of it?

### Going deeper

Because worship was never a *transaction*. That is the liberation hidden inside the humbling.

If God needed your worship, your worship would be a *bill* — a debt you pay, resentfully, watching the clock. And that is precisely how the arrogant heart experiences it: as a tax, a humiliation, a cost. *Why should I bow?* The arrogant question assumes worship is something you *give up* — your dignity, your time, your autonomy.

But the beings near the Lord show us the truth: worship that *adds nothing to God* and yet *never grows weary* can only be one thing — **worship that is its own reward.** They glorify Him not to fill a need in Him, but because being near Him and declaring His perfection is, for them, the most natural and joyful state of existence. A fish does not tire of water. A flame does not tire of burning. The angels do not tire of *tasbīḥ* because glorifying the Perfect One is simply what it is to be fully alive in His presence.

So the deflation we feel when the world runs on without us — that turns out to be a mercy. It frees worship from being about *us being needed.* I do not pray because God would falter without me. I pray because *I* would falter without Him. The need runs entirely one way. And that is exactly what makes it safe to come home.

### What changes tomorrow

So here is the practical turn. The next time the thought rises — *does it even matter if I pray? Would anyone, would anything, notice?* — answer it honestly: **No. The cosmos will not notice. The tasbīḥ continues with or without you.** And then notice that this is the best news you have ever heard. Because it means your prayer is not a payment extracted from you under threat. It is an invitation to join something that was already perfect, already joyful, already underway. You are not propping up the cathedral. You are being invited *in* to a song that has been going since before you were born.

Tonight's prayer, then, is not a debt you owe. It is a door someone left open for you.

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: The War Against Weariness

### Begin where you live

Let me name something you already know in your body.

You have started things on fire and watched the fire go out. The first ten days of a new habit — the new prayer routine, the Quran-every-morning resolution, the dhikr after every salah — they *glow*. And then, somewhere around day fifteen, day thirty, a grey fog rolls in. Not a crisis. Not a temptation. Just... *flatness.* The act that thrilled you now bores you. Your tongue says the words and your heart has wandered off to make a sandwich.

We have a thousand modern names for this now. Burnout. Habituation. The hedonic treadmill. The dopamine system that goes quiet the moment the novel becomes familiar. Scientists will tell you the brain is *built* to stop noticing what repeats — that novelty is the only fuel that reliably fires the reward circuit, and that the price of that design is **boredom with the familiar.** We are wired to get sick of what we have.

This — exactly this — is *saʾam.* Zuhayr named it three thousand years ago: *whoever lives eighty years grows weary.* It is the oldest human ailment. The soul's tendency to go numb.

### Let the ayah illuminate it

And right at this raw nerve, the ayah lays its finger: **وَهُمْ لَا يَسْـَٔمُونَ — and they never grow weary.**

Stop and feel how strange that is. The angels do the *same act* — *tasbīḥ* — by night and by day, for the entire span of creation. By every law of the human nervous system, they should be the most bored beings in existence. The same words, the same posture, the same praise, repeated past all counting. If repetition kills wonder, theirs should have died eons ago.

And it hasn't.

So either the angels are anesthetized — droning on like machines, feeling nothing — or **they have access to something we don't: a relationship with the repeated act that never goes stale.**

It cannot be the first. *Tasbīḥ* is not a machine's output; it is the living acknowledgment of God's perfection. So it must be the second. And that should stop us in our tracks. Because it means *saʾam* — boredom with worship — is **not inevitable.** It is not a law of the universe. It is a feature of *our* condition, not of *worship itself.* Somewhere, beings are doing the very thing that bores us, and it never once bores them.

### Going deeper

So what do they have that we don't? Let's reason toward it.

The angels are *ʿinda rabbika* — **near your Lord.** That word *ʿinda*, "near, in the presence of," is the key. Their worship never goes stale because they are never *distant from its object.* They are not reciting praise *about* a God who is far away; they are praising a God in whose presence they continuously stand. The praise is fresh because the *Praised* is right there, infinitely glorious, infinitely worth praising — and an infinite object never exhausts.

Here is the insight, and it overturns everything we assume about boredom: **boredom is not caused by repetition. It is caused by repetition of the *finite*.** You get bored of a song because a song is finite; play it enough times and you have heard everything it contains. You get bored of a meal, a view, a toy, because each is a bounded thing and you eventually reach its bottom.

But what if the object of attention were *infinite*? What if there were always more of it to discover, no matter how long you gazed? Then repetition would not be a return to the same place — it would be a deeper and deeper plunge into a thing without a bottom. The ten-thousandth *Subḥān Allāh* would not be a copy of the first. It would be a step further into an ocean that has no floor.

That is why the angels never tire. They are not repeating a finite act. They are perpetually discovering a God who is *Subḥānahu* — endlessly beyond every limit. Their *tasbīḥ* is not a loop. It is a descent into the infinite.

[PAUSE]

And now look back at *us.* Why do *we* get bored in prayer? Not because prayer is boring. But because, in the moment of boredom, we have quietly let the *finite* take God's place. We are no longer praising the Infinite One; we are performing a finite ritual, mechanically, with our attention on the clock, the to-do list, the sandwich. We have swapped the bottomless ocean for a small familiar puddle — and of course we got bored of the puddle. The boredom is not a verdict on prayer. **It is a signal that we have lost sight of Who we are praying to.**

### What changes tomorrow

So the cure for *saʾam* is not more willpower. It is not gritting your teeth through the grey fog. The cure is *ʿinda rabbika* — to drag your attention back to the *nearness*, back to the infinite Object. The next time prayer feels flat, do not fight the flatness directly. Instead, in the middle of the *Subḥāna Rabbiya al-ʿAlā* in your sujūd, ask yourself one thing: *Whom am I actually addressing right now?* And let the answer — *the One who is beyond all limit, in whose presence I am standing this very second* — flood back in. The flatness lives on forgetting nearness. Restore the nearness, and the freshness returns on its own.

The angels are not super-humans with stronger discipline. They simply never forgot Who was in front of them. That is the entire difference.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Now stand back and look at the whole ayah at once, because its design is a perfect, sealed argument.

It opens with the human at his worst: **ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟** — *istakbarū* — a creature puffed up with a stolen greatness, too stiff to bow.

It closes with the heavens at their best: **لَا يَسْـَٔمُونَ** — *lā yasʾamūn* — beings who bow without end and never tire.

And between these two poles, the suppressed sentence — the "then..." that never lands on the deniers — *is the bridge.* It refuses to dignify the arrogant with a consequence, and instead lets the contrast itself be the verdict. The arrogant claim greatness and get bored of everything; the angels claim nothing and never tire of the One thing. **The ayah never states the comparison. It builds it into the architecture and lets you arrive at it yourself.**

## Bringing it together

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the two themes are one single insight viewed from different sides.

The morphology of *istakbarū* showed us arrogance is **stolen greatness** — claiming God's coat. The first theme showed us why the theft is futile: the worship of the cosmos runs on *with or without you*, so your refusal is weightless, and — wonderfully — so is the pressure, because God needs nothing from you.

The sound of *lā yasʾamūn* showed us a worship that **never goes stale.** The second theme showed us why: because it is anchored in *nearness* to an *infinite* God, and the infinite never exhausts.

And the two themes meet in one place: **the arrogant person and the bored worshipper are the same person.** Both have lost sight of the Infinite. The arrogant one shrank God small enough to refuse Him. The bored one shrank God small enough to yawn at Him. The cure for both is identical — *ʿinda rabbika* — recover the nearness, recover the infinitude, and both the arrogance and the boredom dissolve, because you cannot be arrogant before, or bored by, the Limitless One who is right in front of you.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I quietly assuming that my participation is *load-bearing* — that something would collapse without me — when the truth is it would close over my absence like water?
2. When I get bored in prayer, have I been blaming *the prayer* — when the real change is that I have stopped looking at *Whom I am praying to*?
3. Is there a posture of *sujūd* my pride still cannot make — not physically, but inwardly? Some bending I keep refusing?
4. The angels claim *nothing* for themselves and never tire. I claim *much* for myself and tire constantly. Is there a connection there I have been avoiding?
5. If my worship adds nothing to God — and it doesn't — what would change in how I pray if I truly believed prayer was an *invitation*, not a *debt*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

When the arrogant refuse to bow, God does not even finish the threat — He simply turns to a heaven that has never once stopped glorifying Him, and never grown weary of it, because nearness to the Infinite is the one thing a soul can never exhaust.

## The Closing Invitation

You will not become an angel. You will get tired. You will hit the grey fog around day thirty. That is your nature, and the ayah names it honestly through Zuhayr's sigh.

But the ayah is not asking you to *never* tire. It is showing you *why* the tiring happens — distance, forgetting, the finite swallowing the Infinite — so that you know exactly where to reach when the fog rolls in. You reach back toward *ʿinda rabbika*. You restore the nearness. And the freshness, which was never really gone, returns.

This is a verse of *sajdah*. The ones who refused could not put their foreheads down. You can. Let your body answer the ayah where their pride could not. Go down — and join, for one moment, the worship that never tires.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who are free of all need of us — we know our refusal cannot diminish You and our worship cannot complete You; let us bow anyway, for our own sake, not Yours. Cure the weariness in our hearts the way You spared the wearying of those near You: by holding us close enough to never lose sight of who You are. And when our backs stiffen with the pride we stole from You, bend them gently back toward the ground.* Āmīn.
