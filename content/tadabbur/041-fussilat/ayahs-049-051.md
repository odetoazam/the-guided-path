# This Belongs to Me: The Tireless Asker and the Wide, Wide Prayer

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by simply placing the words in front of us.

> [ayah:41:49] لَّا يَسْـَٔمُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ مِن دُعَآءِ ٱلْخَيْرِ وَإِن مَّسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ فَيَـُٔوسٌ قَنُوطٌ
>
> *Lā yas'amu l-insānu min duʿāʾi l-khayri, wa in massahu sh-sharru fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭ.*
>
> **"Man does not tire of praying for good, but if evil touches him, then he is despairing, hopeless."**

And then, two verses later, after a verse in the middle we will come back to:

> [ayah:41:51] وَإِذَآ أَنْعَمْنَا عَلَى ٱلْإِنسَـٰنِ أَعْرَضَ وَنَـَٔا بِجَانِبِهِۦ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ فَذُو دُعَآءٍ عَرِيضٍ
>
> *Wa idhā anʿamnā ʿalā l-insāni aʿraḍa wa naʾā bi-jānibihi, wa idhā massahu sh-sharru fa-dhū duʿāʾin ʿarīḍ.*
>
> **"And when We bestow favor upon man, he turns away and withdraws to his side; but when evil touches him, then he is full of wide, sprawling prayer."**

### Rebuilding the world

To feel where this lands, we have to remember what kind of surah we are standing in.

Fussilat — the name means *expounded*, *laid out in detail*, *made distinct*. The whole surah is built around a single tension: Allah has made the message **clear**. He has spelled it out. And still, a heart turns away. Earlier in the surah, the disbelievers of Makkah say, almost mockingly, *"Our hearts are within coverings from that to which you invite us, and in our ears is deafness, and between us and you is a partition"* (41:5). They are not confused. They have *decided* to be sealed.

So by the time we reach our verses, the Quran has finished arguing with the intellect. It does something quieter and far more uncomfortable. It stops describing what the disbeliever *thinks* and starts describing how he *behaves* — specifically, how he behaves toward God across the swings of his own fortune. Al-Ṭabarī, in his commentary, situates these verses precisely: they arrive immediately on the heels of verse 48's devastating verdict, in which the disbelievers' gods are described as going utterly astray from them on the Day of Judgment — taking a different road, offering no protection, no benefit whatsoever. Having diagnosed what becomes of the false objects of worship, the Quran now turns the lens onto the inner architecture of the one who worshipped them: not the destination, but the *disposition that arrived there*. The classical commentators — al-Suddī as cited by al-Qurṭubī, and likewise al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn — read *al-insān*, "man," here as primarily the disbelieving heart. Al-Qurṭubī even reports several traditions naming specific Makkan figures the description was associated with, though he reports more than one and does not settle on a single name. Hold that lightly. Because the deeper you read it, the more you suspect the diagnosis was never meant to stay safely on *them*.

This is a portrait. And the discomfort of a portrait is that you keep thinking it's of someone else until you notice your own posture in it.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to carry into the next hour.

Allah is about to describe a person whose prayer life is, on paper, *intense*. He never tires of asking. When trouble hits, he produces prayer that the Quran will call **wide** — a strange, physical word we will sit with for a long time. By every external measure, this is a praying human being.

And yet this entire passage is a diagnosis of a **spiritual sickness**.

So the question that should unsettle us is this: *How can someone pray that much — and still be the patient in the portrait?* What is it that turns tireless, sprawling, abundant prayer into a symptom rather than a cure?

Stay with that. The answer is hidden in three small words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yas'am* — the man who never gets tired

Let me teach you something about how we speak before we open the word.

In any language, the verbs we use for *stopping* an activity tell you something about the activity itself. You stop running because your legs *fail*. But you stop a hobby because you got *bored* of it. You stop visiting someone because the visits became *tedious*. "Bored," "weary," "fed up" — these aren't about physical exhaustion. They're about the inner appetite drying up. The thing no longer pulls you. You're *done with it* on the inside, even if your body could keep going.

Arabic has a precise word for exactly that inner drying-up. The root is **س-أ-م (s-'-m)**, and the verb is **سَئِمَ (saʾima)** — to become weary *of* something, to grow sick of it, to lose all taste for it. Not "tired" in the muscles. Tired in the *wanting*. The lexicographers gloss it as *malal* — boredom, satiation, the point where the soul says *enough, I no longer have any appetite for this*.

Now open the verse:

> **لَا يَسْـَٔمُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ** — *Lā yas'amu l-insān* — "Man does not get weary..."

Notice the verb is in the **imperfect tense** — *yas'amu*, not *sa'ima*. In Arabic the imperfect carries ongoing, renewing, habitual action. This isn't "he didn't get bored once." It's "he goes on and on and *never* hits the wall of weariness." The appetite never satiates.

And weary of *what*? **مِن دُعَآءِ ٱلْخَيْرِ** — "of praying for good."

Let's sit with this.

The Quran could have praised this. Persistence in prayer is, elsewhere, exactly what Allah loves — the servant who keeps knocking. So why is *tirelessness* the opening note of a portrait of sickness?

Here's what's remarkable. Notice what the man is tireless *about*. He never tires **of asking for good**. The lexicons and the mufassirūn fill in what *al-khayr*, "the good," means here, and listen to the list — al-Qurṭubī: *al-māl, aṣ-ṣiḥḥa, as-sulṭān, al-ʿizz* — wealth, health, authority, status. Al-Baghawī: *al-māl wa-l-ghinā wa-ṣ-ṣiḥḥa* — money, riches, health. Al-Jalālayn concurs, adding *wa ghayrihimā* — "and other things besides" — deliberately leaving the category open, a list that covers the entire spectrum of worldly desire. There is even a recorded reading, al-Qurṭubī notes, in the *muṣḥaf* of ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd: *lā yasʾamu l-insānu min duʿāʾi l-māl* — "man does not tire of praying *for wealth*." Al-Muyassar seals the interpretation with a single adjective: *al-khayr al-dunyawī* — **worldly** good. The word the Quran chose is deliberately general, but every classical scholar fills it in the same direction: downward, toward the earth, toward the hand.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The "good" he never tires of asking for is the good *that comes to him*. The inflow. The acquisition. His prayer is inexhaustible — but it is inexhaustible in one direction. He is not tireless in *gratitude*. He is not tireless in *praise*. He is tireless in **request**. His relationship with God has the stamina of an appetite, not the stamina of love.

And here is the thing about an appetite: it never gets weary because **it is never satisfied**. The reason he *lā yas'amu*, never reaches *malal*, never hits "enough" — is precisely that he was never seeking God in the first place. He was seeking the *thing*. And you do not get bored of the gift the way you might, God forbid, drift in your attention to the Giver. The very tirelessness that looks like devotion is the fingerprint of its absence.

What this single word reveals: **endless prayer is not the same as a living relationship — because an appetite, too, is endless.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Ya'ūs Qanūṭ* — why two words for despair

Now watch what happens the moment the inflow reverses.

> **وَإِن مَّسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ** — "but if evil *touches* him..."

One word here, before the despair, that we cannot rush past: **مَسّ (mass)** — *to touch*. Not "if evil crushes him." Not "if catastrophe levels him." *Massa* is the lightest possible contact — a graze, a brush, the touch of a fingertip. The same root the Quran uses for the *touch* of fire, the *touch* of hardship. It is deliberately small.

And against that *touch* — that mere graze of difficulty — look at the response Allah places:

> **فَيَـُٔوسٌ قَنُوطٌ** — *fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭ.*

Two words. Both meaning, roughly, *despair*. And here is where Arabic does something English cannot.

Let me teach the pattern first. Both of these words sit on the morphological mold **فَعُول (faʿūl)** — *yaʾūs*, *qanūṭ*. In Arabic this mold is one of the great **intensive** forms, *ṣīghat al-mubālagha*: it doesn't say a person *did* a thing, it says the thing has become their *characteristic*, their saturated, defining quality. Not "he despaired" but "he is despair-*soaked*, despair is his very mold."

So already each word alone is intense. But Allah uses **two**. Side by side. With no conjunction softening the blow — *fa-yaʾūsun qanūṭ*, just stacked. Why?

Because they are not synonyms. They are two **stages**, or two **faces**, of the same collapse, and the mufassirūn separate them with surgical care.

**يَـُٔوس (yaʾūs)** is from **ي-أ-س (y-'-s)** — the loss of *hope*. It is the inner state. The lights going out on the inside. Al-Baghawī glosses it: *min rawḥi llāh* — despairing of the *relief*, the breath of mercy, of Allah. The expectation dies.

**قَنُوط (qanūṭ)** is from **ق-ن-ط (q-n-ṭ)** — and the lexicons tie it to despair that *shows*, despair that has *manifested*, *min raḥmatihi*, of His mercy. Al-Qurṭubī adds the deadliest layer of all: *qanūṭ bi-sūʾi ẓ-ẓanni bi-rabbihi* — despairing **through thinking badly of his Lord**. Al-Muyassar confirms this reading independently, word for word: *qanūṭun bi-sūʾi ẓ-ẓanni bi-rabbihi*. This is not a lone opinion. Across the classical commentators, *qanūṭ* consistently names despair that carries with it a **charge against God** — not simply "I've lost hope" but "this says something about *Him*."

[PAUSE]

Do you see the sequence the two words trace?

First *yaʾūs*: the inner hope collapses. *I don't think it will get better.* Then *qanūṭ*: that collapse hardens into something visible, something that **accuses God**. *I don't think it will get better — and that says something about Him.* Hope dies in the chest, and on its way out it slanders the Lord.

And now feel the **sound** of these words in your mouth. *Yaʾūs.* *Qanūṭ.* Both dominated by that long, heavy **ū** — a vowel you have to *sustain*, drawn out, sealed in. And *qanūṭ* ends on **ṭ**, one of Arabic's heaviest emphatic letters, a sound that lands like a door slamming. The words *sound* like what they mean: a long, sealed, thudding closure. The mouth itself enacts the shutting-down.

Here is the diagnosis in full now. The same man who was **tireless** in asking — who never reached *malal*, never hit "enough" — reaches total despair at the mere **touch** of evil. His stamina for *receiving* good was infinite. His tolerance for *any* difficulty is zero. And the gap between those two — bottomless appetite, no endurance — *is* the sickness. A heart anchored to God can absorb the touch of harm, because its hope was never in the inflow. A heart anchored to the inflow shatters at the first graze, because the inflow was its only god.

What these two words reveal: **the speed of our despair is a confession of where our hope was actually resting.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *ʿArīḍ* — the prayer that is wide

We come to the strangest word in the passage, and I have been waiting the whole time to put it in front of you.

In verse 51 the portrait completes itself. When We give him favor — *aʿraḍa wa naʾā bi-jānibihi* — "he turns away and **withdraws to his side**." Hold that image for one second: *naʾā bi-jānibihi* is a physical picture — turning your *flank*, your shoulder, your whole side away from someone. We do it bodily when we are done with a conversation. The Quran uses the body to show you the soul: blessing arrives, and he *gives God the shoulder.*

And then — when evil touches him — **فَذُو دُعَآءٍ عَرِيضٖ** — "then he is a possessor of *wide* prayer."

Let me teach this word, because everything converges here.

**عَرِيض (ʿarīḍ)**, from **ع-ر-ض (ʿ-r-ḍ)**, means **broad, wide** — width, breadth, the horizontal measure of a thing. Now here is the point I need you to feel: in Arabic, when you describe something as *long*, abundant, drawn-out *in the good sense*, you reach for **طويل (ṭawīl)** — *long*. A *long* prayer, a *long* life, a *long* reach. **Length** is the natural, expected word for "a lot of prayer."

But Allah did **not** say *duʿāʾin ṭawīl*. He said *duʿāʾin* **ʿarīḍ** — **wide** prayer. And the Arab ear catches this instantly as strange, almost jarring — because **width** is the wrong dimension for prayer. Width is for *objects*. For land. For a road. For something *spread out flat*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what *width* does to the image, as against *length*.

A **long** prayer rises. It has direction — it goes *up*, it reaches *toward*. Length implies a vector, a destination, a striving toward the One above.

A **wide** prayer just... *spreads*. It sprawls horizontally across the ground. It is *a lot* of prayer with **no vertical at all**. Picture flood water, not a fountain. A fountain is narrow and tall — it goes *up*. Flood water is wide and flat — it goes *everywhere and nowhere*, covering ground but never rising. The man in distress produces an *enormous quantity* of prayer — but Allah measures it by its **width**, because there is no height in it. It is vast in volume and zero in ascent.

And feel the sound one more time. **ʿArīḍ.** It opens with **ʿayn** — a sound made by opening the throat *wide* — and closes on **ḍ**, an emphatic letter that fills the whole mouth, spreading the tongue *broad* against the palate. Even the phonetics of the word are *wide*. You cannot say *ʿarīḍ* with a closed, constricted throat — the articulation physically refuses narrowness. The word insists on spread. And that is precisely what Allah is measuring: a prayer immense in volume, a prayer that blankets the ground — but carrying no upward vector whatsoever.

What this word reveals: **not all prayer is ascent. Volume without direction is the fingerprint of the foxhole.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Four: *Hādhā Lī* — the three words

Now I want to bring you to the middle verse, the one I told you at the beginning held the answer to everything. This is verse 50, and it sits between the two portraits like a confession pulled from the chest of the patient himself.

> **وَلَئِنْ أَذَقْنَـٰهُ رَحْمَةٗ مِّنَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِ ضَرَّآءَ مَسَّتْهُ لَيَقُولَنَّ هَـٰذَا لِى**
>
> *Wa la'in adhaqnāhu raḥmatan minnā min baʿdi ḍarrāʾa massathu la-yaqūlanna hādhā lī.*
>
> **"And if We let him taste mercy from Us after hardship has touched him, he will surely say: 'This belongs to me.'"**

Notice, first, the verb Allah uses for His own action: **أَذَقْنَـٰهُ** — *adhaqnāhu* — "We *let him taste*." The root **ذ-و-ق (dhawq)** is the root of *taste*. And tasting is among the most intimate, involuntary, *received* acts a person can perform. You do not *produce* a taste. You are given it. Al-Ṭabarī specifies the scene: the mercy that arrives is recovery from illness, provision restored after poverty — concrete, bodily, unmistakable gifts. And they are framed as something **Allah places on his tongue**: *raḥmatan minnā* — mercy *from Us*. Those two words are there specifically to make the Source unambiguous.

And what does he say?

**هَـٰذَا لِى** — *Hādhā lī.* Three words. Subject. Preposition. Possessive pronoun.

*This. Belongs. To. Me.*

Al-Muyassar renders the full inner logic without mercy: *"aṭānī hādhā li-annī mustahiqqun lahu"* — **"This was given to me because I deserve it."** The Arabic word is *istihqāq* — merit, entitlement, the sense that the gift confirms a prior status in the recipient. Not *"Allah was gracious"* but *"I am the kind of person good things happen to."* The mercy has arrived, and it has been immediately **re-authored**. The Giver has been edited out. What remains is a story about the recipient's merit.

[PAUSE]

This is the diagnosis at its sharpest edge. The man doesn't deny Allah's existence. He prays — we have established that he prays *enormously*. But the moment blessing comes, the narrative flips: the prayer "worked" not because Allah is merciful but because *I* am deserving. He uses the vocabulary of religion while running an entirely self-referential inner economy. The request went up; the gift came down; and what he concluded from that transaction is his own worthiness.

Al-Muyassar adds something theologically precise about the next clause. When the man says **"I do not think the Hour will come"** — *wa mā aẓunnu as-sāʿata qāʾimatan* — this is not incidental skepticism about the future. Al-Muyassar identifies it as *"inkārun minhu li-l-baʿth"* — **his denial of resurrection**. The word *baʿth* is theologically specific: the raising of the dead, the hinge on which all accountability turns. And notice when this denial is spoken: *while receiving mercy*. Blessing arrives, he claims credit for it, and in the same breath he dismisses the only framework that could hold him accountable for that claim. Easy mercy that generates no gratitude generates no fear either.

[PAUSE]

And then the final turn, the one that should stop us entirely:

> **وَلَئِن رُّجِعْتُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّىٓ إِنَّ لِى عِندَهُۥ لَلْحُسْنَىٰ**
>
> *Wa la'in rujiʿtu ilā rabbī inna lī ʿindahu la-l-ḥusnā.*
>
> **"And even if I am returned to my Lord, surely with Him I will have the very best."**

Even in the hypothetical afterlife he doesn't actually believe in — he is confident he will win there too. The word *al-ḥusnā* is the superlative: not just "good" but **the very best**. He has extended his entitlement logic past the grave itself. The same *istihqāq* that told him worldly blessing was his due now tells him paradise is his due as well. He is, in his own reckoning, simply the kind of person God favors — in this world and the next.

Al-Muyassar captures what has happened to his soul with a single verb: *yaṭghā* — **he transgresses**. The word carries *ṭughyān* — an overflow, a crossing of every limit, the soul that has swollen past its proper boundaries. The man who says *"this belongs to me"* and then says *"and paradise too"* has made himself the measure of all things. That is *ṭughyān*. That is the inner condition being diagnosed.

And notice: the divine response arrives immediately, with no rhetorical delay:

> **فَلَنُنَبِّئَنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ بِمَا عَمِلُوا۟ وَلَنُذِيقَنَّهُم مِّنْ عَذَابٍ غَلِيظٖ**
>
> *"But We will certainly inform those who disbelieved of what they did, and We will certainly make them taste a heavy punishment."*

The verb *adhaqnāhu* — "We let him *taste*" — appeared for mercy. Now *nudhīqannahum* — "We will make them *taste*" — appears for punishment. The same word. The same morphological form. The same divine subject. The Quran has built in a perfect symmetry: the one who tasted mercy and declared *hādhā lī* — *this belongs to me* — will taste punishment and discover, in a way no rhetoric can re-author, that **that** belongs to him too.

What the middle verse reveals: **self-attribution of blessing is not merely ingratitude. It is the origin point of every subsequent wrong belief — about the Hour, about the afterlife, about the soul's own standing before God.**

## PART 2: THE PORTRAIT IN FULL

Let us now stand back and look at the whole portrait at once.

The Quran has drawn three scenes, and their internal consistency is devastating.

**Scene one (verse 49):** Man prays inexhaustibly for worldly good. When evil merely *touches* him, he becomes *yaʾūsun qanūṭ* — despair incarnate, thinking badly of his Lord. His stamina for acquisition is infinite; his tolerance for deprivation is zero.

**Scene two (verse 50):** Mercy returns. He does not give thanks. He claims credit. He denies the resurrection. He extends his entitlement to paradise. Allah responds with the mirror image of His own verb: the same *taste* that was mercy will become punishment.

**Scene three (verse 51):** Favor arrives — he gives God the shoulder, literally and physically (*naʾā bi-jānibihi*, the body enacting the soul's posture). Evil touches him — he becomes the possessor of *wide* prayer, vast in volume, horizontal in direction.

Notice what is consistently **absent** across all three scenes: genuine relationship. In ease, he takes the gift and leaves the Giver. In hardship, he floods God with requests that sprawl but do not rise. In mercy after hardship, he concludes his own merit caused the turning. There is prayer in abundance. There is no *God* in any of it — not as a beloved, not as a sovereign, not as the One to whom gratitude is owed. The man has a relationship with blessing and punishment. He has no relationship with the Blesser.

Al-Jalālayn confirms the primary application: *"hādhā wa mā baʿdahu fī l-kāfirīn"* — **"this and what follows is about the disbelievers."** And yet the Quran placed it under the universal noun: *al-insān* — the human being. Not "the disbeliever." Not "the Makkan." *The human.* The species. Because the classical scholars were diagnosing a *capacity* — one every human being carries — not a uniquely Makkan pathology safely observable from outside the frame.

[PAUSE]

The question, then, is not: *Am I this person entirely?*

The question is: *In which of these three scenes do I recognize my own posture?*

## PART 3: WHERE DO I FIND MYSELF IN THE PORTRAIT?

Here is the hardest part of any portrait: the moment you stop reading about the subject and start reading about yourself.

Three questions, quietly.

**First question.** When blessing comes in your life — health recovered, a worry resolved, an opportunity that opened — what is your first internal narration? Is it *"Allah was generous"* or is it something like *"things worked out,"* *"I figured it out,"* *"I made good choices"* — the passive or self-referential voice that preserves the gift while editing out the Giver? You don't have to say *hādhā lī* out loud. The re-authoring happens much more quietly than that. Al-Muyassar's gloss is worth sitting with here: the man does not say *"God gave me this."* He says *"this came to me because I deserve it."* The difference between those two sentences is the entire distance between gratitude and *ṭughyān*.

**Second question.** When difficulty *touches* you — not overwhelms, just *touches* — how fast does despair arrive? How many hours or days before the inner light dims to *yaʾūs*? And in that despair, what is the shape of the thought about God? Is it mere sadness — or does it carry the charge of *qanūṭ*, the whisper that *"this says something about Him, something unflattering"?* Because that whisper, as al-Muyassar and al-Qurṭubī both name it — *sūʾu ẓ-ẓann bi-rabbihi*, thinking ill of one's Lord — is precisely what the Quran is most concerned about here. Not the sadness. The charge.

**Third question.** And this one is the most important. When you are in the foxhole — when evil has touched you and your prayer becomes wide, sprawling, enormous — *what are you asking for?* Are you asking Him to **return you to the ease** you had before? Or are you asking for **Him**? Is the destination of the prayer the gift you lost, or the Giver you have been neglecting? Because the prayer that comes from genuine need of God — not need of His gifts, but need of *Him* — that prayer has height. That prayer rises. It is not *ʿarīḍ*. It is *ṭawīl*. Wide in desperation, yes, enormous in volume — but *tall* in direction.

[PAUSE]

The Quran does not say to stop praying for worldly things. The prophets prayed for the world — for provision, for healing, for children, for protection. Yūsuf prayed for his father's sight to return. Zakariyyā prayed for a son. Ayyūb prayed for relief from affliction. The request itself is not the sickness. **The sickness is the anchor.**

A heart anchored to God can ask for everything and remain stable in receiving nothing, because the anchor holds regardless of what the tide brings or takes. A heart anchored to the gift shatters when the gift is withheld, because there is nothing else in the harbor. Its prayer was always, at its root, a transaction — and transactions collapse the moment the terms change.

What these three verses want to produce in us is not fewer prayers. They want **better anchored** ones. Prayers that would persist — and not as appetite, not as foxhole reflex, but as love — even if the answer were silence.

## CLOSING

There is a symmetry the Quran has placed across these three verses that I want to leave you sitting with.

In verse 49: evil **touches** him — *massahu sh-sharr* — and he is completely destroyed inside.

In verse 50: hardship **touches** him — *ḍarrāʾa massathu* — and when mercy comes after it, he credits himself.

In verse 51: evil **touches** him again — *massahu sh-sharr* — and he is immediately a possessor of wide prayer.

The word *massa* — *touch* — appears three times. Three scenes, three contacts with difficulty. And in every single scene, the response is a symptom — not because difficulty is wrong, not because prayer is wrong, not because wanting mercy is wrong, but because every response reveals where the anchor was.

The person we want to become is the one described elsewhere in the Quran, in Surah Hūd (11:9–11), in a passage almost structurally parallel to ours: those who *sabarū wa ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāt* — "were patient and did righteous works" — even after mercy was given and then stripped away. That patience in loss, and that gratitude in blessing, are only possible when the anchor is in something the loss itself cannot reach.

Allah is not asking us to be unmoved by hardship. The Quran does not produce people of stone. He is asking us to locate our hope *in Him* — so that when the touch of difficulty comes, the inner cry that rises is not *"I'm going to lose everything"* but *"I know Whose hand this is in."* And so that when blessing arrives — when *adhaqnāhu raḥmatan minnā*, when He places mercy on our tongue — the first word from us is not *hādhā lī*, not *this belongs to me*, but something quieter, something that names the right source: *hādhā min fadli rabbī*. This is from the grace of my Lord.

That is the prayer that is tall.

That is the one that rises.

That is the one that reaches.
