# The Gift With No Wound

### Surah Fussilat, Ayah 8

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ لَهُمْ أَجْرٌ غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ

*Inna alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum ajrun ghayru mamnūn.*

**"Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — for them is a reward never ending."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sentence You've Read a Hundred Times

There is a phrase that runs like a thread through the whole Quran. *Those who believe and do righteous deeds.* You have heard it so many times that your eye slides over it. It has become wallpaper. Familiar to the point of invisibility.

And because the front of the sentence is so familiar, we almost never stop at the back of it.

Watch what happens at the back of this one.

In many places where this phrase appears, Allah ends it the way you'd expect: *for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow.* A picture. Something you can see. But here, in Surah Fussilat, He doesn't paint a picture at all. He ends with two words that are not a picture — they are a **promise about the nature of the reward itself.**

أَجْرٌ غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ.

*A reward — ghayr mamnūn.*

Almost every translation you'll read renders it "a reward never ending," or "uninterrupted," or "without end." And that is true. But hold those translations loosely for the next hour, because the word Allah chose — **مَمْنُون** — is one of the most loaded words in the Arabic language. It comes from a root that means, at the same time, *to cut*, *to weaken*, *to count out*, and — most piercingly of all — *to give someone something and then make them feel small for having received it.*

One word. And by placing the negation **غَيْر** in front of it, Allah is not making one promise about your reward.

He is closing off **every single way** a gift can be ruined.

[PAUSE]

To feel why that matters, we have to stand where this verse stands.

### Rebuilding the World

Surah Fussilat is Meccan. The Prophet ﷺ is speaking to a people who have, in effect, put their fingers in their ears. A few verses earlier they say something astonishing: *"Our hearts are within coverings from that to which you invite us, and in our ears is deafness, and between us and you is a veil"* (41:5). They are not confused. They are *refusing*. They've drawn the curtain themselves.

And then, right before our verse, comes the warning. Listen to exactly what it names:

> *"And woe to those who associate others with Allah — **alladhīna lā yuʾtūna al-zakāh** — those who do not give the zakah, and who, of the Hereafter, are deniers."* (41:6-7)

Stop on that. Of all the things Allah could indict them for, He names a *refusal to give*. They are people who **hold their gift back**. Their hands close. Whatever they have, they keep, and the one who needs it goes without.

Now — verse 8. The very next breath. The turn.

> *"Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — for them is a reward ghayr mamnūn."*

Do you see the mirror?

On one side: human beings who **withhold** their giving. On the other side: a God who **gives** — and gives in a way that strips out every flaw that has ever been found in a gift.

The people of verse 7 won't even hand over the zakah they owe. The God of verse 8 hands over a reward that is never cut off, never shaved down, never counted against you, and never — *never* — thrown back in your face.

Here is the question I want you to carry into the next hour: **What does it tell us about Allah that, when He describes His reward, the thing He emphasizes is not how big it is — but how clean the giving is?**

Let's go find out.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: أَجْرٌ — The Word He Left Open On Purpose

Before we touch the famous word, look at the word right before it. أَجْرٌ. *A reward.* And notice the small mark on its end — the *tanwīn*, the double-vowel "-un" sound. *Ajr-un.*

We need a concept first, before this can land.

### Teaching the concept: definite and indefinite in Arabic

In Arabic, as in English, you can speak of *the* thing or *a* thing. *The* house — a specific one, known, bounded, you could point to it. *A* house — unspecified, one of many possible, open.

In English "a" can sound smaller, vaguer, less impressive. But in Arabic, leaving a noun **indefinite** — *nakirah* — can do the opposite. In certain positions it becomes what the grammarians call *tanwīn al-taʿẓīm*: the indefiniteness of **magnification**. The word is left open precisely because no specific, bounded thing could contain the meaning. You don't say "the reward." You say "a reward" — *a reward of such a kind that I will not pin it down for you, because to name it would be to shrink it.*

When an Arab wanted to say something was beyond reckoning, he would sometimes leave it indefinite and let your imagination fall into the gap.

### Now open the verse

Look at what is definite in this sentence and what is left open. Watch carefully, because the pattern is the whole point.

The deeds are **definite**: ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ — *al-ṣāliḥāt*, "the righteous deeds," with the *al-*. Known. A category you can list. Prayer. Charity. Kindness. Honesty. These are countable, nameable, *finite*. You could, in principle, write down every good deed you ever did. There would be a last line on the page.

And the reward? **Indefinite.** أَجْرٌ. *A reward.* Left wide open. Un-pinned. Un-bounded.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that asymmetry, because it is one of the quiet miracles of this verse.

Your deeds are *the* deeds — defined, limited, with edges. You will run out of them. There is a day your hand stops moving and your record closes. The good you can do is, by its nature, finite.

But what you receive for it is left grammatically **boundless**. *A* reward — of a kind unspecified, unmeasured, refusing to be written down to a last line.

Notice something staggering here. The exchange is not equal. It was never meant to be. A finite, definite, countable handful of deeds — answered with an indefinite, uncountable, open-ended reward. The grammar itself is telling you: **do not expect the math to balance. It won't. It can't. And that imbalance is the mercy.**

This is what the indefiniteness is *for*. Allah did not say "*the* reward you have earned," as if it were a wage matched precisely to your labour. He said "*a* reward" — and let the word open out into something your accounting could never produce on its own.

So before we even reach the famous word, the verse has already told us: what you are being given is out of all proportion to what you brought. **One clear statement: your deeds have a floor and a ceiling; your reward has neither.**

## Element Two: مَمْنُون — One Word, And Every Way a Gift Can Be Ruined

Now we arrive.

غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ.

Let's not rush it. Let me first give you the tool you need to see what is inside it.

### Teaching the concept: how one Arabic root holds many meanings

Arabic is built on roots — usually three letters — and from one root grows a whole family of words, all sharing a hidden thread of meaning. The root here is **م-ن-ن**, *m-n-n*.

Now here is the strange and beautiful thing about this particular root. Most roots have a thread you can name in one breath. This root has a thread that runs through *four* different human experiences — and the classical scholars, when they came to this verse, did not all land in the same place. Ibn ʿAbbas saw one thing. Mujāhid saw another. Al-Suddī saw a third. As-Sā'ib saw a fourth.

Here is the principle we hold onto when that happens: when the great commentators give different meanings for a single word, we very rarely have to choose. More often, each of them has walked around the same word and is describing a different face of it. Put their reports side by side and you don't get four competing answers. You get a **portrait** — one word photographed from four angles. Let's build that portrait.

### The word, in Arabic

غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ. *Ghayru mamnūn.* مَمْنُون is a passive participle — meaning something *has been done to it.* And غَيْر in front means *other than, free of, without.* So: a reward upon which the act of *mann* has **not** been performed. A reward free of *mann*.

But what is *mann*? Here come the four faces.

### Face One: مَقْطُوع — Never Cut

Ibn ʿAbbas — as both al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī report — said it means **غَيْرُ مَقْطُوعٍ**, *uncut, never severed.* And he traced it to a vivid root-usage: *mananta al-ḥabl* — "I cut the rope." Al-Qurṭubī even cites the old line of poetry where *manīn* describes dust so fine and broken it scatters — *al-ghubār al-munqaṭiʿ al-ḍaʿīf*, "the dust that has been cut off, weak, drifting apart."

So the first face: your reward is a rope that is never cut. Every pleasure you have ever known in this world came with a scissors hanging over it. The meal ends. The embrace ends. The youth ends. Even at the height of a joy, somewhere in you, you know the rope will be cut. This reward — the rope is never cut. There is no scissors over it.

And listen to the *sound* of the word for a moment. **Mamnūn.** The lips close on the *m*, and then the two *n*'s — *nūn* — hum on through the nose, a sound that doesn't stop, doesn't snap, just continues. A continuant. Compare it to a word like *qaṭ'* — *cut* — which ends in a hard glottal stop, a guillotine of a sound. The word for "cut off" *sounds* cut off. The word Allah uses for what is *never* cut off **hums on past where you expect it to end.** The phonetics carry the promise.

### Face Two: مَنْقُوص — Never Diminished

Mujāhid and others — reported by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī — read it as **غَيْرُ مَنْقُوصٍ**, *undiminished, never reduced.* And they connected it to another use of the root: *al-manūn* is a name for death and the passage of time, *because it diminishes a person's mannah* — their **strength**. Time *manns* you: it shaves you down, takes a little each day, until you are *manīn* — weak, worn, drifting like that fine dust.

So the second face: this reward is not subject to that erosion. Everything you own in this life is being quietly diminished right now — your body, your savings, your faculties, your very hours. *Al-manūn*, time itself, is filing it all down. This reward is the one thing that the file never touches. It does not lose value over time. It does not depreciate. It is the only asset in existence that is not, at this very moment, being eaten.

### Face Three: غَيْرُ مَحْسُوبٍ — Never Counted Out To You

Mujāhid is also reported — by al-Baghawī — to have read it as **غَيْرُ مَحْسُوبٍ**, *unreckoned, not counted out.*

Think about what it means to receive something *counted*. The giver measures it. Portions it. There is an exactness to it that is also a limit — *this much, and no more; I have done the sum.* A counted gift is a closed gift.

This reward is *not counted out to you.* It is not portioned with a measuring cup. It is poured. Elsewhere the Quran says of the patient, *innamā yuwaffā al-ṣābirūna ajrahum bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — "the patient will be given their reward **without account**" (39:10). Same idea. The hand that gives this does not stop to do the math.

### Face Four — The Deepest: غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ عَلَيْهِم — Given Without Being Thrown Back In Your Face

And now the face that, once you see it, you cannot un-see.

Al-Suddī and others read **مَمْنُون** from the *other* great meaning of م-ن-ن. Because this same root gives us *al-mann* — the act of **reminding someone of a favour you did them, of holding it over their head, of making them feel small for having received.**

You know this *mann*. Everyone knows this *mann*. It is the gift that comes with a hook in it.

So *ghayru mamnūn* on this reading means: a reward **given without reproach** — *ghayru mamnūnin ʿalayhim bih*, as al-Baghawī phrases it. A gift Allah will never bring up again. Never remind you of. Never use to make you feel small. Never attach a string to. He gives it, and then He gives you the *dignity* of having received it cleanly.

[PAUSE]

Hold all four faces in your hand at once now. *Ghayru mamnūn.* A reward that is:

- never **cut** off,
- never **diminished**,
- never **counted out** stingily,
- and never **thrown back in your face**.

One word. Four wounds that ruin human gifts. And غَيْر — *free of* — placed in front of all of them at once.

**The single clear statement of this word: Allah did not promise only that the reward is large. He promised that the giving is flawless.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### The bridge

The grammar has shown us something now. The indefinite أَجْرٌ told us the reward is out of all proportion to the deed. And مَمْنُون told us that the *giving itself* is cleansed of every flaw a gift can carry.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the wound those words are healing. Because Allah did not negate *mann* by accident. He negated it because He knows exactly what *mann* does to a human heart.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Gift That Comes With a Hook

Let me start where you already live.

Think of a time someone gave you something — real help, when you needed it. Money when you were short. A favour when you were stuck. A place to stay. And then think of what happened *afterward*, if the person was the wrong kind of giver.

The mention. *Remember when I helped you?* The little reminder, dropped into a conversation months later. The way the favour became a **lever** — something they could press whenever they wanted you to feel obligated, or quiet, or grateful-on-command. The gift turned into a *debt with no end date.* You received help, and somehow you came away feeling **smaller**.

That is *mann*. And it is one of the most common ways human generosity curdles. We give — and then we cannot resist holding it over the head of the one we gave to. We give, and then we charge interest in the currency of the other person's dignity.

The Quran takes this poison so seriously that it names it directly, in one of the most severe images in the entire book:

> *"O you who believe, do not nullify your charities with **mann** and harm — like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people..."* (2:264)

Read that slowly. Allah says the *mann* — the reminding, the holding-over-the-head — does not just *spoil* the charity. It **nullifies** it. *Lā tubṭilū.* It cancels it to zero. You gave the money. The poor man ate. And because you could not stop yourself from mentioning it, *your* reward was wiped out completely. The food was real. The reward is gone.

[PAUSE]

So now stand back and look at what Allah is doing in our verse.

The very flaw that He warns *us* about — the *mann* that can destroy an act of charity from the giver's side — is the very flaw He removes from *His own giving* to us.

He, who has the most right of anyone in existence to remind us of His favours — He created you, He sustains every cell of you, every breath you have ever taken was a loan from Him — *He* is the one who promises: when I reward you, there will be **no mann.** I will not bring it up. I will not make you feel small for having received it. I will not attach a hook.

Think about how rare this is. The greater the giver, the *more* right they have to remind you. A parent who raised you. A teacher who shaped you. A nation that protected you. The bigger the gift, the heavier the *mann* tends to fall. And Allah is the source of literally every good you have ever had — the one Being with infinite grounds to hold it over you.

And He is the one Being who never will.

[PAUSE]

There is one more turn here, and it is exquisite. The same root م-ن-ن that gives us the *bad mann* — the reproach — also gives us *al-Mannān*, one of the names by which the Prophet ﷺ called upon Allah: *Yā Dhā al-Jalāli wa-l-Ikrām, yā Ḥannānu yā Mannān.* The **Bestower**, the one who gives *mann* in its **original, pure sense** — *favour, grace, bounty poured out.*

So the root contains both: the human *mann* that wounds, and the divine *Mann* that only blesses. And in our verse, Allah uses the word to tell you exactly which one He is. *Ghayru mamnūn.* He is *al-Mannān* — the Giver — *without* being the one who reproaches. He kept the grace and removed the hook.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Two things, in opposite directions.

First, in how you *receive* from Allah. Some of us carry a low, background shame before God — a sense that every blessing comes with a bill, that we are always in arrears, that He is keeping a ledger to use against us. This verse says: no. When He gives you the final reward, He will not itemise your debts. He gives clean. Receive it clean.

And second — *harder* — in how you *give* to others. If the perfect Giver refuses to hold His gifts over your head, then the *mann* in your own giving is not a small flaw. It is you doing, to a fellow servant, the very thing your Lord refuses to do to you. The next time you help someone — and feel the urge rising to *mention* it, to bank it, to press it later — remember that Allah called this poison strong enough to *nullify charity entirely.* Give, and then let it go. Give the way you hope to be given to: *ghayr mamnūn.*

## Theme Two: The Reward That Doesn't Stop When You Do

Here is a fear most of us never say out loud.

We are afraid that we are only worth what we can produce.

It lives under the surface, but you can feel it surface at certain moments. When an illness lays you flat and you cannot pray the way you used to. When age takes your strength and your prayers become short, sitting, slow. When depression empties you out and even *dhikr* feels like lifting stone. In those moments a quiet, cruel voice says: *you've stopped earning. The account is closing. Whatever you were worth, you're worth less now.*

We feel this because the whole world we live in runs on it. Your salary stops when you stop working. The applause stops when the performance stops. Value, everywhere we look, is tied to *output.* Stop producing, and the rewards dry up. The rope gets cut.

Now bring that fear to our verse, and to one of the most tender narrations the commentators preserved about it.

Al-Baghawī reports — on the authority of al-Suddī — that this verse, *ajrun ghayru mamnūn*, was revealed **about the sick, the chronically ill, and the very old** — *al-marḍā wa-l-zaman[ā] wa-l-haram[ā].* And here is what he says it means: when they become **unable** to do the acts of worship they once did — when illness or age or incapacity takes the *doing* away — Allah writes for them the reward **as the best of what they used to do while they were able.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land.

The rope does not get cut when your strength does.

The man who prayed every night, and now cannot stand — the reward keeps being written *as if he still stood.* The woman who fasted every year, and now is too frail — the reward keeps flowing *as the best of what she did.* The output stopped. **The reward did not.** This is *ghayru maqṭūʿ* — never cut — given the most intimate possible meaning: it is not cut *even when you are.*

This is the precise opposite of every other system you have ever lived inside. In every worldly economy, the reward is chained to the production: no work, no wage. Here, the reward outlives the production entirely. Allah is not paying you for *output*. He saw the *direction of your heart* when you were able — and He honours that direction even after your hands fall still.

Now hold this next to the four faces of the word, and watch them all converge on this one mercy:

- *Never cut* — so it doesn't stop when your ability stops.
- *Never diminished* — so it doesn't shrink as you weaken; *al-manūn*, time, files down everything you own except this.
- *Never counted out* — so it isn't rationed to match your reduced "performance."
- *Never reproached* — so He will never say to the broken, the sick, the old: *but look how little you did at the end.*

Do you see? Every face of *mamnūn* was, all along, pointing at the person who is afraid they have stopped being worth anything. The grammar was building a shelter, and this is who it was built for.

[PAUSE]

And there is a wider door here, beyond illness and age. Every one of us, eventually, *stops.* Death is the final cessation of output. The hand stops moving entirely; the record closes. And the deepest promise of *ghayru mamnūn* is that on the *other* side of that final stop, the reward is **still flowing.** *Lā maqṭūʿatin wa-lā mamnūʿah* — "neither ending nor forbidden," as the Quran says of the fruit of the Garden elsewhere (56:33). The Garden's defining feature, again and again, is that it does not *run out.* The single most painful fact about every earthly pleasure — *that it ends* — is the single fact that does not apply there.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** When you find yourself unable — too sick, too tired, too crushed to worship the way you wish you could — do not let the voice tell you the account is closing. Do two things. First, *intend.* Hold, even in your weakness, the sincere wish: *if I were able, I would.* The narration is built on that direction of the heart. And second, while you still *have* strength, *plant.* Pray the night prayer, give the charity, build the habit — not only for today's reward, but because there may come a day when you cannot, and Allah writes for the one who is now unable **the best of what they did when they could.** What you do today in strength becomes the standard by which you are rewarded tomorrow in weakness. Plant now. The harvest does not stop when you do.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Two Roads Meeting

We came in through two doors, and they open into the same room.

**Through the grammar**, we found that your deeds are definite, bounded, countable — *al-ṣāliḥāt* — but your reward was left *indefinite*, un-pinned, *ajrun*, refusing to be measured. The exchange was never meant to balance. A finite handful of deeds, answered with an unbounded gift.

**Through the single word مَمْنُون**, we found that Allah did not merely promise a large reward — He cleansed the *giving* of it. Never cut. Never diminished. Never counted out. Never thrown back in your face.

Put the two together and here is the room they open into: **Allah does not reward like an employer paying a wage. He gives like the only perfect Giver in existence.** A wage is matched to labour, counted, finite, and it stops when the work stops. This is none of those things. It is out of proportion to the deed, free of every flaw, and it keeps flowing after your hands fall still.

And remember where the verse sits. One breath earlier: *woe to those who do not give the zakah* — the closed hand, the withheld gift. And then, immediately: a God whose gift has every closed-handed flaw removed from it. The structure itself is the argument. **They would not give cleanly. He gives nothing else.**

## The Architecture of the Pivot

Look once more at how verses 7 and 8 are built as a mirror:

- **41:7** — *Those who do not give* (la yuʾtūn) → withholding → and they **deny** the reward to come.
- **41:8** — *Those who believe and do* (āmanū wa-ʿamilū) → giving of the self → and they **receive** a reward that nothing can spoil.

The deniers refused both to *give* and to *believe in receiving.* The believers do both — and the verse answers their giving with the cleanest gift in existence. The poison Allah warns against in *us* (the *mann* of 2:264) is the very poison absent from His reward to us. The whole theology of the passage is folded into the contrast between a hand that closes and a Hand that gives *ghayr mamnūn.*

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I given something to someone — and then *kept the receipt*, ready to remind them? What would it take to tear up that receipt the way Allah tears up His?
2. Do I carry a quiet shame before Allah, as if every blessing came with a bill He will one day present? What changes if His giving is truly *ghayru mamnūn* — clean, with no ledger held against me?
3. When I am unable to worship as I wish — sick, exhausted, low — do I believe the account is closing? Or do I believe the rope is *uncut*?
4. What am I planting *now*, in my strength, that I would want written for me *later*, in my weakness?
5. If the perfect Giver refuses to make me feel small for receiving — what right do I have to make anyone feel small for what I gave them?

## One-Sentence Distillation

For the one who believes and does good, Allah promises not just a great reward but a flawless gift — never cut off, never worn down, never rationed, and never held over your head — given the way only the perfect Giver gives.

## The Closing Invitation

You have read *those who believe and do righteous deeds* a hundred times and never stopped at the end of the sentence. Stop there now. The end is not a picture of a garden. It is a promise about *how God gives* — and it is the answer to a fear you may not have known you carried: that you are only worth what you can produce, and that even your reward will, like everything else, eventually be cut, shrunk, counted against you, or used to make you feel small.

Let the verse close every one of those doors. And then let it ask one thing of you in return: **give to others the way you have just been promised you'll be given to.** *Ghayr mamnūn.* Clean. With no hook. With no receipt kept. That is how the verse moves from something you *receive* to something you *become*.

## A Du'a From These Words

*O Allah, yā Mannān — Giver of grace without reproach — write for us a reward that is never cut, never diminished, never counted against us, and never held over our heads. When our strength fails and our hands fall still, keep writing for us the best of what we did when we were able. And teach us to give to Your servants the way You have promised to give to us: cleanly, with no wound in the gift.* Āmīn.
