# Who Is Stronger Than Us?

### Surah Fussilat, Ayah 15

> فَأَمَّا عَادٌ فَٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ بِغَيْرِ ٱلْحَقِّ وَقَالُوا۟ مَنْ أَشَدُّ مِنَّا قُوَّةً ۖ أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا۟ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَهُمْ هُوَ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُمْ قُوَّةً ۖ وَكَانُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِنَا يَجْحَدُونَ

*Fa-ammā ʿĀdun fa-stakbarū fī-l-arḍi bi-ghayri-l-ḥaqqi wa-qālū man ashaddu minnā quwwatan. Awa-lam yaraw anna-llāha-lladhī khalaqahum huwa ashaddu minhum quwwatan. Wa-kānū bi-āyātinā yajḥadūn.*

> "Then as for ʿĀd, they grew arrogant upon the earth without right and said, 'Who is greater than us in strength?' Did they not see that Allah, the One who created them — He is greater than them in strength? And they were, of Our signs, knowingly denying."

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Civilization Built on a Single Sentence

Let me take you somewhere first. Before we touch a single word of this ayah, I want you to stand where ʿĀd stood.

Picture a people who were, by every account the tradition preserves, *enormous*. Al-Qurtubi reports — citing a narration from Ibn ʿAbbas — that the tallest among them reached toward a hundred cubits and the shortest sixty. Take that as the memory the early community carried, not a measuring tape: these were people whose very bodies were a statement. Al-Jalalayn notes that a single man among them could tear a great boulder out of a mountainside and set it down wherever he pleased. They raised pillared cities. They were the superpower of their age — and they knew it.

Now into this civilization of giants walks a man with no army, no fortress, no boulder in his hands. Hud, peace be upon him. One of their own. And he tells them something simple and unbearable: *there is One above you. Turn to Him before a day comes that you cannot push away.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the moment the Quran freezes for us. When Hud warns them of a punishment, they do not laugh. They do not argue theology. They reach for the one thing they have always trusted — their power — and they say six words that summarize an entire worldview:

**"Who is stronger than us?"**

*Man ashaddu minnā quwwatan.*

That sentence is the most honest thing they ever said. It is their creed. It is the load-bearing wall of their whole house. And it is exactly the wall Allah is about to lean on.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows: **A civilization can be destroyed by many things — a flood, a wind, a famine. But can a civilization be destroyed by a single unexamined sentence?** Because that is what this ayah is telling us happened. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Means "To Make Yourself Big"

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we open the word, because without it the verb will pass you by as just "they were arrogant."

Arabic verbs come in patterns — call them *forms* — and each form bends the basic meaning of a root in a specific direction. The root here is **ك-ب-ر** (*k-b-r*), and it simply means *bigness, greatness*. From it you get *kabīr* — great. You get *Allāhu akbar* — Allah is greater. That root, in its pure state, belongs to Allah. *Al-Kabīr*, The Great, is His name. *Al-Mutakabbir*, The Supremely Great, is His name — and it is the *only* place in all of existence where greatness is simply, truthfully *true*.

Now, the Quran does not say of ʿĀd that they *were* great — *kaburū*. It says **فَٱسْتَكْبَرُوا** — *fa-stakbarū*. And the corpus confirms this precisely: this is **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern.

Let me teach you Form X with something from your own life. Form X very often carries the sense of *seeking, claiming, or deeming* something for yourself. *Istaghfara* — to *seek* forgiveness. *Istaʾdhana* — to *ask* permission for yourself. So when you take the root *bigness* and pour it into this mold — *istakbara* — you do not get a man who *is* big.

You get a man who **arrogates bigness to himself.** Who *claims* a greatness. Who reaches for a size that was never his and wears it.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the difference. *Kabīr* is a description of reality. *Istakbara* is a *performance*. It is the verb of someone standing on a box and insisting on his own height. The very grammar of the word tells you the greatness is counterfeit before the ayah ever says so out loud — because real greatness doesn't need the *istafʿala* mold. The mountain doesn't *make itself* tall. It simply is. Only the small man needs the verb that means *to claim*.

And then Allah says it out loud anyway, in case you missed the grammar: **بِغَيْرِ ٱلْحَقِّ** — *bi-ghayri-l-ḥaqq* — "without the right," "without the truth."

Notice what that little phrase quietly admits. It implies there is such a thing as bigness *with* right. There is a greatness grounded in *al-Ḥaqq* — and *al-Ḥaqq*, The Truth, The Real, is again a name of Allah. So the phrase is drawing a line straight through the universe: greatness tethered to its Source is real; greatness cut loose from its Source is theft. Al-Qurtubi reads their arrogance as being directed *over the servants of Allah* — over Hud and those who believed with him. So the bigness was untethered in two directions at once: detached from the Truth above them, and pressed down upon the people beneath them. **Arrogance always travels in those two directions together.** The moment you stop looking up, you start looking down on others.

What does this one verb reveal? That ʿĀd's sin was not strength. Strength is a gift. Their sin was a grammatical one made spiritual: **they conjugated themselves into a greatness that was only ever on loan.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word They Said, Returned to Them Unbroken

Now watch what Allah does with their own sentence. This is, to me, one of the quietest and most devastating moves in the Quran, and you can only see it if you watch the words sit side by side.

They said: **مَنْ أَشَدُّ مِنَّا قُوَّةً** — *man ashaddu minnā quwwatan* — "Who is stronger than us in strength?"

Two things in that sentence. *Ashaddu* — "more intense, more severe, stronger" — from the root **ش-د-د**, the root of *shidda*, of things being hard, severe, taut. And **قُوَّةً** — *quwwatan* — "in strength," from *q-w-y*.

That last word, *quwwatan*, is in a special grammatical case. Grammarians call it **تمييز** — *tamyīz*, the accusative of specification. Let me make it plain. When you say in English "she is greater *in kindness*" — that phrase "in kindness" narrows down *which* greatness you mean. *Tamyīz* is Arabic's way of taking a vague claim and pinning it to one dimension. "Stronger" — stronger *how?* — *quwwatan* — in raw force.

And here is what that reveals about ʿĀd before Allah ever answers them. **They could only conceive of superiority in one currency.** Stronger *in strength*. Not wiser, not truer, not more grateful, not more enduring. The *tamyīz* is a window into the prison of their imagination. When power is the only thing you've ever measured by, "who is stronger" *is* the only question worth asking. They had narrowed the entire question of their existence down to a single axis — and then declared themselves the top of it.

[PAUSE]

Now watch the response. Allah does not introduce a new vocabulary. He does not crush their sentence with a bigger one. **He takes their exact words and hands them back:**

> أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا۟ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَهُمْ هُوَ **أَشَدُّ** مِنْهُمْ **قُوَّةً**

> "Did they not see that Allah — the One who created them — *He* is **stronger** than them **in strength**?"

The same *ashaddu*. The same *quwwatan*. The very *tamyīz* they boasted in is the *tamyīz* turned back on them. It is as if Allah says: *you want to play on the axis of strength? Fine. Let us stay on your axis. You chose the ground; we will meet you on it — and you still lose.*

But the genius is not in the repetition. It is in the **five words He slips into the middle.**

Between their boast and its reversal sits the hinge of the entire ayah: **ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَهُمْ** — *alladhī khalaqahum* — "the One who created them."

[PAUSE]

Feel what that does. Their argument was: *we have the most strength.* Allah's answer is not even "I have more." His answer is: *I am the One who manufactured the strength you are bragging with.* The root **خ-ل-ق** — *khalaqa* — to create, to originate. The strength in their arms, the height of their bodies, the force that could lift a boulder — every ounce of it was *issued*. It came off His workbench.

And once you see that word, the whole boast doesn't get defeated — **it gets dissolved.** Because the question "who is stronger than us?" was never even coherent. It is like a sculpture asking, "who is more skilled than the hand that carved me?" It is like a wave asking, "what is wetter than me?" while sitting in the ocean that made it. The instant you remember *alladhī khalaqahum*, the boast collapses not from the outside but from within. You cannot be stronger than the Source of your strength. It is not a hard truth to prove. It is a truth so simple a child sees it — which is exactly why the verse asks, *awa-lam yaraw* — **"did they not** ***see*** **it?"**

One word, *khalaqahum*, placed in the dead center of their own sentence, and an entire civilization's creed falls silently apart.

That is the second thing this ayah reveals: **the answer to human arrogance is almost never new information. It is the information you already had, set back down in front of you.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Difference Between Not Knowing and Refusing to Know

Which brings us to the last word, and the heaviest. The ayah ends:

> وَكَانُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِنَا يَجْحَدُونَ

> "And they were, of Our signs, **denying**."

The verb is **يَجْحَدُونَ** — *yajḥadūn*, from the root **ج-ح-د** (*j-ḥ-d*). And I need you to feel this word before I define it, because Arabic hides meaning in sound. Say it. *Yaj-ḥa-dūn.* It catches in the back of the throat — the *jīm*, then the deep guttural *ḥāʾ*, then the hard *dāl*. It is not a smooth word. It is a word that resists being said cleanly, the way a denial resists being said cleanly. The mouth has to clench to produce it.

Now the meaning, because this is the word the whole ayah has been building toward. In Arabic there are two very different ways to reject a truth.

There is **جهل** — *jahl* — ignorance. You reject something because you *don't know*. The light never reached you. This is the rejection of the blind man who bumps the wall — there is no guilt in it; he simply could not see.

And there is **جحد** — *juḥūd* — and this is something else entirely. *Juḥūd* is to deny what you **know to be true.** The lexicographers are clear: *jaḥada* is not the denial of ignorance; it is the denial of *recognition*. It is the man who sees the wall perfectly well and insists, out loud, that there is no wall. Allah uses this exact word elsewhere of those who rejected signs **وَٱسْتَيْقَنَتْهَآ أَنفُسُهُمْ** — "while their own souls were *certain* of them" (27:14).

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why the ayah asked *awa-lam yaraw* — "did they not *see*?" It is not a real question. It is an indictment. They *did* see. The strength was obviously made — they could feel it leave them when they were tired, return when they ate, fade when they aged. The evidence of a Lender was woven into every muscle. So when the ayah ends with *yajḥadūn*, it is telling us: **their unbelief was never a failure of evidence. It was a refusal of evidence they had already received.**

And note the grammar one last time. The corpus marks *yajḥadūn* as the **imperfect** tense, wrapped inside **كَانُوا** — *kānū*, "they were." *Kānū... yajḥadūn.* That construction — "were" plus the ongoing verb — does not describe a single act of denial. It describes a **settled habit**. A practice. They didn't deny once in a moment of panic. They *kept* denying, day after day, as a way of life. The denial became the climate they breathed.

What does this final word reveal? **The most dangerous unbelief in the world is not the unbelief of the person who hasn't heard. It is the unbelief of the person who heard, understood, and decided to keep arguing anyway.**

---

### The Bridge

So look at what the three words have shown us, standing together. *Istakbarū* — they inflated themselves into a borrowed greatness. *Khalaqahum* — and the Source of every ounce they boasted with was standing right there, named in the middle of their own sentence. *Yajḥadūn* — and they knew it, and denied it anyway, as a habit, until it killed them.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about three words — but about us. Because ʿĀd are not a fossil. ʿĀd is a *posture*. And it is available to every single one of us this afternoon.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Strength That Forgot It Was Borrowed

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a sentence the modern world teaches us to be proud of. We call it "self-made." We admire the self-made millionaire, the self-made athlete, the self-taught genius. The phrase is everywhere, and it sounds like a virtue. But sit with it for one honest second. *Self-made.* As if you made yourself. As if the intelligence you think with was something you earned before you existed. As if the lungs that have pulled air for you, free of charge, three hundred million times, sent you an invoice you paid.

[PAUSE]

ʿĀd were the original self-made men. And their tragedy is not that they were strong. **Strength is a gift from Allah — He says so in this very ayah by claiming to be its Maker.** Their tragedy is that somewhere along the way, *strength stopped being something they had and became something they were.* It moved from their hands into their identity. And the moment a gift becomes your identity, you can no longer bear to remember it was given — because to remember the Giver is to admit you could be un-given.

This is the quiet mechanism of all arrogance, and I want you to watch it work in your own life, not in a dead tribe's. Take whatever your *quwwah* is. For ʿĀd it was muscle. For you it might be your intelligence — the way you can always win the argument. It might be your money, your degree, your beauty, your discipline, your reputation for being the reliable one, the strong one, the one who doesn't need anyone. Whatever it is — that is your boulder. That is the thing you can lift that others can't.

Now ask the ʿĀd question about it, out loud: *"Who is stronger than me in this?"*

[PAUSE]

And here is what the ayah does to that question. It doesn't tell you to be humble. It doesn't scold you. It just gently inserts five words into the middle of your boast: *alladhī khalaqahum* — *the One who made this in you.* The intelligence you're proud of — who wired the brain? The discipline — who gave you a nervous system capable of habit? The money — who arranged the thousand accidents of birth, body, era, and opportunity that let you earn it, none of which you selected? Every axis on which you might say "who is stronger than me" terminates, if you follow it back far enough, in a Hand that issued the strength.

The believer and the arrogant person are not divided by *how much* strength they have. Sometimes the believer has more. They are divided by a single act of memory: **the believer remembers the loan.** That's it. That's the whole difference. *Lā ḥawla wa lā quwwata illā billāh* — "there is no strength" — *quwwah*, the very word — "except by Allah." The Muslim is taught to say it constantly, almost reflexively, and now you see why. It is the exact antidote to *man ashaddu minnā quwwatan*. It is the sentence ʿĀd should have said and didn't.

What changes tomorrow? This: the next time you feel that flush of self-sufficiency — that "I've got this, I don't need anyone, look what *I* built" — treat it not as pride but as a *symptom*. A symptom that you've stopped remembering the loan. And do the one thing ʿĀd never did. Trace the strength back one step. Just one. *Who gave me this?* You will find you cannot hold the boast and the answer in your chest at the same time. One of them has to leave.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Truth You Argue Against at Three in the Morning

Now the harder theme. Because someone is listening and thinking, *I'm not arrogant about my strength. That's not my problem.* Good. Then let me bring you to the last word — *yajḥadūn* — because this one comes for all of us.

Here is something psychologists have a clean name for: *motivated reasoning.* It is the well-documented human tendency to reason our way *toward* the conclusion we already want, and *away* from the one we fear — and to do it so smoothly that it feels like honest thinking. We do not usually lie to ourselves with a single big lie. We do it with a thousand small, sophisticated arguments, each one carrying us one inch further from a truth we already, somewhere, know.

That is *juḥūd*. The Quran named it fourteen centuries before the studies. *Juḥūd* is not the rejection of a truth you've never met. **It is the active, ongoing argument against a truth you've already shaken hands with.**

[PAUSE]

And here is why this is the more dangerous diagnosis. Ignorance has a cure — *information*. You can teach the ignorant. You can show the blind man the wall and he will thank you. But what do you do with a man who sees the wall, agrees it's a wall, and keeps walking into it on principle? Information can't help him. He has all the information. His problem is not in his eyes. It is in his will.

Let me make this uncomfortably personal, because the ayah means it personally. There is a thing you know about your own life right now. A relationship you know is wrong. A habit you know is hollowing you out. A prayer you know you've been making excuses about. A debt — to a person or to Allah — you know you owe. You know it the way ʿĀd knew their strength was given: not as a theory, but as a quiet certainty that surfaces in the honest hours — at three in the morning, in the silence after the lights go off, in the half-second before you reach for the distraction.

And what do most of us do with that certainty? We do exactly what ʿĀd did. We *yajḥad* it. We build the case against it. We get sophisticated. *It's not that bad. Everyone does it. I'll deal with it later. Who's to say, really.* We argue, fluently and continuously — *kānū... yajḥadūn*, as a settled habit — against a thing we have already, in the quiet, admitted is true.

[PAUSE]

Here is the mercy hidden in the terror of this word. If your problem were *jahl* — ignorance — you'd be stuck, because you can't choose to know what you don't know. But your problem, ʿĀd's problem, is *juḥūd*. And *juḥūd* means **the truth is already inside you.** You are not missing it. You are *suppressing* it. Which means the work is not to go out and acquire some new knowledge. The work is to **stop arguing.** To go quiet for one moment and let the thing you already know come up for air without immediately drowning it in justifications.

The cure for ignorance is a teacher. The cure for *juḥūd* is honesty. And honesty is always available, right now, for free. ʿĀd had every resource a civilization could want — except the willingness to stop, for one second, and admit the obvious. The roaring wind that took them, which comes in the very next passage, was just the outside finally matching the inside: a force they could not argue with, sent to a people who argued with everything.

What changes tomorrow? Find your *juḥūd*. The one truth you've been out-arguing. And instead of marshaling the case against it one more time, try the most radical act available to a human being: **agree with what you already know.** Just say it, once, without the lawyer in your head objecting. That single un-argued admission is the exact opposite of everything ʿĀd was. It is *raʾaw* — they *saw* — actually completed. It is the verse running in reverse.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Now stand back and see how the linguistic discoveries and the human themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

The grammar told us *istakbarū* was a *performance* — a greatness claimed, not possessed, because the *istafʿala* mold is the mold of *seeking what isn't yours*. The human theme made that flesh: the self-made man, the gift that curdled into identity, the strength that forgot it was a loan.

The grammar showed us *khalaqahum* — "the One who created them" — slipped into the dead center of their boast, dissolving it from within. The human theme made that a practice you can do today: trace your strength back one step and watch the boast lose its breath.

And the grammar gave us *yajḥadūn* — knowing denial, the word that catches in the throat, wrapped in *kānū* to mark it as a way of life. The human theme named it *motivated reasoning* and put it in your own three-in-the-morning silence.

Three words. One disease. **A strength forgotten as a gift, a Giver argued out of the picture, a truth known and denied until the wind came.** That is ʿĀd. And the ayah preserved them not as history but as a mirror.

### The Architecture

And see now what was hiding in the ayah's shape the whole time. It is built as a perfect reversal, folded around a center:

- *They grew arrogant* — **stronger** (*ashaddu*) — **in strength** (*quwwatan*) —
- **— the One who created them —**
- — **stronger** (*ashaddu*) — **in strength** (*quwwatan*) — *He.*

Their boast goes out, and it comes back inverted, and the fold in the middle — the axis the whole thing turns on — is *alladhī khalaqahum*, "the One who created them." The architecture *is* the argument. The verse physically wraps their words around the Creator, the way reality itself wraps every created strength around the One who issued it. You cannot escape the center. Neither could they.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is *my* boulder — the strength I can lift that others can't — and when did it quietly stop being something I *have* and start being something I *am*?
2. If I traced that strength back, honestly, one step at a time — where would the trail end?
3. What is the one truth I already know but have been out-arguing? Not ignorance — *juḥūd*. What am I suppressing, not lacking?
4. *Lā ḥawla wa lā quwwata illā billāh* — how many times have I said it without once meaning it the way ʿĀd should have?
5. If the outside of my life were suddenly made to match the inside — like the wind that matched ʿĀd — what would change?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The strength you boast with was made by Someone — and the Maker of your strength is, by definition, stronger than you; to forget this is not ignorance but a denial you are choosing.**

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to be weak. It never once tells ʿĀd their strength was bad. It is asking you to remember a single word — *khalaqa*, "He made it" — and to let that word sit in the center of every boast you're tempted to make, the way Allah set it in the center of theirs. Strength remembered as a gift becomes gratitude. Strength forgotten becomes *istikbār*. The same muscle. The difference is one act of memory. You are invited, today, simply to remember the loan — and to stop arguing with the truth you already hold.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You who created the very strength I am tempted to boast in — let me never say in my heart "who is stronger than me," but always "there is no strength except through You." Pull up from within me the truths I have been arguing against, and give me the honesty to agree with what I already know, before the day comes when arguing is no longer possible. Āmīn.*
