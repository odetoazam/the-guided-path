# The Trench Beneath Them: When the Burned Become the Victors

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse three ways.

In Arabic:

> [ayah:85:11] إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ لَهُمْ جَنَّـٰتٌ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْكَبِيرُ

In transliteration:

> *Inna alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum jannātun tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār. Dhālika al-fawzu al-kabīr.*

And in plain English:

> "Indeed, those who believed and did righteous deeds — for them are Gardens beneath which rivers flow. That is the great success."

Now — if you have read the Qur'an even a little, you have seen a sentence almost exactly like this before. *Those who believed and did righteous deeds, for them are gardens beneath which rivers flow.* It is one of the most repeated promises in the whole Book. Your eye might slide right over it. Another reassurance. We know this one.

I want to ask you to do the opposite today. I want you to refuse to let your eye slide. Because of **where** this sentence is standing.

### Rebuild the world

Walk back just a few lines in this same surah.

Surah Al-Buruj opens enormous. Allah swears an oath by the sky — *wa al-samāʾi dhāti al-burūj* — by the heaven holding its great constellations, its towering formations. The opening feels cosmic, vast, like the camera is pulled all the way back to the edge of the universe.

And then the camera drops. Down through the sky, down through the air, down to a single patch of ground where a fire is burning in a trench dug into the earth.

*Qutila aṣḥābu al-ukhdūd.* Cursed were the people of the ditch. *Al-nāri dhāti al-waqūd* — the fire full of fuel. *Idh hum ʿalayhā quʿūd* — when they sat around it, perched on its edges. *Wa hum ʿalā mā yafʿalūna bi-l-muʾminīna shuhūd* — and they were witnesses to what they were doing to the believers.

Sit with that scene for a moment, because the verse we are studying makes no sense unless you have stood at the lip of that trench.

There is a community of people who believed in their Lord. And a tyrant — the surah does not even bother to name him, his name is not worth recording — decided that this faith would not be permitted. So a long trench was dug. A fire was built in it, fed with fuel until it roared. And the believers were brought to the edge and given one offer: renounce your Lord, or go into the fire.

And the verse tells us the persecutors **sat down**. *Quʿūd.* They pulled up seats at the edge of the burning trench. They made it an event. They watched. *Shuhūd* — they witnessed it, the way you witness entertainment. Men, women, children — thrown into fire — while the powerful sat at the rim and watched the smoke rise.

[PAUSE]

That is the world. That is the room you are standing in when this verse arrives.

Now look at what the world saw that day. The world saw believers **lose**. Completely. Publicly. There is no rescue in this story. The fire is not put out. No army comes over the hill. The believers are not saved at the last second. They burn. The tyrant goes home. The fire dies down to ash. By every measure a human eye can take — power, survival, outcome, who walked away and who did not — the believers were destroyed and the tyrant won.

### The Hook

And then Allah says one word that overturns the entire scene.

He does not say "those people were patient." He does not say "those people went to Paradise" — not yet, not in this word. He reaches, at the very end of the sentence, for a word that means **winning**. Triumph. The thing the tyrant thought *he* had.

*Dhālika al-fawzu al-kabīr.* **That** — the ash, the trench, the ones the world watched burn — *that* is the great victory.

How does the One who watched the whole thing — who saw the fire and the seated spectators and the smoke — how does He look at the people in the ash and call them not the *survivors*, not the *rewarded*, but the **victors**?

That is what we are going to sit with today. Hold the trench in your mind. We are going to come back to it. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Two kinds of time in one sentence — what is finished, and what never finishes

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we open the word, because once you see it you will never read this verse the same way.

Arabic verbs, very broadly, come in two great families. There is the **past / completed** form — grammarians call it the perfect — and it describes an action that is *done, sealed, over*. And there is the **ongoing / unfolding** form — the imperfect — which describes an action that is *happening, continuing, never quite closing*.

An everyday example. In English we have "I built a house" and "water is flowing." The first is finished — the house stands, the building is over. The second has no end built into it — the water is flowing and flowing and flowing. One is a closed box. The other is an open stream.

Hold that distinction. Now open the verse.

> ٱلَّذِينَ **ءَامَنُوا۟** وَ**عَمِلُوا۟** ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ

*alladhīna **āmanū** wa-**ʿamilū** al-ṣāliḥāt* — those who **believed** and **did** righteous deeds.

Both of those verbs — *āmanū* (believed) and *ʿamilū* (did) — are in the **completed** form. Done. Sealed. Over.

Now keep reading, just three words later:

> لَهُمْ جَنَّـٰتٌ **تَجْرِى** مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ

*lahum jannātun **tajrī** min taḥtihā al-anhār* — for them are gardens beneath which the rivers **flow**.

*Tajrī* — flow — is the **ongoing** form. The never-closing stream.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has done in a single breath. He has placed the believers' **deeds** in the finished tense and the believers' **reward** in the unfinished tense.

Let's sit with this, because it is doing something tender.

The believers at the trench — their believing is *over*. Their good deeds are *done*. The moment they were thrown into the fire, the ledger of their action closed. *Āmanū. ʿAmilū.* Past. Finished. There is nothing more they can add now. That is the nature of a human life: it is a completed sentence, and at some point the pen lifts off the page.

But the **river** does not close. *Tajrī.* It flows. It is flowing right now and it will be flowing — present tense, open-ended, with no full stop built into the grammar itself.

Here is what is remarkable. Their deed was a **finite** thing — a believing heart, a span of righteous acts, all of it bounded by a lifetime, much of it bounded by the brief horror at the edge of a fire. And in exchange Allah gives them something whose very grammar **refuses to end**.

Notice the trade. You give Allah a closed box — a finished life of faith — and He gives you back an open stream. You hand over the finite, and He hands back the unending. The deeds had a last day. The river has no last day in its tense.

This is not decoration. This is the entire logic of the relationship between you and your Lord, encoded in the difference between two verb forms. **What you do for Him is small and ends. What He does for you in return does not end.** The Arabic will not even let the river take a completed shape. It just keeps flowing.

And think of who is hearing this. People whose lives were violently *cut short* — finished before they were ready, snuffed in a trench. To them, of all people, Allah gives a reward described in the one tense that **cannot be cut short**. Their time was stolen. He answers with something time cannot touch.

One clear thing this single grammatical choice reveals: **your account closes, but His generosity does not.** You will run out of days to do good. He will never run out of days to reward it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Min taḥtihā* — "from beneath it" — and the trench that became a garden

Now I want to take you to a phrase that, again, you have read a hundred times without stopping: *min taḥtihā al-anhār* — the rivers flow **from beneath it.**

Let me first just point at the word. *Taḥt.* It means **underneath, below.** The rivers run *under* the gardens, *beneath* the feet of the people of Paradise.

A simple question, the kind we are trained not to ask because the phrase is so familiar: why "beneath"? Why does Allah, so often, describe the reward as something flowing *under* you?

There is a general beauty to it — water at the roots, gardens that never thirst, the cool sound of a stream below shaded ground. All true. But in **this** surah, in **this** position, the word *taḥt* is doing something far sharper. And to see it, you have to put it next to the other word in this surah for a thing dug **down into the ground**.

Go back to the trench. *Al-ukhdūd.* What is an *ukhdūd*? Lexically it is a long groove, a gouge, a **trench cut down into the earth.** The defining feature of the *ukhdūd* is precisely that it is **below** — a wound in the ground, dug downward, and filled with fire.

So hold the two images side by side, because the surah built them to be held that way:

The persecutors dug **down** — a trench, *beneath* the believers — and filled the **below** with **fire**.

And Allah's answer, a few lines later, is a garden whose defining feature is that **beneath** it — in the very same direction, the *below* — flow **rivers of water.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? Stay with me, because this is the heart of it.

The tyrant's whole instrument of terror was *what lay beneath* the believer's feet. He made the ground itself the threat. He made *below* mean **fire, fuel, death.** The most frightening direction in that scene was **down** — because down was the trench, down was the flame.

And Allah takes the exact same direction — *taḥt*, beneath, below — and **fills it with the opposite.** Where the tyrant put a trench of fire, Allah puts rivers of water. Where the believer once looked down in terror, the believer of Paradise looks down at flowing streams. Fire becomes water. Threat becomes mercy. The wound dug into the earth becomes a garden watered from its roots.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing at an unseen reality through something you can see. The physical image is a stream under a garden. But what it is *pointing at* is a divine reversal so total that it reaches into the very geometry of the scene. The same word — *below* — that the oppressor weaponized, Allah redeems. He does not just rescue the believers from the trench. He takes the **shape** of their suffering and turns it inside out.

The classical commentators dwell on the richness of what flows beneath: al-Qurtubi notes the streams of water that never stagnate, of milk whose taste never sours, of pure honey — an abundance heaped on people who, in the world, were given nothing but a fire and an ultimatum. Notice the asymmetry. The world offered them one thing: a trench. Allah answers with rivers, plural — *al-anhār* — more than they lost, in the place where they lost it.

What this one word *taḥt* reveals: **Allah does not only reverse your outcome. He reverses the very thing that frightened you.** The direction that meant death becomes the direction that means life.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Al-fawz al-kabīr* — naming the burned ones "the winners"

Now we arrive at the last two words, and they are the verdict the whole verse has been walking toward.

> ذَٲلِكَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْكَبِيرُ
> *dhālika al-fawzu al-kabīr* — **that** is the great success.

Three things are buried here. Let me open them one at a time.

**First, the word *fawz* itself.** *Fawz* does not merely mean "reward" or "good outcome." Lexically, *fawz* is **triumph, winning, the attainment of what you raced for and the escape from what you feared.** It is the word of the **victor**. It is what you shout when you have *won*.

Now remember the scene. Who, on that day, looked like the winner? The tyrant. He had the power, the fire, the seat at the edge, the surviving body, the crowd. He won the way the world counts winning. And the believers — they lost the way the world counts losing. They are ash.

And Allah looks at the ash and says: *that* — **dhālika** — is the **fawz**. The triumph. The win. He gives the believers the very title the tyrant thought he had earned. **He calls the ones in the trench the victors.**

[PAUSE]

This is staggering. Allah does not console the believers ("you suffered, but it's okay, you'll be rewarded"). He **overturns the scoreboard.** He does not say *you lost the world but gained the next*. He says *you did not lose at all. You are the winners. The man at the edge of the fire — he is the one who lost everything.*

**Second, the demonstrative *dhālika* — "that."** *Dhālika* in Arabic is the word for something **far, elevated, pointed at from a distance with significance** — "*that* one, over there, the great one." Allah does not say *hādhā*, "this." He says *dhālika*, **"that."** He is pointing at the gardens, the rivers, the whole reversed reality, and lifting it up — *that, that thing right there, the one worth pointing at across all distance* — **that** is the triumph. The grammar itself raises the reward onto a pedestal and points.

**Third — and this is subtle — the word *kabīr*.** Allah calls it *al-fawz al-**kabīr***, the **great** success.

Here is what you should know. When the Qur'an speaks of *al-fawz*, the supreme success, it most often pairs it with the word *al-ʿaẓīm* — the magnificent, the awe-inspiring. That is the familiar phrase. But in this place Allah reaches instead for *al-kabīr*.

Now, *kabīr* and *ʿaẓīm* are close cousins, and al-Qurtubi glosses *kabīr* here precisely as *al-ʿaẓīm* — "the great, which no other success resembles." So we are not building a wall between them. But feel the texture of *kabīr* for a moment. The root *k-b-r* is the root of **size, of bigness, of magnitude** — it is the root inside *Allāhu **akbar***, *takbīr*, *kibr*. *Kabīr* speaks to something **large**, something that **dwarfs** what is next to it.

And what is next to it in this surah? A "big" fire. A "big" tyrant. A "big" spectacle — a whole crowd seated to watch. The persecutors built something they thought was *enormous*: the power to take life, to terrorize a community, to make the believers' faith look small and crushable.

And Allah, having watched all of it, names the *believers'* outcome *al-kabīr* — **the big one.** As if to say: you thought *that* trench was big? You thought *that* power was large? Look at what I have built for these burned ones. *This* is the *kabīr*. This is the one that dwarfs everything. Next to this river, your fire was nothing. Next to this triumph, your power was small.

[PAUSE]

So let us gather these three words. *Dhālika* — He points across the distance and lifts it up. *Al-fawz* — He calls it a **victory**, handing the burned the title of winners. *Al-kabīr* — He calls it the **big** one, the one beside which the tyrant's whole apparatus shrinks to nothing.

What these final words reveal: **Allah does not merely promise the persecuted a future comfort. He re-judges the present. He stands at the same trench the world stood at, looks at the same ash — and announces a completely different verdict.** The world said: *they lost, he won.* Allah says: *they are the victors, and his bigness was small.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Look at what these three pieces have shown us, woven together. The **tense** told us their finished deeds bought an unending river. The word ***taḥt*** told us the very direction that terrorized them — *below* — was filled with mercy instead of fire. And ***al-fawz al-kabīr*** told us that Allah looked at people the world called losers and crowned them the **great victors**.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because if you have ever felt that doing the right thing made you *lose*, this verse is speaking directly to you.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The terror of dying as a "failure" — and the One who re-scores the game

Let me start somewhere very human, very modern, far from any trench.

There is a specific fear that lives in almost everyone, and most of us never say it out loud. It is not exactly the fear of death. It is the fear of dying **as a failure.** The fear of getting to the end and the final score reading *loss*. The fear that you tried to do the right thing — you were honest when lying would have paid, you kept your faith when dropping it would have been easier, you stayed loyal, you stayed clean — and that it **cost** you. That the people who cut corners pulled ahead. That you watched them get the promotion, the money, the ease, the win — and you got the slower road and the smaller life. And that when it is all added up, the world's scoreboard will say they won and you lost.

That fear is not new. It is the exact emotional reality of the people of the trench, pushed to its furthest extreme. They did everything right. They believed, they held firm. And it cost them **everything** — not money, not a promotion, but their lives, in fire, while their tormentors sat comfortably and watched. If there were ever a moment where doing the right thing looked like pure, total, unanswered **loss** — that was it.

And **that** is the exact moment Allah chose to drop this verse.

Notice He did not place this promise in an easy chapter, attached to easy believers who sacrificed nothing. He placed it right at the lip of the worst-case scenario. As if to say: *I want you to see Me declare the verdict in the hardest possible case. If I call THESE people the victors — the ones who literally burned — then you will never again be able to look at any sacrifice and call it a loss.*

Here is the movement I want you to walk through.

The world runs on a scoreboard. Power, survival, comfort, who walked away and who did not. By that scoreboard, the believers lost. And here is the thing — **that scoreboard is real.** Allah does not deny that they were thrown in the fire. The Qur'an does not pretend it did not happen. The suffering was actual.

But Allah introduces a **second scoreboard.** And on the second scoreboard, the columns are reversed. *Fawz* — victory — sits in the believers' column. And the tyrant, who topped the first board, is — a few verses earlier — given *ʿadhāb al-ḥarīq*, the punishment of the burning. The one who lit the fire gets the fire. The ones who were burned get the rivers.

Now here is the part that should change how you live tomorrow. **Both scoreboards are reading at the same time, right now.** The verse does not say the believers *will eventually* become winners once Paradise arrives. It uses *dhālika* — *that* **is** the great victory. Present. The reframe is not deferred to the afterlife. It is *true now.* The believers in the trench were *already* the winners while the smoke was still rising — the world just couldn't read the right board.

So the question this theme leaves you holding is not "will the right thing pay off eventually?" The question is: **which scoreboard are you actually playing for?** Because you cannot win on both. Every time you choose the honest, faithful, costly thing and watch it "lose" on the world's board, you are scoring on Allah's. The loss you feel is real on one board and a **victory** on the other — at the very same moment.

The practical shift is this. The next time doing right costs you something — and you feel that hot flush of *I'm losing, they're winning* — do not argue yourself out of the feeling. The feeling is reading the first board correctly. Instead, **remember that a second board exists, and Whose hand keeps it, and that on His board the entry for this exact moment may read *fawz*.** The people of the trench teach you that the gap between the two boards can be as wide as the distance between a fire and a river — and that it is the second board that gets the last word, and the *true* word.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: What "big" really means — recalibrating the size of things

Let me come at the second theme through something you already know in your body: the experience of being **overwhelmed by something that later turned out to be small.**

Think of a fear you had as a child. A dark hallway. A dog. A test. At the time it filled the entire sky. It was *enormous* — it was the biggest thing in your world. And now, from the distance of years, you can barely remember why it frightened you at all. The thing did not change size. **You** changed your vantage point, and from the new vantage point its true smallness became visible.

This is what the word *al-kabīr* is doing at the end of our verse — but on a cosmic scale.

To the believers standing at the trench, the tyrant's power was *the biggest thing in the world.* It filled the sky. The fire, the soldiers, the crowd, the absolute authority over life and death — there was nothing bigger in their field of vision. When something can throw you into a fire, it is very hard to see it as small.

And Allah, in one word, **resizes the entire scene.** He does not call the *fire* big. He does not call the *tyrant* big. He takes the word for bigness — *kabīr* — and lays it on the **believers' reward.** *That* is the *kabīr.* The big one. And the instant He does, everything else in the scene shrinks. The fire that filled the sky becomes a small thing next to a river that never ends. The tyrant who held the power of death becomes a small thing next to the One who holds the power of resurrection. The spectacle that seemed enormous becomes, from the true vantage point, almost nothing.

Here is the movement. We spend our lives terrified of the wrong-sized things. We treat small things as big and big things as small. The raise we didn't get feels *huge*. The opinion of people who will forget us feels *huge*. The risk of looking foolish for our faith feels *huge*. Meanwhile the things that are actually *kabīr* — the state of our heart, the river waiting at the root of our deeds, the verdict of the One who watches — those feel abstract, distant, small.

This verse is a **recalibration of size.** It says: let Me tell you what is actually big. Not the fire. The river. Not the power to end a life. The power to give a life that does not end. Not the seat at the edge of the trench. The garden the trench was turned into.

And notice — this is the same surah that *opened* with bigness done correctly. *Wa al-samāʾi dhāti al-burūj* — by the heaven full of its mighty constellations. The surah begins by pointing at things that are *genuinely* vast — the constellations, the cosmos — and ends by pointing at a reward and calling *it* the *kabīr*. The frame is set from the first verse: *here is what real magnitude looks like.* And against that true scale of sky and constellation and unending river, the tyrant and his trench were never big at all. They only looked big to people standing too close.

[PAUSE]

The practical shift here is a daily discipline of **re-sizing.** When something in your life swells up and fills your whole sky — a fear, a loss, a humiliation, a temptation to abandon the right path because the cost looks unbearable — train yourself to ask the *kabīr* question: *On Allah's scale, how big is this actually?* The thing that is threatening to make you abandon your faith or your integrity — measure it against the river. Measure it against *al-fawz al-kabīr.* Almost always, the thing terrorizing you is the dark hallway. It is not the size it is pretending to be. And the thing you are being asked to give up for it — your faith, your honesty, your soul — *that* is the *kabīr.*

What this theme changes about tomorrow: you will catch yourself, mid-panic, mid-temptation, and you will say — *wait. Let me check the size of this.* And more often than you expect, the mountain will turn back into the molehill it always was, and the small faithful choice you were about to abandon will reveal itself as the *kabīr* it always was.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: a verse built as a mirror of the trench

Now that we have done the work, step back and look at how this single verse is built — because it is constructed as a **point-by-point reversal** of the scene right before it. Watch:

- The persecutors dug **down** into the earth (*ukhdūd*) → Allah puts rivers **down** beneath the gardens (*min taḥtihā*). Same direction. Opposite contents.
- They filled the below with **fire** (*al-nāri dhāti al-waqūd*) → Allah fills the below with **water** (*al-anhār*).
- They **sat** as spectators, lording over the scene (*hum ʿalayhā quʿūd*) → the believers are *given* the gardens, *lahum jannāt*, recipients of grace, no longer the watched but the honored.
- The tyrant looked like the **winner** → Allah names the burned ones *al-fawz*, the **victors.**
- The fire and the power looked **big** → Allah calls the believers' reward *al-kabīr*, the **big one**, and everything else shrinks.

The verse is not a generic promise that happened to land here. It is a **custom-built answer**, shaped line by line to invert the exact horror it follows. That is the proof that nothing in this sentence is accidental. Allah took the architecture of their suffering and rebuilt it, beam by beam, into the architecture of their reward.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The **tense** showed us a finished deed buying an unending river — and the theme of the *scoreboard* showed us why: the world measures the finished, visible cost, while Allah is paying out the unending, invisible reward.

The word ***taḥt*** showed us the feared direction filled with mercy — and the theme of *re-sizing* showed us why: what looked like the big threat (the trench below) was small, and what is truly *kabīr* (the river below) was the reality all along.

And *al-fawz al-kabīr* — the burned ones crowned as the great victors — is simply both themes spoken in three words: **the second scoreboard reads *victory*, and on the true scale of things, that victory is the only big thing in the entire scene.**

### Questions to carry

1. When was the last time doing the right thing made me feel like I was *losing* — and which scoreboard was I reading in that moment?
2. Is there something in my life right now that feels *enormous* — big enough to make me bend my faith or my integrity around it? If I measured it against the river that never ends, how big is it really?
3. The believers' deeds were *finished* and small; the river was *unending* and vast. Do I actually believe that trade — or do I live as if my small, finished efforts are a poor investment?
4. The tyrant's name was not even recorded; the believers were called victors. Whose verdict am I actually living for — the seated spectators, or the One who keeps the second board?
5. Allah did not just rescue them — He reversed the very *shape* of their suffering. Where in my own life might the thing I most fear be exactly the thing He intends to turn into a garden?

### One-sentence distillation

When you do the right thing and the world watches you lose, Allah stands at the same scene, looks at the same ash, and calls you the great victor — turning the very ground that threatened you into rivers that never end.

### The closing invitation

I am not going to tell you that the costly choices in your life will stop costing. They may not. The believers at the trench were not spared the fire. What this verse offers is not the removal of the cost — it is the **revelation of the true verdict.** It is an invitation to live, starting today, as someone who knows that a second scoreboard exists, that it is kept by the One who saw everything, and that on it, the faithful, costly, "losing" choice is written down as *al-fawz al-kabīr.* Let that knowledge make you a little braver tomorrow than you were today — brave enough to lose on the first board, because you have glimpsed your name on the second.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, who turned the trench of fire into rivers that never end — let us not be fooled by the scoreboard of this world. When doing right costs us, write our names among *those who believed and did righteous deeds.* And on the day every account is settled, let us look down and find, beneath us, the rivers You promised — and hear from You that *this* was the great victory all along. Āmīn.
