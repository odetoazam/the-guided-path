## INTRODUCTION

### The world you have to stand in first

Somewhere in the years before the migration, in a city that had begun to hurt the people who prayed, a small group of Muslims heard this surah for the first time.

They knew what a burning felt like. Not as history — as a rumour that reached the skin. Bilal on the sand under a rock. Sumayyah. The families with no clan to shelter them, the ones whose protection was a name nobody important carried. And around them, a city of watchers: men who did not personally hold the rope, who simply stood nearby, and did not intervene, and went home to dinner.

Into that, Allah sends a story that is not about them.

It is older, and it happened somewhere else. A tyrant dug a long trench, filled it with fire, and gave a community a choice: leave your faith, or go in. They did not leave. And the ones who had built the trench sat down at the edge of it — sat down — and watched.

The Quran does not tell us where. It does not tell us when, or name the king, or name a single one of the burned. Almost everything a historian would want is withheld. What we are given instead is a camera, and a very particular place to stand.

Feel the shape of that for the believers in Makkah. The surah is not saying *your suffering is like theirs.* It is saying something harder and steadier: **this has happened before, on a scale worse than yours, and here is what it looked like from above.**

### The hook

The passage opens with an oath sworn on the sky — on towers, on a Promised Day, on a witness and a thing witnessed. Then it drops, in one motion, to a ditch in the ground.

Nine ayahs. And by the last one, you will discover that the word chosen for the men sitting comfortably at the edge of that fire is the same word — the very same root — that Allah will use, at the end, for Himself.

Watch that word. It is doing something across the whole passage that you can only see if you are counting.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — بُرُوج: an oath sworn from the greatest possible height

Before any of the horror, an oath. And where an oath is sworn *from* matters.

[ayah:85:1] وَٱلسَّمَآءِ ذَاتِ ٱلْبُرُوجِ

The root is **ب-ر-ج**, and the word has two lives in the Quran. In this ayah and two others, *burūj* are what the sky *has* — Allah says elsewhere that He *placed burūj in the sky and adorned them for those who look*: the great stations of the heavens, what the commentators read as the constellations, the twelve mansions the ancient world navigated by. But the same word appears once more, in Surah An-Nisa, in a completely different register: *even if you were in **burūj** that are fortified* — **towers**. Strongholds. The highest, safest structures human beings knew how to build.

Hold both. The word means *the towers of the sky*. And the ayah where it means human towers is an ayah about how death reaches you anyway, in the strongest fortress you can build.

So the oath is sworn from the top of everything. Constellations. Fortifications. The highest thing there is.

And then — four ayahs later — the camera goes into a hole in the ground.

That drop is the architecture of the opening. From the towers of the sky to a ditch cut in the dirt. And the ditch is where the people of faith are, and the towers are where the oath is coming from, and the passage never once suggests that the people in the ditch were the ones who lost.

### Element Two — ٱلْأُخْدُود: the shape cut in the ground, and in the face

Now the ditch itself, and a word so specific the Quran uses it only here.

[ayah:85:4] قُتِلَ أَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلْأُخْدُودِ

The root of *ukhdūd* is **خ-د-د**, and across the entire Quran that root surfaces exactly twice. Once here — *al-ukhdūd*, which al-Jalalayn glosses simply as **ٱلشَّقّ فِي ٱلْأَرْض**, *the split in the earth.* A long cut furrow.

And once in Surah Luqman, where a father is teaching his son how to be a human being: *and do not turn your **cheek** away from people [in scorn], and do not walk in the earth exultantly.* **خَدَّكَ** — *khaddaka*, your cheek. Same root. In classical Arabic the cheek is named for the same idea as the trench: a groove, a furrow, a long cut line.

One way to sit with that pairing: the Quran's only two uses of this root are a furrow of contempt in a human face, and a furrow of fire in the ground. Luqman's advice names the small version — the turned cheek, the little scorn we perform when we think someone is beneath us. Al-Buruj shows what that shape becomes when it is given power and a shovel. I would not press this as a claim about the Quran's intent; the roots are documented, the resonance is yours to weigh.

Now the verb sitting in front of it. **قُتِلَ** — *qutila* — and note the form: it is **passive**. "Killed were..." But the classical reading takes it somewhere else entirely. Al-Jalalayn glosses *qutila* in one word: **لُعِنَ** — *cursed*. On this reading it is not a report that the trench-people died. It is an **imprecation** — the Arabic idiom that hurls a curse by putting a man in the grammar of the already-destroyed. *Damn the people of the trench.*

Which means the first thing the Quran says about them is not what they did. It is a verdict, delivered before the evidence, in the passive voice, with no one named. The judgement arrives ahead of the story.

And now a disclosure, because the commentators are not unanimous here and the alternative changes who is standing in the sentence.

Al-Tabari leads with the curse-reading — *He is saying: cursed be the companions of the trench* — which is why this reflection follows it. But in the very next breath he records another: **وكان بعضهم يقول** — *some of them used to say* — that the verse is **خبر من الله عن النار أنها قتلتهم**, *a report from Allah about the fire, that it killed them.* Plain passive, no curse. And on that reading the "companions of the trench" are not the tyrants at all. They are the believers, and the sentence is not a damnation of murderers but an epitaph over the murdered.

Al-Tabari then names the disagreement outright: the people of knowledge differed over who the companions of the trench even *are.* Some said they were a People of the Book; other reports in his chapter identify them otherwise.

Two old readings, both on the record. This reflection follows the first — it is al-Jalalayn's gloss, it is what al-Tabari opens with, and it is what he defends later in the passage as the sound view. But you should know the second exists, and know what turns on it: whether the opening line of this story curses the killers or mourns the killed.

### Element Three — قُعُود and شُهُود: two words for what the watchers were doing

And now the scene, which is where the language becomes unbearable.

[ayah:85:6] إِذْ هُمْ عَلَيْهَا قُعُودٌ

*Quʿūd*, from **ق-ع-د**, and in the corpus of Quranic grammar it is not a participle but a **verbal noun** — the naked act itself, *sitting*, used as the predicate. Arabic could have said *they sat*, a finite verb, an event with a beginning and an end. Instead it names the state and hangs the men inside it. They are not people who sat. They are, for as long as the ayah lasts, **sitting**.

And al-Jalalayn fills in the detail the ayah leaves to the imagination: *around it, on the edge of the trench* — **عَلَى ٱلْكَرَاسِيّ** — **on chairs.**

They brought chairs.

[PAUSE]

Stay there for a moment, because the Quran is being very careful. It has not yet used a single word about cruelty. It has told you people were burning, and it has told you the others were seated. That is all. The horror is entirely in the furniture.

[PAUSE]

Now the second disclosure, and this one sits directly under everything I have just said. *Who* is seated in ayah 6?

Al-Tabari's own answer is the one assumed above: those disbelievers among the trench-people were over it — over the fire — meaning they were seated on the lip of the ditch, and the ayah says "over the fire" because the fire was in the ditch. That is the reading al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar carry too, and it is the reading this reflection follows.

But al-Tabari records Qatadah reading the same words the other way. Of *the fire full of fuel, when they were seated over it*, Qatadah said: **يعني بذلك المؤمنين** — *he means by that the believers.* On Qatadah's reading the ones over the fire are the victims, not the audience. Al-Tabari explains why Qatadah lands there: he is following the school that took the earlier verse as a report about the fire, so for him the companions of the trench are the people of faith who died in it.

Al-Tabari does not leave it open. He answers: **وقد دلَّلْنا على أن الصواب من تأويل ذلك غير هذا القول** — *we have already shown that the sound interpretation of this is other than this statement.*

Hold Qatadah's version in view anyway, because of what it would cost. If he is right, there are no chairs in ayah 6 and no spectators in it either, and this passage is not about comfortable watching at all — it is entirely about the burning. Everything this reflection builds from here rests on the majority reading. That reading is well founded and it is al-Tabari's own. It is not the only one on record, and I would rather you knew that than discovered it later.

[PAUSE]

Then the next ayah gives them their title:

[ayah:85:7] وَهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَفْعَلُونَ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ شُهُودٌ

*Shuhūd* — from **ش-ه-د** — the plural of the active participle: **the ones who witness.** Al-Jalalayn glosses it *ḥuḍūr*: present, in attendance. Not perpetrators, in this word. *Attendees.*

And here is what to keep counting. That root — ش-ه-د — has already appeared, twice, in the oath itself. Ayah 3 swears **وَشَاهِدٍ وَمَشْهُودٍ** — *by a witness and a witnessed* — the active participle and the passive participle, side by side, the seer and the seen, both left deliberately unnamed. The classical commentators offer a whole spread of identifications for that pair — al-Jalalayn passes on a reading from hadith that makes them the Day of Jumuʿah and the Day of ʿArafah; others read them as the Prophet and his community, or the witnesses and deeds of the Last Day. The Quran does not adjudicate. It leaves the pair open and enormous.

But watch what the openness *does* to the passage that follows. Allah swears by *a witness and a witnessed* — and four ayahs later there are men sitting on chairs who are called, in that same root, *witnesses*. Something is being set up. The word for what those men thought they were doing is a word that has already been sworn by, from the height of the constellations.

They took a seat at a spectacle. And the vocabulary has begun, quietly, to ask who exactly is watching whom.

[PAUSE]

### Element Four — the sound of the whole passage

One last thing, and you do not need Arabic to hear it. Read the nine endings aloud in order:

*burūj... mawʿūd... mashhūd... ukhdūd... waqūd... quʿūd... shuhūd... ḥamīd... shahīd.*

Nine ayahs. One sound, hammered nine times — a long vowel and a hard stop, over and over, closing every line like a drumbeat that will not vary.

And listen to *which* words are locked into that rhyme with each other: the **trench** (*ukhdūd*), the **fuel** (*waqūd*), the **sitting** (*quʿūd*), the **watching** (*shuhūd*). The instrument, the fire, the comfort, and the audience — the passage makes them rhyme. They are, sonically, the same event.

Then the last two lines break the vowel and land somewhere else: *ḥamīd... shahīd.* The Praiseworthy. The Witness.

[PAUSE]

The oath came from the towers and the constellations, and dropped into a cut in the ground. A verdict arrived before the evidence. Men brought chairs to a burning and the Quran called them *witnesses* — in a root it had already sworn by. And every line of it rhymes with every other. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1: The Crime of Comfortable Watching

*A note before we begin, carried forward from Part 1: this theme stands on the majority reading of ayah 6 — that the seated ones are the persecutors. Qatadah read them as the believers, and al-Tabari, while judging that reading unsound, preserved it. If Qatadah is right, this theme is not in the passage. I follow al-Tabari's verdict, and I want you holding the receipt while you read.*

The Quran's most devastating accusation in this passage is not that they lit the fire. It is that they **sat down.**

*Qu'ood.* Seated. The plural form suggests they were all seated — not one or two but all of them. They had arranged themselves. They had made themselves comfortable — *mulāzimūn,* the commentators say, fixed to their places and unwilling to leave. The fire was burning people alive and they were in the posture of spectators at a performance.

This is the Quran naming something that the modern world knows intimately but rarely confronts: **the violence of comfortable observation.** The bystander who films instead of helping. The society that scrolls past atrocity on its timeline and keeps scrolling. The institution that "monitors the situation" while people die.

The surah doesn't distinguish between the one who threw believers into the fire and the one who sat by the fire and watched. Both are *ashaab al-ukhdood.* Both are "companions of the trench." Both are cursed — *qutila.* The one who watches suffering and does nothing is not neutral. They are a participant. Their sitting is an act. Their comfort is a position.

[PAUSE]

And the imperfect tense in ayah 7 — *maa yaf'aloona* — "what they ARE doing" — makes the crime ongoing. The watching didn't end. It continued. Each moment they sat was a fresh act of complicity. Each moment of comfort was a fresh endorsement.

The modern version rarely involves a literal trench. But the structure is identical: someone is being destroyed, and someone else is watching comfortably. The surah asks: which one are you? And if you're the watcher — if you're *qu'ood,* seated, comfortable, spectating — the surah counts you among the companions of the trench.

---

### Theme 2: Faith That Praises God While Burning

The believers in this passage are described with a single identifier: they believed in Allah. That's all. No names. No numbers. No stories of heroism. Just: they believed. And that alone, the surah insists, was the entire charge against them.

And the God they believed in is named with the two hardest names for this moment: *Al-'Azeez* — the One with absolute power, who could have stopped this — and *Al-Hameed* — the One who deserves praise, even now.

This is the surah forcing a confrontation with the deepest question faith can face: **if God is powerful and He didn't stop this, is He still praiseworthy?**

The believers in the trench answered: yes. They burned and they believed in the *'Azeez.* They died and they died praising the *Hameed.* They didn't have a theological framework that explained why God allowed their suffering. They had something heavier: a conviction that His being *'Azeez* and *Hameed* was not contingent on their comfort.

[PAUSE]

The modern person — even the modern believer — struggles with this. We have built our relationship with God around outcomes. If things go well, God is good. If things go badly, God is being questioned. Our praise is conditional on our circumstances. The believers of the trench stripped that condition away. They praised in the fire. Not after the fire. Not despite the fire. **In** the fire.

And the surah does not explain why God allowed it. It does not offer theodicy. It offers two names: the One with power, and the One who is praised. If that is enough for you, you are with the people in the trench. If it is not enough, the surah will not argue with you. It will simply close with: *And Allah is, over all things, a Witness.* He saw. He knows. His testimony is coming. The Promised Day is real.

The faith of the trench is not reasonable faith. It is faith that survives the burning of every reason. And the Quran places it here — between cosmic oaths and a divine testimony — to say: this is the standard. Not the easy standard. The real one.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

The root ش-ه-د — to witness — runs through the entire passage like a spine:

- **Ayah 3:** Allah swears by *a witness and what is witnessed* — the principle that nothing happens unseen
- **Ayah 7:** The persecutors are *shuhood* — witnesses to their own crime; their watching is part of their guilt
- **Ayah 9:** Allah is *Shaheed* — the ultimate Witness, whose witnessing is His nature, not His hobby

Three appearances of the root. Three different agents. The structure says: in every scene of human history, there are always three levels of watching. The bystanders who think they are the audience. The victims who think they are unseen. And the One who sees everything — the Shaheed whose testimony will be heard on the Promised Day.

The passage moves: cosmic oath (sky, Day, witness) → earthly crime (trench, fire, sitting, watching) → divine identity (Mighty, Praiseworthy) → divine testimony (Witness over all things). The scale zooms in from the constellations to the trench, then zooms out again to the dominion of heavens and earth. The trench is small. The crime is small. But the Witness is vast — and His testimony is eternal.

### Questions to Carry

- Where in the world right now are people being destroyed while others sit comfortably and watch? And which side of the trench are you on?

- If you believe in *Al-'Azeez* — the One with absolute power — how do you hold that belief when He doesn't intervene the way you expect?

- Is your praise of Allah conditional on your circumstances? Would you still call Him *Al-Hameed* in the fire?

- What does it mean to live knowing that the Witness never blinks — that every moment of comfortable watching is itself being watched?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The people who watched believers burn thought they were the audience — but Allah, the Witness whose nature is to see everything, was watching them, and on the Promised Day His testimony is the only one that will matter.**

### The Closing Invitation

The trench was real. The fire was real. The believers who burned were real. And the spectators who sat comfortably were real.

The surah does not end with revenge. It does not end with fire for the persecutors (though that comes later in the surah). It ends with a fact: Allah is *Shaheed.* He saw. He sees now. He will see on the Promised Day.

If you are watching something wrong happen right now — if you are *qu'ood,* comfortable, seated before someone else's suffering — this surah is for you. Not to condemn you. To warn you. The seat is comfortable. The fire is warm from a distance. But the Witness is above the trench. And He is not seated. He is *Shaheed* — witnessing, always, as His nature, without interruption.

Stand up.

### Du'a

*Allahumma, You are the Shaheed who saw the believers burn and who saw the watchers sit. Do not make us companions of any trench. Do not let our comfort become complicity. And if we are ever in the fire — if faith costs us everything — make us among those who called You 'Azeez when You could have intervened and Hameed when You didn't. You saw them. You see us. Your testimony is enough. Ameen.*
