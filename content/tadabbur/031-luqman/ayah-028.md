# Surah Luqman, Ayah 28
## One Soul, All Souls — The Mathematics of Divine Power

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
مَّا خَلْقُكُمْ وَلَا بَعْثُكُمْ إِلَّا كَنَفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ

**Transliteration:**
*Mā khalqukum wa lā baʿthukum illā ka-nafsin wāḥidah. Inna Llāha Samīʿun Baṣīr.*

**Translation:**
Your creation and your resurrection are not but as a single soul. Indeed, Allah is Hearing and Seeing.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuild the World

Place yourself in Makkah, sometime in the middle period of the revelation. Surah Luqman is being recited to a community that is wrestling with one specific scandal in the Prophet's ﷺ message.

It isn't tawḥīd, exactly. The Arabs already believed in Allah as Creator.

It isn't prayer. Most of them prayed in some form.

It is **the resurrection.**

The idea that after you die — after your body decomposes, after your bones become indistinguishable from the dust, after centuries pass and the elements that were once *you* have been scattered across continents, eaten by plants, drunk by animals, inhaled by other humans — that **all of that** could somehow be reassembled, identified, summoned, and held accountable.

To the Makkan ear, this wasn't just unbelievable. It was *mathematically absurd*.

How many humans had ever lived? Millions. How many would live before the end? Many millions more. And every one of them, simultaneously, reconstituted, stood before Allah, judged?

Listen to how their objection sounds in the Quran: *"Who will give life to bones that have rotted away?"* (36:78). *"When we are dust and our fathers — will we really be brought forth?"* (27:67). The mockery wasn't theological. It was about *scale*. It was about *logistics*.

And Surah Luqman has been walking the listener through Allah's signs — the heavens raised without pillars, the mountains anchored, the ships that sail by His mercy, the rain that revives dead earth. The surah has been building. Building. Building.

And then this single sentence lands.

Twelve words. Not a long argument. Not a step-by-step proof.

Twelve words that, if you let them in, dismantle the entire objection at its foundation.

### The Hook

The deniers had a math problem with the resurrection. Allah's answer is not to do better math.

His answer is to tell you that **you've been counting the wrong thing the entire time.**

Stay with me. What we're about to uncover changes not just how we think about the Day of Judgment — it changes how we think about *every* anxiety that has ever felt too big for Allah to handle.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to walk through three features of this ayah. Each of them is small. Each of them is doing structural work.

---

### Element One: The Particle of Exception — *Mā... Illā*

Before we open the ayah, let me teach you a structure in Arabic that, once you see it, you'll start seeing everywhere in the Quran.

In Arabic, the most powerful way to make a statement is not to say "X is Y." It is to say: **"There is nothing — nothing at all — except X."**

This is called *al-ḥaṣr*, restriction. You build a wall around the entire universe of possibilities, exclude every single one, and then carve out the *one* exception. Whatever survives that exclusion lands with thunderous weight.

In English, if I want to emphasize that I only love my mother, I say: "I love my mother." If I want it stronger, I say: "I really love my mother." If I want it stronger still, I say: "Nobody loves me like my mother."

But in Arabic, the construction *mā... illā* — "there is nothing... except" — is built into the bones of the language. It's how you draw a circle around all of reality and point to one thing inside it.

Now look at how this ayah opens:

**مَّا خَلْقُكُمْ وَلَا بَعْثُكُمْ إِلَّا كَنَفْسٍ وَاحِدَةٍ**

*Mā khalqukum wa lā baʿthukum illā ka-nafsin wāḥidah.*

"Your creation and your resurrection — **are not** — except as a single soul."

Notice something. Allah didn't say: *"Your creation and resurrection are like a single soul."* That would have been a comparison. A simile. A useful illustration.

He said: *"They are not — anything — except as a single soul."*

He built the wall first. He excluded every possible misreading. He shut every other door.

Before He tells you what creation and resurrection *are*, He tells you what they are not.

They are **not vast.** They are **not many.** They are **not a burden distributed across an unimaginable scale.**

Let's sit with this. The deniers' entire objection was *quantitative*. "There are too many of us. Too much dust. Too vast a project."

And Allah, before answering the question, **denies the question's premise**. He doesn't say "the project is achievable." He says: *the project you're imagining doesn't exist*. What you are calling "many" — He is calling "one."

[PAUSE]

This is the first move. Hold it.

---

### Element Two: *Ka-Nafsin Wāḥidah* — The Indefinite Singular

Now the second feature. And this one is so small you might walk past it without noticing.

Look at the comparison: *ka-nafsin wāḥidah*. "Like a single soul."

The word *nafs* means soul, self, person. The word *wāḥidah* means "one."

But here's what I want you to notice: *nafsin* is **indefinite**. It is not "*the* single soul." It is not "*one specific* soul." It is "*a* single soul." Any soul. Any one of them.

In Arabic, when you want to say "the soul" — a specific, defined one — you add *al-*. *Al-nafs*. But here Allah leaves the noun bare. Open. Indefinite.

Why does that matter?

Because the moment you make the comparison specific — "your resurrection is like the resurrection of Prophet so-and-so" — you've given the listener a special case. You've allowed them to say: "Well, that one is exceptional."

But by leaving it indefinite, Allah closes that exit too. *Any* soul. *Pick one*. The first one you can think of. The last one ever born. The one sitting next to you. **Whichever one you choose** — your creation, all of you, and your resurrection, all of you, is no more difficult to Allah than that.

Here's what's remarkable: the comparison is not *between two ideas*. It is *between two efforts*. And Allah is saying the effort is identical.

Reach into your own life for a moment. Imagine the effort it takes you to lift a grain of sand off a table. Now imagine the effort to lift two grains. Three. A handful.

For you, every additional grain adds weight. Effort scales with quantity. This is the fundamental rule of *created* existence: **more requires more.**

The ayah is telling you that this rule — the rule that has governed every act you have ever performed — **does not apply to Allah.**

For the One who creates, the act of creating one soul and the act of creating every soul that ever was or will be are **not two different sized tasks**. They are the same task. Or rather — there is no "task" at all, because the word "task" implies effort, and effort implies limitation.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. This is where the ayah opens.

The disbelievers thought they had caught Allah in a logistics problem. They thought: *He can do small things, but resurrection at this scale is too much*.

The ayah's reply is not: *He can scale up*. The reply is: **scale does not exist for Him.**

Numbers — one, a million, a trillion — these are categories that belong to *us*. They describe how *our* effort relates to *our* world. Allah does not stand on our side of that equation.

What you call "many" is, from where Allah is, exactly the same as what you call "one."

---

### Element Three: *Samīʿun Baṣīr* — Hearing and Seeing

Now the third feature. And this is where I want you to slow down, because most translations rush past it.

The ayah ends: **Inna Llāha Samīʿun Baṣīr.** "Indeed, Allah is Hearing, Seeing."

Wait. Stop.

Why does this ayah end with *Hearing and Seeing*?

If the point of the ayah is divine power — that creation and resurrection are effortless for Allah — wouldn't you expect names like *al-Qādir* (the All-Powerful), or *al-Khāliq* (the Creator), or *al-Bāʿith* (the Resurrector)?

Why hearing? Why seeing?

This is the kind of question that, if you don't ask it, you've missed the heart of the ayah.

Let me teach you a principle first. In the Quran, the names Allah uses at the end of an ayah are not decorative. They are not a generic divine signature. They are the **specific attributes that ground the specific claim** the ayah just made.

When an ayah ends in *Ghafūrun Raḥīm* — Forgiving, Merciful — it usually just finished talking about repentance.

When it ends in *ʿAzīzun Ḥakīm* — Mighty, Wise — it usually just finished talking about a decree.

The names at the end **anchor** the ayah. They tell you *which* of Allah's attributes makes what you just heard possible.

So why *Samīʿun Baṣīr* here?

Here's what's remarkable. Hearing and seeing are the two attributes that, for us, **scale matters most for.**

Think about it. If you are in a crowd of ten people and one of them is whispering, you can hear them. If you are in a crowd of ten million people and one of them whispers — you hear nothing. Sound, for you, is overwhelmed by quantity.

Vision is the same. You can see one face clearly. You can see a hundred faces in a group photo, but the detail is lost. You see a stadium full of people, and individuals dissolve into a sea.

For *us*, hearing and seeing **break down at scale.**

But Allah is *Samīʿ* — hearing every prayer of every creature, every whisper of every heart, every cry from every corner of creation, **simultaneously and individually**, with no degradation.

And Allah is *Baṣīr* — seeing every leaf as it falls, every seed in the earth, every cell dividing in every body, every tear in every eye, **simultaneously and individually**, with no loss of detail.

Now do you see what these two names are doing at the end of this ayah?

The ayah said: *creating you all and resurrecting you all is no harder than creating one soul.*

And the closing names *prove the same principle in a different domain*. Just as His hearing and seeing do not degrade with scale — just as one voice in a crowd of billions reaches Him with the same clarity as one voice alone in a room — so His creating and resurrecting do not strain with scale. **The same divine reality** that makes Him hear and see every individual perfectly, **is the reality** that makes resurrecting every individual effortless.

[PAUSE]

The ending isn't a generic flourish. It's the proof.

You already know — even the disbelievers knew — that Allah hears every prayer, sees every act. They acknowledged this. Surah Luqman has been showing it.

And the ayah is saying: *If you accept that His hearing and seeing don't break under the weight of billions of souls — why would His creating and resurrecting?*

The architecture is symmetrical:
- Just as one voice = all voices to His hearing
- Just as one face = all faces to His seeing
- So one soul = all souls to His creating and resurrecting

The closing names are not separate from the opening claim. They are the **same claim** spoken in a register the listener cannot deny.

---

### Bridge

We have walked through three linguistic features:
1. **The wall of exception** — *mā... illā* — that denies the premise of the question, not just answers it.
2. **The indefinite comparison** — *ka-nafsin wāḥidah* — that refuses to let any soul be a special case.
3. **The anchoring names** — *Samīʿun Baṣīr* — that prove divine scale-independence in a domain the listener already accepts.

The grammar has shown us something specific: *scale is a feature of us, not of Him.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the heart that lives inside that reality.

[PAUSE]

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Anxiety of Scale

Let me start here. Not with the Day of Judgment. Not with the deniers of Makkah. With you, right now.

There is a peculiar suffering that belongs to modern life, and it has a specific shape. You feel small in the face of largeness. You feel like the world is too big for your prayer to matter. Like your one duʿāʾ — your one shaking, exhausted, late-night duʿāʾ — gets lost in the chorus of billions of prayers from billions of mouths. Like the One who must attend to wars and pandemics and the cosmos itself surely cannot be bothered with whatever small ache is sitting on your chest at 2 in the morning.

This is the **anxiety of scale**.

It is not new. It is not modern, exactly. But it is sharpened by modernity. Because we now *see* the scale. We have photographs of galaxies. We have counted the species. We know our population. We have measured the indifference of the cosmos. The atheist's challenge of the 21st century is precisely this: *In a universe this vast, you matter so little that no one is listening.*

Notice what the disbelievers of Makkah were doing. They had a primitive version of this anxiety, projected backwards. *We are too many. Our dust is too scattered. The project of resurrecting all of us is too large for any god to manage.*

The shape of the doubt was the same.

Now hear the ayah again: **Your creation and your resurrection are not but as a single soul.**

What does this do to the anxiety of scale?

It dissolves it.

Because the ayah is not promising you that Allah will *make time for you* amid the crowd. It is telling you that *there is no crowd from where He is*. The "crowd" is something you are seeing because you are inside it. From the position of the One who is hearing and seeing, **every soul is the only soul.**

Stay with this. This is not the comforting platitude that "you matter to God." That is the watered-down version. The ayah is making a much sharper claim: *the framework in which you are one among many — that framework belongs to creation, not to the Creator.*

You are not a number to Allah, not because He has decided to treat you well, but because **number itself is a feature of created reality**, and He is not inside it.

Here's the application. The next time you make duʿāʾ and the thought crosses your mind — *who am I that Allah should listen to me specifically?* — answer it with this ayah.

You are not asking Him to filter you out of the noise. There is no noise. The whisper of one heart and the roar of all hearts are, to Him, the same act of listening.

What does this change about tomorrow morning?

It changes the *posture* of your duʿāʾ. You don't have to apologize for taking His time. You don't have to feel like you are interrupting the management of a vast universe. **There is no universe to manage in the sense you imagine.** There is only Him, attending to you, with the entirety of His attention — which is the only kind of attention He has, because His attention is not divided.

[PAUSE]

---

### Theme Two: The Lie of "Hard" and "Easy"

Now let's move to the second theme, and this one is harder. Because it requires us to give up something we have built our whole worldview on.

In our world, there are easy things and hard things.

Picking up a pencil is easy. Building a house is hard. Lifting a stone is easy. Lifting a boulder is hard. Saying *salām* to a stranger is easy. Forgiving someone who broke you is hard.

This distinction — easy / hard — is so fundamental to how we live that we cannot imagine a mind for which it doesn't exist.

But the ayah is forcing us to imagine exactly that.

If creating one soul and creating every soul are, to Allah, the same — then *for Allah, hard and easy do not exist*. He does not strain. He does not exert. He does not prioritize the small over the large because the large is more taxing. **Difficulty is a feature of limitation**, and Allah is unlimited.

Here is where this becomes a knife in your spiritual life.

Because we project our category of "hard" onto Allah constantly.

We say: *Allah, I would ask You for the small thing, but I don't want to ask You for the big thing because that would be too much*.

We say: *I will pray for guidance because that's a spiritual request, but my financial situation is too worldly, too specific, too small*.

We say: *I'll ask for forgiveness but not for healing because healing requires a miracle and I don't want to ask for a miracle*.

Every one of these calculations is a **projection of our category of difficulty onto Allah**. We are quietly assuming that He is like us — that some requests cost more than others.

The ayah destroys this calculation.

If creating *all of you* and resurrecting *all of you* is, to Him, no different than handling one soul — then what category of "too much" could you possibly bring Him?

A famous statement, attributed to multiple early scholars in various forms, captures this: *do not measure the smallness of your need by your own size, but the greatness of the One you are asking by His.*

The duʿāʾ you have been afraid to make because it's "too big" — it is not too big. There is no "too big" in the dictionary of the One you are speaking to.

[PAUSE]

But here is the harder edge of this theme. And I want to sit with it.

If difficulty doesn't exist for Allah, then **the difficulty in our lives is not a measure of His limitation. It is a measure of His wisdom.**

When Allah delays an answer, it is not because He is figuring out how to manage it. When He withholds something, it is not because the request was too taxing. When the resurrection has not yet come, it is not because the logistics are still being worked out.

Every delay, every withholding, every wait — these are choices, not limits.

This is harder to accept than the easy version, which is just "Allah is powerful." Because if He is *unlimited* in capability, then everything in your life that has not yet happened, has not happened because He has chosen to wait. There is no cosmic queue. There is no "He's getting to it."

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes the texture of your relationship with delay. The thing you are waiting for — the marriage, the healing, the child, the breakthrough, the return of someone you love — is not waiting on capacity. It is waiting on wisdom. And wisdom that operates without the constraints of difficulty has access to a timing that you cannot calculate, because you are still calculating from inside the world of "hard" and "easy."

Trust, in this sense, is not believing that Allah *can*. The disbelievers themselves admitted He could create. Trust is believing that the delay has nothing to do with His ability and everything to do with His care.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at how this ayah is built. Look at the architecture.

The opening — *mā khalqukum wa lā baʿthukum* — names *the entirety* of human existence. Creation: where you come from. Resurrection: where you are going. **The full arc.** Beginning and end.

The middle — *illā ka-nafsin wāḥidah* — names *the smallest possible quantity*. One soul.

The ending — *Samīʿun Baṣīr* — names *the most intimate* of Allah's engagements with creation. Hearing every voice. Seeing every face.

So the structure moves: **all of you → one of you → each of you.**

The vastness of the whole human story collapses into one soul, which then opens back out into the perfect attentiveness of Allah to each individual. **The arc closes on you.**

This is the architecture of the ayah: it begins by talking about humanity en masse, denies that scale is real, and ends by speaking about the individual attention you are right now receiving.

You — the one listening to these words — are the destination of the ayah.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

From the linguistic side: scale is not a category that applies to Allah.
From the human side: your anxiety about being too small to be heard, or your fear that your need is too big to be granted, are both rooted in the *same false assumption* — that Allah is on the side of the equation where quantity costs.

He is not.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one duʿāʾ I have been afraid to make because I assumed it was "too big" for what I deserve to ask?

2. When I feel small in the face of the world, am I assuming Allah's attention is divided the way mine is?

3. If Allah's delay in my life has nothing to do with His capacity, what is it teaching me about His wisdom?

4. How would I pray differently tomorrow morning if I genuinely believed that, from where He is, I am the only soul He is attending to in this moment?

5. Where in my life am I projecting my own categories of "hard" and "easy" onto Allah, and quietly limiting what I'm willing to ask Him for?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The reason you can ask Allah for anything is not because He is generous enough to handle large requests, but because in the only place that matters — His knowing — large and small do not exist.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, make one duʿāʾ you have been holding back. The one you have been managing your expectations around. The one you have been quietly assuming was too specific, too worldly, too big, or too small.

Make it. Without apology.

Not because you have earned the right, but because **the One you are addressing has no scale at which He can be overwhelmed.**

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who hear every voice as if it were the only voice, and see every soul as if it were the only soul — let us not shrink from You out of a false humility that imagines our needs are too much for You. Teach our hearts that there is no "much" with You. And carry us, from the smallness of our calculation, into the openness of Your unlimited attention.

*Āmīn.*
