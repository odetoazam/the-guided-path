# Mounted Upon Guidance: The Farmer's Definition of Success

## Surah Luqman, Ayah 5

> أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ عَلَىٰ هُدًۭى مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ ۖ وَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ
>
> *Ulā'ika ʿalā hudan min rabbihim, wa ulā'ika humu-l-muflihūn.*
>
> **"Those are upon guidance from their Lord, and those — they are the truly successful."**

---

## Introduction: The Verdict at the End of the Sentence

Before we open this ayah, sit with the moment in which it is delivered.

Surah Luqman has just opened. The Book has been named *ḥakīm* — wise. A category of human being has been described: the *muḥsinīn*, those who do beauty in their worship. Allah has just said three things about them — they *establish* their prayer, they *give* zakāh, and most strikingly, they have *yaqīn* — certainty — about the Hereafter.

Then comes our ayah. A verdict. Like the last line of a court ruling, except the court is the cosmos and the judge is *al-Ḥakīm*.

But notice — this is also a hinge. The very next ayah will turn sharply and introduce a different group: people who **purchase idle speech** to lead others away from Allah's path. So this ayah is the last word about one group of human beings before the surah turns to look at their opposite. The light, before the shadow it casts.

Here is what should make you pause. Allah does not say in this verdict: *"They will be successful."* He does not say: *"They will be guided."*

He uses the present. **Right now.** Upon guidance. Right now. *They* — and the way He says "they" will undo us — *are the successful*.

[PAUSE]

Most of us live as though success and guidance are destinations. Something we will arrive at, when we are good enough, when we have done enough, when the prayer feels right and the heart finally softens. This ayah quietly contradicts that entire posture. It tells you that those people — yes, the ones still on the road — are *already* there.

But how can a person on a journey already be at the destination?

That is the question this ayah will answer. And it answers it with three words you would not expect: a doubled "those," a preposition that means "*on top of*," and a noun whose root means **to split the earth**.

Stay with me. The whole thing is about to open.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Repeated أُولَـٰٓئِكَ — "Those... Those..."

Before we look at the Arabic, let me show you something about the way human language works.

If I am describing a friend to you, I might say: *"That guy — he's brilliant, he's generous, and he's loyal."* I name him once, then list his qualities. That is the natural shape of a sentence.

But sometimes — usually when we are moved, when we want to underline something, when the person we are describing is being **separated out** from a crowd — we double the demonstrative. *"That guy. That guy. He's the one."*

The second "that guy" is not lazy repetition. It is **emphasis through return**. We bring the listener's attention back to the subject before delivering the verdict. We are saying: *don't forget who I'm talking about. Look again.*

Arabic does this too, with a beautiful precision. And Allah does it here.

Listen to the structure of our ayah:

> **أُولَـٰٓئِكَ** عَلَىٰ هُدًۭى مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ
>
> **وَأُولَـٰٓئِكَ** هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ

**"Those** are upon guidance from their Lord — and **those** — they are the successful."

The word أُولَـٰٓئِكَ — *ulā'ika* — appears **twice**. It is a plural demonstrative, the equivalent of "those ones over there." Allah could have said: *"Those are upon guidance from their Lord and are the successful."* One demonstrative, two predicates. That is shorter, simpler, faster.

He did not.

He stopped the sentence. He went back to the beginning. He pointed again. He said *ulā'ika* a second time.

Notice something. What does it feel like when you are in a room and someone points across it once, then turns to you, then turns and points *again*? It feels like they are saying: *do not lose sight of who I am talking about. The first thing I said, and the second thing I am about to say, are about the same people. They are not two compliments stacked. They are one identity from two angles.*

The classical Arabic rhetoricians call this *iʿādat al-ism al-mubham* — the return of the indicating word — and they note it does something specific. It tells the listener that what follows is not merely a *consequence* of what came before. It is the *same reality* described from a second face.

[PAUSE]

So what are the two faces here?

**Face one:** they are *upon guidance from their Lord*.
**Face two:** they are *the truly successful*.

Allah is not saying: *They are guided, AND, by the way, they are also successful.* He is saying: **being upon guidance from your Lord IS what success looks like.** The two are not stacked. They are the same thing, seen twice.

This is what the doubled demonstrative is doing. It is pulling apart what looks like a list — guidance plus success — and revealing that there is no "plus." There is only one reality. The list is an illusion of grammar.

Sit with what this means for a moment. If you have ever felt that you were religiously committed but not "successful" in some other sense — that you prayed but the career did not lift, that you stayed close to the Book but the marriage did not bloom, that you held the line but the world did not reward you — this ayah's grammar is whispering something to you. It is whispering that what you thought were two ledgers were never two ledgers. The guidance *is* the success. The success *is* the guidance. The "and" is a mercy of language, but it is not a partition of reality.

That is what the doubled أُولَـٰٓئِكَ is doing.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: عَلَىٰ هُدًۭى — "*Upon* a Guidance"

Now look at the next phrase: **عَلَىٰ هُدًۭى** — *ʿalā hudan*.

Translated: "upon guidance." But that English flattens two distinct things, and we need both of them.

### The preposition عَلَىٰ

Before we open this, let me teach you something about how Arabic prepositions work.

In English, we say "in love," "in trouble," "in faith." Our default container preposition is *in*. We tend to imagine emotional and spiritual states as places we are inside of. We are *immersed* in the state.

Arabic has *fī* — "in" — which works the same way. You can say *fī al-jannah* (in the garden), *fī al-īmān* (in faith). It is the immersion preposition.

But Allah did not use *fī* here. He used **ʿalā** — "upon." And *ʿalā* in Arabic does not mean immersion. It means **elevation, mounting, riding**. You are *ʿalā* the horse. You are *ʿalā* the throne. You are *ʿalā* the mountain.

To be *ʿalā* something is to be *above it, carried by it, lifted by it*. It is not the posture of someone drowning in a state. It is the posture of someone **mounted on a mount**.

Notice something. When you say someone is *in* faith, the image is of a person standing inside a room. They are surrounded. The state encloses them.

But when Allah says these people are *ʿalā hudan* — upon guidance — the image flips. The guidance is not surrounding them. The guidance is **underneath them, carrying them**. They are not inside it. They are *on top of it*. It is bearing them somewhere.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The same construction — *ʿalā hudan min rabbihim* — appears at the beginning of the Quran, in Surah al-Baqarah ayah 5, also describing the muttaqīn. It is a phrase Allah reserves for those He is pleased with. And in every appearance, the metaphor holds: they are not just believers; they are **carried**. The guidance is a mount.

What does this open up for us?

A person who is *in* a state can lose it by stepping out. A person who is *upon* a mount can lose it by falling off — but as long as they are on it, **the mount does the work**. The mount moves. The mount knows the path. The mount carries them forward even when they themselves are tired.

This is the gentleness of this ayah. The believer in this image is not a hero striding through a forest by their own strength. The believer is mounted on something *Allah Himself* placed beneath them. The journey is real. The work of staying on the mount is real. But the strength carrying them is not their own.

If you have ever felt that you cannot keep going — that the practice is too heavy, that the consistency is breaking — this preposition is for you. The grammar is reminding you that *you are not supposed to be carrying this. It is supposed to be carrying you.*

[PAUSE]

### The noun هُدًۭى — and its silent indefiniteness

Now look at the noun itself: **هُدًۭى** — *hudan*. Guidance.

It is indefinite. Arabic has the definite article *al-* (the), and it is absent here. In Arabic grammar, when a noun is left indefinite where you would expect definiteness, it carries a specific meaning. The classical balāghah scholars call it **tafkhīm** — magnification, exaltation. It is the difference between saying *"the man"* and *"a man — a real man."* The indefinite version is bigger, not smaller.

So *hudan* — un-articled — is not "a guidance" in the diminished English sense. It is *a guidance of such magnitude that to name it specifically would shrink it.* A guidance of a quality words cannot circumscribe.

Allah does not say they are *ʿalā al-hudā* — upon *the* guidance, as if it were one fixed thing. He says *ʿalā hudan* — upon *a* guidance, a guidance of immense and unspecified vastness, **a guidance whose ceiling you cannot see**.

Why does this matter?

Because if guidance were *the* guidance — a single, finite, completed object — then once you had it, there would be nothing left to receive. You could plateau. You could arrive.

But because guidance is *hudan* — vast, ongoing, unfinished — there is **always more of it above you**. You are upon it, yes. But it extends. It rises. It deepens. The believer is never told they have arrived. They are told they are *mounted on a vehicle whose road is infinite*.

This is why those people who establish prayer and give zakāh and have yaqīn are described as being **on a journey, not at a destination** — and yet they are *already successful*. Because the journey itself, being *carried* by something so vast, is the success.

## Element Three: ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ — The Farmer's Word for Success

Now we come to the word the whole ayah has been building toward: **ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ** — *al-muflihūn*. Usually translated "the successful."

Translation is doing this word an injustice. We need to slow down.

### The shape of the word

*Muflihūn* is a plural active participle on the **Form IV** pattern (*afʿala* → *mufʿil*). Form IV in Arabic typically carries a sense of *causation* or *bringing-about*. To be a *muflih* is not just to passively *be* a thing called *falāh* — it is to be one who **brings falāh into being**, who actively cultivates it, who makes it happen.

So before we even get to the meaning of *falāh* itself, the morphology has told us something: these are people whose success is *worked for*. Cultivated. Caused. They are not the lucky. They are the laboring.

### The root: ف ل ح

Here is where the world opens.

The classical Arabic lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in *al-Mufradāt* — preserve for us the original meaning of the root ف ل ح (*f-l-ḥ*). And it is not "success" in any abstract sense.

It is **to split open the earth**.

The فلاح (*fallāḥ*) in classical Arabic is **the farmer**. The one who splits the soil with his plow. The one who breaks the surface so the seed can go in. *Falāḥ*, success, the word itself — comes from this image.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. Allah is naming the deeply religious — those of prayer, zakāh, and certainty in the Hereafter — using a word that originally meant **the people who work the dirt**.

Why?

Because the farmer's success is structurally different from every other kind of success the world recognizes.

Consider what the farmer does. He breaks the earth — which is hard, sweaty, unglamorous labor. He buries something precious (a seed) in the dark, where nobody can see it. Then he waits. He waters. He weeds. He waits more. For weeks, the field looks **empty**. To any visitor, the farmer is doing nothing. To any visitor, the field has nothing in it.

Then, in a season Allah determines and the farmer cannot rush, the green emerges. And later, the fruit. And later still, the harvest.

**The farmer's success is invisible until it is total.** And it cannot be rushed. And it cannot be faked. And it depends on something the farmer himself cannot manufacture — the soil's response, the rain, the sun, the timing. The farmer can do everything right and a drought can take it. The farmer can do everything right and *Allah* must still send the rain.

This is the word Allah chose for the success of the believer.

Not *fā'izūn* — the prize-winners, the trophy-takers. That word exists. He did not use it here.
Not *rābiḥūn* — the profit-makers, the gainers in trade. That word exists. He did not use it here.

**Muflihūn.** The farmers. The earth-splitters. The patient cultivators of a harvest they cannot see yet.

[PAUSE]

What this is telling us, then, is something the modern world cannot easily hear. It is telling us that real success is:

- **Slow.** The farmer does not get the fruit on day one.
- **Invisible in its middle stages.** The field looks empty when the seed is doing its work.
- **Dependent on the Sender of rain.** The farmer plants, but Allah grows.
- **Total at the end.** When the harvest comes, it comes whole.

And it is telling us that the people upon guidance from their Lord — the people of prayer and zakāh and certainty — *are* this kind of success. Not loud. Not visible. Not instant. But *real*, and *cumulative*, and *coming*.

### The definite article and the separating pronoun

One last linguistic thing, and then we will move into the themes.

Look at the construction in the second half: **وَأُولَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ**.

Three pieces here:
1. *Ulā'ika* — those (the repeated demonstrative)
2. *Humu* — they (a *separating pronoun*, *ḍamīr al-faṣl*)
3. *Al-muflihūn* — the successful ones (definite, with *al-*)

A separating pronoun in Arabic — that little *humu* between subject and predicate — does one thing: it creates **exclusivity** (*qaṣr*). It restricts. It says: *not anyone else.*

And the definite article on *al-muflihūn* says: *the* successful — the category itself.

Put it all together and the sentence is saying, with full force: **"And those — *they alone* — are *the* successful."**

Not: *they are among the successful*. Not: *they are successful too*. But: **the category of true success contains exactly these people, and the door is shut.**

That is a strong claim. It is a claim that Allah, who knows what success actually is, has defined the set of the successful, and the only people in it are the ones described in the previous ayahs — those who establish prayer, give zakāh, and hold certainty about the Hereafter.

Every other definition of success the world will offer you is, on the testimony of this ayah, **outside the category**.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Bridge to the Themes

So look at what the linguistic construction has shown us.

The doubled demonstrative said: *guidance and success are not two things — they are one identity seen from two angles.*

The preposition *ʿalā* said: *the believer is not inside guidance, they are mounted upon it — carried by something Allah placed beneath them.*

The indefinite *hudan* said: *this guidance has no ceiling — there is always more of it above you.*

The word *muflihūn* said: *real success is a farmer's word — slow, hidden, dependent on Allah's rain, total in the end.*

The separating pronoun and the definite article said: *and this category — the muflihūn — is the only category of real success that exists.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being is supposed to live a life.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Hatred of Latency, and Why It Cannot Be Believed

There is a problem in modern life that has no good name. Let me try to describe it.

You order something online and it comes the next day. You text someone and you watch the three dots — and if those three dots take more than fifteen seconds, you feel something close to anger. You open an app and if it takes two seconds to load, the back of your mind registers it as broken. The whole engineering of modern life is to **shrink the gap between desire and fulfillment** until that gap is functionally zero.

Now bring this nervous system into the religious life.

You pray and you do not feel anything. You make duʿā for years about the same thing and nothing seems to change. You read Quran and the heart that you were promised would soften does not soften — not today, not the way you wanted. You give zakāh and your finances do not visibly improve. You stay away from a sin and the reward is not handed to you on the next exhale.

The nervous system that has been trained on next-day delivery now meets the slow, slow soil of the religious life — and **panics**. Because nothing is happening. Because the field looks empty.

This is the place most believers actually live. Not in dramatic doubt. In *latency-induced erosion*. The slow leak of conviction caused by the gap between effort and visible reward.

[PAUSE]

Now let the ayah land.

Allah does not call the successful people *the fast ones*. He does not call them *the immediately-rewarded*. He calls them **muflihūn** — *farmers*. He chooses, deliberately, the one occupation in the human catalogue whose entire structure is **the dignified bearing of latency**.

The farmer has accepted, before he ever picks up a plow, that there is a season between the work and the harvest. The farmer does not check the soil every fifteen minutes for the seed to sprout. The farmer is not panicking that the field looks empty in week two. The farmer **trusts the process Allah built into the soil itself**.

Real spiritual maturity is the same posture. It is the willingness to do the work of splitting the earth — the prayer, the zakāh, the discipline of yaqīn — and then to **wait** without panic. Not because nothing is happening. But because **what is happening cannot be seen yet**.

This is why the ayah right before our ayah says they have *yaqīn* about the Hereafter (31:4). Yaqīn — certainty. The certainty is what makes the latency bearable. The farmer can wait because he has yaqīn that the season turns. The believer can wait because he has yaqīn that the Day comes.

Without yaqīn, latency feels like abandonment. With yaqīn, latency feels like **incubation**.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you read your own spiritual life. Stop demanding that today's prayer pay out today. Stop measuring this week's worship by this week's emotional weather. The field looks empty in the middle stages. **The field is supposed to look empty in the middle stages.** That is the design.

What you owe is the daily breaking of the soil. The slow, ordinary work — the rakʿahs, the dhikr, the patience, the not-doing-the-haram, the choosing of the better word when the worse word would have been easier. You owe the *work of the muflih*.

The harvest belongs to the One who sends the rain. And He does not forget His farmers.

## Theme Two: The Difference Between Walking and Being Carried

Here is a posture that almost every person who tries to be religious eventually arrives at. You wake up exhausted by your own iman. The discipline feels like dragging a heavy weight uphill. You wonder how the truly pious do it. You wonder if you are simply not made for this.

And underneath that exhaustion is a quiet, unexamined belief: *I am the one doing this. My faith is something I generate. My consistency is a function of my willpower. If I run out, the whole thing collapses.*

That belief is what *ʿalā hudan* came to dismantle.

[PAUSE]

Think back to the preposition. The believer in this ayah is not described as *walking* through guidance. They are described as **mounted on it**. The guidance is the mount. They are the rider.

A rider gets tired. A rider can grow weary. But a rider on a strong mount does not provide the locomotion. The mount provides the locomotion. The rider's job is to *stay on*.

Now, staying on is not nothing. Anyone who has ever ridden a horse will tell you it is its own discipline. You have to keep your seat. You have to balance. You have to not jump off when you see something shiny on the side of the road. The work is real.

But the work is not *walking the whole way on your own legs*.

And this is what so many believers get wrong. They think iman is a long march where they themselves are the engine. They wake up every day asking, *do I have enough fuel for today?* — and then they grade their faith by how much fuel they had.

But the ayah is offering a different image. **You are not the engine. You are the rider.** The engine is the guidance Allah has placed beneath you. Your job is to stay on, to keep your balance, to not throw yourself off in a moment of pride or despair.

[PAUSE]

What does it mean to *fall off*?

It means to forget the mount is from your *Rabb*. Notice — Allah said *min rabbihim*, from their *Lord*, their *Nurturer*. Not *min Allah*, from *Allah* the name. The name *Rabb* is the name of the One who develops you, who raises you from one state to the next, who is patient with your growth. To fall off the mount is to forget that the One who put you on it is **invested in your arrival**.

And what does it mean to *stay on*?

To stay on is the daily, small choices that the previous ayahs described. The prayer. The zakāh. The yaqīn. None of those are spiritual heroism. They are the seat you keep on the mount. They are not how you generate the journey. They are how you do not throw yourself off it.

[PAUSE]

So here is the inversion this theme offers.

Most religious exhaustion is the exhaustion of *thinking you are walking when you are supposed to be riding*. Of trying to generate by willpower what was supposed to be received as a gift. Of forgetting that the *hudan* — the vast, ceilingless, magnificent guidance — is not something you are inside of working to escape. It is something you are *on top of*, carrying you toward a Lord who has been carrying you all along.

The cure for that exhaustion is not more willpower. It is the small daily act of remembering whose mount you are on. *Min rabbihim.* From their Lord. The Nurturer is the one moving the journey forward. You are tasked only with not falling off.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the prayer. Tomorrow's prayer is not a wall you climb. It is the saddle of the mount you are already on. When you stand for fajr, you are not generating closeness to Allah by your effort. You are *taking your seat* on something He placed beneath you long before you woke up.

The relief in that, if you let it in, is enormous.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture of the Whole Ayah

Now that we have walked through it, look back at the shape of what Allah said.

> **أُولَـٰٓئِكَ** عَلَىٰ هُدًۭى مِّن رَّبِّهِمْ
> **وَأُولَـٰٓئِكَ** هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ

Two halves. Each begins with **those**. The first half describes a *position* — mounted upon guidance from their Lord. The second half delivers a *verdict* — they alone are the truly successful.

But after our journey, we can see what the doubled demonstrative was doing. The two halves are not stacked descriptions. They are one reality from two sides:

**Position:** carried by a guidance with no ceiling, from a Lord who is developing them.
**Verdict:** they are the farmers — the slow, patient, real-success people, and they alone hold that name.

The position *is* the verdict. The verdict *is* the position. To be mounted on guidance from your Rabb **is** to be one of the muflihūn. There is no third thing to wait for.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar said: *guidance is something you are carried on, not something you generate.*
The themes said: *stop walking; you were always supposed to be riding.*

The grammar said: *muflihūn is a farmer's word.*
The themes said: *stop demanding the harvest in week two of the planting.*

The grammar said: *the demonstrative is doubled because position and verdict are the same.*
The themes said: *you do not earn success by arriving — you live success by staying mounted.*

This is the whole structure of the religious life in one ayah. **The mount is from Him. The work is staying on. The harvest is His to send. The verdict is already pronounced over those who keep their seat.**

## Questions to Carry

Take these with you, slowly:

1. Where in my life have I confused **walking** with **being carried** — and exhausted myself accordingly?

2. What is the field in my life that currently looks empty — and am I in the panicked, checking-every-day posture, or the patient *muflih* posture?

3. What does it change to call the One who placed the mount beneath me **Rabb** — my Nurturer, the one developing me — rather than just *Allah* in the abstract?

4. If I had to point across a room and say *ulā'ika — those* — about the people whose lives carry the structure this ayah describes, who would I be pointing at? And could anyone honestly point at me?

5. When the harvest from years of small, quiet worship has not yet visibly arrived, do I keep splitting the soil — or do I put down the plow?

## The One-Sentence Distillation

**Real success is not a prize you arrive at — it is the patient farmer's seat on a guidance your Lord placed beneath you, and those who keep that seat are already, right now, the only people the word "successful" actually fits.**

## The Closing Invitation

If something in you tightened during this reflection — at the farmer image, at the *ʿalā* image, at the doubled *those* — pay attention to that tightening. It is the place the ayah was speaking to.

You do not need to overhaul your life tonight. You need only one small adjustment of posture. Tomorrow, when you pray fajr, do not stand up as a person *generating* their religion. Stand up as a person *taking their seat* on a mount that has been carrying them all along. Stand up as a *muflih* — a farmer with a plow, splitting the daily soil, trusting the slow rain of a *Rabb* who has never forgotten a single farmer of His.

That is the whole shift. Not bigger. Not louder. Just true.

## A Brief Du'a

اللّٰهُمَّ اجْعَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَلَىٰ هُدًۭى مِّنكَ — لَا نَحْمِلُهُ بَلْ يَحْمِلُنَا.
وَٱجْعَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلْمُفْلِحِينَ — صَابِرِينَ عَلَىٰ شَقِّ ٱلْأَرْضِ، مُنْتَظِرِينَ غَيْثَكَ، وَاثِقِينَ بِحَصَادِكَ.
يَا رَبِّ، لَا تَدَعْنَا نَسْقُطُ عَنِ ٱلْمَطِيَّةِ ٱلَّتِي أَجْلَسْتَنَا عَلَيْهَا.

*O Allah, make us among those who are upon guidance from You — not carrying it, but carried by it. Make us among the muflihūn — patient with the splitting of the earth, awaiting Your rain, certain of Your harvest. O my Lord, do not let us fall from the mount You have placed us upon.*

Āmīn.
