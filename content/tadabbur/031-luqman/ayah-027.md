# The Ocean With No Shore

## Surah Luqman, Ayah 27

---

### The Words

**Arabic:**

> [ayah:31:27] وَلَوْ أَنَّمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ مِن شَجَرَةٍ أَقْلَـٰمٌ وَٱلْبَحْرُ يَمُدُّهُۥ مِنۢ بَعْدِهِۦ سَبْعَةُ أَبْحُرٍ مَّا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَـٰتُ ٱللَّهِ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa law annamā fī l-arḍi min shajaratin aqlāmun wa-l-baḥru yamudduhū min baʿdihī sabʿatu abḥurin mā nafidat kalimātu Llāh — inna Llāha ʿAzīzun Ḥakīm.*

**Translation:**
"And if whatever trees there are upon the earth were pens, and the sea — replenished thereafter by seven more seas — were ink, the words of Allah would still not be exhausted. Indeed, Allah is Mighty, Wise."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World This Ayah Walks Into

Before we open this sentence, sit for a moment in the world it first dropped into.

Mecca. Late Meccan period. The Prophet ﷺ has been reciting the Quran for years now. And one of the loudest accusations being thrown at him — repeated across more than one surah — is some version of: *this is just words. This is the speech of a man. We could produce something like it if we wanted to. These are old fables, dressed up.*

There is also a quieter accusation, woven through the same surahs: *we already have what we need. Our fathers had answers. Our poetry has beauty. Our gods have stories. Why do we need more words?*

Surah Luqman has been answering both, but answering them sideways. It hasn't been arguing. It has been *displaying.* It opened by calling itself **a guidance and a mercy for those who do good.** Then it turned and showed us a man — Luqman — who was given **wisdom** by Allah, and who used that wisdom to whisper to his son. *Yā bunayya...* O my little son. The whole surah has been a slow, deliberate display of what wisdom looks like when it is real and what noise looks like when it is empty.

And then, just a few ayahs before our verse, the surah lifts its gaze and starts pointing — *did you not see how Allah subjected to you what is in the heavens and the earth, and lavished upon you His blessings, both the seen and the unseen?* (31:20). Sun, moon, ships, mountains, the sea splitting under wind, the wind itself. Sign after sign after sign. Not as decoration. As **proof that the One being denied is the One whose handwriting is on everything they're standing on.**

Then comes our ayah.

It is going to take that entire pointed-at world — every tree, every drop of every sea — and turn it into a *pen and ink set.* And then find that it isn't enough. Not even close.

### The Hook

Notice something before we even open the words.

The argument the Meccans were making — *we could produce speech like this* — was an argument about a finite thing. Even the longest poem ends. Even the best orator sits down. Speech, in the human world, is something with a bottom. Something you can run out of.

And the response, when it comes, doesn't argue. It doesn't say *you couldn't produce this.* It says something far more dangerous to a person who thinks they've heard everything:

**You could not even contain it. Not if you used everything.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with how strange that is. We're going to spend the next forty minutes trying to feel the size of that strangeness.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: A Single Tree, And Therefore Every Tree

Open the ayah and look at the second clause:

> مِن شَجَرَةٍ أَقْلَامٌ
> *min shajaratin aqlāmun*
> "of (any) tree — pens"

Now, before we touch this, let me teach you a small but important feature of how Arabic carries meaning.

In English, when we want to say "every single one without exception," we say it with quantifier words: *every, all, each, any, the entirety of.* We pile up adjectives. *Every single solitary one of them.*

Arabic does this differently. One of its quietest and most powerful moves is to take an indefinite singular noun and place it after a partitive **مِن** — *min* — meaning *from / of.* The construction *min* + indefinite singular doesn't just mean "a tree." In context like this, it generalizes. It scoops up **every single tree, one by one, without exception, none excluded.** The grammarians call this the **min al-istighrāqiyyah** — the *min* of comprehensive scope.

So when Allah says *min shajaratin* — He is not saying "if some trees were pens." He is not even saying "if all the trees were pens" in the bulk sense. He is doing something more intimate. He is taking *every single tree on the earth* — the date palm in your grandfather's courtyard, the olive tree on the hillside in Palestine, the redwood in California you've never seen, the mangrove tucked into the river delta — every individual one — and saying: *every one of them, considered one by one, made into pens.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what happens to your mind when it tries to do this.

You start counting and you immediately stop, because you can't count. You shift to bulk: *the forests of the world.* But the grammar resists you. *Min shajaratin* — singular. The verse will not let you abstract. It wants you to feel each tree, and then collapse under the weight.

This is the first move of the ayah: it makes the finite **feel** infinite. Every tree on earth, every individual one, processed into pens. And the grammar has just made you intuit that this is already a number you cannot hold.

Stay with this — because we have not yet reached the impossibility. We have only reached the *largest finite thing the verse will let you imagine.* The pens.

Now the verse adds the ink.

### Element Two: A Sea That Keeps Refilling — And A Verb That Will Not Sit Down

Look at the next clause:

> وَالْبَحْرُ يَمُدُّهُ مِن بَعْدِهِ سَبْعَةُ أَبْحُرٍ
> *wa-l-baḥru yamudduhū min baʿdihī sabʿatu abḥur*
> "and the sea — replenished thereafter by seven more seas"

There are two grammatical things happening here that I want to name, because together they do something the English translation cannot do.

**The first thing is the verb itself.** *Yamuddu* — from the root **م-د-د** — *to extend, to stretch, to supply continuously, to keep replenishing.* You may already know its sister word, *madad* — the supply that an army keeps coming up behind the front line. *Madad* is what arrives so the front never runs out. *Yamuddu* is the act of keeping that supply flowing.

Now: what tense is *yamuddu*?

It is **present.** Mudāriʿ. Ongoing. *Is replenishing. Keeps supplying. Right now, and now, and now.*

Notice that the verse did not say "and seven seas were added to it" — past, completed, finished. It said the sea **keeps being supplied** by seven more. The supply line is open. The faucet is on. *Now.* And again *now.*

**The second thing is the number.** *Sabʿatu abḥur* — seven seas.

The classical commentators are clear that **seven**, in this idiom, is not a count. In Arabic — as in many of the Semitic languages — seven is the number of *completeness, of multitude, of an unbounded set.* When the Arabs said *I have warned you a thousand times,* they hadn't counted. When the Quran says *if you ask forgiveness for them seventy times* (9:80), it does not mean the seventy-first time would have worked. Seven, seventy, seven hundred — these are the language of *however many it would take.*

So the ayah is doing something that, in English, is almost untranslatable. It is saying:

> *The sea — and behind it however many more seas it would take to keep it full — keeps refilling.*

Put the two grammatical features together. Every tree on earth as pens. The sea as ink. And — here is the genius — that ink **never runs low**, because the supply is always coming. The image is engineered so that you cannot find an exit. There is no point at which the writing instrument fails. Pens, infinite for all practical purposes. Ink, *actually* infinite by ongoing replenishment.

[PAUSE]

And after the verse has constructed for you the largest writing apparatus the human imagination can hold — every tree on earth, an ever-refilling ocean of ink — it drops a single verb on the entire structure and breaks it.

> مَّا نَفِدَتْ كَلِمَاتُ ٱللَّهِ
> *mā nafidat kalimātu Llāh*
> "the words of Allah would not be exhausted."

**Mā nafidat.** Past tense. *Were not exhausted.*

Why past?

Because — and this is what makes the Arabic almost laugh — the *result is already settled.* The exhaustion is being spoken of as if the experiment had already been run. Even though the supply is still flowing in present tense (*yamudduhū*), the failure of the supply to keep up is in the *past* tense (*nafidat*). It is over before it began. It is decided.

Hear the architecture once:

- The pens — every tree, individually — *finite, but engineered to feel infinite.*
- The ink — the sea, *continuously* refilled by seven more — *actually infinite, in ongoing time.*
- The result — the words of Allah were *already* not exhausted. **Past.** **Done.**

The verse is not saying *they would not run out.* It is saying *they did not run out — even with all of that. Try it. The trial is over. You lost. The ink is gone. The pens are dust. Allah's words are still here.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: What Is a "Word" of Allah?

Now we come to the word the whole verse pivots on.

> كَلِمَاتُ ٱللَّهِ
> *kalimātu Llāh*
> "the words of Allah"

In Arabic, the word *kalimah* — كلمة — has more than one valid plural. There is *kalim* (كَلِم) — used for *speech as a flowing whole.* And there is *kalimāt* (كَلِمَات) — the feminine plural — used for *individuated, distinct words.*

The verse uses *kalimāt* — distinct ones. Countable units. Specific, individuated speech.

Now, this is where we have to walk slowly, because the classical scholars, when they reached this word, took different paths to its meaning. And their paths are not contradictions. They are camera angles on the same reality.

**One angle:** *Kalimāt Allah* refers to His speech in the linguistic sense — His revealed Books, the Quran, the Tawrāh, the Injīl, all His communicated speech. On this reading, the ayah is saying: **My speech to creation is so vast that no created instrument can transcribe it.** What you have in front of you — this Mushaf — is one slice of an infinite reservoir.

**A second angle:** *Kalimāt Allah* refers to His commands by which things come into being — the *kun* (be) by which He creates, the decrees by which He sustains. Recall the Quran's recurring formula: *His command, when He wills a thing, is only to say to it: be — and it is.* On this reading, every leaf, every breath, every star, every cell-division, every molecule of water in that imagined ocean — is itself an enacted *word* of Allah. To write down His words would be to write down the entire ledger of created and decreed reality.

**A third angle:** *Kalimāt Allah* refers to His knowledge — the meanings, the sciences, the wisdom — that *could* be expressed if He chose to express them. On this reading, the ayah is pointing to the depth of what Allah *knows*, of which any expression is a small surface.

Do you see what happens when you let all three sit at once?

The trees are not enough to write down even *one* of these meanings — not the speech, not the decrees, not the knowledge. And the verse leaves it *unspecified* which sense of *kalimāt* is meant. Why? Because all three are true and all three break the same instrument. **Whichever way you take the word, the pens snap.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge Between Linguistic and Thematic

We have done three things:

We have watched the grammar take every individual tree and pile them up into pens. We have watched a present-tense verb keep an ocean topped up forever, only to be broken by a past-tense verb that says *the trial is already over.* And we have seen that the *very word* on which the verse pivots — *kalimāt* — is a word large enough to mean three different infinities at once.

But notice — this is not a verse about Allah's *speech* in the abstract.

This is a verse about what it does to a human being to stand in front of speech that has no bottom.

Now let us ask what this reveals not about words — but about how we *live.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Human Disease of Things That Run Out

Let me start somewhere small.

You know the feeling of finishing a really good show. You watch the last episode. There is a moment where the credits roll and you sit there, slightly empty, and the first thought that comes is: *what now?*

You know the feeling of scrolling. The reels load. You watch one. You watch ten. You watch a hundred. And after a while, somewhere behind your eyes, a tiredness arrives that has nothing to do with sleep. It is the tiredness of *having reached the bottom* of everything you were just consuming. There is nothing here. There was never going to be anything here. The fountain was shallow.

You know the feeling of being a young person who has "seen everything." Every meme has been done. Every joke has been told. Every relationship looks like every other relationship someone older than you went through. Every job is the same job. Every city is the same city. The world has been pre-explained, pre-experienced, pre-summarized. Nothing is new.

This feeling has a clinical name in some literatures — *anhedonia*, the loss of ability to feel pleasure. It has a more popular name — *burnout*. It has an older Islamic name — *qaswat al-qalb*, the hardness of heart. But the underlying physics is the same:

**Everything in the dunya is a fountain with a bottom. Sooner or later, you reach it.**

And human beings are creatures who *cannot live well at the bottom of fountains.* We need depth. Not novelty — depth. Novelty is the cheap substitute. We keep moving from fountain to fountain, hoping the next one will be deeper, when actually all of them, *by the nature of being created*, have a floor.

[PAUSE]

Now turn back to the ayah.

What is Allah doing? He is not handing you a deeper fountain. He is telling you that **He, the Speaker, has no bottom.** And by extension, His speech — which is the only door creatures have to His meaning — *has no bottom either.*

This is why the Quran can be read by a child of seven and a scholar of seventy and *neither one is reading the same book.* This is why the same ayah you've heard a thousand times can, on the thousand and first time, break you open. This is why some of the greatest scholars in Islamic history — al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, Ibn ʿAshur — wrote tens of thousands of pages on this Book and, on their deathbeds, said *I have not begun.* They were not being humble. They were being **accurate.** They were standing on the shore that the ayah is describing. They had used up the trees and the seas of their own life. The words had not run out.

Notice what this does to the way you live.

If you take this verse seriously, you can never again say *I've already read that surah.* You may have read the *ink and paper* of it. You have not read the *words of Allah* in it. The ink ran out in your hands. His speech did not.

A practical implication, immediate and concrete: **the next time you sit with a passage of the Quran you think you know, sit with it for fifteen extra minutes, and actively look for the thing you have not seen.** Treat your prior reading as the empty pen and the dry ocean. Treat the ayah itself as the inexhaustible reality that you have only barely touched. You will find something. The verse promises this — by promising that you cannot, in principle, exhaust what is there.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Asymmetry, Or — What It Means That Tools Cannot Hold the Maker

Here is the strangest thing about this verse, and it took me a long time to see it.

The verse does not contrast Allah with humans. It contrasts Allah with **all of His creation, used as instruments to describe Him.**

Read the architecture again. The trees are His. The sea is His. The seven extra seas are His. The pens, the ink, the writing surface — all of it is created. Allah is not contrasting His infinitude with the finitude of *people.* He is contrasting it with **the entire created order, marshalled at maximum capacity, deployed as a single transcription machine.**

And the machine fails.

Now ask yourself what this implies — and walk slowly here.

It implies that **created things cannot, even in principle, contain their Creator.** Not because they are weak. Not because they are too few. But because of an *asymmetry written into the structure of being itself.* Finite things, no matter how many, no matter how multiplied, cannot capture the infinite. This is not a quantitative statement. It is a *qualitative* one. You cannot reach infinity by adding more finitude. You can fill the seven seas with ink, and the difference between what you have written and what remains unwritten is not a *bigger number* — it is *the entire infinite gap.*

This is why the Prophet ﷺ taught his Companions to say, when they tried to praise Allah: *lā uḥṣī thanāʾan ʿalayk, anta kamā athnayta ʿalā nafsik* — *I cannot enumerate Your praise; You are as You have praised Yourself.* He was saying: *the only being capable of describing You — is You. My instruments are not enough. The instruments of all creation are not enough.*

Now — here is the door this opens for the soul, and it is one of the most healing doors in the entire Quran.

[PAUSE]

If the entire created order cannot capture Allah, then **the smallness of your worship, the inadequacy of your remembrance, the poverty of your tongue when it tries to thank Him — none of this is a personal failure of yours.** It is the structural condition of being a creature. Your *dhikr* will always be smaller than His worthiness of *dhikr.* Your *shukr* will always be smaller than His worthiness of *shukr.* Your love will always be smaller than His worthiness of being loved. *This is not a bug. This is the math of being created.*

What this should free you of is **the despair of the perfectionist worshipper** — the one who thinks *my prayer is so weak, my reading is so shallow, my love is so flickering, why bother?* The ayah answers: *if every tree on earth and every sea, used at maximum capacity, would not be enough to write down the words of the One you are trying to praise — what made you think your inadequate worship was unique?*

The asymmetry was never your fault. **It was always the truth.**

And what it should give you, in return, is **the joyful humility of the small worshipper** — the one who knows that the pen in their hand is dry and the ocean is empty and the words are still there, and so the only sane response is to keep writing, and to know that the infinite One is the one who reads and accepts the writing, not the one who measures it against His own scale. Because if He measured it against His own scale, no creature would ever have prayed.

A practical implication: **stop measuring the quality of your worship against an internal standard of "this should have been better."** That standard is a lie. The truer standard is *this is what a finite creature can offer to an infinite Lord, and He, in His mercy, accepts what we cannot make adequate.* Pray the prayer you can pray. Read the Quran in the Arabic you have. Make the duʿāʾ in the language you have. The trees and seas will run out before His words do — and they will run out before your sincerity does too.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Look back at the whole ayah, with everything we have done in view.

Allah builds a writing apparatus out of His own creation. Every tree as pens. The sea as ink. The supply of ink continuously refilled by seven more seas — *yamudduhū*, present tense, ongoing — so that the apparatus can never break down for lack of resources. He maximizes the finite to its absolute ceiling.

And then He drops one past-tense verb on it. **Mā nafidat.** *Were not exhausted.*

And then He closes with two divine names that are not decoration:

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ حَكِيمٌ
> *Inna Llāha ʿAzīzun Ḥakīm.*

**ʿAzīz** — the One whose might has no rival. *No one can match the supply. No one can compete with the source.* The infinitude of His speech is a function of His incomparable might.

**Ḥakīm** — the Wise. *And no one of those infinite words is wasted.* This matters. It would be one thing to be told that Allah's speech is infinite. That alone could be terrifying — an infinite torrent of meaning. But it is *infinite **and** wise.* Every word He has spoken — every command, every revelation, every decree — is placed exactly where wisdom places it. Nothing in His speech is noise. Nothing is filler. The infinitude is not chaos. It is **infinite ḥikmah.**

And remember the surah we are in — Sūrat **Luqmān**, the surah of wisdom transmitted from a father to a son, the surah of *ḥikmah* given as a divine gift. The whole surah has been about the value of true wisdom against the noise of false speech. And here, at the end, the surah signs the lesson: *the source from which all wisdom flows is itself inexhaustible.* Luqman could pass *some* wisdom to his son. Allah's wisdom cannot be passed in full to all the sons of Adam combined. Even with every tree as pens.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar — every individual tree, an ocean continuously refilled, a verb whose result is already settled in the past — does the same work as the human reality: that we live among things that run out, and we are made for One who does not.

The asymmetry of the writing apparatus failing — created instruments unable to hold the Creator — is the same truth as the worshipper's small prayer being acceptable not because it is large, but because it is *honest.* The instrument was never going to be adequate. That is not the disqualification. That is **the human condition. And He knows it. And He accepts the small offering anyway.**

### Questions to Carry

1. *What in my life have I treated as a fountain with a bottom — and what would change if I treated the Quran as the only thing in my world that does not have one?*
2. *When I sit down to recite or read, am I bringing the assumption "I have been here before" — and what if that assumption is the only thing keeping me from seeing what is actually here?*
3. *Where in my worship am I being a perfectionist — measuring myself against an adequacy that no creature could meet — and how would my prayer change if I let that standard die?*
4. *If every tree on earth as pens and every sea as ink would not be enough to write what Allah has to say — what makes me think the answer to my confusion is not in His speech, but only that I have not yet sat with His speech long enough to find it?*
5. *What does it do to my heart, today, to know that the Speaker of the Quran cannot be exhausted — and so the Quran cannot be exhausted — and so my relationship with this Book has, in principle, no ceiling?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah, in a single sentence, takes the entire created order, turns it into a writing instrument at its absolute maximum capacity, finds it not nearly enough to record His speech, and tells the human being holding the Mushaf in their hands: **the Book in your lap is a window onto an ocean that has no shore — so do not read it like something you have already finished.**

### The Closing Invitation

What might shift, after sitting with this?

Not *I should read more Quran.* That is too small.

The shift is: **I have been treating things that have a bottom as if they had depth, and the only thing without a bottom as if I had already been through it.** That is the inversion the ayah is asking us to repair.

The dunya is shallow. We have been pretending it is deep. The Quran is bottomless. We have been pretending it is finite. Both of those pretenses are exhausting us. Letting them go, even partially, is a relief the size of an ocean.

### A Closing Duʿāʾ

*Allāhumma, You whose words the seas cannot hold and the trees cannot transcribe — open in our hearts a door to even one of Your kalimāt that we have not yet seen. Free us from the lie that we have already read Your Book. Free us from the lie that our small worship is too small for You to accept. And by Your name al-Ḥakīm — let what little we receive of Your speech place itself, with Your wisdom, exactly where our hearts most need it.*

*Āmīn.*
