# The Heaviness in His Ears: When Turning Away Becomes a Body

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> وَإِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ءَايَـٰتُنَا وَلَّىٰ مُسْتَكْبِرًا كَأَن لَّمْ يَسْمَعْهَا كَأَنَّ فِىٓ أُذُنَيْهِ وَقْرًا ۖ فَبَشِّرْهُ بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

**Transliteration:**
> Wa idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā wallā mustakbiran ka-an lam yasmaʿhā ka-anna fī udhunayhi waqrā. Fa-bashshirhu bi-ʿadhābin alīm.

**Translation:**
> And when Our verses are recited to him, he turns away in arrogance as if he had not heard them, as if there were a heaviness in his two ears. So give him glad tidings of a painful punishment.

---

## Rebuilding the World

Before we step into this ayah, we have to feel where it sits.

Surah Luqman is, in its bones, a surah about **how a heart learns to listen**. The center of this surah — the part everyone remembers — is a father, Luqman, sitting with his son. He calls him *yā bunayya* — "O my little son" — and softly, patiently, layer by layer, he teaches him how to hear. How to hear tawhid. How to hear the watching of Allah even in things the size of a mustard seed. How to hear a mother who carried you. How to hear your own voice when it gets too loud. How to walk. How to speak.

The entire middle of this surah is a tutorial in **becoming someone whose ears work**.

But before Allah gives us that tutorial, He shows us its opposite.

Right at the opening, He sketches two figures. The one who *buys* idle speech to mislead others — and the one we're about to meet: the one to whom Allah's own words are recited, and his body simply refuses them. Not his mind. His body.

So picture the scene the first Muslims would have known instantly. A small gathering in Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ, or one of the companions, begins to recite. The words are unmistakable — not poetry, not soothsaying, not the cadence of the marketplace. Something else. Something that, if you let it, would split you open.

And there is a man in earshot. He hears the first syllables. Something in him registers it. And then — without argument, without rebuttal, without engagement of any kind — **he turns**. His shoulder lifts. His chin tilts. His back appears. He walks away.

[PAUSE]

That walking away. That is what this ayah is going to dissect, atom by atom.

Here is the hook. Allah is not going to tell us this man *didn't hear*. He is going to tell us he heard — and then his body **manufactured a deafness** that wasn't there. And then Allah is going to say something so strange, so jarring, that for fourteen centuries scholars have stopped on this one word: **bashshirhu**. *Give him glad tidings.* Of punishment.

Why use the word for *glad tidings* to announce a punishment?

Let's sit with that until we see what He is doing.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wallā Mustakbiran* — When the Posture Is the Verdict

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word.

In English, when we describe someone refusing something, we usually narrate their state of mind. *He didn't agree. He disliked it. He rejected it.* We go inward — into thought, opinion, mental position. Arabic can do that too. But Arabic has another move, and the Quran uses it constantly: instead of describing the inner state, it describes **the body's movement that betrays the inner state**.

The Quran does not say, "he didn't believe." It says, *wallā* — **he turned**.

The root **و-ل-ي** carries the idea of one thing turning toward or away from another, of presenting one side and hiding another. *Walī* (a friend, a guardian) is someone whose face is *turned toward* you. *Tawallā* is its mirror image — to turn the face *away*. And in Form II (*wallā*), it carries the sense of a deliberate, decisive turning — not a drift, not a hesitation. A full rotation of the body away from a thing.

Now stay with me. Allah does not say, "when Our verses are recited to him, he disagreed." He says: when Our verses are recited to him, **he turned**.

[PAUSE]

The verdict is the posture.

This is something we know in our own lives but rarely say out loud. You can tell a person what they actually feel about a thing not by their words but by which way their shoulders point. We have all sat at a dinner table with someone whose mouth is saying yes but whose chair is slowly sliding away. We have all watched someone "listen" with their whole face turned thirty degrees off-center, eyes already on the door. The body is honest in a way the mouth often isn't.

Allah is telling us: when the verses come, look at his body. Don't waste time on his arguments. **His shoulders have already answered.**

And then — *mustakbiran*.

Here is where the precision gets surgical. The root **ك-ب-ر** means greatness, bigness. *Kabīr* — great. *Akbar* — greater. But the form *mustakbir* — Form X — is one of the most psychologically exact constructions in the Arabic language. Form X carries the meaning of *seeking* or *claiming* a thing for oneself. So *istakbara* is not "to be great." It is **to claim a greatness one does not actually possess**. To posture as great. To *make oneself* big.

This is the difference between *kabīr* (great) and *mustakbir* (acting big).

Allah is *al-Kabīr* — actually great. Truly, in reality, in essence. The human being who turns from the ayah is *mustakbir* — performing a greatness, a self-inflation, a chest-puffing that has no substance behind it.

Notice something. Allah did not say: *wallā kāfiran* — he turned away as a disbeliever. He could have. He chose not to. He said: *wallā mustakbiran* — he turned away **performing bigness**.

Why?

Because the surah is telling us the root of refusing the ayah is not, in the first instance, intellectual disagreement. It is **posture**. It is a self-image that cannot survive sitting still and listening. To sit and let the Quran wash over you is to be made small for a moment, to admit you don't already know, to risk being moved. The *mustakbir* cannot afford that. So before the words can reach him, **he turns his body away to protect his image of himself**.

Here's what's remarkable. Centuries before modern psychology gave us the language of *defensive grandiosity* — the way the most fragile self-concepts produce the most aggressive posturing — Allah named it in a single word. The greater the inner emptiness, the louder the outer bigness must shout. The man who is actually great does not need to *seek* greatness. He has it. The one who is rotting at the core is the one who must *perform* size.

So when the ayah is recited — and the ayah is a mirror — what does the *mustakbir* see? Not himself. He sees the gap between what he is pretending to be and what he actually is. And rather than collapse the gap by becoming honest, **he turns his back so the mirror cannot finish forming**.

[PAUSE]

One sentence to carry: *He did not turn away because the words were unconvincing. He turned away because they were working.*

### Element Two: Two *Ka-anna*s — The Sentence That Mimics a Lie

Now look at the architecture of what Allah does next. He gives us *two* similes, stacked one after the other:

**كَأَن لَّمْ يَسْمَعْهَا** — *as if he had not heard them*
**كَأَنَّ فِىٓ أُذُنَيْهِ وَقْرًا** — *as if there were a heaviness in his two ears*

In Arabic rhetoric, *ka-anna* is the particle of *as if* — a comparison that signals: this is not actually the case, but here is how it appears. It is the particle of pretense. The particle of make-believe.

Allah stacks it twice. Why?

Let me teach you the device first. In Arabic, doubling a *ka-anna* with the same subject is a way of building intensity through layered pretense. The first *ka-anna* describes the act. The second *ka-anna* describes the alibi for the act.

So look what's happening in the man's body:

The first *ka-anna* — *as if he had not heard them*. This is the **behavior**. He acts as though the verses never reached his ears. He continues his day. He picks up his conversation. He shows no sign of having been spoken to.

But that's not enough. Because **he did hear**. The verses did enter. So a second *ka-anna* has to come in — a deeper alibi, a body-level explanation for why his behavior could possibly be honest. *As if there were a heaviness in his two ears.* He has to manufacture a phantom deafness. He has to invent a physical reason inside his own body for not responding to what he just responded to by turning away.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done? **The sentence itself imitates the lying.** The two *ka-anna*s are not redundant. They are the two layers of the lie. *I didn't hear it* — and when that's not believable — *something must have been wrong with my ears*.

This is how self-deception actually works. We have all watched it. A person hears something true about themselves, and within seconds you can see them constructing the story for why they did not really hear it: *I was distracted, I was tired, I was thinking about something else, the speaker was unclear, the moment was wrong.* The second *ka-anna* is always the alibi for the first.

And then comes the word that makes this image visceral: **وَقْر** — *waqr*.

*Waqr* in classical Arabic does not mean a small impairment. The root carries the meaning of **weight, gravity, something that has been placed and now sits heavily**. *Waqār* (dignity, gravitas) comes from the same root — that quality of someone who carries weight, who is *grounded*. A *waqr* in the ear is not a ringing or a faint hearing. It is a **packed-in heaviness**, like a stone wedged inside.

Notice what Allah has done with this image. He is borrowing a physical, tangible sensation — *something heavy stuffed into the ear* — and using it to describe a spiritual condition the listener cannot otherwise see.

This is the principle of pointing toward the unseen by way of the seen. Allah is not saying the man is medically deaf. He is saying: **the man's refusal has become so dense, so packed, so heavy that it functions in him the way a physical obstruction would.** He has so trained himself to turn away from the ayah that his *capacity to hear it* has thickened over with calcification. The refusal has accreted into tissue.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. There is one more detail in this single word *waqr* that you can miss in fifty readings.

He did not say *fī udhunihi waqrā* — in his ear. He said *fī udhunayhi waqrā* — **in his two ears**.

Arabic has a special grammatical form for *exactly two* of something — *muthannā*, the dual. Most languages don't have this. Arabic does. And Allah uses it here precisely.

Why? Because in Arabic rhetoric, the dual is invoked when the speaker wants to emphasize the **totality** of a paired faculty being affected. If only one ear had been mentioned, there would still be a route in. The other ear remained open. By saying *both ears*, Allah is saying: **there is no remaining channel**. The closure is complete. Whatever he might have heard with the other side, he has stuffed shut too.

Now, here's what is remarkable. The Quran tells us elsewhere that the ear is one of the faculties Allah will question us about: *innas-samʿa wal-baṣara wal-fuʾāda kullu ulā'ika kāna ʿanhu mas'ūlā* — *hearing, sight, and heart, all of these shall be questioned* (17:36). The ear is not just biology. It is a **trust**. It is an instrument given by Allah for a purpose, and on the Day of accountability, both of those ears will be asked: *what did you let through?*

And here, Allah is showing us a man who has taken both of those entrusted instruments and **packed them shut** — not because they are damaged, but because he could not bear what they were beginning to let in.

One sentence to carry: *The first ka-anna is the act. The second ka-anna is the alibi. The waqr is the alibi made flesh.*

### Element Three: *Bashshirhu* — The Word That Should Not Be There

Now we come to the word that has stopped scholars for fourteen centuries.

**فَبَشِّرْهُ بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ** — *So give him glad tidings of a painful punishment.*

The root **ب-ش-ر** is one of the warmest roots in the Quran. *Bashar* is the human being — named for the *skin* (*basharah*), the visible surface where joy flushes the face. *Bushrā* is good news. *Mubashshir* is one who brings glad tidings — a title used for messengers, for the angels who announce a child, for the breeze that announces rain after a long drought.

When you say to an Arab, *abshir* — it means *receive joy*. It is the word you use when the news is so good you want to see the smile arrive on their face.

So why — *why* — does Allah, in describing the punishment of this man, use *bashshirhu*?

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most powerful examples of *tahakkum* — Quranic irony — in the entire revelation. Let me build it carefully, because the power of it is in how it lands, not in being told about it.

The classical scholars sat with this and saw multiple layers — and as always, these are not competing answers. They are camera angles on the same reality.

**One angle:** The *mustakbir* spent his life chasing glad tidings of a worldly kind. He built his self-image on being celebrated, congratulated, told he had won. He turned away from the ayah because the ayah threatened to interrupt his stream of self-congratulation. So Allah, with surgical precision, uses *the very word his ego was always reaching for* — and attaches it to the only news he was actually earning. *You wanted glad tidings? Here is your glad tidings.* The form he craved, with the content he refused to hear.

**Another angle:** *Bashshir* announces something that will *show on the face*. Joy makes the skin flush. So does dread. *Bashshirhu bi-ʿadhābin alīm* — let the news arrive on his face. Let him receive it the way one receives news that changes a body. Just as his refusal had become a body — packed into his ears — his recompense will become a body too. The denial was somatic. The reply is somatic.

**A third angle:** The irony itself is part of the punishment. To be told *receive glad tidings* — and have the news be *a painful punishment* — is to have your entire framework for what counts as good news inverted in a single sentence. It strips you of the language you used to insulate yourself. The man spent his life polishing the word *bashshir* on his own tongue. Now Allah hands it back to him reshaped.

These angles do not contradict. They are three windows into one image. Each one says: **the word that should not be there is exactly where it belongs**.

Now here's what's remarkable. Notice how this final sentence completes the architecture of the ayah.

In the middle of the ayah, the man **manufactured a heaviness in his ears** so he would not have to hear. He inserted a *waqr* — a weight — between himself and Allah's speech. He blocked the channel.

In the final sentence, Allah uses *bashshirhu*. *Make this reach him.* **Announce it to him.** It is as if Allah is saying: *you closed the channel for what would have saved you. Now I will open a channel for what will reach you anyway.*

The waqr could keep out the Quran. It cannot keep out the consequence.

[PAUSE]

One sentence to carry: *The bashshir is not an error. It is the mirror of the istikbār — the inflated self that craved glad tidings now meets the only glad tidings its life was actually composing.*

### Bridge

So look at what these three elements have given us, side by side.

The body turned. The ears were packed. The ironic announcement was made.

A posture, a faculty, a verdict. Three coordinates of a single human catastrophe — the catastrophe of a person to whom Allah's words came, and who could not let them in.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How Refusal Becomes a Body

You know this experience even if you have never named it.

You go to the doctor and the doctor begins to explain something about your blood work, and within the first sentence you can feel your mind unhook. You hear the doctor's voice but the words are not landing. You're nodding. You're saying *mm-hmm*. But nothing is actually getting through.

Or: you are in an argument with someone you love, and they are saying the one thing you most need to hear, the one accurate sentence — and you can feel your whole body, *your whole body*, redirect. Your jaw sets. Your eyes flick away. Your hearing goes mechanical. You're still in the room. You're still pointed at them. But something inside you has **walked out**.

Or: you sit down to read the Quran and your eyes pass over the words, and ten minutes later you realize you have absorbed nothing, even though you can confirm with technical accuracy that the sounds were made and the page was turned.

What just happened?

This is the *waqr*. Not a medical condition. A psycho-spiritual one. A heaviness that the self places into its own ears when what is being said threatens the structure of who the self has decided to be.

Here is the part modern psychology only recently named: **the body participates in our refusals**. When we hear something we cannot afford to hear, we do not just disagree mentally. Our autonomic nervous system fires. Our muscles brace. Our hearing literally narrows — the brain selectively filters auditory input based on threat assessment. This is not metaphor. This is documented neurology.

Allah described it in three words: *ka-anna fī udhunayhi waqrā*.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with this, because here is where the ayah opens into something terrifying and tender at once.

The *mustakbir* in this ayah did not start the day deaf. He acquired his deafness. Each turning of his shoulder — each instance of choosing his self-image over the encounter — laid down a new layer of *waqr*. Until eventually the heaviness was so packed that the ayah could be recited two feet from him and never reach the place inside that decides.

This is not just about a disbeliever in seventh-century Makkah. This is about **what happens inside any of us, including believers**, when we make a thousand small refusals to be moved.

You hear a khutbah and the imam says something you needed to hear. You feel it land. And on the drive home you decide it wasn't really for you. Next week you hear something similar. You feel it land a little less. The week after, less still. After two years, you can sit through any khutbah, on any topic, with full attendance and zero arrival. The *waqr* has set in.

This is the warning the ayah is whispering to every one of us: **refusal compounds**. Each time you turn the shoulder, the next turning is easier. Each time you decide a verse "wasn't really for me," the next verse will have a harder time reaching you. **The ear is a muscle of the heart**, and it atrophies the way every muscle atrophies — through unuse.

But there is a tender side to this image, and we have to see it.

If *waqr* is **acquired**, it is also **dissolvable**. Allah did not say this man was born without ears. He said *as if there were a heaviness*. The heaviness is something that was placed there, packed in, layer by layer. Which means it can also be taken out, layer by layer.

The instrument is still intact. The hardware works. What has changed is what the self is willing to let through.

So the cure for the *waqr* is not a new pair of ears. It is the deliberate act of, **once**, sitting still through one ayah you would normally turn from. Refusing to let the shoulder lift. Refusing to manufacture the alibi. Letting the verse finish reaching you, even if it costs you the image of yourself you had been protecting.

One verse, fully heard, dissolves more *waqr* than a hundred recited at distance.

**Practical application:** Tomorrow, when you sit with the Quran, choose one ayah — one — that you have always slid past. Maybe because it convicts you. Maybe because you don't understand it. Maybe because some part of you, every time you reach it, redirects. Sit with that ayah. Read it aloud. Read its translation. Read one short tafsir on it. And do not move on until you have let it finish reaching you. That single act is the unpacking of one layer of *waqr*.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Two Greatnesses

There is *kabīr* and there is *mustakbir*.

There is one being in existence who is actually great — *al-Kabīr*, *al-Mutakabbir* — and there is everyone else, who is great only insofar as Allah has made them so, and who becomes ridiculous the instant they begin performing a greatness they do not possess.

The disease at the center of this ayah, the disease that makes a man pack his own ears shut, is *istikbār*. The pursuit of a greatness one is not.

Let me show you why this is the deepest theme in the ayah, and why the surah opens with it.

When Iblīs refused to bow to Adam, the Quran does not say he disagreed with the command. It says: **استكبر** — *istakbara*. He sought a greatness. He performed a size. *I am better than him*, he said. *You created me from fire and him from clay*. He did not actually become better. He just claimed the position.

That single word *istakbara* is the root of every refusal in the Quran to receive what Allah is offering. Pharaoh did it. The chiefs of Quraysh did it. And every human heart, every single day, does some version of it.

[PAUSE]

Here is the part that should stop us.

*Istikbār* is **the most counterproductive defense the human soul has ever invented**. Think about it. The *mustakbir* makes himself big in order to feel safe. But by making himself big, he insulates himself from *the very source* of actual largeness. The Quran was being recited *to him*. The connection to *al-Kabīr* was being offered. And he turned his shoulder because admitting he needed it would have made him *small*.

The tragedy of *istikbār* is that it costs you the thing you were performing in order to obtain.

Real greatness — the *waqār*, the gravitas, the substance — is the property of the one who can sit still and be made small for a moment by the Quran. Because what is happening in that moment of being-made-small is **the only mechanism by which a soul gets actually large**. The ego shrinks; the soul expands. They are inversely related.

The *mustakbir* puffs the ego. So the soul, by the same mechanism, withers. He ends his life having defended every inch of an inflated self-image — and arrives at the grave with almost nothing inside it.

The believer who learns to weep at one ayah, who lets one verse undo him, who admits *I did not know, I was wrong, I needed this* — that person is, by the inverse motion, growing.

This is why Luqman in the very next section of the surah will tell his son: *wa lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka lin-nās* — *do not turn your cheek away from people in arrogance* — *wa lā tamshi fil-arḍi maraḥā* — *and do not walk in the earth exultantly*. Because Luqman, who has *waqār* — actual weight — knows that the only way to acquire that weight is to refuse, at every step, the false weight of *istikbār*.

[PAUSE]

The ayah is asking each of us: when the verses come — *when they actually come*, in a moment when they are addressing exactly the part of you that needs addressing — **which greatness do you reach for?** The performed one that turns your shoulder? Or the real one that lets you sit?

**Practical application:** The next time you feel the impulse to defend yourself against a true thing — whether it comes from a verse, a friend, a spouse, your own conscience — pause. Notice the impulse. Notice the shoulder beginning to turn. And, just once, do not turn. Just once, let the true thing finish arriving. That single act of *not-turning* is a deposit in the only greatness that compounds.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Look now at how the ayah is built, with everything we have seen.

It begins with a recitation — *idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā*. The verses come.

Then a body — *wallā mustakbiran*. The body turns, performing size.

Then two layers of pretense — *ka-anna lam yasmaʿhā, ka-anna fī udhunayhi waqrā*. The pretense of not hearing, the pretense of why.

And then — the inversion. *Fa-bashshirhu bi-ʿadhābin alīm*. The word from his lexicon of self-congratulation, handed back to him with the only content his life was earning.

Notice the symmetry. The ayah opens with Allah's words being **recited to him**. The ayah closes with a different announcement being **made to him**. He blocked the first. The second will arrive regardless. The channel he closed was the only one that would have saved him. The channel that opens in its place is the only one that remains.

The first recitation was love. The second announcement is justice. He chose which one he was willing to hear.

### Bringing It Together

The three linguistic elements and the two themes are saying the same thing from different sides.

The *mustakbir* is one half of the picture. The *waqr* is the other half. They are cause and effect. *Istikbār* is the disease of the self. *Waqr* is the disease made flesh in the body. And the *bashshirhu* is the divine response that mirrors the human refusal with surgical precision — using the man's own favorite word against him.

The ayah is a portrait of a closed circuit. The man wanted glad tidings. He refused the only words that could have given them. So Allah gives him the *form* of what he wanted, with the *content* he refused.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time the Quran was recited near me — and I was the one who turned the shoulder? Not dramatically. Just inwardly. Just enough not to be moved.

2. Which ayah in the Quran do I consistently slide past? What does my sliding past it tell me about what I am protecting?

3. Where in my life am I performing a greatness I do not actually possess — and what am I refusing to hear in order to keep performing it?

4. If I were honest, which of the two *ka-anna*s do I use more often: the act (*pretending I didn't hear*) or the alibi (*pretending there's a reason I couldn't hear*)?

5. What is one ayah I could sit with this week — fully, without alibi — until it finishes arriving?

### One-Sentence Distillation

*The man who turns from the ayah is not refusing words; he is packing his own ears with the weight of an inflated self that cannot survive the smallness of being moved — and the only news that will reach him in the end is the ironic mirror of the glad tidings he spent his life chasing.*

### The Closing Invitation

If anything has been opened in you by this ayah, do not let it close again. The danger this ayah is naming is exactly the danger of nodding along, feeling moved for a moment, and walking away unchanged. That nodding-and-walking-away **is the very motion the ayah is describing**.

So before you move from this reflection, decide one thing. One ayah you will sit with this week. One refusal you will not perform. One alibi you will not construct. Let one layer of *waqr* be unpacked.

That is the invitation. Not to feel something here, but to **not turn the shoulder** the next time.

### Du'a

*Ya Allah, You who are al-Kabīr in truth, save us from the disease of performing a greatness we do not possess. Unpack the heaviness from our ears, layer by layer. When Your verses come to us, hold our shoulders still long enough for the words to arrive. And let us never be among those to whom the only remaining announcement is the mirror of the glad tidings they refused. Āmīn.*
