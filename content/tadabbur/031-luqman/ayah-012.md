# The Wisdom That Returns to Its Owner

## Surah Luqman 31:12

**Arabic:**
[ayah:31:12] وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا لُقْمَـٰنَ ٱلْحِكْمَةَ أَنِ ٱشْكُرْ لِلَّهِ ۚ وَمَن يَشْكُرْ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَنِىٌّ حَمِيدٌ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa laqad ātaynā Luqmāna-l-ḥikmata ani-shkur lillāh. Wa man yashkur fa-innamā yashkuru li-nafsih. Wa man kafara fa-inna-llāha ghaniyyun ḥamīd.*

**Translation:**
*"And We had certainly given Luqman wisdom — [saying]: 'Be grateful to Allah.' And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself. And whoever denies — then indeed, Allah is Free of need, Praiseworthy."*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

There is a man in the Quran who is not a prophet. He does not bring scripture. He does not call a nation. He does not split a sea. He is a sage — perhaps an Ethiopian, perhaps a Nubian, perhaps a slave according to many of the early reports, certainly an outsider to the lineage of prophethood that runs from Ibrahim through Musa through Isa.

And Allah names a surah after him.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that does. The surah before Luqman is *al-Rūm* — empires, victories, the cycles of civilizations. The surah after Luqman is *al-Sajdah* — prostration, the cosmic order, the bowing of all things. Between these two enormous frames — empires and prostrations — Allah places a single man giving advice to his son.

Not a king. Not a prophet. A father.

The pagans of Mecca were obsessed with lineage. *Who is your father? Who is your tribe? What is your bloodline?* And the Quran, in choosing to name this surah, deliberately reaches outside the prophetic lineage entirely. It picks a man whose name carries — in many of the classical narrations — the weight of being a non-Arab servant, and it crowns him with the heaviest single word in the moral vocabulary of revelation.

*Ḥikmah.* Wisdom.

### What we are walking into

The passage that follows this ayah is one of the most beloved in the entire Quran. Luqman speaks to his son. He warns him about *shirk*. He tells him about the mustard seed in the rock that Allah knows. He tells him to establish the prayer, to bear patiently what afflicts him, not to walk arrogantly upon the earth, to lower his voice.

But before any of that — before a single word of fatherly counsel — Allah pauses. He tells us what Luqman's wisdom actually consisted of.

And He gives a definition that will, if you sit with it, rearrange your understanding of what it means to be wise.

### The hook

Here is what is strange about this ayah. Allah says He gave Luqman *al-ḥikmah* — wisdom. And then He immediately explains what that wisdom was. We expect a philosophy. We expect a system. We expect the kind of thing the Greeks would have written — a treatise on virtue, an analysis of the soul.

Instead Allah gives us two words.

*Ani-shkur lillāh.* **Be grateful to Allah.**

That's it. That's the wisdom.

[PAUSE]

How can two words be wisdom? How can being grateful — something a child can do — be the substance of *ḥikmah*, the highest cognitive virtue in the Quran? And then, in the same breath, Allah says something that should stop us cold: *whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself.*

What does that mean? Gratitude returns to the one who gives it?

Stay with that question. We're going to walk into it slowly.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1: *Ḥikmah* — and why Allah defines it before letting Luqman speak

Let's first sit with what *ḥikmah* even is.

In English we say *wisdom*, and we usually mean accumulated insight — the kind of thing old people have. But the Arabic root *ḥ-k-m* doesn't begin there. It begins in the workshop of the saddler and the architect.

The classical lexicons trace *ḥ-k-m* to the act of restraining a horse with a bridle — the *ḥakamah* — the piece of the bridle that goes around the horse's mouth, the part that prevents the animal from running wherever it wants. From there the root spreads outward: to *judge* (because a judge restrains chaos), to *firm construction* (because well-built things don't wobble), to *wisdom* (because the wise person restrains impulse and places things where they belong).

So *ḥikmah* is not "having a lot of insight." *Ḥikmah* is **the capacity to place each thing in its proper place** — and to refuse to place it anywhere else.

Hold that.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the strange thing. Allah doesn't tell us Luqman wrote treatises. Allah doesn't say He gave Luqman knowledge of the stars or medicine or jurisprudence. He says He gave him *ḥikmah* — the capacity to put things in their proper place — and then immediately, without pause, He defines that capacity:

*Ani-shkur lillāh.*

The particle *an* here is what classical grammarians call *an al-tafsīriyyah* — the explanatory *an*. It's the *an* that introduces a clarification of what came before. So Allah is saying: *We gave him wisdom — meaning, namely, that is to say — be grateful to Allah.*

Wisdom is *equated* with gratitude.

Let me say that again, because it should disturb something in us.

**The highest cognitive virtue in the Quran is being equated, in this ayah, with the simplest emotional response a creature can have toward its Creator.**

Why?

Because if *ḥikmah* is placing each thing in its proper place — then the most fundamental act of placement is this: recognizing that what you have came from Someone, and locating yourself, accurately, as the receiver of it. Everything else in wisdom is downstream of that single placement. Every misplaced thing in a human life — every ungrateful entitlement, every misdirected praise, every wrong assumption about whose hand the gift came from — is a failure of this one placement.

You can know everything else and still be a fool, if you have not done this one placement correctly. You can know almost nothing else and still be wise, if you have.

[PAUSE]

Luqman is not a prophet. He cannot tell his son about revelation that he received. He cannot say *Allah said to me.* But he has done the one placement. He has located himself as the receiver. And from that single correct placement, everything else he is about to say to his son flows — like a river from a spring that has finally been opened.

This is why Allah defines the wisdom before letting Luqman speak. So that we don't think the wisdom is in the advice. **The wisdom is in the orientation that produced the advice.**

### Element 2: *Li-nafsih* — the gratitude that returns to its owner

Now here is the line that has bewildered readers for fourteen hundred years.

*Wa man yashkur fa-innamā yashkuru li-nafsih.*

*And whoever is grateful — he is grateful only for himself.*

Sit with the strangeness of that for a moment. We have just been told to be grateful to Allah. And now Allah tells us that when we are grateful — *the gratitude is actually for ourselves.*

This is not how gratitude usually works in human language. If I thank you, the thanks goes to you. The benefit, presumably, accrues to your sense of being seen, being honored, being recognized. That's the whole point of thanks — it moves *outward* from the thanker *toward* the one being thanked.

But Allah inverts the entire flow.

Let me show you what's happening grammatically. The verb *yashkur* — *he is grateful* — is being followed by *li-nafsih*: *for/to himself*. The preposition *li* in Arabic carries the sense of *for the benefit of*, *for the sake of*, *belonging to*. And the word *nafs* — *self*, *soul* — is then bound to the pronoun *-hu*: *his own self*.

So the literal architecture reads: *whoever is grateful — then the gratitude is, in its actual benefit, going to his own soul.*

And then the structure tightens further with *innamā* — the restriction particle. *Innamā* in Arabic means **only**, **exclusively**, **nothing but**. It excludes everything outside what follows it. So Allah is not saying *part of the gratitude returns to the self*. He is saying — *whoever is grateful, the gratitude returns* ***exclusively*** *to the self.*

[PAUSE]

What is going on here?

Here is the first layer. When we thank a human being for a gift, the human being is genuinely augmented by our thanks. Their sense of mattering grows. Their dignity is fed. Something real is transferred. But Allah is not augmented by anything. He is *al-Ghaniyy* — the Free of need — and that name is about to land in this very ayah. So when a creature thanks Allah, **nothing reaches Allah from the thanks**. There is no augmentation. There is no completion. He is complete before, during, and after the thanks.

So where does the thanks go?

It goes back. It curves. It returns to the one who offered it.

But — and here is the deeper layer — *what is it that returns?*

The classical mufassirūn observed that the benefit of *shukr* to the *shākir* (the grateful one) is twofold. First, there is the worldly benefit: Allah promises in Surah Ibrahim — *if you are grateful, I will surely increase you* (14:7). So gratitude is a multiplier in the realm of *ni'am* — the realm of blessings. Second, there is the soul-level benefit: the act of gratitude *forms* the one who is grateful. It carves a certain shape into the *nafs*. It makes the *nafs* into the kind of *nafs* that sees gifts, that locates givers, that places each thing where it belongs.

In other words: **gratitude is not what you give to Allah. Gratitude is what Allah uses to build you.**

[PAUSE]

Let me sit with this. Because this single insight, if you let it in, changes the entire feel of worship.

We often think of worship as a tax. We pay Allah His due. We hand over the prayer, the fast, the *dhikr* — and we hope it is accepted. And it is true — these are *ḥuqūq Allāh*, the rights of Allah. But the deeper truth this ayah is revealing is that **the form of the act is shaped to return to you**. You do not lose anything by being grateful. You do not give up something that then resides with Allah. The entire transaction is a closed loop where the giver, the act, and the receiver are all — in a hidden sense — the same person. The one being formed by the act *is the one doing the act*.

You bow in prayer, and the prayer returns to your spine.
You fast, and the fast returns to your hunger.
You give *zakāh*, and the *zakāh* returns to your fingers.
You are grateful, and the gratitude returns to your soul.

This is why Allah says *innamā* — *exclusively*. Because nothing is leaking out of the loop. Nothing is being lost into Allah's bottomless need — because Allah has no bottomless need. **Every drop of gratitude you ever offer is preserved and returned, with increase, to you.**

### Element 3: *Ghaniyyun Ḥamīd* — the pairing that should not work, and does

Now look at the second half of the ayah.

*Wa man kafara fa-inna-llāha ghaniyyun ḥamīd.*

*And whoever denies — then indeed Allah is Free of need, Praiseworthy.*

Notice the asymmetry. When Allah speaks about the grateful, He talks about *them*: the gratitude returns to *their* selves. But when He speaks about the ungrateful, He does not talk about them at all. He says nothing about what happens to the *kāfir*. The *kāfir* is mentioned — and then immediately the camera turns away from the *kāfir* and rests on Allah.

*Allah is Ghaniyy. Allah is Ḥamīd.*

This is a deliberate withholding. We expect a threat. We expect *fa-inna lahum ʿadhāban alīmā* — *for them there is a painful punishment*. That is the standard Quranic register for ingratitude. But here, Allah substitutes the threat with two of His names. And in that substitution lives a quiet, devastating truth.

Let me show you what these two names do together.

*Ghaniyy* comes from the root *gh-n-y*, the root of *richness*, *self-sufficiency*, *not needing*. *Al-Ghaniyy* is the One who needs absolutely nothing. He is independent of every creature, every act of worship, every prayer, every act of *shukr*. If every human being on earth fell into *kufr* at once — Allah's *ghinā* would not be reduced by an atom's weight.

*Ḥamīd* comes from the root *ḥ-m-d* — the root of praise. But *ḥamīd* in this *fa'īl* form is dense. It can mean *the One who is praised* (in the passive sense — *maḥmūd*) and it can mean *the One who is praiseworthy* (in the active sense — deserving of praise whether or not anyone is doing it). The classical lexicographers note that the *fa'īl* form here carries both meanings simultaneously. So *Ḥamīd* is the One who is being praised — by every creature, every leaf, every atom — *and* the One who would be praiseworthy even if nothing in existence were praising Him.

[PAUSE]

Now look at these two names side by side.

*Ghaniyy* — He does not need your praise.
*Ḥamīd* — He is praised anyway, and would be praiseworthy anyway.

This pairing is doing something almost cruel in its precision. It is closing every escape route the ungrateful soul might try to take.

The ungrateful person, in his heart, often believes one of two things. Either: *Allah needs my worship — so if I withhold it, I am punishing Him, I am asserting my independence.* Or: *No one is praising Him anyway, so what does it matter if I add my voice or not?*

Allah closes both doors.

*Ghaniyy* — you cannot punish Me by withholding. There is nothing in Me that needs what you have. Your withholding is not a wound to Me. It is a wound to you.

*Ḥamīd* — and you cannot make Me unpraised by your silence. The praise is happening. The whole cosmos is doing it. Your silence does not subtract from the chorus. It only subtracts you from the chorus.

[PAUSE]

This is why the ayah does not bother to describe what happens to the *kāfir*. Because the *kāfir* has not damaged Allah. The *kāfir* has simply removed himself from the loop that was going to return gratitude — *with increase* — to his own soul. The damage is local. The damage is *li-nafsih*. The damage is the exact mirror image of the benefit promised to the grateful.

The grateful one's gratitude returns to him.
The ungrateful one's ingratitude — also returns to him.

That is the symmetry. That is what Allah is leaving unspoken. He doesn't need to spell it out, because He has already given us the equation in the first half: *whatever you do with this — it comes back to you.*

### The bridge

We have walked through three pieces of architecture.

We saw that *ḥikmah* — the highest cognitive virtue — is defined here as the simplest possible act: *be grateful*. Wisdom is the correct placement of one thing — yourself as receiver. Everything else flows from that single placement.

We saw that gratitude, in Allah's economy, does not flow outward. It curves back. It returns *innamā li-nafsih* — *exclusively to the self*. Worship is not a tax paid to a needy God. Worship is the instrument by which Allah carves the worshipper into the shape that can hold what He intends to give.

And we saw that the two names *Ghaniyy* and *Ḥamīd* close every door the ungrateful soul might use to imagine its ingratitude matters to Allah. He does not need it. He is praised anyway.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not about words, but about how to actually live a human life.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1: The economy where giving and receiving are the same act

Let me start with something every human being has experienced.

You have, somewhere in your life, given a gift to someone who did not appreciate it. And you remember what that felt like. You remember the slight contraction in your chest — the *I won't be doing that again*. You remember the way your sense of having been *seen* deflated. The gift, in some way, came back to you spoiled.

Now think of the opposite. You have given a gift to someone who *received* it with their whole face. Who held it like it mattered. Who told you a week later that they were still thinking about it. And you remember what *that* felt like — the way the gift, after leaving your hand, somehow grew on its way to them, and the warmth of its growing reached you back.

[PAUSE]

This is the structure Allah is pointing to. But He is taking it and pushing it to its absolute limit.

In human exchange, gratitude is a two-way completion. The giver gives. The receiver receives. The receiver thanks. The thanks honors the giver. Everyone is augmented.

But Allah does not need to be augmented. So when *He* gives — and we give thanks — the entire augmentation cannot flow back to Him. He is full. There is no empty space in His being for the thanks to fill. So the thanks, having nowhere to go in Him, *curves back to us*.

Imagine pouring water into a vessel that is already infinitely full. The water cannot enter. So it overflows — and the overflow lands back at your own feet, around your own ankles, filling your own basin.

This is what *shukr* is doing. Every act of gratitude you have ever offered to Allah is sitting in your basin. Not lost. Not consumed by His need. Returned to you.

And here is where the practical implication starts to glow.

We have all had the suspicion, somewhere in the dark moments of our worship, that we are *losing something* by worshipping. We are giving up sleep for the night prayer. We are giving up food for the fast. We are giving up money for *zakāh*. We are giving up time for *dhikr*. And the *nafs* whispers: *this is a sacrifice; you are emptying yourself.*

This ayah is saying — **you are not emptying yourself. You are filling yourself.** The act looks like giving, but in the economy of *Ghaniyy*, the act *is* receiving. The verb is the same on both sides. *Yashkuru — yashkuru li-nafsih*. He gives thanks — he gives thanks to himself.

The ungrateful person believes he has *kept* what he refused to give. He has *kept* the night for sleep, the wealth for spending, the time for entertainment. But this ayah is saying he has not kept it. He has lost the loop. The thing was going to multiply if he had let it curve back through the act of gratitude. By refusing the loop, he has refused the multiplication.

**Application: stop framing worship as cost.** Tonight, in your prayer, try this. Don't tell yourself *I am giving Allah this prayer*. Tell yourself *Allah is using this prayer to build me*. Watch what happens to the heaviness of the act. The prayer doesn't become easier — but you stop resenting it. Because you can't resent a gift you are giving yourself.

### Theme 2: Why Allah named a surah after a non-prophet — and what that means for you

Now let's step back and ask the harder question.

Why Luqman? Why does Allah, in the entire ocean of names available to Him — the prophets, the angels, the great kings — choose a man who, by every account, was not part of the chosen lineage? In some narrations he was a slave. In some he was a tailor. In some he was a shepherd. None of the narrations make him royalty. None make him a prophet.

And then — through this man — Allah delivers some of the most piercing parental counsel in the entire Quran.

[PAUSE]

I think Allah is doing something here that should land directly on your chest.

Most of us, when we think about whether we are *qualified* to teach our children, to advise our siblings, to guide our friends — we measure ourselves against an impossible standard. We think: *I am not a scholar. I have not memorized the Quran. I have not studied at Azhar. I have not earned the right to speak.*

And then Allah names a surah after a man who was none of those things. A man who, by the lights of his society, had no platform, no lineage, no formal authority. And Allah crowns him with *ḥikmah*.

What was the *ḥikmah*? Allah told us. *Ani-shkur lillāh.* Be grateful to Allah.

The wisdom is not credentialed. **The wisdom is oriented.** Luqman's authority to speak to his son does not come from a degree, a tribe, or a prophetic appointment. It comes from the fact that he has placed himself correctly in relation to Allah. Once that placement is correct, *everything he says downstream of it is wisdom*, because every sentence is being spoken from the correct vantage point.

Think of two people standing at the edge of a city. One is facing the wrong direction. He gives you directions to the train station — eloquently, confidently, with great detail. Every word is wrong, because his orientation is wrong. The other one is just a child. He turns, points, and says *that way*. The child's direction is wisdom — not because the child is learned, but because the child is facing the right way.

This is what *ḥikmah* means in the Luqman frame. Face the right way. Place yourself correctly. Then speak.

[PAUSE]

And here is the second half of this theme, which I want you to feel:

The Quran does not require you to be a prophet to leave wisdom for your children. It does not require you to be a scholar to advise your son or daughter. The advice Luqman is about to give — *don't associate partners with Allah, establish the prayer, command the good, bear patiently, don't walk arrogantly, lower your voice* — is the kind of advice any parent who has placed themselves correctly in relation to Allah can give.

You don't need to invent it. You don't need to be qualified to deliver it. You need to **be the one who has been grateful, and from that gratitude, speak.**

**Application: there is a child, a sibling, a friend in your life who needs to hear something from you.** And you have been silent because you don't feel qualified. Luqman is the answer to that silence. The qualification is not credential. The qualification is orientation. Have you placed Allah in His proper place in your own life? Then speak. The words, once they pass through a heart that has done the one placement correctly, become wisdom on their way out.

And the words you speak — they too will return to you. *Innamā yashkuru li-nafsih.* The good you transmit to your children is, in the deepest economy, a gift you are giving to your own *nafs*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, now visible

Look at how the ayah is built.

**First half:** *And We gave Luqman wisdom — namely, be grateful to Allah. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself.*

**Second half:** *And whoever denies — then indeed Allah is Free of need, Praiseworthy.*

The first half is a closed loop: wisdom → gratitude → returns to the self.
The second half is a closed loop: denial → Allah unaffected → (implied: returns to the self).

Two parallel loops. Same architecture. Same direction of flow. **Whatever you put into this system comes back to you.** The only question is whether what comes back is *increase* (the promise of 14:7) or *loss* (the silent threat of the *kāfir* clause).

And at the very center of the ayah, holding the two loops together, sits the definition of *ḥikmah*: be grateful. The wisdom is in seeing the loop. The folly is in not seeing it.

### Bringing it together

We saw, through the linguistics, that *ḥikmah* is the correct placement of things — and that the most fundamental placement is recognizing yourself as receiver. We saw that gratitude, in Allah's economy, does not flow outward to a needy God but curves back to fill the one who offers it. We saw that the names *Ghaniyy* and *Ḥamīd* close every escape route the ungrateful might try to take.

And through the themes, we saw the same insight from the other side. We saw that worship is not loss — it is the instrument of being built. We saw that Allah chose a non-prophet to deliver some of the deepest counsel in the Quran, because the qualification for wisdom is not credential but orientation.

The linguistics and the themes are the same insight from two directions. **You are not giving anything up. You are being given to.**

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life have I been framing worship as a cost — as something I am giving up — when this ayah is telling me it is a gift I am giving to my own *nafs*?

2. Is there an act of *shukr* I have withheld today — a prayer, a *dhikr*, a moment of acknowledgement — because some part of me believed Allah needed it? What changes when I see that He does not?

3. Who in my life is waiting for me to speak — and I have been silent because I felt unqualified? What would it look like to speak from the orientation, not from the credential?

4. *Innamā yashkuru li-nafsih* — exclusively to himself. What in me still doesn't quite believe that every act of worship is preserved, with increase, for me? What would living as if I believed it actually look like?

5. The kāfir's denial does not damage Allah. It only removes the denier from the loop. Am I, in any subtle area of my life, withholding something from Allah under the secret illusion that I am punishing Him by withholding it?

### One-sentence distillation

**Wisdom is recognizing that everything you give to Allah is returning to you, and everything you withhold from Him is being withheld from yourself.**

### The closing invitation

This ayah is the doorway to the Luqman passage. Before we hear a single word the sage says to his son, we are told what made him a sage. He saw the loop. He oriented himself correctly inside it. From that orientation, every word he spoke was wisdom.

The invitation is not to become Luqman. The invitation is to see what Luqman saw. The economy of *Ghaniyy* is a closed loop where the gift you offer is the gift you receive. Step into that loop. Stop calculating worship as cost. And then — from inside that orientation — speak to your son, your daughter, your sibling, your friend, yourself.

The wisdom is the orientation. Everything else flows.

### A closing du'a

*O Allah — You are al-Ghaniyy, who needs nothing from us, and al-Ḥamīd, who is praised whether we praise You or not. Place us, by Your mercy, on the side of those who are grateful — not because You need our gratitude, but because we need to become the kind of soul that gives it. Let every act of worship we offer return to us as Your building of us. And let us speak to those we love from the orientation of Luqman, even when we have nothing else to give them.*

*Āmīn.*
