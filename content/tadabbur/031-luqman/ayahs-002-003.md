# The Book That Becomes a Mercy — Only to Those Who Reach For It

## Surah Luqman, Ayahs 2-3

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:31:2] تِلْكَ ءَايَـٰتُ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ ٱلْحَكِيمِ
> [ayah:31:3] هُدًى وَرَحْمَةً لِّلْمُحْسِنِينَ

**Transliteration:**
> *Tilka āyātu-l-kitābi-l-ḥakīm*
> *Hudan wa raḥmatan li-l-muḥsinīn*

**Translation:**
> Those are the verses of the Wise Book —
> a guidance and a mercy for those who do beautiful deeds.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Behind These Words

Imagine for a moment what it was like to receive these verses in Makkah, when the Quran was still being revealed piece by piece. The community of believers was small. Persecuted. Many of them had family members who refused the message, who mocked it, who said *this Book changes nothing*. Around them, the people of Makkah heard the same recitation they did. The same Arabic. The same cadences. The same words.

And yet — something was happening to one group and not the other.

One group was being transformed. Marriages were being repaired. Slaves were being freed. People who had buried their daughters were weeping at the mention of it. And another group was hearing the same words and walking away harder than before. Same Book. Same air. Same ears.

Why?

That is the question this surah is about to answer — before it ever introduces Luqman, before it tells the story of the wise father teaching his son. Before any of that, it opens with a doorway. And the doorway says: *this Book is not a Book for everyone. It is a guidance and a mercy — for a specific kind of person.*

### The Strand

Surah Luqman is part of a remarkable cluster. Open the surahs just before it — Ash-Shu'arā', An-Naml, Al-Qaṣaṣ — and you will find each of them opening with the same phrase: *tilka āyātu-l-kitāb* — "those are the verses of the Book." The Quran is, in this stretch, repeatedly pointing at itself and asking the reader: *do you see what you are holding?*

But Surah Luqman does something different. It adds one word the others don't. It calls the Book **al-Ḥakīm** — the Wise. And then it does something even more striking: it narrows the audience. It says the Book is a mercy — but not to everyone who picks it up.

### The Hook

[PAUSE]

Read those two ayahs again. Notice what is — and what isn't — there.

It does not say: "a guidance and a mercy for humanity."
It does not say: "a guidance and a mercy for the believers."
It does not say: "a guidance and a mercy for those who read it."

It says: **a guidance and a mercy for the *muḥsinīn*** — the ones who do *iḥsān*. The ones who do beautifully.

That should stop us. Because if you've spent any time around the Quran, you've heard it called a guidance for *people* — for humanity, for the muttaqīn, for the believers. But here, at the threshold of Luqman, Allah is doing something unusual. He is saying: this Book becomes mercy *only when it meets a certain kind of person*.

What kind?

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Strange Pronoun — *Tilka* (Those, not These)

Before we even reach the noun, there is a pronoun. And the pronoun is worth sitting with.

In Arabic, when you point at something close to you — something in your hand, something right in front of you — you use *hādhā* (this) or *hādhihi* (this, feminine). When you point at something far away — something distant, elevated, removed — you use *dhālika* (that) or, for feminine plurals, *tilka* (those, those there).

Let me show you how this works in everyday speech. If you held a book in your hand, in Arabic you would say *hādhā-l-kitāb* — "this book." But if the book were across the room on a high shelf, or in the hand of someone honored, you would say *dhālika-l-kitāb* — "that book over there."

Now hold that in your mind and look at the opening of Surah Al-Baqarah: *Dhālika-l-kitābu lā rayba fīhi* — "*That* Book, no doubt in it." Not *this* Book. *That* Book.

And here, at the opening of Luqman: *Tilka āyātu-l-kitāb* — "*Those* are the verses of the Book."

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The Quran is right there. It is being recited to them. It is in the air around them. By every physical measure, it is *close*. By every physical measure, the right word should be *hādhihi* — *these* verses. But Allah uses the word for the far. The elevated. The distant.

Why?

It is honest to note first what al-Tabari says here. In his commentary on this very verse, he glosses *tilka* directly as *hādhihi* — he explains the meaning as "these are the verses of the Wise Book," treating the two pronouns as functionally equivalent in this context. So before we rest too much weight on the near/far distinction, it should be held carefully: the elevation reading is a *valid* interpretation of a well-attested Arabic practice — using the pronoun of distance for *tafkhīm*, for exaltation — but it is one reading among those that the classical tradition holds. Al-Tabari did not foreground it as the primary meaning; he moved through it.

With that honesty in place — the practice of *tafkhīm* through the far pronoun is real, and it opens something worth sitting with. The words are on your tongue, but their source is on a throne above the seven heavens. You are holding paper and ink. But what you are *actually* holding — its true location, its true rank — is somewhere you cannot reach.

There is a humility that the grammar can invite before any meaning even arrives. Before the Book tells you anything, there is a reminder: *do not think that because you can pronounce these words, you possess them.*

And notice something else. The pronoun *tilka* points. It doesn't describe. It says *look at that*. Allah, in the very first word, is directing your gaze. He is not saying "here is some content for you." He is saying "look — at that — over there." The whole surah is about to teach you wisdom from Luqman, a father to a son, finger pointed at the heavens. And before any of that, the opening word itself is a pointing finger.

The surah is going to be about a sage who teaches by directing attention. And the sage above the sage opens by directing attention first.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Wise Book — *al-Kitāb al-Ḥakīm*

Now look at how Allah names the Book.

He could have called it many things. He has, elsewhere. *Al-Kitāb al-Mubīn* — the Clear Book. *Al-Kitāb al-Karīm* — the Noble Book. *Al-Qur'ān al-Majīd* — the Glorious Recitation. Each name reveals a different facet.

But here, He chooses **al-Ḥakīm** — the Wise.

In Arabic, the root *ḥ-k-m* is one of the richest in the language. It originally meant — and lexicographers like Ibn Fāris and al-Rāghib document this carefully — *to prevent, to restrain, to hold back*. From this root comes the word for a horse's bridle — *ḥakamah* — the strap that prevents the horse from running wild. From this root comes *ḥukm* — judgment, the thing that prevents chaos. From this root comes *ḥikmah* — wisdom, which is the inner restraint that prevents a person from acting foolishly.

So *ḥakīm* is not just "wise" the way English uses the word — clever, insightful, having good ideas. *Ḥakīm* in Arabic carries the meaning of *one whose knowledge prevents error*. One whose wisdom restrains the chaos that would otherwise spill out.

Al-Muyassar, commenting on this opening, reaches for the phrase *ḥikmah bāligha* — *far-reaching, penetrating wisdom* — to capture what is meant by *al-ḥakīm* here. Not wisdom as a static attribute, but wisdom that *arrives*, that *reaches its mark*, that penetrates through. And al-Tabari adds a revealing angle: he glosses *al-ḥakīm* in terms of *bayān wa tafṣīl* — clarification and elaboration. The Book's wisdom is not a vague grandeur; it shows itself in the precision with which it lays things out, taking every matter apart until no harmful ambiguity remains. This is the *ḥikmah* of the bridle: it does not merely adorn the horse. It *functions*. The Book is wise in the way that a precise instrument is wise — nothing in it is where it should not be.

Now — here is what should stop us.

The word *ḥakīm* in Arabic is **ambiguous in a beautiful way**. Grammatically, it can mean either *one who possesses wisdom* (active — the wise one) **or** *one who has been made wise, made firm, made unbreakable* (passive — the perfected one). Both readings are valid Arabic. Both readings are documented by the classical commentators.

So when Allah calls this Book *al-Ḥakīm*, He is saying — and you cannot pick just one — He is saying:

This Book is wise, in the sense that **it itself possesses wisdom** — it speaks wisely, it teaches wisely, it deals with you as a wise teacher would.

And He is saying:

This Book is *ḥakīm*, in the sense that **it has been perfected, made firm, made unbreakable** — every verse is in its place, every word is exact, nothing in it can be moved.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment. The Book is wise — meaning it is itself an agent. It is not a passive object you read. It reads you. It restrains you when you would run wild. It bridles you the way the *ḥakamah* bridles the horse. And at the same time, it is perfected — meaning what you receive when you open it is not raw material you have to fix. It is finished. Complete.

This is why classical scholars say: when you come to the Quran with a problem, do not think you are coming to fix the Book by your questions. The Book has been made firm. You are coming to be fixed *by it*.

And here's what is remarkable about the choice of name in *this* surah specifically. The surah you are about to enter is the surah of *Luqmān al-Ḥakīm* — Luqman the Wise. The sage. Allah is going to spend the central section of this surah quoting a human teacher giving wisdom to his son. And before He does that, He tells you: the Book itself is *al-Ḥakīm*. So that when you reach Luqman's wisdom, you understand — Luqman's wisdom is a *droplet* from a Book that is itself the wisdom.

The human sage in this surah is wise because he drank from a Book that is Wise.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Absence That Speaks — *Hudan wa Raḥmatan*

Now we move to the second ayah. And here is where absence speaks loudest.

*Hudan wa raḥmatan li-l-muḥsinīn* — "a guidance and a mercy for the muḥsinīn."

Two words: *hudan* (guidance) and *raḥmatan* (mercy). They are both **indefinite**. In Arabic, when you make a noun indefinite — when you don't put *al-* on the front of it — you are usually doing one of two things. You are either being non-specific (*some kind of* guidance, *some kind of* mercy), or you are doing something the grammarians call *tafkhīm* — exalting the noun. Making it grand. Saying *what a guidance, what a mercy*.

The classical commentators read this as *tafkhīm*. This is not just any guidance. This is not just any mercy. The indefiniteness here is *amplification*. As if Allah is saying: *guidance unlike any guidance — mercy unlike any mercy*.

There is also, here, a notable grammatical ikhtilaf among the transmitting authorities of the reciters. The majority read *hudan wa raḥmatan* in the accusative — as a circumstantial clause, a *ḥāl*, describing the quality of the Book as it issues guidance and mercy from its verses. Al-Tabari records this majority reading and leans toward it himself. But the reciter Hamza diverged — he read *hudan wa raḥmatun* in the nominative, treating the second ayah as a new, independent declaration entirely: *It is guidance. It is mercy. For the muḥsinīn.* In the majority's reading, the mercy flows out of the Book as its moving quality; in Hamza's reading, it stands as its own announcement. Both are established recitations. Both are the Quran. The difference is one of rhetorical weight — the accusative weaves, the nominative proclaims — and both are simultaneously true.

Good. Now look at the structure. *Hudan wa raḥmatan* — guidance *and* mercy. Two things, joined.

Here's what we have to ask: why both? Why not just guidance? Why not just mercy? Why both, and in this order?

Because **guidance without mercy is law without love**. And **mercy without guidance is sentimentality without direction**.

Let me give you an analogy. Imagine a parent who only ever gives rules. Wake at this time. Eat this. Don't do that. No softness, no warmth, no mercy in how the rules are delivered. The child gets *hudan* — direction — but no *raḥmah*. That child grows up correct and cold.

Now imagine the opposite. A parent who only ever gives mercy. Hugs without limits, comfort without correction, indulgence without instruction. The child gets *raḥmah* but no *hudan*. That child grows up loved and lost.

The Quran refuses to be either of those parents. It comes to you as *hudan wa raḥmah* — direction *and* tenderness, both. The same Book that says *do not approach unlawful gain* also says *if anyone is in difficulty, give him time*. The same Book that prescribes the punishment also says *take what is given easily, and command kindness, and turn away from the ignorant*.

Direction *and* tenderness. Both, woven together. That is the Book.

[PAUSE]

But now — the absence.

Read the ayah one more time: *Hudan wa raḥmatan **li-l-muḥsinīn*** — for the *muḥsinīn*.

It does not say *for humanity*. It does not say *for the believers*. It does not say *for all who read it*. It restricts the audience.

But the same Quran says elsewhere — *hudan li-n-nās* — "guidance for *all* people" (Al-Baqarah 2:185). Same Book. Different ayah. *Guidance for all people.* Here in Luqman — *guidance for the muḥsinīn.*

Is this a contradiction?

No. The commentators resolve this beautifully — and it is one of the most important things you will ever hear about how the Quran works.

The Quran is *offered* as guidance to all people. The doors are wide open. Anyone can walk in. But the Quran *becomes* guidance — actually transforms a heart, actually changes a life, actually moves into a person — only for those who reach toward it with iḥsān.

Imagine the sun. The sun rises on everyone. It is *offered* to all. But who is *warmed* by it? Only those who step out of the cave.

The Book is offered to humanity. But it warms only the ones who step out of the cave of their nafs and turn their face toward it. That stepping out — that turning — is what iḥsān is.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So we now have three things. We have a pronoun that points to a Book physically close but ontologically far. We have a Book named *Wise* — itself an agent, itself perfected, its wisdom showing in the precision with which it elaborates every matter. And we have an audience narrowed: *for the muḥsinīn*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Who are these *muḥsinīn*? And why does the entire mercy of the Quran turn on the question of whether you are one of them?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why Some Books Change You and Most Don't

I want you to think about every book you have ever read.

Most of them passed through you. You don't remember them. The information went in and most of it went out. A few of them lingered for a week. Two or three of them — maybe — actually changed you. You can name them. You know who you were before you read them and who you became after.

What was different about those two or three books?

It was not that the books were objectively better. It was that *you came to them differently*. You came hungry. You came humble. You came with the willingness to be changed. The book met you in a posture, and the posture is what let it in.

Now — this is the secret of the second ayah of Surah Luqman. Allah is telling us something true about all books, but supremely true about *this* Book. **A Book can only become guidance and mercy to a heart that has prepared itself to receive both.**

What is iḥsān? The Prophet ﷺ gave us the definition, in the famous hadith of Jibrīl: *that you worship Allah as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then know that He sees you.*

[PAUSE]

Look at that definition carefully. Iḥsān is not a description of what you do. It is a description of how you *see* — or more precisely, how you orient yourself to the One who sees you even when you cannot see Him. It is the posture of the heart in the act, not just the act itself. You can pray with iḥsān. You can give charity with iḥsān. You can open the Quran with iḥsān. The question is always: are you doing this as someone who is *seen*? As someone who knows they stand in the presence of the One who sees?

This is why the *muḥsinīn* are the ones the Book becomes mercy for. When you open the Quran as someone who is seen — not performing, not interrogating, not armoring yourself against what you might find — you are already in the posture of receptivity. The Book does not need to break through your defenses. There are no defenses. There is only the text and a heart turned toward it.

Now notice what the classical commentators add, quietly but with precision. Al-Muyassar specifies that the *muḥsinīn* are those who "act beautifully upon what Allah revealed in the Quran, and what His Messenger ﷺ commanded them" — both together. The text and its living embodiment in the Prophetic life. Iḥsān is not a free-floating spiritual quality, untethered from either the Book or the one who carried it. It has a channel, a track. The one who opens the Quran and also follows the one through whom the Quran came — that is the *muḥsin*. Al-Tabari, for his part, defines the *muḥsinīn* simply as those who "did beautifully in acting upon what Allah revealed in this Book" — the beauty is inseparable from the doing.

[PAUSE]

There is something important in this insistence on *action*. The *muḥsinīn* are not those who feel beautifully. They are not those who are moved emotionally by the recitation. They are those who act upon what they receive. The iḥsān flows from the hearing into the life — otherwise it has not yet arrived.

### Theme Two: The Contrast the Surah Is Already Forecasting

Ibn Kathir, when he opens his commentary on this surah, frames these opening ayahs against what arrives just four verses later. In verse 6, Allah will speak of those who prefer *lahw al-ḥadīth* — idle speech, frivolous distraction — over the remembrance of Allah. The ones who, when the ayat are recited to them, turn away as though they did not hear them, as though there is weight in their ears.

These are the same people who were in Makkah when this surah descended. The same air, the same recitation. And they turned away.

Ibn Kathir draws on the Quran's own description from Surah Az-Zumar — the picture of a Book that causes the skins of those who fear their Lord to shiver at its recitation, then their skins and their hearts to soften at the remembrance of Allah. The *muḥsinīn* are those who shiver. The ones who soften. And the others — who find the idle talk easier, who prefer the distraction over the demand of the text — they receive no guidance and no mercy from it, not because the Book withholds itself, but because they never arrived at the door.

The surah's opening is already structural foreshadowing. Before the stories begin, these two ayahs tell you what determines which group you will be in. Not your tribe. Not your literacy. Not even your proximity to the recitation. But the quality of your turning.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most sobering things about how the Quran works. It doesn't sort by what you know. It sorts by how you come.

The split between those who shiver and those who turn away is not arbitrary. It is not a decree imposed without cause. It is a description of what the Book does to the two kinds of person who encounter it — and both were present in Makkah when the ink of these ayahs was still settling.

### Theme Three: The Book as the Wiser Luqman

There is one more thing these two ayahs are doing that we should not pass over.

The surah is called Luqman. Its most famous passage is Luqman speaking wisdom to his son — several ayahs of a father directing his child's heart toward Allah, toward gratitude, toward integrity. Classical commentators note that Luqman was not a prophet. He was a wise man, gifted with *ḥikmah*. Someone we are to learn from, to admire, to model.

But the surah does not begin with Luqman. It begins by calling the Book *al-Ḥakīm* — the Wise, the One that holds and embodies wisdom. The Book receives the title before the sage does. In doing so, the surah places Luqman in correct proportion from the very first breath. He is remarkable. His wisdom is real. But he is a *recipient*. The source of his wisdom is the same source being named in these opening ayahs.

When you reach Luqman's speech to his son, you are meant to hear it through this frame: a human wise man, drinking from the Book that is Wisdom itself. His counsel is not self-generated. It is not the fruit of his own intelligence alone. It is what happens when a *muḥsin* engages the *ḥakīm* Book — not just reads it, but acts upon it — over a lifetime, until the Book has made him into something.

Every Luqman was once a student at the feet of a Book that was wiser than he was.

## CONCLUSION

### Becoming the Reader the Book Is Looking For

So we come back to the question with which we started. Same words. Same air. Same ears. Why does one group get transformed and the other gets nothing?

These two ayahs are the answer.

The Book is *al-Ḥakīm* — it is itself wise, itself perfected, its wisdom manifest in the precision with which it clarifies and elaborates every matter. It lacks nothing. Its guidance is not dependent on you. The sun is already shining.

But *hudan wa raḥmatan li-l-muḥsinīn* — the guidance and the mercy go to the *muḥsinīn*. The ones who come with iḥsān. The ones who approach as if they are seen, without armor, willing to be shaped by what they find. The ones who act beautifully upon what the Book says and what the Prophet ﷺ embodied. The ones whose skins shiver at the recitation, then soften — because something in them was already open.

[PAUSE]

Here is the personal question these ayahs leave us with, and it is worth sitting with for as long as it takes.

*Am I a muḥsin — or am I only reading?*

Reading the Quran and being transformed by the Quran are not the same thing. Many people read. Very few are transformed. The difference is not how many pages you cover. It is not how much you can recite from memory. It is not even how much you understand intellectually.

The difference is iḥsān. The posture. The opening. The willingness to be seen by the Book that is already seeing you — and then to act on what it shows you.

[PAUSE]

The Quran is waiting at the threshold it described in its own opening lines. *Tilka* — those, the verses of the Wise Book. The finger was pointed fourteen centuries ago. It has not moved.

The question is whether we are *muḥsinīn* enough to look where it is pointing.

And here is the mercy — the *raḥmah* — woven into even the restricting of the audience: iḥsān is something you can choose to become. The Book would not have named the *muḥsinīn* as its audience if the door to iḥsān were closed. It is not reserved for the scholars. It is not given only to those born into this faith. It is the one quality that determines everything — and it is the one quality available, right now, in this moment, to anyone willing to step out of the cave and turn toward the light.

The Book has been made firm. The Book is wise. The mercy is ready.

The only remaining question is the posture of the heart.
