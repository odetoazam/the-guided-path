# The Inventory You Never Took

## Surah Luqman, Ayah 20

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> أَلَمْ تَرَوْا أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَخَّرَ لَكُم مَّا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَأَسْبَغَ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعَمَهُۥ ظَـٰهِرَةً وَبَاطِنَةً ۗ وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ مَن يُجَـٰدِلُ فِى ٱللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَـٰبٍ مُّنِيرٍ

**Transliteration:**
> *A-lam taraw anna-llāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī-s-samāwāti wa mā fī-l-arḍ, wa asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahū ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah. Wa mina-n-nāsi man yujādilu fī-llāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa lā hudan wa lā kitābin munīr.*

**Translation:**
> *Do you not see that Allah has made subservient to you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and has lavished upon you His blessings — both seen and unseen? Yet among people are those who dispute about Allah without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating Book.*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

To stand at this ayah, you have to feel where it sits.

Just a few verses ago, we were eavesdropping on the most intimate scene in the Quran — a father, Luqman, leaning toward his son, naming the worst injustice, the heaviest weight, the dignity of prayer, the dignity of a moderate walk, the dignity of a lowered voice. It was hushed. It was close. It felt like sitting on a doorstep at dusk while an old man unpacked, slowly, what he had spent his life learning.

And then, with no warning, the camera pulls back.

The doorstep recedes. The father and son shrink to two figures in the distance. And the surah opens onto the entire visible sky and everything beneath it, and a voice that is no longer addressing a child but addressing *all of you*.

> *Do you not see...*

That phrase is doing something we don't always notice. The advice has stopped. Now there is a question. And it is not a small question. It is the question of whether you have ever, in your entire life, looked up.

### The Strand This Ayah Belongs To

Surah Luqman is having a sustained conversation about two ways of inheriting the world. One person inherits the world through *gratitude* — they see what they've been given, they trace it back to its Source, and that tracing reshapes their entire posture. Another person inherits the same world through *disputation* — they argue, they push back, they trade in opinions about the Sacred that they did not pay for in study or in submission.

The surah opened with this contrast. It will close with it. And right here, in ayah 20, the two postures are placed beside each other so we cannot miss them. On one side: an ocean of blessing. On the other side: a man arguing about Allah with empty hands.

### The Hook

There is something in this ayah you have never been given before — not in a Friday sermon, not in a comforting reminder, not in a grateful Instagram caption. It is hidden in plain sight, in two ordinary-looking words that everyone translates and almost no one slows down for.

Allah is about to divide every blessing you have ever received into two categories. And the first category — the one we are most confident about — is not actually the larger one.

Let's earn our way in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to slow down at three places in this ayah. Not to be clever. To let the words do what they were sent to do.

---

### Element One: *Sakhkhara* — The Verb of Cosmic Service

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about Arabic verbs.

Arabic has a system of verb forms — patterns that take a root and bend it into different shades of meaning. The most common form is what scholars call **Form I** — the simple, basic action. *He did something.* But there is another form — **Form II** — which doubles the middle letter, and that doubling carries enormous weight. Form II usually means one of three things: *to do something intensively*, *to cause something to be a certain way*, or *to do something repeatedly and completely*.

Think of the difference between "he taught" and "he taught thoroughly." Between "he broke" and "he shattered." Between "he killed" and "he annihilated." The doubling of the middle letter is the difference between an action and a complete, deliberate, sustained operation.

Now look at the verb Allah uses here.

> *sakhkhara*

This is Form II. The root س-خ-ر (s-kh-r) in its basic form carries the sense of bringing something into service, putting it to work, harnessing it. But Allah does not use the basic form. He intensifies it. He doubles it. He says: *sakhkhara*.

What does this intensification do?

Stay with me. The classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in *al-Mufradāt* — when they treat this root in this form, they describe something specific. *Taskhīr* is not just harnessing. It is a *sustained, willing, complete subservience* in which the thing harnessed cannot resist, cannot refuse, and cannot stop performing its function.

The sun does not get tired. It does not consider whether to rise. The wind does not file a complaint about being moved. Gravity does not negotiate. The cells of your body do not vote on whether to metabolize the food you just ate.

**Everything Allah names here is in a posture of continuous, unbroken obedience to a function that benefits you.**

And notice the preposition that follows: *lakum* — *for you*. Not *to you*. Not *over you*. *For you*. The entire cosmic machinery of stars and seasons and seas and soil — Allah has subjected the whole apparatus, intensively, completely, to your benefit.

Here's what's remarkable. The verb *sakhkhara* is exactly the verb a king would use when describing a servant whose entire existence has been organized around the king's interest. The hierarchy is total. And in this ayah, the hierarchy runs *from* the heavens and the earth *toward you*.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the strangeness of that.

You — the small, anxious, half-distracted person reading these words — have a sun that has been serving you for as long as you have been alive. You have a moon that adjusts the tides on your behalf. You have an atmosphere that has not stopped filtering radiation for a single second of your existence. You have an entire planet's worth of plants that have spent your lifetime exhaling the exact gas your lungs need to inhale.

This is not poetry. This is what the verb says.

And the form Allah chose — Form II, the intensified, doubled, sustained form — tells us that none of this is a one-time gift. It is a *posture*. The universe is *in the posture* of serving you. Right now. While you read.

What does this single word reveal? It reveals that the question of whether the universe is hostile or hospitable is not philosophy. It is a question Allah already answered in one verb.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Asbagha* — The Verb of Lavish Pouring

Now to the second word. And this one is even more striking, because almost no English translation can carry it.

The verb is *asbagha*. The root is س-ب-غ (s-b-gh).

Let me teach you what this root *originally* meant, before you read this ayah.

In the classical Arabic lexicons, the root س-ب-غ is associated with two everyday images — and the lexicographers preserve both. The first image is a **garment that is long and full** — a robe that doesn't just barely cover you, but flows down past your feet, drapes generously, covers you completely with extra to spare. When the early Arabs described a wealthy person's clothing, they used this root: *thawbun sābigh* — a flowing, generous, ample robe.

The second image is **water poured in a complete ablution** — wuḍūʾ done not stingily, not splashing the minimum, but *isbāgh al-wuḍūʾ*, where the water flows fully over every limb being washed. The Prophet ﷺ used this very word when he commanded perfect, generous wuḍūʾ.

So the root carries this picture: *something that flows down and over, completely, generously, with abundance to spare.*

Now Allah takes this root and puts it into Form IV — *asbagha* — which is a causative form. Form IV typically means *to make something happen*, *to cause something to occur*. So *asbagha* means: *He caused-to-flow-down-and-cover-generously.*

> *wa asbagha ʿalaykum niʿamahu*

*And He has poured down over you His blessings — like a robe that drapes past the feet, like water that overflows every limb.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference between this and how we usually think about blessings?

We usually think of blessings as *items*. A list. A count. We say *alhamdulillah* for our health and our family and our job — discrete things, like coins in a pocket. We can imagine ourselves picking each one up and examining it.

But the verb Allah chose is not a verb of items. It is a verb of *pouring*. A verb of *draping*. A verb of *covering completely*.

This means the blessings are not coins in your pocket. They are the *fabric you are dressed in*. They are the *water you are bathing in*. You are not holding them; you are *inside* them. You are *covered by* them. They are flowing over you so generously and so completely that you cannot even see where they begin and end.

Here is the load-bearing image: imagine a person standing in a pool of water that rises to their chest, and the water is warm, and the sun is on it, and they have been in this pool their whole life. If you asked them, *Are you wet?*, they might pause. The question would feel strange. They are not *wet*. They are *immersed*. The water is not an event happening to them. It is the medium of their existence.

That is what the verb *asbagha* says about Allah's blessings on you.

You are not someone who has received some blessings. You are someone who is currently, at this very moment, *immersed* in a flowing robe of mercy that is so total you have stopped noticing it.

**And the gratitude problem of every human being is this: we have mistaken our immersion for our baseline.**

That's the diagnosis hidden in the verb.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Ẓāhiratan wa Bāṭinah* — The Two Categories of Blessing

Now we arrive at the most quietly devastating part of the ayah.

Allah does not just say He has poured blessings on you. He divides those blessings into two categories — and the division is doing structural work we usually miss.

> *ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah*

Both words are adjectives, feminine, singular — agreeing with *niʿam* (blessings) understood as a collective.

*Ẓāhiratan* — from the root ظ-ه-ر — means *outward, apparent, visible, on the surface, perceptible.*

*Bāṭinah* — from the root ب-ط-ن — means *inward, hidden, concealed, in the depths, imperceptible.*

In ordinary Arabic, *ẓāhir* and *bāṭin* are a pair, like *outside* and *inside*. They are the surface and the depth. The skin and the heart. The wall and what is behind the wall.

Now here is where you have to stay with me carefully — because the classical mufassirūn read this division in multiple ways, and each reading is *a camera angle* on the same reality. Let me show you the composite.

**One reading:** the visible blessings are the ones you can name — health, wealth, family, food, shelter, limbs that work. The hidden blessings are the ones you cannot see — faith in the heart, protection from harm that nearly happened, the angels who guard from front and back, the diseases that did not come, the accidents that did not occur.

**Another reading:** the visible blessings are the ones in this world. The hidden blessings include the promised mercy of the next world.

**Another reading:** the visible blessings are bodily — what you feel and use. The hidden blessings are spiritual — what your soul receives without your knowing.

**Another reading:** the visible are the ones you recognize as blessings. The hidden are the ones you have not yet realized were blessings — including the things you currently consider hardships.

Now do not pick one of these. Lay them all on top of each other. Each angle reveals something the others cannot. The composite picture is what Allah meant.

**Every blessing you can name is in the first category. Every blessing you cannot name is in the second.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question I want you to sit with. The question that almost no one asks of this ayah.

*Which category is bigger?*

Look at the words again. Allah lists *ẓāhiratan* first and *bāṭinah* second. In Arabic, when you have a balanced pair like this, neither word is being diminished. But notice: the verb is *asbagha* — *He has caused to flow down and cover generously*. The verb itself implies *more than you can count*. And then He divides this uncountable abundance into two categories. Visible. And hidden.

Here is what the linguistic structure forces us to consider:

The hidden blessings — the *bāṭin* ones — are not the small remainder. They are not the asterisk. The grammatical equality of *ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah* tells us that the unseen blessings are at minimum *as vast* as the seen ones.

And once you start tallying — every accident that did not happen, every illness that did not strike, every harm Allah turned away that you never knew about, every guidance He placed in your heart that you mistook for your own insight, every door He closed because it would have destroyed you, every person He kept out of your life, every du'ā He answered in a form you didn't recognize — once you start tallying these, the math becomes terrifying.

**The category of blessings you don't know about may be larger than the category of blessings you do.**

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider: every gratitude inventory you have ever taken in your life has been an inventory of *one half* of the gift. Possibly the smaller half.

The *alhamdulillah* you say at dinner is gratitude for the visible. There is an entire second *alhamdulillah* — for everything you cannot see and never will — that you have never said. That you cannot say. Because you don't know what's in it.

The only person who can thank Allah for the *bāṭin* blessings is Allah Himself. Which is why every traditional du'ā for thanking Allah for His blessings ends with a phrase like *kamā yanbaghī li-jalāli wajhik* — *as befits the majesty of Your face* — because we are admitting that we cannot match the gift with our gratitude.

This single grammatical pairing — *ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah* — rearranges the whole posture of the believer. You are not a person trying to remember to be thankful. You are a person who has been given so much that you literally cannot count, cannot see, and cannot name the majority of it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### The Bridge

Three words. *Sakhkhara*. *Asbagha*. *Ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah*.

The first told us that the universe is in a posture of sustained service toward us. The second told us that we are not holding blessings in our pockets — we are immersed in a flowing robe of them. The third told us that what we can see is only one half.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Illusion of Self-Authorship

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a very specific feeling that comes when you finish a hard week. You sit down, exhausted but satisfied, and look at what you've built. The presentation that landed. The project that shipped. The diploma on the wall. The relationship you held together through the rough patch. The body you trained. The income you generated.

And there is a quiet voice — not arrogant, not even loud — that says: *I did this*.

This voice is not evil. It is the voice of human agency, and Islam does not ask us to silence it. Allah holds us accountable for our choices precisely because our choices are real. But the voice has a *blind spot*, and that blind spot is what this ayah is excavating.

The voice that says *I did this* is operating with a budget it did not write. It assumes — silently, automatically, without ever auditing the assumption — that the room it built in was provided. That the body it used was working. That the oxygen was filtering. That the parents who raised it existed. That the language it used to think with was learned in childhood by means it cannot remember. That the food that fueled the work was grown somewhere, transported by someone, paid for with money earned in a world that hadn't collapsed that morning.

The voice presumes the universe, and then claims credit for what was done *inside* the universe.

This is what *sakhkhara* exposes. Allah is not saying *you live in a hostile environment that you have heroically tamed*. He is saying *the entire environment was already subdued for your benefit before you arrived*. The taming was done. You inherited a tamed cosmos. You did not build it. You were born into it.

And then *asbagha* goes further. It says that not only is the environment subdued — but you are immersed, this very moment, in a flowing garment of mercy you did not weave.

The illusion of self-authorship is not the illusion that you did nothing. It is the illusion that what you did was the whole story. That you wrote the chapter without acknowledging the book.

Here is what this changes about tomorrow:

The next time you complete something you are proud of — close the laptop, finish the workout, hand in the project — try a small experiment. Before you say *I did this*, say *Allah subjected this to me so I could do this*. Say it once. Quietly. Just to yourself.

Notice what shifts. Not the pride — the pride is allowed, it is healthy, it is human. What shifts is the *direction* of the gratitude. Instead of bouncing back to yourself, it travels upward. Instead of inflating you, it grounds you.

This is the posture *sakhkhara* is asking for. Not the denial of your effort. The proper *contextualization* of it.

### Theme Two: The Disputer With Empty Hands

Now turn to the second half of the ayah. Because Allah does not let the celebration of blessings stand alone. He places, right next to it, a figure who is the opposite.

> *Wa mina-n-nāsi man yujādilu fī-llāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa lā hudan wa lā kitābin munīr.*

> *And among people are those who dispute about Allah without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating Book.*

Why is this here?

Stay with the juxtaposition. Allah has just shown us the universe pouring itself out for us. He has shown us a robe of mercy draping over us. He has shown us a category of hidden blessings we cannot even count.

And then He says: *and yet* — among people — *there are those who argue with Me.*

The verb *yujādilu* is from the root ج-د-ل. This root, in its original sense, referred to *twisting a rope tightly* — pulling strands against each other to make the rope strong. From this came the metaphorical meaning: contention, disputation, the back-and-forth twisting of arguments. *Jadal* is not honest inquiry. It is the *posture* of pulling against. It is argument as identity — argument for the sake of being the one arguing, not for the sake of arriving anywhere.

And then — pay attention to this — Allah lists what the disputer is *missing*. Not in one phrase. In three.

> *bi-ghayri ʿilm* — without knowledge
> *wa lā hudan* — and without guidance
> *wa lā kitābin munīr* — and without an illuminating Book

Three empty hands.

The first — *ʿilm* — is knowledge gained through rational inquiry, through study, through evidence. *I do not know this, but I will dispute about it anyway.*

The second — *hudā* — is inner guidance, the soft light Allah places in a sincere heart. *I have no inward conviction either, but I will dispute about it anyway.*

The third — *kitāb munīr* — is revelation, an *illuminating* book. The adjective *munīr* is from the root ن-و-ر — light. A book that *gives light*. *I have no revealed scripture supporting me, but I will dispute about it anyway.*

The disputer has nothing. No reasoning. No intuition. No revelation. And he is arguing about Allah.

[PAUSE]

Now read this with your own life in view.

There is a posture available to every human being in every era — and our era has industrialized it — where you offer opinions about the Sacred that you have not paid for. You did not study. You did not pray. You did not cultivate inner stillness. You did not read the Book seriously. And yet you have a *take*. You have a *position*. You have a *strong feeling* about what Allah should or should not have said, done, allowed, forbidden.

This is *jadal bi-ghayri ʿilm*. Disputation with empty hands.

And here is the connection to the first half of the ayah — and this is where the architecture of the verse becomes breathtaking.

The disputer is *also* drowning in *asbagha*. The disputer is *also* under the flowing robe of *ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah*. The sun is rising for him. The earth is feeding him. His lungs are filtering air for him in the same instant he forms a sentence pushing back against the One who designed his lungs.

The ayah is not just describing a bad person. It is describing the most extreme form of ingratitude possible: *to be immersed in a mercy so vast you cannot count it, and to spend your tongue pushing back against the One pouring it.*

[PAUSE]

Why does Allah place these two halves of the ayah together?

Because gratitude and disputation are two ways of holding the same reality. One person looks at the *sakhkhara* and the *asbagha* and falls into thankfulness. Another person looks at the same things and looks for a flaw. Same universe. Same blessings. Two postures.

The question this ayah leaves you with is not *Are you the disputer?* You are probably not the disputer in his fully developed form. The question is more uncomfortable: *Have you ever held an opinion about Allah that you did not earn?*

Have you ever pushed back against an ayah without studying it? Have you ever decided Allah's command was unreasonable without sitting with a single classical commentary? Have you ever defended a position on the Sacred that you adopted because it felt modern, not because you investigated it?

The disputer's tools are *bi-ghayri ʿilm, wa lā hudan, wa lā kitābin munīr*. Knowledge, guidance, an illuminating Book. When you are about to form a position on Allah, on His Messenger ﷺ, on a verse, on a ruling — pause and audit your hands. *Do I have any of the three?* If not, the right posture is silence and study. Not opinion.

Here is what this changes about tomorrow:

When the next hot-button question about Islam crosses your feed — and one will — instead of forming a quick opinion, try saying, just to yourself: *I do not have ʿilm here, I do not have hudā here, I do not have an illuminating book on this here. Let me be quiet until I do.*

This is not intellectual cowardice. It is intellectual integrity. The Quran is asking for *epistemic humility about the Sacred*. Not silence — but silence *until you have something*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Let me show you what we have actually been reading.

This single ayah has two halves, and the halves are mirrors.

The first half describes a *reality*: a cosmos in service, a robe of mercy, blessings seen and unseen.

The second half describes a *response*: a human being who, faced with all this, picks up an argument instead of a *sajdah*.

The first half is what Allah *gives*. The second half is how some people *receive*.

And right between them sits a single word — *wa* — *and yet* — which is the hinge of the entire ayah. The hinge between mercy and refusal. The hinge between immersion and ingratitude.

You stand at that hinge every time you wake up.

---

### Bringing It Together

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Sakhkhara* taught us that the universe is in a posture of sustained service to us — and the theme of *self-authorship* showed us that we routinely mistake the gift of that service for our own achievement.

*Asbagha* taught us that we are immersed in a robe of mercy — and the theme of *empty-handed disputation* showed us that the disputer is also immersed, also covered, also benefiting, and that this is what makes his disputation so devastating.

*Ẓāhiratan wa bāṭinah* taught us that the blessings we cannot see may exceed the ones we can — and the entire arc of the ayah asks us: which posture are we taking toward this immeasurable gift?

---

### Questions to Carry

1. If half of my blessings are hidden from me, what does it mean to live as someone who knows they are receiving gifts they cannot count?
2. When I last said *alhamdulillah*, was I thanking Allah for the visible category only — or did I include the second?
3. Where in my life am I living as if I built the room I was placed in?
4. Have I ever held a position on Allah, on the Quran, on the Sunnah, that I did not earn with knowledge, guidance, or an illuminating Book?
5. The disputer is breathing air Allah gave him while arguing with Allah. Where in me does this pattern exist in smaller forms?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

You are immersed at every moment in a flowing robe of mercy whose hidden folds outnumber its visible ones — and the only proper response is a tongue that thanks, not a tongue that argues.

---

### The Closing Invitation

If something should shift after this ayah, let it be small and concrete.

Tonight, before you sleep, try this: do not list your blessings. Listing is the *ẓāhir* mode — and we are not very good at it anyway. Instead, simply say:

*Yā Allāh, I cannot see most of what You have given me. Most of Your mercy on me is in the bāṭin. I thank You for what I cannot count, what I cannot name, and what I will never know about until the Day You show me.*

That sentence is more accurate than any blessing inventory you have ever taken.

### Du'ā

*O Allah, You have subjected for me a universe I did not build, and draped over me a robe of mercy I cannot measure. Teach me the gratitude that fits the visible blessings, and forgive me for every hidden blessing I have received and never thanked You for. Keep my tongue from disputing about You with hands that hold no knowledge, no guidance, and no illuminating Book. Make me of those who see — and seeing, fall silent in thanks.*

*Āmīn.*
