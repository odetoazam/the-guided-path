# Surah Luqman, Ayah 25 — The Admission That Indicts

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَلَئِن سَأَلْتَهُم مَّنْ خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ لَيَقُولُنَّ ٱللَّهُ ۚ قُلِ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ۚ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
Wa la-in sa'altahum man khalaqa s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa la-yaqūlunna Allāh. Quli l-ḥamdu lillāh. Bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn.

**Translation:**
"And if you were to ask them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' — they would surely say, 'Allah.' Say: All praise belongs to Allah. But most of them do not know."

---

## INTRODUCTION

Imagine for a moment that you're a prosecutor in a courtroom. You have a defendant who has spent years living a certain way — making certain choices, dismissing certain claims, ignoring certain calls. You ask them one question. Just one. And their answer to that question, given honestly, contradicts everything they've been doing for years.

You don't need a second witness. You don't need more evidence. Their own mouth has just indicted them.

That is the texture of this ayah.

Now let's situate ourselves. We are deep in Surah Luqman. This surah began by introducing **the Book** as guidance for *al-muḥsinīn* — those who excel in goodness. Then it set up the central contrast: a man named Luqman, full of wisdom, sitting his son down and teaching him how to live — versus the people who **buy idle speech** (*lahw al-ḥadīth*) to mislead from Allah's path without knowledge. Wisdom versus distraction. Knowing versus being seduced into not-knowing.

By the time we arrive at ayah 25, the surah has been pressing on one question for verses: **what does it actually mean to know?** Because clearly there are people who, when you ask them directly, will say the right thing. They'll affirm Allah. But their lives don't bend to that affirmation. They walk away from the question unchanged. They had the data but it never became them.

[PAUSE]

And here's the moment of pressure this ayah responds to: the early Meccan community — the polytheists the Prophet ﷺ was speaking to — were not atheists. They were not people who had concluded there was no creator. **They believed Allah created the heavens and the earth.** That was never in dispute. What was in dispute was everything that followed from that belief: prayer, accountability, resurrection, leaving the idols of their fathers, restructuring their economic lives, sitting at the feet of an unlettered man from their own tribe.

So Allah doesn't ask them to prove Allah exists. He does something far more cutting. He shows them that they already know — and asks why their lives don't match the knowing.

Here's the question you're going to want answered before we're done:

**How can someone say "Allah" with their tongue, and still — by Allah's own testimony — "not know"? What kind of knowing is the Qur'an talking about?**

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to open three doors into this ayah. Each one quiet. Each one devastating.

### Element One: The Conditional That Already Knows the Answer

Let's start with how the sentence opens: **وَلَئِن سَأَلْتَهُم** — "and if you were to ask them..."

In Arabic, when you want to say "if," you have choices. There is *idhā* — "when," used for things you expect to happen. There is *in* — "if," for genuine uncertainty. And then there is **لَئِن** — *la-in* — which is *in* preceded by an emphatic particle. It's a heavier "if." A more theatrical "if." The kind of "if" you use when you already know exactly what's going to happen if the condition is met.

Let me bring it into your world. Imagine a parent talking about their teenager. The parent could say, "If you ask him whether he did his homework, he'll mumble." Or the parent could say, "**I swear to you, if** you ask him whether he did his homework, he'll mumble." The second version has built into it the certainty of the outcome. The asking is hypothetical. The answer is not.

That's what *la-in* does here. Allah is not entertaining the possibility that they might say something else. The particle is loaded with the certainty of the response before the response is given.

And then look at how the response itself is rendered: **لَيَقُولُنَّ** — *la-yaqūlunna*. Two emphasis markers stacked. The *lām* at the start. The doubled *nūn* of emphasis at the end. In English we don't have a clean equivalent, but it's something like: "they will absolutely, definitely, without question say."

Notice something. Allah is using the heaviest possible Arabic emphasis — the kind reserved for oaths and certainties — to describe **the answer of someone who is wrong about everything else.**

Stay with that.

The polytheists, in Allah's own grammar, are made to swear-by-the-construction-of-the-sentence that Allah is the Creator. The form of the verb leaves them no escape route. **The grammar itself becomes a witness against them.**

[PAUSE]

Here's what this single linguistic element reveals: there is a category of truth that the human being knows so deeply that even when they spend their lives denying its implications, **you can still pull the admission out of them with one question.** Allah is not telling us they were unsure and came around. He is telling us they were certain the whole time. The disbelief was never an intellectual problem. It was something else.

What that something else is — we'll get to.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The One-Word Sermon — *Quli l-ḥamdu lillāh*

Now look at what comes next. They've answered. The admission is on the record. And Allah does not say to His Prophet ﷺ: "Now argue with them. Now prove the next step. Now build the case for prophethood."

He says: **قُلِ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ** — "Say: All praise belongs to Allah."

Stop here. This is one of the most underread moments in the surah.

Why this response? Why not a refutation? Why not a follow-up question? Why *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — the phrase we say after we sneeze, after we eat, when something good happens?

Let me teach the word first. *Ḥamd* in Arabic is not exactly "thanks" and not exactly "praise" — it's both fused into one. *Shukr* is thanks for a specific favor you received. *Madḥ* is praise you might give to anyone for any quality, even insincerely. But **ḥamd** is praise that simultaneously acknowledges the praised one's qualities AND expresses gratitude for what flows from them — and it's only fitting for one who is genuinely worthy.

And the definite article *al-* in *al-ḥamd* matters. It's not "*some* praise belongs to Allah." It's not "*a* praise." It's **the** praise. The entirety of the category. Every instance of genuine praise, wherever it occurs, properly belongs to Him.

So now look at what's happening in the ayah. They have just confessed, with the heaviest grammatical emphasis available, that Allah created the heavens and the earth.

And the Prophet ﷺ is told to respond: **"Then al-ḥamd belongs to Him."**

Notice something. He is not being told to *thank Allah for the polytheists' admission.* He is being told something far stranger and far more powerful:

**Their own admission has just established the case.**

If Allah created the heavens and the earth, then everything good, everything beautiful, everything functional, every breath those polytheists are taking as they make their admission — all of it traces back to Him. Their entire lives are running on infrastructure He built. And the proper response to that — the only coherent response — is that **all praise is His.**

So when the Prophet ﷺ is told to say *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, he is not exclaiming. He is **drawing the logical conclusion their tongues just demanded.**

Let me make this concrete. Imagine someone in a courtroom admits, under oath, that they were the only one in the building. The prosecutor doesn't need to argue anymore. The prosecutor just says: "The record shows."

*Quli l-ḥamdu lillāh* is the Prophet ﷺ saying: **the record shows.**

It is the one-word verdict pronounced over their own testimony. They affirmed creation. Praise follows creation as inescapably as a shadow follows a body. **They have, with their own mouths, established that Allah alone deserves the praise they are giving to others.**

[PAUSE]

And here's what's remarkable. He is not told to scold them. He is not told to threaten them. He is told to **praise Allah.** Because the most devastating thing you can do to a person whose life contradicts what they just admitted is not attack them — it is calmly state the conclusion that follows from their own words and let them sit with the gap.

This is the surgical precision of prophetic rhetoric. No volume. No argument. Just: *"Then all praise is His."* And the silence after.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Word "Most" — *Bal Aktharuhum*

Now the final movement of the ayah. **بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ** — "Rather, most of them do not know."

Let me first teach the particle. **بَلْ** — *bal*. In Arabic grammar this is a particle of *iḍrāb* — of "rather," "no, actually," or "in fact." It functions in two ways: sometimes it cancels what came before. Sometimes it *deepens* what came before — going past the obvious to something more subtle.

Here, it is the second. They said "Allah." Their tongues spoke truth. *Bal* — *but underneath that tongue-truth, most of them do not actually know.*

Now look at the word **أَكْثَرُهُمْ** — *aktharuhum*. "Most of them."

This word is doing precise theological work. Allah does not say *kulluhum lā yaʿlamūn* — "all of them do not know." He says **most**. Why?

Because among those very polytheists, there were people whose hearts were quietly turning. There were people for whom this admission — when they really heard themselves say it — was the moment the wall cracked. *Most* doesn't know. **Some** are about to.

The word *aktharuhum* is a door left open. Allah is making a statistical statement about a community in motion. He is not closing the case on every single individual. He is telling the Prophet ﷺ: the majority will not let their own admission move them. **But not all. Watch for the ones who will.**

[PAUSE]

And now the heart of it. **لَا يَعْلَمُونَ** — "they do not know."

But wait. They just said "Allah created the heavens and the earth." How can Allah say they don't know?

Here is where Arabic does what English struggles to do.

*ʿIlm* in the Qur'an is not just data possession. It is not just being able to give the right answer on a test. *ʿIlm* in the Qur'anic sense is knowledge that has **traveled from the tongue to the chest and from the chest to the limbs.** Knowledge that organizes a life. Knowledge that, once you have it, you cannot live the same way you did before.

There is a knowing that says "Allah created everything" and walks past a beggar without flinching. There is a knowing that says "Allah created everything" and bows the head before the next sunrise. The first is information. **Only the second is *ʿilm* in the Qur'anic sense.**

So Allah, with surgical accuracy, says: their tongues affirmed. *Bal* — but most of them, **the affirmation never became knowledge.** It stayed at the lip. It never reached the limb.

[PAUSE]

Let me build a bridge before we close this section. Listen for it in this ayah, and in 29:61, and 29:63, and 39:38, and 43:9, and 43:87 — every single one of these is the same construction: *"And if you ask them..."* — they answer correctly — and then a corrective comes. **The Qur'an returns to this scene again and again.** It is one of the most repeated argumentative moves in the Book.

Why? Because Allah is teaching us something about the human condition that we will spend a lifetime mistaking. **The biggest danger to the human soul is not unbelief in the explicit sense. It is the *unbelief that lives inside a confession of belief.*** The "yes" that doesn't change anything. The "Allah" on the tongue with no Allah in the heart.

[PAUSE — major pause]

The linguistic construction has now shown us three things:

1. The admission of Allah's creatorship was certain — so certain that Allah used the heaviest grammar to render it.
2. That admission, properly understood, hands the entire case to Allah — *al-ḥamdu lillāh.*
3. And yet most who make the admission do not actually *know* what they have just said.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself...

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Quiet Tragedy of the Mouth That Outpaces the Heart

I want you to think about something that happens to every one of us.

You're standing at a graveside. Someone is being lowered into the earth. Maybe a relative. Maybe a friend. And in that moment, with the soil being shoveled, something inside you tightens. You think: *this is real. This is going to be me. There's nothing on the other side of this except whatever Allah has prepared.*

You feel it. For about ninety seconds, you actually *know* you are going to die.

Then you get in the car. You start scrolling. You're back to whatever you were before. The funeral becomes a memory. The knowing dissolves.

Now ask yourself: did you *know* you were going to die? Or did you only *say* you knew?

[PAUSE]

This is the human condition the ayah is diagnosing. Not the condition of someone who never heard about Allah. The condition of someone who has heard, who can recite, who can answer the question — and whose answer cannot follow them out of the funeral home.

The polytheists in Mecca were not strangers to Allah's name. They built their lives around the Kaʿba. They circumambulated a house they knew Ibrahim had built. They had pieces of Abrahamic memory in their ancestry. And yet — when the Prophet ﷺ came calling them to the implications of what they already affirmed, they could not move. The affirmation was real on their lips and inert in their chests.

Allah is showing us something terrifying: **it is possible to have the right words your whole life and never let those words rearrange you.**

And here is the deeper twist. The ayah does not call them liars. It does not say "they say Allah but they're hiding the truth." No — it says *bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn.* The diagnosis is not dishonesty. **The diagnosis is shallowness.** Most of them really did believe what they said. They simply never let it travel inward. They were sincere, and ignorant, simultaneously.

[PAUSE]

Now turn this on yourself. Because if you are reading this — and you say the *shahāda*, and you pray, and you fast, and you read the Qur'an — the question this ayah is going to put to you is not "do you believe?" It is:

**Has what you believe actually become knowledge inside you, or are you a sincere reciter of statements that have not yet moved into your chest?**

You believe Allah sees you. Do you live like you are seen?

You believe death is coming. Do you live like it is coming?

You believe every word produces a record. Do your words come out like words that are being recorded?

The gap between the *qawl* (saying) and the *ʿilm* (knowing) — that is the gap the entire Qur'an is trying to close. **That gap is the work of a lifetime.** That gap is what tadabbur is for. That gap is why this Book is read and reread and reread, because every reading is an attempt to push another inch of confession down from the tongue into the chest.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Try one thing. Pick **one** statement of faith you make routinely — *al-ḥamdu lillāh, alḥamdu lillāh ʿalā kulli ḥāl, Allāhu akbar, lā ilāha illa Allāh*. Just one. And for one week, every time it leaves your mouth, pause for two seconds and ask: *do I actually mean this right now, or am I one of the polytheists in 31:25 — answering correctly with a tongue that is racing ahead of my heart?*

That two-second pause is the work of moving from *yaqūlunna* to *yaʿlamūn.* From saying to knowing.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Apologetic of Allah — Why He Asks Instead of Proves

There is something else stunning happening here that we have to name before we close. Notice what Allah does *not* do in this ayah.

He does not prove He exists. He does not list signs in the heavens. He does not point to the orbits of the stars. He does not argue from design. He does not invoke a kalām-style first-cause argument.

He just **asks one question.** And lets their answer be the proof.

This is one of the most distinctive moves in the Qur'anic argument, and it tells you something about how Allah sees the human soul. Allah is not operating from the premise that humans are blank slates who need to be convinced of His existence. He is operating from the premise that **the recognition of Allah is already inside every human being** — and the work of revelation is not to *install* it but to *uncover* it.

This is what the scholars have sometimes called the argument from *fiṭrah* — the original orientation Allah placed in the human soul. Every child born is born oriented toward their Creator. Every adult, even one who has spent decades arguing against, still carries the trace of that orientation. **And on any given day, with one well-placed question, you can pull it up to the surface.**

Look at the strategy in this ayah. Allah does not say: "Prove to them I exist." He says to His Prophet: **"Ask them."** Because their answer is already inside them. The Prophet ﷺ is not being told to be a philosopher. He is being told to be a midwife — to deliver what is already in the womb of their hearts.

[PAUSE]

This has a profound implication for how we engage with people in our own lives — Muslim and non-Muslim alike. We have inherited, mostly from secular discourse, the assumption that belief is established by *argument* — by stacking evidence until the case is closed. But the Qur'anic strategy is different. **Belief is established by asking the question that lets the soul remember what it already knows.**

When you sit with a friend who is in pain, who has been running from God for years, you do not need to convert them with a debate. You need to ask the question that gives their fiṭrah permission to speak. "What do you think happens when you die?" "Have you ever felt watched even when you were alone?" "Where do you think this all came from?"

These are not gotcha questions. They are *Luqman 31:25* questions. They are invitations for the soul to admit what it has been carrying all along.

And the same applies to your own inner work. When your heart is in turmoil, when faith feels thin, when the practice feels mechanical — the answer is not always to read another book or hear another lecture. **Sometimes the answer is to ask yourself the question and listen for what your soul already knows.** *Who created the heavens and the earth?* Sit with it. Let your own *la-yaqūlunna* rise up. Then say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* and notice — for one honest second — what your own confession just placed on your shoulders.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Two things.

One — when you meet someone in doubt or struggle, lead with a question, not a sermon. The Qur'an is teaching us that *the question is the dawah*. The asking is what cracks the shell.

Two — practice asking yourself the obvious. Every few days, in a quiet moment, put the most basic question to yourself: *Who made all this?* And let your own answer hit you again. Not as data you already have. As something you are admitting fresh. Because the ayah is telling us that **the act of confession is not a one-time event. It needs to be renewed**, or the mouth keeps saying it while the heart forgets.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Now Visible

Look at the shape of the ayah one more time. It has three movements, and now we can see how they lock together.

**First movement:** the question and its inevitable answer. Allah builds the courtroom and elicits the testimony. *Wa la-in sa'altahum man khalaqa s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa la-yaqūlunna Allāh.* The construction is so emphatic that the answer cannot be evaded.

**Second movement:** the verdict drawn from the testimony itself. *Quli l-ḥamdu lillāh.* Not an argument. A logical consequence. If Allah is the Creator, all praise is His. Their confession has just signed the deed.

**Third movement:** the diagnosis of why, despite the confession, life does not change. *Bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn.* They say without knowing. The mouth has outpaced the heart.

Now hold all three together. The ayah is **a complete forensic procedure in eighteen words.** Question. Testimony. Verdict. Diagnosis. It is the Qur'anic argument compressed to its essence. And it works on the polytheist in 7th-century Mecca, and it works on the Muslim in 21st-century anywhere, and it will work on whoever opens this Book tomorrow.

[PAUSE]

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

The grammar told us Allah uses the heaviest emphasis for the polytheists' answer — because the affirmation of Allah's creatorship is one of the most certain things in the human heart.

The theme told us the tragedy is not in the answer but in what comes after — that this most-certain truth often produces the least-changed life.

The grammar told us *al-ḥamdu lillāh* is the verdict, not the celebration.

The theme told us that praise is the only coherent response to creation — and that to keep saying *al-ḥamdu lillāh* without letting it move you is to be the very person the ayah is diagnosing.

The grammar told us *aktharuhum* — most, not all.

The theme told us the door is open. Some will let the confession become knowledge. **The ayah is also asking which group you intend to be in.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one thing I confess with my tongue that has not yet reached my limbs?
2. When was the last time I said *al-ḥamdu lillāh* and actually felt the weight of what those words mean?
3. If someone asked me, today, "Who created the heavens and the earth?" — and my answer was put under the same emphatic grammar Allah uses for the polytheists — would my life, examined separately, give the same answer?
4. Who in my life is a person whose fiṭrah needs a question, not an argument?
5. Am I in the *aktharuhum* group, or am I one of the ones whose confession is quietly becoming knowledge?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The most dangerous spiritual condition is not denying Allah — it is affirming Him with a tongue that runs faster than the heart, and Allah's mercy is that He will keep asking us the question until our knowing catches up with our saying.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to do anything dramatic with this ayah. You do not need to change your whole life by Maghrib.

You need to do one small thing: pick one phrase you say often — *al-ḥamdu lillāh* is the natural choice, since this ayah handed it to you — and for one week, before you say it, take a breath and mean it. Just one phrase. Just one breath. Just one week.

That is how the *yaqūlunna* of the lip becomes the *yaʿlamūn* of the chest. Not by force. By patient, deliberate, repeated narrowing of the gap.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who do not need to prove to us what You have already placed inside us — do not let us be among the *aktharuhum* who say and do not know. Take every *al-ḥamdu lillāh* that leaves our tongues and press it gently into our chests until it reaches our limbs. And let our confession of You as Creator be the door through which the rest of our lives finally walk in. Āmīn.
