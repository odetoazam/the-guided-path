# The Signature of False Worship — When Your God Cannot Answer

> أَفَلَا يَرَوْنَ أَلَّا يَرْجِعُ إِلَيْهِمْ قَوْلًا وَلَا يَمْلِكُ لَهُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا نَفْعًا
>
> *Afalā yarawna allā yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan wa-lā yamliku lahum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā.*
>
> **"Did they not see that it could not return to them a single word — and possessed for them no power to harm, nor to benefit?"**
>
> *— Surah Ta-Ha, ayah 89*

---

## Introduction — The World Beneath the Mountain

To feel this ayah, we have to stand where they stood.

A people who have just walked through the sea. Who saw a wall of water collapse on the army that had enslaved them for generations. Who are camped now at the foot of a mountain that smokes and trembles because their leader has gone up to speak with God.

He is gone, what — forty days? Long enough. Long enough for the silence to feel heavy. Long enough for someone to whisper: *what if he doesn't come back?*

They have gold. Bracelets, anklets, rings — the spoils of a civilization they finally walked out of. Heavy gold, nervous gold. And there is a man among them — the Quran calls him *al-Sāmiriyy* — who has a plan.

He gathers the gold. He throws it into a fire. He throws something else in too — a handful of dust from somewhere — and from that fire emerges a shape: a **calf**, formed of gold, and from it comes a sound. A *lowing*. The Quran describes it precisely: **lahu khuwār** — *it has a lowing.* The sound a cow makes.

And the people look at this glittering, sounding object — gold catching the firelight, a noise emerging from its mouth — and they say what no people had said since the sea split open for them:

> *"This is your god, and the god of Musa, but he has forgotten."*

Harun stands among them shouting. Begging. *Your Lord is the Most Merciful — follow me — obey my command.* They will not listen.

[PAUSE]

And then, mid-story, the Quran itself stops.

The narrative voice, which had been reporting their words and their movements, suddenly turns and asks a question. Not to Musa. Not to Harun. Not even to them, exactly. To us. To everyone reading. To every soul that has ever bent itself toward something that gleamed and made noise.

> *Did they not see — that it could not return to them a single word? That it owned for them no harm, and no benefit?*

Here is what is stunning. The object made a sound. They have just been told it makes a sound. The previous ayah said so explicitly. So why does Allah ask whether they noticed it could not **speak**?

The answer is going to break open something we did not know was inside this verse — something about the difference between **noise** and **answer**, between **spectacle** and **relationship**, between a god you can see and a God who can be **called**.

Stay with me.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Afalā yarawna* — The Question That Is Not a Question

Before we look at the Arabic, let me show you a small thing in our own speech.

When a parent walks into a kitchen where the milk has been left out, the bread is on the floor, and a child stands in the middle of the wreckage — the parent rarely says *"Please clean this."* They say:

> *"Do you not see what you've done?"*

That is not a question. The child has eyes. The parent knows the child has eyes. The form of the sentence is interrogative, but the **function** is something else — a rebuke wrapped in a question, an invitation to look at what is already in front of you. In Arabic rhetoric this device has a name: *al-istifhām al-inkārī* — the question of denial, the question of rebuke. Allah uses it constantly with people who have stopped looking at what is plainly there.

Now look at the opening of our ayah:

> **أَفَلَا يَرَوْنَ** — *Afalā yarawna* — "Do they not, then, see?"

Three small particles do enormous work here. The hamza is the question. The *fa* is the connective — *given everything I just said*. The *lā* is the negation. *Given everything — do they not, then, see?*

And the verb — *yarawna* — is from ر-أ-ي, the root of physical sight. Not *do they not understand,* not *do they not realize.* **Do they not see.** With their eyes. The thing they need to notice is not hidden. It is not subtle. It is not buried in scripture or argument. It is **right in front of them**, in the very object they are bowing to.

Let's sit with this. The calf is in their hands. They have built it. They are circling it. Some classical narrations describe them dancing around it. Their eyes are full of it.

And Allah's question is: *do they not see?*

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider — and this is interpretive, but the rhetorical device opens it — is that **the seduction of an idol is not that it hides the truth about itself**. The seduction is that it makes you stop asking the questions you would ordinarily ask. The calf is not concealing its silence. The calf is openly silent. The people are simply no longer noticing.

This is what worship of the wrong thing does to perception. It does not blind you. It does something stranger. It **uninterests** you in the obvious. The questions you would have asked of any other claimant — *can you hear me? can you respond? can you do anything?* — those questions go quiet, because the gold is bright and the noise is novel and everyone else is bowing.

The first job of this ayah is to wake you up enough to ask the questions again.

### Element Two: *Yarjiʿu ilayhim qawlan* — The Verb That Means *Come Back*

Here is where the surface meaning of the ayah begins to crack open.

We will read most translations: *"that it could not speak to them."* That is not wrong. But it is so far from the actual texture of the Arabic that the real insight is lost.

Look:

> **أَلَّا يَرْجِعُ إِلَيْهِمْ قَوْلًا**
>
> *allā **yarjiʿu** ilayhim qawlan*

The verb is *yarjiʿu*. The root is ر-ج-ع — *rajaʿa* — and it does not mean *to speak*. It means **to return**. To come back. This is the same root as *al-rujūʿ* — the return — and *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* — *to Him we are returning.*

So a word-by-word reading of what Allah is actually saying is this: **the calf cannot *return* to them a word.** It cannot **send back** speech. It cannot **come back** with an answer.

This changes everything.

Because remember — the calf is making a sound. *Lahu khuwār.* It is lowing. It is producing noise. The previous ayah just told us. So what does Allah specify is missing? Not sound. **Return.** What the calf cannot do is **answer**. It can emit. It cannot reciprocate.

Let me give you a picture from your own life so this lands.

You can call your mother and have her not pick up. The phone rings. Eventually her voicemail plays — her voice, recorded — and you hear her speaking. There is sound. There is even her sound. But the phone has not *yarjaʿa ilayka qawlan*. Nothing has been *returned* to you. You have spoken into a one-way channel.

That is the calf.

The lowing is voicemail. It plays. It produces a recognizable noise. But nothing on the other side of that noise knows you exist. Nothing is **listening**. Nothing will **come back**.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this implies about worship.

The Quran is, in a single verb, defining what worship of the True God *is* by negating it of the false god. Worship of Allah is **conversation that returns**. *Udʿūnī astajib lakum* — *call upon Me, I will respond to you* (40:60). *Wa idhā saʾalaka ʿibādī ʿannī fa-innī qarīb, ujību daʿwata al-dāʿi idhā daʿān* — *when My servants ask you about Me, I am near; I respond to the call of the caller when he calls upon Me* (2:186).

Notice the word *ujību* — **I respond**. *I return* the call. The signature of the True God is **rujūʿ** — return.

The signature of every false god, the verse is telling us, is one-way. The flow is from the worshipper to the object, and then it stops. It piles up. The worshipper sends prayer, attention, fear, hope into the gold — and the gold takes it, and the gold is silent. Nothing comes back.

This is so important I want to slow down here.

The greatest tragedy of false worship in this verse is not that the worshipper is fooled about reality. It is that the worshipper is **lonely** and does not realize it. They have made for themselves a relationship in which only they speak. They mistake their own echo for a voice.

And here is what is remarkable: the word *qawlan* is **indefinite**. Not *the word*, not *its word* — just *qawlan*. **A word.** A single word. The verb of negation has been pushed to its smallest possible object. The calf cannot return — even **one word**. Even a syllable. Even an *aywa*. Nothing.

Compare this to your relationship with the One who, when you call Him, responds before you finish forming the sentence. Allah is so responsive that classical scholars wrote that *al-istijābah* — His response — precedes the worshipper's awareness of having asked.

The calf cannot manage one word.

This is the first thing the verse wants you to **see**.

### Element Three: *Lā yamliku lahum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā* — The Order That Should Not Be

Now look at what is negated next:

> **وَلَا يَمْلِكُ لَهُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا نَفْعًا**
>
> *wa-lā **yamliku** lahum **ḍarran** wa-lā **nafʿā***

The verb *yamliku* is from م-ل-ك — *milk*, *mulk* — the root of **kingship and possession**. To *malaka* something is to **own** it, to **have dominion** over it, to **be able to command it**. From this root comes *al-Mālik* — the Owner. *Mālik al-mulk* — the Owner of all dominion.

The verse is saying: this calf does not **own** for them — not even — *ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā* — **harm, nor benefit**.

Pause on the order. *Ḍarr* — harm. *Nafʿ* — benefit. **Harm comes first.**

Why is this strange? Because in human worship, when we make a god, we usually want it to **bless** us. We approach our idols for *benefit*. The order our hearts expect is *naf' wa lā ḍarr* — first the gift, then mention of the danger.

But Allah inverts the order. *Ḍarran wa-lā nafʿā.* He places **harm** in front. And classical commentators noticed this and asked: why?

The answer most of them converge on is devastating. They say: even an enemy can *harm* you. Even a stone you trip on can *harm* you. Even a wild animal that hates you, even a *shayṭān*, can do that much. The most basic, baseline form of agency in the world is the ability to inflict damage. And what Allah is saying is: **this thing they are bowing to cannot even manage that**.

It is so inert, so stripped of any kind of dominion, that it falls beneath even the threshold of an adversary. An adversary at least is **someone**. The calf is not even **someone**. It cannot reach you to hurt you. So how, the verse implies, could it possibly reach you to **help**?

This is the second face of the same insight. The first negation — *no return of speech* — exposed the calf as **silent**. This second negation — *no ownership of harm or benefit* — exposes the calf as **powerless**.

Together they form a complete portrait of false worship: **silent and powerless**. It cannot speak to you. It cannot do anything for you. It cannot even do anything **to** you.

[PAUSE]

And now feel the architecture of the ayah.

Allah names two things the calf lacks: **speech** and **dominion**. *Qawl* and *milk*. **Word** and **kingship**.

These are precisely the two things by which the True God reveals Himself in this very surah. Ta-Ha opened with Musa being addressed by Allah at the burning bush — *innī anā rabbuka* — *I, indeed, am your Lord.* That was **qawl** — divine speech. And what was Musa told that day? That he had been **chosen**, that signs would be given, that Pharaoh would be confronted, that the sea would split — *that is **mulk***. That is divine dominion.

The God on the mountain has *qawl* and *mulk*. The god they have built at the foot of the mountain has neither. They have, in their panic, traded a Lord who **speaks** and **owns** for an object that **cannot speak** and **cannot own**.

Of all the trades a heart can make, this is the worst. And it is the one we are most prone to making — because the gold is right there, and the mountain is far, and the silence of the mountain feels like absence even when it is appointment.

---

### Bridge

Three small things have shown us the architecture: a question that is a rebuke, a verb of return that exposes silence, and a verb of ownership that exposes powerlessness.

But the verse is not finally about a calf in a desert. The calf is the **stepping stone**. The unseen reality it is pointing toward — the real subject of the verse — is something that lives much closer to us than gold or fire.

Now let's ask what this verse reveals not just about words and ancient idolatry — but about the worship architecture of a human heart, including ours.

## PART 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Idols We Have That Cannot *Return a Word*

You probably do not have a golden calf in your house.

But you almost certainly have something that you pour yourself into, that takes everything you give it, and that has never *returned a word*.

For some people it is **a career**. You wake for it. You stay awake for it. You miss your children's bedtimes for it. You shape your whole sense of self around it. And one day you ask it, *do you see me? do you know what I have given?* — and what comes back is not a word. The career is a calf with a lowing sound. It produces noise — emails, deadlines, a paycheck, the rumor of more — and the noise sounds like life. But it has never *yarjaʿa ilayka qawlan*. It has never returned to you a single word. Whatever conversation you thought you were having, you were having with yourself.

For some people it is **status**. The look on people's faces when you walk into a room. The number of followers. The way your name sounds when someone introduces you. We pour worship into status. We arrange our lives around it. And status — like the calf — produces sound. Likes. Notifications. Compliments. But none of it is *return*. Status does not love you back. Status does not even know you. It cannot return a word.

For some people it is **a person**. Sometimes a person who never asked to be made into a god. We make them our north. Our *shams* and *qamar*. We bow our days toward their approval. And even the most loving human cannot bear that worship — and even if they could, no human has the dominion the heart is asking of them. They cannot *yamliku lakum ḍarran wa-lā nafʿan* in the way you are demanding. They are not the Owner. They cannot answer the prayer you are silently sending them every hour.

[PAUSE]

What this ayah is doing — by stopping the story to ask the question — is offering us the **same diagnostic test the Bani Isra'il failed**.

The test is one question. It is the question Allah Himself names: *Does it return to you a word?*

You can run this test on anything. Sit with whatever has the most weight in your interior life right now. The thing you wake up thinking about. The thing whose absence would feel like the ground falling away. And ask:

*When I speak to it — does it speak back?*

Not *does it produce sound*. The calf produced sound. The career produces sound. The phone produces sound. The question is: **does anything come back that was not generated by my own mind reflecting off the surface of the thing?**

Most of what we worship fails this test instantly. We just have not been asking.

And here is the mercy. The reason Allah stops the story to ask this question is not to embarrass them — or us. It is because **it is not too late**. The story is paused mid-collapse precisely so that the listener can apply the diagnostic before the consequences are sealed. Every reader gets the same offer the Bani Isra'il got: *do you see now? before Musa returns? before the reckoning?*

The applied form of this theme is something you can actually do tomorrow. **One time, before you reach for whatever you usually reach for, speak instead to the One who has promised *ujību daʿwata al-dāʿi*.** Not because the request is urgent. Just to feel the difference between calling something that can return a word and calling something that never will.

Once you have felt that difference, the calf becomes harder to mistake.

---

### Theme Two: The Spectacle of the Visible vs. The Relationship of the Unseen

Here is what makes the calf episode so painful, and so close to us: **everything about it was easier to look at than the truth**.

The calf was visible. It glittered. It made a sound you could record and replay. You could touch it. You could walk around it. You could hold a ceremony around it. There was something **happening**.

The Lord on the mountain offered none of that. The mountain was silent to the people below. They could not see what was happening up there. There was no glitter. There was, from their angle, just smoke and absence and a leader who had not come back yet.

So they did what frightened humans do. They traded **the unseen relationship for the visible spectacle**.

This is the deepest move the ayah is interrogating. *Afalā yarawna* is not just *do they not see this calf is silent*. It is also *do they not see how their eyes have been deciding for them what is real?*

The calf was real to the eye. Allah was real to the heart. They preferred their eyes.

[PAUSE]

We do this hourly.

The phone in our hand is **visible**. The duʿāʾ unsaid is **invisible**. And so we open the phone. The notification is **visible**. The presence of Allah in the room is **invisible**. And so we attend to the notification. The performance of religion — the post, the photo, the visible piety — is **visible**. The two rakʿāt no one will ever see are **invisible**. And so the scale tips.

We are not — most of us — building golden calves. We are doing something more subtle, and the verse is sharp enough to catch it. We are letting the **visible** out-compete the **unseen** for the throne of our attention. Bit by bit. Until one day we look up and realize that whatever we have been most loyal to, most afraid to lose, most willing to organize our lives around — was *visible*. And almost nothing of what was *unseen* — the relationship with our Lord, the appointment with our soul, the quiet hours of remembrance — has been allowed near the throne.

The Bani Isra'il at the bottom of the mountain are not stupider than us. They are us, in compressed time. Forty days of silence and they cracked. We crack inside an afternoon, every afternoon, for less.

But notice the location of the calf episode in this very surah. **Where is it?** It is sandwiched between Musa's appointment with Allah on the mountain (the unseen meeting) and Musa's return to the people (the visible reckoning). The unseen relationship was the whole point. The mountain was where the *qawl* was being given that would shape the whole nation.

And the people below could not bear forty days of the unseen.

So the second movement of the theme is this: **the discipline of the believer is the discipline of trusting the unseen relationship over the visible spectacle**, especially when the visible is loud and the unseen is silent.

This is what *patience* in religion is, beneath everything else. It is the ability to keep facing the mountain when the gold is already glittering at your feet.

The applied form of this is small and concrete. **The next time the visible is loud — a notification, an opportunity, a fear, an offer — pause for a count of three and ask: *what is the unseen relationship asking of me right now?*** The pause is everything. The pause is what the Bani Isra'il did not give themselves. They reacted to forty days of silence with the speed of forty seconds. The pause is the cure.

## Closing Synthesis

Look now at what the architecture of the ayah has shown us.

Allah stops a story mid-collapse and asks a question that is not a question. The question has two halves: *can it return a word?* and *does it own harm or benefit?*

The first half exposes the **silence** of all false worship.
The second half exposes its **powerlessness**.

Together they amount to a single insight, and the insight is the inverse portrait of the True God: a Lord who **speaks** — *qawl* — and a Lord who **owns** — *mulk*.

Every idol fails on one or both axes. Every idol is either a thing that cannot answer, or a thing that has no real dominion, or — usually — both.

And the deeper architecture is this: the linguistic discoveries and the thematic discoveries are the **same insight from two directions**. Linguistically, *yarjiʿu* is the verb of return — and the True God is the One who **returns** the call. Thematically, the test of true worship is reciprocity — and the True God is the One who **reciprocates**. The grammar and the lived spiritual reality are pointing at the same thing. Worship is **conversation**. False worship is **monologue**.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the loudest thing in my interior life right now — and if I apply the test, *does it return a word*?
2. Where am I letting the visible spectacle out-compete the unseen relationship for the center of my attention?
3. When I last called upon Allah, did I really call — or did I send words into the air the way someone sends words into a calf?
4. What forty-day silence am I refusing to sit through, and what gold am I tempted to throw into the fire instead?
5. If Musa returned tomorrow and saw what I have been bowing to in his absence — what would he say?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The signature of false worship is a one-way channel — a thing that takes everything from you and never returns a single word — and the signature of the True God is that He answers.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to break a calf today. You only need to do one thing: **call upon the One who has promised to return a word**, and notice the difference between that and everything else you have been speaking into.

Once you have felt the *rujūʿ* — the return of the call — the silence of the calves becomes audible. You will not need anyone to point them out. You will recognize them by what does not come back.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who responds to the caller when he calls upon You — let me not mistake the lowing of any calf in my life for Your voice. Teach my heart the diagnostic of return. Pull me back from every silent thing I have been pouring myself into, and seat me in the conversation that does not end. You speak. You own. You answer. Let that be enough.*

*Āmīn.*
