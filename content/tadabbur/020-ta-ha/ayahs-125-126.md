# The One Who Was Raised Blind

## Surah Ta-Ha 125-126

> قَالَ رَبِّ لِمَ حَشَرْتَنِىٓ أَعْمَىٰ وَقَدْ كُنتُ بَصِيرًۭا
>
> قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ أَتَتْكَ ءَايَـٰتُنَا فَنَسِيتَهَا ۖ وَكَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ تُنسَىٰ

> *Qāla rabbi lima ḥashartanī aʿmā wa qad kuntu baṣīrā. Qāla kadhālika atatka āyātunā fa-nasītahā wa kadhālika al-yawma tunsā.*

> "He will say: My Lord, why have You gathered me blind, when indeed I used to be seeing?
>
> He will say: Thus did Our signs come to you, and you forgot them; and thus today you are forgotten."

---

## Introduction: The Voice of the Forgotten

I want you to picture a moment.

A person opens their eyes on a day they did not choose to wake into. The light around them is total — but they cannot see it. Crowds press from every side. Voices everywhere. And somewhere in the architecture of that light, they feel a presence they once knew about, vaguely, in the manner one knows about distant relatives.

And then they speak.

The first thing out of their mouth is not despair. It is not a confession. It is not silence.

It is a question.

**"My Lord — why?"**

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most extraordinary moments in the entire Qur'an. Because of *who* speaks. The one speaking is described in the verse just before this one — verse 124 — as someone who turned away from Allah's remembrance in the worldly life. Someone who decided, day after day, to look elsewhere. To listen elsewhere. To be tuned in elsewhere. And here he is, on the Day of Gathering, raised up blind. And his very first reaction is to address Allah as **rabbī** — *my Lord* — and ask Him to explain Himself.

Stay with that for a moment.

Even now, raised blind in the press of resurrection, he still has the vocabulary of intimacy on his tongue. He calls Allah "my Lord." He still uses the relational language of belonging. The word doesn't leave him. But notice what *has* left him.

What has left him is **sight**.

And it is precisely on this — the absence of sight — that he protests.

His complaint has the structure of a man who feels he has been wronged. Not "what did I do?" Not "where am I?" Not "what is this place?" But: **why have You done this to me, when I used to be seeing?**

His question reveals what he believed. He believed he was a seeing person. He believed his eyes — the ones that worked, the ones that read street signs and recognized faces and tracked the prices on screens — those were what made him *baṣīr*. Seeing. A seer. A witness.

[PAUSE]

The arc we're going to walk is this: by the time Allah finishes His one-sentence response, every assumption inside that man's protest will be lying in pieces. And ours will be too — if we let the verse do its work.

Because the response Allah gives him is not a punishment. It is a **mirror**. He is being shown, for the first time, the shape of his own life. Not a new condition imposed on him from outside — but the inner condition of his soul finally rendered visible on the outside, where he can no longer look away from it.

This passage falls near the end of Surah Ta-Ha — a handful of verses still follow, carrying further warnings for those who wrong themselves before the surah reaches its final word. But it is here, in this stark dialogue, that the surah's entire theological arc crests. The surah began with comfort to the Prophet ﷺ — *we did not send down the Qur'an upon you that you be distressed* — and before it closes, it arrives here, in the harshest dialogue in the surah, between Allah and a man who refused the very comfort that was being offered. We have walked through Musa and Pharaoh, through Adam and Iblīs, through the calf and the Sāmirī. And now, before the surah's final word is spent, we arrive at a single human voice on the Day of Resurrection, asking why he is blind.

The whole surah has been preparing us for these two verses.

Let's open them.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Past-Continuous of "I Used To Be Seeing"

Look at how the man frames his protest:

> **وَقَدْ كُنتُ بَصِيرًا**
>
> *wa qad kuntu baṣīrā*
>
> "when indeed I used to be seeing."

I want you to notice how Arabic builds this phrase, because if you don't see what's happening grammatically, you'll miss the entire psychology of the speaker.

In English, we say "I was seeing" or "I used to see." That's two words. Maybe three. We don't think much about it.

In Arabic, the same idea is built in two pieces, and each piece is doing real work. The first is **kuntu** — from *kāna* — meaning "I was." But *kāna* in Arabic isn't a flat past tense. When *kāna* is paired with another descriptor, it stretches the past out. It says: this wasn't a single moment. This was an ongoing condition. **I used to be. I was, and remained.**

And what was he? **baṣīrā.**

Now this word. *Baṣīr.* This isn't just "seeing" as a one-off action. *Baṣīr* is what's called the *faʿīl* form in Arabic — a form that turns a verb into a quality, an identity, an established trait. Not "he saw" but "he is **a seer**." Not someone who had vision once. Someone for whom seeing is the standing condition.

So when the man says **kuntu baṣīrā**, he is not just saying "I had functioning eyes."

He is saying: **"Seeing was *who I was*. It was my standing identity. It was the mode in which I lived."**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because his entire protest depends on this self-image.

He believed himself to be a seeing person. Not just biologically, but as an identity. He moved through life as one of the people who *see*. The ones with eyes open. The ones who notice things. The ones who don't miss what's in front of them.

And now those eyes are gone, and he wants to know **why this discrepancy**.

But here's the question Allah's response is going to plant inside us: **what was he actually seeing?**

What did his eyes — those eyes he was so proud of, the ones whose absence he is now protesting — what did they look at, all those years he was their owner?

Did they look at the verses that were sent to him? Did they look at what the messengers brought? Did they look at the sky in the way the Qur'an asks people to look at the sky? Did they look at his own approaching death? Did they look at the people he was responsible for? Did they look at the moments of mercy that arrived in his life?

Or did they look only at what he wanted them to look at?

Did they look at screens, prices, threats, comparisons, the next thing, the next thing, the next thing?

[PAUSE]

Notice something remarkable. The Arabic word *baṣīr* is one of the names of Allah. *Al-Baṣīr* — the All-Seeing. When Allah uses *baṣīr* of Himself, it means He sees not only what is visible but what is hidden, the inward as well as the outward, the meanings of things and not just their surfaces.

When this man calls himself *baṣīr*, he is — without realizing it — using a word that, properly understood, means seeing into the heart of things. And he is using it to describe a life in which he saw only the surface.

His own protest gives him away.

**He claims a kind of vision he never actually had.**

This is part of why the Day of Resurrection is called *yawm al-dīn* — the Day when the truth of things is laid bare. It is the day on which the gap between what we said about ourselves and what we actually were is finally closed. He thought he was a seer. The day reveals that he was not. The blindness on that day is not new. It is the **disclosure** of what was already true.

What's remarkable is that even now, even after being raised in this state, he is still defending the old self-image. He hasn't yet noticed that his protest is the proof of the verdict. He still thinks the problem is the verdict and not the life.

Here is what this single word — *baṣīrā* — invites us to consider: **what we call our vision may be only our preferences having a good time.** What we call seeing may just be the limited, curated set of things our attention was willing to register.

A truly seeing person sees what they would rather not see. A truly seeing person sees the signs of their Lord even when those signs ask something of them. A truly seeing person notices their own mortality. A truly seeing person registers the moments of mercy that arrive unannounced.

To merely have eyes that work is not yet to be *baṣīr*.

[PAUSE]

And here the classical tradition holds that this word carries more than one register — more than one kind of loss. Al-Tabari records, on the authority of Mujahid — one of the most distinguished of the Tābiʿīn, whose commentary al-Tabari transmits across multiple chains — that *aʿmā* in this verse can be read as *lā ḥujjata lī*: not "without sight" but "without argument." And that *baṣīrā* can mean *ʿāliman bi-ḥujjatī* — "knowing in my proof," "clear in my case." On this reading, the man's protest is not about eyes at all. He is saying: *I had a logic. I had reasons. I thought my standing before You was established. I thought I could make my case. Why do I arrive now without one?*

Both readings have classical warrant. Both are devastating in their own way. The physical reading says: you had working eyes and used them only for the visible surface of things, and now the deeper seeing is denied you. The argumentative reading says: you thought you had reasons on your side — reasons why the signs could be postponed, reasons why your life made sense without them, reasons that would hold — and now you find you have no standing at all. Whether the man has lost his eyes or his arguments, the loss is the same in kind: the faculty by which he claimed to navigate reality — the thing he staked his identity on with that proud *kuntu* — has not followed him across the threshold of that day. Perhaps the word is large enough to hold both failures at once. The man who could not truly see was also the man who, in the end, had nothing to say.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Kadhālika ... wa kadhālika* — The Architecture of Symmetry

Now we come to Allah's response. And the response is a piece of architecture.

Listen to it again:

> **قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ أَتَتْكَ ءَايَـٰتُنَا فَنَسِيتَهَا ۖ وَكَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ تُنسَىٰ**
>
> *Qāla kadhālika atatka āyātunā fa-nasītahā, wa kadhālika al-yawma tunsā.*

I want you to notice a structural feature that English translations often flatten.

The same word appears twice. **Kadhālika.** And then again — **wa kadhālika.**

*Kadhālika* means "thus" or "in this way" or "like that." It's a demonstrative. It points. It says: *the thing I am about to describe has the same shape as something else.*

So the response is built like this:

> **In this way** — Our signs came to you, and you forgot them.
>
> **And in this way** — today, you are forgotten.

Let me teach you what's happening here as a piece of language before I tell you what's happening as a piece of theology.

When Arabic uses *kadhālika* twice in quick succession like this, it is doing something English cannot easily do. It is welding the two halves of the sentence together with a single bolt. It is saying: **these two events have the same form. The shape of what happened to the signs in your life is the shape of what is happening to you today.**

This is not "you sinned, so we are punishing you." This is something deeper and stranger. This is: **what you did to the signs, the universe is now doing to you. The pattern continues. The shape persists. Today is the same shape as yesterday — just with the participants reversed.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because once you see this structural feature, you cannot un-see it.

Allah is not telling him: I've decided to make you blind to teach you a lesson.

Allah is telling him: **You taught the universe to do this. The way Our signs came to you and met your forgetting — that very pattern is what now greets you. The signs encountered your back; today you encounter the back of recognition.**

The two halves of the response are mirror images. *Kadhālika atatka āyātunā fa-nasītahā* — thus Our signs came to you, so you forgot them. *Wa kadhālika al-yawma tunsā* — and thus today, you are forgotten.

The first half: **the signs arrived → you turned away.**

The second half: **today → you arrive → and recognition turns away from you.**

You closed a door. The same door is now closed from the other side.

This is the most precise possible answer to his question. He asked, "why have You gathered me blind?" Allah responds: *I have not done something new to you. I have allowed the pattern of your own life to complete itself in your presence.*

[PAUSE]

I want you to feel the weight of how language is doing this work. Two *kadhālikas*. Two parallel scenes. The same demonstrative welding them together.

In a different verse — Surah al-Jāthiyah 45:34 — Allah says something almost identical: *al-yawma nansākum kamā nasītum liqāʾa yawmikum hādhā* — "today We forget you as you forgot the meeting of this day of yours." Same pattern. Same architecture.

This is a recurring grammatical signature throughout the Qur'an: the law of *jazāʾ wifq al-ʿamal* — the recompense matches the action in form, not just in weight. The shape of what you did is the shape of what comes back.

The man asked: why am I blind?

The answer: because in your life, you treated Our signs as if they were not there. You walked past them. You let them arrive at your gate and you did not open. You decided they were background noise.

And here it is worth pressing into what the classical commentators mean by *nasītahā* — "you forgot them." Al-Tabari's gloss on the word is precise: *tarakahā wa aʿraḍa ʿanhā wa lam yuʾmin bihā wa lam yaʿmal* — he abandoned them, turned away from them, did not believe in them, did not act on them. This is not the *nisyān* of a distracted mind that lets a name slip between the cracks of a busy day. This is *tark* and *iʿrāḍ*: deliberate abandonment, the sustained turning of the face. The man did not gradually drift away from the signs. He refused them. The forgetting the verse condemns is a posture held over time, a recurring choice dressed in the clothing of simply living one's life.

**Today you find that you have become, in this gathering, the kind of thing that arrives and is not registered. You have become — to the universe of light and meaning — what you made the signs become to you.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what this invites us to consider: **on the Day of Resurrection, we do not enter a foreign country. We enter the full, externalized version of the country we built inside ourselves.** The blind one is blind because his life was a slow practice of looking the other way. The forgotten one is forgotten because his life was a slow practice of forgetting. The dignity of that day is that nothing arbitrary is added. The terror of that day is that nothing concealed is left.

And what does it mean to be forgotten? Al-Muyassar gives *tunsā* a precise location: *tutraku fī al-nār* — he is left in the Fire. The forgetting is not vague cosmic indifference, some abstract absence of divine attention. It is spatial. It is the specific condition of the one who remains. What defined his practice in the world — leaving the signs untended, letting them lie in the corners of his days unattended — becomes the shape of where he ends: left, burning, unattended. *Kadhālika.* The same shape. The pattern complete.

[Major PAUSE]

### Element Three: From "You Forgot" to "You Are Forgotten" — The Voice Shift

Now let me draw your attention to something so quiet you can miss it. But once you see it, you will hear the entire passage differently.

Look at the two verbs of forgetting in Allah's response.

The first is **fa-nasītahā** — *so you forgot them.* This is in the active voice. *You* are the one doing the forgetting. *You* are the agent. *You* are choosing where your attention does and does not go.

The second is **tunsā** — *you are forgotten.* This is in the **passive** voice. The agent has disappeared. You are no longer the one doing. You are the one being done to.

In Arabic grammar, this is a precise and terrifying shift. In one half of the response, you are the subject of the verb of forgetting. In the other half, you have become its object.
