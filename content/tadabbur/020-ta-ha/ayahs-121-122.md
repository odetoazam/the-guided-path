# Eight Verbs from Fall to Guidance: The Grammar of How Allah Moves First

## Surah Ta-Ha 20:121-122

> فَأَكَلَا مِنْهَا فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ۚ وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ فَغَوَىٰ ۝ ثُمَّ ٱجْتَبَـٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ وَهَدَىٰ

*Fa-akalā minhā fa-badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni ʿalayhimā min waraqi'l-jannah. Wa-ʿaṣā Ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā. Thumma'jtabāhu rabbuhu fa-tāba ʿalayhi wa-hadā.*

> "So they both ate from it, and their nakedness became exposed to them, and they began patching upon themselves leaves of the Garden. And Adam disobeyed his Lord and went astray. Then his Lord chose him, and turned to him, and guided."

---

## Where We Are

To hear these two ayahs, we have to feel where in the surah they land.

Ta-Ha has been, from its first letters, a surah about guidance carried into hostile country. A staff thrown down before a tyrant. A child placed in a basket on the river. Israelites led between walls of standing water. The whole surah is about how Allah's guidance enters a world that does not want it.

And then — almost without warning — the surah turns away from Musa entirely, away from Pharaoh, away from the Samiri and the calf, and reaches back into a much older story. It reaches back to the very first guidance ever offered to a human being.

The surah ends where humanity began.

Why? Because every story of guidance in this surah has been a footnote to *this* story. Before there was a Musa to be guided to confront Pharaoh, there was an Adam to be guided home. Before there were Israelites who needed to be led, there was a man and a woman, alone in a garden, who needed to be lifted from the floor.

So the frame closes. And in two ayahs — twenty-eight words — Allah gives us eight verbs that contain everything about how a human being falls and how Allah lifts them.

[PAUSE]

The previous ayah, 120, ended with Iblis whispering. Now we will hear what happened next.

But here is the hook. **Read the ayahs again, slowly, and watch the grammar of who moves first.** Watch when the subject is Adam, and when the subject is his Lord. Watch where the verbs hand off.

There is something hidden in that handoff. Once you see it, you will never read this story the same way again.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## The First Element — *fa-badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā* (the exposure that was always there)

Before we open the word, let us teach the concept.

In Arabic, when a word's root meaning travels into a derived noun, the original meaning often remains buried inside it. The way an old riverbed remains beneath a city. You cannot see the river anymore, but the streets curve around where the water once ran.

So when we open a word, we are not just asking what it means today. We are asking what was happening underneath it the day it was first used.

Now look at this phrase: **فَبَدَتْ لَهُمَا سَوْآتُهُمَا** — "and their *sawʾāt* became apparent to them."

We translate *sawʾāt* as "nakedness" or "private parts." But that is not what the word literally means. The word *sawʾāt* (سَوْآت) comes from the root **س-و-أ** — *sā'a, yasūʾu* — to be **bad, evil, ugly.** *Sūʾ* in the Quran is the word for harm, for badness, for what disturbs the soul.

So *sawʾāt* doesn't mean "private parts." It means **the bad things. The ugly things. The things that should be hidden.**

The Arabic word for this part of the body *is* the word for what is shameful. Embedded in the language itself is the verdict that what becomes apparent here is something whose nature is to be covered.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that. And look at the verb: **فَبَدَتْ** (*fa-badat*) — "became apparent." The root **ب-د-و** means *to be open, to become visible, to step out into view.* It is the same root as *bādiyah* — the open desert, where there is nowhere to hide.

So the picture in the Arabic is precise. What was hidden stepped into open desert. Was *brought* into open desert. Without protection. Without curtain.

But — and this is where you have to slow down — **what appeared was always there.**

The eating did not create the *sawʾāt*. The eating did not generate them. They were not summoned into existence by the act of disobedience. They were *revealed*.

The Quran does not say "their *sawʾāt* came into being." It says "their *sawʾāt* became apparent *to them*." *Lahumā.* To them. They had not seen them before, because something had been covering them. Al-Tabari notes this with precision: the *ʿawrāt* had been veiled *from their own eyes* — *kānat mastura ʿan aʿyunihimā*. Not hidden from onlookers. Not concealed from the outside world. Hidden from their own vision. The covering was not merely social. It was a mercy on their own perception — a grace that let them move through the Garden without seeing themselves the way the exposure would later compel them to see.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that.

This is not a small linguistic point. This is the entire theology of human nature in one verb.

Every human being has *sawʾāt*. Every human being carries within themselves things that, if they were uncovered, would show themselves to be ugly. The vanity. The cowardice. The petty cruelty. The version of yourself that surfaces when you are tired and someone tests you. The thoughts you would not say aloud.

These were always there.

What we lose, when we lose taqwā, when we lose the protection of Allah's covering, is not our innocence. **We lose our cover.** And what was already there — what was always there — steps out into desert.

The pious person and the impious person are not two different kinds of human. They are the same human, one of whom is covered and one of whom is exposed. The covering is what differs. Not what is underneath.

This is why the Quran will say later, *"O children of Adam, We have sent down upon you clothing that covers your sawʾāt and adornment — but the clothing of taqwā, that is best"* (7:26). Allah names taqwā as a kind of clothing because that is what it does. It covers what is always there.

[PAUSE]

So when we read *fa-badat lahumā sawʾātuhumā*, we are not reading about a unique event that happened once to two people. We are reading about what happens to every human being the moment Allah's covering is withdrawn — by their own hand or by His decree.

The eating did not make Adam ugly. It made his ugliness visible.

That is the first thing the language is doing.

## The Second Element — *wa-ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni... min waraqi'l-jannah* (patching the wound with the leaves of the place that wounded them)

Now look at what they do next.

**وَطَفِقَا يَخْصِفَانِ عَلَيْهِمَا مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ**

"And they began patching upon themselves leaves of the Garden."

The verb *ṭafiqā* (طَفِقَا) is what Arabic grammarians call an **incipient verb** — *fiʿl al-shurūʿ.* Its sole job is to mark the *beginning* of an action. It does not describe a leisurely act. It marks the *moment of starting.* "They started in." "They set about." It carries urgency. The thing that comes after begins immediately, without pause, the instant the previous thing has happened.

Grammarians classify *ṭafiqa* as a semi-auxiliary of commencement — it does not stand as a primary verb in its own right but serves to mark the onset of the action that follows it, so *ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni* counts as a single verbal unit, not two. This is why the title counts eight semantically distinct verbal actions across these two ayahs rather than nine: the incipient verb and the act it launches move together as one.

So between *fa-badat* (the exposure) and *yakhṣifāni* (the patching), there is no gap. No deliberation. The exposure happens, and they are *already* moving to cover it. The reaction is immediate, instinctive, frantic.

Now the verb itself: **يَخْصِفَانِ** (*yakhṣifāni*). From the root **خ-ص-ف**, which the classical lexicons describe as *to layer one thing upon another, to patch, to cobble shoes by sewing layer to layer.* The word in Arabic for a cobbler — someone who repairs sandals by stitching pieces together — comes from this root.

So the picture is not someone gracefully fashioning a garment. The picture is someone *patching*. Layering. Frantically stitching one leaf over another, trying to make a covering out of fragments. Al-Muyassar's rendering adds a detail that brings the desperation into focus: they were *yalṣiqānihi ʿalayhimā* — gluing the leaves onto themselves, pressing them against their bodies to make them hold. Not sewing. Not weaving. Adhering. The kind of covering that is held in place by pressure rather than by craft — because there is no time for craft, only for the frantic need to be covered again.

[PAUSE]

Look how perfect the image is. Two people, suddenly exposed to themselves, panicking, grabbing whatever they can reach, trying to layer it fast enough to hide what they have just realized about themselves.

But notice — and this is the part you cannot miss — **مِن وَرَقِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ.** *From the leaves of the Garden.*

They are trying to cover themselves with leaves taken from the very Garden where the exposure happened.

Where else were they going to get leaves from? They were *in* the Garden. The Garden was the only place. So the answer is obvious. But Allah does not let the obvious answer pass without putting it on record. He records, in the text, that the material with which they tried to fix themselves came from the same context that revealed them.

[PAUSE]

This is the most human thing in the Quran.

When we are exposed — when our cover is pulled and we suddenly see what is in us — what is our first instinct? **To fix it ourselves.** With whatever is at hand. With whatever resources are around us. With leaves from the same garden where we fell.

A person discovers a sin in themselves and immediately tries to cover it with another sin's opposite. Their pride is exposed and they overcorrect into self-flagellation. Their anger is exposed and they perform a meekness that has no roots in their soul. They patch, and patch, and patch — but everything they patch with comes from the same material as the exposure.

A person discovers their dependency on the world's approval and tries to fix it with more of the world's approval — for being humble. They build a new identity on top of the broken one, made from the same leaves as the broken one.

It does not work. The leaves wilt. The patching falls apart. Anyone who has tried to fix their own soul with the leaves of the soul knows this. The covering you make for yourself, from the same material as the wound, will not hold.

[PAUSE]

The Arabic word *yakhṣifāni* tells you the patching was *layered.* They didn't put one leaf. They put many, on top of each other, trying to make the covering thick enough. There is something almost desperate in it. The layering itself is the confession that no single leaf would do.

We have all done this. We are all still doing this.

This is what the Quran is showing us in one verb: the human reflex to repair the soul with the materials of the place that broke it.

It will not be enough. Allah will not let it be enough. Because the covering Adam needs cannot come from inside the wound. It has to come from the One who made the original cover.

And that is exactly where the next ayah goes.

## The Third Element — *thumma'jtabāhu rabbuhu fa-tāba ʿalayhi wa-hadā* (the four-verb sequence where Adam is no longer the subject)

[PAUSE — major pause]

Here we have to be very careful. Because what is about to happen is so subtle that most readers walk straight past it.

In ayah 121, the subject of every verb is Adam, or Adam and his wife. *They* ate. *They* were exposed. *They* began patching. *He* (Adam) disobeyed. *He* erred.

Five verbs. All human subjects. All falling action.

Then comes ayah 122. And listen to who is doing what.

> ثُمَّ ٱجْتَبَـٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ وَهَدَىٰ

*Thumma — then. Ijtabāhu rabbuhu — his Lord chose him. Fa-tāba ʿalayhi — and turned to him. Wa-hadā — and guided.*

**The subject changes.**

In ayah 121, Adam acts. In ayah 122, Allah acts. From the moment of *thumma*, every single verb has Allah as its subject. Adam is no longer doing anything. Adam is being acted upon.

[PAUSE]

This is decisive. Because we have a story about — what most people would assume is — Adam *repenting*. Adam *coming back*. Adam *seeking forgiveness*.

That is not what the Quran says here. That is not the grammar.

The Quran says **his Lord chose him.** **His Lord turned to him.** **His Lord guided.**

The verbs of return — they all belong to Allah.

But before we open those three divine verbs, we need to pause over the two human verbs that immediately precede the pivot. Because *ʿaṣā* and *ghawā* are not interchangeable words, and their pairing is doing something precise.

---

### *ʿAṣā* and *ghawā* — the act and the state it produces

**وَعَصَىٰٓ ءَادَمُ رَبَّهُۥ** — *wa-ʿaṣā Ādamu rabbahu.* He disobeyed. The root **ع-ص-ي** is the classical Arabic root for disobedience — *ʿaṣā, yaʿṣī.* There is nothing euphemistic about it. The Quran does not soften what Adam did. It names it directly, with Adam's own name attached: not "it was done" but "Adam disobeyed his Lord."

Al-Muyassar confirms: he disobeyed his Lord's command and went against it by eating from the tree Allah had forbidden him from approaching.

Then: **فَغَوَىٰ** — *fa-ghawā.* He erred, went astray. The root **غ-و-ي** — *ghawā, yaghwī* — means to be led into loss, to veer from the right path, to end up in a state of going-astray. It is related to *ghāwī*, the one who has gone astray, and to *ighwāʾ*, the very word Iblis uses when he pledges *"I will lead them astray"* (15:39). Having followed Iblis's path, Adam finds himself inside the word Iblis had once used as a promise.

[PAUSE]

Note the structure: *ʿaṣā* first, then *ghawā.* The disobedience came first; the state of being-led-astray followed it. The single act produced a condition. There is a two-stage quality to the fall: the moment of transgression, and then the spiritual disorientation that sets in after it. The *ʿiṣyān* opened a door; the *ghawāyah* is what walked through it.

This is why the lifting will also require more than one verb. One act and the state it produced will need — as we are about to see — a choosing, a turning, and a guidance: three divine movements to undo both the transgression and what the transgression had done to the soul.

The two verbs of falling set up the three verbs of lifting. The grammar is exact.

---

### *Ijtabāhu* — the verb of selection that comes *after* the sin

**ٱجْتَبَـٰهُ** — *ijtabāhu.* From the root **ج-ب-ي**, which classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, al-Rāghib's Mufradāt) link to *to gather, to collect what is best from a larger quantity, to pick out the choicest fruit.* When water is collected into a cistern, the verb is from this root. When you sift through a heap of dates and choose the finest ones for a guest, the verb is from this root.

It is selection by gathering. Picking out *this one* from among many.

And it is in **Form VIII** — *iftaʿala* — which in Arabic carries a sense of *taking the action upon oneself with deliberate effort.* Allah did not casually approve of Adam. He *gathered him,* deliberately, the way you reach into a basket and pick out the one fruit you want.

Al-Tabari glosses *ijtabāhu* as *iṣṭafāhu* — "He elected him, chose him as the best." Al-Muyassar adds *qarraba-hu* — "He brought him close." The election was not a distant administrative act. It was a movement toward. Allah selected Adam and in the very same gesture drew him near. The distance between the floor and the nearness was crossed by Allah's movement, not Adam's.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the timing.

The selection — *ijtabāhu* — comes **after** *ʿaṣā* and *ghawā.* After the disobedience. After the going astray.

This is staggering. Read it again. *Wa-ʿaṣā Ādamu rabbahu fa-ghawā. Thumma'jtabāhu rabbuhu.*

"Adam disobeyed his Lord and went astray. **Then** his Lord chose him."

Not: chose him *before* and forgave him *after.* The choosing comes *after* the falling.

Why does this matter?

Because there is a voice inside every fallen human being that says: *I have disqualified myself. The selection is over. I was a candidate, and now I am not.* When we sin gravely, when we fall hard, the immediate reflex is to assume that whatever was special about us — whatever Allah was building in us — is now finished. That the sin has erased the account. That the relationship between who we were supposed to be and who we have now become is severed beyond repair.

That is not what happened to Adam.

Al-Tabari's commentary is precise: *iṣṭafāhu min baʿdi maʿṣiyatihi iyyāhu* — "He elected him, after his disobedience to Him." The phrase *min baʿdi* — "after" — is not incidental. It is the whole point. Allah chose Adam full-eyed. Knowing the disobedience. Knowing the erring. Knowing the frantic leaf-patching. Knowing the full weight of what the man had done and how he had tried and failed to fix it.

And then He chose him.

[PAUSE]

The *iftaʿala* form matters here too. Form VIII in Arabic carries a sense of deliberate, effortful, intentional action taken toward oneself. Allah did not passively let Adam back in. He *reached* for him. He moved toward him. The selection was an act of will — a deliberate gathering — and the timing of that act — after, not before — is the Quran's answer to the voice that says *you have disqualified yourself.*

You have not. The election comes after the fall. That is precisely when it comes.

---

### *Fa-tāba ʿalayhi* — the direction that changes everything

Now we reach the most carefully constructed phrase in the two ayahs.

**فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ** — *fa-tāba ʿalayhi.* We translate it as "and turned toward him" or "and relented toward him." The root is **ت-و-ب** — *tāba, yatūbu* — to turn, to return, to come back.

But watch the preposition.

In Arabic, tawbah — the act of turning — is directional. When the servant turns toward Allah, the verb is *tāba ilā Allah*: he turned *toward*. When Allah turns toward the servant, the Quran consistently uses *tāba ʿalayhi*: He turned *upon him*, over him, covering him with the return. The preposition *ʿalā* here carries a sense of descent, of coming down over. Like shade descending over a hot surface. Like a covering coming down.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what the classical commentators see in this phrase that must not be missed.

Al-Tabari's gloss of *fa-tāba ʿalayhi* is not "He accepted Adam's repentance." It is: *fa-razaqahu al-rujūʿa ilā mā yarḍā ʿanhu wa'l-ʿamal bi-ṭāʿatihi* — "He provided him with the return to what pleases Him, and with the ability to act in His obedience." And then al-Tabari states explicitly: *wa-dhālika huwa kānat tawbatuhu allatī tābahā ʿalayhi* — "and **that** is the tawbah that He gave back to him."

Read that again slowly.

The tawbah Allah gave to Adam was not Allah receiving Adam's repentance. It was Allah **giving Adam the capacity to repent at all.** The tawbah is what Allah provided. The turning was something Allah placed inside Adam, not something Adam performed on his own and brought to Allah.

This is not a minor distinction.

[PAUSE]

When we imagine Adam's repentance, we typically imagine Adam prostrating, weeping, calling out to his Lord, and Allah accepting the call. That sequence places Adam's action first and Allah's response second. But al-Tabari is reading the grammar more carefully than that. The Quran places Allah's tawbah — His turning-toward — before any mention of Adam doing anything. And al-Tabari says: that IS the tawbah. Allah's initiative is not the response to Adam's return. It is the provision that makes Adam's return possible.

The servant repents because Allah has already turned toward the servant. The door is opened before the knock is made. This is the mechanics of hidayah, compressed into three words.

[PAUSE]

There is a companion to this ayah in Surah al-Baqarah, 2:37, which belongs to the same Adam narrative: *"Then Adam received from his Lord words, and He turned toward him."* In that telling too, the words came from Allah — they were received, not generated. Adam did not compose his own prayer. He was *given* words. And then Allah turned. The entire apparatus of return, across both surahs, begins on Allah's side.

This is the grammar of how a human being comes back from the fall: not by building a ladder tall enough to reach — but by the One above sending the ladder down first.

---

### *Wa-hadā* — guided, and to what

**وَهَدَىٰ** — *wa-hadā.* Just two syllables. No object stated.

He guided.

The absence of an explicit object here is itself significant. The Quran does not say *wa-hadāhu* — "and guided him." It says *wa-hadā* — "and guided," without qualification. The guidance is stated as if naming its destination would diminish it. It is complete. Open. Unconditional in its expression.

Al-Tabari unpacks what this guidance was: *wa-hadāhu li'l-tawbati fa-waffaqahu lahā* — "and He guided him to tawbah, and He gave him *tawfīq* — enablement — for it." The guidance and the enabling arrived together. To be guided to the door and to be given the capacity to walk through it are presented as a single act of divine provision, not two separate transactions.

Al-Muyassar adds one further word: *wa-hadāhu rushdahu* — "and guided him to his *rushd*." *Rushd* in Arabic is a rich concept — it is soundness, maturity, right-headedness, the capacity to navigate one's life with sound judgment. It is the word used of someone who has come into their right mind, who is capable of wise discernment. Allah did not merely guide Adam back to where he had been before the eating. He guided Adam to his own *rushd* — to a version of himself capable of carrying what would come next.

[PAUSE]

So the sequence of *wa-hadā* is: guidance given → tawbah made possible → *rushd* restored → the life that follows. The single unremarkable word contains the entire arc of what Allah restores when He lifts a fallen person. Not just forgiveness. Not just the slate wiped clean. A person guided into their own soundness. Their own right capacity. Their own *rushd*.

This is the end of the verb sequence. Five verbs of falling. Three verbs of lifting. And after the three, a human being stands — not merely where he was before the fall, but further along than he might have been without it.

# Part 2: What the Grammar Reveals

[PAUSE — major pause]

Let us stand back now and look at the shape of what we have read.

The two ayahs have eight verb-events, and they divide along a single axis.

The first five: *fa-akalā* (they ate), *fa-badat* (it appeared), *ṭafiqā yakhṣifāni* (they began patching), *ʿaṣā* (he disobeyed), *fa-ghawā* (he erred). Every subject is human. Every direction is downward.

The last three: *ijtabāhu* (He chose him), *fa-tāba ʿalayhi* (He turned toward him), *wa-hadā* (He guided). Every subject is Allah. Every direction is toward.

The handoff — the word *thumma* — is where the whole architecture pivots. Before *thumma*, the human is the agent. After *thumma*, the human is the object. Before *thumma*, falling. After *thumma*, being lifted.

[PAUSE]

And this is the answer to the question the title was asking.

*The grammar of how Allah moves first.*

Allah moves first because after the fall, Adam has no more moves. The five verbs of ayah 121 exhaust the human capacity for action in this situation. The eating. The exposure. The panicked patching. The disobedience. The erring. What more can Adam do? He has no more verbs. He is on the floor.

This is the exact moment — not before, not after — when the subject changes.

*Thumma.*

Then. When the human has no more movement left, the divine movement begins. Not as reward. Not as response to some hidden act of virtue still waiting to be performed. But because this is how Allah is. This is the original grammar of guidance, placed at the beginning of human time so that every fallen person afterward would know: the moment you run out of verbs is not the moment the story ends. It is the moment the subject changes.

[PAUSE]

There is a reason this surah ended here, with this story, after everything it had told us about Musa and Pharaoh and the Israelites. The surah ends here because this is where every story of guidance begins. Musa at the burning bush was a man who had run out of verbs — a fugitive in a desert, forty years past his greatest failure. The Israelites at the sea had no verbs left — water behind them, armies ahead. The leaf-patching human has no more moves.

And then: *thumma.* The subject changes.

The surah that is all about guidance under hostile conditions ends by showing us the grammar of how guidance works in the first place. It works because Allah moves first. It has always worked that way, from the first human being, in the first garden, in the oldest story we carry.

# Part 3: The Living Inheritance

[PAUSE]

Before you close this reflection, sit with what it means that this story is told to you — to us — in the twenty-first surah of the Quran.

It is not told to condemn Adam. It is not told as a warning primarily. It is told as a *mirror.* Because the Quran is not describing what happened to two people in a garden that no longer exists. It is describing the structure of what happens to every human being who eats from the wrong tree, and what happens to them after.

You have your own *sawʾāt* — things in you that, when the cover is withdrawn, you do not like to look at. Things that were not created by your sins but were already there, held back from your own eyes by a mercy you did not fully appreciate until it was gone. You have your own leaf-patching — the reflexive attempts to cover yourself with the materials of your own fallen state, pressing them against yourself, hoping they hold. And you have your own moment of *thumma*. The moment when you run out of verbs. When the self-repair fails, when the garden leaves do not hold, when the exposure is too complete to manage on your own.

What this passage says to that moment is: that is the moment He begins.

Not *despite* the state you are in. Because of it. The *ijtibā* — the choosing — comes *min baʿdi* — *after.* After the fall. After the leaf-patching that did not work. After the full evidence is in. That is when the Lord of Adam chose Adam. And as al-Tabari saw clearly: He did not wait for Adam to climb back up. He provided the very capacity to turn. He gave the turning before Adam could perform it.

This is the shape of rahma. Not the rahma that rewards the effort. The rahma that gives the effort to the one who has none left. The rahma that descends — *ʿalā*, over, like shade — on the exposed person before they can find their own shade.

The surah of guidance ends with the grammar of guidance. And the grammar says: your Lord moves first. He chose Adam after the disobedience. He provided the tawbah before Adam could compose it. He guided Adam to his own *rushd* — his own capacity for soundness — and then handed that capacity back to him to use.

This is not the story of one man's recovery in one garden. This is the template. The Quran placed it here, at the end of a surah about guidance under every kind of hostile condition, so that we would know: the hostility is not the last word. The *thumma* is.

*Allahumma kamā ajtabayta Ādama min baʿdi maʿṣiyatihi wa-tubta ʿalayhi wa-hadayta-hu — fajtabinā wa-tub ʿalaynā wa-hdinā.*

*O Allah, just as You chose Adam after his disobedience, and turned toward him, and guided him — so choose us, and turn toward us, and guide us.*
