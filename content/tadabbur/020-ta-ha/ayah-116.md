# The One Who Refused

## Surah Ta-Ha, Ayah 116

**وَإِذْ قُلْنَا لِلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةِ ٱسْجُدُوا۟ لِءَادَمَ فَسَجَدُوٓا۟ إِلَّآ إِبْلِيسَ أَبَىٰ**

*Wa-idh qulnā li-l-malā'ikati-sjudū li-Ādama fa-sajadū illā Iblīsa abā.*

**"And [remember] when We said to the angels: 'Prostrate to Adam.' So they prostrated — except Iblis; he refused."**

---

## Introduction — Where We Are in the Surah

Stay with me for a moment before we open this ayah, because where it sits matters as much as what it says.

We are deep into Surah Ta-Ha. The surah opened with one of the most tender openings in the entire Quran: *Ta-Ha. We did not send down the Quran upon you to make you suffer.* From there, Allah took us into the long, intimate story of Musa — Musa called by name in the valley, Musa given his staff, Musa standing before Pharaoh, Musa parting the sea, Musa returning to find his people worshipping a golden calf.

For over a hundred ayahs we have been inside one man's biography. We have watched a prophet plead, tremble, succeed, fail, and beg his Lord for help.

And then — without warning — the surah does something startling.

It leaves Musa entirely. It leaves Egypt. It leaves the desert. And it travels backward in time. Past the prophets. Past the nations. Past the earth itself. All the way back to the very first scene of human history.

Why?

Because Ta-Ha is asking a question that the Musa story alone cannot answer. The Musa story shows us a man who suffered. But Ta-Ha wants to know: where did suffering itself begin? What is the deepest root of why we struggle, why we slip, why we forget, why we run?

Al-Tabari, in his commentary, notes something precise about the purpose of this sudden backward turn: Allah is informing His prophet ﷺ about Adam's neglect of his covenant — and establishing that Adam's descendants will follow that same pattern, except those whom Allah protects. Read in that light, the flashback is not a digression. It is a diagnosis. The long Musa narrative that preceded it had just shown us forgetting in action — the Children of Israel, an absent prophet, a golden calf constructed in the interval. Now the surah asks: where does that kind of forgetting come from? And it goes all the way to the beginning. The Quran itself, arriving in this surah at Muhammad ﷺ, is the covenant renewed for a people who carry within them the same disposition their father carried.

To answer where suffering began, the surah has to go to the moment before there was struggle. To the moment when something first went wrong in the universe.

And when it gets there, when it arrives at the origin, it shows us a scene with three elements.

A command.

A response.

And one figure — just one — who would not bend.

[PAUSE]

Here is what we are going to discover together today. The whole story of human pain, in this surah's reading, can be traced to a single word. Not a long speech of arrogance. Not a manifesto of rebellion. One word.

And that word is the verb at the end of this ayah: **abā** — *he refused.*

In most tellings of this scene across the Quran, the refusal comes with additional language — the word *istakbara*, meaning *he was arrogant*, or a quoted defense from Iblis about his superiority, or an extended account of what exactly he refused to do. There are surahs that give him more words, more explanation, more anatomy of his pride. But Ta-Ha gives us the barest construction of all. And as we will discover in the linguistic journey below — even after placing this ayah alongside every other version of this story the Quran tells — this single verb stands alone among all seven tellings, stripped of every qualification, every complement, every object, until nothing remains but the pure, unmodified fact: **he refused.**

That stripping-away is not casual. It is the whole tafsir of evil compressed into an editorial choice. And by the end of this reflection, I hope you will see why a surah named after two opening letters of mystery, a surah about a prophet who pleaded for ease, would point to refusal — not arrogance, not lust, not violence — as the first wrongness in creation.

Let's open this together.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Architecture of Exception — *illā Iblīs*

I want to start not with the dramatic verb at the end, but with a small grammatical word in the middle of the ayah.

The word **illā** — *except.*

Before we look at how Allah uses it, let's understand what an exception construction actually does in any language. Imagine you are describing a classroom. You say, *Every student passed the exam.* That is one statement. It tells you something about the group.

Now imagine you say, *Every student passed the exam, except one.*

That sentence is doing two things at once. It is describing the group — they passed. And it is isolating a single member from the group — that one didn't. The exception construction *separates*. It puts one figure outside the circle that everyone else stands inside.

In Arabic grammar, **illā** — *except* — performs this same isolating work, but with a sharper grammatical edge than English does. The thing that comes after **illā** is grammatically *cut off* from the noun that came before. It's called *istithnā'* — exception. The excepted item stands grammatically alone.

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah.

*Fa-sajadū illā Iblīsa.*

**So they prostrated — except Iblis.**

Notice the architecture. There is a group action: *they prostrated* (*fa-sajadū*). Plural. Collective. A whole congregation of angels lowering themselves before the new creation.

And then, with one word — *illā* — that whole congregation is broken open, and one figure is stood up and pointed to.

[PAUSE]

Before we go further, let us be precise about the nature of what the angels were doing. Classical commentators specify that this was a *sujūd of taḥiyyah and ikrām* — a prostration of greeting and honor, not an act of worship directed at Adam. The angels were not worshipping a creature of clay. They were, at divine command, recognizing and acknowledging the dignity Allah had placed in the new vicegerent — honoring what God had chosen to honor. This distinction reshapes everything we understand about Iblis's refusal. He was not, in some twisted way, protecting the oneness of God from misdirected worship. He was refusing to recognize the worth that Allah had assigned. The entire angelic assembly looked at Adam, saw the divine bestowal upon him, and bent in acknowledgment of it. Iblis looked at the same Adam and declined — not to worship a creature, but to honor what God had honored. The refusal was, at its core, a refusal of *recognition*. And that is a very different thing.

[PAUSE]

Now let us sit with the grammatical structure, because the *illā* construction is doing theological work.

In every other story of disobedience in the Quran — Pharaoh, Qarun, the people of 'Ad, the people of Thamud — the disobedient one is part of a group of disobedient ones. There is a community of refusal. There is shared responsibility, shared rebellion.

But here, at the very origin of evil, Allah shows us something different. The refuser is *isolated* by the grammar itself. He is not part of a movement. He is not surrounded by allies. He is the *only* one. The angels — *all of them* — bow. He alone stands.

This isolation is not just a description. It is a doctrine.

Because what the *illā* construction is teaching us, before we even get to the verb of refusal, is that disobedience is fundamentally *isolating*. It does not place you inside a community. It separates you. It singles you out. It makes you the exception in a universe that, by its nature, submits.

Look at the cosmos as Allah describes it elsewhere. *The seven heavens and the earth and whoever is in them exalt Him* (17:44). *Have you not seen that to Allah prostrates whoever is in the heavens and whoever is in the earth, and the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the moving creatures...* (22:18). The default state of creation is submission. Bowing is the native posture of existence.

To refuse to bow is not to join some other natural movement. There is no other movement. To refuse is to step out of the only choreography there is.

Here is what's remarkable about this: when Allah introduces the original act of disobedience, He does not introduce it as a *competing* allegiance. Iblis does not bow to something else. He simply does not bow. The grammar mirrors the metaphysics. *Illā Iblīs* — except him. One name, hanging in the silent space outside the group.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this implication. We often imagine the spiritual life as a contest between two pulls — toward Allah or toward something else. Toward the soul or toward the world. Toward purity or toward sin. Two teams, two flags, two directions.

But this ayah suggests something subtler. The original sin was not joining the wrong team. There was no wrong team. There was only *the team* — the entire created order in sujūd — and one figure who stepped outside of it.

To rebel, in this ayah's geometry, is not to defect to a rival. It is to become alone.

Notice how this changes the felt texture of disobedience in your own life. The next time you refuse a call to prayer, the next time you turn away from a moment of dhikr you know you should enter, the next time you withhold the apology you know you owe — the inner experience is rarely *I am joining the other side.* It is almost always: *I am stepping back. I am keeping myself separate. I am not going with them.*

That feeling — *I am holding myself out* — is the original feeling. It is older than humanity. It is what Iblis felt when the angels bent and he stood.

The grammar of this ayah names that feeling.

*Illā* — *except.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Verb That Stands Alone — *abā*

Now let's go to the final word of the ayah. The whole weight of the sentence falls on one verb: **abā** — *he refused.*

I want to teach you something about this verb that will change how you hear it.

In Arabic grammar, verbs are classified by what they need to complete their meaning. Some verbs need an object. *He hit* is incomplete — *he hit what?* Some verbs need a preposition — *he turned away from*. Some verbs are *lāzim* — they stand alone, complete in themselves, requiring no object.

When you read this verse, ask yourself: what would you expect to come after *abā*?

In English you might expect: *he refused **to do it***. Or: *he refused **the command***. Or: *he refused **to prostrate***.

But Allah does not give you any of that.

The verb sits at the end of the ayah, completely bare. *Abā.* He refused. And then — silence.

[PAUSE]

This is one of those moments where Arabic grammar is doing something English cannot easily reproduce. The absence of an object after *abā* is not an oversight. It is the entire point.

Because if Allah had said *he refused to prostrate*, the refusal would have been about a specific act. He didn't want to do that one thing. Maybe under different circumstances he would have done a different thing.

But by leaving *abā* completely unmodified, completely without an object, Allah is showing us something deeper. The refusal is not about the act. The refusal is about who he is.

He didn't refuse a command.

He refused.

That is now his name. That is now his being. That is now his orientation. He is the one whose default verb is *no*.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you an analogy from how we know human beings.

There is a difference between someone who is angry and someone who is an angry person. Someone who is angry is responding to a specific provocation — they got cut off in traffic, they were insulted, they lost something. The anger is bound to its cause. Remove the cause and the anger fades.

But an angry person is something else. The anger is not a response to a particular thing. The anger is the *furniture* of who they are. They will find reasons. They will produce justifications. The anger arrives first, and the trigger is just whatever showed up that day.

When you describe an angry person, you don't say *they were angry at X*. You just say *they are angry*. The anger has no object because it has become the person.

This is what *abā* without an object is doing. Iblis is not refusing a particular command. He has *become* refusal. The verb has no object because the verb has consumed the noun. He is no longer a being who refused. He is, from this moment forward, *refusal in the form of a being*.

This is why later in the Quran Iblis becomes a figure who *whispers* — not who reasons, not who debates, not who argues. He whispers because his nature has narrowed to a single function: producing nos. Producing turning-away. Producing the inner click that says *not now, not this, not yet*.

The verb without an object names the entire pathology.

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you why this matters for your own soul.

When the verb of refusal is bound to an object, refusal is *containable*. *I refused to make peace with my brother today.* That refusal has an end. Tomorrow, perhaps, you can return. The object — making peace — still stands there waiting. The refusal hasn't eaten you.

But there is a state of the heart that is different. A state in which refusal floats free of any particular object. *I just don't want to.* *I don't feel like it.* *Not now.* The refusal does not point to a specific thing. It is a general posture. A shrug. A tightness. A small, persistent *no* that is not directed at anything because it has become the texture of the self.

That state — that floating, objectless refusal — is the most dangerous state of the heart. Because there is nothing to negotiate with. There is no specific thing to address. The refusal is not a response to a problem. It is the ambient air the heart now breathes.

This ayah is teaching us, through grammar alone, what that state looks like at its purest.

*Abā.*

He refused. Period. No object. No condition. No specific item.

He had become refusal.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else about this verb. Classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in *Mufradāt al-Qur'ān* — record that *abā* carries a sense of *persistent, settled refusal*. It is not the refusal of a moment. It is the refusal of someone who has dug in. The Arabs would say *abā fulān ʿalayya* — he refused me, meaning he set himself against, he would not budge.

So when Allah closes this ayah with *abā*, He is not naming an instant. He is naming a settling. A digging-in. A choosing of refusal as a permanent posture.

The angels said *yes* in a moment.

Iblis said *no* — and then stayed there.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: What Allah Did NOT Say — The Asymmetric Absence

Now I want to show you something that will, I hope, take your breath away.

This story — the prostration of the angels, Iblis's refusal — is told across seven passages in the Quran. And to understand what Ta-Ha is doing, we need to lay all seven alongside each other and look at the gradation.

In Surah al-Baqarah (2:34): *They prostrated, except Iblis. He refused **and was arrogant** (*abā **wastakbara***), and he was of the disbelievers.*

In Surah al-Aʿrāf (7:11-12): Allah quotes Iblis explaining his refusal — *I am better than him, You created me from fire and created him from clay.*

In Surah al-Isrā' (17:61): Iblis speaks again — *Shall I prostrate to one You created from clay?*

In Surah al-Kahf (18:50): Iblis is described not with *abā* at all, but as one who *transgressed against the command of his Lord* (*fasaqa ʿan amri rabbihi*) — an entirely different verb, a different angle on the same event.

In Surah Ṣād (38:74-76): *He was arrogant and was of the disbelievers*, followed again by a quoted defense from Iblis.

And then there is Surah al-Ḥijr (15:31): *They prostrated, except Iblis — he refused **to be among those who prostrated*** (*abā an yakūna maʿa al-sājidīn*).

[PAUSE]

Now look at what this spread of tellings reveals, because there is a real gradation here — and it matters precisely.

The fullest versions — al-Baqarah, al-Aʿrāf, Ṣād — give us multiple layers: the act of refusal, the word *istakbara* (he was arrogant), and a quoted justification. They give us the psychology, the pride, the self-defense. They anatomize the interior of what happened. If you want to understand Iblis as a figure with motivations, a creature whose reasoning you can trace and critique, these are the surahs that give you that portrait.

Then there is al-Ḥijr. Al-Ḥijr removes the *istakbara* label — it does not call him arrogant, does not add that diagnostic word. It gives us *abā* alone. But then it continues: *an yakūna maʿa al-sājidīn* — he refused *to be among the prostrators*. The arrogance label is gone, but the content of the refusal is still specified. Al-Ḥijr still tells you *what* was refused. The object — the community of those who bow — is named. You can point to it.

And then there is Ta-Ha.

[PAUSE]

Ta-Ha is the only telling in the Quran where *abā* stands absolutely alone. It shares with al-Ḥijr the absence of the *istakbara* label — two surahs, out of the seven, decline to name his arrogance directly. But unlike al-Ḥijr, Ta-Ha does not give you a complement clause. It does not say *he refused to prostrate*. It does not say *he refused to be among them*. The verb ends the ayah, and then the sentence ends, and there is nothing after it.

This is the deepest stripping in the Quran's telling of this moment. And it is a real asymmetry: every other instance where *abā* appears in this scene — whether surrounded by *istakbara* and quoted speeches, or paired with a minimal complement like al-Ḥijr's *an yakūna maʿa al-sājidīn* — tells you something about the content of the refusal. Ta-Ha alone withholds even that.

[PAUSE]

Think about what this gradation reveals.

The fullest tellings give you a portrait. You see the layers: the act, the arrogance, the spoken justification. You understand Iblis as a creature who reasoned himself into refusal — and whose reasoning you could, in principle, argue with.

Al-Ḥijr strips away the psychological label but keeps the content — what he would not do, what community he would not join. Even here, even without *istakbara*, you can ask: *what did he refuse?* And the text will answer: he refused to be among those who prostrate. There is still an object to point to.

Ta-Ha strips even that away.

In Ta-Ha, you cannot ask *what did he refuse?* because the verb gives you nothing to point to. The act of prostration is described — *fa-sajadū*, they prostrated — but the refusal that follows has no grammatical attachment to it. It does not say *he refused that*. It says: *abā.* What is left, when everything is taken away, is not a description of an act undone. It is a characterization of a being. Not what he did not do. Not even what he was. Just the settled, permanent, objectless fact: **he refused.**

[PAUSE]

Notice that this stripping is doing something that the other tellings cannot do. The surahs that give Iblis words — *I am better than him, fire is superior to clay* — give you an Iblis with a position you can engage. You can examine the argument. You can point to its flaw. The logic is wrong and it can be shown to be wrong.

But the Iblis of Ta-Ha has no argument. He has only the verb. And a verb without an object cannot be reasoned with, because there is nothing specific being refused. The refusal is not a response to a particular situation that could have been different. It is simply what he is.

This is one reason why — if you look at how Iblis operates in the Quran after this moment — he does not argue. He whispers. He suggests. He produces the small inner movement of *not now*, *not this*, *I don't feel like it*. He never presents you with a philosophy. He presents you with a refusal so sourceless and ambient that it seems to come from you.

That is the nature of what was born in this moment.

Not an argument.

A *no* so old and bare it has forgotten what it was ever refusing.

And this surah — the surah of mercy, the surah of Musa, the surah that is about to show us Adam's own forgetting — knew that the deepest picture of that first wrongness required no words beyond a single verb.

*Abā.*

He refused.
