# Waylakum: The Prophet Who Warned His Enemies Out of Mercy

## Surah Ta-Ha, Ayah 61

> قَالَ لَهُم مُّوسَىٰ وَيْلَكُمْ لَا تَفْتَرُوا عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا فَيُسْحِتَكُم بِعَذَابٍ ۖ وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنِ ٱفْتَرَىٰ

*Qāla lahum Mūsā waylakum lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhābin wa-qad khāba mani'ftarā.*

**Moses said to them: Woe to you! Do not invent a lie against Allah, lest He uproot you with a punishment. And he has surely failed who invents [against Him].**

---

## INTRODUCTION

Picture the morning.

Pharaoh has set the day. He has called it *yawm al-zīnah* — the day of adornment, the festival day, the day when all of Egypt would gather. He wanted witnesses. He wanted the largest possible audience to watch this stranger from the desert get publicly humiliated.

The magicians have come from every corner of the empire. Tradition says they were not amateurs — they were the elite, the ones who had spent decades perfecting their craft. They knew exactly what they were doing. They had been promised wealth and proximity to the throne if they won. They had been threatened with destruction if they lost. They came hungry — hungry for reward, hungry for status, hungry to defend the Egypt they knew.

And then there is Musa. One man. Standing on the open ground. With his brother. With a staff.

The crowds have arrived. Pharaoh sits on his elevated throne. The magicians stand in their rows. They are coiled. They are ready. They have brought ropes — long, prepared ropes treated with mercury or quicksilver — and staffs that would, when thrown, *appear* to slither and writhe. The Quran will tell us their illusion was so vast that even Musa himself, for a moment, *felt fear in his soul*.

But before any of that happens — before the ropes are cast, before the illusion fills the field, before the crowd gasps — there is a moment. A breath. Musa turns to face them. To face the men who came to defeat him. The men who, in minutes, will throw what looks like a thousand serpents at him.

And he opens his mouth.

[PAUSE]

Of all the things a person could say in that moment — of all the threats, of all the rallying cries, of all the curses he could have hurled — what comes out of Musa's mouth?

**Waylakum.**

*Woe to you.*

But the word is not what it sounds like. And the sentence that follows is not a battle cry — it is a warning given in the same tone a mother gives a child who is about to step in front of a moving cart. It is the voice of a prophet who, in the very moment of confrontation, *worries* for the souls of the men sent to destroy him.

This is the moment we will sit with. Because in this single ayah is buried something about prophetic character that, once you see it, will change what you understand about what it means to be on the side of Allah.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Waylakum* — The Word That Sounds Like a Curse but Speaks Compassion

Let me start with something simple from your own experience.

A small child runs toward a hot stove. A parent — heart pounding — shouts: *"No! No, you'll get hurt!"* The shout sounds, to anyone outside, like anger. The volume is loud. The face looks fierce. But what is actually animating the shout?

Fear *for the child*.

Compassion in the shape of urgency.

Now hold that picture, because the Arabic word *waylakum* operates on this exact register — and almost every English translation flattens it into something it isn't.

**The word.** *Waylakum* (وَيْلَكُمْ) is built from the noun *wayl* (وَيْل), addressed to "you all" (the *kum* ending). The classical lexicons — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in his *Mufradāt* — note something important about *wayl*. It is **not a curse**. It is not the same as *laʿna* (cursing) or *thubūr* (ruin invoked upon someone). *Wayl* is closer to **an exclamation of grief, a sound someone makes when they see another person about to fall into harm**.

Al-Rāghib explains it as a word used "for one who falls into ruin" — but classical commentators note that when used in direct address as a warning, it often functions as what we would call **an expression of compassion in the form of alarm**. It is the parent's cry, not the enemy's curse.

Some classical commentators offer a sharper register where *waylakum* contains rebuke as well. So which is it — compassion or rebuke?

This is where the **composite sketch** matters: it is *both at once*. It is a sound that contains warning, and the warning is fierce *because* the speaker actually cares whether the addressee survives the next moment. The rebuke and the mercy are not in tension. They are the same breath.

Notice now: who is Musa addressing?

The men who have come to defeat him. The men whose ropes are about to fly at him. The men who, if they win, will see him executed. These are the *enemies in the room*.

And the first word out of his mouth is the word a parent uses for a child running toward fire.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Let it land.

The methodology of the prophet, in the moment of maximal threat, is to speak as though the threat to *them* is greater than the threat to him. He is not afraid for his own life — Allah has already promised him victory. He is afraid for *their* afterlife.

**Waylakum** is what mercy sounds like when it has run out of polite words and is now shouting — but is still mercy.

### Element Two: *Lā Taftarū ʿalā Allāhi Kadhiban* — The Verb of Cutting Lies from Cloth

Now let me teach you a small piece of Arabic morphology before we open the next phrase, because without it the depth of what Musa says will pass you by.

In Arabic, verbs have what we call **forms** — different patterns built from the same three root letters that bend the meaning in specific directions. There is a base form (Form I) — the simple action. And then there are derived forms (II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X) — each pattern adding a specific layer.

**Form VIII** (called *iftaʿala*) typically adds a layer of **reflexivity, deliberateness, and effort**. It often signals that the actor *takes the action upon himself* — does it intentionally, with full participation of the self.

Now hold that idea, and let's look at the verb Musa uses: *taftarū* (تَفْتَرُوا). It comes from the root **f-r-y** (ف-ر-ي).

The base meaning of f-r-y in classical lexicons is striking. *Lisān al-ʿArab* and the *Mufradāt* both note that *farā* originally meant **to cut**, **to slit**, **to fashion something by cutting** — like cutting and stitching a leather waterskin from a hide, or cutting cloth to make a garment. It is the verb of *manufacture by cutting from raw material*.

Now apply Form VIII reflexivity to that root. *Iftirā* — the act of taking that "cutting and fashioning" *upon oneself*, deliberately, with effort.

What is the word's meaning? **To fabricate. To invent. To deliberately stitch together a thing that was not.**

In English, "fabricate" comes from the Latin *fabricare* — to construct, to manufacture. The Arabic does the same trick. The word for *lying about Allah* is **the word for tailoring a garment that was never given**. You sit down with thread and cloth, and from your own hand you cut and stitch a thing — and then claim Allah handed it to you.

This is why the Quran reserves *iftarā* almost exclusively for **religious fabrication** — claiming Allah said something He did not say, claiming Allah ordered something He did not order, claiming a sign or a revelation that was not given.

Now read what Musa actually says: *Lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban* — **"Do not stitch a lie against Allah."**

Here is what is going on at the deepest level of this confrontation: Musa knows that in *minutes*, the magicians will throw their ropes. And what those ropes will do is exactly *iftirā*. They will manufacture, by craft and deliberate effort, an appearance of life — serpents that are not serpents — and the crowd will believe these snakes were real, were powers, were signs.

The magicians are not just performing magic. They are about to **fabricate a sign**. They are about to claim — implicitly, by performing it — that the gods of Egypt have power equal to the God of Musa. They are about to *stitch a garment of divinity* and present it to the crowd.

And Musa's warning is precise: *Don't do this thing. Don't sit at the loom of falsehood and weave a god from your own hands.*

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is that *iftirā* — this fabrication — is not just a sin of speech. It is a sin of **manufacture**. You are taking raw material — your own thoughts, your own desires, your own pride — and you are *cutting and stitching* it into something that you then drape on God.

That is what Musa is naming. And he is naming it before the act, while there is still time to step back from the loom.

### Element Three: *Fa-yusḥitakum* — The Verb of Being Scraped from the Earth

Now we come to the consequence Musa names. And the word he uses is one of the most physically violent words in the Quran.

*Fa-yusḥitakum* (فَيُسْحِتَكُم). The root is **s-ḥ-t** (س-ح-ت). And it has a very specific physical meaning that the classical lexicographers preserve carefully.

*Sahata* in its base form means **to peel, to scrape off, to strip away**. The root is used for *peeling bark from a tree*, for *scraping the surface off a thing*, for *removing the skin*. It is the verb of removal-by-friction, of stripping something down to nothing by abrasion.

Then it intensifies. In Form IV (*asḥata*), it carries the meaning **to root out completely, to annihilate, to extirpate** — to so thoroughly remove a thing from existence that no trace of it remains, like a tree pulled out by its deepest root and the soil scraped clean afterward.

This is a word the Quran uses *very sparingly*. It does not appear in the everyday vocabulary of warning. It is reserved for moments where the punishment described is **not just death — it is erasure**.

Listen to the word phonetically: *yus-ḥit-akum*. Notice what your throat does when you say it. The *ḥ* is a deep, scraping consonant — the sound of friction. The *t* at the end is a hard cut. The word physically *scrapes* the throat as it is spoken. The mouth performs what the meaning describes.

This is what the Arabs call *jiras al-lafẓ* — the texture of the word matching the texture of the meaning. The word *sounds like* the act it names. To pronounce it is to peel.

[PAUSE]

Now hold the whole sentence together: *lā taftarū ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban fa-yusḥitakum bi-ʿadhābin* — **"Do not stitch a lie upon Allah, lest He scrape you from existence with a punishment."**

The verbs are in conversation. *Iftirā* is to **construct**. *Sahata* is to **deconstruct**. You sit at the loom and weave a false garment for God; He sits in His throne and reaches down and *peels you off the earth*. You manufactured a thing that was not; He removes you so completely that no trace of *you* remains.

The justice is precise. The punishment matches the crime in its very mechanics. They build; He scrapes. They cut and stitch; He uproots and erases.

And notice — there is no specified object for what is destroyed *of* you. *Yusḥitakum* — He scrapes *you*. Not your wealth, not your reputation, not just your bodies — *you*. Whole. The pronoun stands alone. The whole self, peeled away.

---

### Element Four (Brief): *Wa-qad Khāba Mani'ftarā* — The Universal Without an Object

The ayah closes with something quiet and absolute: *wa-qad khāba mani'ftarā* — **"and surely he has failed who fabricates."**

Two things in one breath.

First, *khāba*. The verb means **to fail at one's hope, to be disappointed in what one sought**. It is the verb of the seeker who reaches and finds nothing in his hand. Not just "he failed" in the abstract — *he reached out for what he wanted and it was not there*.

Second — and this is what most translations miss — there is **no object** to *iftarā*. In the prohibition, Musa specified: *do not fabricate against Allah a lie*. But in the universal close, the verb stands alone: *mani'ftarā*. **Whoever fabricated.**

Fabricated *what*? About *whom*?

The Arabic leaves it open. **Anyone who fabricated anything has failed.**

The principle is universal. The specificity has dropped away. The fabricator at the loom of falsehood — whatever cloth he is cutting, whatever lie he is stitching, whatever garment he is draping on whom — *that man has failed*. The verb *khāba* is in the perfect tense — it has *already happened*. The failure is sealed in the moment of the fabrication itself, before any external punishment arrives.

This is what classical scholars call a *qaḍiyyah kulliyyah* — a universal proposition. Musa has lifted his particular warning to the magicians up into a permanent law of reality.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Now let us bring these three movements together. *Waylakum* — the cry of compassion. *Iftirā* — the manufacture of false garments for God. *Yusḥitakum* — the scraping away of those who weave such garments. *Khāba mani'ftarā* — the universal that this fabricator has *already* failed.

Let's now ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the inner life of the prophet, and the inner architecture of the lie itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Prophet Who Worries for His Enemies

Think for a moment about what it means to confront someone who has come to harm you.

If you have ever been in a real argument — the kind where the other person has made up their mind to defeat you — you know what your mind does. It narrows. The other person stops being a full human being and becomes a *problem*. Their soul disappears from your view. What remains is the thing they are about to do *to you*, and your job is to stop them.

This is the natural human response to threat. The amygdala fires. The world contracts. Empathy goes offline. Your own survival rises up and crowds out the other person's interiority. We are wired this way for good reason — in actual physical danger, you don't have time to wonder how your attacker is feeling.

But notice what is happening in this ayah.

Musa is in the moment of *peak* threat. The magicians are coiled. The crowd is watching. Pharaoh is on his throne. If Musa loses this contest, his life is over and the message of monotheism is publicly humiliated for a generation. By every measure, this is the moment for *focused enmity* — the moment when even the most generous heart should narrow into a single edge of self-defense.

And what is Musa doing?

He is **worrying for the magicians**. He is concerned that *they* might fabricate a lie. He is afraid that *they* might be uprooted. The first word out of his mouth is a word of mercy aimed at *them*.

[PAUSE]

This is what prophetic character looks like. And it is almost impossible to comprehend without dwelling on it.

The prophet, in the moment when his life is on the line, *does not stop seeing the souls of those who came to kill him*. They have not become objects to him. They have not been reduced to the role of "enemy." He looks at them and sees men who are about to do something that will *destroy them in the next world*, and the thought of that destruction moves him before the thought of his own danger does.

Notice what this implies about how Musa understands the contest. To him, the question is not *will I win?* The question is *will these men ruin themselves?* Allah has already told him he is the higher one (*innaka anta'l-aʿlā* — Ta-Ha 68). He is not anxious about his own outcome. He is anxious about *theirs*.

Now apply this to your own life.

Think of the last person who hurt you. Or the person you are in conflict with right now — the family member, the colleague, the public figure you cannot stand, the person who said the thing about you that wounded. Sit with that picture.

When you imagine that person, do you still see their soul? Do you still see them as someone Allah is dealing with — someone whose afterlife is precariously poised, someone whose end *matters* to you?

Or have they become a problem to be defeated?

The hardest part of being on the side of guidance is this: **the people on the other side do not stop being real to Allah just because they have become unreal to you**. The angels record their deeds with the same care. Their hearts are still fields where rain might one day fall. Their children might one day pray. The mercy of Allah is hovering over them, looking for an opening.

Musa's prophethood is not just in the staff that becomes a serpent. It is in the heart that, while the serpent is being prepared by the other side, *still cares whether the man holding the rope makes it to Paradise*.

And here is what moves me most about this scene: hours later — *hours later* — these same magicians will fall to the ground in prostration. *We have believed in the Lord of the worlds, the Lord of Musa and Harun.* Pharaoh will threaten to crucify them on palm trunks, and they will not flinch. They will become — these men who came to destroy Musa — among the *highest of believers* mentioned in the Quran.

Do you think their conversion happened only when they saw the staff swallow the ropes?

Or do you think the seed was planted *in this very ayah* — when the man they had come to defeat, in the breath before the fight, looked at them and said *waylakum* with the voice of a father?

The miracle of the staff was the staff. But the miracle of Musa's character was *waylakum*. And it may be that the second miracle did more spiritual work in their hearts than the first.

[PAUSE]

The practical question is sharp: **In your conflicts, is the other person still real to you as a soul before Allah?**

This is not a call to passivity. Musa fought. Musa won. The truth must be defended and falsehood must be exposed. But the inner posture of the one defending the truth determines whether his victory is the *kind* of victory Allah wants.

A victory delivered with hatred in the heart converts no one. A victory delivered with *waylakum* in the heart cracks open the very enemies it defeats.

### Theme Two: Why "Lying Against Allah" Is the Lie That Unmakes the Liar

Let me start somewhere mundane.

Imagine you have a friend whom you love. And you find out that someone is going around the city saying *your friend said this, your friend believes that, your friend ordered this thing done in his name* — none of it true. The lies are using your friend's name as authority. They are putting words in his mouth. They are making him the warrant for things he never said.

What would happen in your heart? Anger, of course. But also a strange feeling of *desecration*. Not just because your friend is being lied about, but because his very *name* is being used as a weapon. His authority is being borrowed without permission. His face is being placed on someone else's argument.

Now amplify this infinitely.

This is what *iftirā ʿalā Allāh* — fabricating a lie against Allah — actually is. And it is the reason this single category of sin is treated, throughout the Quran, as the *gravest of all human acts*.

Every other sin damages the soul of the sinner. *Iftirā* damages the very *name of Allah* in the world.

When you steal, you wronged a person. When you lied, you wronged a hearer. When you fornicated, you wronged a covenant. These are grave. But when you fabricated *against Allah* — when you said *Allah said this* and He did not, when you said *Allah ordered this* and He did not, when you crafted a religious claim from your own desire and stitched His name onto it — you have done something different in *kind*. You have used the most sacred Authority in existence as a costume for your own agenda. You have placed His face on your fabrication.

[PAUSE]

Notice that this is exactly what the magicians are about to do. They are not just performing tricks. They are performing tricks *in the name of Pharaoh's gods*, in defense of a system that calls Pharaoh divine. Every coil of every rope, when it appears to slither, is an implicit claim: *the gods of Egypt have power equal to the God of Musa*. They are stitching divinity onto Pharaoh's throne. They are fabricating against Allah.

And Musa is warning them: *the moment you let those ropes leave your hand in service of this lie, you are not just performing a trick — you are entering the most catastrophic category of human action.*

This is why the consequence is *yusḥitakum* — being scraped from existence. The crime is *constructive* — they are building a false garment for God. The punishment is *deconstructive* — He unbuilds them. The crime touches His name; the punishment removes theirs.

Now apply this honestly to ourselves.

We do not have ropes to throw. But we do have *iftirā* available to us in subtle forms.

When we attribute to Allah a position He did not take — *Allah definitely wants me to do this, Allah is on my side in this dispute, Allah hates the same people I hate* — we are stitching. When we reduce religion to a costume for our own preferences — using verses to win arguments we already wanted to win, dragging Allah's name into our personal feuds — we are at the loom. When we declare other people outside Allah's mercy because *we* are angry with them — we are cutting and sewing.

The Quran's most repeated description of those who fabricate is *fa-man aẓlamu mimmani'ftarā ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban* — **"who is more unjust than the one who fabricates against Allah a lie?"** This phrase, with slight variations, appears more than a dozen times in the Quran. Allah keeps returning to it. He keeps naming it. Because of all the ways human beings injure themselves, this is the one that pulls them out by the deepest root.

[PAUSE]

The practical question is uncomfortable: **What have you said, in your life, that you placed in Allah's mouth that He did not say?**

What ruling did you declare on someone else's behalf? What verdict did you assume about another soul? What position did you ascribe to your faith that was actually just your culture, your ego, your fear?

The mercy in this ayah is that Musa speaks the warning *before* the fabrication happens. There is still time to lower the rope. There is still time to step away from the loom. The magicians, hours later, *will* take that exit. They will see the truth and abandon the fabrication and prostrate to the Lord of Musa and Harun.

The exit is always there. *Waylakum* is given so that *yusḥitakum* never has to arrive.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have seen. Three linguistic movements, two themes — but they are not separate. They are the same insight from inside and outside.

From outside, the architecture of the ayah is a **descending warning of perfect symmetry**. The verbs of the ayah trace an arc:
- *Waylakum* — the alarm (compassion)
- *Lā taftarū* — the named act (construction of falsehood)
- *Yusḥitakum* — the consequence (deconstruction of the constructor)
- *Khāba mani'ftarā* — the universal seal (the failure already complete)

Notice the structure: the prophet's *mercy* names the act, names the consequence, and then lifts the whole thing into a universal principle. The progression moves from *you here, now* to *anyone, ever*. From this moment in Egypt to the end of time.

From inside, the ayah is a portrait of two opposed motions in the universe.

There is the motion of *iftirā* — humans sitting at the loom, cutting and stitching false garments to drape on God, manufacturing claims He did not make, weaving authority from their own desires.

And there is the motion of *yusḥitakum* — the response of reality to such weaving. Allah does not negotiate with the loom. He scrapes the weaver from the earth.

In between these two motions stands the prophet. And his job, his entire prophetic function, is captured in a single word at the start of this ayah: *waylakum*. He is the warning that stands between the loom and the punishment. He is the voice that tells the weaver: *step away, before the scraping comes*.

This is what the Quran is. This is what it has always been. *Waylakum* on the largest possible scale. The mercy that stands between humanity and the consequence of its own fabrications, calling out, calling out, calling out — *do not stitch what you cannot wear; do not place on Allah what He did not place on Himself; come back from the loom*.

### Questions to Carry

1. In your current conflicts — with family, with colleagues, with public figures, with anyone — has the other person stopped being a real soul before Allah and become only a problem? What would it take to see *waylakum* in your own mouth toward them?

2. What positions have you attributed to Allah that may actually be your own preferences in a borrowed costume? Where in your religious life have you been at the loom?

3. When someone warns you fiercely — a parent, a teacher, a friend — can you hear the *compassion* underneath the volume? Or do you only hear the volume?

4. The magicians had been at the loom for years before this morning. Yet within hours, they would prostrate. What does this say about the speed at which a heart can move once the warning is finally heard?

5. *Khāba mani'ftarā* — the failure of the fabricator is already complete in the moment of the fabrication. What does it change in you to know that some failures are sealed in the act itself, before any external punishment arrives?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The prophet of Allah, in the moment his enemies came to destroy him, opened his mouth not with a curse but with a cry of mercy — because he understood that the true catastrophe was not what they would do to *him*, but what they were about to do to *themselves* by fabricating a lie against the One who would, if they continued, scrape them from existence.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

Two invitations emerge from this ayah, and they are connected.

**First**: in your conflicts, ask whether you can find your *waylakum*. Not as performance. Not as condescension. But as a real moment of looking at the person across from you — the one who hurt you, the one you cannot stand, the one who is wrong about everything — and *still* feeling, somewhere in your heart, that their soul is real before Allah and that you would rather they be saved than be defeated. This does not mean abandoning the truth. Musa fought and won. But he won with mercy still alive in his chest. That is the prophetic mode.

**Second**: walk away from your loom. Wherever in your life you have been weaving a costume for Allah's authority — using His name to win arguments, claiming His ruling on questions you have not actually researched, ascribing positions to Him that come from your culture or your wound — step back. *Lā taftarū*. The exit is open. The magicians took it. So can you.
