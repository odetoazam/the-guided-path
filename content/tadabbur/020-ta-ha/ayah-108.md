# The Day Voices Were Hushed Before Ar-Rahman

## Surah Ta-Ha, Ayah 108

> [ayah:20:108] يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَّبِعُونَ ٱلدَّاعِىَ لَا عِوَجَ لَهُۥ ۖ وَخَشَعَتِ ٱلْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ فَلَا تَسْمَعُ إِلَّا هَمْسًا
>
> *Yawma'idhin yattabiʿūna ad-dāʿiya lā ʿiwaja lah, wa khashaʿati al-aṣwātu lir-Raḥmāni fa-lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā.*
>
> "On that Day they will follow the Caller — no crookedness in him. And the voices will be hushed before the Most Merciful, so you will hear nothing but a whisper."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before the Ayah

Sit for a moment with the world you actually live in.

Open your phone. Walk into any street. Step into a marketplace, a school, an airport, a family gathering. What dominates? **Sound.** Voices layered on voices. Notifications. Engines. Music in shops. Someone arguing two tables over. A baby crying. A podcast in your earbuds laid over the murmur of the cafe. We are immersed in noise the way fish are immersed in water — so completely that we have forgotten what its absence would even feel like.

Now hold this in your mind, and step into the scene Allah is about to paint.

In Surah Ta-Ha, after rehearsing the entire story of Moses — Pharaoh, the magicians, the parting of the sea, the calf, Samiri — Allah turns the camera toward the end. The mountains will be uprooted (107). The earth will be left a level plain. And then, in this ayah, He shows us the gathered creatures. The greatest assembly in the history of existence. Every soul that ever drew breath. **Billions upon billions, packed onto a single plain.**

And what is the dominant feature of this scene?

Not screaming. Not chaos. Not thunder.

**Silence.**

Silence so complete that Allah Himself stops to describe it. Silence so total that the only sound He says you will perceive — the only ambient sound across the entire assembled creation — is a *whisper*.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

The Day of Judgment is the loudest event in the history of existence — and Allah describes it as the *quietest moment a human being will ever experience.* That paradox is not decorative. It is the door into something this ayah wants us to see about ourselves, about Allah's mercy, and about the noise we are currently drowning in.

By the end of this reflection I want you to feel why the whisper of that Day is louder than every shout we currently produce.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: خَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ — "The Voices Were Hushed"

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, when you want to say "people went silent," you have an obvious construction: *sakata an-nāsu* — "the people fell silent." Subject: the people. Verb: silenced themselves.

That is not what Allah says here.

He says: **خَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ** — *khashaʿati al-aṣwātu* — "the voices were hushed."

Notice the subject. Not the people. **The voices.**

Let's sit with this.

In our experience, voices are something we *produce*. They belong to us. We control them — we raise them, we lower them, we project, we whisper, we shout. The voice is the instrument of the self. It is downstream of the will.

But here, Allah grammatically detaches the voices from the people. The voices become their own subject. They become a thing that *acts upon itself* — or rather, a thing that has had something done to it.

#### Opening the verb

The verb Allah chose is **khashaʿat**, from the root خ-ش-ع.

This root is not the standard word for "to be silent." Allah has another verb for that — *sakata*. He doesn't use it. **Khushuʿ** is something else entirely.

Khushuʿ is the word used for what *should* happen in salah. *Qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn alladhīna hum fī ṣalātihim khāshiʿūn* — "successful are the believers who in their prayer are *khāshiʿūn*."

What is khushuʿ? It is the inward bowing of something proud. It is when something that was tall stands lower. When something that was loud goes quiet not because it has been *gagged* but because it has *recognized*. It is reverence pressed down upon the body until the body stops resisting it.

So when Allah says **khashaʿati al-aṣwātu** — He is not saying "the voices were silenced by external force." He is saying:

**The voices themselves recognized. The voices themselves bowed.**

The instrument that humans used in this world to argue, to boast, to lie, to sing falsehood, to drown out the call of truth — that instrument, on that Day, recognizes its Lord and lays itself down.

[PAUSE]

#### Read the absence

Look at what Allah did *not* say. He did not say: "the people fell silent." He could have. He chose not to.

Why?

Because on the Day of Judgment, **the people are not yet the focus**. The mountains have already gone. The earth is leveled. And now even the voices — the most defiant feature of the worldly self — have receded. What is left is the soul, exposed, with nothing between it and its Lord. The voices have already given up.

A man in this world hides behind his voice. He raises it when he is afraid. He laughs to mask his shame. He argues to prevent reflection. The voice is the **screen**.

And Allah is telling us: on that Day, **the screen falls before the man does.**

#### What this single phrase reveals

By choosing *khashaʿat* over *sakatat*, and by making the voices the subject rather than the people, Allah shows us that the silence of Judgment Day is not a silence imposed from outside. **It is a silence that emerges from within.** Every voice — even the proudest, even the loudest, even the one that shouted *anā rabbukum al-aʿlā* — recognizes. And recognition, by its nature, lowers itself.

### Element Two: لِلرَّحْمَنِ — "Before *Ar-Rahman*"

#### Teaching the structure first

Allah has ninety-nine names. Each name is a different angle of His being. The Quran does not deploy them randomly. Every time a name appears in an ayah, it is the most precise word for that exact moment.

If I say: "I went to my mother for comfort," and you ask me, "Why did you say *mother* and not *parent*?" — you understand immediately. The word *mother* has a particular tenderness. *Parent* would have been generic. *Mother* carries the meaning the moment needed.

So when an ayah deploys a divine name, the question always to ask is: **why this name here?**

#### Opening the choice

This is a Day of Judgment ayah. A moment of reckoning. A scene of awe and fear so total that voices can no longer function.

What name would you expect?

You would expect — perhaps — **Al-Jabbar** (The Compeller). **Al-Qahhar** (The Subduer). **Al-Muntaqim** (The Avenger). **Al-ʿAzīz** (The Mighty). **Al-Jalīl** (The Majestic). Names that match the weight of the scene.

Read the ayah again.

**وَخَشَعَتِ الْأَصْوَاتُ لِلرَّحْمَنِ** — "And the voices were hushed before **Ar-Rahman**."

*Ar-Rahman.* The All-Merciful. The name whose root *r-ḥ-m* refers to mercy so vast and so unconditional that it covers even the disbeliever in this world. The name Allah used in the second verse of the Book — *Bismillah ar-Rahman ar-Rahim*. The name we recite seventeen times a day in al-Fatiha.

That is the name that names Him on the Day the voices die.

[PAUSE]

#### Stay with this

This is one of the most theologically dense word choices in the entire Quran.

It tells us something we desperately need to hear: **the mercy is not switched off on Judgment Day.**

Many of us, deep down, carry an image of God in which He is loving in this world and angry in the next. As if Ar-Rahman is the name He uses *now* and Al-Muntaqim is the name He uses *then*. The Quran refuses this.

On the Day when the voices fall — when the proud are flattened, when the deeds are weighed, when justice is finally complete — the One presiding is still *Ar-Rahman*. The name that animated creation is the same name that presides over the gathering.

What does this mean?

It means: **the entire structure of Judgment is itself a structure of mercy.**

The fact that there will be accountability — that is mercy. (Imagine a universe where the oppressor was never confronted by his oppression. That would be a horror.)

The fact that intercession will be allowed for some — that is mercy. (The next ayah, 109, tells us this directly.)

The fact that even on that Day, voices that bow are met by a Lord whose first name to His creation is *Ar-Rahman* — that is mercy.

#### Read the asymmetry

Here is what is staggering. The voices are hushed *for* Ar-Rahman — *li-ar-Raḥmān*. The preposition *li* in Arabic carries the sense of *to, for, in submission to*. The voices submit *to* the very name that, in this world, was ignored most.

How many of those silent figures, in their lives, drowned out *Ar-Rahman*'s call? They heard the name in adhan. They heard the Quran on someone's lips. They heard the Bismillah at the start of a meal they were invited to. They turned the volume up on something else.

And now — at last — every voice is hushed *for that very name.*

There is a tragic poetry here. **The name they would not listen to in this world is the name in whose presence their voices finally cannot speak.**

[PAUSE]

#### What this single name reveals

The choice of *Ar-Rahman* over Al-Jabbar tells us that the Day of Judgment is not the *contradiction* of mercy — it is the *completion* of mercy. The same Lord. The same name. The same hand. What changes is not Allah. What changes is that finally, on that Day, every soul will see clearly what was always true.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: هَمْسًا — "A Whisper"

#### Teaching the concept first

There is a category of word in Arabic — and in every language — where the *sound* of the word is doing some of the work of its meaning. We call this onomatopoeia in English. *Buzz. Hush. Whisper. Murmur.* Read those four words aloud. The mouth itself enacts the meaning.

The Arabic Quran uses this technique with extraordinary precision. Allah does not just *describe* a reality — He sometimes lets the word's *phonetic shape* enact it.

Hold that in mind, then look at the final word of this ayah.

#### Opening the word

**هَمْسًا** — *hamsan.*

Say it aloud. Slowly.

*hams.*

Three letters: ه - م - س. A breath, a closing of the lips, a soft hiss.

The word **sounds like what it means.** The mouth is barely opening. There is no vowel that demands projection. No hard consonant that strikes. It is a word a person could speak without anyone two feet away hearing them. **The word itself is a whisper.**

The classical lexicons document several meanings for this root:

- The **soft sound of footsteps on sand** — the muffled padding of a foot on soft ground
- The **soft murmur of camels chewing** at night
- A **secret spoken between two people** so quiet it does not reach a third
- The **breath of a voice** when speech has died down to almost nothing

[PAUSE]

#### Stay with this

Look at the architecture Allah built.

Earlier in the ayah: **lā tasmaʿu illā hamsā** — "you will not hear *anything except* a whisper."

The exception structure (*illā*) is doing work. It tells us: there *is* sound. The Day is not absolute silence. There is something to be heard. **But the only thing your ears can pick up across the entire gathered creation is *hams*.**

What are some interpretations the classical mufassirūn offered?

Some said: it is the **sound of feet** as the assembled creatures shuffle toward the place of standing. Billions of feet moving across the leveled earth — and the dominant ambient sound of that motion is *hams*. A muffled footfall.

Others said: it is the **last breath of speech** — the residual hum of a creation that has lost its voice but has not yet been individually called.

Others said: it is **secret whispered confessions** — souls whispering to themselves what they already know.

The composite picture: a vast plain. Trillions of feet. Trillions of souls. Mountains gone. Sky changed. The Lord present. The voices hushed. And in that ocean of presence — the only audible thing — is the whisper of a footstep, the breath of a fading voice, the murmur of a secret no one needs to confess because everything is already known.

[PAUSE]

#### Read the sonic rhetoric

The ayah ends on this word. *Hamsan.* That is the last sound Allah leaves in your ear. Listen to how the verse closes:

*…fa-lā tasmaʿu illā **hams-an**.*

The word, falling at the end, fades the way a whisper fades. The ayah does not slam shut. It hushes. **The verse itself ends in a whisper.**

This is sonic theology. Allah is not just *telling* you about the Day's silence — He is *sounding* it in your mouth as you recite. The architecture of the verse is the architecture of the scene.

#### What this single word reveals

By ending with *hams* — a word whose phonetic body is itself a whisper — Allah closes the door of the ayah the same way the door of the worldly noise will close on that Day. **Soft. Final. Total.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge Between Linguistics and Themes

We have stood with three things:

- **Khashaʿati al-aṣwātu** — the voices themselves bowed
- **Lir-Raḥmān** — and bowed before the One whose name is Mercy
- **Hamsan** — leaving only the whisper

These three are not three observations. They are one architectural truth, viewed from three angles. Together they tell us that on the Day of Judgment, the noise we currently mistake for life will be revealed for what it is — a temporary scaffolding around a soul whose only true posture is silent submission to the Most Merciful.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way we are living *right now*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Our Noise Is Borrowed

Begin with this: **why do we make so much noise?**

Watch yourself for one day. Notice every time you reach for something to fill silence. The phone in the elevator. The podcast on the walk. The radio in the car. The TV in the background while you eat. The notification you check three times before going to sleep. The mental chatter you generate when there is finally external quiet.

Why?

Most of us, if we are honest, fill the silence because **silence terrifies us.** In silence, we begin to hear a quieter voice — the voice of conscience, the voice that asks unanswered questions, the voice that whispers *what are you doing with this life?* And so we drown it out. We talk over it. We shout it down.

Now read the ayah again.

*And the voices will be hushed before the Most Merciful, so you will hear nothing but a whisper.*

[PAUSE]

This ayah is showing us a future. But it is also exposing a present. **The reason the silence of that Day will be terrifying is the same reason silence is terrifying now.** Silence is the natural environment in which a soul stands before its Lord. Noise is what we use to pretend that environment is not real.

Here is the heartbreaking irony. The ayah tells us that **on that Day, even the voice itself bows.** The instrument we use in this world to drown out the call of truth — *that very instrument* will, on the Day of recognition, recognize. The voice will betray its owner by simply going quiet. It will refuse to lie any longer.

What does this mean for us today?

It means: **the noise we are using to fill our lives is borrowed.** It is not ours to keep. The voices that won't stop talking will, one day, stop talking — and what is underneath them, who we *actually* are, will be the only thing that remains. The question is not *whether* we will meet that silent self. The question is *whether we will get to know that self before the silence is forced upon it.*

#### A movement deeper

There is a kind of person — perhaps you know this person, perhaps you are this person — who cannot pray without thinking about what they will say to someone afterwards. Who cannot read Quran without scrolling between ayahs. Who cannot sit in their own home without the TV on. The salah becomes another performance, another stretch of voice-time. *Khushuʿ* in salah, by the way, is the same root — *khashaʿa* — as the voices in this ayah.

So the connection is exact. **The khushuʿ of voices on the Day of Judgment is the khushuʿ Allah is calling you to find in your salah today.** What He guarantees later, He invites now. The believer who finds true silence in salah — whose voice bows in the world before being commanded to bow at the gathering — that believer is rehearsing what the universe will eventually do.

[PAUSE]

#### A movement deeper still

There is a hadith that says paradise is for the one who controls their tongue. Now read that hadith with this ayah in your ear. The tongue is the voice's gate. The voice is what *will* be hushed at the end. The believer who learns to hush the voice now — to refuse gossip, to abandon argument, to choose silence over the cheap satisfaction of being right — that believer is *practicing eschatology*. They are training the voice to do, willingly, what the voice will eventually be made to do.

The ayah does not just describe the Day. **It hands us a way of life.**

#### One concrete shift

After this reflection, choose one slot of silence. Five minutes a day, no input. No phone, no music, no podcast, no conversation. Sit in it. Notice what surfaces. Notice the urge to fill it. Notice — slowly, over weeks — that the silence stops being terrifying and becomes the place where you finally hear the call of *Ar-Rahman* that the world's volume has drowned out for years.

### Theme Two: The Mercy That Survives the Reckoning

Begin with the question every honest soul has asked.

How do I reconcile the God who made me, who fed me, who answered my prayers when I was a child — with the God who will judge me on a Day so awful that mountains will be uprooted and the earth flattened?

If you have not asked this question, you have not yet looked at faith honestly. We all sense — somewhere — a perceived gap between the *Ar-Rahman* who held us when we were small and the Judge who will hold us accountable.

The ayah closes the gap.

*And the voices were hushed **before Ar-Rahman.***

Allah does not name Himself, in this verse, with a name of wrath. He names Himself, on the Day of Judgment, with the name *Ar-Rahman*. **The same name. The same Lord. The same mercy that gave you breath this morning is the mercy that will preside over the moment your soul stands.**

[PAUSE]

This is meant to do something specific to your heart. It is meant to crack open the false image of a divided God — the loving Father in this world and the angry Judge in the next. There is no division. There is one Lord. The mercy that animated creation animates accountability. The mercy that gave is the mercy that asks.

#### A movement deeper

Why is accountability *itself* mercy?

Imagine a universe in which the oppressor never met his victim. In which the cruel parent never had to answer for the child. In which the murderer walked away. In which every wrong went into the dark and was forgotten. Would that universe be merciful?

It would be a horror.

The fact that there *is* a Day — the fact that voices will be silenced because finally truth will speak — is itself an act of *Ar-Rahman*. The mercy is not just *to the believer*. It is to **the wronged**. It is to **the truth itself**. It is the universe's promise that nothing real is ever lost, and nothing false is ever final.

The Day of Judgment is *Ar-Rahman*'s promise to every soul that has ever cried out *why* without an answer. And the answer, on that Day, will be *given.*

#### A movement deeper still

Now look at the next ayah, 109. Allah says intercession will not benefit *except for one whom Ar-Rahman has given permission and whose word He has accepted.*

There it is again. *Ar-Rahman.* In the ayah of *judgment*. In the ayah of *intercession*. The name that frames the moment is the name of mercy.

So the believer's posture before this Day should not be a posture of paralyzed fear. It should be a posture of **awe-soaked hope**. The Lord they are walking toward is the Lord they have been walking *with* the whole time. The same name. The same nearness. The same mercy.

This is why the believer's heart is a strange thing — it is at the same time *afraid* of the Day and *longing* for the One presiding over it. Because the One presiding is the One whose mercy held them this whole life.

[PAUSE]

#### One concrete shift

After this reflection, when the thought of Judgment Day arises in your heart — and it will, more often as you grow — do not let your soul flee into the false comfort of *forgetting it*. Do not push it away. Allow yourself to remember it. And as you remember it, remember **the name**. The name of the One presiding is *Ar-Rahman*. The hand that holds you in life is the hand you are walking toward. Fear becomes longing when you remember the name.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Made Visible

Look now at the whole ayah, with everything we have stood with.

> *On that Day they will follow the Caller — no crookedness in him. And the voices will be hushed before **Ar-Rahman**, so you will hear nothing but a **whisper**.*

The verse moves in three stages:

1. **The pull** — the Caller calls, and souls follow him with no deviation. The first time in human history that following has been perfectly straight.
2. **The hush** — the voices, the loudest feature of the worldly self, lay themselves down. Not before The Compeller. Before *Ar-Rahman*.
3. **The whisper** — what remains is *hams.* A footfall. A breath. The verse itself ends on this word and fades.

Reading the verse is a journey from movement, into bowing, into silence. **The verse enacts what it describes.** It is not a statement about the Day. It is a *miniature performance* of the Day, scaled to fit on the tongue of a reciter.

This is why the Quran is not just information. The Quran is **rehearsal**. Every time you recite this ayah and let it close on the whisper, you are tasting, in miniature, the silence of that Day.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing the Linguistic and the Thematic Together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic insights are the same insight from two directions.

- *Khashaʿati al-aṣwātu* (the voices bowed) and *the noise we live in is borrowed* — both say: the loud self is not the real self. The real self is silent before its Lord.
- *Lir-Raḥmān* (before *Ar-Rahman*) and *the mercy that survives the reckoning* — both say: the Day of Judgment is not the end of mercy. It is the place where mercy completes itself.
- *Hamsan* (a whisper) and *the believer's rehearsal in salah* — both say: silence is not the absence of meaning. Silence is the *medium* in which the soul finally meets its Lord without distortion.

### Questions to Carry

1. What in your life is loud because the silence underneath it would expose something you are not ready to face?
2. When was the last time you sat in silence — no input, no voice — for long enough that something underneath surfaced?
3. If your voice were silenced tomorrow, what is the last thing it spoke? Was it worth its breath?
4. Have you been carrying an image of God in which mercy and judgment are *opposed*? What would it change if the same name presides over both?
5. The ayah ends on a whisper. When your life ends, what whisper will be left?

### One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day the universe goes silent, the One presiding is *still* the Most Merciful — and the voice that recognized Him in the noise of this world is the voice that will be at peace in the hush of that one.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not wait to be silenced. **Choose silence now.** Choose the silence of khushuʿ in salah. Choose the silence of restraining a tongue that wants to argue. Choose the silence of a phone put down, a podcast paused, a mind allowed to be alone with its Lord. Every choice of silence in this life is a rehearsal of the silence of that Day — but with a difference. *That* silence will be imposed. *This* silence is offered. *That* silence will arrive whether you want it or not. *This* silence is your gift to your future self — the soul who will, in the hush before *Ar-Rahman*, be glad it had practiced.

### Du'a

O *Ar-Rahman* — the same name we say at the beginning of every recitation, the same name in whose presence the voices will one day bow — teach our voices to bow now. Quiet in us the noise we have used to drown out Your call. And on the Day the voices are hushed and only a whisper remains, let our whisper be the whisper of a soul that already knew Your name. *Āmīn.*
