# Do Not Stretch Your Eyes

## *Surah Ta-Ha, Ayah 131*

> وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ إِلَىٰ مَا مَتَّعْنَا بِهِۦٓ أَزْوَٰجًۭا مِّنْهُمْ زَهْرَةَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ ۚ وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌۭ وَأَبْقَىٰ

> *And do not stretch your eyes toward what We have given certain pairs among them to enjoy — the bloom of the life of this world, that We may test them through it. And the provision of your Lord is better and more enduring.*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

To feel this ayah, you have to feel where the Prophet ﷺ was standing when it came to him.

He was in Mecca. The revelation had been coming for years. The mockery had grown sharper. His followers were a small band — many of them slaves, many of them poor, several of them tortured, some buried under stones, some forced to flee their homes. He himself was passed over in the streets by men who used to call him *al-Amīn* — the trustworthy one — before he opened his mouth about *Lā ilāha illā Allāh*.

And meanwhile, the chiefs of Quraysh — the ones laughing at him — they had everything. Caravans. Sons. Networks. Houses cooled in summer and warmed in winter. Banquets. Standing. The very wealth that came from the Sacred House his ancestor built was flowing into the pockets of the men rejecting his message.

The whole surah we have been sitting in — Surah Ta-Ha — has been a long meditation on this exact ache. Adam slipped because something *looked* better than what he had. The Children of Israel, fresh out of slavery, watching the sea split for them — they slipped because a calf gleamed in front of them while Musa was on the mountain. The Samiri took dust from the footstep of an angel and made it shine. Pharaoh stood in his courts dripping with gold and called himself a god.

The whole surah has been asking: **what pulls a human being off the path?**

And here, at the end of the surah, Allah turns directly to His Prophet ﷺ and tells him the deepest answer.

It is not enemies.
It is not torture.
It is not even the lies of false gods.

It is **the eye that drifts sideways**, toward what someone else got. [PAUSE]

### The hook

Allah does not say: *do not envy.* He does not say: *do not desire.* He uses a verb so physical, so kinetic, that when you understand it you will never read this ayah the same way again.

He commands His Prophet ﷺ — the most beloved human being to walk this earth — **not to stretch his eyes**.

Why is the command physical? Why is it phrased like the eye is a rope being pulled? And why, when Allah finally offers the consolation, does He withhold the very thing you would expect Him to specify?

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1 — *Lā tamuddanna* — "Do not stretch / do not extend"

Before we look at this word, let's notice something about how human beings work.

When a baby sees a bright object, the baby reaches. Not just with hands. The baby's whole body extends toward it. The neck cranes. The eyes widen and lock. We say in English that a child's eyes "go big." We say they "can't take their eyes off it." That is not metaphor — it is anatomy. The eye, when it desires, *reaches*.

Now in Arabic there is a root **م-د-د** (*m-d-d*) which means **to extend, to stretch, to prolong, to draw out**. From this root come words like *madd* — the lengthening of a vowel in recitation, the stretching of a sound. *Imdād* — to give a steady supply, to extend resources. *Mudd al-baṣar* — as far as the sight reaches. The image is always the same: something that has a normal length is being **drawn out beyond it**.

Now look at what Allah says: **وَلَا تَمُدَّنَّ عَيْنَيْكَ** — "And do not *stretch* your two eyes."

He could have said *lā tanẓur* — do not look. He could have said *lā targhab* — do not desire. He did not. He chose a verb that treats the eye like something with a natural resting length, a natural focus, that can be **pulled**, **dragged**, **extended** beyond where it would naturally rest.

Notice something: the verb has the heavy emphatic *nūn* on the end — **tamuddanna**, not *tamudd*. In Arabic this is what grammarians call **nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah** — the heavy emphatic suffix. It is the difference between saying "don't do that" and "don't *you* dare do that." It locks the prohibition down. This is not a polite suggestion. This is Allah taking the verb and pressing it firmly onto the chest of the listener. [PAUSE]

And one more thing. The eye is in the dual form — **ʿaynayka** — "your *two* eyes." Not "your gaze." Not "your sight" as an abstract faculty. **Your two eyes**, the physical organs in your face. The two specific instruments through which the world reaches in and grabs you.

So put it together. Allah is saying to His Prophet ﷺ:

*Do not let your two eyes be stretched — pulled like cords — toward what We have given them.*

Here is what this single word reveals:

**Envy and longing are not abstract feelings that begin in the heart and travel down to the eyes. They begin in the eyes and travel up to the heart.** The pull starts at the surface. The eye lingers a half-second too long on someone's car, someone's house, someone's wedding, someone's holiday photos. And in that half-second, the cord goes taut. The heart begins to be dragged.

Allah is not telling His Prophet ﷺ to manage his feelings. He is telling him to **not let the cord get pulled in the first place**. Cut it at the eye. Before the heart even has a chance to ache. [PAUSE]

This is why scholars and people of *taqwā* across the centuries have spoken of *ghaḍḍ al-baṣar* — the lowering of the gaze — not just as a sexual ethic but as a spiritual one. The eye is the door. Whatever you let in through it, your heart will eventually have to deal with.

### Element 2 — *Zahrat al-ḥayāt al-dunyā* — "The bloom of the life of this world"

Now Allah names what is on the other end of that cord.

He uses one word — **زَهْرَة** (*zahrah*) — and with that one word He does something that I want you to feel before we analyze it.

The root **ز-ه-ر** (*z-h-r*) carries the meaning of **brightness, blossoming, flowering, shining**. From it comes *al-Zahrāʾ* — the radiant one (the title given to Sayyidatuna Fāṭimah). From it comes the word for the planet Venus when it shines at dusk. And from it comes *zahrah* — **the blossom of a plant, the flower at peak bloom**.

Allah does not say *māl al-dunyā* — the wealth of dunya. He does not say *zīnat al-dunyā* — the adornment of dunya, the way other ayahs do. He says **the *flower* of the life of this world**.

Think about a flower for a moment. Really look at one in your mind.

A flower is the most beautiful thing a plant ever produces. Its color is at peak. Its fragrance is at peak. Bees come to it. Humans pluck it. We give them on wedding days. We lay them on graves. We arrange them in vases on tables.

Now here is the truth nobody wants to say out loud: **a flower is the dying part of a plant**.

The leaves can last a season. The roots can last decades. The trunk of a tree can outlive your great-grandchildren. But the *flower* — the most beautiful thing — opens, blazes, and is dead in days.

The very thing that makes the flower captivating — its bloom — is the proof that its time is short. You cannot have an eternal flower. The moment a plant blooms, the countdown has started. [PAUSE]

This is what Allah is calling the wealth, the status, the houses, the standing of those wealthy chiefs of Quraysh. **A bloom.** A magnificent, fragrant, attention-grabbing bloom — that is, by its very nature, **already in the process of dying.**

Stay with this. This is not a Sufi exaggeration. This is a botanical fact recruited as theology.

When you see someone's wedding photos and your eye stretches toward them — what you are looking at is a *zahrah*. When you see someone's car, their kitchen renovation, their corner office, their child accepted to a famous university — *zahrah*. When you see the influencer in front of their backdrop with the soft lighting and the perfect arrangement — that is *exactly* what Allah is naming. **The bloom.** Bright. Fragrant. Withering in real time as you scroll.

And here is the second layer. Notice the grammar. *Zahrata al-ḥayāt al-dunyā* is in the accusative case. Most classical grammarians read it as a *ḥāl* — a state or condition. So the meaning is: *We gave them this enjoyment **as a bloom of dunya** — that is its nature, that is its character, that is what it actually is, regardless of how it appears to your eye.*

Allah is not just describing the wealth. He is telling you what category to file it under. When your eye stretches toward something that someone else has and your heart begins to ache, the first thing you must do is name the thing correctly:

**This is a bloom. It is dying. Even now, as I look at it, it is dying.** [PAUSE]

And then Allah twists the knife — gently, mercifully — with three words: **لِنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ** — *that We may test them through it*.

The wealthy disbelievers are not winning. They are being tested. The thing your eye is stretching toward is not their reward — **it is their exam.** The very *māl* you are envying is the heat being applied to see if they will buckle under it.

There is a phrase in Arabic that captures this: when fire is applied to gold, it is called *fitnah*. The same word. Because that is what testing is — heat applied to see what is real and what is false. Allah is using the same word here. *We give them the bloom — to apply heat to them.*

Your envy, then, is misplaced even on its own terms. You are envying someone who is **standing inside an oven you cannot see**.

### Element 3 — *Wa rizqu Rabbika khayrun wa abqā* — and the asymmetric absence

Now we come to the consolation. And this is where Allah does something so subtle that if you read quickly, you will miss it entirely.

You would expect Allah, after taking away the appeal of *their* enjoyment, to describe what *you* have instead. You would expect a list. *I gave you patience, I gave you knowledge, I gave you Companions, I gave you the Quran.* Or at least: *what I will give you in the next world is better.*

He does not do that.

He says: **وَرِزْقُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ** — *and the provision of **your Lord** is better and more enduring.*

Notice carefully what is here and what is not.

**What is named:** *your Lord*. Not *Allah*. Not *the Most High*. Not *the One who created the heavens*. Specifically **رَبِّكَ** — *your* Lord. The pronoun *ka* is singular, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ first and through him to every single believer who reads this. Your. Particular. Lord.

**What is not named:** *what* the rizq actually is.

Allah says **the rizq of your Lord** — and then He does not specify the contents. There is no list. There is no description. There is only a comparison — **better and more enduring** — without ever pinning down what "it" is.

This is what we have been calling **asymmetric absence**, and here it is doing the heaviest work of the entire ayah. [PAUSE]

Here is why I think Allah did this.

If He had named the contents — *your rizq is Paradise* or *your rizq is the Quran* or *your rizq is the closeness you have to Me* — He would have framed the consolation the same way the test was framed. *They got X; you got Y.* And then, somewhere in your heart, you would still be doing the math. *Is Y really worth more than X?*

But by leaving the *what* unspecified, Allah forces the comparison to leap up one whole level. It is no longer **what they got** vs. **what you got**. It is **what they got** vs. **the One who is your Lord**.

Read it again with this in mind: *The bloom of dunya — vs. the **rizq of your Lord**.* The contrast is not between two objects. The contrast is between **a flower** and **the One who grows flowers**. Between a thing and the Source of all things.

The bloom is dying. The Source is not.
The bloom is theirs for a moment. The Source is *yours*.
The bloom does not know the one to whom it was given. The Source knows your name. [PAUSE]

And then come the two adjectives: **خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ** — *better and more enduring*.

*Khayrun* — better in quality. *Abqā* — from the root *b-q-y*, to remain, to last, to endure. The very root from which we get *al-Bāqī* — one of Allah's names, **the Eternally Remaining**.

So Allah is saying: what your Lord gives is *better* than the bloom (because a bloom dies and *His* gift does not), AND it is *more enduring* (because *He* is *al-Bāqī*, and what comes from *al-Bāqī* carries His character).

The bloom withers because it comes from a withering source — dunya itself. What Allah gives carries His attribute: *bāqin*. **It lasts because He lasts.** [PAUSE]

### Bridge

So we have three movements. *Do not let your two eyes be stretched.* What is on the other end of that cord is a *flower* — beautiful, fragrant, dying. And what you have instead is not even a *thing*; it is a relationship — *your Lord*, whose provision lasts because *He* lasts.

But this isn't just about words. This is about the deepest psychological wound a human being carries — and how Allah heals it. Let us ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1 — The wound of the sideways glance

You know this wound. Everyone alive in 2026 knows this wound, even if they cannot name it.

You are sitting at your desk. The work is fine. The day is fine. Your apartment is fine. Your spouse is fine. Your children — *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — are fine. And then you open your phone for a moment, and your thumb scrolls, and your eye lingers on a post — half a second too long. Someone you used to know in school just bought a house. Someone else is in Bali. Someone else's children are accepted somewhere your children are not. Someone else's career is in a place yours is not.

And in that half-second, something inside you gets quietly poisoned.

It is not that the work is suddenly bad. It is not that your apartment shrunk. It is not that your children became less. **Nothing in your life has actually changed.** What changed is that your eye stretched sideways. The cord went taut. And what was *fine* a moment ago now feels *not enough*.

This wound has a name in our tradition. The classical scholars called it *ḥasad* when it is at its sharpest — the desire that what someone has would be taken from them. But there is a softer, more pervasive version that doesn't even rise to *ḥasad* — the simple sideways drift that just makes you feel poor, late, behind, unblessed. The drift Allah is naming when He says *lā tamuddanna ʿaynayka*.

Here is what is so devastating about this wound, and why Allah went to the eye to address it:

**It does not require any actual lack to function.**

You can have everything you ever asked for. You can be in *sajdah* on a Persian rug in a house you own with children sleeping safely upstairs and a freezer full of food. The moment your eye stretches toward what someone else has, **all of it goes dark**. Not because anything is missing — because comparison itself has the power to extinguish gratitude.

This is why Allah does not say *fix your nafs* or *fix your heart*. He goes to the eye. **Cut the cord at the door.** [PAUSE]

There is a profoundly merciful psychology in this command, and we should sit with it.

Allah is not asking you to feel a certain way. He is not asking you to *not be jealous*. Feelings are wild horses. They cannot be commanded directly. What He is asking you to do is **not to keep looking**. The look is what feeds the feeling.

Try it. Tonight, when your thumb drifts toward the app that tells you what everyone else has, ask yourself: *am I about to stretch my eyes?* And if the answer is yes — close the app. Not because the people in it are bad. Not because you cannot enjoy beauty. But because **you are about to do the one specific thing your Lord told you not to do.**

The Prophet ﷺ — *the most beloved* — was given this command. Not because he would have envied. But because Allah, *al-Raḥīm*, knew that even brushing past such an image too long is dangerous for a heart. If the Prophet ﷺ needed this protection, what about us, scrolling through curated bloom-feeds at midnight?

What changes about how you live tomorrow if this is true: that your eye has a leash, and it is being pulled, and you are the one who decides whether to let it stretch?

### Theme 2 — The provision that is *yours*

Now let us sit with the second healing.

When we feel the sideways drift, we instinctively reach for one of two consolations. The first is comparison-as-counter: *yes, but they don't have inner peace*, or *yes, but they will lose it*, or *yes, but in Paradise...* This works sometimes, but notice — it is still the same machinery. It is still measuring **their thing** against **your thing**.

Allah refuses to let us heal that way.

He does not say *what they have is bad and what you have is good*. He does not give us **a thing** to clutch as compensation. He gives us **a relationship**. *Wa rizqu **Rabbika** khayrun wa abqā.* The provision **of your Lord**.

Here is the analogy I want you to sit with.

A child in a large family — let's say there are seven children — comes to her mother crying. *Why does she get the bigger slice? Why does he get to stay up later? Why did they get new shoes this year and I didn't?*

A poor mother — a mother in panic — would scramble to even the score. She would cut a new slice. She would stay up arguing about why this child got and that child didn't.

A wise mother does something else.

She picks the child up. She looks her in the eye. And she says: *come here. You are my daughter. Your slice is in my hands. Have I ever forgotten you?*

Notice — the mother did not name what is in her hands. She did not say *and tomorrow I will give you a bigger slice than her*. She did not enter the comparison. She said: **I am your mother. What I give you comes from me. And I have not forgotten.**

That is what Allah is doing in this ayah.

He does not say *and you will get more bloom in Paradise*. He does not even say *and you have the Quran which is better than their gold*. He says: **the provision of your Lord** is better and more enduring.

The healing is not a better object. **The healing is the relationship.** [PAUSE]

This is why the pronoun *ka* — "your" — is so important. He is *your* Lord. The same Lord who, before you were born, decreed your *rizq* down to the last morsel. The same Lord who, every breath, sends air into your lungs. The same Lord who, when you are forgotten by everyone, knows your name and whispers it through the angels who write your deeds. **Your** Lord.

When you understand that, the sideways glance loses its power. Not because what they have became uglier. Not because you trained yourself into apathy. But because the moment you say "*my Lord*," the entire frame of comparison collapses. There is nothing to compare. **They have a flower. I have a Lord.** [PAUSE]

There is a practical implication here, and I want to name it directly.

Whenever you feel the cord pull — whenever your eye stretches sideways — try this. Do not list what you have. Do not count your blessings (that comes later). First, before anything else, **say the words back to yourself**: *and the provision of **my Lord** is better and more enduring*. Replace the *ka* with *ī*. *Wa rizqu Rabbī khayrun wa abqā.*

You are not telling yourself you are rich. You are not telling yourself their wealth is fake. You are reminding yourself **whose** you are. And from whose hand your portion comes. And that the hand has not, will not, and cannot forget.

Then — and only then — the gratitude comes back online. Not as an effort. As a consequence.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at the architecture of this single ayah.

It begins with **the eye** — the most physical, most surface organ — and the command to not let it stretch. It travels through **a flower** — the most fragile, most temporary image of beauty. It ends at **your Lord** — the most stable, most enduring, most *yours* of all realities.

The ayah moves you from the surface of your face → through the outer beauty of the world → to the deepest center of who you are: someone who has a Lord.

And along the way, every linguistic feature reinforces the journey. The eye is **dual** — *two* eyes, the doors of the wound. The verb is **emphatic** — locked down, no negotiation. The bloom is named with the *flower* of all words for beauty. The test is named with *fitnah* — heat applied to gold. The provision is left **unnamed** — because the point isn't *what* He gives but *that He is the One who gives*.

The whole ayah is one command and one consolation, perfectly calibrated to the deepest human wound. *Don't stretch toward their flower. Your Lord's provision is better and more enduring.* [PAUSE]

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. Don't answer them quickly.

1. When did you last let your eye stretch sideways? What were you looking at? What was the cord pulling toward? Was it really worth what it cost your heart?

2. If someone you envy is standing inside an oven you cannot see — what does it change about your envy?

3. If you replaced "*your Lord*" with "*my Lord*" and said this ayah back to yourself the next time the cord pulled — what would actually happen in your chest?

4. What in your life right now is a *zahrah* — a bloom that you are mistaking for a tree? What in your life right now is *bāqin* — actually enduring, actually from the Eternally Remaining?

5. If the Prophet ﷺ — the most beloved — needed this command, what does that say about the spiritual seriousness of your scrolling?

### One-sentence distillation

**Do not let your eyes be dragged toward the dying flowers in someone else's hand, because the One whose hand holds yours does not die.**

### The closing invitation

This is not a command to look away from beauty. It is not an instruction to become joyless. Allah, *al-Jamīl*, loves beauty.

The invitation is this: **train your eye to know the difference between a flower and a Lord.** Between a bloom and a Source. Between what is dying as you look at it and what is *bāqin* — eternally remaining.

When you scroll, when you walk through the mall, when you sit at a wedding, when you visit a house bigger than yours — you do not have to lower your eyes from beauty. You have to refuse to let them be **stretched** by it. Look. Smile. Say *bāraka Allāhu lahum*. And do not let the cord go taut.

Because the rizq of *your* Lord is better. And more enduring. And *yours*.

### Du'a

> *O Allah — You who are al-Bāqī, the Eternally Remaining — protect our two eyes from being stretched toward the blooms You have placed in others' hands as their test. Let us see beauty without being dragged by it. And when our hearts ache with comparison, return us, swiftly, to the words: **wa rizqu Rabbī khayrun wa abqā** — and the provision of my Lord is better and more enduring. Āmīn.*
