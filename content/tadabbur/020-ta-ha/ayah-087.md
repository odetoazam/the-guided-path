# Ḥummilnā: The Grammar of Moral Evasion

> [ayah:20:87] قَالُوا۟ مَآ أَخْلَفْنَا مَوْعِدَكَ بِمَلْكِنَا وَلَـٰكِنَّا حُمِّلْنَآ أَوْزَارًا مِّن زِينَةِ ٱلْقَوْمِ فَقَذَفْنَـٰهَا فَكَذَٰلِكَ أَلْقَى ٱلسَّامِرِىُّ
>
> *Qālū mā akhlafnā mawʿidaka bi-malkinā wa-lākinnā ḥummilnā awzāran min zīnati'l-qawmi fa-qadhafnāhā fa-kadhālika alqā as-sāmiriyy.*
>
> "They said: We did not break our promise to you of our own accord, but we were made to carry burdens from the ornaments of the people, so we cast them — and thus did the Samiri cast."

## Introduction

### The Moment Before the Words

To hear this ayah, you have to hear what came right before it.

Moses has come down from the mountain. Forty days. Forty days of speaking with his Lord. Forty days of Tablets, of light, of the closest proximity any prophet has ever had to direct divine speech. He carries the Tablets in his arms.

He descends.

And what does he find?

He finds his people — the same people he carried out of Egypt, the same people he split the sea for, the same people who saw Pharaoh drowned, the same people fed manna and quail in the desert — he finds them dancing around a statue. A golden calf. A statue made of melted-down jewelry. A statue that lows. And they are saying: *this is your god, and the god of Moses.*

He looks at his brother Aaron. He grabs his beard. He turns to the people. And in the previous ayah — ayah 86 — he says, almost shaking with grief:

*"Did your Lord not promise you a beautiful promise? Has the time become long for you? Or did you want wrath from your Lord to descend on you, so that you broke your appointment with me?"*

The question hangs in the desert air.

A community has just done the unspeakable. Their prophet stands before them. He does not strike them. He does not curse them. He asks them a question. He gives them the chance to speak first.

And ayah 87 is **what they say**.

These are the first recorded words from the children of Israel after the calf. The first sentence out of the mouth of a community caught in the most flagrant sin imaginable. And the Quran preserves this sentence with surgical care — not to shame them, but because **the structure of this sentence is the structure of every excuse human beings have ever made.**

### The Hook

Watch what they do.

In twenty-one Arabic words, they will perform five distinct moves of self-exculpation. They will negate their own intent. They will switch from active to passive voice. They will reframe their desire as a burden. They will minimize their act with one verb and shift the blame with another. And they will end by making themselves equivalent to the very person who engineered the entire catastrophe.

Five moves. One sentence. The complete grammar of evasion.

[PAUSE]

We do not need a degree in linguistics to feel what they are doing. Because **we have spoken this sentence ourselves**. In different languages. In different centuries. About different sins. The architecture is the same.

So let us slow down. Let us read this sentence like a confession the speaker did not realize they were giving.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### A Word About How Languages Hide People

Before we open this ayah, a small lesson — because the linguistic move at the heart of these words depends on something that exists in every language and is used the same way everywhere.

It is called the **passive voice**.

In English, the active voice says: *I broke the vase.* The subject is named. The agent is visible. The act and the actor are stitched together.

The passive voice says: *The vase got broken.* Now the actor has vanished. The act floats in the air. Something happened, but no one is responsible for it. The vase, somehow, broke itself.

This is not a moral failing of the passive voice. The passive voice has its uses — when the actor is genuinely unknown, when emphasis belongs on the object, when the agent is irrelevant. But because it can hide the agent, **it has been the favorite voice of every guilty speaker in human history.**

When the politician says *"mistakes were made,"* not *"I made a mistake,"* he is using the passive voice.

When the addict says *"things happened,"* not *"I did this,"* he is using the passive voice.

When the cheater says *"the affair occurred,"* not *"I had an affair,"* he is using the passive voice.

The passive voice is the cloak that the *nafs* slips on when it does not want to be seen.

Arabic has a passive voice too. And Arabic has it in a particular form — Form II, the *faʿʿala* form, which intensifies the verb. So when the verb *ḥamala* (he carried) becomes Form II passive *ḥummila*, it does not just mean "he was carried." It means **"he was made to carry — heavily, intensively, by some unspecified agent who pressed this burden upon him."**

Hold that grammar in your hand. We will need it in a moment.

---

### Element One — *bi-malkinā*: "Of Our Own Power"

Listen to how the sentence begins:

> *qālū mā akhlafnā mawʿidaka bi-malkinā*
>
> *"They said: We did not break your appointment by our own power."*

The first word out of their mouths is a denial. *Mā akhlafnā* — "we did not break." The negation is loud. Before they admit anything, they refuse something.

But what do they refuse?

Read carefully. **They do not deny breaking the promise.** They cannot — Moses has already seen the calf. They deny something more specific. They say: *we did not break it* **bi-malkinā** *— "by our power, by our authority, by our own ownership."*

The root is *m-l-k* — the root of *mulk*, kingship; *milk*, possession; *malik*, king. Classical lexicographers note that *bi-malkinā* in this context means *"by our own command, by our own initiative, by our own will."* They are saying: **whatever happened, it did not happen because we owned this decision.**

This is the **first move** of evasion. Notice they do not say "we did not break the promise." They concede that the promise was broken. What they deny is **authorship**. They deny ownership of the act.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how subtle this is. They are admitting the deed and disclaiming the doing in the same breath. They are pleading guilty to the body of the crime and pleading innocent to the will behind it.

We do this every day.

*"I did not eat the cake on purpose."*
*"I did not snap at her on purpose."*
*"I did not miss the prayer because I wanted to."*

Notice what we are doing. We are not denying the cake or the snap or the missed prayer. We are denying that we **owned** them. We are denying *bi-malkinā*. We are saying: this happened, but I was not the king of this happening.

And here is what should make us shudder. The Quran preserves this exact move from the people of Moses — not because it was a unique sin, but because **it is the move every human being makes at the threshold of accountability.** The moment we are asked to speak about what we have done, the first sentence out of our mouths reaches for *bi-malkinā*. Not by my power. Not by my will. Not by my ownership.

What this single phrase reveals: **before there is denial of the act, there is denial of the self that did the act.** The first thing the *nafs* refuses to surrender is its claim that it was not really there when this was done.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two — *ḥummilnā awzāran*: "We Were Made to Carry Burdens"

Now watch the voice of the sentence shift. They have just said *"we did not break."* That is active. *We did not.* But look at what comes next:

> *wa-lākinnā ḥummilnā awzāran min zīnati'l-qawm*
>
> *"But rather we were made to carry burdens of the ornaments of the people."*

**Ḥummilnā.** Form II. Passive.

We were made to carry. Loaded. Saddled. Pressed under a weight. Some unnamed agent — a someone, a something, a force outside us — placed this burden on our backs and we, helpless beasts, had no choice but to bear it.

This is the **second move**. The voice has flipped. A moment ago they were the subject of a verb they were denying. Now they are the object of a verb they are claiming. *We did not break — we were made to carry.* Active denial, passive admission. They are erasing themselves from the agent slot of the sentence.

But who made them carry? **Allah does not let them name the agent.** And the speaker certainly does not name the agent. The passive hangs there with no one in it. As if the burden materialized on their backs by some operation of weather. As if the gold leapt onto them and demanded to be melted.

[PAUSE]

Now listen to what they call the gold.

*Awzāran.* The plural of *wizr*. And here we must slow down, because this is one of the most important moves in the ayah.

*Wizr* in classical Arabic means a heavy load. A burden. Something that weighs you down. But in Quranic usage, *wizr* almost always means **a burden of sin**. Allah will use this exact root in the next ayah's neighborhood — *man aʿraḍa ʿanhu fa-innahu yaḥmilu yawma'l-qiyāmati wizrā* — *"whoever turns away from this, on the Day of Resurrection he will carry a burden"* (20:100). Same root. Same shape.

So when these people say *"we were made to carry burdens of the ornaments of the people,"* listen to what their language is doing. **They are calling the gold itself a burden.** They are calling the jewelry — the same jewelry they wore freely out of Egypt, the same jewelry they decorated themselves with — they are calling it *awzār*, sin-weights, burdens that fell on them against their will.

This is the **third move**, and it is the most psychologically devastating of the five.

**They are reframing the object of their desire as the source of their oppression.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. They wanted the gold. They kept the gold. They carried the gold out of Egypt and through the desert. The gold was beautiful to them. *Zīna* — adornment, ornamentation, the very word in this ayah — is what one *chooses* to put on. You do not get *adorned* by accident. *Zīna* is a verb of agency.

But now, suddenly, in this moment of being caught, the *zīna* becomes *awzār*. The adornment they chose becomes the burden they were saddled with. The gold that hung around their necks is now the weight that bent their backs.

This is what we do.

When we are caught with what we wanted, we tell the story of how it oppressed us. The drinker who chose the bottle calls it "the disease that took me." The browser who clicked through twenty pages calls it "the content I was exposed to." The gossip who passed the rumor along calls it "the story I had to share." The very thing we reached for, in the moment of accountability, becomes the thing that reached for us.

This is the *nafs* performing its most ancient trick: **misnaming desire as compulsion.** Calling the thing you chose a thing that happened to you. Calling adornment a burden.

What this single phrase reveals: when we are caught, our language does not just hide our agency — it inverts the moral direction of our desire. The thing we chased becomes the thing we were chased by. The ornament becomes the load.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — *qadhafnāhā... alqā*: Two Verbs for the Same Throw

Now we come to the fifth move — and this one is so subtle that you can read past it a hundred times without noticing.

> *fa-qadhafnāhā fa-kadhālika alqā as-sāmiriyy*
>
> *"So we cast them — and thus did the Samiri cast."*

Read it once. It sounds like a simple statement. *We threw the gold in. Samiri threw too. We all just threw.*

But Allah has used **two different verbs** for the same act of throwing. And the people speaking do not seem to notice. Their grammar betrays the equivalence they are trying to claim.

**Verb one:** *qadhafnāhā* — from *q-dh-f*. To hurl. To fling. To toss something carelessly. A *qadhīfa* in Arabic is a missile. The verb carries a sense of **rough, unpremeditated discarding**. You *qadhafa* something when you want to be rid of it.

**Verb two:** *alqā* — from *l-q-y*, Form IV. To cast. To set forth. To deliberately deposit. This verb is used elsewhere in the Quran for **purposeful casting** — Moses casting his staff that becomes a serpent (20:20), Allah casting tranquility into hearts (48:4, 9:26). It carries weight, intention, deliberateness.

So the people say: *we hurled the gold (qadhafnā) — and likewise (kadhālika) Samiri cast (alqā).*

They are using the word *kadhālika* — "and thus, and likewise" — to draw an equivalence between their throwing and Samiri's. They want to say: **we are no different than him.** *He threw. We threw. We are all on the same level.*

But Allah, narrating this for us, **uses different verbs.** Their verb is the verb of careless discarding. Samiri's verb is the verb of deliberate casting. The text itself refuses the equivalence they are constructing in real time.

[PAUSE]

Notice what is happening here. This is the **fourth and fifth move** of evasion combined.

**Move four:** Minimize the act. *Qadhafnāhā* — "we just hurled the stuff." Not "we contributed our gold to the construction of an idol." Not "we surrendered our wealth to a project we should have known was forbidden." Just — *we threw it.* It was almost incidental.

**Move five:** Equate yourself with the actual culprit. *Fa-kadhālika alqā as-sāmiriyy.* "And thus did Samiri." As if Samiri's deliberate engineering of the calf — the casting of the dust from the angel's footprint, the construction of the sound, the entire theological catastrophe he authored — is the same as their casual throwing of jewelry.

It is a kind of sociological camouflage. *We are not worse than the average. We did what everyone did. Look — even Samiri just threw.* By dragging Samiri down to "he just threw too," they elevate themselves to "we are no worse than the worst."

[PAUSE]

But Allah has set the trap with His vocabulary. *Qadhafa* and *alqā* are not the same word. The text shows you in the very grammar what their hearts will not admit: **Samiri did something deliberate. They did something they want to call accidental. The two are not the same. The equivalence is a lie.**

What this single contrast reveals: when we are caught, we will instinctively flatten the moral landscape. We will compare ourselves to the worst person in the room and claim parity. *We all sin. Everyone does it. He's no better than me.* The flattening is the evasion. The Quran refuses to flatten — even at the level of word choice.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge to the Themes

So in twenty-one words, we have watched a community perform five distinct moves:

One — denying the **ownership** of the act (*bi-malkinā*).
Two — switching to **passive voice** so no agent can be named (*ḥummilnā*).
Three — **reframing** the object of desire as the source of oppression (*awzāran min zīnati'l-qawm*).
Four — **minimizing** the act with the verb of careless discarding (*qadhafnāhā*).
Five — claiming **equivalence** with the actual architect of the sin (*fa-kadhālika alqā as-sāmiriyy*).

These are not five separate excuses. These are the five operations of a single machine — a machine the *nafs* assembles instantaneously when caught. The Quran has slowed the machine down for us, frame by frame, so that we can see how it works.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the kind of creature we are.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The Architecture of Self-Deception

There is something you have done that you will not say out loud.

Not the thing itself — the *language* you use about it inside your own head.

Maybe it is a relationship you ended badly. Maybe it is a friendship you let die. Maybe it is money you spent on something you should not have. Maybe it is a habit you cannot break. Maybe it is a prayer you keep missing.

Notice the sentence you tell yourself.

Listen for how it starts. *I didn't really mean to.* *Things just kind of fell apart.* *I was under so much pressure.* *Everyone does it.* *It just happened.*

Listen for the verbs. Are they active? Or have they slipped into passive? *I broke this* — or *this got broken*? *I chose this* — or *this happened to me*?

Listen for the objects. Is the thing you wanted called the thing you wanted? Or has it been renamed — into a temptation that *came at you*, a circumstance that *pressed you*, a burden you were *made to carry*?

[PAUSE]

This is what the people of Moses are doing. And the reason the Quran preserves their words with such care is because **this is what we are doing.** The grammar of self-deception is universal. The vocabulary changes. The structure does not.

Modern psychology has names for the moves the Quran is showing us.

Psychologists call it *neutralization*. The sociologists Sykes and Matza, in 1957, identified five techniques of neutralization that delinquents use to commit crimes without seeing themselves as criminals: *denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties.* Different lists, but the same insight: humans construct elaborate verbal architectures to commit the act and keep the self-image clean.

Therapists call it *cognitive distortion*. *Externalizing*. *Splitting*. *Minimization*. Whole vocabularies have been built to describe what one ayah of the Quran shows us happening live in twenty-one words.

[PAUSE]

But here is what the Quran sees that modern psychology often misses.

Modern frameworks treat self-deception as a *cognitive bug* — a quirk of the brain, an evolved heuristic, a defense mechanism. Something that happens because of how minds work.

The Quran treats self-deception as a **moral act**. A *choice*. Not just *what happens to thinking* but *what we are doing to language*. When we move from active to passive, when we rename desire as burden, when we flatten ourselves into equivalence with the worst — these are not mental glitches. **These are decisions.** Each grammatical move is a decision to remain unconfronted. Each sentence is built brick by brick by a self that does not want to be seen.

This is why the cure cannot be cognitive. You cannot reason your way out of a fortress your nafs is rebuilding faster than you can dismantle it. The cure has to begin somewhere else.

[PAUSE]

### What does this change?

Tonight, before you sleep, listen to your own sentence.

There is something you said today — about why you did what you did, or did not do what you should have. Listen back to the sentence. Not the act. The sentence about the act.

Find the *bi-malkinā*. Find the place where you denied that you were the king of your own choice.

Find the *ḥummilnā*. Find the verb that slipped into passive — the place where the actor disappeared.

Find the *awzāran*. Find the desire you renamed as a burden.

Find the *qadhafnāhā*. Find the act you minimized into a careless throw.

Find the *kadhālika*. Find the comparison — *everyone does it, no one is innocent, I'm no worse than* — that flattened your specific responsibility into a general human condition.

You will find all five. Not because you are a worse human than the people of Moses. Because you are made of the same clay, and the clay always builds the same fortress.

The act of finding them is the beginning of the cure. The cure is not making yourself feel terrible. The cure is **letting your sentence be small.** *I did this. I wanted this. I owned this. I am sorry.* The shorter the sentence, the closer the heart to repair.

### Theme Two — The Two Voices in the Surah

Now lift your eyes to the architecture of the entire surah.

Surah Ta-Ha is a long surah. It begins with two letters. It ends with the description of the Day. In between, two great stories.

The first story — Moses, from his calling on Sinai (vv. 9-36) through his confrontation with Pharaoh through the exodus through the calf, through this very ayah, through Moses confronting Aaron and Samiri, through the eschatological warnings.

The second story — Adam (vv. 115-127). His covenant with Allah. His forgetting. The whisper of Iblis. The eating of the tree. The descent.

These are placed in the same surah on purpose. Both are stories of a covenant given and a covenant broken. Both involve a tempter (Samiri / Iblis). Both involve forbidden objects (the calf / the tree). Both involve those who *forgot* what they had been told.

Now listen — listen carefully — to **how each broken covenant is answered.**

The people of the calf, when caught, say what we have just spent an hour examining. The whole verbal machinery of evasion. *We did not break it of our own power. We were made to carry. We just threw. Samiri threw too.*

Adam, when caught — and yes, Adam in the broader Quranic narrative (7:23) — says something stunningly different:

> *"Rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā wa-in lam taghfir lanā wa-tarḥamnā la-nakūnanna mina'l-khāsirīn."*
>
> *"Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves. And if You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will be among the losers."*

[PAUSE]

Look at the grammar.

*Ẓalamnā.* Active voice. *We* wronged. Not *we were caused to wrong.* Not *circumstances oppressed us.* Not *Iblis made us.* Just — *ẓalamnā.* We did this.

*Anfusanā.* Ourselves. The object of the wrongdoing is *us.* We did not just break a rule — we wronged our own souls. The frame is not "we got caught" but "we are the ones who have been damaged by our own act."

No *bi-malkinā.* No passive voice. No reframing of the tree as a burden. No equating themselves with Iblis.

Just: **we did this. We wronged ourselves. Forgive us, or we are lost.**

Two communities. Two broken covenants. Two answers. **One sentence saves a soul. The other condemns it.**

[PAUSE]

This is the architectural argument the surah is making. The Quran is showing us that **the difference between Adam and the people of the calf is not the size of the sin.** Eating from a tree and worshipping a calf are not equivalent in scale. But the Quran is not measuring the sins. It is measuring the *responses*.

The surah is teaching: when you fall, your falling does not damn you. **What damns you is the sentence you say when you stand up.**

Adam fell. Adam said *"we wronged ourselves."* Adam was forgiven. Adam became the first prophet.

The people of the calf fell. The people of the calf said *"we were made to carry."* The Quran preserves their words across fourteen centuries as a warning — not because their gold was particularly heavy, but because **their sentence was the wrong sentence.**

[PAUSE]

### What does this change?

You will fall. Tonight, this week, this year. You will fail something you promised yourself or promised your Lord.

The Quran is not asking you to never fall. The Quran has already told you, through the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ, *"every son of Adam is a sinner, and the best of sinners are those who repent."*

The Quran is asking you, when you fall, **to choose Adam's sentence.**

When you stand back up, do not begin with *bi-malkinā.* Do not slip into the passive voice. Do not rename your desire as a burden. Do not minimize. Do not flatten yourself into "everyone sins."

Begin with: *Rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā.* Or its equivalent in any language. **I did this. I wronged myself. Forgive me, or I am lost.**

This is the shortest path between a fall and a prophet. It is also the shortest path between a sin and its erasure.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at the whole.

We started with twenty-one words spoken in the desert by a community caught at their worst. We watched five moves of evasion unfold inside those words: the denial of ownership, the passive voice, the reframing of desire, the minimization of the act, the equivalence with the architect of sin.

Then we lifted our eyes and saw that this ayah is not alone. It sits across the surah from another sentence — Adam's sentence — that does the opposite. *We wronged ourselves.* Active. Owned. Specific. Short.

The surah's architecture is the argument. Allah has placed two responses to broken covenants in the same chapter, deliberately, so that the contrast becomes the lesson. **The difference between salvation and ruin is not whether you sinned. It is the grammar of how you spoke about your sin.**

This is why the Quran preserves twenty-one words from a panicked desert community thirty-three centuries ago. Because **we are them.** Every time we are caught, our mouths reach for the same machine. And every time, we are being given the chance to choose a different sentence.

### The Linguistic Discoveries and the Themes Are One Insight

The five moves we found inside the ayah — *bi-malkinā*, *ḥummilnā*, *awzāran*, *qadhafnāhā*, *kadhālika alqā* — are not five linguistic curiosities. **They are the five hinges of self-deception.** And the contrast with Adam's sentence is not a separate insight. It is the same insight viewed from the other side: *what does the language of repentance look like when self-deception has been refused?*

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same journey. The Quran is teaching us **how to speak honestly about ourselves.** Because honest speech is the precondition for repentance, and repentance is the precondition for return.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I described something I did using the passive voice — and what was I hiding by doing so?

2. What is the desire in my life I have most successfully renamed as a burden? *(The job that "demands" what I really wanted to do anyway. The relationship that "happened to me." The habit that "took hold of me.")*

3. Who is the Samiri I have flattened myself into equivalence with — *"I'm no worse than him"* — to avoid examining what is specifically mine?

4. What would it cost me to say, today, about one thing: *"I did this. I owned this. Forgive me"*?

5. If the difference between Adam and the people of the calf was not the size of the sin but the shape of the sentence — what is the sentence I have not yet said?

### The Distillation

When we are caught, our first instinct is to grammatically dissolve our agency — and the Quran preserves the exact mechanics of this dissolution so that we can refuse it, choose Adam's sentence instead, and find the door of return that only honest speech opens.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to perform a great act of worship tonight. You do not have to weep through a prayer. You do not have to make a list of sins.

You have to do one thing.

**Find one sentence in your inner life that begins with *bi-malkinā* — and rewrite it in the active voice.** One sin you have been narrating in the passive. One desire you have been calling a burden. One act you have been minimizing into a careless throw. One person you have been flattening yourself into.

Just one. Tonight.

Rewrite it. *I did this. I wanted this. I am the one who threw.*

Then say what Adam said. *Rabbī ẓalamtu nafsī, fa'ghfir lī.*

You will find that the door which seemed sealed when you were speaking the language of the calf opens easily — almost suspiciously easily — the moment you begin to speak the language of Adam.

That is what this ayah was preserved for.

### Du'a

اللَّهُمَّ عَلِّمْنَا لِسَانَ آدَمَ، وَجَنِّبْنَا لِسَانَ قَوْمِ الْعِجْلِ. اللَّهُمَّ إِذَا أَخْطَأْنَا، اجْعَلْ أَوَّلَ كَلِمَةٍ نَقُولُهَا لَكَ: ظَلَمْنَا أَنْفُسَنَا. وَلَا تَجْعَلْنَا مِمَّنْ يَقُولُ حُمِّلْنَا حِينَ كَانَ هُوَ الَّذِي حَمَلَ.

*O Allah, teach us the tongue of Adam, and protect us from the tongue of the people of the calf. O Allah, when we have erred, make the first word we say to You: "We have wronged ourselves." And do not make us among those who say "we were made to carry" when it was we ourselves who carried.*

آمين.
