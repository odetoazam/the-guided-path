# When They Ask About the Mountains

## Introduction

Listen to these words first.

> *Wa yas'alūnaka 'ani-l-jibāl. Faqul yansifuhā Rabbī nasfā. Fa-yadharuhā qā'an ṣafṣafā. Lā tarā fīhā 'iwajan wa lā amtā.*

> **And they ask you about the mountains. Say: My Lord will pulverize them to dust. Then He will leave it a level, smooth plain — in which you will see no crookedness, and not even the smallest hump.**

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

Picture where this question is being asked.

Makkah. A city built into a valley between mountains that have stood since before any human memory. Jabal Abū Qubays. Jabal Ḥirā'. Jabal Thawr. The Quraysh oriented their entire sense of place by these mountains. Travelers gave directions by them. Poets used them as the metaphor for permanence itself. When an Arab wanted to swear that something would never change, he would compare it to a mountain.

Mountains were not just geography. They were the **emotional anchor of stability**. The unmoved thing in a moving world.

And so when the revelations of this Day began to arrive — when the Prophet ﷺ conveyed a Day in which the heavens would split and the seas would burn — his people asked what every listener eventually asks when they try to map an unfamiliar claim onto a familiar world. They asked about the thing they were most certain could not move.

*And what about the mountains?*

It is a genuine question. Ibn Kathir states its register directly: the question is whether the mountains will remain on the Day of Resurrection or cease to exist. Al-Muyassar reads it the same way — the people are asking about the *maṣīr*, the fate, of the mountains on that Day. Classical commentators read it as sincere inquiry, not as taunt. Not ridicule, but the weight of a worldview that cannot picture these peaks ever moving. The mountains were the test case. If the scope of this Day could account for them, nothing was exempt.

The answer Allah gives Muḥammad ﷺ to deliver is more devastating than the questioner anticipated. It does not just answer the question. It dismantles the worldview that produced the question.

### The Hook

Notice — they asked about the mountains. They did not ask whether anything was solid. They asked specifically about *the* solid thing.

And Allah's answer, in three short ayahs, does something extraordinary. He does not just say the mountains will be destroyed. He chooses a word for destruction so specific it changes what mountains are. He pairs two words at the end — one that across the Quran reaches into the territory of moral and spiritual deviation, the other for the smallest physical bump — and says you will find neither.

By the time we reach the end of the third ayah, the question itself has been undone. Because the answer reveals that the questioner was not really asking about geology. He was asking about whether anything in his world is truly an anchor.

And Allah, with surgical kindness, is telling him: **no.**

Let's enter the words.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The verb that means "to winnow"

Allah does not say the mountains will be moved. He does not say they will be destroyed. He chooses a word that, before this ayah lands, you have probably never thought hard about.

> **يَنسِفُهَا رَبِّى نَسْفًۭا** — *yansifuhā Rabbī nasfā*
> *My Lord will nasf them, a [thorough] nasf-ing.*

Before we open this word, let's teach a small piece of how Arabic expresses emphasis.

In Arabic, you can take a verb and then place its own verbal noun right after it as an object. So instead of just saying "He pulverizes them," you say "He pulverizes them — a pulverizing." Grammarians call this **al-mafʿūl al-muṭlaq**, the absolute object. In English it sounds redundant. In Arabic, it does something English can barely imitate. It says: *not just the action, but the action in its complete form.* When Allah says *nasfan*, He is closing the verb. He is saying — not one stone unbroken, not one fragment uncrushed.

Now the verb itself.

The root **n-s-f** in classical Arabic does not primarily mean "to destroy." Lisān al-ʿArab and the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib both record its core meaning: **to winnow grain.** It is the action of the farmer who lifts up his pitchfork after the threshing, tosses the wheat into the air, and lets the wind carry the chaff away while the kernels fall. *Nasf* is what you do to **lightweight things** — to husks, to dust, to the parts that the wind can lift.

And this is not only a lexicographer's reading. Al-Tabari, paraphrasing the ayah in his own words, does not reach for a verb of demolition. He reaches for a second farming verb: *yudhrīhā Rabbī tadhriyatan* — *my Lord will winnow them, a winnowing.* *Dharw* is the word for tossing threshed grain into the wind and letting it fall where it falls. Asked to explain *nasf*, the mufassir explains it with the threshing floor. And notice that he keeps the doubled structure intact while he does it — *tadhriyatan* answering *nasfan* — because he can hear that the completeness is not decoration. It is part of the meaning.

[PAUSE]

Let that sit.

The mountains. The Jabal Abū Qubays of the Makkans. The mountain you climbed as a child and could not get your arms around. **The thing in nature that humans use as the metaphor for what cannot be lifted** — Allah uses, for that thing, the verb you would use for *chaff*.

Classical commentators — al-Tabari among them — describe the full process compressed into this verb: the mountains are first uprooted from their very roots (*istī'ṣāl min uṣūlihā*), then crushed against each other, then made to fly as scattered dust (*habā'an munbaththa*). Uproot, crush, scatter — the full arc of dissolution, collapsed into a single agricultural image. The *nasf* names not only the final dispersal but the whole sequence that precedes it.

Allah does not say *He will smash them*, which would treat them as heavy. He does not say *He will move them*, which would treat them as obstacles. He says *He will winnow them* — He will treat them, on that Day, **as if they were husks of grain.**

The mountain is heavy *now.* The mountain is heavy *to us.* The mountain is heavy *in this realm of cause and effect.* But on that Day, in the hand of *Rabbī* — *my Lord* — it is no heavier than the dust your breath blows across a table.

Notice what this single verb just did. It did not merely say the mountains will be destroyed. It told you what the mountains *actually weigh* in the only ledger that matters. They are heavy because of where you are standing. Move the standing point — to the position where Allah is — and the weight evaporates.

And then the cognate accusative: *nasfā*. **A complete winnowing.** Not a partial scattering. Not a metaphorical disturbance. The wind keeps blowing until there is no kernel left to fall.

This is the first word. We are not yet at the second ayah, and the worldview of the questioner has already been disassembled.

### Element Two: The verb with no past tense

The next ayah begins with a verb so quiet you could miss it.

> **فَيَذَرُهَا قَاعًۭا صَفْصَفًۭا** — *fa-yadharuhā qā'an ṣafṣafā*
> *Then He will leave it as a level, smooth plain.*

Stay with the verb *yadharu* — *He leaves.*

Here is something the classical Arabic grammarians noticed about this root. The verb **wadhara** — "to leave" — has, in classical usage, **no perfect tense form.** No *waḍara*. No "He left" in the past. It exists only in the present and the imperative. Classical Arabic grammarians record it as one of the rare Arabic verbs whose past tense the language quietly does not use.

Why does that matter here?

Because there is another Arabic verb that means "to leave" — *taraka* — which is fully conjugated in past, present, and future. Allah could have said *fa-yatrukuhā*. Both verbs would have been grammatically clean. The meaning would have been similar.

But Allah chose **yadharu**. The verb that, in the texture of Arabic itself, **does not really live in the past.**

There is a quietness to it. A kind of indifference. *Yadharu* in classical usage often carries a connotation of leaving something behind without further attention to it — the way you walk away from something that no longer concerns you.

Even al-Tabari's paraphrase keeps us inside that gap. To explain *yadharuhā* he writes *fa-yadaʿu* — *then He leaves their places in the earth, once He has winnowed them.* It is a telling substitution: *yadaʿu* belongs to the one other common verb of leaving that the grammarians group with *wadhara* for exactly the same quiet absence in its conjugation. To gloss a verb with no past, the mufassir reaches for its twin.

What this invites us to consider is this: Allah will pulverize the mountains, and then — *yadharuhā* — He will simply *leave* what remains. He will not look back. He will not survey His work. The thing that was a mountain, that was the metaphor for permanence, gets one verb, and the verb walks away from it.

[PAUSE]

We treat mountains the way we treat the houses we own and the careers we built and the bodies we maintain — as things that require attention to remain solid. We assume permanence requires upkeep.

The ayah is showing us something stranger. The mountains were never solid because *they* worked at it. They were solid because Allah kept them solid. And on a Day He chooses, He will withdraw that attention — *yadharuhā* — and they will not need to be destroyed. They will simply *cease being held up*.

And notice what the commentators keep repeating in that gap. Ibn Kathir: He takes them away *from their places*, wipes them out, removes them completely. Al-Muyassar: He removes them *from their places* and makes them scattered dust. Al-Tabari, moving to this second ayah: He leaves *their places in the earth* level, once the winnowing is done. A mountain is the one thing in a landscape that seems to *have* a location the way a person has a name — you navigate by it, you name the valley after it. On that Day the mountain loses its place first, and then the place loses every mark that anything was ever standing there.

Now look at the two words after the verb.

**Qāʿan ṣafṣafā** — *a level, smooth plain.*

*Qāʿ* is a flat, low piece of ground. The Arabs used it for the floor of a desert valley after rain has washed it bare. *Ṣafṣaf* is a doubled root — *ṣafṣafa* from *ṣaffa*, "to arrange in a row." The doubling intensifies. *Ṣafṣaf* is **flat the way a row is straight** — uniformly, repeatedly, perfectly level, as if every grain has been laid in line with every other. Al-Tabari's commentary draws out what is encompassed by this description: not only is there no raised prominence, there is no elevation at all — smooth, level, stripped of every feature. Ibn Zayd, in his transmission, needs only one word for it: *mustawī* — level. The whole of what a mountain was is answered with a single flat adjective.

Ibn Abbas, in al-Tabari's transmission, adds one detail that sharpens the image further: this leveled ground has no vegetation in it either — not a blade of grass, not a root holding. Al-Muyassar confirms: the earth spread out, level, smooth, no plant life remaining. The *nasf* stripped not only the peaks but everything the earth had been holding up. The leveling is total.

So the pair: not just flat — *floor-flat, row-straight, the desert after the wind has finished with it.*

Where the mountain stood — nothing. Not even a memory of where it was.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: Two words for "nothing left"

The third ayah is the one that should keep us awake at night.

> **لَّا تَرَىٰ فِيهَا عِوَجًۭا وَلَآ أَمْتًۭا** — *lā tarā fīhā 'iwajan wa lā amtā*
> *You will see in it no crookedness, and not even the smallest hump.*

Two words at the end. **ʿIwaj** and **amt**. They are paired here, and the pairing is the point.

Al-Tabari opens his commentary on this verse with a telling note: scholars of tafsir held different views (*ikhtilāf*) on the precise meanings of these two words in this context. The reading transmitted from Ibn Abbas understands *ʿiwaj* here as valleys — the depressions and hollows of the earth — and *amt* as hills and raised ground (*rawābī*). On this reading the pairing is comprehensive: neither the low places nor the high places remain. Every feature of the earth's relief — every depression and every elevation — is erased. Al-Muyassar draws out the full scope: the observer will see neither incline, elevation, nor depression. Three dimensions of topographic reality, all gone.

But another group of commentators understood *ʿiwaj* more broadly, as any deviation or crookedness from straightness. And here something opens in the word's Quranic career that invites further reflection.

In Sūrat al-Kahf, ayah 1, Allah praises Himself for sending down a Book *lam yajʿal lahu ʿiwajā* — "in which He placed no *ʿiwaj.*" In that ayah, *ʿiwaj* carries the sense of **moral and spiritual crookedness** — the kind of distortion that makes a doctrine bend away from truth. In Sūrat al-Zumar (39:28), the Quran is described as *ghayra dhī ʿiwaj* — "without any *ʿiwaj.*"

And there is one more use, closer than either of those — it sits in the very next ayah. In 20:108, the people follow the Caller *lā ʿiwaja lahu*: there is no *ʿiwaj* in him, no swerving away from him, no crookedness in the summons. The word lands twice inside a single breath of recitation — first on the emptied earth, then on the Call that crosses it. That adjacency does not settle which reading the commentators intended in 20:107; the two verses may simply be using the word in its two registers, side by side. But a listener who keeps reciting hears it again immediately, and the second time it is unmistakably not about topography.

Across these uses, *ʿiwaj* is the word for the **inner crookedness of a thing** — the way a path can be morally bent, the way a heart can lean off-true, the way a teaching can deviate from straightness. The Mufradāt of al-Rāghib records this dimension: the word sits close to the realm of meaning, value, moral geometry. Whether this layer is fully operative in 20:107 is a question the classical commentators do not settle unanimously — the primary readings here are physical, as Ibn Abbas's transmission makes clear. But the word does not arrive in the Arabic ear without this resonance, and what follows is what that resonance opens for the tadabbur tradition.

Now look at *amt*. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Tāj al-ʿArūs — define *amt* as the smallest physical lump, hump, or unevenness in the ground. The slight rise where a stone is buried just under the surface. The dimple in the sand where a rabbit dug. *Amt* is the most physical, smallest possible bump.

So Allah pairs them. **No ʿiwaj** — no valley, no deviation, no inner bent. **No amt** — not even the smallest physical hump.

[PAUSE]

What this resonance opens is staggering.

When you flatten ground, you eliminate hills. You do not, by flattening, eliminate **crookedness**. Crookedness is a moral category. The flatness of a parking lot does not address whether the lot was built honestly. The geometry of evenness has nothing to do with the geometry of moral bend.

But Allah, describing what is left where the mountains stood, uses a word that — across the Quran — points to *spiritual* deviation, and applies it to the **emptiness left behind.**

What this invites us to consider is this: the mountain we built our worldview on was not just physically large. It was **the place where crookedness hid**. It was where humans built their pretensions of permanence, of self-sufficiency, of a world that runs by its own laws without a Lord. The mountain was not just a hill. It was a **hiding place for ʿiwaj** — for the crookedness in the heart that imagines itself anchored to something more reliable than its Maker.

When Allah pulverizes the mountains, He is not just rearranging geology. He is removing the **architecture of self-deception**. The places where we hid the *ʿiwaj* in our hearts behind the apparent solidity of the dunyā — those places are gone. *Lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajā* — you will not see in it any crookedness. Because the crookedness was never in the mountain. It was in us, and the mountain only let us forget that.

And then *wa lā amtā.* And not even the smallest hump.

Allah pairs the resonance of inner crookedness with the physical bump to seal the meaning. He is saying: I have removed the hiding place for deviation, and I have removed even the outer geometry that made the hiding feel solid. **Nothing of what you used to lean on remains** — neither the deception, nor even the smallest hump that made the deception feel real.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have three words doing structural work. *Nasfā* — what was heavy is winnowed. *Yadharuhā* — what stood is simply left, no longer held. *ʿIwajā wa lā amtā* — what hid the crookedness is gone, and so is the smallest hump that could remind you it was ever there.

The linguistic construction has shown us something. Allah has not described destruction. He has described **withdrawal**. The mountains do not so much die as become weightless when the One holding them up steps away.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about mountains — but about every "mountain" we have built our lives on.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The mountains in your life

Begin where you actually live.

You do not, in any practical sense, lean on Mount Everest. Most of us have never even seen a mountain up close. So when Allah describes the pulverizing of mountains on the Day, our first reaction is to turn the image into geology and walk away.

But the original listeners did not hear geology. They heard their own anchor.

What is *your* mountain?

For many of us, it is the career. The thing we have spent fifteen years building. The position, the reputation, the body of work that — when we think of ourselves — is the first thing that rises up in the imagination. The mountain we look at and feel: *this is solid. This is what I have. This will hold.*

For others, it is the marriage. The decade-and-a-half partnership that has weathered enough that we have stopped believing it could break. *This is solid.*

For others, the children. The savings. The home that is paid off. The reputation in the community. The body — the running pace at 40, the mind that still remembers names.

Each of us has at least one mountain. Often several.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again. *Yansifuhā Rabbī nasfā.* My Lord will winnow them. As chaff. Completely.

We are not being told the dunyā will be destroyed. We are being told that the things in our lives that *feel* most solid have always been **no heavier than chaff** to the One who is holding them up. Their weight to us comes entirely from the fact that we are inside this realm. Step outside it — to the position from which Allah sees — and they weigh nothing.

This is not nihilism. The Quran is not saying your career is meaningless. It is saying your career is **not what is holding you.** What is holding you is Allah. And the moment we mistake one for the other — the moment we say "my career is what is holding me" rather than "Allah holds me, and my career is one small thing He has placed in my hand" — we have built a mountain. And Allah has just told us, with quiet finality, what He does to mountains.

Stay with this.

The Companions did not hear this ayah and stop building. They built mosques, raised armies, traded in markets, raised children. But they did not put their hearts inside what they built. They built with their hands and worshipped with their hearts, and they understood that any structure they raised was being held up by Allah, not by them. So when Allah took the structure — when Abū Bakr lost his wealth in the cause, when ʿUmar lost his sons in battle, when the whole world they had known dissolved into a new one — they did not lose their footing. Their footing was never on the structure. It was on Allah.

The ayah is asking us a question. Not about the mountains on the Day. About the mountains *now.* If Allah were to *yansif* — to winnow away — the thing you have built your sense of self on, would your soul remain standing?

If the answer is no, the mountain has become an idol. And what Allah does to mountains, He has already told you.

The practical movement is small but seismic. **Today, identify your mountain.** The one whose loss feels unsurvivable. And every time the heart leans toward it as the source of stability, return the heart to its actual source. *My Lord holds me. The mountain He gave me is small in His hand. If He keeps it, alḥamdu lillāh. If He winnows it, He has not winnowed me.*

This is not asceticism. It is **the right map of who is holding what.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The crookedness that hid behind the hump

Now move to the third ayah through the resonance that *ʿiwaj* carries across the Quran's other uses — its reach into the realm of inner crookedness and moral bend.

*Lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajan wa lā amtā.* You will see in it neither crookedness nor hump.

There is a strange phenomenon in the spiritual life that this pairing names exactly.

We tend to hide our spiritual deviations behind our outer structures. The arrogance hides behind the title. The greed hides behind the business that "needs" the money. The neglect of family hides behind the demanding job. The lack of dhikr hides behind the busy calendar. The harshness of tongue hides behind the "personality." The avoidance of accountability hides behind the reputation we have so carefully built.

Every *ʿiwaj* — every inner crookedness — finds an *amt* to hide behind. A hill, a hump, a structure in the dunyā that makes the deviation invisible.

We tell ourselves: *yes, my prayer has gotten weak, but look at all I'm carrying. Yes, I haven't checked on my parents in three months, but look at the project I'm shipping. Yes, my heart has hardened, but look at how successful the family is.*

The hill is hiding the crookedness. As long as the hill stands, we can keep the crookedness without ever having to look at it.

[PAUSE]

Now listen to what Allah does in the third ayah.

*Lā tarā fīhā ʿiwajan wa lā amtā.* You will see in it neither crookedness nor hump.

He pulverizes the hump first. *Yansifuhā nasfā.* Every structure you used to hide behind — gone. The career, the title, the reputation, the family standing — all winnowed.

And then He says: *and now you will see no crookedness either.*

This is the part that should stop us.

Why does removing the hump remove the crookedness? Because **the crookedness was being kept alive by the hump.** As long as the structure stood, the deviation it sheltered could persist. The greed needed the business to justify it. The arrogance needed the title to feed it. The harshness needed the "personality" to defend it. Take away the structures, and the deviations have nothing left to lean on. They die not because Allah destroys them separately, but because their hiding place was destroyed.

What this invites us to consider is staggering. **We will not have to be punished for our crookedness on that Day in some separate proceeding. The same act that flattens the dunyā exposes the crookedness that was hiding inside it.** The pulverizing of the mountain *is* the unveiling of the *ʿiwaj.* They are one motion.

And here is the mercy: Allah is telling us this **now.** Before the Day. While there is still time.

The motion He performs on that Day is a motion we can volunteer for now. We can do our own *nasf* on our own mountains — not by destroying our work, but by refusing to let our work hide our crookedness any longer. We can ask: *what spiritual deviation is my structure currently sheltering? What part of me is staying alive because something I'm proud of is keeping it hidden?*

The honest answer is the doorway.

If we find the crookedness while the mountain is still standing — and we repent, and we level the inner ground ourselves — then on the Day when the mountain is winnowed, there is no *ʿiwaj* hiding inside it to be revealed. The flatness Allah describes in this ayah is not just what He will do to the earth. It is what we are invited to do to our own hearts. **A heart with no crookedness, no hump, no hiding place** — *qāʿan ṣafṣafā* — a soul leveled before its Lord, with nothing left to defend.

That heart is what survives the *nasf.* Because the mountains it leaned on were never the source of its stability. *Allah* was. And Allah is the one thing that, on that Day, will not be winnowed away.

The practical movement: **Tonight, before sleep, ask one question.** *What in my life is my mountain — and what crookedness is it hiding?* Not as performance. As honesty. The Day is coming. The hump goes. The crookedness gets exposed. The only question is whether you let Allah expose it now, while there is still room to repent — or whether you wait until the *nasf*, when there is no longer any room at all.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are saying the same thing from two directions.

The verbs — *yansifu*, *yadharu* — told us that what we treat as solid is solid only because Allah is holding it. The pairing — *ʿiwajan wa lā amtā* — told us that the structures we lean on are also the structures we hide our crookedness behind. The single act of *nasf* dissolves both at once. The hump goes; the crookedness it sheltered has nowhere left to live.

This is the same message: **whatever in your life feels permanent is borrowed permanence. Whatever in your soul feels unaddressable is hiding behind something borrowed.** When the borrowing ends, both end together.

### Questions to Carry

- What is the mountain in my life — the thing whose loss would feel like the end of *me?*
- What spiritual crookedness is my mountain currently hiding — what *ʿiwaj* is staying alive because the hump is keeping it invisible?
- If Allah were to *yansif* my mountain tomorrow, would my heart still know where it stood?
- What would it look like, this week, to begin leveling the ground myself — to expose my own *ʿiwaj* before the Day exposes it for me?
- Whose hand is actually holding my life upright right now — and have I been thanking the wrong source?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The mountains we lean on are weightless to the One who holds them, and on the Day He withdraws His holding, both the structures and the crookedness they sheltered will vanish in a single motion — so the work is to lean on the One who is holding, not on what He is holding up.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to wait for the Day to feel what these ayahs are describing. You can volunteer for the *nasf* now. Identify the mountain. Identify the crookedness it shelters. Let Allah level the ground inside you while there is still time to walk on it.

The Day is coming when *qāʿan ṣafṣafā* will describe the earth. May it also describe our hearts before then — leveled, defenseless, with no hump to hide behind, *lā ʿiwajan wa lā amtā*, only the soul and its Lord.

### Brief Du'a

*Yā Rabb, You who winnow the mountains as chaff — winnow from our hearts every false anchor before the Day You winnow it for us. Show us the crookedness our structures are hiding, and give us the courage to level the ground ourselves. Make our standing not on what You have given us, but on You. Āmīn.*
