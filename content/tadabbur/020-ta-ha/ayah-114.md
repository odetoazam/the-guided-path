# Ta-Ha 114 — The Only Increase

> [ayah:20:114] فَتَعَـٰلَى ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمَلِكُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۗ وَلَا تَعْجَلْ بِٱلْقُرْءَانِ مِن قَبْلِ أَن يُقْضَىٰٓ إِلَيْكَ وَحْيُهُۥ ۖ وَقُل رَّبِّ زِدْنِى عِلْمًا
>
> *Fa-taʿālā-llāhu al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq. Wa lā taʿjal bil-Qurʾāni min qabli an yuqḍā ilayka waḥyuh. Wa qul: Rabbi, zidnī ʿilmā.*
>
> "So exalted is Allah — the True King. And do not hasten with the Qur'an before its revelation has been completed to you. And say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge."

## Introduction — Stand Where the Surah Has Brought You

Before we open this ayah, we have to understand where in the surah we're standing. Because Surah Ta-Ha has been a long conversation about **false kings and the sound of their collapse**.

Reconstruct it for a moment.

A few pages earlier, Pharaoh declared himself the supreme authority of the land. He turned to his court and asked, with theatrical confidence, "Have you believed in him before I gave you permission?" — as if belief itself required his royal seal. Then the sea folded over him.

Then Sammiri — the man who fashioned the calf out of melted gold while Musa was on the mountain — stood briefly as a counterfeit prophet, speaking from the sleeve of revelation he didn't possess. Musa exposed him, and his sentence was *lā misās* — "do not touch me, do not be touched" — a king of nothing, banished into untouchability.

Then the surah lifted you to the Day of Judgment. And there too, the kings collapsed. Voices were hushed. *"And all faces shall be humbled before the Living, the Sustaining"* (20:111). The very next ayah after that says no soul will be wronged. The one after that says this Qur'an is being sent down so people might fear, or so it might generate for them remembrance.

And then, in that hush — after every false crown has rolled across the floor — Allah opens the next ayah with a single word: **fa-taʿālā**.

So.

So Allah is exalted. **Al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq.** The True King. The only one whose throne didn't fall.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what should stop us. After all of that — after Pharaoh, after Sammiri, after the whole panorama of judgment, after the formal coronation of the only real King — what do you expect Allah's very next words to be? A warning? A threat to the disbelievers? A command to the believers to obey?

Instead, the True King turns to His most beloved servant, Muhammad ﷺ, and says two things no one would have written into this script.

**Don't rush.**

**And ask Me for more.**

That's the entire ayah. Two commands to the Prophet, sandwiched between a coronation and a closing. And inside those two commands is a teaching about how the True King treats those who serve Him — and how He wants them to treat the gift of His speech.

Let's earn it.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Fa-taʿālā* — The Verb Whose Shape Is the Meaning

The first word is **fa-taʿālā**. Translators usually flatten it to "exalted" or "high." But the Arabic is doing something the English cannot.

Before we open the word, let me teach you a feature of the language.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns. The simple base form (Form I) gives you the bare action — *ʿalā*, "he was high." But Arabic has nine more forms layered on top, each one bending the meaning. There's a form for making something happen (causative). There's a form for doing something to yourself (reflexive). There's a form that intensifies — does the act over and over.

And there's **Form VI** — the *tafāʿala* pattern — which classical grammarians often describe as *takalluf* or *mubālagha*: "an intensified, deliberate, manifest doing of the act, often in a way that displays the trait." When you say *taʿāẓama*, you don't just mean "he was great." You mean "he displayed greatness, he was great in a way that was beyond measure, beyond comparison."

So when Allah says *taʿālā* — Form VI — about Himself, He is not saying "He is high."

He is saying: **He is high in a way that towers, that exceeds, that cannot be reached by the act of climbing**.

Now hold that next to what came before in the surah. Pharaoh tried to climb. He built towers. He said in another surah, *"Build me a high structure, perhaps I might reach the God of Musa."* That word — high structure — *ṣarḥan*. Pharaoh's whole project was *taʿālī* counterfeited: a vertical performance of self-elevation.

Sammiri tried to climb. He grabbed *qabḍatan min athari ar-rasūl* — a fistful of dust from the messenger's footprint — and tried to manufacture sanctity from a particle.

The kings of the earth in the previous ayahs tried to climb. They wore their crowns and their faces.

And every single climb collapsed.

Then Allah opens the next ayah with **fa-taʿālā** — "So He is the One who *truly* towers." The fa- ("so") is doing structural work. It's saying: *given everything you just witnessed*, given that every counterfeit altitude crumbled, **so He** — the only real One — towers.

Stay with that. The exaltation is not announced before the false kings fall. It is announced *after*. Allah lets the earth empty itself of pretenders first. Then the word *taʿālā* lands into the silence.

[PAUSE]

There's one more thing to notice, and this is where it gets tender. Form VI (*tafāʿala*) often carries a sense of **manifestation** — not just being, but being-in-a-way-that-becomes-evident. So *taʿālā* doesn't only mean "He is exalted." It means "His exaltation has now made itself unmistakable."

The verb is the shape of the moment. The false kings have just been removed from the field. And what remains, having made itself manifest by everyone else's collapse, is the True altitude.

What this single word reveals: **Allah's height is not a claim He had to make. It is what is left standing after every other claim falls.**

[PAUSE]

Classical commentators extend this further. Al-Muyassar reads *taʿālā* as carrying the sense of *taqaddasa ʿan kulli naqṣin* — **sanctified and purified from every deficiency**. This is not only elevation above rivals; it is the complete absence of any flaw, any gap, any need that would make His height dependent on something outside Himself. Every false throne required scaffolding — armies, treasury, propaganda, the sustained performance of invincibility. *Taʿālā* says: there is nothing holding Him up. His height has no scaffolding. He was exalted before creation existed and will be exalted when it ends. The fall of the false kings reveals this; it does not produce it.

### Element Two: *Al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq* — The Two Words Pharaoh Could Not Pair

Allah follows the verb with two of His names placed side by side: *al-Malik*, the King — *al-Ḥaqq*, the True/Real.

You need both of them to feel what's happening.

*Al-Malik* by itself is something Pharaoh also claimed. Pharaoh had a kingdom, a court, a treasury, an army. By worldly definition, he was *malik*. The word does not, on its own, distinguish a true ruler from a counterfeit one. Plenty of people in this dunya hold the title.

That's why Allah immediately attaches **al-Ḥaqq**.

The root *ḥ-q-q* in Arabic is one of the densest in the language. It means: that which is real, that which is true, that which is established, that which is *due*, that which is the rightful claim. *Ḥaqq* is the opposite of *bāṭil* — and *bāṭil* doesn't just mean "false," it means "void, vacant, hollow, that which has no substance underneath the appearance."

So when Allah says *al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq*, He is saying: the King whose kingship has reality underneath it. The King who is not performing. The King whose throne does not depend on convincing anyone.

Now go back to Pharaoh's last words in the sea, in another surah: *"I believe that there is no god but the One in whom the Children of Israel believed."* What was that sentence? It was a man finally noticing, in his last second, that the ruler he had been performing as was *bāṭil* all along — hollow, with nothing underneath. He had been *al-malik al-bāṭil*. The hollow king. The empty throne.

Sammiri was the same. *"I made it for myself"* — a kingdom of one, fashioned from a fistful of nothing.

And then, after all of them have evacuated their thrones, Allah says — almost quietly — *al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq*. Not loud. Not boastful. Form VI does the boasting. The two names just *stand*.

Notice something the classical mufassirūn point to here. The *al-* in front of both names is the *al* of *istighrāq* — totality, comprehensiveness. *Al-Malik* doesn't mean "a king" or "the king of this." It means *the* King — exhaustively, with no room left for any rival category. *Al-Ḥaqq* doesn't mean "He has some truth in Him." It means *the* Real — such that everything else is real only by borrowing from Him.

So the two names together construct a sentence that has no exits: there is one King, He is the only One who is real, and the realness of every other thing in existence is a loan He extends.

Al-Ṭabarī sharpens this further by describing *al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq* as the King whose *sulṭān* — whose governing authority — overpowers not only every *malik* but every *jabbār*. That last word deserves a moment. *Jabbār* is not a neutral synonym for king. It names the ruler who compels — who moves people through force, who holds power through the terror of non-compliance. The Qur'an uses it for Pharaoh and for the arrogant powers of the earth. Pharaoh's throne was *jabr* incarnate: the architecture of compulsion, the kingdom that cannot exist without fear. And al-Ṭabarī is saying that Allah's authority overpowers even this — not by out-compelling the *jabbār*, but because His *sulṭān* is of an entirely different order. Recognizing His kingship is not a compelled act. It is what clarity looks like when compulsion has run out of road.

What this reveals: **everything we have been calling "real" in our lives — institutions, careers, status, currencies, certainties — is real only as long as the True King keeps lending it reality. The moment He withdraws the loan, it becomes Pharaoh's last second.**

### Element Three: *Lā Taʿjal* — The Prohibition That Was Never Aimed at the Enemy

Now we come to the part of the ayah that has stopped attentive readers for fourteen centuries.

Allah, having just crowned Himself the True King, turns His face toward His most beloved servant — the man who stayed with Him through Ṭāʾif, through the cave, through the loss of his wife and his uncle — and says:

**"And do not hasten with the Qur'an before its revelation has been completed to you."**

Don't rush.

Now to feel why this is shocking, you have to understand what *ʿajila* means in the Quranic vocabulary, and who normally gets accused of it.

*ʿAjila* is one of the deepest diagnoses Allah makes about humanity. In Surah al-Anbiyāʾ He says, *khuliqa al-insānu min ʿajal* — "man was created from haste." Not just *capable of* haste — *fashioned out of* it. Haste, in the Quran's vocabulary, is the substance of human nature when it forgets its Lord. It's the mode of those who can't trust the timing of the True King.

Pharaoh was hasty. *"Have you believed before I gave permission?"* — the haste of the threatened. The magicians were hasty in their throwing — until Musa's staff slowed them down. Sammiri was hasty — he couldn't wait the forty days for Musa to return; he had to manufacture a god *now*. Adam was, in a sense, hasty in 20:115 — the very next ayah — when he reached for the tree before the moment of mastery.

Haste is the distinguishing mark of those who don't yet trust that the King's timing is enough.

And here — after framing the entire surah around that diagnosis — Allah turns to the Prophet ﷺ and says: *wa lā taʿjal*. **Do not be among those.**

Stay with how stunning this is.

Allah is not warning the Prophet ﷺ about lying. Not about disobedience. Not about anything we would associate with prophetic vulnerability. He is warning him about *the same trait Adam fell on, the trait Sammiri fell on, the trait Pharaoh fell on*. Haste.

But — and this is the part the early scholars dwelled on at length — what was the Prophet ﷺ being hasty *toward*?

He was being hasty **toward the Qur'an itself.**

When Jibrīl ﷺ would come down with revelation, the Prophet ﷺ in his eagerness to capture every word, in his terror of forgetting any of it, in his hunger for it, would move his lips and tongue along with Jibrīl mid-recitation, racing to keep up. Allah addresses this elsewhere too — *lā tuḥarrik bihi lisānaka li-taʿjala bih* (75:16) — "Do not move your tongue with it to hasten with it."

So the haste being prohibited here is not the haste of disobedience. It is the haste of *love*. The haste of someone afraid the gift will run out before he can hold it. The haste of a student who reaches for the next page before the teacher has finished the current sentence.

And Allah corrects it.

[PAUSE]

It is worth pausing here, because classical scholars did not all read *lā taʿjal* the same way — and both readings deepen the teaching. Al-Muyassar follows the line just described: the Prophet ﷺ racing Jibreel mid-recitation, his tongue moving to capture each word before the angel had finished. Al-Ṭabarī, however, offers a broader reading: the prohibition is against reciting the Qur'an to companions, or reading it to them, before its *bayān* — its full explanation and clarification — had been revealed to the Prophet ﷺ himself. On this reading, *lā taʿjal* does not govern only the moment of reception from the angel. It governs the entire chain of transmission: **you may not give others what you yourself have not yet fully received.**

Both readings are within the ayah's range. And together they are more instructive than either alone: the prohibition against haste runs from the first moment Jibreel speaks, through the moment of comprehension, all the way to the moment of passing the message on. No stage of this journey with divine speech is exempt from deliberate, unhurried receiving.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the verb that follows: **yuqḍā ilayka waḥyuh** — "until its revelation has been *completed* to you."

Notice the passive. Allah does not say "before *I* complete it to you." He does not name the active agent. *Yuqḍā* — "is completed" — agent hidden.

Why?

The classical grammarians point out that *yuqḍā* is the passive of *qaḍā* — to decree, to fulfill, to bring to completion. The passive removes the act from your field of vision. It says: the completion is not your task. You are the receiver. The doing is being handled — by Someone whose name does not need to be inserted because everyone knows whose name it is.

So the syntax itself is teaching the lesson. *Don't hasten — because the active agent of completion is not you.* Your job is not to complete the revelation. Your job is to **let it be completed *to* you.**

The preposition is *ilayka* — "to you." The Qur'an is not something the Prophet ﷺ produces. It is something delivered to him, finished, when the One delivering decides it is ready. His role — and ours, every time we approach Allah's speech — is the role of the receiver, not the manufacturer.

What this reveals: **the deepest temptation of the lover of revelation is to confuse love with manufacture — to think that because we want it badly we should grab it fast. Allah closes that door. The True King does not need His servant to rush. The True King gives.**

### Bridge from the Linguistic to the Real

Look at what these three elements have already shown.

A verb whose shape *is* the manifestation of true height after false heights collapse — and whose classical reading encompasses not only elevation above rivals but sanctification from every deficiency, a height that needs no scaffolding.

Two names that build a kingship with no exits — one whose governing authority overpowers every tyrant, not by superior compulsion but by being the only One whose rule requires no compulsion at all — and reveal every other claimed reality as a loan He extends.

A prohibition aimed not at the enemy but at the beloved — naming the most human of impulses as the one thing the receiver of revelation must not do, whether at the moment of receiving from the angel or at the moment of conveying to others, because neither stage of this journey permits haste.

Now let's ask what these three elements together are revealing — not just about the Prophet ﷺ, not just about that historical moment, but about the posture any human being must hold when approaching the speech of the True King.

The revelation is complete. The Qur'an is in our hands. *Yuqḍā* has already happened — it has been brought to completion and delivered. And yet the ayah does not retire when the revelation closed. It lives on inside every human being who picks up the text, because we are still in the position of the receiver. We are still told: *lā taʿjal*.

## Part 2 — The Commanded Duʿāʾ

Halfway through the ayah something shifts. The first half corrects. The second half *commands* — and what it commands is one of the most quietly astonishing things in all of Quranic discourse.

*Wa qul: Rabbi, zidnī ʿilmā.*

And say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge.

Two words follow *wa qul* — "and say." But notice that *wa qul* is itself a command. Allah is not merely permitting this duʿāʾ. He is telling the Prophet ﷺ to make it. This is a commanded prayer. And when you go through the Qur'an looking for moments where Allah explicitly dictates the wording of a duʿāʾ to the Prophet ﷺ — places where He says not just *"ask Me"* but *"say this, exactly, to Me"* — the prayer for increase in knowledge stands out for what it asks.

Not for victory. Not for patience, though patience has been a theme of the entire surah. Not for comfort from grief. Not for protection from enemies.

*Rabbi, zidnī ʿilmā.* My Lord, increase me in knowledge.

[PAUSE]

The word **zidnī** comes from *z-y-d* — to increase, to add, to be in excess of what was already there. This is not a request for the gift to begin. It is a request for the gift that has already been given to grow larger.

Al-Muyassar renders the complete thought as: *Rabbi, zidnī ʿilman ilā mā ʿallamtanī* — "My Lord, increase me in knowledge **beyond what You have already taught me**." That phrase makes explicit what is already implicit in *zidnī*: the starting point is not zero. The Prophet ﷺ was already the most knowledgeable of human beings about divine reality. He was being told to ask for *more* — which means the posture of asking for more is never inappropriate, no matter how much has already been given.

This is quietly radical. The command is not to a student beginning his studies. It is to the seal of the prophets, the one who received the final revelation. And he is commanded: *keep asking for increase.* The *ilā mā ʿallamtanī* contains both a gratitude and a hunger. What I have been given is real and acknowledged. And I am not done receiving.

The teaching this contains for us is uncomfortable in the best way. We often make our peace with what we know. We find a level of religious literacy that feels sufficient and we stay there. We read the meanings we already understand and pass over the words whose depths we haven't yet reached. *Wa qul* says: that peace is premature. The Messenger himself — with all that he carried — was told not to be satisfied. The duʿāʾ for increase in knowledge is not the prayer of the beginner. It is the prayer of the one who has received enough to know how much more there is.

[PAUSE]

Now hold *lā taʿjal* and *rabbi zidnī ʿilmā* in the same hand for a moment.

Don't rush. And ask for more.

These two commands, sitting inside the same ayah, are not in tension. They are the two wings of the same flight. *Lā taʿjal* describes the posture of receiving — patient, unhurried, letting the meaning land fully before moving. *Rabbi zidnī ʿilmā* describes the disposition of the receiver — never saturated, never declaring the well empty, always turning back to the Source and asking for another draw.

The surah has shown you what happens when you rush toward a throne that isn't yours. Pharaoh rushed. Sammiri rushed. Adam reached before the moment was ready. And in every case, the haste was a form of distrust — a hidden conviction that if you didn't grab now, the thing would be taken.

*Lā taʿjal* is the correction of that distrust in the domain of knowledge: **you don't need to grab, because the One who gives has already decided to keep giving.** And *rabbi zidnī ʿilmā* is the active form of that same trust: **I know You are still giving, so here I am again, asking.**

Together they build the only posture that the True King recognizes in His servants: not grasping, not passive — but expectant. Unhurried expectancy before the One who completes what He begins.

## Part 3 — Where You Are Now Standing

The surah is almost finished. Surah Ta-Ha — which opened with *Ṭā-Hā. We have not sent down this Qur'an to cause you distress* — closes on this.

Not on a final condemnation of Pharaoh. Not on a legal ruling. Not on a theological proposition. It closes with the True King — having emptied the field of every counterfeit — turning to His servant and saying: **slow down when you receive My speech, and never stop asking for more of it.**

Sit with what that framing does.

The Qur'an's reason for existence, according to this surah's own framing, is *taqwā* and *dhikr* — fear of Allah and remembrance. And what makes both possible, what undergirds both, is *ʿilm* — knowledge. You cannot fear One you do not know. You cannot remember what you have never understood. The commanded duʿāʾ is not an addendum to the surah's agenda; it is the engine the surah was always building toward.

And it is commanded for us too. Every time you read *rabbi zidnī ʿilmā* in this surah, the Prophet ﷺ is not the only one receiving the instruction. The Qur'an was not revealed as a historical document about one man's relationship with revelation. It was revealed as a living address — and the *wa qul* that commands this duʿāʾ addresses every tongue that will ever recite it.

[PAUSE]

What would it mean to actually take this seriously?

*Lā taʿjal bil-Qurʾān.* Not just "slow down when you recite." Slow down when you encounter a word you haven't broken open yet. Don't pass *taʿālā* as if it means simply "high." Don't pass *al-Ḥaqq* as if it means simply "true." Don't pass *yuqḍā* as if the passive were accidental. The True King spoke in Arabic, in form patterns, in root choices, in grammatical decisions — and al-Ṭabarī reminds us that even the act of teaching from the Qur'an requires waiting until comprehension is complete, not just reception. None of it is arbitrary. All of it is being *completed to you* — and the only wrong response is to rush past it.

And then *rabbi zidnī ʿilmā* — not as a phrase you add to the end of a dua list, but as a genuine acknowledgment that you are the receiver, the Qur'an is the completed gift, and your relationship with it is not done being deepened. Ask for more. Not because what you have is insufficient for salvation — but because the True King is still giving, and the correct response to a King who keeps giving is to keep receiving.

[PAUSE]

The surah began with Allah speaking to His Prophet directly, with a tenderness unusual even in the Qur'an: *We have not sent down this Qur'an to cause you distress.* It ends by addressing him again, with the same directness — but now not to comfort, to instruct.

**Don't rush. And ask Me for more.**

Both of those sentences, if you let them, will reshape how you sit with the Book for the rest of your life. Because they are not addressed only to the Prophet ﷺ. They are addressed, through him, to every human being who has ever loved Allah's speech and been afraid of not receiving it fully. The fear is real, and Allah names it. And His answer to it is not "relax, it's fine." His answer is: *the One who gives completes what He gives. Your job is to receive — patiently, expectantly, without grasping — and then to ask for more.*

That is the teaching of the True King to those He trusts enough to call His servants.

---

*Rabbi, zidnī ʿilmā.*

My Lord — increase me in knowledge.
