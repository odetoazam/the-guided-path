# Neither Wrong Added Nor Right Eaten Away

## The Verse

> وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌۭ فَلَا يَخَافُ ظُلْمًۭا وَلَا هَضْمًۭا

> *Wa man yaʿmal mina al-ṣāliḥāti wa huwa muʾminun fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman wa lā haḍmā.*

> **"And whoever does of righteous deeds while he is a believer — he will fear neither wrong added nor right eaten away."**

[PAUSE]

---

## Introduction: The World Before This Promise

Picture a courtroom. Not the polite, procedural kind on television — the kind that haunts every honest worker in every bureaucracy that has ever existed. The kind where you have done your work, kept your records, lived as straight as you knew how, and yet you still cannot sleep the night before your case is heard.

Why? Because every human being who has ever lived under any system of power — at any job, in any government, in any family — knows in their bones that **two things can go wrong** when your case is finally weighed.

The first is that **something can be added** to your account that was never yours. A mistake you didn't make. An accusation you didn't earn. A line item that appeared from nowhere. Someone else's failure that quietly migrated onto your ledger.

The second is that **something can be taken away** that was rightly yours. Your hours, quietly trimmed. Your contribution, attributed to another. Your due, eroded by small subtractions until what remains no longer reflects what you actually gave.

Every human heart knows these two fears. They are the twin anxieties of any creature standing before a system more powerful than itself.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that in your chest, and listen to the surah you are sitting inside.

Surah Ta-Ha is the surah of guidance and the surah of memory. It opens with Allah comforting His Messenger ﷺ — *"We have not sent down the Quran upon you that you be distressed."* It walks us through the long journey of Mūsā: from the burning bush, through the contest with Pharaoh, into the parting sea, to the moment Mūsā returns from his forty nights to find his people worshipping a calf made of melted gold. It is a surah about how easily human beings forget — and how patiently Allah remembers them anyway.

And then, near its end, the surah turns its face toward the Day. Toward the moment all of these stories were rehearsing for. Ayah 108 — *"On that Day they will follow the Caller; there will be no deviation from him; and voices will be hushed before the Most Merciful, so you will not hear except a faint sound."* Ayah 110 — *"He knows what is before them and what is behind them, but they cannot encompass Him in knowledge."* Ayah 111 — *"And faces shall be humbled before the Living, the Self-Subsisting; and whoever carries wrong has indeed failed."*

The atmosphere is reverent, hushed, weighty. The verbs slow down. The voices drop. Every soul in the surah is now standing in a courtroom — but not a human one.

And then, into that hush, comes the promise of ayah 112.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook I want you to carry. The verse uses two Arabic words for fear's two directions. One word every Muslim has heard a thousand times — *ẓulm.* But the second word — *haḍm* — appears in the entire Quran in this **eschatological** sense exactly once. Allah chose a word so specific, so visceral, so anatomically precise, that it changes what justice on the Day of Judgment actually means.

He did not just say: *you will not be wronged.* He could have. The Quran says that elsewhere. Here He says something more surgical. He named the **two ways** justice can fail — and removed them both.

Stay with that. Because once you see what *ẓulm* and *haḍm* are doing together in this verse, you will never read about that Day the same way again.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Partitive *Min* — "*Of* the Righteous Deeds"

Before we get to the famous pair, look at what is hiding in plain sight at the start of the verse.

> *Wa man yaʿmal **mina al-ṣāliḥāti**...*

In English we usually translate this: *"whoever does righteous deeds."* Clean. Functional. Forgettable.

But that is not what the Arabic says. The Arabic says: *whoever does **from** the righteous deeds.* *Mina al-ṣāliḥāt.*

[PAUSE]

Let me teach you a small piece of grammar before we go further, because this is one of those moments where a single preposition is doing more theological work than most full paragraphs.

In Arabic, the preposition *min* (مِن) has many uses. The two most relevant here are:
- *Min* of **origin** — "from somewhere"
- *Min* of **partition** — "some of, a portion of"

When *min* attaches to a definite plural — *mina al-ṣāliḥāt*, "of the righteous deeds" — classical grammarians like Ibn Hishām and al-Zamakhsharī generally read it as **partitive**. It means: *some of, a portion of, even a part of.*

Why does that matter?

Because Allah did not say *whoever does **all** the righteous deeds.* He did not say *whoever does **the** righteous deeds* in some completed, totalized sense. He said: *whoever does — even a portion — of the righteous deeds.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. We tend to imagine the believer who is safe on that Day as some imagined perfect Muslim — the one who never missed a fajr, never spoke an unkind word, never slipped. And we look at our own lives and we know we are not that person. So a quiet voice in the back of the heart starts to whisper: *this verse isn't about you.*

The partitive *min* is Allah closing that whisper.

He is not asking for the complete catalogue. He is asking that *something of the righteous deeds* be in your account. Some salah. Some kindness. Some effort. Some small act of faithfulness no one ever saw. Some dhikr in a parking lot at 3am. *Some.*

This is the same Allah who tells us elsewhere — *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it."* The grammar of His mercy is consistent across the whole book.

Stay with what this means. The doorway into the security of this verse is not high. It is **astonishingly low**. You do not have to be a saint to walk through it. You have to be the kind of person who has, somewhere in your life, **done some of the righteous deeds while believing**.

[PAUSE — major pause]

But Allah did not stop there. Because the partitive *min* is only the first half of the threshold. The second half is the small clause that follows it.

## Element Two: *Wa Huwa Muʾminun* — The Circumstantial Clause That Conditions Everything

> *...wa huwa muʾminun...*

> *...while he is a believer...*

[PAUSE]

This little phrase — three words in English, two in Arabic — is what classical Arabic grammar calls a **ḥāl** clause, a circumstantial clause. The *waw* at the front is the *waw al-ḥāl* — the *waw* of state. It tells you the **condition under which** the action of the main verb is happening.

Let me show you why that matters with a tiny everyday example.

If I say: *"He worked at the factory and he was tired,"* — those are two facts side by side.

But if I say: *"He worked at the factory **while he was the owner's son**,"* — now the second clause is reframing the first. The work he did is taking place inside a particular condition that changes what the work means.

That second sentence is a *ḥāl* construction. The *and* is no longer additive — it is conditional. *And* means *while*. The state colors everything.

Allah uses this construction here with extraordinary precision.

> *Wa man yaʿmal mina al-ṣāliḥāti **wa huwa muʾminun**.*
> *Whoever does of the righteous deeds **while he is a believer**.*

The deeds are not the standalone unit. The deeds done *inside the state of īmān* are the unit.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. *Muʾmin* here is an *ism fāʿil* — an active participle. In Arabic, an active participle in the indefinite often carries the sense of an **ongoing state**, not a one-time event. Not *he believed once and that was enough.* Not *he will believe later.* But *he is, in this moment, in the state of belief.*

So Allah is describing the kind of soul whose deeds count: the soul whose actions and faith are happening **at the same time, in the same body, as one continuous state**.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is where the verse opens up something that has been a quiet theological puzzle since the beginning of the religion.

Are you saved by works? Or by faith?

The Christian tradition has fought a five-hundred-year war over this question. Reformation. Counter-reformation. Pamphlets. Splits.

The Quran's grammar answers it differently. Not by choosing one over the other. By **fusing them through a single circumstantial clause**.

*Whoever does — of the righteous deeds — while he is a believer.*

The works are real. They have to happen. *Mina al-ṣāliḥāt.* But the works without the state of īmān are like a beautiful bowl with no bottom — they hold nothing, because the soil they grew in cannot hold them. And the īmān without works is a claim no one has been asked to verify yet.

Allah does not separate them. He does not weigh them against each other. He **stacks them** — works performed *while the heart is in faith* — and then declares the result.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this invites us to consider. We sometimes imagine the religion as a list of tasks to complete. *Pray. Fast. Give. Don't lie.* The list is real. But the *huwa muʾmin* clause is reminding us that the list is not where the person lives. The person lives in a **state**. The state is what makes the list mean anything.

Two people can pray the same salah. One does it in distracted habit while inwardly somewhere else. One does it *huwa muʾmin* — while he, right now, in the act, is a believer pointed toward his Lord. The motions look identical. Allah's grammar tells you they are not the same act.

[PAUSE — major pause]

We have crossed two thresholds now: *some of* the deeds, while *being* a believer. Allah has been gentle about the quantity and exact about the substrate. Now He turns to the promise itself. And here the language becomes surgical.

## Element Three: *Ẓulm* and *Haḍm* — The Two Directions of Injustice

> *...fa-lā yakhāfu **ẓulman wa lā haḍmā.***

> *...he will fear neither wrong added nor right eaten away.*

[PAUSE]

We need to slow down here. This is the heart of the verse, and the language demands the patience.

First, look at the structure. *Lā... wa lā...* — *neither... nor...* This is in Arabic an emphatic double negation. Not *he will not fear one or the other.* He will fear **neither one nor the other** — both possibilities are sealed off.

Second, look at the case. Both nouns are in the indefinite accusative — *ẓulman, haḍmā.* The indefinite is doing real work. He will not fear *any* injustice. Not a kind. Not an instance. Not a small one. Not a great one. Indefinite means: not a particle of either.

Third — and this is where everything happens — look at the **two nouns themselves**.

[PAUSE]

### *Ẓulm* — Wrong Added

You know this word. *Ẓulm.* It is one of the most common words in the Quran, and one of the most often translated as simply *injustice* or *oppression*.

But the Arabic root tells you something specific. *Ẓ-l-m* — the root from which we get *ẓulmah*, **darkness**. *Ẓulumāt* — *darknesses*, what Allah brings the believer out of. Why does the same root mean both *darkness* and *injustice*?

Classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib in the *Mufradāt*, Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab* — explain it like this: *ẓulm* is **putting something where it does not belong.** Darkness is the absence of light placed where light should be. *Ẓulm* is wrong placed where right should be. The act of *ẓulm* is the act of **adding** something to the wrong place — a charge to an innocent ledger, a punishment to one who did not earn it, a sin's weight to one who did not commit it.

So when the verse says *fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman* — *he will fear no wrong* — the specific image is: he will not fear that **anything will be added** to his account that he did not earn. No false accusation. No mistakenly assigned sin. No darkness placed where his light should be.

[PAUSE]

This is the first fear — and it is the fear that earlier ayahs of this same surah have been preparing us for. Just one verse before, in 111 — *"and whoever carries *ẓulm* has indeed failed."* Allah used the same word. The one who *carries* his own wrong fails. The one who *does not commit* wrong does not need to worry that wrong will be **placed on him**. The two ayahs are mirror images. The first describes the soul who comes to the Day already loaded. The second describes the soul who comes empty of that load — and is told: nothing will be loaded onto you against your will either.

This alone would be a stunning promise. It is not the whole promise.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### *Haḍm* — Right Eaten Away

Now we come to the word that does work no other word in Arabic could do.

*Haḍm.* Root *h-ḍ-m.*

In classical Arabic — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib, the early lexicons all converge on this — the primary, physical, concrete meaning of *haḍama* is **to digest**. *Haḍama al-ṭaʿām* — he digested the food. The stomach's slow, invisible breakdown of what was eaten. Food turning into nothing you can see.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that image, because the metaphor is the whole point.

From there the word extends. *Haḍama lahu min ḥaqqihi* — he diminished his right; he ate away at what was due to him. *Haḍm al-ḥaqq* in classical legal language means: the **slow erosion of someone's rightful claim.** Not the violent seizure. The quiet digestion.

Do you feel the difference between this and *ẓulm*?

*Ẓulm* is something **added**. A weight placed on a scale that was not yours. A wrong put where right was.

*Haḍm* is something **subtracted**. A weight quietly **taken from** a scale that was full. A right slowly digested down to less than what it was.

[PAUSE]

These are the two — and only two — directions injustice can move. Either something wrong is added, or something right is taken away. There is no third direction. Allah named both. He sealed both.

Now read the verse again with this in your hand:

> *He will fear neither wrong added nor right eaten away.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

Here is what this invites us to consider, and it is heavier than it first appears.

We can imagine a system that prevents *ẓulm.* That is a high bar but a familiar one. *No false charges. No fabricated accusations. No one will be hurt by something they didn't do.* Most legal systems aspire to this and almost none achieve it. But the concept is intelligible.

What no human system has ever achieved — what no human system **could** ever achieve — is to also seal off *haḍm.* Because *haḍm* is the slow leak. The forgotten favor. The kindness no one saw. The night prayer no human being witnessed. The patience that was real but invisible. *Haḍm* is the steady erosion of what was actually owed to you because no one was watching closely enough to remember.

Every employee has felt *haḍm.* Every parent has felt *haḍm.* Every quiet servant of God who never told anyone what they did has feared *haḍm.* The fear is: *the one thing I did, the one thing I held onto when no one knew — will it survive into my final account, or will it have been digested away by then?*

Allah's answer is one word. *Wa lā haḍmā.* **Not even an atom of it will be eaten away.**

[PAUSE]

There is a sonic dimension here that is not decorative. *Ẓulman, haḍmā.* The two words rhyme. They both end in *-man*. The two heaviest letters in the language — the *ẓā* of darkness and the *ḥāʾ* with its sister sound *ḍād* — sit at the front of each. They are heavy, slow, weighted sounds. The mouth has to work to say them. They feel like injustice feels — like something pressing down. Allah took the two heaviest sound-shapes and parked them next to each other so that even the **ear** of someone who didn't know Arabic could feel: *these are the two things that are now removed.*

[PAUSE]

And one more grammatical fact to seal this. Both *ẓulman* and *haḍmā* are **maṣādir** — verbal nouns. Verbal nouns in Arabic, when used indefinitely like this, often denote the **abstract reality of the act itself**. Not just a particular instance. The very thing. Allah is saying: not a wrong-as-such will reach him; not a digestion-of-right-as-such will touch him. The very category is sealed.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### The Bridge Between Linguistic and Thematic

So here is what the language has placed in our hands.

A man — even a woman with the smallest record of righteousness — even a soul whose deeds were merely *some of* the righteous list — provided that those deeds were done while she was, *at the time*, a believer — will, on the Day faces are humbled before the Living and Self-Subsisting, fear neither **wrong added nor right eaten away**.

The threshold is low. The substrate is everything. The result is total security in the only courtroom that finally matters.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of justice itself, and about the two fears that quietly run a human life.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Two Fears That Govern Every Honest Life

You have lived this verse before you ever read it.

Think back to the last time you were waiting for some authority — a manager, a teacher, a tax office, an immigration officer — to render a verdict on your life or your work. The waiting feels like a particular kind of dread. If you sit with that dread, if you press into it, you will find it is not one feeling. It is two feelings braided together.

The first is the fear: *what if I am charged with something that was not mine?* What if a coworker's mistake gets attached to my name? What if a missing form they lost makes me look like I never filed? What if someone is in the room saying things about me I will never have the chance to answer?

The second is the fear: *what if what was actually mine doesn't show up?* What if all the late nights I worked don't appear in their records? What if the kindnesses I did to colleagues are forgotten because I never asked anyone to notice? What if my real contribution gets shaved down by the time it reaches the page?

These are the **same two fears** the verse is naming. *Ẓulm* — wrong added. *Haḍm* — right eaten away.

[PAUSE]

The honest soul lives between these two anxieties for their whole life. And the religious soul carries them with even more weight, because the stakes are infinite. *What if I am charged on the Day with something I never did? What if all the small good I did when no one was watching gets digested into nothing before I arrive?*

Many believers do not name these fears clearly. But they show up in our spiritual life in disguise.

The fear of *ẓulm* often disguises itself as **scrupulosity**. The constant nagging worry — *did I just commit a sin? Was that thought a sin? Will that be on my record?* — is, at its root, a fear that *something will be added* that I did not knowingly do.

The fear of *haḍm* often disguises itself as **despair**. The voice that says — *what's the point of this small effort, no one sees it, it doesn't even count* — is, at its root, the fear that what I do *will be eaten away* before it ever reaches the scale.

Allah, in this single ayah, addresses **both at once**. He does not condescend to either anxiety. He does not say *don't worry about it* in the dismissive way human comfort sometimes does. He uses two precise words for two precise fears and tells the believer who lives in *huwa muʾmin* — both are removed. **Sealed off. Indefinite. Not an atom of either.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what this should change in us, practically.

The next time you catch yourself in the spiral of *did that thing I did three years ago count against me, did this thought just register as sin, am I being recorded as something I'm not* — pause and remember: *fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman.* Allah will not allow a wrong to be **placed** on you that you did not commit. Not by an angel's mistake. Not by a forgotten file. Not by anything. The Just is also the Precise. *Ẓulm* — additions of darkness where you did not earn it — is sealed at the door.

And the next time you catch yourself in the deeper spiral of *the small thing I did that no one saw, the dhikr I made driving to work, the sadaqah I dropped without telling anyone, the patient silence I held when I wanted to lash out — none of that will count, none of that will survive* — pause and remember: *wa lā haḍmā.* Allah will not allow what was rightly yours to be **digested**. Not by time. Not by forgetting. Not by anyone's failure to witness. He saw. He recorded. He will return it whole.

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is what the *ḥafīẓ* in *Allah ḥafīẓun ʿalayhim* means. This is what the *raqīb* means. This is what *al-Khabīr* means. The names are not abstract decorations. They are the ontological guarantee behind the promise of this single ayah. The deeds you did *while you were a believer* are kept by the One whose names include *the Preserver, the Watching, the Aware.* They cannot be eaten away. The system prevents it.

[PAUSE]

So let me put one specific application in your hand before we move to the second theme.

Tonight, before you sleep, do **one small righteous act** that **no one will know about**. Not for the sake of being seen, even by yourself. Just one. A small charity given anonymously. A duʿāʾ for someone who has hurt you. A dhikr made silently while you wash dishes. A nightlight left on for a tired roommate.

Then say to yourself: *that is now in the account of the One who does not allow wrong to be added or right to be eaten away.*

The economy of that act is different from the economy of every other act you do all day. You did not do it for human eyes; therefore *haḍm* — the slow erosion of what depends on being witnessed by humans — does not touch it. Its preservation depends on Allah's *ḥifẓ*, and Allah's *ḥifẓ* never fails.

This is what it means to live, even now, inside the security of ayah 112.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: Faith as the Substrate That Makes Deeds Count

We sit with the second theme now, and it is the harder one.

Why did Allah pair the works with *huwa muʾmin*? Why insist on the circumstantial clause? Why not just say: *whoever does righteous deeds will fear neither ẓulm nor haḍm*?

[PAUSE]

To answer this honestly we have to walk into something uncomfortable. We have to ask: *what happens to the good a person does without faith?*

This is one of the hardest questions for the modern believer to sit with — especially the believer raised in a pluralistic society, who has friends and colleagues and beloved relatives who do beautiful things, who give to charity, who care for their parents, who never lie, but who have not — at least visibly — entered into the state Allah is naming with the word *muʾmin*.

Different scholars have engaged this question across the centuries with care, and we are not going to resolve it tonight. But the verse, if we let its grammar speak, is making one specific claim that we should hear clearly.

It is saying: the **state of īmān is the soil that allows a deed to retain its weight on the Day.**

[PAUSE]

Let me build the analogy carefully, because this is one of those places where if we do not work patiently, we end up either too harsh or too vague.

Imagine you grow tomatoes in a garden. You can grow tomatoes. They look like tomatoes. They taste like tomatoes. But if your soil has no minerals — no nitrogen, no calcium, no roots-deep nutrient base — those tomatoes will wither in the cold. The plant produced a fruit. The fruit was real. But the fruit has no system behind it that can hold it through the winter.

A deed performed *huwa muʾmin* is a tomato grown in living soil. The fruit and the system are connected. When the cold of the Day arrives, the system *holds* the fruit. The roots reach down into the substrate of īmān, and the substrate is itself rooted in the One who keeps what is His.

A deed performed *not* in that state is a fruit without a system. It still happened. It is still good. We are not allowed, as Muslims, to say it was nothing — Allah will reward worldly good with worldly response, as He says in many places. But the system that *carries* deeds across the threshold of death is the system of īmān. Without that system, the deed has nowhere to be carried to.

[PAUSE]

This is why the *waw al-ḥāl* in our verse matters so much. Allah did not say: *whoever does righteous deeds and also believes.* As if these were two items on a checklist. He said: *whoever does the deeds **while in the state of belief**.* The deeds and the state are happening **in the same continuous breath**.

The grammar is teaching us a metaphysics. **Faith is not an item alongside works. Faith is the medium in which works become permanent.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

There is a quiet but important corollary here for those of us who *are* believers.

Sometimes we worry: *I did this good thing, but my heart was not fully present. Was I really 'a believer' in the moment? Did this deed make it through?*

The verse uses *muʾmin* — the active participle — which in Arabic indicates an enduring state, not a perfect moment-by-moment intensity. You are, on the whole, a person who believes. You are, in the substrate of your life, oriented to your Lord. You may have been distracted; you may have been tired; you may have been a hundred other things. But the **state** was there. The participle held.

The verse is more merciful than we often imagine. It is not asking *was every neuron firing in iman the moment you gave that sadaqah?* It is asking *were you, in your life, a person of īmān when this happened?*

If yes — the deed enters the substrate. The substrate is held. The Day arrives. *Lā ẓulm wa lā haḍm.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the practical application, and I want you to feel it.

If īmān is the substrate that lets deeds count, then the **most important investment of your life** is not adding more deeds. It is **deepening the soil**.

A garden with rich soil and few plants will, over time, fill in. A garden with poor soil and many plants will, over time, hollow out.

We tend to tally our deeds and despair at how few there are. The verse tells us: *some* of the righteous deeds is enough — provided the soil is right. Tend the soil. Tend the *huwa muʾmin*. Tend the daily, repeated, slightly-boring practice of orienting your heart to your Lord. The deeds will grow up out of that soil naturally, and they will be of the kind that *cannot be eaten away* on that Day, because the soil they came out of was never going to be digested. The soil of īmān is held by *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm* — the Living, the Self-Subsisting.

That is the same name Allah used in the verse just before this one. Ayah 111 ends: *faces are humbled before al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm.* Ayah 112 begins: *whoever does of the righteous deeds while he is a believer.*

The faces that bow are bowing before the One whose Life and Self-Sufficiency are the only thing capable of preserving any deed against any erosion. The believer's deeds are held by the very names being honored at that moment. **The justice and the names are the same justice and the same names.**

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architectural Reveal

Now look back at the verse with the whole journey in your hand:

> *Wa man yaʿmal mina al-ṣāliḥāti — wa huwa muʾminun — fa-lā yakhāfu ẓulman wa lā haḍmā.*

There is a structure here we could not see at the start. Watch what Allah has done.

He gave us a **threshold** — *some of the righteous deeds.* (Low.)
He gave us a **substrate** — *while he is a believer.* (Total.)
He gave us a **promise** — *no wrong added, no right eaten away.* (Sealed in both directions.)

The verse is built like a doorway. The threshold low so anyone can step over. The lintel — *huwa muʾmin* — the load-bearing beam that holds the whole architecture. And the room you walk into has its two opposite walls sealed: nothing can come in from the *ẓulm* side, nothing can leak out from the *haḍm* side.

[PAUSE]

The doorway is also the answer to the verse before it. Ayah 111 said: *whoever carries ẓulm has indeed failed.* Ayah 112 says: *whoever does ṣāliḥāt while believing — no ẓulm and no haḍm will reach him.*

The structure is mirror-perfect. Carrying *ẓulm* into the Day = failure. Receiving no *ẓulm* on the Day = security. Allah used the same word in adjacent verses to point you toward two opposite outcomes that depend entirely on **what is in your hand when you arrive**.

This is not coincidence. This is composition.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight in two voices.

The linguistic: a partitive *min* + a circumstantial *ḥāl* + a double-negation indefinite accusative. Three grammatical structures, each doing surgical work — *some* of the deeds, *while* in faith, *no* wrong-adding and *no* right-erasing.

The thematic: a low threshold, a deep substrate, and a sealed verdict. The two great fears of human life — being charged with what wasn't yours and having what was yours quietly taken — are named by their precise Arabic names and removed by their precise Arabic negations.

[PAUSE]

What the language is telling you is what your life has been telling you — that justice is harder than the world admits. That just because nothing has been added does not mean nothing has been subtracted. That the small good you did in the dark is exactly the kind of good most likely to be lost in any human accounting.

And what the verse is promising you is that the accounting that finally matters is **not human accounting**. It is the accounting of the One whose name is *al-Ḥayy* — Living, so He never lapses into forgetting; *al-Qayyūm* — Self-Subsisting, so He needs no record-keeper, no witness, no archive other than Himself; *al-Ḥafīẓ* — the Preserver, the One whose preservation does not erode; *al-ʿAdl* — the Just, who places no wrong where wrong was not, and removes no right where right was.

Under that accounting, the deed you did at 3am that no one saw is **as preserved** as the one you announced from a minbar. The dhikr you whispered driving home is **as recorded** as the night prayer at the Kaʿbah. The patience you held in your home when no one was watching is **as weighted** as the patience of any prophet — measured by what it was, not by who saw it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Questions to Carry

1. When you imagine standing on the Day, which of the two fears is louder in you — *ẓulm* (something will be charged that wasn't mine) or *haḍm* (something I did won't be remembered)? Sit with which one. It tells you something about where your heart lives.

2. Where in your life are you currently performing deeds **without the substrate** — going through motions that have lost their *huwa muʾmin*? What would it look like to bring the state back, even into one of those motions tomorrow?

3. What is one small righteous act in your life that no one — no one — has ever witnessed? Can you let it be? Can you trust that it is held by the One whose name is *al-Ḥafīẓ* and not need to ever speak it aloud?

4. If the partitive *min* makes the threshold low, what is the smallest, most accessible *ṣāliḥah* you have been postponing because you imagined the bar was higher than it is?

5. The verse before this one — *whoever carries ẓulm has indeed failed* — and this one — *no ẓulm will reach the one who comes righteous and believing* — together form the entire spectrum of the Day. Which of these two doors are you, by your daily life, walking toward?

## The Closing Invitation

This week, do not try to do more.

Do, instead, **two small things**.

The first: a deed you do **only for Allah**, that no human eye will see, that you will not later mention. Place it deliberately into the account that this verse describes. Watch yourself want to tell someone about it, and choose not to. Notice that the deed itself becomes lighter and stranger and more luminous when it is hidden.

The second: when the spiral comes — the spiral of *did I just commit a sin, was that thought a sin, will I be charged with something I didn't do, will what I did be remembered* — answer the spiral with one Arabic phrase. *Lā ẓulm wa lā haḍm.* Not as a magic word. As a reminder of who is doing the accounting and what He has promised.

That is enough for this week.
