# The Word That Held Back the Verdict and the Tasbih That Holds You Together

## Surah Ta-Ha 129–130

> **وَلَوْلَا كَلِمَةٌ سَبَقَتْ مِن رَّبِّكَ لَكَانَ لِزَامًا وَأَجَلٌ مُّسَمًّى ۚ فَاصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ الشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ غُرُوبِهَا ۖ وَمِنْ آنَاءِ اللَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْ وَأَطْرَافَ النَّهَارِ لَعَلَّكَ تَرْضَىٰ**

> *Wa lawlā kalimatun sabaqat min rabbika la-kāna lizāman wa ajalun musamman. Faṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn, wa sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika qabla ṭulūʿi'sh-shamsi wa qabla ghurūbihā, wa min ānāʾi'l-layli fa-sabbiḥ, wa aṭrāfa'n-nahāri laʿallaka tarḍā.*

> "And were it not for a word that already went forth from your Lord, and a named term — [the verdict] would have been binding. So be patient over what they say, and exalt the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting; and during the watches of the night exalt Him, and at the edges of the day — that you yourself may, perhaps, become pleased."

---

## Introduction: The Voice That Was Going Hoarse

Let's place ourselves where these words were first heard.

It is late in the Meccan period. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling for years. Years of standing in the marketplace and being mocked — *sāḥir*, *majnūn*, *shāʿir*, the three arrows the deniers kept reaching for: sorcerer, madman, poet. Each label was a category the Arab world recognized and dismissed, and they were aimed at the one who had brought them something none of their categories could contain. Years of walking through the streets and having dust thrown on his head. Years of watching his small community — Bilāl pinned under a rock, Sumayya killed with a spear, Yāsir dying in the sand — pay the price for what he is teaching them. The opposition has not softened. If anything, it has hardened.

And in this Meccan moment, a question is pulsing through the heart of every believer, the same question that has pulsed through the heart of every believer who has ever stood in front of an injustice and watched it not be corrected: **when?** When does the verdict come? When does Allah finally settle the account against those who are crushing us? When does the silence of the sky finally break?

The whole of Surah Ta-Ha has been an answer to that question, but answered slantwise — through the story of someone else. Musa عليه السلام also asked it. Musa stood in front of Pharaoh and saw the children of Israel beaten, killed, drowned, conscripted, and the verdict against Pharaoh did not come immediately. Musa waited. Through the staff and the snake. Through plague after plague. Through the negotiation, the treachery, the chase. The verdict came — but only at the appointed hour.

Now the surah turns back to its primary addressee. After eighty-some verses of Musa, the camera pulls back. The same voice that consoled Musa is now speaking directly to Muḥammad ﷺ. And it says something extraordinary.

It says: *the verdict you are waiting for has not arrived not because it is undeserved. It would be deserved. It would have arrived already. It would have arrived **bound to them, inseparable from them, gripping them with no exit.** **Were it not for a word.***

[PAUSE]

A word that already went forth. A word that *preceded* the offense. A word older than the sin it now restrains.

What word? Allah does not specify. He leaves it open. And when Allah leaves something open in the Quran, the openness is the meaning.

Then, having revealed why the silence of the sky is not absence but active restraint, He gives His Messenger — and us — the only thing that will hold a heart together while it waits. Not a strategy. Not a counter-attack. A rhythm. A schedule. A precisely timed pulse of remembrance, mapped onto the joints of the day itself: before sunrise, before sunset, in the watches of the night, at the edges of day. Five points. Five hinges. Five places where the heart can be reset. And the classical commentators — al-Tabari and al-Muyassar among them — recognize in these five markers the exact contours of the canonical prayers: Fajr before the sun rises, Asr before it sets, Isha in the watches of the night, and Zuhr together with Maghrib at the day's edges. The *tasbih* being prescribed in this Meccan moment is not informal or incidental; it is already tracing the architecture of what would become the five daily prayers, mapped onto the hours Allah Himself named.

And then, at the very end of the verse — a phrase that should make us stop breathing for a moment.

*Laʿallaka tarḍā.* "That **you** may be pleased."

Not 'that He may be pleased with you.' Not 'that I may forgive you.' That **you** — you, the one waiting, the one being mocked, the one whose voice is going hoarse — that **you** may finally arrive at a place of contentment.

Classical commentators hear a promise of divine recompense in this phrase, not merely the natural peace that dhikr produces. Al-Muyassar reads *laʿallaka tarḍā* as the tasbih earning you a reward: through these prescribed moments of remembrance, you will be given *what* pleases you, as recompense for them. The contentment is not only the inner quiet that flows from remembrance itself — it is something Allah actively bestows in exchange for the discipline of showing up at the day's five hinges. Both readings live in the tradition: one says the act produces the peace, the other says the act earns it. Either way, the peace is real. Either way, it is *yours*.

Stay with that ending. We will spend a long time with it before we are done. Because what Allah is telling His Messenger here is something almost no human authority has ever told a person under siege: **I am building this rhythm not for My satisfaction. I am building it for yours.**

Let's open it slowly.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### Element 1: *Kalimatun Sabaqat* — The Unspecified Word That Got There First

Before we open the verse, let's understand a feature of Arabic that carries everything in this opening line.

In English, when you say "a word" versus "the word," you mean either some unspecified word or a particular known word. The article does the work. In Arabic, the same distinction exists — *al-kalimah* (the word) vs. *kalimah* (a word, with the indefinite *tanwīn* on the end: *kalimatun*) — but the indefinite carries something English does not. In Arabic rhetoric, when a noun is left indefinite in a context where you would expect specification, the indefiniteness almost always carries one of two meanings: either **the thing is unknowable**, or **the thing is so vast, so weighty, that no single specification could contain it.**

A simple everyday example: in Arabic, if someone tells you "I met a man yesterday" and leaves the man indefinite where you would expect a name, the indefiniteness itself communicates *and what a man he was.* The vagueness, in context, becomes magnification.

Hold that in mind. Now look at what Allah says.

> **وَلَوْلَا كَلِمَةٌ سَبَقَتْ مِن رَّبِّكَ**
> *Wa lawlā kalimatun sabaqat min rabbika*
> "And were it not for **a word** that already went forth from your Lord…"

Notice. *Kalimatun.* Indefinite. *A* word. Not *the* word. Not a named decree. Not a specified covenant. Just — a word. From your Lord.

What word?

Let's sit with that question. Allah does not answer it inside the verse. He leaves it suspended. And the classical scholars, looking at this gap, did not pick one answer and shut down the others. They built a composite. Each opinion is a different camera angle on the same divine restraint.

One angle: the word is what Allah inscribed for Himself, recorded in the ḥadīth qudsī — *inna raḥmatī sabaqat ghaḍabī*, "indeed My mercy went forth ahead of My wrath." The same verb. *Sabaqat.* It got there first. Before any human committed any sin worthy of wrath, mercy had already taken its position in front of that wrath. It is standing in the way.

Another angle: the word is the principle Allah states elsewhere in the Quran — that He does not destroy a community until He has sent them a Messenger and given them time to respond. The word is the ethics of warning. The word is the rule that *no verdict falls on anyone who has not been addressed.*

Another angle: the word is the specific decree that the punishment of the deniers among the Prophet's people would be deferred to the Day of Judgment, with only partial reckoning in this life — itself a form of mercy, because it leaves the door of repentance open until the very last breath.

Another angle still: the word is the governing principle behind the *ajalun musamman* — the named term — that Allah goes on to mention in the very next phrase. Al-Tabari reads it this way: the *kalimatun sabaqat* is Allah's own prior decree that for everyone for whom He has assigned a term, He will not cut that term short before they reach it. The 'word' and the 'named term' are not two separate walls standing side by side; they are the same commitment seen from two directions. The word is the guarantee that the named term will be honored. Together they say one thing: *the end is fixed, and it will not be moved forward.*

Notice — these are not four competing answers. They are four facets of one truth. The word that *sabaqat*, that "got there first," is the entire architecture of divine forbearance, of which mercy-precedes-wrath, the necessity of warning, the deferral to Judgment Day, and the immovability of the named term are all expressions.

Here is what is remarkable. Allah is not saying *I have decided to be merciful right now in your case.* He is saying something deeper. He is saying: **before the offense was even committed, the structure of restraint was already in place.** The word is older than the sin it holds back.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with the implication of this.

When the Prophet ﷺ — or you, or any believer waiting on a verdict that has not come — looks at the silence of the sky and asks *why is nothing happening?*, Allah is not answering *because I am too slow.* He is not answering *because I have not yet decided.* He is answering: **because before any of this began, I wrote a word. And that word is doing its work right now. The reason nothing is happening is that something is happening. Mercy is standing in the place where the verdict would have stood. Warning is standing in the place where destruction would have stood. A named term is standing in the place where instant judgment would have stood. The silence of the sky is not empty. It is full of the word.**

This is the first move of the verse, and it is one of the most consoling things Allah has ever said to a human heart.

The verb chosen here makes the consolation even more precise. *Sabaqat* — from the root *s-b-q*, to outrun, to outpace, to get there first. This is a racing verb. In Arabic, *sabaqa* is what a horse does when it crosses the finish line ahead of the others. Allah is using a verb of speed and priority. The word didn't merely exist before the sin; it **outran** the sin to the place where the verdict was supposed to fall. It got there first and took the spot.

Picture it almost cinematically. The sin happens. The angels move toward it with the verdict. They arrive — and the word is already there, holding the position. The verdict cannot be delivered, because mercy has the seat.

That is what your life is right now. That is what every silent moment after every secret sin has been. That is why the floor did not split open the moment you spoke that word, the moment you nursed that grudge, the moment you turned your back on the prayer. **The word got there first.**

And Allah, having revealed this, then names the second restraint.

> **وَأَجَلٌ مُّسَمًّى**
> *wa ajalun musamman*
> "and a named term."

*Ajalun* — a term, an appointed period, a span of time with an end. *Musamman* — named, given a name, specified. From the same root as *ism*, name. The named term. Time itself has been given a name by Allah. The end of the matter has already been written down with a specific designation. Not "soon." Not "eventually." A *named* term.

This is the second wall. Even if mercy were to step aside — *ajalun musamman* would still be there. The clock has been pre-set. The bell has been pre-tuned. The day has been pre-named. Nothing about your waiting is open-ended in the cosmic sense. It is bounded. It is finite. It has a name in the Book that you do not yet know how to pronounce.

What this combination does to the heart is enormous. The first wall — *kalimatun sabaqat* — assures you that the silence is not abandonment. The second wall — *ajalun musamman* — assures you that the silence is not infinite. You are held between two assurances: **you have not been left, and you will not be left forever.** Both walls are made of words. Allah's words. Words that pre-existed your suffering and that name the moment of its end.

Now look at what would have happened without these two walls.

> **لَكَانَ لِزَامًا**
> *la-kāna lizāman*
> "[the verdict] would have been *lizāman.*"

We need to slow down on this word.

### Element 2: *Lizāman* — The Punishment That Sticks

Let's teach the root before we open the word.

The Arabic root *l-z-m* carries one core image: **to attach, to cling, to be inseparable from something.** The classical lexicons illustrate it with the metaphor of two things glued together such that they cannot be pulled apart. *Lazima'sh-shayʾu'sh-shayʾa* — one thing has clung to the other so tightly that they are now one. From this root come words for *obligation*, because an obligation is something that has glued itself to your responsibility and cannot be peeled off; words for *insistence*, because an insistent person is one who has clung; and words for *creditor*, because a creditor is one whose claim is glued to you until paid.

Now consider what Allah says the verdict would have been if mercy and the named term had not held it back.

*La-kāna lizāman.* It would have been *lizāman.* It would have **stuck**. It would have **gripped**. It would have been the kind of punishment that, once it landed, **could not be peeled off.**

Al-Tabari's gloss opens this further: the destruction would have clung to them *ʿājilan* — immediately, without interval or pause. That single adverb does quiet but enormous work. Notice that *ʿājil* (what is immediate, what admits no deferment) and *ajal* (the appointed term, the deferred horizon) share the same letters and root, only pointing in opposite directions. This is the contrast the verse is built around. The *kalimatun sabaqat* and the *ajalun musamman* together accomplish one precise conversion: they transform what would have been *ʿājil* destruction — instant, unannounced, irremovable — into an *ajal*, a named future. They relocate the verdict from the column of the immediate to the column of the appointed. That is the shape of divine forbearance: not canceling what the deniers deserve, but moving it to a horizon that is fixed, named, and not yet arrived.

Al-Tabari also observes that *lizāman* is a *maṣdar* — a verbal noun — from the verb *lāzama* (*yulāzimuhu*: he did not leave him, did not part from him). It is not simply an adjective describing the punishment from the outside. The punishment *would be* the clinging itself, nominalized and total. There is no version of that verdict that eventually lifts.

It clings to your ear the way the verdict it describes would cling to its target. This is not arbitrary.
