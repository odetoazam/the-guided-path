# The Singular Suffering: Why Allah Said "You" (One) When He Meant "You" (Two)

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:20:117] فَقُلْنَا يَـٰٓـَٔادَمُ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا عَدُوٌّ لَّكَ وَلِزَوْجِكَ فَلَا يُخْرِجَنَّكُمَا مِنَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ فَتَشْقَىٰٓ

**Transliteration:**
> *Fa-qulnā yā-Ādamu inna hādhā ʿaduwwun laka wa-li-zawjika fa-lā yukhrijannakumā mina-l-jannati fa-tashqā.*

**Translation:**
> "So We said, 'O Adam, indeed this is an enemy to you and to your spouse. So let him not drive you both out of the Garden — for then you would suffer.'"

---

## Rebuilding the World

Take a breath and step back. Surah Ta-Ha is, on its surface, the surah of Musa. The story of Musa occupies the heart of it — the burning bush, the staff, Pharaoh, the magicians, the sea, the wandering, the calf. By the time we arrive at ayah 117, we have walked with Musa through most of his prophetic life.

And then — without warning — Allah pulls the camera all the way back. Past Musa. Past every prophet. Past every nation. Back to the very first conversation. Back to the moment **before** there was any human history at all.

Why?

Because Surah Ta-Ha is teaching us something about the structure of the human condition itself. Every story it told us about Musa — the temptation, the forgetting, the calf, the fall and recovery — was a fractal. A small version of the original pattern. And the original pattern starts here, with Allah speaking to Adam.

This ayah comes immediately after Allah commands the angels to prostrate to Adam, and Iblis refuses. So picture the scene: the angels are still in the act of prostration. The newly-honored Adam stands before his Lord. And in the corner — refusing, simmering, marked by his refusal — stands Iblis.

Allah turns to Adam and gives him **the first warning ever given to a human being.** [PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir, drawing on Ibn Abbas, notes that the name *al-insān* — the human — has been classically linked to forgetting: *the creature who was given the covenant and forgot it.* The warning in this ayah comes against that backdrop. Allah is giving the reminder to the one He already knows will need it most. Every word of what follows is therefore an act of pre-emptive mercy.

Listen carefully to what that warning is. It is not what you would expect.

---

## The Hook

Allah is going to warn Adam about the consequences of being expelled from the Garden. He has every word in the divine vocabulary at His disposal. He could say: *for then you would die.* He could say: *for then you would be punished.* He could say: *for then you would be lost.*

He does not say any of these.

He says — *fa-tashqā.* "For then you would suffer." But the word He uses for "suffer" is one of the strangest, richest, most uncomfortable words in the Quran. And — this is the part that should make us sit up — He addresses it to **Adam alone.** Not to Adam-and-his-wife, even though He just mentioned her. Not to "you both." To **Adam. Singular.**

Why? What is being said in that singular?

That is the door we are about to walk through.

But first, note how al-Tabari's commentary opens: the declaration *"this is an enemy to you"* is not merely a warning — it is an **explanation** rooted in what Adam has just witnessed. *Because of his enmity for you, he refused to prostrate.* The refusal to prostrate was not a separate matter; it was the declaration of the enmity, already made, already visible. Allah is not asking Adam to believe in an abstract threat on faith alone. He is interpreting, for Adam, the meaning of the event that Adam just watched unfold in front of him. The enemy has already shown his hand. This word — *ʿaduww* — is the name for what that refusal was.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Pronoun Choreography — Singular, Dual, Singular

### Teaching the concept first

Arabic does something English cannot. Where English has only one form of "you" — whether you're addressing one person or twenty — Arabic distinguishes precisely. Singular. Dual (a special form for exactly two). Plural. The grammar tracks **how many people the speaker is looking at** in any given sentence.

This means in Arabic, every time the speaker shifts the number of the "you" — that shift is visible. It cannot be hidden. It is a deliberate move on a chessboard where every piece is counted.

Now hold that in mind, and watch what happens in this single ayah.

### Opening the structure

Look at how Allah's address to Adam moves:

> *Yā-Ādamu* — **"O Adam"** — singular vocative.

> *inna hādhā ʿaduwwun **laka** wa-**li-zawjika*** — "indeed this is an enemy **to you (singular)** and **to your (singular) spouse**."

> *fa-lā yukhrijanna**kumā*** — "so let him not drive **you both (dual)** out."

> *fa-**tashqā*** — "for then **you (singular)** would suffer."

Let's sit with this. Allah begins addressing Adam alone. Then He widens — He brings in the spouse. Then He addresses them as a pair. And then, in the final word of the warning, **He narrows back to Adam alone.**

This is not a stylistic accident. In Arabic, you could very easily have said *fa-tashqayā* — "for then **you both** would suffer." It would scan, it would fit, it would rhyme with the surrounding text. Allah did not say it.

He said *tashqā.* Singular. **You — Adam — would suffer.**

### Deepening

Notice something. The expulsion is dual: *yukhrijannakumā* — he drives **both** of you out. The enemy is enemy to both of you: *laka wa-li-zawjika.* But the **suffering** that results from the expulsion — Allah locates that on Adam.

Al-Tabari reads the singular directly: the *tashqā* falls on Adam because *"your living will come from the toil of your hands."* The responsibility of provision — of wresting sustenance from a resistant earth — lands representatively on him. Others have understood the singular as Adam standing for the human species named in its representative, so the "you" is not "Adam the man" as opposed to "Hawwa the woman" but the human being as such, addressed in the one from whom it descended.

Here's what's remarkable: both readings converge on the same point. Whether you read *tashqā* as "Adam personally bears the labor" or as "humanity, named Adam, will be the suffering one" — the result is identical. **The state of being outside the Garden is the state of laboring.**

The expulsion is the event. The suffering is **what life becomes** after the event. And Allah, in His mercy and precision, separates them grammatically. The expulsion is something that happens to *both.* The shaqā — the laboring, the wretchedness — is the **ongoing condition** that defines the post-Garden human being. [PAUSE]

### What this single feature reveals

The pronoun choreography is teaching us this: **expulsion is a moment, but suffering is a mode of existence.** Allah did not warn Adam about the moment of leaving. He warned him about the **state he would enter** — and that state, that mode, is named with one word, in the singular, applied to the human as such.

You are not warned about an event. You are warned about a way of being.

## Element Two: The Word *Tashqā* — Labor and Wretchedness Fused

### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, words often hold what English would split into two. The English language separates "labor" (physical work, exertion) from "misery" (emotional anguish). They feel like different categories to us. One is what your hands do. The other is what your heart feels.

Arabic, in many of its oldest words, refuses this split. The root **ش-ق-ي** (sh-q-y) is one of the most stunning examples. It does not mean "labor." It does not mean "misery." It means — *both at once, as one indivisible reality.*

To do *shaqā* is to **labor under hardship in a way that wears down the soul.** It is what an ox does pulling a plough through hard ground. It is what a worker does at a job that grinds them. It is what a farmer does in drought. The word fuses **what your body does** and **what it costs you** into a single concept.

Hold that in mind. Because this is the word Allah chose.

### Opening the word

> *fa-tashqā*

Allah did not say *fa-taʾlam* — "you would suffer pain." He did not say *fa-tahlik* — "you would perish." He did not say *fa-takūnu min al-khāsirīn* — "you would be among the losers."

He said: *tashqā.* From the root that means **to labor in a way that breaks you.**

### Deepening

Stay with me. Think about what the Garden actually was. The Garden was the place where things were given. Not earned. Not labored for. Not extracted from resistant soil. **Given.**

In the Garden, Adam did not need to plant the fruit, irrigate it, weed around it, fight off pests, harvest it before it spoiled. He stretched out his hand. The fruit was there.

So when Allah names what is lost by leaving the Garden — He names it precisely. Not "blessing." Not "ease." Not "happiness." He names it with the verb that captures **the entire economic and existential reality of being outside.** The reality where everything must be wrested from a world that does not want to give it up.

You wake up early. You commute. You strain. You provide. You build something and a storm tears it down. You earn money and inflation eats it. You feed a family and the work never ends. You age and your body declines. You die and your children inherit the same labor.

That is *shaqā.*

Al-Tabari preserves a narration that gives this word its most concrete image: after the fall, a red bull was sent down to Adam. He would plow the earth with it, wiping the sweat from his brow. The commentator notes simply: *that was his shaqā.* There it is — no theology, no abstraction. An old man and a bull and hard ground. The first labor. The original occupation of the father of humanity. Al-Tabari is saying: you want to know what *fa-tashqā* looks like? It looks like that. Every difficult morning you have ever had was already in that image.

And here is the part that should stop us: the classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in *al-Mufradāt* — note that *shaqā* and its noun *shaqāwa* came also to mean **wretchedness in the next life.** The same root. The same word. Because the deepest theological insight in this verb is that **the labor of this world and the wretchedness of the next come from the same source: being separated from the Garden.**

This world is the **first half** of *shaqā.* The fire, for those who end there, is the second half.

The Garden was Adam's starting point and is meant to be his ending point. Everything in between — *this entire life that you are living right now* — is named, by Allah Himself, with one word: *shaqā.* [PAUSE]

### What this single word reveals

When you feel tired and don't know why — *that* is what Allah is naming.

When you sense that no matter how much you achieve, the achieving never ends — *that* is what Allah is naming.

When you wonder why life feels like rowing upstream against a current that never relents — Allah named it for you, in one word, in the warning to your father Adam, before you were even born. **You are not failing at life. You are simply living in the *shaqā* that was predicted.**

This is not pessimism. This is **diagnostic precision.** Allah, in mercy, is telling Adam — and through him telling you — that the discomfort you feel is not a malfunction. It is the **signature of where you are.** The labor is the marker. The exhaustion is the data.

If you ever find a place where there is no *shaqā* — no labor, no exhaustion, no friction — that place is not earth. That is the Garden you are returning to.

## Element Three: The Heavy Emphatic — *Yukhrijannakumā*

### Teaching the concept first

Arabic has a feature that English does not: the **nūn al-tawkīd** — the "emphasis nun." It is a sound — *anna* or *an* — that you can attach to a verb to load it with intensity. It is the grammatical equivalent of a finger jabbed into your chest. It says: *I am not just stating this — I am underlining it, pressing it, insisting on it.*

There are two strengths. The light emphasis (*nūn al-tawkīd al-khafīfah*) and the heavy emphasis (*nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah*). The heavy is the stronger. It doubles the consonant.

When Arabic uses the heavy emphasis, the speaker is not making a casual observation. The speaker is **swearing on the gravity of the matter.**

### Opening the verb

Look at the verb Allah uses for "let him not drive you out":

> *yukhrijannakumā* — *yu-kh-r-i-j-**anna**-kumā*

That doubled *nn* — that is the heavy emphasis nun. Allah is not saying "please don't let him expel you." He is not saying "try to avoid being expelled." He is using the strongest grammatical intensifier in the Arabic language to say: **DO NOT — under any circumstance, in any moment of weakness, in any rationalization — let him drive you out.**

### Deepening

Notice the construction. Allah uses *fa-lā yukhrijannakumā* — literally, "so let him not — emphatically — drive you both out."

In Arabic, this is a **prohibition routed through the third person.** Allah is not directly commanding Adam: "you, don't leave." He is directly forbidding **Iblis** from doing the driving out.

Why is this remarkable? Because grammatically, Adam is being warned about an action whose **agent** is Iblis. The verb is in Iblis's hand, not Adam's. Yet the warning is to Adam. The structure is saying: *Iblis is the one who acts — but you are the one who must not let it happen.*

This is the original psychological geography of temptation. The enemy moves. You hold the line. The drive-out comes from outside, but the failure to resist comes from inside.

Al-Tabari makes the mechanism of that drive-out explicit: Iblis expels them *through their disobedience to their Lord and their obedience to him.* The grammar says Iblis is the agent — but the mechanism is relational. When a creature obeys the enemy and disobeys the Creator, the exile follows from the inversion itself. Iblis does not push anyone through the gate; he engineers the conditions under which they cross it themselves. The prohibition — *do not let him drive you out* — is therefore also: *do not hand him the instrument by which he does it.* Disobedience is that instrument. It has always been that instrument.

And the heavy emphasis — *yukhrijann-* — is Allah's voice raised. Not in anger. In **the urgency of love.** A father grabbing his child by the shoulders before sending them into a dangerous world. *This is real. This will come for you. Do not — do not — let him win.*

Now hold this against the word that follows. Allah warns with maximum emphasis: *do not let him drive you out.* And then, with no emphasis at all, gently and almost as if quietly stating a fact, He says: *fa-tashqā.* For then — you would suffer.

The contrast is everything. **The warning is loud. The consequence is quiet.** Because Allah does not need to dramatize the consequence. He has named it with one word. *Shaqā.* And every human being who has ever lived, every body that has ever ached at the end of a long day, every heart that has ever wondered "why is it always like this" — every one of us is the **proof** that the consequence does not need to be raised to the level of the warning.

The warning had to be loud because Adam had not yet experienced *shaqā.* Once *shaqā* arrives, it speaks for itself. [PAUSE]
