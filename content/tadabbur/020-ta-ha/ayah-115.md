# The Forgetting of Adam — Why the Quran Had to Come

## The Ayah

> [ayah:20:115] وَلَقَدْ عَهِدْنَآ إِلَىٰٓ ءَادَمَ مِن قَبْلُ فَنَسِىَ وَلَمْ نَجِدْ لَهُۥ عَزْمًا

**Wa laqad ʿahidnā ilā Ādama min qablu fa-nasiya wa lam najid lahu ʿazmā**

> *And We had already taken a covenant with Adam from before — but he forgot. And We did not find in him any firm resolve.*

---

## INTRODUCTION — Where This Ayah Sits

Spend a moment with the architecture of the surah you are at the end of.

Surah Ta-Ha opened with two letters — **Ṭā Hā** — and then immediately a sentence that has comforted believers for fourteen centuries: *"We have not sent down the Qur'ān upon you to cause you distress."* The whole surah began as a kind of consolation. To the Prophet ﷺ, exhausted under the weight of revelation. To anyone, really, who has ever felt that the demands of God feel too heavy to carry.

Then the surah moved into Mūsā. Eighty ayāt of him. A burning bush. A staff that becomes a serpent. A hand that emerges white from his side. A confrontation with the most powerful man on earth. A sea split open. A people delivered. And then — almost immediately — that same delivered people fashioning a golden calf out of their own jewelry while their prophet was on the mountain receiving the very Tablets that would have warned them not to.

You read all of that and you ask the obvious question. *How?*

How does a generation that watched the sea fold back upon Pharaoh's chariots manufacture a god out of melted earrings six weeks later? How do humans who have *seen* — actually seen — turn around and *forget*? How is this possible?

[PAUSE]

The surah answers. Not with a sermon. With a single ayah, dropped quietly between the long story of Mūsā and the closing story of Ādam:

*And We had already taken a covenant with Adam from before — but he forgot. And We did not find in him any firm resolve.*

This is forensic anthropology. Allah is taking the entire pattern He has just shown you — Pharaoh denying after seeing nine signs, Banī Isrā'īl bowing to a calf weeks after the sea split — and tracing it back to the source. *Min qablu.* From before. Before Mūsā. Before the calf. Before Pharaoh. Before any of you. The diagnosis runs deeper than a single nation. It runs back to the first human.

And here is the strange part. The strange, almost merciful part.

Classical tafsir is unambiguous: Adam transgressed. He obeyed Iblīs. He violated the command. Al-Ṭabarī uses the phrase *"he opposed My command"* and *"he obeyed Iblīs"*; al-Muyassar is equally direct. But **what characterized his transgression was nothing like what characterized Iblīs's.** Iblīs *refused* outright. Iblīs was arrogant. Iblīs argued. Iblīs disputed. The surah will name every shade of that conscious defiance in the very next ayah. What characterized Adam's sin was something qualitatively different. Something so ordinary that you and I do it before breakfast.

He forgot.

That is the diagnosis. That is what is wrong with the human creature. Not arrogance. Not malice. *Forgetfulness.* And paired with it, an absence so quiet it would slip past you if Allah hadn't pointed at it: **we did not find in him resolve.** Allah looked. He searched the heart of the first human for the iron grip that would have held the covenant in place when temptation arrived. And He did not find it.

[PAUSE]

This is the surah's thesis statement, hidden five ayāt before the end. The whole book that began *"We have not sent down the Qur'ān upon you to cause you distress"* — this is *why* it had to be sent at all. Because the human, by his very making, forgets. And because the human, by his very making, does not have the resolve to remember on his own.

Stay with that for a moment before we go any further. Everything in this reflection grows out of it.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to look at three things in this ayah. A preposition. An elided word. And an indefinite noun. Three small grammatical features that, once you see them, change what this ayah is doing.

### Element One: ʿAhidnā **ilā** — The Preposition of Intimate Trust

In Arabic, the verb **ʿahida** — the verb behind covenant, contract, promise — usually takes the preposition **ʿalā** when it is being formal. *ʿAhida ʿalayhi* — "He bound a covenant *upon* him." That is the language of obligation imposed from above. A king pressing terms onto a vassal. A creditor binding a debtor. *ʿAlā* — *upon*. The weight rests on the receiver.

But Allah does not say that here.

He says **ʿahidnā ilā Ādama**. *We made a covenant **toward** Adam.*

[PAUSE]

The preposition **ilā** — "to," "toward" — is the preposition of direction and arrival. It is the preposition of someone going *toward* someone else. *Sirtu ilā al-bayt* — *I traveled toward the house.* When **ʿahida** takes **ilā**, classical lexicographers note, the covenant is colored differently. It is less the imposition of terms and more the entrusting of a charge. *I have entrusted this **to** you.* The covenant moves *toward* Adam, the way a confidence is shared with a friend, the way a father places something precious into his son's hands.

The content of that trust was precise. The classical commentators, drawing on the surrounding verses, read it as a specific charge: *Iblis is your enemy and your wife's enemy. Do not let him drive you out of the Garden, lest you be wretched in the world.* A named adversary. An identified method. A spelled-out consequence. Not a vague moral teaching but a pointed warning, delivered with the intimacy of a parent telling a child: *this is the particular danger; this is who is working against you; this is what you stand to lose.*

Notice what this preposition is doing emotionally before you ever get to the betrayal.

The very first thing Allah tells you about the relationship is that the covenant was *intimate*. It was a *trust handed toward him*, not an *obligation pressed onto him*. Adam did not stand under this covenant the way an employee stands under a contract. He stood inside it the way a child stands inside a parent's expectation — held, named, trusted.

And then — *fa-nasiya*. **He forgot.**

Sit with the contrast. The most intimate kind of covenant. Trust extended *toward* the one being trusted. The directionality of the preposition itself communicating: *this is between us, and I am leaning toward you with it.* The content was specific enough to name his enemy. And he forgot.

Here is what the preposition reveals: **forgetting feels different in different relationships.** If your boss tells you to do something and you forget, that is one kind of forgetting. If your spouse tells you something they were excited about and you forget, that is a different kind altogether. The pain is not in the forgetting. The pain is in *what relationship the forgetting was inside.*

Allah did not press a contract onto Adam. Allah leaned *toward* Adam with a trust. And the very first thing the Qur'ān tells us about us — about Adam, about all of us — is that we forgot something that was given to us with that kind of nearness.

This single preposition is the difference between a forgotten chore and a forgotten anniversary. It tells you which kind of forgetting we are dealing with.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: **Min qablu** — Before What?

In Arabic, when you see the phrase *min qabli kadhā* — "from before such-and-such" — there is normally a noun that follows. *From before the dawn. From before this moment. From before the messengers came.* The phrase usually completes itself.

But here Allah ends it on the preposition.

*Min qablu.* And then — nothing. No noun. The phrase hangs.

In classical Arabic grammar this is called **al-mabnī ʿalā al-ḍamm** — the word **qablu** is built on a final ḍamma precisely because the noun it would govern has been *removed*. Grammarians have a name for this construction because it is doing something specific: **the object is omitted on purpose so the phrase becomes generalized.** Before what? Before *anything you can put there.* Before this revelation. Before Mūsā. Before the messengers. Before the calf. Before you. Before all of it.

This is not lazy grammar. This is open grammar. The omission widens the ayah.

Notice what it does. If Allah had said *min qabli Mūsā* — *before Mūsā* — the ayah would just be a chronology note. A historical placement. *Yes, Adam came before Mūsā, this is what happened.* The verse would be locked into one slot in time.

Instead, by leaving **qablu** dangling, Allah lets every listener fill in their own *what*. The Qurashī listener hearing this in Makkah fills it in: *Before our forefathers. Before the idols on the Kaʿbah.* The Israelite scholar fills it in: *Before our prophets, before our calf, before us.* The modern listener fills it in: *Before me. Before everything I think is normal. Before there was a Tuesday morning to be late for.*

Adam's forgetting is not a date. It is a **template.** *Min qablu* — before everything that came after. He forgot first, and the forgetting has been with us ever since. We are all living *after* the *qabl*.

[PAUSE]

Here is what makes this point land. You might be tempted to read the story of Adam in the Garden as something distant — a primeval scene that happened to someone else, a long time ago, in a place you have never been. The omitted noun forbids that reading. By refusing to specify *before what*, Allah is saying: *this is before everything. Including you.*

You are not an observer of Adam's forgetting. You are a *consequence* of it. The scene is upstream of every choice you have made today.

This is what one missing word does. It is the grammatical equivalent of a long lens pulling back until the original sin is no longer a story but the air you breathe.

[PAUSE]

Ibn ʿAbbās, recorded in the early tafsir tradition, pressed this observation further. He noted that the very name by which Allah designates the human being — *insān* — carries within its root the trace of this same reality. *The human was named insān only because he was given a covenant and forgot it (nasiya).* The Arabic word for *human being*, on this reading, is the Arabic word for *the forgetting creature*. The blank hanging at the end of *min qablu* — *before everything* — is not just a syntactic omission. It is encoded in the noun we use for ourselves. We are, by name, those who were given something and did not hold it. The grammar of this ayah and the grammar of our identity are telling the same story.

### Element Three: Lam najid lahu **ʿazmā** — "Any Resolve at All"

This is where the ayah cuts deepest.

Allah does not say *we found him weak.* He does not say *we found his resolve to be insufficient.* He does not say *his resolve was weak.* All of those would still imply that there was *some* resolve, however small, however inadequate.

He says: **lam najid lahu ʿazmā.** *We did not find in him resolve.*

The word **ʿazmā** is **indefinite.** **Nakira.** Not "*the* resolve" — *any* resolve. Any at all. In Arabic, the indefinite in this construction has a generalizing force the way English would say *we found in him no resolve whatsoever.* The grammatical shape is that of complete absence.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the verb. **Najid** — "we find." From the root **w-j-d**, which means *to find, to come upon, to encounter the existence of something.* This is the same verb used when the Qur'ān talks about not *finding* a refuge, not *finding* a helper, not *finding* a way out. It is the verb of searching.

And the implied subject is *We* — Allah Himself. *We searched. We did not find.*

Stay with that.

Allah, who does not need to search to know — for whom searching is a courtesy of language used so we can hear ourselves — is using the language of looking. He is saying: *I looked into Adam for resolve. And I did not find any there.*

Al-Muyassar renders this with clinical precision: *We did not find in him strength of resolve by which he would preserve what he was commanded.* Not resolve in the abstract — but resolve specifically calibrated to the function of *preserving a command across time.* That is what **ʿazm** does, in the Qur'ān's grammar of the soul: it is the faculty that keeps what was entrusted from slipping. It is the grip that prevents the covenant from subsiding from active force into dormant memory.

This is not condemnation. This is **diagnosis.** Allah is not reporting that Adam *failed* a resolve test. He is reporting that resolve was *not part of the original equipment.* The human, by composition, is not built with iron determination already inside him. You can search. It is not there.

[PAUSE]

Now zoom out to the root **ʿ-z-m**. This same root is the root behind one of the most exalted titles in the entire Qur'ān: **Ulū al-ʿazm min al-rusul** — "those of firm resolve among the messengers" (46:35). Nūḥ. Ibrāhīm. Mūsā. ʿĪsā. Muḥammad ﷺ. The five who stood when standing was unbearable.

So **ʿazm** is the rarest, most prized human quality in the Qur'ān's vocabulary. The thing that distinguishes the highest tier of prophets from everyone else.

And here, at the very beginning of the human story, Allah is saying: this is exactly what the original human did *not* have.

Read those two ayāt together — 20:115 and 46:35 — and you have the entire arc of human spiritual growth. We begin with Adam: *no resolve found in him.* We end, at the summit, with the prophets of *ʿazm*. The journey of the human is the journey from absence-of-resolve to resolve. From forgetting-by-default to remembering by sustained will. The ʿazm we are missing at the start is *exactly* what we are climbing toward by the end.

[PAUSE]

### Bridging — What the Linguistic Construction Has Shown

Step back from the three elements and look at what the ayah has done.

The preposition told you: *the covenant was intimate — and its content was precise.*
The omitted noun told you: *Adam's forgetting precedes everything, and is written into what we are called.*
The indefinite told you: *resolve was not present at all — not insufficiently, not partially. Not there.*

Together they construct a single, devastating, merciful diagnosis: **the human being is a creature whose original equipment includes intimate access to Allah, a named knowledge of the enemy, an immediate tendency to forget, and an absence of the iron resolve that would close the gap between the two.**

This is not a moral failing being narrated. This is a **structural feature** of what we are.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about what it means to be a human walking through a Tuesday.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Forgetting Is Our Primal Wound — Not Rebellion

Think about what you assumed before this ayah opened.

If someone asked you, *"What was Adam's sin?"* — I imagine you would say something like *"He disobeyed."* Maybe *"He ate from the tree."* You would frame it as an act. A choice. A movement of the will against a command.

That is how we frame our own failures, too. *I did the wrong thing. I chose poorly. I knew better and I went the other way.*

Allah does not frame Adam that way.

Allah frames it as **fa-nasiya** — *and he forgot.*

[PAUSE]

This reframing is theologically enormous, and I want to slow down here because almost nobody points it out.

In the Qur'ānic account, Adam is not narrated as a man who looked at the tree, weighed the command, and decided to defy it. He is narrated as a man who had been told something — and in the moment of pressure, **the command was not present in his consciousness.** The word for what happened to him is the word for *forgetting.* Iblīs *whispered.* The whisper found no active resistance from memory. The instruction that should have surfaced — *this is your enemy; this is exactly what you were warned against* — did not come. Not because the command had been revoked. Because it was not present in the moment it was needed.

[PAUSE]

And yet — and here the tradition asks us to be careful — two of the greatest first-generation authorities on tafsir would not let that picture stand unchallenged.

Mujahid and al-Hasan, both recorded by Ibn Kathīr, read **nasiya** in this verse not as literal forgetting but as *abandonment.* He let go of the covenant, they said. The Arabic word they use is **tarakahu** — *he left it.* On their reading, Adam is not a man whose mind went blank in the critical moment. He is a man who loosened his grip — who, when the whisper arrived, chose not to cling.

The two readings carry different weights.

If Adam *forgot* — in the cognitive sense — then he was undone by a structural limitation of the creature Allah made. Cognitive frailty is not entirely within our control. The implication is almost merciful: *this is the human condition, and so revelation was sent.*

If Adam *abandoned* — if *nasiya* here means *tarakahu*, as Mujahid and al-Hasan maintained — then the forgetting was not passive. It was a volitional relaxing of attention. He was not seized by amnesia. He put the covenant down.

Both readings are attested. Both carry the weight of early scholarly authority. And the honest reflection on this ikhtilaf is that the lived experience of temptation may not honor the distinction in the way our analysis does. Whether the hand opened because the mind went blank, or because the will grew tired — the covenant fell. The gap between *I forgot* and *I let go* is, in most real moments of human weakness, thinner than any lexicon can measure.

You have known that gap. The thought you could have had, but did not reach for. The reminder you could have summoned, but did not insist on. *Was I really distracted? Or did I decide not to remember?*

That gap — between genuine forgetting and the forgetting you do not interrupt — is where most human failures actually live. And both readings of *nasiya* point at it from different sides.

[PAUSE]

Here is what makes the ikhtilaf itself instructive: it does not change the diagnosis in the second half of the ayah at all. **Lam najid lahu ʿazmā** — *We did not find in him any firm resolve.* Whether *nasiya* is cognitive lapse or volitional abandonment, the underlying cause is the same. The resolve that would have prevented either — the grip that turns active knowledge into active resistance — was not there. Both Mujahid's reading and the literal reading converge on the same structural absence. Adam did not forget *in spite of* having resolve. He forgot *because* resolve was not yet part of him.

The diagnosis is the same. The prescription, as the rest of the surah will make clear, is also the same.

### Theme Two: The Covenant That Named the Enemy

What was the covenant Adam forgot?

The surah does not dwell on its content here — it trusts you to carry the surrounding verses in mind. But the classical commentators are specific. Al-Muyassar reads the connected ayāt as a single unit: Allah had warned Adam that **Iblīs is your enemy and your wife's enemy. Do not let him drive you out of the Garden, lest you be wretched in the world.**

Sit with the architecture of that warning. It is not generic. It does not say *be good* or *avoid evil.* It says: *here is the adversary, by name. Here is his method — he will work to drive you out. Here is the consequence — not punishment in the abstract, but a specific form of hardship in the dunya.* A named threat. An identified mechanism. A spelled-out cost. The covenant was targeted instruction, delivered with the intimacy we saw in the preposition **ilā**.

And Adam forgot *this.* Not a vague moral principle. He forgot the **named** enemy. He forgot the **identified** method. He forgot the **spelled-out** consequence.

[PAUSE]

There is a particular kind of forgetting that happens when we are inside the experience we were warned about. You can read about the psychology of manipulation and then, months later, find yourself inside the exact dynamic you studied — and not recognize it. You can know, in some part of yourself, that a certain kind of temptation works on you — and then, in the moment it arrives, the knowledge fails to activate. The data is there. The warning was given. It simply is not present as a *force.*

This is what Adam experienced. Not ignorance. *Inactive knowledge.* He had been told. The telling had not become a shield.

The covenant was given to him **ilā** — *toward him*, as an intimate trust. It did not become in him **ʿazm** — a resolve strong enough to make the knowledge operative when pressure arrived. And this is precisely what al-Muyassar's formulation of the absent quality names: *strength of resolve by which he would preserve what he was commanded.* **ʿAzm**, in this reading, is not just willpower in the generic sense. It is the faculty that keeps a true thing *active* — that prevents the covenant from subsiding into background memory and ensures it remains a present, functioning force at the exact moment it is needed.

[PAUSE]

This is why the covenant's specificity matters so much. A vague moral instruction might have been forgotten simply because vague things are hard to grip. But Adam was not given vagueness. He was given a name — *Iblīs* — and a prediction — *he will try to drive you out* — and a consequence — *you will be wretched*. The warning was everything it needed to be. What was missing was the internal architecture to keep that warning lit.

You have had this experience. Someone you trusted told you something precise: *this person will use you; this situation will go this way; this feeling is not what it says it is.* And then you walked right into it. Not because the warning was insufficient. Because in the moment, it was not active.

That is Adam's forgetting. That is your forgetting. That is the thing this ayah is naming.

### Theme Three: The Climb from *Nasiya* to *ʿAzm*

The two phrases at the heart of this ayah — *fa-nasiya* and *lam najid lahu ʿazmā* — are not only a diagnosis. Read the surah as a whole, and they become coordinates on a map.

The surah opens: *We have not sent down the Qur'ān upon you to cause you distress* (20:1–2). Near its center: *And establish the prayer for My remembrance* — **li-dhikrī** (20:14). And just after the story of Adam's fall comes a consequence named with striking directness: *Whoever turns away from My remembrance — his will be a constricted life* (20:124).

These three moments form a reply to the ayah we are sitting in.

The human forgets — *nasiya* — by nature. The remedy is not willpower summoned from nowhere. The remedy is **dhikr** — *remembrance* — made structural. Made habitual. Made obligatory. That is what the prayer is, in the grammar of this surah: not just an act of worship but a *system for not forgetting.* Five times a day, the covenant is renewed. Five times a day, the warning is recalled. Five times a day, the human who forgets by nature is handed the covenant again — not as new information, but as activation. *Li-dhikrī.* For the sake of My remembrance. So the knowledge does not sleep.

[PAUSE]

And here the far end of the map comes into view. Back in 20:115: *We did not find in him ʿazm.* Forward to 46:35: **Ulū al-ʿazm min al-rusul** — *those of firm resolve among the messengers.* Nūḥ. Ibrāhīm. Mūsā. ʿĪsā. Muḥammad ﷺ.

These five are what Adam was not. They are the answer to the question this ayah poses. What does a human look like when resolve has been built, sustained, and made absolute? What happens when the covenant stays active — not as a memory, but as a present force — through persecution, through exile, through decades of rejection, through things that would dissolve any ordinary resolution?

They happen.

The human arc, as the Qur'ān draws it, begins here — in a garden, with a creature who forgot — and ends at the summit, with a lineage of those who did not. The distance between Adam's forgetting and Ibrāhīm's resolve is not the distance between two people. It is the distance between what we begin with and what we are meant to become. It is not a distance that shames us. It is a distance that *orients* us.

[PAUSE]

And one more thing, before we leave the surah entirely.

Al-Ṭabarī frames this ayah not as a meditation on primordial history but as a word spoken *to the Prophet ﷺ directly*, about the people around him. His reading of the verse's context: *And if those to whom We have repeatedly sent warnings in this Qur'ān squander My covenant, O Muḥammad, and follow the command of their enemy Iblīs instead of My command — their father Adam did this very thing long before them.* The ayah, on this reading, is not only backward-looking. It is pastoral. Allah is telling His Prophet: *do not be surprised. This is old. This is the original wound. They are not failing in a new way. They are failing in the first way.*

That frame makes the ayah both heavier and more merciful at once. The human pattern that exhausted the Prophet ﷺ — people hearing the warnings, receiving the covenant, and then turning toward what Iblīs whispers — is not a special failure of his generation. It is the original condition. The surah that began *we did not send this down to distress you* ends by showing you why the sending was necessary in the first place. Because the human was always this. Because forgetting is older than any of us.

## CLOSING — Why the Quran Had to Come

Return to the opening line of the surah. *We have not sent down the Qur'ān upon you to cause you distress.*

You have now spent time with the ayah near its end. And you can see — can you not — what connects them.

The Qur'ān was not sent to burden. It was sent because the human, by composition, forgets. The covenant was given *ilā* Ādam — toward him, with intimacy, with precision. He forgot. Every messenger that came after was sent, in some sense, to hand the covenant back again. And every page of the Qur'ān is, in this reading, a renewal of what Adam held and lost.

The surah does not end in despair. It ends with return. After Adam's fall — after Allah says *lam najid lahu ʿazmā* — the next scene shows something remarkable: *Then his Lord chose him, and turned to him in mercy, and guided him* (20:122). The story does not end at the diagnosis. The diagnosis was given so the cure could be understood.

[PAUSE]

And this is the mercy hidden inside what looks, at first glance, like a verdict.

When Allah says *We did not find in him any firm resolve* — this is not a condemnation. It is a description of what the human comes into the world without. And descriptions of what is absent are the very foundation of what can be given.

You are not damned for having no ʿazm pre-installed. The absence is named so you know what you are building toward. The naming of the wound is already the beginning of healing.

The five who had ʿazm did not receive it as a gift at birth. They built it. Through fire and exile and years of refusing to let go of what mattered. The prophets of ʿazm were once, like all of us, children of Adam. They began in the same place. With the same absent resolve. With the same structural tendency toward forgetting. They became what they became through a lifetime of turning back — of letting the dhikr do what Adam's memory did not.

[PAUSE]

*Wa lam najid lahu ʿazmā.*

No. Not yet. Not in the beginning. Not in the garden, before the whisper.

But the surah that names the absence is also the surah that says: *We have not sent this down to cause you distress.*

The sending is the remedy. Every recitation is the covenant, extended toward you again — **ilā**k, toward you — trusting that this time, you will find in yourself enough grip to hold it a little longer than before.
