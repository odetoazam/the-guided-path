# The Gate of Mercy and the Limits of Knowing

## Ta-Ha 109-110

> يَوْمَئِذٍ لَّا تَنفَعُ ٱلشَّفَـٰعَةُ إِلَّا مَنْ أَذِنَ لَهُ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ وَرَضِىَ لَهُۥ قَوْلًۭا ۝ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِهِۦ عِلْمًۭا

*Yawma'idhin lā tanfaʿu al-shafāʿatu illā man adhina lahu al-Raḥmānu wa raḍiya lahu qawlā. Yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum wa lā yuḥīṭūna bihi ʿilmā.*

> "On that Day, intercession will not benefit except for one to whom the Most Merciful has given permission and whose speech He has accepted. He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them, but they cannot encompass Him in knowledge."


## Introduction: Standing in the Hush

Sit for a moment with the texture of this passage.

The surah has been a long story. Musa lifting his staff. The fire on the mountain. Pharaoh's defiance. The magicians falling in prostration. The drowning of an army. The wandering of a people. The whispering of the Sāmirī. The calf that lowed. The tablets that broke. Forty years.

And then, suddenly, all of that is gone.

The voice of the surah lifts up out of history and places us somewhere else — somewhere none of us have stood before, but which all of us are walking toward. The trumpet is blown. The earth is shaken until it is flat. The voices that filled the world — the bargaining, the protesting, the explaining — go quiet.

Allah uses an extraordinary phrase a few verses earlier:

> "On that Day they will follow the Caller — there is no crookedness in him — and the voices will be hushed before the Most Merciful, so you will hear nothing but a faint footfall." (Ta-Ha 108)

A *footfall.* The whole creation, reduced to the sound of soft footsteps walking toward a Reckoning.

That is the silence we are entering. That is the climate of the two ayahs we are about to open.

In that hush, every accumulated strategy a human being has used to manage their life collapses. The connections you knew. The lawyer you hired. The argument you rehearsed. The friend who would always speak up for you. None of it travels through. The voices have gone quiet.

And in that silence, Allah tells us what *does* still work.

He tells us, very precisely, what makes a plea valid.

And then, in the very next breath, He tells us something even more disorienting — that even now, standing where we are, looking at what we are looking at, we cannot wrap our knowing around what is happening to us.

[PAUSE]

Two ayahs. One about the gate. One about the horizon. Both about the same truth, approached from two angles.

Stay with me. There is something here we do not normally see.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Root of *Shafāʿah* — Why Intercession Even Exists

Before we open the word, let me teach you the concept it sits on.

In Arabic, there is a beautiful pair of words: *shafʿ* and *witr*. *Witr* means *odd* — a number that stands alone, one, three, five, seven. *Shafʿ* means *even* — a number that has been *paired*, two, four, six, eight.

The verb that gives us *shafʿ* — the verb ش ف ع — literally means *to add a second to what was alone*. *To pair*. *To make even what was odd*.

Stay with that picture. *To add yourself to what was alone.*

When a person stands before a king with a request, and someone of stature steps up beside them and says, *"My lord, this case is also mine — I add myself to this petition,"* — that is *shafāʿah* in its original sense. The petitioner was *one*. Now, with the intercessor, they are *two*. *Witr* has become *shafʿ*. The lonely petition has been *paired*.

That is the picture the Arabic carries.

Now look at what Allah says:

> *"On that Day, intercession will not benefit except for one to whom the Most Merciful has given permission and whose speech He has accepted."*

Let's sit with this carefully.

In every other court — every court of human history — the entire **point** of finding an intercessor is to bring someone to the king who is *not under the king's control.* The whole strategy is to find a powerful figure whose own standing forces the king to listen — a noble, a relative, a creditor, someone the king *needs.*

Notice something. In this Court, that strategy is impossible from the start. There is no one who is not under His authority. The intercessor cannot be brought from outside. The intercessor must be granted from within.

So Allah does not abolish *shafāʿah*. He does not say *"there will be no intercession."* He keeps the institution. He keeps the pairing. But He locates it entirely inside His own permission. *Illā man adhina lahu al-Raḥmān*. *Except for one to whom the Most Merciful gave permission.*

Here's what is remarkable.

The thing we usually call *intercession* is, in the human world, an act of *external pressure*. Someone with leverage exerts that leverage on our behalf. In the divine economy, intercession is not pressure at all. It is the **outflow of mercy that has already been decided** — given a voice to speak it, given a face to wear it, given a name to belong to.

The intercessor on that Day is not bringing a stranger's case to a reluctant king. The intercessor is being permitted to *vocalize* a verdict the King has already inclined toward.

[PAUSE]

That is what *shafāʿah* under permission means. It is not bargaining. It is not magic. It is not bypass. It is mercy taking the form of a relationship — your name on someone's tongue who has been *allowed* to say it.

This is why the linguistic root matters so much. The verb says *adding a second*. Allah's permission says *the second cannot be added without My granting it*. Together they paint a picture you cannot get anywhere else: there will be voices speaking on your behalf — but only voices He has authored.

And the classical commentators are precise about who receives this gift. Al-Muyassar states it plainly: intercession reaches only *al-mu'min al-mukhliṣ* — the sincere, devoted believer. The mechanism of mercy is not broadcast uniformly across creation on that Day. It is anchored to *īmān* carried genuinely through a life.

### Element Two: *Al-Raḥmān* — The Gatekeeper Is Mercy Itself

Now look very carefully at *which name* of Allah is in this ayah.

It is not *Allāh* — the comprehensive name. It is not *al-Mālik* — the King. It is not *al-Jabbār* — the Compeller. It is not *al-Ḥakam* — the Judge.

It is *al-Raḥmān*.

> *"...except for one to whom the Most Merciful has given permission..."*

Let me teach you what this name means before we use it.

*Al-Raḥmān* comes from the root ر ح م — the same root as *raḥim*, *womb*. Allah's mercy as *Raḥmān* is the mercy of the womb writ across the cosmos: the mercy that holds you before you can speak, that nourishes you before you can ask, that exists for you in the structure of reality whether you have earned it or not.

The classical scholars draw a careful distinction. *Al-Raḥmān* is the mercy that pertains to *this world's structure* — the mercy that lets the believer and the disbeliever both breathe, both eat, both be loved by their mothers. *Al-Raḥīm* is the mercy that pertains to *the believer's destination* — particular, accepted, intimate.

*Al-Raḥmān* is the mercy of the framework itself.

Now ask the question this ayah is forcing on us. Why this name? Why is it *al-Raḥmān* whose permission is required, when the picture we expect to see on that Day is justice, accountability, severity?

[PAUSE]

Here is what Allah is teaching us — and once you see it, it does not leave you.

The gate of intercession is staffed by Mercy itself.

When you imagine the Day, you might picture a courtroom. You might picture a tribunal. You might picture a King so absolute that no plea reaches Him without going through layers of severity. That is what we project, because that is how we have built our courts.

Allah corrects the picture by telling us: the One whose permission opens the door of pleading on that Day is *the very name that gave you the womb*. The One you must pass through to get the gift of someone speaking for you is the same One whose mercy already structures your existence.

Even on the Day of accounting, the door is *raḥmah*-shaped.

This is profound. It means that when the Day arrives and intercession is granted to whomever it is granted, you are not seeing mercy *override* justice. You are seeing mercy *be* the gate of the courtroom. The architecture itself is mercy. The permission slip is signed by *al-Raḥmān*, not by some other attribute that has stepped in to soften what He would otherwise do.

Notice something. Many people, when they think of the Day of Judgment, secretly imagine a Judge whose default is severity, and mercy as something He sometimes allows. The ayah inverts this. The default of the gate is *Mercy*. Severity is what mercy declines to extend in particular cases.

You did not come into the world through wrath. You will not enter the next world through anything other than the same hand that knit your bones in the womb — whether that hand opens for you or closes against you, it is *the same hand*.

[PAUSE]

This is what the choice of *al-Raḥmān* in this ayah does. It tells you the climate of the door before you ever reach it.

### Element Three: *Wa Raḍiya Lahu Qawlan* — Two Conditions, Not One

Now look at the second clause. The ayah does not stop at "permission." It adds a second condition.

> *"...and whose speech He has accepted."*

*Wa raḍiya lahu qawlan.*

Let's slow down here.

The verb *raḍiya* means *to be pleased with*, *to find acceptable*, *to approve*. It is one of the most beautiful words in the Quran when used of Allah's relationship with His servants — *raḍiyallāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh* — *Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him.*

Here, *raḍiya* governs *qawlan*. *He approved [his] speech.*

There are two conditions in this ayah, not one.

First: *adhina lahu* — Allah gave him permission to speak.
Second: *raḍiya lahu qawlan* — Allah approved the speech itself.

Stay with the difference. The first is permission given to a *person*. The second is approval given to a *word*. The intercessor is approved. *And* what the intercessor *says* is approved.

Both. Not one or the other.

Why does Allah do this? Why double the condition?

Here is what the doubling reveals: even after permission is granted, what is *said* still matters. The intercessor on that Day is not a magic figure whose mere presence cancels accounts. The intercessor must speak — and what is spoken must be true, must be just, must be in accord with Allah's wisdom.

This destroys a certain folk-religion picture that exists in every faith tradition — the picture of the saint who can simply *override* the Judge by virtue of their proximity. Allah is showing us: there is no override. The saint does not bend the King's will. The saint, *if they are permitted at all*, says only what the King has *already* approved.

The intercessor is a voice for a verdict, not a force against it.

It is worth pausing here on a point where classical scholars genuinely diverge, because both readings enrich the picture. Al-Tabari reads both conditions in this ayah as attaching to the intercessor: the Most Merciful has given *him* permission to intercede, and *his* plea is the speech approved. Ibn Kathir and al-Muyassar, however, read the second condition as attaching to the *one being interceded for*: the *qawl* that must be approved is the intercessee's own declaration — the speech of faith, the testimony of a life, the words they carried before anyone stepped forward to speak for them. On this reading, the filtering is not only at the moment of intercession; it is embedded in an entire lifetime of speech.

These two readings are not opposed. They describe two dimensions of the same gate. If al-Tabari's reading is primary, every syllable of the intercessor's petition is weighed in that moment. If Ibn Kathir and al-Muyassar's reading is primary, what matters most is the *qawl* of your life — formed through every honest or dishonest moment, already weighed before anyone steps forward on your behalf. The intercessor's permitted plea meets a ground prepared — or unprepared — by the speech of a lifetime. Both filters operate; the question is which one you are building right now.

Now notice something else. Look at the indefinite *qawlan*. Allah did not say *the speech*. He said *a speech* — *qawlan*, indefinite, accusative.

In Arabic, the indefinite often carries a sense of *a particular instance* or *a kind*. Here it suggests: He approved *a* word from him — a specific word, in that specific moment, on that specific case. Not a blank check. Not a permanent license.

Each word, on that Day, is approved or not approved on its own merits.

[PAUSE]

This means something for us right now.

If, on that Day, even the intercessor's *speech* is weighed for whether Allah is pleased with it — what does that tell you about how seriously Allah takes the *content* of what is said in His presence?

What does that tell you about the words you speak in His presence *now*, in your salah, in your duʿā, in the silence of your heart?

If the most beloved figures of creation, granted the privilege of intercession, must have what they say *approved*, word by word — then the speech of the rest of us is not casual either. The pattern of our speech with Allah, *which we are forming right now*, is the pattern of what would be acceptable then.

The ayah, looking forward to the Day, is also bending back to instruct us: *what you say to your Lord matters. He listens with the precision of someone who will, on that Day, weigh every word.*

[PAUSE]

Now let's bridge.

We have looked at three things. We have seen that *shafāʿah* in its root means *adding yourself to the lonely*. We have seen that the gatekeeper of that pairing on the Day of Judgment is *al-Raḥmān*, the womb-mercy itself. We have seen that even after permission is given, *the speech* of the intercessor is weighed.

Together these three reveal a structure — a structure of how mercy actually flows in Allah's universe. Mercy is not chaos. Mercy is not arbitrary. Mercy is a permitted, witnessed, approved current that reaches you through specific channels Allah has authored.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: When Every Strategy Collapses, Only Mercy Remains

Let me start where you live, before we go to the ayah.

You have spent your whole life building strategies for safety.

Some of those strategies are good. Skills. Education. A profession. Friends who can help. A reputation that opens doors. A bank account that absorbs shock. Some of those strategies are subtler. Knowing how to read a room. Knowing whom to charm. Knowing how to pre-empt criticism by being your own first critic. Knowing how to look like you have it together, even when you don't.

Every adult human being has assembled, often without realizing it, a kind of personal *intercessor system*. A web of people, accomplishments, and impressions that, in the small daily judgments of life, can be summoned to make our case.

We do this so consistently that we no longer notice we are doing it.

And then there are moments in life when this system *fails*.

You have been to a hospital where the doctor's face turned grave and you realized that *no one you know* can fix what is happening. You have been to a graveside where the relationship you spent years cultivating no longer answers your phone. You have been in a marriage breaking apart where every charming explanation you've ever used to repair things suddenly does not work. You have lain awake at three in the morning and discovered that your accomplishments do not comfort you, your reputation does not reach you, and the friends you would normally call cannot be called.

Those moments are previews of the Day.

[PAUSE]

The Day of Judgment is not a foreign event. It is the moment when the failure of every strategy — which we have already tasted in flashes — becomes total and unrelieved. The voices that we counted on go silent. The people who would always speak up are themselves silent before the Caller. The accomplishments are weighed and found to be either there or not there.

Here is the question Allah is teaching us to ask: *what remains when the strategies collapse?*

The ayah answers: *al-Raḥmān*.

Mercy remains.

Not as an emergency exception. Not as a special carve-out. As *the whole structure itself*.

The intercessors on that Day are extensions of *al-Raḥmān*'s mercy. The verdicts of mercy on that Day are *al-Raḥmān*'s verdicts. The voices permitted to speak on that Day are voices that He has authored as channels of His own *raḥmah*.

This means something both terrifying and consoling.

It is terrifying because every strategy we have built that is *not* a relationship of mercy — every accomplishment, every connection, every reputation that does not have *raḥmah* flowing through it — will not arrive there. It will not travel. It will be left behind, like luggage you cannot carry through the gate.

It is consoling because *raḥmah* is the most accessible currency in the universe. A glass of water given. A grudge released. A whispered duʿā for a stranger. A patient breath taken with a difficult parent. The mother who got up at night with a sick child. The believer who forgave the unforgivable for Allah's sake. These are the things that travel.

The ayah is teaching: *invest in what mercy recognizes*. Because on that Day, mercy is the only thing that will be at the gate.

[PAUSE]

Here is the practical application. Take it home with you tonight.

Look at your life. Look at the channels through which you have been pouring your time, your effort, your worry, your ambition. Ask of each one: *will this travel?* Will *al-Raḥmān* recognize this when I arrive at His gate? Will this have woven itself into the fabric of mercy, or only into the fabric of this world?

The strategies we have built that are not *raḥmah* — they are not necessarily evil. They are simply *local*. They work where the doors are local. They do not work at *that* door.

Tomorrow, take one strategy that is purely worldly — one place where you have been investing yourself in something that will not travel — and rebalance it. A few minutes redirected. An apology given. A relationship softened. A duʿā made for someone you usually compete with.

That is the currency of the gate.

### Theme Two: The Humility of Knowing — *Lā Yuḥīṭūna Bihi ʿIlmā*

Now turn to the second ayah.

> *"He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them, but they cannot encompass Him in knowledge."*

The verb here is *yuḥīṭūna* — they encompass. From the root ḥ-w-ṭ. The same root that gives us *ḥā'iṭ* — a wall. *Iḥāṭah* is *to surround something on all sides*. To wrap your knowledge around a thing such that nothing of it escapes you.

Allah's knowledge of *us* is described as encompassing — *He knows what is before them and what is behind them*. Past, future, this side, that side. It surrounds us. There is no angle we are escaping from.

Al-Tabari, drawing on Qatadah, gives these two directions a specific theological content: *what lies before them* is the affair of the Day of Judgment they are stepping directly into — the Reckoning, its outcomes, where they will go. *What lies behind them* is the world they have already exited, the dunya abandoned at the trumpet's sound. The two directions are not merely temporal coordinates; they name two distinct realms — the one being entered and the one already left. He knows both in their entirety: the life you lived and the reckoning you are now facing, neither horizon obscure to Him, both together unreachable by any created mind.

Our knowledge of *Him* is described as *not* encompassing — *they cannot encompass Him in knowledge*.

Stay with this asymmetry. It is one of the most important things the Quran ever says.

Let me bring you to where you actually live with this.

We live in a civilization that has trained us to believe that *to know* is *to encompass*. To know a topic is to have surveyed it. To know a person is to have profiled them. To know a phenomenon is to have measured it from all sides.

You can feel this every time you open your phone. The whole interface is built on the promise of *iḥāṭah* — that you, with this rectangle in your hand, can wrap your knowing around the world. News from every continent. Profiles of every person you ever met. Encyclopedias on demand. Maps that zoom from the galaxy to your front door.

Modern life trains the soul to *expect to encompass*. And when it cannot — when there is something it cannot wrap its knowing around — it experiences that as a failure, an itch, a problem to be solved by more data.

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah.

He confronts that habit head-on. He says: *there is something you will never encompass*. And He names the thing: *Him*.

[PAUSE]

Notice what happens when this lands.

Most people, when they first meet a religious tradition, want to *understand Allah*. They read tafsir. They study aqidah. They ask: what is His relationship to time? What is His relationship to space? Does He change? Does He feel? They are doing what every modern person has been trained to do: build a model that encompasses.

The Quran, gently and persistently, says: *you will not encompass Him*.

This is not a rebuke. It is liberation.

Here is the freedom in it. If you do not have to encompass Him, you do not have to *fail* to encompass Him. You are released from a project that was never your project. You can stop carrying the burden of having to *get Him right* in a totalizing way. You can begin the actual work of relationship: drawing near, listening, obeying, loving — all of which can happen without encompassing.

Look at the grammar one more time. The word for *knowledge* at the end — *ʿilman* — is in a special grammatical position called the *tamyīz*, the accusative of specification. It restricts the verb. It is saying: *they do not encompass Him* — and the *way* in which they do not encompass Him is *by knowledge*.

This is not closing the door. This is showing you which door is closed.

The door of encompassing-by-knowledge is closed. But other doors are open. The door of *witnessing* (*shuhūd*). The door of *love* (*maḥabbah*). The door of *awe* (*haybah*). The door of *worship* (*ʿibādah*). These are not encompassing relationships. They are vertical relationships. The lover does not encompass the beloved; the lover *attends* to the beloved. The worshipper does not encompass the Lord; the worshipper *bows*.

Allah is teaching us: *the relationship I have built for you with Me is not the relationship of an analyst with a specimen. It is the relationship of a creature with their Creator. You will not surround Me. You will stand before Me.*

[PAUSE]

There is a profound consolation in this for anyone who has ever felt that their faith is incomplete because they cannot answer every theological question.

Allah is telling you: *you are not supposed to.* The ones closest to Him on the Day of Judgment are not the ones with the most complete map of Him. They are the ones whose hearts have known *taqwā* — awe in the absence of total seeing. *Tawakkul* — trust in the absence of total predicting. *Maḥabbah* — love in the absence of total comprehending.

Faith *is* the relationship that does not require encompassing.

Notice how this loops back to the first ayah. On the Day, intercession is granted by *al-Raḥmān* and approved word by word. We were just told that even those at the gate of mercy do not control its operations. Now we are told that even our knowledge of the One who governs the gate does not encompass Him.

These two ayahs, together, are dismantling the human project of *control through understanding*. Mercy is not yours to engineer. Knowledge of the One who grants it is not yours to complete. What is left to you?

To stand. To listen. To trust. To bow.

[PAUSE]

Here is the practical application.

There is an experience available to you tonight that most people in your civilization cannot access. The experience of *not needing to know* — and finding that *not knowing* is not absence but *presence of a different kind*.

Tonight, in your salah, when you say *Allāhu Akbar* — *Allah is greater* — let that *Akbar* mean greater than your model of Him. Greater than the picture you have built. Greater than the answers you wish you had. Greater than your need to encompass.

Stand in front of Him as the Quran has placed you: aware that He sees what is before you and what is behind you, aware that He does not similarly fit inside your knowing — and let that asymmetry not be a defect but a *relationship*. The relationship of a creature with the One who made them.

The freedom of *lā yuḥīṭūna bihi ʿilmā* is the freedom to stop performing knowledge in front of the One who knows.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture of Two Verses

Now look at these two ayahs side by side.

> *"On that Day, intercession will not benefit except for one to whom the Most Merciful has given permission and whose speech He has accepted."*

> *"He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them, but they cannot encompass Him in knowledge."*

Notice the architecture.

The first ayah is about the *mercy that flows toward you* — and it tells you that this mercy is not yours to engineer; it is His to grant.

The second ayah is about the *knowledge you can have of Him* — and it tells you that this knowledge is not yours to encompass; it is His to grant.

Both ayahs locate the source in Him. Both ayahs locate the limit in us. Both ayahs are the same lesson approached from two directions: *you are a creature, He is the Creator, and the relationship is not symmetrical.*

But here is what is breathtaking. The first ayah ends with His name *al-Raḥmān*. The second ayah ends with *ʿilman* — *knowledge*. Mercy at the close of one. Knowledge at the close of the other.

Together, these two words name what we tried to control and what we will not control: the mercy that reaches us and the knowledge we have of Him. Allah is telling us: both of these flow *from* Him *to* us. Not the other way.

And that is exactly the posture the Day will require of us.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing.

*Shafāʿah* rooted in *pairing* tells us that intercession is not magic — it is the granting of a voice paired to the petitioner by Allah's permission. *Al-Raḥmān* tells us that the gate of pleading on that Day is staffed by Mercy itself. *Raḍiya lahu qawlan* tells us that even the speech is weighed.

*Lā yuḥīṭūna bihi ʿilmā* tells us that even our most ambitious project — the project of knowing Him fully — is not within our reach.

In every direction, the ayah is teaching: *receive, do not engineer.*

The Day of Judgment is not the day when our strategies finally pay off. It is the day when our strategies are revealed for what they were — local, partial, temporary — and the only currency that travels is *raḥmah received and lived*.

The relationship with Allah is not the relationship of mastery. It is the relationship of *standing before* — receiving His mercy, bowing within His knowing, speaking what He approves, hearing what He says.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I trying to engineer mercy that is only His to grant — building leverage and connections to "make my case" before a Court that does not work that way?

2. Which of my words to Allah, if weighed by *raḍiya lahu qawlan*, would I want to keep? Which would I want to take back? What is the pattern of speech I am forming right now in His presence?

3. Have I confused *encompassing knowledge of Allah* with *relationship with Allah*? Have I made my faith hostage to questions that *lā yuḥīṭūna bihi ʿilmā* tells me will never close?

4. What strategies have I built for safety in this world that will not travel through the gate? What would it look like to begin transferring weight from those strategies into the currency of *raḥmah*?

5. When I stand in *salah* and say *Allāhu Akbar*, do I mean it? Do I let Him be greater than my model of Him?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day when every strategy collapses, the gate is staffed by Mercy and the One who governs it cannot be encompassed by our knowing — and so the only posture left to us, then and now, is to stand and receive what He grants and to speak words He would approve.

### The Closing Invitation

You are walking toward this Day. Every breath is a step.

The ayah is not asking you to fear. It is inviting you to *practice now the posture that will be required then*. To begin to release the strategies that will not travel. To begin to invest in *raḥmah* — given and received. To begin to enjoy the freedom of not having to encompass Him. To begin to weigh your words to Him with the seriousness of one who knows that on that Day even the intercessors' words are weighed.

Nothing about this is heavy. It is, in fact, the lightest invitation in the world. To stop carrying what you were never meant to carry. To stand before the One whose mercy is the architecture of the gate, the One whose knowing surrounds you on every side, and to be — finally — a creature, before a Lord, exactly as you were made to be.

### A Brief Du'a

Yā Raḥmān, You whose mercy is the gate of every Day —
make us among those whose names are spoken in voices You have permitted,
whose words You approve when we stand in front of You,
and whose hearts have stopped trying to surround what was never theirs to surround.

Let *Allāhu Akbar* be true on our tongues and true in our chests.
And when the voices are hushed and only footfalls are heard,
hush our hearts in the same direction —
toward You, the Most Merciful, the One who knows us before and behind,
and whom we will never encompass, and never need to.

Āmīn.
