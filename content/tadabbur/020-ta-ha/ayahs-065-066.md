# When the Ropes Seemed to Run

## The Passive Voice That Tells the Truth About Illusion

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let's place ourselves in the scene before we open a single word.

The court is open. This is not a private audience with Pharaoh. The whole city has been summoned — Surah al-Shu'ara' tells us they were called on the day of festival, in the bright morning, with the people gathered. *"Are you assembled?"* Pharaoh asks his crowd. The stadium is full.

On one side: the most accomplished sorcerers in Egypt. They have been promised wealth and proximity to the throne if they win. They are the experts of perception. They are the ones who shape what people see.

On the other side: a man who, only verses earlier, was speaking to a bush in a quiet valley. A man who, in his own internal monologue, had asked Allah for help with his speech. A man whose own brother had to be sent to him because he was afraid to stand alone.

Now he stands. The crowd is waiting. The magicians look at him.

And they offer him a courtesy.

> **"O Musa, either you throw, or we will be the first to throw."**

Notice what's happening underneath that sentence. They are pretending to give him a choice. *Symmetrical, fair, gentlemanly.* But the choice is rigged. If he throws first, his single staff will be visually drowned in the spectacle that follows. If they throw first, the field will be flooded with their illusion before he ever moves. Either way, the magicians believe they have him.

What Musa says next is one of the calmest sentences in the Quran. And what happens *after* he says it contains a single word — one passive verb — that quietly tells us everything about how illusion works, how it has always worked, and how it works on us today.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because we are not going to start with the magic. We are going to start with how Musa answered them.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### Element One: **بَلْ أَلْقُوا** *(bal alqū)* — "Rather, you throw."

Before we open Musa's words, let's teach a small grammatical concept that most translations cannot carry into English.

In Arabic there is a particle called **بَلْ** *(bal)*. It is a particle of *redirection*. Not "no" — that would be **لا** *(lā)*. Not "but" in the sense of softening — that would be **لكن** *(lākin)*. **Bal** is the word you use when someone has framed a situation incorrectly and you intend to correct the frame entirely.

If a person says to you, *"Did you fail because you were lazy or because you weren't capable?"* — and you answer, *"No, neither,"* — that is **lā**. If you answer, *"Well, partly,"* — that is **lākin**. But if you answer, *"Bal — actually, I succeeded,"* — that is **bal**. It is not a response to the question. It is a refusal of the question's premise.

Now hold that in your mind, and look again at what the magicians said.

> *"O Musa — either you throw, or we throw first."*

That is a framed question. It pretends symmetry. It pretends choice. It pretends a contest between two performers.

And Musa answers:

> **بَلْ أَلْقُوا**
> *"Rather, you throw."*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. He does *not* say, *"Okay, you throw."* He does not say, *"Fine, go ahead."* He does not even acknowledge the false courtesy of "either/or." He uses **bal** — the particle that quietly refuses the entire framing.

What is the framing he is refusing?

The framing was: *we are two parties in a competition, and someone has to go first.* Musa's **bal** says: *we are not two parties. There is no competition. Whatever you bring, bring it.*

Listen to how the imperative lands. **أَلْقُوا** — *throw, all of you.* Plural imperative, no soft particle, no negotiation. Not *"if you wish"* — just *"throw."*

There is a kind of authority in the human voice that comes only when the speaker is no longer trying to win. That voice is not aggressive. It is not raised. It does not flinch. It simply names the next action, because the speaker already knows the ending.

That is the voice of **bal alqū**.

Here's what's remarkable. We know from earlier in this very surah that Musa was anxious. He had said, *"O my Lord, expand for me my chest, and ease for me my task, and untie the knot from my tongue"* — that is verse 25 through 27 of this same surah. He had asked for Harun because he was worried about being unable to speak. He had said in Surah al-Shu'ara', *"They will deny me, and my chest will be constricted, and my tongue will not be fluent."* This is a man who *knew* he was not naturally a public speaker.

And now, in the most public setting of his life, before the most hostile audience he will ever face, his entire response to the framing of the contest is two words: **bal alqū**.

[PAUSE]

What changed? Nothing about him. Something about what he was leaning on. Allah had told him, *"Do not fear; verily you are the highest"* — that comes two ayahs later, but the structure of certainty was already entering him. The grammar of his answer is the grammar of a man who has stopped negotiating with the framing of the world.

Sit with this for a moment. How much of our anxiety is the anxiety of accepting a question that should have been refused? How many of our exhausting debates begin because we said *"option A or option B"* when the right answer was **bal** — *neither; the frame is wrong*.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: **يُخَيَّلُ إِلَيْهِ** *(yukhayyalu ilayhi)* — "It was made to appear to him."

Now we come to the word that, if you understand nothing else from this entire reflection, will change how you see illusion for the rest of your life.

First the concept. In Arabic, verbs have *forms* — patterns of derivation that change the meaning. From a single root, you can build active voice and passive voice, intensification, causation, reflexivity. The form a word takes is part of its meaning.

Form II — the doubled-middle pattern, *fa''ala* — often carries intensification or causation: *to make something happen.*

The passive voice — *yuf'alu* — is what you use when you do not want to specify the doer; when the action is being done *to* someone, not *by* them.

Now hold both of those, and look at this word: **يُخَيَّلُ** *(yukhayyalu)*.

It comes from the root **خ-ي-ل** *(kh-y-l)*, which gives us words like *khayāl* — *imagination, image, mental picture.* Form II of this root, **خَيَّلَ** *(khayyala)*, means *to cause something to appear in the imagination of another; to project an image into someone's perception.*

Now make that passive: **يُخَيَّلُ** — *it is being projected into someone's perception.* It is being made to appear. The shape of the word is itself confessing what is happening.

Look at how Allah describes what happened in the courtyard:

> **فَإِذَا حِبَالُهُمْ وَعِصِيُّهُمْ يُخَيَّلُ إِلَيْهِ مِن سِحْرِهِمْ أَنَّهَا تَسْعَىٰ**
> *"And suddenly their ropes and their staffs — yukhayyalu ilayhi — were made to appear to him, from their magic, as if they were moving with purpose."*

[PAUSE]

Stay with the verb. Allah does not say *"their ropes became snakes."* Allah does not say *"their staffs were transformed."* Allah does not even say *"they moved."* Allah uses the passive of Form II of *kh-y-l* — the verb of mental projection.

Here is the precision. The ropes did not change. The staffs did not change. The *perception* of the ropes and staffs is what was being acted upon. The magicians' magic was not changing reality; it was bending sight.

This is one of the most important theological precisions in the entire Quran. And it is carried by *the form of a single verb*.

Now look at the prepositional phrase that follows. **مِن سِحْرِهِمْ** — *from their magic.* That **min** is doing surgical work. *Min* names the source. The illusion is sourced *from* their magic — meaning the cause of the appearance is the sorcery, not any actual change in the staffs themselves.

And then the most chilling part. Look who the illusion is happening *to*: **إِلَيْهِ** — *to him.* To Musa. *To the prophet.*

[PAUSE]

Let that land. The Quran is not telling us, *"the magic worked on the crowd but Musa saw through it."* The Quran is telling us the opposite. *Even Musa* — chosen, sent, addressed by Allah on the mountain — *even his eyes* received the projection. He saw what everyone else saw. The ropes appeared, *to him*, to be moving.

This matters more than you might think. It means the Quran is *not* claiming that prophetic certainty grants you immunity from illusion. It is not promising you, *"if you have iman, you will always see clearly."* No. It is telling you that perception, even the perception of the prophet, can be acted upon.

So what was the difference between Musa and the crowd?

Not what his eyes saw. What he was *standing on* while his eyes saw it.

Now look at the second word inside the illusion: **تَسْعَىٰ** *(tas'ā)*.

The root **س-ع-ي** *(s-'-y)* is one of the great verbs of the Quran. It is not just *to move.* It is *to move with intent, with purpose, with striving.* It is the same verb used for the running of Hagar between Safa and Marwa. It is the verb used in *"And that man shall have nothing except what he strives for"* — *illā mā saʿā*.

This is the verb of *purposive motion* — the motion of a creature that *wants* something.

And it is used here for *inanimate ropes and staffs.*

[PAUSE]

This is how vivid the illusion was. The ropes did not just *appear* to move — they appeared to move with *intent*. As if they were creatures who *wanted* to come at him. The eye did not just register motion; it registered *agency*. A rope that looks like it is sliding is one thing. A rope that looks like it is *hunting you* is another.

This is what the magicians were experts at. They did not change matter. They projected agency onto matter, in the eye of the beholder.

[PAUSE]

Now bring it together: **yukhayyalu** — *was made to appear* — passive, projection. **min siḥrihim** — *from their magic* — sourced not in reality but in their craft. **annahā tas'ā** — *that they were moving with purpose* — projected agency.

Three words, in the order Allah placed them, build a complete theology of illusion: *something is made to appear, from a deceptive source, as if it had a purposeful life of its own.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

We have now seen two things.

We have seen Musa refuse the framing of the contest with one particle: **bal**.

And we have seen Allah describe the illusion not as a transformation of reality but as a projection onto perception, with a passive verb whose very shape carries the truth.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about how a human being is supposed to stand inside an age in which what we *see* and what *is* are constantly being separated by powerful, well-funded craftsmen.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: Illusion Is Real in Perception, but It Is Not Reality

You have, almost certainly, had this experience.

You see a video of someone you know. Their face moves, their voice speaks, the words come out in their cadence. And you find out later that none of it happened. The image was generated. The voice was synthesized. The face was real but the words were never spoken.

Or this. You read a thread on a social platform. A scandal. Photos. Quotes. Outrage. Within a day you have an opinion, an emotion, a position. A week later it turns out the thread was wrong, or stitched, or out of context. But the feeling — the feeling lingers in your body even after the story is corrected. Some part of you still feels what you felt the first time.

This is the world we live in. Not metaphorically — literally. The most lucrative industries on earth right now are industries of *projecting agency onto matter that has none*. Of making things *seem to move with purpose* when they have none. Of making things *appear, to you, from a craft of theirs.*

We have a word for these industries. The Quran also has a word. The Quran's word is **siḥr**.

Now, before you say, *"I don't believe in magic"* — listen carefully to what the Quran is actually claiming about *siḥr* in this very ayah. It is not claiming the staffs became snakes. It is claiming that *images of moving snakes were projected into the eyes of those watching*. The Quran's category of *siḥr* is *the manipulation of perception by skilled craft, sourced not in truth but in deception, made to seem like agency*.

Read that again. **The manipulation of perception by skilled craft, sourced not in truth but in deception, made to seem like agency.**

That is a description of half the screens you look at every day.

[PAUSE]

So here is the first thing this ayah teaches us. **Reality is not the same as your perception of reality.** The ropes were ropes the whole time. While Musa was looking at them, while the crowd was screaming, while Pharaoh was nodding — the ropes were ropes. They had not become snakes. They were not moving. The motion was in the eye, sourced in the craft.

This means: *something can be vivid, terrifying, universally seen, emotionally devastating, and still not real.* The vividness of an experience is not proof of its truth. The unanimity of a crowd's response is not proof of its truth. The power of your own emotional reaction is not proof of its truth.

There is a craft that makes things *appear* to move with purpose. There is another craft that makes them *actually* move with purpose. These two crafts are not the same, even when they look identical to the eye.

This is the first reorientation. *What you see is not, by itself, evidence of what is.*

[PAUSE]

So what changes things? Look at what happened next, in verse 69. Allah tells Musa, *"Throw what is in your right hand. It will swallow what they have crafted; what they have crafted is only the trickery of a magician — and the magician does not succeed wherever he comes."*

Notice the phrase: **kayd sāḥir** — *the trickery of a magician*. Not the *power* of a magician. The *trickery* — the skilled deception. And then Musa throws his staff, and it does not *appear* to do anything. It actually *swallows* the ropes. The ropes vanish. The illusion dissolves. Reality reasserts itself, suddenly and totally.

When the truth comes, illusion does not need to be argued with. It needs to be *swallowed*.

This is why, at the very end of this scene, the magicians themselves are the first to fall in prostration. *They* knew what they had done was illusion. They were the experts. So when they saw something that was *not* illusion, they recognized it instantly. The crowd had to be told. The magicians did not.

This means something quietly powerful: *the people most likely to recognize truth are sometimes the people who have most intimately known how illusion is made.* The crowd may take longer. The magicians know immediately.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes one thing. The next time something on a screen, in a thread, in a public spectacle, in a courtroom, in a stadium of voices — the next time something appears to you to be *moving with purpose* against the truth, *pause*. Ask the question Musa was already living inside: *Is this real, or is this yukhayyalu? Is this what is, or is this what was made to appear, from someone's craft, as if it had agency?*

You will not always know the answer. Even Musa's eyes saw the illusion. But there is a different question you *can* answer: *what am I standing on while I look at this?*

Which brings us to the second theme.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: Even Prophets Are Not Immune to Illusion — But They Are Not Destroyed by It

Here is something we tend to skip over because it embarrasses our piety.

The very next verse — verse 67 — says: **فَأَوْجَسَ فِى نَفْسِهِۦ خِيفَةً مُّوسَىٰ** — *"And Musa sensed within himself a fear."*

He felt fear. He, Musa. The man who refused the framing with *bal*. The man chosen on the mountain. He saw the ropes appearing to move, and *something inside him stirred with fear*.

There is a tendency, when we read about prophets, to imagine them as untouchable. As if iman is a coat of armor that means you do not feel fear, do not flinch, do not have your perception affected. As if the goal of the religious life is to become someone whose surface is undisturbed by anything.

This ayah will not allow that fantasy. *Yukhayyalu **ilayhi*** — *it appeared to him.* The prophet's eyes received the projection. The prophet's heart sensed fear.

[PAUSE]

So what is the religious life, then, if not the absence of fear?

Look at what Allah said to him in verse 68: **لَا تَخَفْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ الْأَعْلَىٰ** — *"Do not fear; verily, you — you are the highest."*

Allah did not say, *"There was no reason to be afraid."* Allah did not say, *"You should have known better."* Allah said, *"Do not fear, because of who you are standing with — verily, you are the highest."*

This is enormous. The religious life is *not* the elimination of fear. The religious life is the relocation of *what you stand on* while fear is happening.

Musa's eyes still saw the ropes moving. His body still felt the stir of fear. *And he did not move.* He did not retreat, did not negotiate, did not flinch from the spot. Because his standing was not in his eyes and not in his body; his standing was in **innaka anta al-aʿlā** — *verily, you are the highest, by what We have placed under you.*

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest help this ayah offers a person living in the modern world.

You will see things that frighten you. You will see things that appear to be *moving with purpose* against everything you love. You will see, on screens and in real life, illusions so vivid that your body will register them as real and your heart will sense fear.

This is not a sign of weak iman. This is a sign of being a human being inside the world Allah created, in which sorcery — the projection of false agency — has always been one of the trades of those who do not love truth.

What is asked of you is not that your eyes stop seeing the illusion. What is asked of you is that *while* your eyes see it, *while* your heart stirs with fear, *you do not move from your place*. Because what you stand on is not what you see.

[PAUSE]

There is a final image. After the magicians fall in prostration, Pharaoh threatens them with crucifixion. They answer, *"We will never prefer you over what has come to us of clear proofs."* The very people who, an hour ago, were the experts of illusion — are now the people most willing to die for what is not illusion.

Why? Because they knew, more than anyone in the stadium, the difference between *yukhayyalu* and *real*. They had spent their whole lives making things *appear*. So when they saw something that did not merely appear — when they saw the ropes actually *swallowed*, not visually, but in fact — they had no resistance left.

Sometimes the people who have lived inside illusion the longest are the ones who recognize truth the fastest.

This is hope. For yourself. For others. For people you have given up on. The same eye that has been deceived a thousand times is also an eye that, when it finally sees, sees with the certainty of a former magician.

What does this change about how you live tomorrow? It changes how you treat the people in your life who *have* been deceived — including yourself. Their having been deceived is not, in itself, a verdict on them. It may be the very thing that, when truth arrives, makes them prostrate first.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

---

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have seen.

The linguistic discoveries: **bal** — the particle that refuses a false frame. **Yukhayyalu** — the passive that confesses where illusion lives. **Min siḥrihim** — the source-marker that names the craft. **Tas'ā** — the verb of *purposive* motion, projected onto matter that has none.

The themes: *Illusion is real in perception but not in reality. Even prophets are not immune to illusion, but they are not destroyed by it.*

These are not two different lessons. They are the same lesson approached from two doors.

The lesson is this: **Allah, in this ayah, is teaching you exactly what was happening, in His own grammar — not in the magicians' grammar.** The magicians wanted you to believe the staffs *had become* snakes. Allah's verb refuses that lie. The grammar itself is on the side of truth. Even when Musa's eye was deceived, the Quran's verb was not.

This is why we read the Quran. The world is full of voices telling us what is happening. Some of them are sourced *from their magic*. The Quran is sourced from the One who knows what is.

And when you read it carefully — when you let a single passive verb correct your seeing — you begin to inherit a way of looking that is no longer at the mercy of the projections of the age.

### Questions to Carry

1. What in your life right now is **yukhayyalu** — *appearing* to be something, projected from a craft you cannot see, dressed in *tas'ā* — the appearance of purposeful motion against you? And what would it mean to ask, with Musa's calm: *is this real, or is this made to appear?*

2. Where, today, are you arguing inside a framing that you should be refusing with **bal**? Where is your exhaustion the exhaustion of accepting a false either/or?

3. When fear stirs inside you while looking at something powerful and frightening, what are you standing on? Not what do you see — but what is *under* you while you see it?

4. Who in your life has spent years inside illusion — and might, the moment truth lands, prostrate before anyone else does?

5. If you could only inherit one verb from this ayah and let it govern how you watch the screens of your life — would it be **yukhayyalu**?

### The Closing Invitation

Read the news tomorrow with this verse under your tongue. Watch a thread, an image, a viral spectacle. And let one Arabic word travel with you: **yukhayyalu**. *Made to appear.* From a source. As if it had purpose.

That word will not stop you from feeling what you feel. Even Musa felt fear. But it will quietly, repeatedly, ask you: *what is real, and what is being projected?*

Over time, that question rebuilds the eye.
