# No Touch, and the God Who Encompasses All Things

## The Ayah

> [ayah:20:97] قَالَ فَٱذْهَبْ فَإِنَّ لَكَ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ أَن تَقُولَ لَا مِسَاسَ ۖ وَإِنَّ لَكَ مَوْعِدًا لَّن تُخْلَفَهُۥ ۖ وَٱنظُرْ إِلَىٰٓ إِلَـٰهِكَ ٱلَّذِى ظَلْتَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفًا ۖ لَّنُحَرِّقَنَّهُۥ ثُمَّ لَنَنسِفَنَّهُۥ فِى ٱلْيَمِّ نَسْفًا
>
> [ayah:20:98] إِنَّمَآ إِلَـٰهُكُمُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِى لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۚ وَسِعَ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا

*Qāla fa-dhhab fa-inna laka fī-l-ḥayāti an taqūla lā misāsa, wa inna laka mawʿidan lan tukhlafah. Wa-nẓur ilā ilāhika-lladhī ẓalta ʿalayhi ʿākifā, la-nuḥarriqannahu thumma la-nansifannahu fī-l-yammi nasfā. Innamā ilāhukumu-llāhu-lladhī lā ilāha illā huw, wasiʿa kulla shayʾin ʿilmā.*

**"He said: 'Then go! For you, in this life, it is that you will say: *No touch.* And indeed, you have an appointment you will never fail to keep. And look at your god, the one you stayed bent over in devotion — we will surely burn it, then scatter it, utterly, into the sea. Your god is only Allah, other than whom there is no god. He has encompassed all things in knowledge.'"**

## Standing at the Foot of the Mountain

Let me set you down inside the moment before we touch a single word.

Mūsā has been gone forty nights. Up on Ṭūr, in a solitude so complete that Allah spoke to him without intermediary — the same surah opened with that voice, *"Innī anā rabbuka"*, "Indeed, I am your Lord" — Mūsā received the Tablets. He came down the mountain carrying the literal handwriting of guidance, the direct speech of God pressed into stone, still warm from the encounter.

And he walked into a disaster.

In his absence, the people he had led through a parted sea — the people whose enemy had been drowned in front of their eyes just weeks before — had melted down their gold, and a man among them, the one the Qur'an calls *as-Sāmirī*, had shaped it into a calf. And the calf did something. It made a sound. *Khuwār* — a lowing, a hollow bovine moan pushed through the metal by the wind. And that sound was enough. A shape that could low. A god that could make a noise. And the nation bowed.

Sit with the vertigo of that for a moment. These are not pagans who never knew better. These are people fresh out of the water of their own salvation. And the instant their prophet's back is turned, a lowing piece of jewelry is enough to pull them down onto their faces.

Now understand where this sits in the surah, because Ta-Ha is doing something deliberate. This whole surah is the story of Mūsā, and it is being told to console the Prophet ﷺ in the loneliest stretch of Makkah — *told to a man whose own people are rejecting him.* And the surah keeps circling one question underneath the story: **who really speaks, and who really knows?** It opened with the true Voice — Allah speaking to Mūsā at the fire. And here, at its low point, we get the counterfeit voice: a calf that can low but cannot answer, cannot guide, cannot know. Two sounds. One is the speech of the One who encompasses everything. The other is wind in a hollow statue.

Mūsā returns in fury. He confronts his brother Hārūn — grabs him by the beard. And then he turns to the man who built the thing. He questions the Sāmirī, and the Sāmirī gives his infamous answer in the verse just before ours: *"I saw what they did not see, so I grabbed a handful from the track of the messenger, and I threw it in."* He reached out his hand. He grabbed. He took hold of something.

Hold onto that. He grabbed with his hand.

Because in the very next breath — our ayah — Mūsā pronounces the sentence. And [PAUSE] the sentence is going to rhyme with the crime in a way so precise it should make the hair on your arms stand up.

Here is the hook, and I'm going to leave it hanging over us for the next while: **the punishment Allah chose for this man is not fire, not the sword, not stoning. It is a single phrase he will have to say, out loud, to every human being he ever meets again, for the rest of his life.** Two words. And when you understand why *those* two words, you will understand something about sin, about isolation, and about what it costs to be devoted to the wrong thing — that you will not be able to un-see.

Let's begin.

## Part One: The Journey Through the Words

### The word he must say for the rest of his life — *lā misāsa*

Before we open the phrase, let me put a very ordinary human scene in front of you, because Arabic is about to do something you'll feel faster if you've already felt it in your own body.

Think about what it means to be *un-touchable.* Not metaphorically. Physically. Think of the moment in a hospital when someone is behind glass and you press your hand to the glass and they press theirs to the other side and the whole grief of it is that the hands do not meet. Think of the child nobody will sit next to at lunch. Think — and I'm sorry to make you feel it — of the last time you badly needed someone to just put a hand on your shoulder, and no one did. Human beings are held together by touch. A newborn who is fed but never held will fail to thrive and can die. We do not merely *enjoy* being touched. We are assembled by it.

Hold that. Now the phrase.

*Lā misāsa.* "No touch."

The word *misās* comes from the root *mīm–sīn–sīn* — *massa*, to touch, to make contact, skin to skin. And notice, first, the sound of it. Say it. *Misās.* It's soft. It hisses — that doubled *sīn* is almost a whisper, almost a caress. **The word for the thing he can never again have is shaped like the very gentleness it denies him.** The sound of the sentence is a hand reaching out; the meaning of the sentence is the hand pulled back. The phonetics and the punishment are pulling against each other, and that tension is the ache of the whole sentence.

Now, watch the grammar, because there is a gap here that most people walk right past — and the gap is the whole thing.

Mūsā does not say, "No one will touch you." He does not say, "You are cast out." He says: *"Laka fī-l-ḥayāti an **taqūla** lā misāsa"* — "For you, in this life, is that **you will say**: no touch."

Do you feel what just happened? [PAUSE]

**The punishment is not that others avoid him. The punishment is that *he* must be the one to announce it.** The verb *taqūla*, "you say," puts the sentence in his own mouth. For the rest of his life, whenever another human being draws near, *he* is the one who has to call out — "no touch, no touch, stay back." He becomes the herald of his own exile. He has to publish his own untouchability with his own voice, over and over, at every approach, until he dies.

Now go back and pick up the thread I told you to hold. What did he do to earn this? *He reached out his hand and grabbed a handful.* His crime was an act of the hand — a taking, a grasping, a contact. And the sentence Allah selects, out of all possible sentences, is an eternity in which no hand may ever meet his again, and in which his own mouth must forbid it.

This is not random severity. This is *justice with a mirror in it.* The Qur'an calls this pattern out elsewhere — *jazāʾan wifāqā*, a recompense that fits, that matches the shape of the deed. The hand that grasped what it should not have grasped is condemned to a life where no hand will ever grasp it in warmth again. What this invites us to sit with is unbearable in its precision: **that the very faculty we abuse can become the exact site of our loss.**

And there is one more layer, and it is the deepest, and I want to be honest that I am now moving from the grammar into what the grammar *opens.* The classical commentators note that this "no touch" made the Sāmirī a wanderer, shunned, and that some of his descendants carried the phrase. But sit with what isolation *is* as a punishment. He built a thing to draw the crowd to himself. He wanted to be the center. He said, "I saw what they did not see" — *I, I, the one who knows better.* And the sentence takes the man who made himself the center of the people and makes him the one point in the nation that no one may approach. **The reward for pulling a whole community toward a false center is to spend your life being the thing everyone steps around.** He got exactly what he was reaching for — a life defined entirely by other people's relationship to him — and it was turned inside out into horror.

That is the first word. *Lā misāsa.* No touch. [PAUSE — let it sit.]

### Two gods, side by side — the one you can burn, and the One who cannot be measured

Let me build you a second everyday picture before we go back in, because the next thing the ayah does only lands if you've felt the contrast in something you can hold.

Imagine two things on a table in front of you. On the left, an object — anything you own that you'd panic to lose. Your phone, with your whole life on it. On the right, nothing you can see — just the fact of your own awareness, the thing doing the looking. Now imagine I take the object on the left, set it on fire, and grind the ash into powder, and blow the powder off my palm into the wind until not one particle of it exists in any recoverable form. It is *gone.* Fully. And now notice: the awareness on the right — the "you" watching me do it — is completely untouched. You cannot burn the second thing. You cannot even locate it to burn it. One of these things can be reduced to nothing by a match. The other cannot be reached by fire at all.

Hold that difference. **A god you can destroy, and a God fire cannot find.** Now watch the ayah lay both of them on the table in a single breath.

First, the false one. Mūsā turns to the Sāmirī and says: *"Wa-nẓur ilā ilāhika-lladhī ẓalta ʿalayhi ʿākifā"* — "And **look** at your god, the one you stayed bent over in devotion."

Two words there are doing quiet, brutal work.

*ʿĀkifan* — the one bent over it, devoted, clinging. This is from the root *ʿayn–kāf–fāʾ*, and here is the thing that should stop you: it is the exact same root as *iʿtikāf* — the sacred retreat, when a believer secludes himself in the masjid in the last nights of Ramaḍān, bent low in devotion to Allah, refusing to leave His house. **The Qur'an describes the calf-worship using the vocabulary of worship at its most beautiful.** The Sāmirī performed a perfect *iʿtikāf* — the posture, the constancy, the refusal to leave — aimed at a lump of ringing metal. This is what makes idolatry so quietly terrifying: from the outside, the devotion can look identical. The bent back, the long hours, the refusal to be pulled away. The only difference — the *only* one — is what you are bent over.

And then Mūsā says, *"look at it."* *Unẓur.* Really look. Because he is about to show the Sāmirī exactly what his god is made of.

*"La-nuḥarriqannahu thumma la-nansifannahu fī-l-yammi nasfā"* — "We will **surely burn** it, then we will **surely scatter** it, into the sea, an utter scattering."

Listen to the sounds now, because Arabic is not describing the destruction, it is *performing* it. *La-nuḥarriqannahu* — that heavy doubled letter and the emphatic *nūn* land like the crackle of the fire itself. And then *nasfan* — *nūn, sīn, fāʾ* — say it — it's a puff of air, a scattering, the sound of powder blown off a hand. **The very phonetics disintegrate as the calf disintegrates.** And notice both verbs carry the emphatic *lām* and the doubled *nūn* — in Arabic this is the strongest possible form of a promise. Not "we might," not "we will." *We absolutely, unfailingly will.* The same emphatic form the ayah just used for the Sāmirī's own appointment — *mawʿidan lan tukhlafah*, "an appointment you will never fail to keep." The god's destruction and the servant's judgment are pronounced in identical, unbreakable grammar. Their fates are welded together by the syntax itself.

And then that last word: *nasfā.* This is what Arabic calls the *maf ʿūl muṭlaq*, the absolute object — where you repeat the verb's own root as a noun to intensify it. *"We will scatter it — a scattering."* There is no clean English for it. It means: *thoroughly, completely, leaving nothing, a scattering that is the definition of scattering.* Not a trace. Not a filing. Not one recoverable grain.

Now — where does it go? *Fī-l-yammi* — into *the yamm*, the sea. And here is a thread that runs through this very surah, so quietly you'd miss it. The word *al-yamm* appears earlier in Ta-Ha twice: it is the *yamm* that Mūsā's mother was told to cast his basket into as an infant — and the sea gave him back. And it is the *yamm* that closed over Pharaoh and his army and did not give them back. **In this one surah, the sea is the hand of God's reversal** — it returns the one it was meant to return, it swallows the one it was meant to swallow. And now the calf's ashes go into that same sea, to be swallowed like Pharaoh, dispersed beyond recovery. The false god ends where the false king ended: erased by the water.

So there is the first thing on the table. A "god" you can burn, grind, and pour into the sea until it is nothing.

And now — the second thing. The next breath.

*"Innamā ilāhukumu-llāhu-lladhī lā ilāha illā huw, wasiʿa kulla shayʾin ʿilmā."* "Your god is **only** Allah, other than whom there is no god. He has **encompassed all things** in knowledge."

Feel the whiplash. We just watched a god get reduced to powder that fits in the palm of a hand. And now: the true God, described by the single attribute of *wasiʿa kulla shayʾin ʿilmā* — He whose knowledge **contains every single thing that exists.** The calf could be contained in a fist. Allah *contains* everything. The calf could not even know it was being burned. Allah's knowledge encloses the fire, the ash, the sea, the man, the atoms of the scattered dust, and the very thought you are thinking right now about all of it.

Look at that last word: *ʿilman.* Knowledge. It comes at the end as another *tamyīz*, a word of specification — it answers "encompassed all things *in what?*" In knowledge. He didn't just say "Allah is vast." He specified the mode of the vastness: not merely power, not merely presence — *knowing.* The false god was a thing that made a hollow sound and knew nothing, not even itself. The true God is defined here by the one thing the calf most conspicuously lacked: **He knows.** Remember the surah's buried question — who really speaks, and who really knows? The calf could make a sound. It could not know. And here, at the summit, is the answer: your god is the One whose knowing has no edge.

That is the second thing on the table. And you cannot burn it. You cannot find it to burn it. Fire is inside its knowledge, not the other way around.

**One god fit in a hand. The other holds the hand, the fire, and the one who lit it.** [PAUSE]

### The turned shoulder — from "your god" to "your god"

There is one more thing in these two ayat, and it is so subtle that in English it vanishes completely. But once you see it, you will feel it every time you read the passage.

Let me teach you the tool first. In Arabic, "you" is not one word — it changes with number. *-ka* is "you," singular, one person. *-kum* is "you," plural, a group. And the Qur'an will sometimes *switch* mid-passage, turning from one person to a crowd, or from a crowd to one person. The classical scholars have a name for this pivot — *iltifāt*, literally "the turning of the face," like a speaker who is talking to one man and then physically turns his shoulders to address the whole room. It is never decorative. When the address turns, *someone's relationship to the speaker has just changed.*

Now watch the pronouns walk across these two verses.

The entire sentence against the Sāmirī is singular. *Laka* — for **you** (one). *Ilāhika* — **your** (one) god. *ẓalta*, *ʿalayhi* — **you** (one) stayed bent over it. Every syllable is aimed at one man. This is a private sentencing. Mūsā is standing face to face with the criminal, and the pronouns pin the Sāmirī in place like a spotlight on one figure.

And then — the second verse — the shoulder turns.

*"Innamā **ilāhukumu**-llāh"* — "**Your** god" — and now it is *-kum.* Plural. The whole nation.

Do you feel him turn? [PAUSE] Mūsā finishes with the Sāmirī — *"go, and say 'no touch'"* — and then he lifts his eyes off the one guilty man and turns his whole body to the crowd, the thousands who bowed, and he says to *all of them*: "Your god — every one of you — is only Allah."

And here is why that turn breaks your heart if you let it. **The moment of judgment becomes the moment of mercy.** He does not spend one extra word on the Sāmirī. The individual is sentenced and released from the frame. But the nation — the ones who were seduced, who are standing there ashamed, who worshipped a calf weeks after being saved — *they* get taught. He turns from the man beyond saving to the people who can still be saved. The turning of the face *is* the mercy: it says, "you, the crowd, are not the Sāmirī. There is still a lesson being spoken *to* you, which means there is still a door open *for* you."

The absence here is as loud as the presence. Notice what the Sāmirī does *not* get: no correction, no invitation, no "your god is Allah" addressed to him. He gets a sentence and a turned back. The nation gets the sentence's opposite — a teaching, a "you." **Allah withheld the invitation from the one and gave it to the many, and the withholding and the giving happen in the same breath, marked only by one changed letter** — *-ka* becoming *-kum.* One letter is the whole difference between "you are finished" and "you can still come home."

That is what the grammar has shown us: a punishment that mirrors the crime, two gods weighed against each other on a single table, and a face that turns from the lost to the reachable. Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not about words, but about the life you are living right now.

## Part Two: The Depths

### On the thing you are bent over

I want to start somewhere completely ordinary, because I suspect you already know this feeling and just haven't named it.

There is a certain kind of exhaustion that comes not from working hard, but from *worshipping something that cannot love you back.* You've felt it. The person who has poured fifteen years and every waking thought into a career, and one reorganization email erases the whole identity overnight. The person whose entire sense of self is the number on the scale, or the number of followers, or the reflection in the mirror — bent over it, checking it, tending it, the last thing at night and the first thing in the morning. The parent who has made a child into a god and cannot breathe when the child pulls away. We don't call it worship. We call it "being really committed," "being driven," "caring a lot." But look at the *posture.* Bent over. Constant. Refusing to be pulled away.

That posture has a name in our ayah. *ʿĀkifan.* Bent low in devotion, in retreat, unwilling to leave.

And here is what stops me every time: the Qur'an uses the *worship* word for it. The same root as the believer's sacred retreat in the masjid. Because from the outside — and this is the uncomfortable part — **your devotion to your career and a righteous man's devotion to his Lord can look identical.** Same early mornings. Same sacrifice. Same refusal to be distracted. Same all-consuming focus. The bent back looks the same. The Qur'an is telling us the entire difference lives in one place and one place only: *what you are bent over.*

So the question the ayah puts to you is not "are you devoted?" You are. Everyone is. Human beings are worshipping creatures; we cannot *not* bow to something. The real question is the one Mūsā forces on the Sāmirī: *unẓur ilā ilāhika* — **look at your god.** Really look at it. And then the ayah hands you the test, the single diagnostic that cuts through everything: *la-nuḥarriqannahu.* **Can it burn?**

That's the test. Take the thing you are most bent over. And ask: could it be burned and scattered into the sea? The career — yes, one economic collapse, one illness, one email. The face in the mirror — yes, time is a slow fire, and it is already burning. The wealth — ash. The follower count — a server goes down, a platform dies, ash. The child, may Allah protect them — is mortal, and to make a mortal thing your god is to build your whole house on a floor that everyone eventually falls through.

Everything you can bow to *except One* can be reduced to powder in the palm of a hand. That is not cynicism. That is the ayah's mercy, delivered as a warning. **It is telling you where not to put the full weight of your soul, because it knows what happens to a person whose god burns while they're still bent over it.**

So what changes tomorrow? Not that you stop caring about your work or your body or your people — the believer in *iʿtikāf* still has a body, still has a family, still has work. What changes is the *object at the bottom.* You do the work, but you are bent over the One who gave you the work. You love the child, but you are bent over the One who lent you the child. Everything else moves up one level — from *god* to *gift.* And a gift you can lose without being destroyed, because you never mistook it for the Giver. The next time you feel that specific exhaustion — the frantic tending of a thing you're terrified to lose — let it be an alarm. It is the feeling of being bent over something that can burn. [PAUSE]

### On the sentence we pronounce over ourselves

Now I want to talk about the loneliest word in the passage — *lā misāsa*, "no touch" — because I think it is describing something happening in your city, on your street, maybe in your own chest, right now.

We are living through what public-health researchers have started calling, without exaggeration, a loneliness epidemic. People are more connected by device and more untouched by hand than at any point in human history. And notice the phrase that has entered our own language for how relationships end now: *"going no contact."* We say it about family members, about exes, about former friends. *No contact.* It is almost a literal translation of *lā misāsa.* We have reinvented the Sāmirī's exact sentence and we hand it out casually, and sometimes we deserve to, and sometimes it is the only sane thing to do — and I don't want to flatten any of that. But I want you to notice something about how isolation actually arrives in a life, because the ayah saw it with terrible clarity.

Recall the grammar. The Sāmirī's punishment was not "people will avoid you." It was *"an taqūla lā misāsa"* — "**you will say** 'no touch.'" **He is the one who has to announce his own isolation.** And this is the piece I cannot get past, because it is so true to how it actually happens. We imagine that the lonely person is a victim of others turning away. And sometimes they are. But the ayah shows us the deeper, more common, more haunting mechanism: **isolation is very often self-published.** The person who has done something they cannot face becomes the one who pushes everyone back. "No touch. Don't come close. You wouldn't want to know me." The shame builds the wall, and then the ashamed person mans it, calling out the warning to everyone who approaches, and mistakes the wall they are guarding for a wall others built.

The Sāmirī reached out his hand to grab something forbidden, to make himself the center of everyone's attention. And the exact result was a life spent holding everyone at arm's length. Think about how precisely that describes the arc of so much sin. It always promises *connection* — the affair promises intimacy, the deception promises approval, the grasping for status promises to make you beloved. And it delivers, with perfect irony, the opposite: a self you now have to hide, a distance you now have to enforce, a "no touch" you now have to say. **Sin reaches for closeness and hands you a wall, and then makes you the one who has to keep saying "stay back."**

But — and this is where the two ayat finally embrace each other, and why Allah did not let the passage end on *lā misāsa* — look at what comes immediately after the loneliest word. The verse of the untouchable man is followed *instantly* by *wasiʿa kulla shayʾin ʿilmā* — the God whose knowledge **encompasses all things.**

Sit in that sequence. The last word of the human condition apart from God is *no touch* — walled off, unreachable, self-exiled. And the very next breath is the One whose knowing **contains you** — from whom no distance is possible, whose knowledge has already encompassed the ash, the sea, the wall you built, and the shame that built it. **You can put yourself beyond the reach of every human hand and you cannot put yourself outside the knowledge that encompasses all things.** For the person drowning in the specific loneliness of "no one could touch me if they knew" — the ayah's answer is devastating and it is a mercy: *One already knows, has always known, has encompassed all of it — and is still the One calling you back with the word "your god is only Allah."*

So what does this change on a Tuesday? It changes what you do with shame. Shame's instinct is *lā misāsa* — hide, wall off, push back, self-publish the exile. The ayah offers the opposite motion: turn toward the One whose knowledge already encompasses the very thing you're hiding, so there is nothing left to hide *from Him,* and therefore nothing that has to make you unreachable to *everyone else.* The wall was only ever built to keep out people who might learn what God already knows. When you make your peace with the One who encompasses all things, the wall loses its job. You can let a hand land on your shoulder again. [PAUSE — a long one.]

## Bringing It Together

Now let me show you the architecture, because I have been walking you up the mountain and there is a view from the top that proves the whole climb was real.

Watch the two verbs at the two ends of this passage.

Over the false god, the verb is *nasf* — **to scatter, to disperse, to blow apart** until nothing holds together. *Nansifannahu fī-l-yammi nasfā.* Scattered, utterly.

Over the true God, the verb is *wasiʿa* — **to encompass, to contain, to hold** everything within. *Wasiʿa kulla shayʾin ʿilmā.* He contains all.

That is the hidden spine of the whole passage: **scattering versus encompassing. Dispersal versus containment.** And once you see it there, you see it everywhere in these two ayat:

The false god is *scattered* into the sea — dispersed to nothing. And its devotee, the Sāmirī, is *scattered* out of the human family — *lā misāsa*, dispersed from every hand. He bowed to the thing that comes apart, and he came apart. **What you worship, you resemble. Bow to what scatters, and you will be scattered.**

The true God *encompasses* all things in knowledge. And the nation that turns to Him — *ilāhukum*, "your god," plural, the crowd gathered back in — is gathered *into* the embrace of that knowing, held, contained, never beyond reach. **Bow to the One who encompasses, and you are encompassed. You are held.**

This is why the linguistic journey and the human journey were never two things. The word-level discovery — that the punishment mirrors the crime, that "no touch" is self-announced, that the address turns from one to many — and the life-level discovery — that idolatry exhausts and isolates while *tawḥīd* holds — are the *same insight* seen from two sides. The Sāmirī's *nasf* out of humanity and the calf's *nasf* into the sea and the nation's return into the *wasiʿa* of God's knowledge are one architecture. **Devotion to what disperses will disperse you. Devotion to what encompasses will hold you.** That is the mountain. That is the whole thing.

### Questions to Carry

Not rhetorical. Genuinely sit with these, maybe with a page and a pen.

1. When you look honestly at your own bent back — the thing you tend first in the morning and last at night — *what are you actually bent over?* And could it burn?

2. Is there a *lā misāsa* you are currently saying — a "no touch," a wall, a "you wouldn't want to know me" — and who, exactly, are you keeping out with it? Yourself included?

3. If it is true that One already encompasses in knowledge the very thing you are most hiding — what would change, today, if you stopped hiding it *from Him?*

4. Where in your life have you mistaken a *gift* for a *god* — loved a thing at the level of ultimate weight that was only ever meant to bear the weight of a gift?

5. When you were last shown mercy right in the middle of judgment — a turned shoulder, a "you" that let you back in — did you recognize it as the mercy it was?

### One Sentence to Keep

**A god you can burn is no god at all; a life bent over one ends in "do not touch me" — and the only One worth being bent over is the One whose knowing has already encompassed you, so that no fire can reach Him and no wall can keep Him out.**

### The Invitation

You do not have to tear anything down tonight. You are not being asked to stop loving your work or your people or your life. You are being asked only to *look* — *unẓur* — at what sits at the very bottom of your devotion, and to ask it one honest question: *can it burn?* And if it can, to gently, without panic, move it up one level — from god to gift — and set beneath it the only floor that has never given way beneath any soul that stood on it: *lā ilāha illā huwa.* There is no god but He. Everything else is on loan. He alone encompasses all things.

That is not a smaller life. It is the only life that cannot be scattered.

### A Closing Duʿā

*O Allah, You who have encompassed all things in knowledge — You already know the thing I am most bent over, and You already know the walls I have built and the hands I have pushed away. Un-bend me from every god that can burn, and bind me to You alone, the One no fire can reach. And where I have said "no touch," and made myself unreachable in my shame, draw me back — for I cannot flee the knowledge that encompasses me, and I no longer wish to.* Āmīn.
