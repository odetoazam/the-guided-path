# Those of Reason and the Walk Through Ruins

## Introduction

**Surah 20, Ayahs 127-128**

> وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي مَنْ أَسْرَفَ وَلَمْ يُؤْمِن بِآيَاتِ رَبِّهِ ۚ وَلَعَذَابُ الْآخِرَةِ أَشَدُّ وَأَبْقَىٰ
>
> *Wa kadhālika najzī man asrafa wa lam yu'min bi-āyāti Rabbih, wa la-ʿadhābu l-ākhirati ashaddu wa abqā.*
>
> "And thus do We recompense whoever exceeded and did not believe in the signs of his Lord. And surely the punishment of the Hereafter is more severe and more enduring."

> أَفَلَمْ يَهْدِ لَهُمْ كَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُم مِّنَ الْقُرُونِ يَمْشُونَ فِي مَسَاكِنِهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِّأُولِي النُّهَىٰ
>
> *Afalam yahdi lahum kam ahlaknā qablahum mina l-qurūni yamshūna fī masākinihim, inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-ulī n-nuhā.*
>
> "Has it then not become guidance for them — how many generations We destroyed before them, as they walk among their dwellings? Indeed in that are signs for those of discerning reason."

### Rebuilding the World

To stand inside these ayahs, you have to stand at the end of a long surah.

Surah Ta-Ha opened with two letters, then a remarkable consolation: **"We have not sent down to you the Quran that you should be distressed."** The surah came to a Prophet ﷺ whose nights were exhausting his body and whose days were exhausting his heart. Makkah was not relenting. The same faces, the same mockery, the same refusal to see what was being shown to them.

And then — without obvious transition — Allah took the Prophet ﷺ on the longest sustained narrative of Musa عليه السلام anywhere in the Quran. Musa at the burning bush. Musa with Hārūn before Pharaoh. Musa parting the sea. Musa returning to find his people worshipping a calf made of their own jewelry. Musa confronting the Sāmirī. Eighty-some ayahs of one prophet's life.

You read it and you wonder: why? Why this story, this long, at this moment?

Because the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah was being shown himself in another era. He was Musa. Quraysh was Pharaoh. The believers wavering under pressure were the Banū Isrā'īl who had crossed the sea and then asked for an idol. The whole arc of that journey — from a man trembling in the desert to a community wandering in confusion — was being placed in his ﷺ heart so he would know: you are walking a path that has been walked. The opposition is not new. The straying is not new. **And the divine response is not new.**

Then, near the end of the surah, the camera lifts. We come back to Makkah. To the present. To the people right outside the Prophet's ﷺ door who still refuse.

And the surah does something remarkable. It pivots from the storytelling voice — *qāla, qāla* — to the addressing voice. To the reasoning voice. To the *look around you* voice.

These two ayahs are part of that pivot.

The trade caravans of Quraysh ran two routes — north toward Shām (Syria), south toward Yemen. Both routes passed through landscapes haunted by ruins. The dwellings of ʿĀd. The carved facades of Thamūd at al-Ḥijr. Cities buried by sand, abandoned wells, broken stone. Every Qurashī merchant had walked among these places. Every camel driver knew them.

So when Allah says, **"as they walk among their dwellings"** — *yamshūna fī masākinihim* — He is not speaking abstractly. He is naming a literal experience. They were *literally* walking through the ruins of those whom Allah had destroyed for the same crimes the Quraysh were now committing.

The dwellings were not in a museum. They were on the road home.

### The Hook

Here is what's stunning.

In all 6,236 ayahs of the Quran, Allah uses many descriptors for the people who actually understand. **Ulū l-albāb** — those of *cores*. **Ulū l-abṣār** — those of *sight*. **Ulū l-ʿilm** — those of knowledge. **Ulū l-aydī wa l-abṣār** — those of effort and vision.

But there is one descriptor He uses **only twice in the entire Quran.** Both times in Surah Ta-Ha. Once at the beginning of the Musa narrative, when He describes the signs in nature as proofs *li-ulī n-nuhā*. And once here, at the close of the surah.

**Ulū n-nuhā.**

Translators reach for "intelligence." They reach for "understanding." They reach for "wisdom." Classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, the compilers of *al-Muyassar* — universally gloss أُولُو النُّهَى as *ulū l-ʿuqūl*: those of intellects, those of minds. That is the primary meaning. But Arabic names this particular intellect in a peculiarly telling way. The root of *nuhā* is the same root as *nahy* — to forbid, to restrain, to say no.

The mind is called *nuhā* because it restrains. The intellect earns its name from the function that defines it.

What does that mean? Why is *that* the rare faculty Allah names? And how does it connect to the very first sin Allah names in this passage — *asrafa*, exceeded?

Sit with me. Let's open this slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Asrafa*: The Sin That Comes Before Disbelief

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we look at the word.

In Arabic, the root س-ر-ف (s-r-f) carries a single, vivid image: **going past the limit**. The water that overflows the bowl. The traveler who walks beyond the road. The hand that takes more than its share. The merchant who weighs heavier on his side of the scale. *Saraf* is not "wrong" in some abstract moral sense. It is *exceeding what is yours*.

Now look at the word in the ayah. Allah uses Form IV: **asrafa**. In Arabic morphology, Form IV (*afʿala*) very often takes a state and makes it deliberate, makes it active, makes it *something the person did, not something that happened to them*.

So *asrafa* is not an accident. It is not "he found himself with too much." It is **"he chose to exceed."**

Now watch what Allah does. Listen to the order of the verse:

> *najzī man **asrafa** wa lam yu'min bi-āyāti Rabbih*
>
> "We recompense whoever **exceeded** and did not believe in the signs of his Lord."

What comes first? The disbelief? No.

The disbelief comes second.

The exceeding comes first.

Stay with me. This is doing real work.

In our minds — in the modern moral imagination — we tend to think of disbelief as the source crime. The root sin. The one that, if a person commits, leads them to all the others. *He didn't believe, so he transgressed.*

But Allah, in the precise grammar of this ayah, **inverts the order.**

He doesn't say "whoever disbelieved and exceeded." He says "whoever **exceeded** and did not believe."

What the ayah is naming is a sequence, and the sequence matters. The sequence is: a person first refuses limits. They first decide that no boundary is going to bind them. They first decide that they will eat past full, take past need, speak past truth, claim past entitlement, own past justice. **And then, having gotten used to refusing limits in their life, they refuse the ultimate limit — that they are not God.**

The denial of the signs of the Lord is downstream of the denial of any binding limit at all.

Notice something. This is not a metaphysical claim only. This is a psychology. Allah is describing how a heart actually decays.

A man does not begin by saying *I deny my Creator*. He begins by saying *this rule does not apply to me*. *This boundary is for other people*. *I have earned the right to exceed this just once*. *I am special; I am tired; I deserve.*

And every time the soul exceeds and the sky does not fall, the soul learns a lesson — the wrong lesson. The soul learns: *the limits are negotiable. The limits were never real.*

By the time such a soul stands before the signs of its Lord, it cannot believe in them. Not because the signs are unclear. Because **a heart that has spent a lifetime refusing limits cannot suddenly accept the ultimate limit.** Belief in Allah is, in its essence, the acceptance that I am not Him. That there is something I cannot exceed. That there is a Reality outside my preferences.

A soul trained on transgression cannot make that final acceptance.

[PAUSE]

Now observe how Allah frames the consequence. The verse ends: **"and surely the punishment of the Hereafter is more severe and more enduring."** Classical commentators note that the ayah's logic implies a graduated sequence. Al-Ṭabarī observes that the recompense begins in this world, then extends to the barzakh — that intermediate realm between death and resurrection — where the one who exceeded will face the *maʿīsha ḍanka*, the constricted, grinding life that this surah already named in ayah 124 as the fate of whoever turns away from the reminder. And then, beyond the barzakh, the akhirah punishment awaits: more severe even than what the grave has already delivered, and more enduring.

More enduring — *abqā*. Al-Ṭabarī glosses this word with precision: the akhirah punishment is *ilā ghayri amadin wa lā nihāya* — without term, without end, without any boundary.

Sit with the irony. The one who spent a lifetime *asrafa* — crossing every limit, refusing every boundary — now faces something that itself has no limit. No boundary. No end. The person who refused to be bound encounters something unbounded. The one who would not accept *any* term has been given a consequence that accepts no term.

The Quran's precision is not accidental. It is a kind of terrible symmetry.

[PAUSE]

Now hold the word *asrafa*. We will come back to it. Because Allah is going to name, just one ayah later, the diagnostic of the saved. And what He names will be the **exact opposite** of *asrafa*.

He will name *nuhā*.

### Element Two — *Ulū n-Nuhā*: The Reason That Restrains

The root of this word is ن-ه-ي (n-h-y).

You already know this root. You have heard it your whole Muslim life.

It is the root of **al-amr bi-l-maʿrūf wa-n-nahy ʿani-l-munkar** — commanding the good and *forbidding* the wrong. *Nahy*. To forbid. To restrain. To pull back.

Now in classical Arabic, *nuhā* — with the long final ā — is a noun derived from this root. The lexicographers (al-Rāghib, Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*) note its meaning: it is the *ʿaql* — the intellect, the reasoning faculty — but specifically named by what it *does*, not by what it *contains*. Lisān al-ʿArab is explicit: *النُّهْيَة: العَقْل، سُمِّيَ بذلك لأنه يَنْهَى عن القبيح* — "called thus *because* it restrains from the reprehensible." The classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī, consistently gloss *ulū n-nuhā* as *ulū l-ʿuqūl*: those of minds, those of reason. Al-Muyassar renders the phrase as *ahl al-ʿuqūl al-wāʿiya* — those who possess **attentive, retaining intellects**. Not just any minds, but minds that hold things. Minds that do not let lessons pass through them like water through sand.

Read that again. The mind is described not only by what it produces but by what it *retains*.

The intellect is called *nuhā* because it **forbids its owner from acting on every impulse**. It is the part of the mind whose function is to say *no*.

Compare this with the other words for intellect:

- **ʿAql** itself comes from a root meaning "to tie up, to bind." The same root used for tying up a camel. The intellect is what *binds* the impulse so it does not run.
- **Lubb** (plural *albāb*) comes from a root meaning "core, kernel" — the inner essence that remains after the outer is stripped.
- **Fu'ād** comes from a root associated with burning, the heart's emotional fire.
- **Ḥilm** comes from a root meaning "to be patient, to forbear."

Notice how almost every Arabic word for the deeper mind has a **restraining** quality at its heart. *ʿAql* binds. *Ḥilm* forbears. *Nuhā* forbids.

The Arabic moral imagination did not equate intelligence with cleverness. It did not equate it with verbal facility, or pattern-recognition, or strategic foresight. It equated intelligence with the **capacity to say no to oneself** — and then, with retaining the lesson so it remains operative the next time.

[PAUSE]

Now, here is the modern bridge. Because some of you are listening and thinking — *this is interesting linguistically, but is it true psychologically?*

Open any contemporary textbook on cognitive neuroscience. Find the section on the prefrontal cortex. Read what it says about executive function.

The prefrontal cortex — the part of the brain whose maturation defines adult cognition — has, as one of its primary functions, **inhibitory control**. Its job is not, primarily, to generate ideas. Ideas come from many places in the brain. Its job is to *stop* the brain from acting on most of them. To suppress impulse. To delay gratification. To override the automatic.

The famous "marshmallow test" — Walter Mischel's experiment where children were offered one marshmallow now or two if they could wait — was a test of inhibitory control. And decades of follow-up showed: the children who could say *no* to themselves, who could delay, performed better on every measure of life outcome — academic, social, financial, emotional — for the next forty years.

What modern psychology calls *executive function*, what neuroscience calls *inhibitory control*, what economists call *self-regulation*, what philosophers call *practical reason* — Allah named in one word fourteen centuries ago.

**Nuhā.**

The reason that restrains. The intellect that, as al-Muyassar says, *retains* — that holds the lesson inside it rather than letting it escape.

But here is what's astonishing. Allah is not making a psychological observation. He is making a **theological** one.

Look at the ayah again:

> *Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-ulī n-nuhā.*
>
> "Indeed in that are signs for those of discerning reason."

The signs are the destroyed civilizations. The ruins on the road. And Allah is saying: the people who can read these ruins as signs are not the smart ones. Not the educated ones. Not even the religious ones, necessarily.

They are the ones whose reason has a working capacity for *no* — **and whose minds hold what they see**.

Why?

Because to read a ruin as a sign requires the reader to do something violently uncomfortable. It requires the reader to apply the lesson **to themselves**. To see in the destroyed city not a curiosity, not an archaeological detail, not a story about *those people back then* — but a warning about *me, now*.

And to do that, the reader must override every cognitive defense the human mind throws up to protect us from disturbing self-knowledge. *That couldn't be me. I'm not like them. They were ancient; I'm modern. They were primitive; I'm sophisticated. They were arrogant; I'm just confident. They exceeded; I am simply pursuing what I deserve.*

These defenses are loud. They are persistent. They are running in your mind right now, as I speak, building reasons that the warnings of this ayah are about somebody else.

The faculty that lets a sign become guidance is the restraining intellect — *nuhā*, reason named for what it does: it says *no* to the defenses the self erects between you and a difficult truth, and it *retains* what it has seen so that the next ruin on the road does not arrive as a surprise.

### Element Three — *Yahdi*: When Guidance Means Making Clear

Now look at the verb with which ayah 128 opens: *afalam **yahdi** lahum* — "has it not **guided** them?"

In Arabic the verb is from the root ه-د-ي (h-d-y): hidāyah, guidance. You know this root from *ihdina l-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, from *Hudan li-l-muttaqīn*. Guidance is the Quran's supreme gift, the thing the surah opened with naming.

But al-Ṭabarī, commenting on this exact word in this exact ayah, renders *yahdi* as *yubayyinu* — **to make clear, to make evident, to demonstrate plainly**. The question becomes not only "has it not guided them?" but "has it not *clarified* the matter for them?" — has it not made the evidence unmistakable?

This gloss matters. It shifts where the failure lies.

If the word is guidance in the spiritual sense alone, the failure might be located outside the addressee — perhaps the guidance has not reached them, perhaps they need more. But if *yahdi* here carries the force of *yubayyinu* — making clear, presenting evidence — then the failure is not in the evidence. **The ruins are abundant. The evidence is plain. The clarity is there.** The question is whether there is a faculty present in the listener to receive what has been made unmistakably clear.

This is why the ayah ends with *li-ulī n-nuhā*. Not: these ruins are guidance for the spiritually open. Not: these ruins will guide whoever seeks. But: in this is clear evidence — *for those with the retaining intellect to receive it*.

The ruins are not ambiguous. The Quran's argument is not that the Quraysh lacked evidence. It is that they lacked the *nuhā* to let evidence do its work.

Al-Ṭabarī adds something else here. He notes that the verse is addressed — in its intimate grammar — directly to the Prophet ﷺ about *his own people*: *afalam yahdi **li-qawmika***. The Arabic of 20:128 is addressed to the Messenger himself, making the Quraysh's resistance a personal burden he must witness. And al-Muyassar makes this explicit: the one being asked the question is the Prophet ﷺ, and the ones who should have been guided are *qawmuka* — **your people**.

The surah opened consoling him. It ends asking him a question that carries all the weight of the consolation within it. Has it not become clear to your people? Has the evidence not spoken? Yes, it has spoken. And if they still do not hear — then you know what Musa knew. And you carry forward.

### Element Four — *Masākin* and *Qurūn*: The Habitations They Have Inherited

One more element before we pull back.

Look at the word *masākin* — dwellings, habitations. Not ruins. Not ancient sites. The word comes from the root س-ك-ن (s-k-n): to settle, to dwell, to find stillness. *Masākin* are places of *sukūn* — places where people came to rest, to sleep, to raise children, to make a life. Every *miskan* was once full of the ordinary weight of human existence: a fire kept burning, a child learning to walk, a merchant counting his stock.

The Quran does not call them *kharābāt* — ruins. It calls them *masākin* — homes. The word keeps the humanity in the stones. The one who built here was like you. He wanted what you want. He was afraid of what you fear. He also thought the warnings were about someone else.

And *qurūn* — generations. The plural of *qarn*, which in Arabic means a generation, a century, but also a *horn*: the raised protrusion of something that stands above the plain. Civilizations that rose up, that asserted themselves, that reached — and were cut back. The word *kam* before *qurūn* — **how many** — signals not a small number but an accumulation that staggers the imagination. Not one Pharaoh. Not one ʿĀd. Not one Thamūd. Generation after generation that rose and refused, and was answered.

The Quraysh knew these generations. They traded through their ruins. They were walking, at that very moment, *fī masākinihim* — **in their homes**. Present tense. The verb *yamshūna* is active, ongoing. The walking was not past history. The walking was happening.

The homes were still there. The people were gone.

Al-Ṭabarī is precise about what seeing these places should have triggered. He uses a fourfold sequence: that those who walked through should have taken heed (*yattaʿiẓū*), taken lesson (*yaʿtabirū*), returned in submission (*yunībū ilā l-idhʿān*), and believed (*yu'minū*). Four steps. Observation, internalization, turning, faith. Not a leap. A walk — through ruins that had been placed on the very road they were already traveling.

But steps require *nuhā*. They require reason that restrains the self-protective instinct to look at the ruined dwelling and say: *fascinating archaeology* — rather than: *warning*.

## PART 2: WHAT THE RUINS ARE SAYING

### The Surah's Arc Completes

We are near the end of Surah Ta-Ha. Stand here for a moment and feel the arc.

The surah opened with a letter: **Ṭā Hā**. Then it said to the Prophet ﷺ: *We have not sent down to you the Quran that you should be distressed.* It was a release of pressure. A hand on a burdened shoulder. *You are not failing. The weight you carry is real, but it is not yours to carry alone.*

Then it spent eighty ayahs on Musa. And in those eighty ayahs it told him: your predecessor also stood trembling before what he could not control. He also faced people who refused. His own community — the ones he had led through the sea — melted their jewelry for a calf while he climbed to receive the Torah. Even the ones who crossed with him strayed.

And he survived it. And the mission survived it.

Now, at the close of the surah, the Quran looks up from the narrative and addresses the present moment: *and thus We recompense those who exceed and do not believe.* It is not abstract theology. It is the machinery of history being named aloud. The same machinery that processed Pharaoh. The same machinery that leveled ʿĀd and Thamūd. The same machinery that your caravans pass through evidence of, season after season.

The surah that began with *we did not send this to distress you* ends, effectively, by saying: **and here is why you do not need to be distressed.** Not because the opposition will stop. Not because the resistance will soften. But because the pattern is established, the record is clear, and the ruins are on the road for anyone with *nuhā* to read.

### The Responsibility That Signs Create

There is a disquieting reversal inside these two ayahs.

We usually think of signs — *āyāt* — as gentle invitations. Here is evidence; consider it. Here is beauty; perhaps it will open you. And there is truth in that. The Quran is full of signs offered with patience.

But ayah 128 does something sharper. It says: the ruins are signs for those with *nuhā*. Which means the ruins have done their part. The evidence has been placed. The demonstration has been made. And what remains — what is now in question — is whether the witness **has the faculty to receive the evidence that is staring them in the face**.

This is not cruelty. It is precision. The Quran is not presenting a God who withholds signs and then punishes for not seeing. It is presenting a God who has surrounded every civilization with signs — in nature, in history, in their own bodies — and is asking one question: **does your mind have what it needs to let a sign be a sign?**

The answer to that question is not fixed at birth. *Nuhā* is a faculty, and faculties are trained. They are exercised or they are neglected. A mind accustomed to overriding its own impulses builds the neural architecture — to borrow the modern language — for saying *no* to the defenses that keep truth at a distance. A mind that has never been made to pause, to hold a lesson, to apply a warning to itself — that mind, when it stands before the ruins on the road, will see stones.

Only stones.

### The Question That Does Not End

*Afalam yahdi lahum* — has it not made clear to them?

The question is addressed, we noted, to the Prophet ﷺ. And it is a question he cannot answer on behalf of his people. He can present. He can recite. He can be the most eloquent, the most patient, the most compelling voice in human history. And still, the question of whether the clarity has landed — whether it has penetrated to where *nuhā* lives — is not his to control.

This is the consolation wrapped inside the challenge.

The Prophet ﷺ was not failing. He was not failing when Quraysh refused to see the ruins as warnings. He was not failing when his own community's resistance felt like a wall. The surah opened by telling him: your job is not to ensure that people see. Your job is to present what is already clear.

The same is true for anyone who carries this message forward. You are not responsible for whether the ruins speak. You are responsible for whether *you* walk through them with *nuhā*. And you are responsible for speaking clearly to whoever will listen — and then releasing the outcome.

The outcome belongs to the One who placed the ruins on the road.

### The Mirror Turns

One final inward turn.

These ayahs were revealed about the Quraysh. They were preserved — and you are reading them now — because they are not only about the Quraysh.

Every person carries their own ruins. Not the carved facades of Thamūd. The ruins that look like: relationships that collapsed because someone kept exceeding what the relationship could hold. Careers that crumbled because a habit of exceeding — in pride, in dishonesty, in self-serving calculation — finally hit a wall. Health that broke because the body's limits were treated as optional. Communities that fractured because someone — maybe you — decided the rules were for other people.

Allah is not surveying ancient archaeology when He speaks of *masākin*. He is naming the universal experience of a soul that has watched consequences arrive, in real time, and then — this is the hard part — **has had to choose whether to let those consequences teach it something**.

*Asrafa* is not only a Pharaonic sin. It is the morning when you pushed past what you knew you should do, and told yourself a story about why this time was different. The evening you took more than your share of something — attention, credit, rest, space — and felt the small internal register of *this is wrong* and overrode it.

Every one of those moments was a ruin being built.

The question *afalam yahdi lahum* is, ultimately, *afalam yahdi **laka*** — has it not made clear to you? Have the ruins in your own life not spoken? Have the consequences not made the pattern visible?

The people with *nuhā* — the people with attentive, retaining reason — are the ones who can hear that question land without immediately reaching for the explanation that deflects it. They are the ones who can stand in the middle of their own *masākin* — the dwellings of things they have lost or broken or let slip past their limit — and say: *yes, I see. And I will not continue past what I have been shown.*

That is not weakness. That is the rarest thing in the Quran's anthropology.

That is the mind that earns the name: **نُهَى**.

## Closing Reflection

The surah that opened with a consolation ends with a challenge. But the challenge is only hard if you come to it defending yourself.

If you come to it with *nuhā* — with the faculty that can say *no* to the story that protects you from difficult clarity — then the challenge becomes, itself, a consolation.

Because what it says is: *the ruins are readable. The signs are clear. The machinery of the universe is not opaque, and you are not living in chaos. There is a pattern. There is a consequence. There is a mercy in the consequence — because it means that limits exist, and limits mean that nothing is bottomless, and nothing bottomless means that every turning, every return, every moment of saying "I exceeded; I will not exceed again" — that moment is possible, and real, and received.*

The surah closes, a few ayahs later, by returning to its opening register: *Say: My Lord, increase me in knowledge.* After all of this — after Musa, after the calf, after the ruins on the trade route, after the question about whether your people have received the clarity — after all of it, the final note is not threat. It is request. Increase me.

The one who asks that is the one who has *nuhā*. Who has stood in the ruins — the external ones and the internal ones — and come away not defensive, not paralyzed, but asking to understand more than they currently do.

That is the posture of the people who let signs be signs.

May Allah make us among the *ulū n-nuhā* — those of attentive reason, those whose minds retain what the ruins say, and whose impulses do not carry them past the boundaries of what they have been shown.

*Rabbī zidnī ʿilmā.*
