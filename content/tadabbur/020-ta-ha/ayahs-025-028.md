# The Four Petitions: How Musa Prayed Before Facing Pharaoh

> **قَالَ رَبِّ ٱشْرَحْ لِى صَدْرِى ۝ وَيَسِّرْ لِىٓ أَمْرِى ۝ وَٱحْلُلْ عُقْدَةً مِّن لِّسَانِى ۝ يَفْقَهُوا۟ قَوْلِى**

> *Qāla Rabbi-shrah lī ṣadrī. Wa yassir lī amrī. Waḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī. Yafqahū qawlī.*

> "He said: My Lord, expand for me my chest. And make easy for me my task. And untie a knot from my tongue. That they may understand my speech."

---

## Introduction: The Moment Before the Walk

Stand for a moment where Musa is standing.

He is in the valley of Tuwa, alone. He had been a shepherd. Forty years of quiet, after forty years of palace life that ended in blood — an Egyptian dead by his hand, a panicked flight across the desert, a marriage in Madyan, sheep in the hills. He has been hidden. Deliberately, mercifully hidden.

And then — fire on the mountain. A voice. *"Innī anā Rabbuk"* — *I am your Lord.* Take off your sandals. You are in the sacred valley. And then, after the staff has become a serpent and his hand has flashed white, the commission lands:

**اذْهَبْ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّهُ طَغَىٰ**

*Go to Pharaoh. He has transgressed.* (20:24)

[PAUSE]

Sit with what those words contain. Pharaoh is not an abstraction to Musa. Pharaoh is the man whose palace raised him. Pharaoh is the throne whose authority he fled. Pharaoh's court contains people who watched Musa grow up, who would recognize his face, who remember the death of the Egyptian. Pharaoh commands armies. Pharaoh claims divinity. Pharaoh has built an empire on the broken backs of Musa's own people, and the most recent decree from Pharaoh's court was the killing of Israelite infants.

And Allah says: *go to him.*

Alone.

This is the most asymmetrical commission in human history. One man, fugitive, unarmed, with a known speech impediment — sent to confront the most fortified absolute power on earth, in the very court he escaped from, with a message guaranteed to be received as treason.

Now watch what Musa does next.

He doesn't argue. He doesn't refuse. He doesn't even, at this moment, ask for a delay or for his brother to be sent in his place — that comes a few verses later. The very first thing he does, the first words out of his mouth after the commission lands, is a prayer.

But notice what he prays *for*.

He doesn't pray "Allah, give me victory." He doesn't pray "destroy Pharaoh." He doesn't pray "soften his heart." He doesn't even pray "protect me."

He turns inward. He prays for **four things about himself**.

And here is what should stop us at the door: the things he asks for are not what we, in our urgency, would think to ask for. They are not the equipment of war. They are not even the equipment of persuasion in the way we usually picture it. They are the equipment of the **soul standing inside the body about to walk forward**.

Watch the order. Watch what comes first, what comes second, what comes third, what comes fourth. Because the order of Musa's prayer is itself a teaching — about how a human being is supposed to be assembled before they attempt something impossible.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Architecture of Four — Sadrī, Amrī, Lisānī, Qawlī

Before we open the words, look at the shape.

Four petitions. Each ends in the same sound — the kasrah followed by the yāʾ of the first-person possessive. *Ṣadrī*. *Amrī*. *Lisānī*. *Qawlī*. *My* chest. *My* task. *My* tongue. *My* speech.

Read them aloud and feel the rhythm:

> **Rabbi-shrah lī ṣadrī**
> **wa yassir lī amrī**
> **waḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī**
> **yafqahū qawlī**

Four short breaths. Each one ending on that same possessive note. This is the rhythm of a man praying with his whole body — petitions short enough to be said on a single exhale, returning again and again to *me*, *me*, *me*, *me*. Not in arrogance. In honesty. He is laying out the parts of himself that need to be touched before he can do what he has been asked to do.

Now let's teach a small grammatical thing before going further, because the architecture depends on it.

In Arabic, when you address someone with several requests, you can attach them by `wa` — *and* — or you can let them stand without conjunction. Here Allah preserves Musa's prayer with `wa` between the petitions: *and* ease, *and* untie. Each one is its own complete petition. They are not collapsed into one sentence. They are kept distinct, like four separate doors he is knocking on, in sequence.

But there is one more thing — the fourth petition is grammatically different from the first three. The first three (*ishrah*, *yassir*, *uḥlul*) are imperatives — direct requests. The fourth (*yafqahū*) is a jussive verb in the form of a result — *so that* they may understand. It is not a separate request. It is the **outcome** the first three are aiming at.

So the architecture is actually:

- Three petitions about himself.
- One sentence about the *purpose* of those three petitions.

Three things he needs Allah to do *to him* — and one thing that will follow when those three are granted.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what those three petitions name, in order:

- **Ṣadrī** — my chest. My internal capacity. The space inside me where what I carry is held.
- **Amrī** — my task. My external work. The thing I have been asked to do.
- **Lisānī** — my tongue. The instrument that translates the inside into the outside.

Inside → action → speech.

Self → task → tool.

He starts at the deepest layer — the chest. Then he moves outward to the work. Then he moves to the tongue, which is the bridge between himself and the people he has been sent to. And only then does he name the outcome — *that they may understand*.

Al-Tabari's reading of *amrī* sharpens what "the work" actually is: not a task in any general sense, but specifically the carrying out of the *risāla* — the prophetic mission just charged to him — and the bearing of *ṭāʿa*, divine obedience, as two inseparable dimensions of a single burden. The word *amrī* here names the full weight of prophethood itself: both the outward charge and the inward submission it requires. No wonder it needs to be made easy.

This sequence is not random. He is praying for himself in the order in which a human being actually has to be prepared. The chest must expand before the task can be carried. The task must be made easy before the tongue can speak it. The tongue must be loosed before any understanding can land.

Here's what's remarkable: most of us, when we pray about a hard situation, **start at the wrong end**. We pray for the outcome — *let them understand*, *let it go well*, *let them say yes*. Musa starts at his chest. He prays for the vessel before he prays for the message.

We will return to this. But hold the architecture in mind. **Four petitions. Three about him, one about the result. In strict order: inward, then outward, then between, then receiver.**

### Element Two: *Ishraḥ lī ṣadrī* — What It Means to Expand a Chest

The first word he uses is *ishraḥ*.

Let's teach the verb before opening it.

The root **ش ر ح** in Arabic carries a physical meaning that English doesn't quite have a single word for. It is the act of *opening up* — but specifically opening up something that was closed or compressed, in a way that makes the inside *visible* and *spacious*. A butcher's term for laying meat out flat is from this root. A surgeon's term for opening a body to examine it is from this root. So is the word *sharḥ* — a commentary, an explanation that *unfolds* a text so its contents are laid out in the open.

In all of these usages, *sharḥ* means: take what is folded, compressed, hidden, knotted up — and **open it out so it has space and so its inside can be seen and breathed into**.

Now apply that to *ṣadr*.

The *ṣadr* in Arabic is more than what English means by "chest." It is the seat of what you carry. It is where weight settles. The Qur'an speaks of breasts being constricted (*ḍayyiqan ḥarajan*, 6:125), of breasts being soothed, of what is hidden inside breasts. When Allah says elsewhere *alam nashraḥ laka ṣadrak* — *did We not expand for you your chest* (94:1) — He is using the same root, in the same kind of moment, to describe what He did *for the Prophet ﷺ before he carried what he was asked to carry*.

So *ishraḥ lī ṣadrī* is not a request for emotional comfort. It is a request for **internal volumetric capacity**.

Stay with me.

A small chest can carry a small thing. A chest that has been compressed by fear cannot hold a heavy commission without bursting. Musa is about to be asked to hold the entire weight of confronting Pharaoh, leading a people, parting a sea, receiving a Law, surviving a forty-year wandering. He cannot carry that with the chest he currently has.

So the very first thing he asks for is not *help me with the task*. It is *make the inside of me bigger*.

Notice the precision: he doesn't ask Allah to *remove* what is inside him — the fear, the memory, the trauma of the killed Egyptian. He asks Allah to *expand the container*. The fear may stay. The memory may stay. But if the chest is wide enough, those things no longer crush him. They fit.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most important moves in the spiritual life and we miss it constantly. We pray *take this away from me*. Allah's gift is often *I will not take it away — I will make you bigger so it no longer dominates you*. The fear becomes proportional. The grief becomes carryable. The weight that would have broken yesterday's chest is now held in a chest that has been opened up.

Al-Tabari's commentary surfaces a second dimension: *jurʾa* — boldness, the specific daring to act. His reading is that the sharḥ serves two simultaneous purposes — to allow Musa to *comprehend* what Allah is depositing of revelation, and to generate the courage to stand before Pharaoh and speak. Ibn Zayd's gloss, preserved in al-Tabari's transmission, distills the whole petition to just two words: *jurʾatan lī* — boldness for me. The chest, in this reading, is not only the vessel that holds what we carry. It is the source of the daring to move through it. A constricted chest cannot dare. A chest that has been opened out has room not only for the fear and the grief, but for the forward motion through them.

And note the preposition — **lī**. *Expand for me.* Not *expand me*. The lām of benefit. Open it *for my sake*, *to my advantage*. The expansion is not just a structural fact — it is a gift. It is something done for him, in his favor.

Notice also what he does *not* ask. He does not ask Allah to remove Pharaoh. He does not ask for the difficulty to disappear. He asks only that the inside of him be made wide enough for the difficulty.

This is, in one phrase, a complete theology of how to face what is bigger than you.

### Element Three: *ʿUqdatan min lisānī* — The Knot, and Why It Stays Partial

Now we come to what is, I think, the most quietly stunning word in the four petitions.

*Wa-ḥlul ʿuqdatan min lisānī.*

Let's go slowly.

**Uḥlul** — root ح ل ل — to *untie*, to *loosen*, to *make permissible* (a related meaning that comes from the same image: a thing that was bound is now released). Imperative form. *Untie.*

**ʿUqdatan** — root ع ق د — a *knot*, a *binding*, something tied. A *contract* in Arabic is *ʿaqd*, from the same root — because a contract is something you "tie up" between two parties.

**Min lisānī** — *from my tongue*.

The image is concrete and physical: there is a *knot* on Musa's tongue. Tradition tells us he had a speech impediment, possibly from an incident in childhood when he grasped a hot coal before Pharaoh's court. The Qur'an itself reports Pharaoh later mocking Musa: *and he can hardly express himself clearly* (43:52). Musa is aware of this. He knows his own tongue.

So far, so straightforward. He is asking for help with his speech impediment.

But now we need to look very, very carefully at two pieces of grammar that change everything.

**Piece one: *ʿuqdatan* is indefinite.**

He does not say *al-ʿuqdah* — *the* knot. He says *ʿuqdatan* — *a* knot. Indefinite. Singular. Unspecified.

**Piece two: *min lisānī* — the partitive *min*.**

In Arabic, *min* attached this way can be partitive — it means *some of*, *part of*. Untie *a* knot, *from* my tongue.

Put these two pieces together and what Musa is asking for is **not the complete removal of his speech impediment**. He is asking for *enough*. *A* knot, *from* my tongue. Some of it. The portion that prevents the message from being understood. Not all of it.

[PAUSE]

This is extraordinary. Stay with it.

Musa, who has just been commissioned to confront the most powerful orator-king on earth, who is about to face a court of trained debaters and magicians, who knows that Pharaoh will mock his speech — does *not* ask for perfect eloquence. He does not ask for fluent oratory. He does not ask Allah to give him the tongue of his enemy.

He asks for *enough*.

He asks for the part that blocks comprehension. Not the part that makes him *Musa*. Not the part that distinguishes him. Not the part Allah, in His wisdom, allowed his tongue to carry.

While some classical paraphrases render this petition broadly — al-Muyassar, for instance, glosses it as a request for *faṣīḥ al-manṭiq*, fluent and clear speech — the grammatical texture of the original Arabic holds something more measured. The indefiniteness of *ʿuqdatan* and the partitive force of *min* anchor the more precise reading that classical scholars including Ibn ʿAṭiyyah and al-Rāzī surface: this is a calibrated, minimal request, not a bid for rhetorical perfection. Musa's du'a is precisely measured — sufficiency for the task, not the erasure of what his tongue has always carried.

Some traces of his speech difficulty would remain throughout his prophetic life, and this is theologically significant. It would be a mark of Musa's humanity. It would mean that when Pharaoh's court was overwhelmed by the message, no one could say *the message succeeded because of his beautiful tongue*. The success of the message would clearly be from Allah, not from Musa's eloquence.

Now compare this to how we tend to pray.

We tend to pray *fix me completely*. *Make me eloquent. Make me confident. Make me successful. Make my anxiety go away. Make my impediments disappear.*

Musa prays *untie enough of the knot for the message to land*. He doesn't ask to be reshaped into a different person. He asks for the precise, minimal adjustment that the task requires.

This is the prayer of someone who knows that **his weaknesses are also his**. They were given. They have a purpose. He is not trying to escape his nature. He is asking for the smallest possible accommodation — the partial loosening of a partial knot — sufficient for what he has been asked to do.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing. Look where this petition sits in the sequence: it is *third*. After the chest. After the task. The tongue comes *last* among the petitions about himself.

We would have started here. *Allah, fix my tongue.*
