# The Sermon of the Saved: Neither Dying Nor Living

## Introduction

**The Ayah**

> إِنَّهُۥ مَن يَأْتِ رَبَّهُۥ مُجْرِمًا فَإِنَّ لَهُۥ جَهَنَّمَ لَا يَمُوتُ فِيهَا وَلَا يَحْيَىٰ
>
> وَمَن يَأْتِهِۦ مُؤْمِنًا قَدْ عَمِلَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ لَهُمُ ٱلدَّرَجَـٰتُ ٱلْعُلَىٰ
>
> جَنَّـٰتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ خَـٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ جَزَآءُ مَن تَزَكَّىٰ

*Innahu man ya'ti rabbahu mujriman fa-inna lahu jahannama la yamutu fiha wa la yahya. Wa man ya'tihi mu'minan qad 'amila al-salihati fa-ula'ika lahumu al-darajatu al-'ula. Jannatu 'adnin tajri min tahtiha al-anharu khalidina fiha, wa dhalika jazau man tazakka.*

> Indeed, whoever comes to his Lord as a criminal — for him is Hell; he will not die in it, nor will he live.
>
> And whoever comes to Him as a believer, having already done righteous deeds — for those are the highest ranks:
>
> Gardens of perpetual residence, beneath which rivers flow, abiding eternally therein. And that is the reward of whoever purifies himself.

---

### Rebuilding the World

To hear these ayahs the way they were first heard, you have to stand in the dust of the public square in Egypt.

Pharaoh had summoned every magician in the kingdom. This was supposed to be the end of Musa. The magicians arrived expecting payment. Their fingers knew rope. Their voices knew the chants. They threw down their staffs and the ground writhed with what looked like a thousand serpents.

And then Musa threw his staff.

What happened next was not a magic trick. The magicians knew the difference. They had given their lives to the craft. They could see, the way a master forger sees a real signature, that what Musa had thrown was not from their world. The staff did not perform — it *consumed*. It ate the illusion whole.

And the magicians fell.

Not bowing. *Falling.* The Quran uses a word that suggests gravity took them. They were prostrate before they had decided to prostrate. It was as though they were believers before Pharaoh had finished registering what had happened.

What makes this falling more remarkable still is something the tradition preserves: Pharaoh had not merely hired these magicians. He had *compelled* them to learn magic. The craft was not a vocation freely chosen — it was a service extracted from them, a skill pressed into the architecture of his court. And yet that same craft, turned in an instant against its master's purposes, was the instrument of their recognition. The skill Pharaoh had coerced from them in order to defeat Musa became the very skill that let them see Musa's staff for what it was. He had armed the men who would bear witness against him.

Pharaoh's response was immediate and total. *You believed in him before I gave you permission?* He named them traitors. He promised to cut off their hands and feet from opposite sides. He promised to crucify them on the trunks of palm trees. He promised they would learn whose punishment was more severe and more lasting.

These three ayahs are the magicians' reply.

[PAUSE]

Notice what they do *not* say. They do not beg. They do not argue. They do not say "we were just doing our job." They do not say "we'll renounce it if you spare us."

They preach.

Hours ago they were Pharaoh's coerced servants. Now they are addressing him about the eschatology of the universe. They are explaining to the most powerful man on earth what is going to happen to him when he dies.

Hold that image. Because what we are about to read are the last recorded words of people who had been transformed so completely that the threat of crucifixion did not interrupt their teaching. They are about to die. And they are using their last breath not to plead for life — but to describe what life and death actually mean.

### The Hook

There is a phrase in these ayahs that is one of the most haunting in the entire Quran. Five short words in Arabic. *La yamutu fiha wa la yahya.* He will not die in it, nor will he live.

Most punishments in the Quran are described in vivid imagery — fire, chains, scalding water. But this phrase doesn't describe a *thing*. It describes a *condition*. A condition we already have a name for in our own lives, even if we have never said it aloud.

We are going to find out what the magicians knew that made this — and not crucifixion — the thing worth warning about.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "Comes to His Lord" — *ya'ti rabbahu*

Before we look at this phrase, let me ask you to think about a habit Arabic has that English does not.

In English when we describe a wrongdoer's fate we usually say something like *"the criminal will face justice"* or *"he will get what he deserves."* The grammar puts the wrongdoer in front of an abstraction — justice, consequences, the law.

Arabic, in this ayah, refuses that move. It puts the criminal in front of *a Person*. And not just any Person. **His Lord.**

Look at the verb: *ya'ti* — he comes. Not "he is dragged." Not "he is sent." He *comes*. The same verb you would use for someone walking into a room.

Now look at the object: *rabbahu* — **his Lord.** With the possessive pronoun attached. Not "the Lord." Not "Allah." *His* Lord.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

The criminal in Hell is not someone who escaped his Lord into some other realm. He is not exiled into a void. The relationship of Lordship is not severed by sin. *Rabbuhu* is still *rabbuhu*.

This is one of the most quietly devastating features of Quranic eschatology. The disbeliever in Hell does not get to stop being owned, stop being known, stop being seen. The Lord of the believer is the Lord of the criminal. The criminal's tragedy is not that he is far from his Lord; it is that he came *to* his Lord — and arrived as the wrong kind of person.

There is no "outside" of Allah's dominion to flee to. The mujrim's Hell is not "away from God." It is *in* the relationship, on the wrong side of it.

Notice something else. The same verb *ya'ti* is used for both the criminal and the believer. *Man ya'ti rabbahu mujriman* — whoever comes to his Lord as a criminal. *Wa man ya'tihi mu'minan* — and whoever comes to Him as a believer.

Both come. Both arrive. The destination is identical. The verb is identical. **The only thing that differs is the state in which they walk in.**

This is what the magicians wanted Pharaoh to understand. Pharaoh thought he could prevent them from coming to their Lord by killing them on a palm trunk. The magicians knew that there is no version of the future in which Pharaoh does not come to his Lord. Pharaoh is coming. The crucifixion does not change that. The only question is what state Pharaoh will be in when he arrives.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: "He Will Not Die in It, Nor Will He Live" — *la yamutu fiha wa la yahya*

Arabic has a feature called *al-jam' bayna al-naqidayn* — the binding together of two opposites. Sometimes a writer joins two opposites by negating both: *not this and not that.* What sits between two negations is a kind of meaning English struggles to hold. We will need to be slow here.

Look at the phrase: *la yamutu fiha wa la yahya.* **He does not die in it, and he does not live.**

Two verbs. *Yamutu* — he dies. *Yahya* — he lives. Both negated.

In our ordinary world, *not dying* means *living*. And *not living* means *dead*. The two words exhaust the possibilities. There is no third option.

But the Arabic here insists on a third option. **He is not dying. He is not living.**

[PAUSE]

What is being described?

The classical scholars worked at this from several angles, and rather than choose one, let us let each angle add a layer.

One reading: he does not die — meaning he does not get the relief of annihilation. The fire does not consume him into nothingness. Death, which in this world is feared as the end, becomes in the next world the longed-for escape. The criminal wishes to die. The wish is denied.

Al-Muyassar sharpens this precisely: *la yamutu fiha fa-yastarih* — he does not die **and thereby find rest**. The point is not merely survival. It is the specific and permanent denial of *rest*. Death in this world, even at its most terrible, arrives eventually with the finality of stillness. In Hell, that finality is exactly what is withheld.

Another reading: he does not live — meaning his existence is stripped of every quality we associate with *being alive*. Al-Muyassar again: *wa la yahya hayatan yataladhdhadhu biha* — nor does he live **a life in which he finds pleasure or delight**. There is no nourishment that nourishes. No friendship. No joy. No project. No future. The biological fact of continued existence persists, but every quality that made existence *worth having* has been emptied out.

A third reading: he hangs in a permanent crisis state. The way a person in an unbearable moment will say *"I want to die"* — not literally, but to express that this *state* is unbearable. The criminal is in that state. Forever. The unbearable moment never resolves.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari adds an image so specific it has occupied commentators for centuries. Glossing *la yamutu wa la yahya*, he writes that the soul neither exits the body — which would be death — nor settles in its dwelling place and finds peace — which would be life. Instead: *tataʿallaqu bi'l-hanajirim minhum* — **their souls hang suspended at their throats**. Not departed. Not at rest. Hanging at the threshold between inside and outside, between leaving and staying.

The image is anatomically exact. The throat is the passage. Every human death is the soul's departure through this passage, and after departure the body knows stillness. In Hell, the soul reaches that passage and cannot cross it. It is held at the threshold, reaching for exit and unable to exit, forever.

These readings do not contradict each other. They are camera angles on the same horror. **He is suspended between the two states the human soul can occupy.** Death is denied as escape. Life is denied as content. The soul hangs at the throat. What remains is the bare fact of continued conscious existence — without the mercies that make existence livable, and without the mercy of its ending.

[PAUSE]

Now hear what this is *not*. The Quran does not say "he will be tormented." It does not say "he will scream." It does not say "he will burn." All those things are described elsewhere. Here the description is purely *ontological* — about the kind of *being* he has. He is a being who is neither dying nor living.

This is not the language of pain. It is the language of *suspended existence*. And suspended existence is something the human soul understands too well.

You have known days when you were not exactly alive. Not depressed enough to sleep. Not awake enough to act. The TV played. The hours passed. You were *there* and you were *not there*. We have a word for this in modern psychology — *anhedonia*, the inability to feel. There is a kind of suffering more severe than pain: the suffering of *not being able to feel anything*, including pain.

The Quran is not telling us Hell is more painful than we imagined. It is telling us Hell is the *eternalization* of a state we already have a sample of in this life. The mujrim already knew this state. It used to be a Tuesday afternoon. In the next world it becomes the only afternoon there is.

And here is the deepest layer the magicians wanted Pharaoh to hear: this is what the criminal *already is*. The criminal in this world is a person who has chosen to be neither fully dead to wrong nor fully alive to truth. He has built a life of suspended ethics. Hell is not a punishment imposed *on* him. Hell is the unmasking of what he already chose to *be*.

[PAUSE]

One further note on the word *mujriman* itself. The root j-r-m carries the sense of cutting or severing — the criminal is one who cuts himself off. Ibn Kathir reads the word broadly: whoever meets Allah on the Day of Judgment while being a criminal. But al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both give a more precise eschatological gloss: *mujriman* in this context means one who has acquired *disbelief in Him* — *muktatsibu al-kufri bihi*. The magicians were not issuing a general warning about every moral failure. They were warning Pharaoh about the fundamental severance: cutting oneself off from acknowledgment of one's Lord. This is the specific *jurm* that makes Jahannam one's address. The broader root meaning of wrongdoing includes it, but the classical tradition narrows the referent here to the most decisive of cuts.

### Element Three: "Whoever Purifies Himself" — *man tazakka*

The closing word of this passage is one of the most consequential words in the Quran. We need to teach the form before we open the word.

Arabic verbs have a numbered family of forms — Form I, Form II, Form III, all the way to Form X. Each form alters the basic root. Form II often intensifies or makes a verb causative. Form III often involves another party. Form V — the form we have here — is reflexive. It is the form you use when **the action turns back on the doer**.

Take the root *zaka* — to be pure, to grow, to be wholesome. In its basic form it just describes purity. *Zakka* in Form II means *to purify someone*. But *tazakka* in Form V means **to purify oneself**.

Now look at the closing line of our passage: *wa dhalika jazau man tazakka.* And that is the reward of whoever **purified himself**.

[PAUSE]

The grammar is doing extraordinary work here. The reward is not given to *whoever was purified*. Not *whoever was forgiven*. Not *whoever Allah cleaned*. **Whoever purified himself.**

The verb requires you to be the agent of your own purification.

This is theologically subtle, because of course no one purifies themselves without divine help. The Quran elsewhere is explicit — *law la fadlu Llahi alaykum wa rahmatuhu ma zaka minkum min ahadin abada* — were it not for Allah's grace upon you and His mercy, none of you would ever have been pure (24:21). Allah's purification is the foundation.

But notice what *this* ayah emphasizes. Not the foundation. The work. *Tazakka.* The reflexive form. The work *you do* to participate in what Allah is doing in you.

Al-Muyassar draws out what this purification specifically targets: purification from *al-danas wa'l-khubth wa'l-shirk* — from filth, from corruption, and from shirk. The word *danas* names the everyday grime of the soul, the accumulation of small moral compromises that coat the heart gradually. *Khubth* is something deeper — it names the corruption that has gone all the way through, the rot that is not merely on the surface. And *shirk* — associating anything in worship alongside Allah — is described not as a further item on the list but as the root condition that generates the other two. Tazakka is the sustained work of clearing all three layers. Not a single event. A way of living toward one's Lord.

The reflexive form holds something else. The one who *tazakka* is, by definition, in motion. The Form V tells us: he is doing this *to himself*. He is not waiting for purity to be applied from outside. He is the one clearing. He is the one choosing, repeatedly, which direction he is facing. And according to al-Muyassar, the culmination of this work is a particular moment: meeting one's Lord without associating *any* of His creation in worship. The purification that began as interior clearing arrives, finally, at the threshold — at that same *ya'ti* we saw in ayah 74 — and the soul crosses it in the condition it chose.

[PAUSE]

Why does the magician end his sermon to Pharaoh on this word?

Because Pharaoh's whole worldview is that purity, righteousness, and salvation are conferred by power. Pharaoh decides who is honored and who is crucified. Pharaoh decides who eats and who starves. Pharaoh's name on your file is the only currency that matters.

The magicians, in their last words, demolish that. **Salvation is not conferred. Salvation is something you do to yourself.** No matter what Pharaoh writes on your file. No matter what Pharaoh does to your body. The reward of the highest ranks is for the one who *purified himself* — a verb Pharaoh cannot perform for anyone, and cannot prevent anyone from performing.

Pharaoh's threat is suddenly small. He can dismember a body. He cannot un-purify a soul that has chosen its own purification.

[PAUSE]

There is one more linguistic touch worth noting. Earlier in the ayah Allah described the believer as *qad 'amila al-salihat* — *had already done* righteous deeds. The little word *qad* before a past-tense verb in Arabic emphasizes completion: *had already*, *has indeed*, *has truly*. The deeds are done. They are in the past. The believer arrives with them in hand.

This too is a quiet rebuke to Pharaoh. The magicians had not lived a long pious life. They had been Pharaoh's magicians — coerced into the craft, their skill pressed into the service of his court for years. Their resumes did not include "righteous deeds." But the moment they prostrated, the moment they declared belief, *qad 'amila al-salihat* applied to them. The single righteous deed of *bowing when truth made itself undeniable* was, in that instant, enough. The word *qad* sealed it: it was done. It was complete. It was theirs.

You cannot accumulate enough wrong to disqualify a sincere moment of right. And Pharaoh, standing there planning crucifixions, could not disqualify it either.

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE SERMON

Ibn Kathir reads these three ayahs as a deliberate paired structure: warning *and* encouragement — the magicians held both before Pharaoh simultaneously. They did not only threaten. They invited. They said: here is what awaits the one who comes as a criminal, and here is what awaits the one who comes as a believer. The address was complete, and the completeness was intentional.

Look at the architecture of the three ayahs as a whole:

*Man ya'ti mujriman* → Jahannam → *la yamutu wa la yahya* — suspended, never resolving.

*Man ya'tihi mu'minan qad 'amila al-salihat* → *al-darajat al-'ula* → *jannat 'adn* → *khalidina* — dwelling, permanently arrived.

Two arrivals. Two conditions at arrival. Two outcomes. The symmetry is total and the contrast is absolute.

Notice what happens to *time* in each case. The criminal is suspended: neither dying nor living. Time does not resolve for him. The believer is also eternal: *khalidina fiha* — abiding always. Time does not end for him either. But the quality of existence in each case is opposite. One is the eternalization of suspension. The other is the eternalization of arrival.

*'Adn* — the word translated "perpetual residence" or, in some traditions, "Eden" — comes from the root ʿ-d-n, meaning to settle, to dwell, to remain in a place continuously. Al-Muyassar renders *jannat 'adn* as "gardens of permanent, eternal residence" — *al-iqama al-da'ima*. The believer does not merely visit the garden. The garden becomes his permanent dwelling. He has not merely escaped Jahannam; he has arrived somewhere. He has a place. The soul that *tazakka* — that worked at its own purification in a world where Pharaoh could threaten the body — finds in the end that it has arrived, fully and permanently, in the place it was always moving toward.

[PAUSE]

The magicians understood their own lives in this architecture. Hours before, they had been instruments of the *mujriman* project — employed in the service of the great refusal. And then they had crossed. In one public, irreversible moment, they had moved from one side of the ledger to the other. From the company of whoever comes as a criminal to the company of whoever comes as a believer, with deeds in hand.

Pharaoh thought the column you were born in — or coerced into — determined which column you would die in. The magicians, by the evidence of their own morning, knew otherwise.

## PART 3: WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE ROOM

Pharaoh was standing in this room when the magicians said this.

Let that settle for a moment.

The most powerful man in the world — a man who called himself god, who controlled the food supply of an empire, who held the magicians' lives in his hands at that very moment — was being given a theological lecture by the men he was about to execute.

The lecture was not for us. It was *for him*. These three ayahs were spoken to Pharaoh's face.

And the magicians knew — they had to know — that he would not accept it. The invitation in their words was real, but the probability of its acceptance was near zero. To accept what they were saying would require Pharaoh to become a person who *tazakka*, who cleared himself of *al-danas wa'l-khubth wa'l-shirk*, who arrived at his Lord as a believer. That was not a small ask. That was the dismantling of an entire world.

They said it anyway.

This is the detail that makes these ayahs different from a doctrinal statement about Heaven and Hell. The words are spoken *to someone*, in a real moment, by people who are about to be killed. The words cost something. And because they cost something, they mean more.

When the Quran quotes the magicians here, it is not simply providing eschatological information. It is showing us what conviction looks like when tested to its limit. It is showing us the magicians *becoming* the thing they were describing — people who had already arrived at their Lord as believers, who were already on the side of *man ya'tihi mu'minan*, even before they physically died.

[PAUSE]

They were living their own last lecture.

That is the final thing to hold. These are not people speaking theory about a distant future. These are people who have, in one morning, already crossed the divide they are describing. They know from the inside what it feels like to move from one side to the other. They know the weight of *qad 'amila al-salihat* because they just did it. They know the lightness of *tazakka* because the grime of Pharaoh's court is still on their hands and they have just washed it off publicly, in the sight of an empire.

When they say *la yamutu fiha wa la yahya*, they are not reciting a doctrine they were taught. They are describing the escape they just made — and warning the man they left behind.

## Distillation

Three ayahs. One chiasm. Two conditions of arrival. One word for what makes the difference.

*Mujriman* — one who cuts. One who severs himself from acknowledgment of his Lord, layer by layer, day by day, until the severance is the shape of his soul. In this life, a Tuesday afternoon. In the next, the only afternoon there is.

*Mu'minan qad 'amila al-salihat* — one who arrived with something in hand. Something completed. Something real. Even if the thing completed was one single bow, on one single morning, in front of one single staff that consumed every illusion he had ever built.

*Tazakka* — one who turned the work of purification on himself. Who said: whatever filth is on me, whatever rot, whatever association I have given to things that are not my Lord — I will clear it. Not because Pharaoh will reward it. Because I have chosen which condition to be in when I arrive.

The magicians had been Pharaoh's coerced instruments hours before. Their hands had performed the tricks of his court for years. And yet by the end of that morning, they were the ones standing free — not free from the crucifixion, but free from the thing the crucifixion could not touch.

Pharaoh could write no verdict over a soul that had already been acquitted by the One whose verdict is final.

## Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let us arrive suspended — neither dead to wrong nor alive to truth, our souls hanging at the threshold. Purify us with the purification that comes from within — from our own turning, our own clearing, our own choosing of which direction we face. Make us among those who arrive having already done the work. And grant us what the magicians of Pharaoh received that morning: the sudden clarity that sees what is real, and the courage to bow before it costs us nothing.*
