# Aaron's Unheeded Voice — The Psychology of "Until Moses Returns"

## Surah 20 (Ta-Ha), Ayahs 90-91

### The Arabic

> وَلَقَدْ قَالَ لَهُمْ هَارُونُ مِن قَبْلُ يَا قَوْمِ إِنَّمَا فُتِنتُم بِهِ ۖ وَإِنَّ رَبَّكُمُ الرَّحْمَٰنُ فَاتَّبِعُونِي وَأَطِيعُوا أَمْرِي ۝ قَالُوا لَن نَّبْرَحَ عَلَيْهِ عَاكِفِينَ حَتَّىٰ يَرْجِعَ إِلَيْنَا مُوسَىٰ

## Introduction: The World Before This Verse

Imagine the scene the Quran is asking us to enter.

Moses has gone up the mountain. He went for forty nights to receive the Tablets, the law, the speech of Allah Himself. He left his brother Aaron in charge — and Aaron is not a substitute, not a placeholder. Aaron is a prophet. Earlier in this very surah, Moses had begged Allah for him. *"And appoint for me a minister from my family — Aaron, my brother. Strengthen through him my back. And let him share my task."* Allah granted it. *"You have been given your request, O Moses."*

So when Moses leaves, the people are not abandoned. They are left in the hands of a prophet of God.

And then a man called the Sāmirī takes the gold the people were carrying — gold they had stripped from the Egyptians as they fled — and forms it into a calf. A calf that produces a sound — a *khuwār*, a lowing. Classical accounts diverge on the mechanism: some narrations describe a hollow form through which wind passed; others trace the quality back to the *turāb al-athar*, the dust the Sāmirī had gathered from beneath Jibrīl's footprint, which some traditions held gave life to whatever it touched. What the Quran fixes as certain is the sound itself — and what the people did in response to it. They fall before it. They begin to worship it. They begin to say, *"This is your god and the god of Moses — but he forgot."*

[PAUSE]

Now, sitting in that camp at that moment is a prophet. Aaron. Watching this happen. Watching the people he was entrusted with collapse into idolatry. And he has to do something.

What does he do?

He raises his voice. He says: *Stop. You are being tested. Your Lord is the Most Merciful. Follow me. Obey my command.*

And they answer him.

This ayah is the answer.

This ayah is what Aaron heard back from his people while he stood there, alone, watching them prostrate to a piece of metal that mooed in the wind.

The Quran does something remarkable here. It reaches *backwards* in the narrative. By the time we hear these two ayahs, Moses has already returned, already confronted Aaron, already grabbed him by the beard. The story has moved on. But the Quran says: *Wait. Stop. Before you judge Aaron — before you ask why he didn't do more — listen to what he said. And listen to what they said back.*

Here's what's stunning. We're going to look at the words Aaron chose. We're going to look at the words they chose. And we're going to discover that the difference between a prophet and a mob is not what they are loyal to — it is what they do with *waiting*. It is what they do with *legitimate authority*. It is what they do when truth shows up at an inconvenient time.

Stay with me. Because this is not just an ancient story.

This is a diagnostic of how *every* generation refuses what it knows.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Passive Voice of *Futintum* — and Why Aaron Refuses to Blame Them

Let me start somewhere that may surprise you. Aaron's first substantive sentence to his people is not an accusation.

Read it again. *"O my people — you are only being tested by it."*

In English, that sounds gentle. It sounds almost like an excuse. But the Arabic is doing something very precise. Aaron uses a word here — *futintum* — and the form of this word is going to teach us something about how a prophet speaks to a people who are sinning in front of him.

Let me teach the grammar before I open the word.

In Arabic, as in English, a verb can be **active** or **passive**. *He hit the ball* is active. *The ball was hit* is passive. The difference is not just stylistic. The difference is about *who you put in the spotlight*. Active voice spotlights the doer. Passive voice spotlights the receiver — and often, deliberately leaves the doer in shadow.

In Arabic, the passive is formed by changing the vowel pattern of the verb. The active verb *fatana* ("he tested") becomes *futina* ("he was tested"). And if you address it to a group — "you all were tested" — it becomes *futintum*.

Now. Here's what's happening.

Aaron sees them worshipping the calf. He could say:

*"You have done a terrible thing."* (Active. Subject: you. Verb: did. They are the doers of the offense.)

*"This calf has deceived you."* (Active. Subject: the calf. Verb: deceived. The calf is the doer.)

*"The Sāmirī has led you astray."* (Active. The Sāmirī is the doer.)

He could say any of those. They would all be true. The Quran itself, just five ayahs earlier, said *"and the Sāmirī has led them astray."*

But Aaron doesn't choose any of those. Aaron chooses the **passive**: *futintum*. *"You have been tested."*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. He doesn't even tell them *who* the agent of the testing is. The passive in Arabic — like in English — can completely conceal the subject. *Futintum bihi* — *"you have been tested by it"* — places the calf as the *instrument* (by means of *bi-*) but leaves the *tester* unnamed.

Why?

Here is the first thing to sit with. When you are speaking to people who are mid-sin, mid-collapse, mid-fall — there is a kind of speech that is technically true but pastorally lethal. If Aaron had said, *"You have done this. You. You chose this. You are guilty,"* — even though that's true — what would have happened?

The defenses would have gone up. The argument would have begun. The face-saving would have started. The mob would have entrenched.

Aaron knows this. So he reaches for a verb that does something extraordinary. He places them in the position of *receivers*. He turns them — for one merciful moment — from *perpetrators* into *patients*. From *the ones doing wrong* into *the ones to whom something is being done.*

Watch what this accomplishes.

It tells them: *something has happened to you. Something has come at you. You were targeted.* It validates the experience that something powerful has been working on them — because that is true. The Sāmirī did target them. The fitna did come at them. The gold and the molten calf and the strange sound — all of that was a force.

And then Aaron places a single restrictive particle in front of the verb. *Innamā*. **Only.** *Only* a test.

Let me sit with *innamā* for a moment, because in Arabic this word carries enormous weight. *Innamā* is what's called a *ḥarf al-ḥaṣr* — a particle of restriction. It builds a fence around the meaning. It says: *this and nothing else.* When Allah says in this same surah, ayah 14, *innanī ana Llāhu lā ilāha illā ana* — *"Indeed, I am Allah. There is no god but Me"* — the *inna* there is doing similar work: locking the meaning down so it cannot leak.

So when Aaron says *innamā futintum bih*, what he is saying — at the deepest level — is:

*"What is happening to you right now is **only** a test. **Only** a trial. **Nothing more than that.** This calf is not what you think it is. This experience is not what you think it is. You are not standing in the presence of a god. You are standing in a fitna. **Only** a fitna. Don't let the size of the experience fool you about its nature."*

And this is the prophetic move. This is what Aaron does that the people *cannot* do for themselves. He names the moment correctly. He says: *the thing in front of you is smaller than it looks. The voice you are hearing is not from where you think it's from. The God you are looking for is not here.*

Al-Tabari presses into this dimension with precision. The trial by the calf, he explains, was designed *to distinguish the sincere believer among you from the sick-hearted — the one who doubts his religion.* The fitna was not punishment. It was disclosure. It did not manufacture a condition in the heart; it revealed what each heart already carried. The calf could not create devotion that was not already latent. It could only surface what was there. This is what Aaron means when he calls it *only* a test: not that it is trivial, but that it is *diagnostic*. It has no power of its own. It is a mirror, not a god.

[PAUSE]

Aaron has now named what is *not* there. Now he must name what *is* there. And he chooses a name that should stop us in our tracks.

He says: *wa inna Rabbakumu r-Raḥmān.*

*"And indeed, your Lord is the Most Merciful."*

Let me ask you to notice something. Aaron does not say *Allah*. He does not say *al-Qādir* — the Powerful. He does not say *al-Jabbār* — the Compeller. He does not say *al-Muntaqim* — the Avenger.

He says ***Ar-Raḥmān***.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. They are *worshipping a calf*. They are committing the most serious act a covenant people can commit. The opposite of what their entire deliverance from Egypt was *for*. And Aaron, looking at this, names Allah by *Ar-Raḥmān*.

Why?

Here's what's remarkable. *Ar-Raḥmān* is the name that opens this entire surah's theological vocabulary. Surah Ta-Ha, ayah 5: *Ar-Raḥmānu ʿala l-ʿarshi stawā* — *"The Most Merciful, established over the Throne."* The Throne — the seat of all power, all dominion, all sovereignty — Allah does not describe Himself sitting upon it as the Powerful, or the King, or the Mighty. He describes Himself as ***Ar-Raḥmān*** sitting upon it.

This entire surah is the surah of *Ar-Raḥmān on the Throne*. And Aaron, in this moment of crisis, reaches for that exact name.

Why does this name matter *here*, of all places?

Because Aaron understands something about why people fall to idols. People run to idols because they think the real God is too far away, too high, too severe, too unapproachable. They make a *closer* god. A *visible* god. A *touchable* god. A calf you can stand in front of and pat on the side. The hidden assumption underneath all idolatry is: *the real God will not have me; the real God will not draw close; the real God is wrath.*

And Aaron, in the very moment they are running from the real God, says: *"Your Lord is **Ar-Raḥmān**."*

He is saying: *the One you are running from is the One who will receive you. The God you think is too far is the God whose mercy is closer than this calf could ever be. Stop running. Turn around. He is **Ar-Raḥmān**. Even now. Even at this moment, with this filth in your hands — He is **Ar-Raḥmān**.*

The name carries more than tenderness. Reading this passage, Ibn Kathir observes that Aaron was invoking a God who *created everything and decreed for everything its just measure* — the very Owner of the Mighty Throne, the One who does whatever He wants. The Merciful One is not a softer, more approachable face of power. He *is* the sovereign Power. The mercy does not dilute the dominion; it *holds* it. When Aaron says *Ar-Raḥmān*, he is not offering them a gentle deity in place of a severe one. He is telling them that the One whose Throne governs everything that exists — the calf included, the Sāmirī included, this entire moment included — is the One whose essential character is mercy toward them. There is nowhere else to stand that is safer than that.

[PAUSE]

This is the prophetic voice. This is what Aaron does in three short clauses:

He names the sin without crushing them — *futintum* in the passive.

He fences off the calf's claim to divinity — *innamā*, **only** a test.

He opens the door home — **Ar-Raḥmān**, with all His sovereignty intact.

And then, only after all that, does he give the imperative: *fa-ttabiʿūnī wa aṭīʿū amrī* — *"so follow me and obey my command."*

He doesn't lead with the command. He leads with the welcome.

This is what it looks like when a prophet speaks to his people in the middle of their fall.

Now let's see what they say back.

## Element Two: *ʿĀkifīn* and the Mouth That Stole a Sacred Word

Listen to their answer. *Qālū lan nabraḥa ʿalayhi ʿākifīna ḥattā yarjiʿa ilaynā Mūsā.*

*"We will never cease being devoted to it until Moses returns to us."*

Two words in this short answer are doing enormous work. Let me open them one at a time.

The first is **ʿākifīn**. Let me teach the root before I open the word.

The root *ʿ-k-f* in classical Arabic carries a very specific meaning. *ʿAkafa ʿalā shayʾ* means: to sit firmly upon something, to hover over it, to be devotedly attached to it, to refuse to leave it. It is not a casual sitting. It is the sitting of attachment, of vigil, of devotion. The participle *ʿākif* describes someone who has parked himself before something and will not move.

Now. There is a place in Islamic life where this exact word lives. In Ramadan, a Muslim performs *iʿtikāf* — the spiritual retreat in the masjid in the last ten nights. The worshipper stays. He does not leave. He sits before Allah. He is *ʿākif*. The Quran uses this very root in surah al-Baqarah, ayah 187, when it speaks of those who are *ʿākifūn fi l-masājid* — those who are devoting themselves in the mosques.

The word *ʿākif* is, in its native habitat, a **worship word**. It is reserved for sacred attachment. It is what you do before *Allah*.

[PAUSE]

Now read what these people are saying.

*"We will never cease being **ʿākifīn** upon it."*

Listen to that. They are using a worship word. They are using a sacred word. They are using a word that should have been spoken about *Allah on the Throne* — and they are spending it on a hollow piece of gold that lows when the wind blows.

And it matters to notice who, precisely, speaks this sentence. Al-Muyassar is specific: it was *ʿubbād al-ʿijl minhum* — the *worshippers of the calf among them*. Not the whole community. Some may have stood in silence, or wavered in the uncertainty of the crowd. But those who answered Aaron with this defiant *ʿākifīn* were the ones already committed — those who had already crossed the threshold into active devotion. The grammar of *ʿākifīn* belongs to the already-*ʿākif*. By the time they used the word, they had already become what it described.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most chilling features of idolatry. It is not just that the heart is misdirected. It is that the *vocabulary* is misdirected. The language of devotion gets transferred onto something that does not deserve it. The grammar of love is used about the wrong thing.

Notice something. Aaron told them: *Your Lord is the Most Merciful.* He gave them the proper object of *ʿakf* — the One who created everything and decreed for everything its just measure, the One established upon the Throne. And they refused that proper object — and instead, took the very disposition that should have been pointed at *Ar-Raḥmān*, and pointed it at the calf.

This is the geometry of every misdirected loyalty. Notice it in your own life. We do not lack the capacity for devotion. We do not lack stamina. We do not lack what the Arabs called *thabāt* — firmness, staying-power. We have all of that. We are constantly *ʿākifīn upon* something. The question is what.
