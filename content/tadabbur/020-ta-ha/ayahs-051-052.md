# Neither Errs Nor Forgets — Musa's Answer to Pharaoh's Trap

## INTRODUCTION

> قَالَ فَمَا بَالُ ٱلْقُرُونِ ٱلْأُولَىٰ
>
> *Qāla fa-mā bālu-l-qurūni-l-ūlā*
>
> "He said: Then what about the former generations?"
>
> قَالَ عِلْمُهَا عِندَ رَبِّى فِى كِتَـٰبٍۢ ۖ لَّا يَضِلُّ رَبِّى وَلَا يَنسَى
>
> *Qāla ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī fī kitāb. Lā yaḍillu rabbī wa-lā yansā.*
>
> "He said: Their knowledge is with my Lord, in a Book. My Lord neither errs nor forgets."

### Where We Are

Stand for a moment in the throne room of Egypt.

Musa, who fled this place as a hunted man, has walked back in carrying nothing but a stick and a brother. He has just delivered the core of his message — that the Lord he calls people to is the One **"Who gave everything its creation, then guided it"** (20:50). It is a sentence of extraordinary scope. Every creature, every faculty, every path — all of it sourced and steered by One.

Pharaoh listens. Pharaoh, who has spent his life cultivating an image of unanswerable authority, who keeps a court of magicians and priests precisely so that no question ever lands on him without a managed answer.

He cannot refute what Musa just said. So he does what cornered power has done in every age. He **changes the subject**. And he changes it in a way designed to make Musa look small.

"Then what about the former generations?"

It is not an innocent question. It is a verbal trap.

### The Strand This Ayah Belongs To

Surah Ta-Ha is, among many things, a manual on how a believer speaks to power. Allah has just rehearsed Musa for this exact moment — softening his speech (20:44), giving him a brother for support (20:30-31), placing in his hand a sign (20:17-22). All of that preparation now meets its first real test in dialogue.

And the test isn't a question Pharaoh actually wants answered. It's a question designed to see if Musa will overreach.

### The Hook

The whole exchange turns on a single sentence — six words in Arabic — that one Companion-era scholar described as containing more theology than entire treatises:

**Lā yaḍillu rabbī wa-lā yansā.**

*My Lord neither errs nor forgets.*

What we are about to discover is that the second of those two verbs — "forgets" — comes from the very root that Arabic grammarians say gives us the word for **human being** itself. The contrast Musa is drawing is not poetic. It is etched into the architecture of the language. [PAUSE]

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Bāl* — The Word Pharaoh Uses to Disguise His Trap

Let's start with Pharaoh's question. In English we hear *"what about the former generations?"* It sounds like a fair query. In Arabic, the construction is sharper than that.

The word he uses is **bāl** (بال).

Before we open the Quranic use, sit with the word as Arabs used it. *Bāl* in everyday speech meant "state of mind," "concern," "the inner condition of a thing." When you say to someone, *mā bāluka?* — you're asking, "what is the state of you?" — you're poking at their inner condition.

Now hear Pharaoh's question with that texture: *fa-mā bālu-l-qurūni-l-ūlā?* — "So what is **the inner condition** of the former generations?"

He's not asking a chronological question. He's asking a metaphysical one. **What about their souls? Their fates? Their accountability? Their guidance? Their welfare?**

Notice something. Pharaoh has deliberately left the question open. He hasn't specified what he's asking about. This is a classic rhetorical ambush — what one classical commentator called *al-suʾāl al-mubham*, the deliberately vague question.

Why deliberate vagueness? Because if Musa answers about their fate, Pharaoh can shift to ask about their knowledge. If Musa answers about their knowledge, Pharaoh can shift to ask about their punishment. The vagueness is the trap. **No matter what Musa specifies, Pharaoh will pivot.**

It's the oldest rhetorical move in the world. Today we have a name for it: **whataboutism**. *You're telling me to follow this message? What about all the people who came before? What about my ancestors? What about the people who never heard? What about — what about — what about —*

Stay with me. The function of this move is never genuine inquiry. It is **decentering**. It tries to take the conversation that was just placed at your doorstep and scatter it across all of human history so that nothing can be addressed.

What is remarkable about Musa's response is that he refuses to take the bait — but he also refuses to be dismissive. He doesn't say "that's a distraction" (which would be true). He doesn't say "I don't know" (which would weaken him). He does something much more subtle. [PAUSE]

### Element Two: *ʿIlmuhā ʿinda Rabbī fī Kitāb* — The Geography of Knowledge

Look at how Musa begins his answer:

*ʿIlmuhā ʿinda rabbī fī kitāb.*

**Their knowledge is with my Lord, in a Book.**

Before we unpack what this *says*, notice what it *does*. Musa receives Pharaoh's vague mega-question and, in five words, **relocates the entire question**. He doesn't try to answer it. He moves it.

Three prepositions are doing the work here. Let's slow down on each.

**First: ʿilmuhā** — "their knowledge." Notice the pronoun *-hā*. The "their" of "the former generations." Musa preserves Pharaoh's vague question intact — whatever Pharaoh meant by "their condition," that information *exists*. Musa doesn't say "you don't need to know" or "that's not the point." He affirms there is something to be known.

**Second: ʿinda rabbī** — "with my Lord." The word *ʿinda* in Arabic is more intimate than the English "with." It means "in the presence of," "at the immediate disposal of." When something is *ʿinda* someone, they have direct access to it.

Notice something. Musa doesn't say *ʿinda Allāh* — "with Allah" as a remote being. He says *ʿinda rabbī* — "with **my Lord**." The pronoun *-ī*, the "my," is doing real work. Musa is announcing relationship. *My Rabb. My Sustainer. My Owner.* In the very moment Pharaoh is trying to decenter the conversation, Musa centers it on the relationship that makes him unshakeable.

**Third: fī kitāb** — "in a Book."

Here is one of the most consequential metaphors in the Quran. Allah's knowledge is described again and again as being *fī kitāb* — in a written record. We meet it in 6:59: *"and not a leaf falls but He knows it; nor is there a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything fresh or dry, but is in a clear Record."*

Why a Book?

The classical commentators noted something beautiful here. A book has two qualities that pure mental knowledge does not. **It is fixed** — what is written cannot slip. **And it is retrievable** — what is recorded can be returned to. Allah, of course, does not need a book in the way we do. The metaphor is not for Him. The metaphor is for us. It is the language in which our limited minds can grasp the un-graspable: that nothing — no soul, no act, no inner state, no whisper of any "former generation" — has been lost.

Stay with that. *Nothing has been lost.*

The vague mega-question Pharaoh threw — Musa is saying: every detail of it is somewhere. Recorded. Findable. Not to *Pharaoh*. Not to *Musa*. **To the One Who keeps the Book.**

What this invites us to consider is that the believer's epistemology is not "I know everything." It is also not "nothing can be known." It is *the knowledge exists, and it is in safe hands*. [PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Lā Yaḍillu wa-Lā Yansā* — The Architecture Built into the Language

And now we come to the sentence that stops the conversation.

*Lā yaḍillu rabbī wa-lā yansā.*

**My Lord neither errs nor forgets.**

Two verbs, both negated, paired by a *wāw*. In English it sounds like one statement said two ways. In Arabic, these are two distinct attributes — and they describe the two distinct ways that knowledge can fail in a created being.

Let's open them.

**Yaḍillu** — from the root *ḍ-l-l*. This is the same root we say every day in Surah al-Fātiḥah: *ghayri-l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa lā-ḍ-ḍāllīn*. *Ḍāll* — "the one who has lost the way." *Ḍalāl* — "going astray." The root means: to lose track of where something is, to misplace, to be off-target.

When you negate *yaḍillu* about knowledge, you are saying: **He never misplaces a piece of information.** Nothing is filed in the wrong drawer. Nothing is mistaken for something else. There is no mis-knowing.

**Yansā** — from the root *n-s-y*. To forget. To have something fall out of active awareness.

Now — listen carefully — the classical Arab grammarians, including those cited in *Lisān al-ʿArab* and the *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib, transmitted a famous derivation: that the very word for human — **insān** — is etymologically connected to **nisyān**, forgetting. Imam Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said: *"He was named insān only because he was given a covenant and he forgot."*

Whether or not we accept that specific derivation as linguistically definitive, the resonance is undeniable. The Quran itself plays on it. In Surah Ta-Ha, just a few ayahs later than where we are, Allah will say of Adam: *"And We had taken a covenant from Adam before, but he forgot — wa nasiya — and We did not find in him firm resolve"* (20:115).

So here is what Musa is doing — and it is breathtaking once you see it.

He is standing before Pharaoh, a *human being*, talking to a *human being*, about other *human beings* — the "former generations." And humans, by the very semantics of what we are called, are forgetters. We are the species whose covenant slips. Whose memory fades. Whose grasp of "former generations" — even the names of our own great-grandparents — is gone within four generations.

And in this room of forgetters, asking about forgetters, about other forgetters who came before — Musa points to the One Who is **lā yansā**. **Not a forgetter.**

The contrast is not rhetorical. It is built into the language. *Insān* — forgets. *Allah* — *lā yansā*.

Notice something. Pharaoh's question presupposed that the records of those former generations had been *lost*. That's why he thought the question would land. *They're gone, Musa — what are you going to say about them?* Musa's answer says: **only because they're lost to you, Pharaoh. They are not lost.** [PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. In two short verbs, Musa has named the only two ways knowledge can fail:

1. **Yaḍillu** — knowing wrongly (misplacement)
2. **Yansā** — knowing no longer (forgetting)

There is no third category. If a being neither *misplaces* what it knows nor *forgets* what it knew, that being's knowledge is **complete and permanent**. Musa has, in six words, given a full description of divine omniscience — described not by piling on attributes, but by **denying the only two ways knowledge can ever lapse**.

This is a classical Arabic rhetorical move: defining a thing by the impossibility of its opposite. And Musa's choice of those particular two verbs is not random. They are the two verbs that describe **what humans do all the time**.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

What this linguistic construction has shown us is not just a description of Allah's knowledge. It is a portrait of two opposing epistemologies in the same room. Pharaoh, the king who pretends to know but actually depends on the *ignorance* of others to maintain his power. Musa, the prophet who admits he doesn't carry the answer — but knows where it lives.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the kind of life it is possible to live when your Lord is *lā yansā*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When You're Asked a Question Outside Your Domain

You've been here.

You're in a conversation that mattered. You said something true — something you had carefully thought through. And the person across from you, instead of engaging it, threw a question at you that was *bigger than the conversation*. *Yeah, but what about all those other people? What about historical injustice? What about the people who never heard? What about my uncle who was a kind man and didn't pray? What about —*

And you froze. Because the question was real, even if the asker wasn't sincere. And you didn't have an answer.

Most of us, in that moment, do one of two things. We either **bluff** — we manufacture an answer to keep the appearance of authority — or we **collapse** — we say "I don't know" in a way that lets our original point be discarded along with our credibility.

Musa is teaching us a third move.

He doesn't bluff. He doesn't claim to have the case file on every former generation in his pocket. That would be Pharaoh-style — pretending to know what only Allah knows.

He doesn't collapse. He doesn't say "I don't know, so let's drop the original message." That would be the believer's classic mistake — letting the unanswered tangent dissolve the answered core.

He **redirects to the rightful authority**. *I don't carry that. My Lord does. And He carries it perfectly.*

Notice what this does. It allows Musa to say, with full integrity, *"I don't have that knowledge"* — without making his lack of knowledge into an indictment of the message. The message stood on its own when he gave it (20:50). His not-knowing about former generations does not unmake it.

This is one of the most useful spiritual movements available to a believer in any conversation about faith. **You are not Allah's defense attorney.** You are a witness. A witness testifies to what they have seen. When asked about something outside their seeing, an honest witness says, *"That's beyond what I observed — but the One Who observed it is trustworthy."*

Stay with this. The reason most of us cannot do this comfortably is that we've absorbed, often without noticing, an idea that **a believer must have an answer to every question or the faith is shaken.** That's not faith. That's apologetic anxiety. Real *īmān* is at peace saying, *"I don't know — and the One Who knows neither errs nor forgets."*

The application — and this matters — is to **rehearse this sentence before you need it**. *ʿIlmuhā ʿinda rabbī.* Their knowledge is with my Lord. The next time someone hands you a vague mega-question to derail what you were saying, that sentence is your shelter.

### Theme Two: A Lord Who Does Not Forget — and What That Changes About Your Life

Now let's turn the second half of the verse around and let it speak to your life directly.

*Lā yansā* — He does not forget.

We talked about how the Arabic root for "forget" is the same root that gives us the word for human. We are forgetters. Now sit with what your forgetting has cost you.

Think for a moment about acts of yours that you considered significant when you did them. The kindness you showed someone fifteen years ago. The hidden charity you gave when no one was looking. The night prayer you stood when no one knew. The time you forgave someone who never apologized. The time you bit your tongue when you had every right to lash back.

Where are those acts now?

If you depend on **other people's memory** to keep them alive — they are mostly gone. The person you helped probably forgot. Witnesses moved away. Records were not kept. *To human memory, your best moments are largely lost.*

If you depend on **your own memory** to keep them alive — they are also fading. You can't recall most of the kind things you've done. Your best moments are not even fully present to you.

This is the human condition. We are creatures whose deeds slip past the only memory systems we know about — ours and other people's.

Now hear Musa's six words again, but this time addressed to *you*:

***Lā yaḍillu rabbī wa-lā yansā.***

Your Lord does not misplace. Your Lord does not forget.

That night you stood and no one saw you — He saw, and **He has not forgotten**. That moment of restraint that cost you something — **not forgotten**. That charity you slipped under the door — **not forgotten**. That tear of repentance you cried at 3 a.m. that even you no longer remember — **not forgotten**.

And — this is the harder side, and we have to say it honestly — that wrong you did and rationalized away, that harm you caused and never apologized for, that pattern you keep telling yourself isn't really a problem — **also not forgotten**. Allah's *lā yansā* is not selective. It is the architecture of His justice and the architecture of His mercy at the same time.

Here is the paradox, and it deserves to sit:

A Lord Who *forgets* would be a Lord Who lets your worst moments quietly fall off the ledger. Most humans, in our worst hours, secretly want that kind of god. *Just let it go away.*

But — stay with this — **a Lord Who forgets your worst would also forget your best.** A god of selective amnesia is a god of total amnesia, because once you accept that He forgets, you have no ground to claim what He remembers. The mercy of *lā yansā* and the justice of *lā yansā* are the same attribute. You cannot have one without the other.

What this opens for the believer is a way of living the small moments with seriousness. Because a Lord Who does not forget means **no act is small to Him**. The same Quran that tells us *lā yansā* tells us, in Surah al-Zalzalah, *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it; and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* The atom-weight only matters if the Recorder is *lā yansā*.

There is also a second consolation here, and it is for those carrying long-buried wounds. Things were done to you that no one acknowledged. Wrongs that you bore alone, that no court tried, that no one ever said "I'm sorry" for. Sometimes the loneliness of injustice is not the injustice itself — it is the fear that **the world has forgotten** what was done to you. That you are alone with a memory the universe doesn't share.

*Lā yansā.*

**Not a single moment of what was done to you has been misplaced or forgotten.** It is *fī kitāb*. In the Book. Witnessed by the One Whose witnessing does not lapse. The day will come when every one of those moments will be opened, and your case will be heard by a Judge Who has all of it, with nothing missing.

The application here is two-sided. **First**, live as though every small good is being recorded — because it is. The hidden act, the unseen patience, the unwitnessed restraint — these are where the bulk of your record will be written, not in the public deeds. **Second**, when you have been wronged and no one came to your defense, give your case to the Recorder. Not as a slogan. As a real act of trust. *Yā Rabb, You did not forget.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now that we've been through the journey, look back at how this exchange is built.

Pharaoh asks one question and uses one verb: *qāla* — "he said." Vague, fishing, decentered.

Musa answers and uses the same one verb: *qāla* — "he said." But what follows is structured as a perfect arc:

1. **Locate the knowledge**: *ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī* — its knowledge is with my Lord
2. **Locate where He keeps it**: *fī kitāb* — in a Book
3. **Affirm its perfection negatively** — by denying the two and only two failures of knowledge:
   - *lā yaḍillu* — He does not misplace
   - *wa lā yansā* — and He does not forget

The structure moves from **with Whom** → **in what** → **how completely**. From relationship, to record, to the unfailing nature of the record.

It is, in 11 words, a complete doctrine of divine knowledge.

And notice the architecture of the whole encounter: Pharaoh's verb count vs. Musa's. Pharaoh said five words. Musa said eleven. Pharaoh's question expanded into vagueness. Musa's answer compressed into precision. **The one with less to defend speaks longer; the one whose ground is firm speaks tighter.** That is the signature of a believer who knows where his knowledge ends and Whose knowledge begins.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two sides.

The linguistic side showed us that Musa diagnosed Pharaoh's vague question with a precise answer — and that the precision came from describing Allah's knowledge by the negation of its only two failures. The thematic side showed us what it means to live as a forgetter (insān) under the gaze of a Lord Who does not forget (*lā yansā*).

They meet here: **You will face questions you can't answer. Both from others, and from yourself in the dark. Both about the world's history, and about your own. The believer's home, in every one of those moments, is the same five words: ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī fī kitāb. The knowledge of it is with my Lord, in a Book.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time someone derailed me with a "what about" question — and how did I handle it? Did I bluff, collapse, or redirect to the rightful Knower?

2. What good have I done that I am quietly grieving because no one remembers it? Can I let *lā yansā* be enough?

3. What wrong has been done to me that I fear the world has forgotten? What changes if I sincerely place that case in the Book?

4. What in my life am I hoping Allah *will* forget? And does my hope for His forgetfulness about my failures hold up against my hope for His memory of my goods?

5. If my Lord truly does not forget, what is the smallest hidden act I could do today that I would want recorded for me?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the world hands you a question larger than your knowledge, do not bluff and do not collapse — relocate it to the One Whose Book never errs and Whose memory never fades.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before sleep, return to those five words: *ʿilmuhā ʿinda rabbī fī kitāb.* Take one thing you've been anxious about — one piece of unfinished business, one wrong done to you, one private ache no one knows about — and place it, deliberately, in His record. Not as a metaphor. As a real act of handover. *This is in Your Book. You did not misplace it. You did not forget it. I trust the keeping of it to You.*

That handover is the rest the forgetters are made for.

### Du'a

*Yā Rabb — You Who do not err and do not forget — we are the children of forgetting. We have lost the count of our own deeds, and we have lost the names of those before us. Let our hearts learn to rest in what is recorded with You. Make the small unseen good of our days something Your Book preserves. And on the Day the Books are opened, let our forgetfulness be covered by Your remembering, and our failures be covered by Your forgiving. Āmīn.*
