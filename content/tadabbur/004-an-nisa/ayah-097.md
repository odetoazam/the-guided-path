# The Earth of Allah Was Spacious

## When the Excuse Meets the Angels

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read the words to you first. In Arabic, then in sound, then in a meaning you can hold.

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ تَوَفَّىٰهُمُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ ظَالِمِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ قَالُوا۟ فِيمَ كُنتُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ كُنَّا مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ قَالُوٓا۟ أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا۟ فِيهَا ۚ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ مَأْوَىٰهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ ۖ وَسَآءَتْ مَصِيرًا

*Inna alladhīna tawaffāhumu-l-malā'ikatu ẓālimī anfusihim qālū fīma kuntum — qālū kunnā mustaḍ'afīna fi-l-arḍ — qālū alam takun arḍu-llāhi wāsi'atan fa-tuhājirū fīhā — fa-ulā'ika ma'wāhum jahannam, wa-sā'at maṣīrā.*

"Indeed, those whom the angels take in death while they were wronging themselves — the angels will say, *'In what condition were you?'* They will answer, *'We were made weak in the land.'* The angels will say, *'Was not the earth of Allah spacious enough for you to emigrate within it?'* For those, their refuge is Hell — and an evil destination it is."

### Rebuild the world

To feel this verse, you have to stand in a city that is splitting in two.

Mecca, in the years after the Hijra. The Prophet ﷺ has left for Medina. The believers have followed, family by family, abandoning houses and date-palms and livelihoods, walking out into the desert with nothing because staying meant living a lie. But not everyone went.

The classical commentators — al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and others — preserve narrations that this verse came down about a specific group. Al-Baghawī names some of them: people of Mecca who had **spoken the words of Islam** — who believed, in some chamber of the heart — but who did not emigrate. They stayed. They kept their homes, their standing, their safety among their clan. And when the people of Mecca marched out to fight at Badr, these men marched out *with them* — and were killed there, among the ranks of the disbelievers they had never found the courage to leave.

I want you to hold the weight of that. These were not enemies of the Prophet ﷺ. These were people who, by their own tongues, were on the right side. They simply never moved their feet to match their tongues. And the gap between the word and the foot is the entire subject of this ayah.

And notice how the verse labels them before they say a single word: *ẓālimī anfusihim* — *wronging their own souls.* The classical commentators are exact about what that wrong was. It was not some separate sin smuggled in alongside. Al-Jalālayn glosses it plainly: they wronged themselves *by remaining among the disbelievers and abandoning the hijra*; al-Muyassar reads it the same way — *by sitting still in the abode of disbelief and leaving off the migration.* The staying *was* the wrong. The sitting-still *was* the self-injury. That is the thread reaching back to the verses just before this one — those who *sit back* while others strive in the path of Allah — and here the sitting-back is finally named for what it does to the one who chooses it: it wounds him.

There are, the scholars note, **more than one narration** about exactly who is meant — some say a broader group of those who professed and stayed, some name particular men. We do not need to settle the names. What we need is the moment they all share: the moment the soul is collected, and a question is asked that no excuse survives.

### The Hook

Here is what should keep you leaning in.

The people in this verse have a defense ready. It is not a wild defense. It is *reasonable.* It is the same sentence millions of us say, in a thousand forms, every single day of our lives: **"I had no choice. The situation was bigger than me. I was made this way by forces I couldn't fight."**

And the angels do not argue with their psychology. They do not say *you were strong.* They ask one question — a question about **geography** — and the entire architecture of the excuse falls down in silence.

What is that question? And why does a question about the *size of the earth* demolish a defense about the *weakness of the self*?

Let's go find out. Slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — تَوَفَّىٰهُمُ — "Taken in Full"

Before we touch the word, let me give you a small picture from your own world.

Imagine you've done work for someone all month. At the end of the month you go to collect your wages. There's a particular feeling in being paid *in full* — every hour accounted for, nothing rounded down, nothing left owing. The Arabs had a word for exactly that act: **wafā** — to fulfill a debt completely, to pay every last portion of what is due, to leave no remainder.

Hold that feeling: *complete collection. Nothing left behind.*

Now look at the word the Quran chooses for death here.

It does not say *qatalathum* — the angels *killed* them. It does not say *amātathum* — caused them to die. It says:

> تَوَفَّىٰهُمُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ
>
> *tawaffāhumu-l-malā'ikah*

The angels **tawaffā** them. And *tawaffā* is built on that very root — **w-f-y**, the root of paying in full. The grammarians log it as a **Form V verb**, *tafaʿʿala* — a form that turns the act inward and completes it. *Wafā* is to pay in full. *Tawaffā* is **to take in full, to collect every portion of what is owed.**

So in the Arabic, death is not a destruction. It is a **collection.** The angels arrive not as executioners but as **agents of full settlement** — and what they collect, in full, is *you.* Your soul. Your record. Every hour you were given, accounted for, nothing rounded down.

Let's sit with this.

When you frame death as *killing*, the dying person is a victim — something is being done *to* them, against them, ending them. But when you frame death as **tawaffī — being taken in full** — the dying person is suddenly an **account being closed.** And an account that is closed *in full* is an account where every entry will be read.

Notice something. The very verb that opens this verse already contains the courtroom that follows. The moment Allah says *the angels took them in full*, He has already told you: **nothing was missed. No portion was overlooked. The collection is complete.** And so when the questioning begins three words later, you understand — there is no part of this person's life that escaped the ledger.

Here's what's remarkable. These were people who spent their whole lives *managing* what was seen of them. Professing faith with the tongue while withholding the feet. Keeping the appearance among one crowd, the reality among another. Their entire strategy was the **partial** disclosure — show this, hide that. And the first word that meets them in death is **tawaffā** — the *full* collection. The one thing they spent their lives avoiding is the precise thing the angels come to do.

The strategy of the half-life ends at the hand that takes the whole.

[PAUSE]

That is what one verb has shown us. Death is not the thing that interrupts the account. Death is the **closing of the account in full.**

### Element Two — مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ — The Grammar of "I Was Made Weak"

But first, hear the question they are answering, because the commentators sharpen it for us. The angels ask *fīma kuntum* — literally, *"in what were you?"* The reflexive translation is "in what condition," but al-Jalālayn and the muyassar reading both narrow it: the question is not about *circumstance,* it is about *allegiance* — **"In what state were you regarding your religion? On which side were you standing?"** And they tell us the *tone,* too. The angels ask this **as a rebuke** — *tawbīkhan* — not as neutral curiosity. This is not an interview at the threshold of death. It is a reproach with a question mark. And the dying, the same commentators note, answer **as people offering an excuse** — *muʿtadhirīn.* So before we even reach the grammar, the classical readers have already staged the scene exactly: a reproach, met by an apology. Now hear the apology.

We come to the heart of the verse. And to do it justice, I need to teach you something about how Arabic builds a word — because in *this* word, the grammar **is** the argument.

In Arabic, you can take a simple meaning and bend it into shapes that change who is responsible. Let me show you with the idea of *weakness*, the root **ḍ-ʿ-f.**

The plain word for *a weak person* is **ḍaʿīf.** Simple. It describes a state. "I am weak." It points at me and stops.

But Arabic has a heavier machine, called **Form X** — *istafʿala.* When you run a root through Form X, it often means **to deem something, to consider it, to treat it as, to make it into** that thing. *ʿAlima* is to know; *istaʿlama* is to seek out knowledge. And **ḍaʿīf** — weak — run through Form X becomes **istaḍʿafa**: to *deem* someone weak, to *treat* them as weak, to *render* them weak, to grind them down into weakness.

Now hold your breath, because here is what the people in this verse actually say. They do not say *kunnā ḍuʿafāʾ* — "we were weak." They say:

> كُنَّا مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ
>
> *kunnā mustaḍʿafīna fi-l-arḍ*

**Mustaḍʿafīn** — the **passive participle of Form X.** Not "we were weak." But: **"we were the ones *made* weak. We were *deemed* weak. We were rendered powerless — *by them, by the situation, by forces acting upon us.*"**

Do you hear the move? It is the most sophisticated sentence in the entire verse, and it is a sentence of **defense.** They have reached, in the final instant of their lives, for the exact grammatical form that **relocates the cause of their weakness outside themselves.** "We did not fail. We were *failed.* We were not weak. We were *weakened.* Something was done *to* us."

And the commentators fill in the *content* of the plea — what, exactly, they claimed to have been made too weak *to do.* Al-Jalālayn reads their answer as *ʿājizīna ʿan iqāmat al-dīn* — *"we were powerless to establish our religion."* Al-Muyassar has them say *we were too weak in our land, unable to repel the oppression and the subjugation pressed down on us.* Notice what robe the excuse wears: it is the robe of piety. They do not say *we loved our homes too much.* They say *we couldn't practice our faith there; we were too overpowered to live as believers.* It is the most defensible-sounding excuse a believer can possibly make — religious failure blamed on religious persecution — and it is precisely the one the angels are about to take apart.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because I do not want you to hear this as mockery of suffering. The form *mustaḍʿaf* is a real category in the Quran, and it is sometimes a category of **genuine, honored victims.** That is precisely what makes this moment so sharp. These people reached for a true word — and applied it falsely.

How do we know it's false here? Because **the Quran itself tells us, one verse later.** Look at 4:98, the very next ayah: *"Except the truly oppressed — the mustaḍʿafīn — among the men, women, and children who **have no means** and **cannot find a way.**"* Allah confirms there *is* a real *mustaḍʿaf*: the one with **lā ḥīlatan** — no capacity, no resource — and **lā yahtadūna sabīlā** — who cannot find a road out.

So the Quran draws the line with a scalpel. The word *mustaḍʿaf* is true for the one who has no means and no path. The people in verse 97 used that same word — **but they had both.** They had means. They had a road. We know, because the angels are about to name the road.

Notice the absence here, and read it as carefully as the presence. When the angels ask *"in what condition were you?"* the dying do not say *"we tried and were stopped."* They do not say *"we set out and were turned back."* They reach immediately, reflexively, for the *identity* of weakness — *mustaḍʿafīn* — as though weakness were a fixed thing that happened *to* them rather than a choice they kept making. **They named themselves victims before they named a single thing that victimized them.**

And that is the most modern sentence in the Quran.

### Element Three — أَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةً — and فَتُهَاجِرُوا — The Spacious Earth and the Severance Never Made

The angels' reply does not attack the feeling. Watch how surgical it is. It is a single question, and it changes the subject from the *self* to the *earth*:

> أَلَمْ تَكُنْ أَرْضُ ٱللَّهِ وَٰسِعَةً فَتُهَاجِرُوا۟ فِيهَا
>
> *alam takun arḍu-llāhi wāsiʿatan fa-tuhājirū fīhā*

"Was not **the earth of Allah** **spacious** — so that you could **emigrate** within it?"

Two words here are doing enormous work. Let me open each.

First: **wāsiʿah** — spacious — from the root **w-s-ʿ**, breadth, expanse, room. But notice the construction the angels choose. They do not say "was not *the earth* spacious." They say **arḍ-ullāh** — "the earth *of Allah.*" And the instant they attach the earth to Allah's name, the excuse has nowhere left to hide. Because the *mustaḍʿaf* claim — "we were weak *in the land*" — depended on the land belonging, in practice, to the oppressors. *Their* city. *Their* clan. *Their* power. And the angels answer: it was never theirs. **It was the earth of Allah, and it was wide.** You spoke as though you were trapped in *their* corner. You were standing the whole time in *His* expanse.

The people made the earth small by accepting that one city was the whole of it. The angels make the earth large by naming its true Owner. **Your sense of how trapped you are is exactly the size of the god you've forgotten.**

And the commentators complete the angels' logic, closing the circle on the excuse. The question, al-Jalālayn explains, means: *was the earth not wide enough that you could go out from the land of disbelief to some other land?* — and al-Muyassar supplies the purpose left implicit: *to a land where you would be secure in your religion.* See how surgically this answers their own plea. They had said, *we were powerless to uphold our faith there.* The angels reply: *then His wide earth held a place where you could have.* The very inability they offered as their defense already had its remedy sewn into the size of the world — a land where the dīn they claimed they couldn't practice would have been safe. The cure was a road. They simply declined to walk it.

Now the second word, and it brings us back to grammar one final time: **fa-tuhājirū** — "so that you could emigrate." This is **Form III**, *fāʿala*, from the root **h-j-r.** And Form III is the form of **a relationship, a mutual or directed action between two parties.** The bare root *hajara* means to abandon, to cut off, to sever ties. But run it through Form III — **hājara** — and it becomes something with two faces: **to part from a place *and* to be parted from it. A deliberate severance, in both directions.**

That is why the Hijra is not called *a journey* — *safar.* It is called *hijra* — a **severing.** To emigrate in the way of Allah is not to relocate your body. It is to **cut a tie** — to let the place you loved let go of you, and to let go of it, at the same time. The Form III tells you it costs something on *both* ends. The home loses you; you lose the home.

And here is the quiet devastation of the verse. The people refused the *hijra* — the small, painful severance from a city — so that they would not have to lose their home. And the verse ends by telling us what they lost instead:

> فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ مَأْوَىٰهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ
>
> "For those, their **refuge** is Hell."

The word is **maʾwā** — from the root **A-w-y** — a place you *take shelter in*, a place of return and safety. Feel the blade in it. They stayed in Mecca *seeking maʾwā* — shelter, the security of the familiar home. They would not sever one earthly shelter. And so the verse hands them a *maʾwā* — the very thing they were protecting — but its name is now **Jahannam.** The shelter they refused to leave becomes the shelter they cannot leave.

[PAUSE]

Let me name what these three words have shown us together, because the verse built a single machine out of three verb-forms. **Tawaffā** — Form V — the soul collected *in full.* **Mustaḍʿaf** — Form X — the self declared *made weak* by others. **Tuhājirū** — Form III — the severance *they would not make.* The full taking. The false weakness. The refused cut. That is the whole drama in three grammatical shapes.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Cage You Build From Reasons

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a phenomenon psychologists have studied for decades, and it has a sober name: **learned helplessness.** In the original experiments, creatures were placed in a situation where their actions could not change the outcome — and something broke. Later, when escape became fully possible — when the door was wide open — they *did not even try.* They had learned, in their bones, a single sentence: *nothing I do matters.* And so they sat in an open cage.

Sit with how exact that is to our verse. The people in 4:97 are standing before an **open door** — the earth of Allah, spacious — and they are reciting the sentence of the cage: *we were made weak.* The tragedy is not that they were chained. **The tragedy is that they were free, and could no longer feel it.**

Now bring it home, because this verse is not really about seventh-century Mecca. It is about the cage *you* have furnished with reasons.

Think of the thing you know you should change and have not. The relationship that is quietly corroding your soul. The habit you confess in every Ramadan and resume by Shawwāl. The prayer you keep meaning to anchor. The distance from Allah you've narrated to yourself as *a phase, a season, a thing that happened to me.* And listen to the grammar of your own excuse — because I promise you it is **Form X.** *I was made this way. My family did this to me. The culture, the schedule, the depression, the people — they rendered me unable.* **Mustaḍʿaf.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what is so merciful and so terrifying about this verse at once. Allah does not, anywhere in it, deny that you were hurt. He does not say *you were never weakened.* The next verse goes out of its way to honor those who **genuinely** have no means and no way. So this is not a verse that mocks the wounded.

It is a verse that asks one question, the angels' question, of every excuse we carry: **was the earth of Allah spacious?** Was there, anywhere in your situation, **a door** — a smaller life, a harder road, a humbler option, a single step you could have taken and chose not to because the step required you to *sever* something you loved more than you loved the change?

Because that is the line. The line between the honored *mustaḍʿaf* of verse 98 and the rebuked one of verse 97 is not the *amount* of suffering. **It is the presence of a door you would not walk through.** One had no way and could not find a road. The other had a wide earth and a clear road, and stayed — because leaving would have cost a *hijra*, a severance, and the comfort of the cage was dearer than the freedom past the door.

What does this change about tomorrow? Tomorrow, when you reach for the Form X sentence — *I was made this way* — stop and ask the angels' question on your own tongue, while you still have a tongue to ask it with: **Is the earth of Allah spacious? Is there a door I am calling a wall because the door requires me to leave something behind?** Name the door out loud. You do not have to walk through it today. But you are no longer allowed to pretend it isn't there.

### Theme Two — The Severance That Saves You

Let me tell you why leaving is so hard, and why the Quran calls it *hijra* and not merely *moving.*

Everything that traps us, traps us through **love.** No one stays in the cage because they enjoy the bars. They stay because something *inside* the cage is loved — the familiar faces, the known streets, the identity, the comfort of not having to begin again. The Meccans who stayed did not stay because they loved disbelief. They stayed because they loved **home** — the clan, the property, the standing, the warmth of belonging to the only world they'd ever known.

And this is why the word is *hijra* — that Form III severance that cuts on **both** ends. To do it, you must let yourself lose something you love, *and* let it lose you. It is a small death. The early Muslims walked out of Mecca and felt their own homes let go of their hands. That is the price the verb names.

Now here is the movement I want you to follow, because it turns the whole verse inside out.

The people who refused the *hijra* were **protecting a shelter** — a *maʾwā.* They would not sever their tie to home, because home *was* their refuge. And the verse hands them, at the end, a *maʾwā* — the exact word, the exact thing they prized: a refuge. Except it is Jahannam.

Do you see what Allah is teaching through the repetition of that one word? **You will have a refuge either way.** The only question is *which* one. Refuse the painful severance from the small shelter, and you inherit the unbearable shelter you can never sever from. Accept the small death of the *hijra*, and Allah —

— look at where this passage *goes*, because the surah does not leave us in Hell. Read three verses on, to 4:100: *"Whoever emigrates in the way of Allah will find in the earth **many a place of refuge** — murāghaman — and abundance."* The same root-idea of refuge returns, but now it is **wide, plural, open.** The one who *severs* is given **many** shelters. The one who *clings* is given **one**, and it is fire.

[PAUSE]

This is the paradox at the floor of the verse, and I do not want to resolve it too quickly, because it is the law of the whole spiritual life: **the shelter you refuse to leave becomes a prison; the shelter you are willing to lose becomes a door to many.** The grip that protects you is the grip that traps you. The hand that opens is the hand that is filled.

You feel this, even now, in things much smaller than migration. The job you cling to out of fear becomes a slow suffocation; the leap you finally take opens three roads you couldn't see from inside the first one. The version of yourself you refuse to release — the grudge, the image, the story of who hurt you — becomes the room you live the rest of your life inside. And the self you are finally willing to *bury* is the only self that gets resurrected into something wider.

That is *hijra* as a law of the soul, not just a chapter of history. Every real growth in your life — closer to Allah, freer of a sin, kinder, more honest — will require one severance you are afraid to make. There is always a door. There is always a thing the door asks you to leave behind. And there is always the quiet lie — *I was made weak, I have no choice* — waiting to excuse you from walking through it.

So here is tomorrow's practice, and it is small and exact. Identify **one tie** — one comfort, one grudge, one habit, one fear — that you know, in the honest chamber of your heart, is the bar of your cage *disguised as your shelter.* Do not vow to sever it forever. Make one *hijra-*sized motion away from it: one prayer reclaimed, one apology sent, one number deleted, one morning begun differently. Let the small shelter lose a little of its grip on your hand. Test whether the earth of Allah is as spacious as He swore it was.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Step back now and see what was built. The verse is a **trial in three exchanges**, and it is held together by three verb-forms that mirror three movements of a life:

- **تَوَفَّىٰهُمُ** — *the soul is collected in full* (Form V). The account closes. Nothing was missed.
- **مُسْتَضْعَفِينَ** — *"we were made weak"* (Form X). The defense: the cause of my failure lies outside me.
- **فَتُهَاجِرُوا** — *"that you might have severed and left"* (Form III). The rebuttal: there was a door, and the door cost only a parting you refused to pay.

And it is sealed by a single repeated word, **maʾwā — refuge** — appearing here as Hell and three verses later, for the emigrant, as *many* refuges. The whole passage turns on that one word: *everyone gets a shelter; you only choose which.*

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same truth seen from two sides.

From the side of *grammar*: the people reached for **Form X** — the form that puts the cause of weakness *outside* the self — and the angels answered with **Form III** — the form of the *severance they could have made and didn't.* The argument of the verse is the collision of two verb-forms: *"I was acted upon"* versus *"you could have acted."*

From the side of the *heart*: that is the exact collision inside every excuse you have ever made. **"It was done to me"** meeting **"there was a door."** And the deciding fact, the one the angels name, is never the size of your weakness. It is the size of the earth: **was it spacious? Was there a way out you would not take?**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the Form X sentence I say most often — *"I was made this way by ___"* — and is it the whole truth, or the comfortable half?
2. Where in my life am I a true *mustaḍʿaf* with no means and no way — and where am I standing in a wide earth, calling it a cage?
3. What shelter am I protecting that has quietly become my prison?
4. What single *hijra-*sized severance have I been postponing — and what, exactly, am I afraid to lose by making it?
5. If my account were collected *in full* tonight — *tawaffā* — which door would I most wish I had walked through?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the angels collect your soul in full, the defense *"I was made weak, I had no choice"* will be met not with an argument about your strength, but with one quiet fact — that the earth of Allah was always wider than the corner you chose to stay trapped in.

### The Closing Invitation

This is not a verse meant to crush you with guilt over every limitation you carry. The next verse — Allah's own mercy — protects the genuinely powerless by name. So receive this instead as the most freeing question a soul can be asked while it still has time to answer: *is there a door?* If, in honesty, there is none — then you are among those Allah Himself excuses, and you may rest. But if there is a door, however narrow, however much it asks you to sever — then the spaciousness of His earth is not a rebuke. **It is an invitation.** The way out was made wide on purpose. It was made wide *for you.*

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who take every soul in full — do not let my account close on an excuse I could have walked past. Where I am truly powerless, be my refuge; where I have been calling a door a wall, give me the courage of the severance and the eyes to see how wide You made Your earth. Make my *maʾwā* the refuge of those who left for You, and not the shelter of those who would not move. *Āmīn.*
