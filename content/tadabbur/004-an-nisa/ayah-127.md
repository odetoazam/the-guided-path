# The Advocate of Those Who Cannot Ask

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first. Hear it before we touch a single word of it.

> وَيَسْتَفْتُونَكَ فِى ٱلنِّسَآءِ ۖ قُلِ ٱللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ فِيهِنَّ
>
> *Wa yastaftūnaka fi n-nisāʾ — quli-llāhu yuftīkum fīhinn...*
>
> "They seek your ruling concerning women. Say: Allah gives you the ruling about them..."

And then it keeps going — much longer than you'd expect an answer to be:

> "...and so does what is recited to you in the Book concerning the orphan girls to whom you do not give what is ordained for them, while you desire to marry them; and concerning the oppressed among the children; and that you stand for orphans in justice. And whatever good you do — indeed, Allah is ever Knowing of it."

I want you to notice something before we even begin, just from that first reading.

**A question was asked. And the answer is much, much bigger than the question.**

Somebody came with a narrow concern — "tell us the ruling about the women." And the response does not stay where the question was. It widens. It pulls in orphan girls. It pulls in children too weak to speak. It pulls in a command to *stand in justice*. It ends on a verse about God's total knowledge of every good you do.

Hold that. We're going to come back to it, because it is the entire secret of this ayah.

### Let's rebuild the world

To feel this verse, you have to put yourself in a particular room.

It is Madinah. A community is being rebuilt, ayah by ayah, out of the wreckage of a pre-Islamic order — an order in which inheritance flowed to the strong: the men who rode out and fought. Women didn't inherit. Children didn't inherit. In some of the worst cases, women *were* the inheritance — a man would die and his widow would be passed down like property. An orphan girl in a household was not a responsibility to be protected. She was an asset to be managed.

Now into this world, Surah An-Nisa has been descending. And right from its opening verses it had done something seismic: it gave the orphan a right, it gave the woman a fixed, named, calculated share. *Written.* Not a favor. A decree.

But here is what tends to happen when you legislate against the interests of the powerful: people start looking for the gaps. They start asking very *specific* questions. "Okay — but what about *this* situation? What's the ruling here exactly?" Some questions are sincere. Some are searches for a loophole. The classical commentators — al-Qurṭubī among them — tell us this ayah came down in response to people of the community asking the Prophet ﷺ about the affairs of women and their inheritance. And among the narrations preserved, ʿĀʾisha (raḍiya Allāhu ʿanhā) — in a report Ibn Kathīr traces to Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī — describes a very particular abuse: an orphan girl in the guardianship of a man — and if she was beautiful and had wealth, he'd want to marry her himself, *but pay her less than her fair due*; and if she was plain, he'd refuse to marry her *and* refuse to let her marry anyone else — so her wealth would stay locked in his house. (Another tradition, reported from Ibn ʿAbbās, ties it to the daughters of Umm Kuḥḥa and their inheritance. The early authorities preserve more than one occasion here, and we should hold them all.)

And here is a subtlety the classical commentators turn over carefully. The verse says only *wa targhabūna an tankiḥūhunna* — and the verb *targhabūna* (root r-gh-b) has been left *without* the little preposition that would normally pin down its meaning. Followed by *fī*, the verb means *to desire* — you are keen to marry her. Followed by *ʿan*, it flips to the opposite — *to turn away*, to want nothing of the marriage. Al-Jalālayn reads it as aversion: the guardian who finds the girl plain, *refuses her* (*wa taʿḍulūhunna* — and you prevent them from marrying), and blocks her from any other husband *out of greed for her inheritance*. Al-Muyassar holds both senses open at once — "you desire to marry them, or you are averse to marrying them." So the bare verb is doing double duty: it indicts the guardian who covets the rich, beautiful ward *and* the one who imprisons the plain one to keep her wealth. ʿĀʾisha's two scenarios are not two unrelated stories. They are the two faces of one undecided verb.

So picture it. The strong have walked up to the Prophet ﷺ with their question. They want a ruling. They want it clarified.

### The Hook

And here is what should stop us in our tracks.

The people standing in that room and asking — they had a voice. They could walk up. They could *frame* the question.

But the verse, in its answer, keeps naming people who were *not* in that room. The orphan girl whose dowry is being shortchanged. The child too weak to advocate. People who could not ask a question if their lives depended on it — and their lives sometimes did.

So who is really being answered here?

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. Because I want to show you something in the very grammar of this verse — in two words built on the very same pattern — that reveals exactly who Allah leans toward when the powerful and the powerless are in the same room. And once you see it, you will not be able to un-see it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: They *seek* a ruling — and Allah *issues* it

Let me teach you something simple first, before we open the word.

In Arabic, verbs aren't just words — they're *shapes*. The same three-letter root gets poured into different molds, and each mold does a different job. Think of it like a single metal — the metal is the meaning — that you can cast into a hook, or a blade, or a key. Same metal. Different function.

There is one mold that means *to seek something, to ask for it, to try to get it out of someone.* Grammarians call it Form X — the *istafʿala* pattern. You can almost hear the "seeking" in it: that little *ista-* on the front is the sound of reaching out for something. *Istaghfara* — to seek forgiveness. *Istaʿāna* — to seek help.

Now look at the very first word of our verse:

> وَيَسْتَفْتُونَكَ — *wa yastaftūnaka*

The root is **f-t-y** — the root of the *fatwa*, the authoritative ruling. And it has been poured into that Form X "seeking" mold. *Yastaftūnaka* — **"they seek a ruling from you."** They are reaching toward the Prophet ﷺ, trying to draw a verdict out of him. The whole posture of the word is the posture of someone who has the standing to walk up and ask.

Now watch what happens in the reply. Same root — f-t-y — but recast:

> قُلِ ٱللَّهُ يُفْتِيكُمْ — *quli-llāhu yuftīkum*

*Yuftī* is a different mold — the one that *issues*, that *delivers* the verdict. So the two halves snap together perfectly: **they seek the ruling (Form X), and the ruling is issued (Form IV).** Reaching out — and the answer coming down.

But here is what's remarkable, and it's so easy to read straight past it.

They came and sought the ruling *from the Prophet* — *yastaftūna-ka*, that final *-ka* is "you," addressed to him ﷺ. And the answer does not say "I, the Prophet, rule for you." It says:

**"Say: *Allah* gives you the ruling about them."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the move that just happened. The question was aimed at a man — the most honored man who ever lived, but a man. And the answer lifts the eyes straight past him to **God Himself**. The Prophet ﷺ is commanded — *qul*, "say" — to step aside as it were, and announce: it is not me who issues this verdict. *Allah* is your Muftī here.

Do you understand what that does to the weight of everything that follows?

When a scholar gives you a ruling, you weigh it. When a judge rules, you can appeal. But when the verse declares that **Allah Himself is the one issuing the ruling on these women, these orphan girls, these children** — there is no higher court. The case of the powerless has just been moved to the highest possible bench, and the Judge is the One who created both the guardian and the orphan he is cheating.

And the verse does not stop at *Allāhu yuftīkum*. It immediately adds: *wa mā yutlā ʿalaykum fi l-kitāb* — "**and what is recited to you in the Book.**" The classical commentators — al-Jalālayn says it in so many words — identify this as the verses of inheritance already sent down earlier in this very surah (the *āyat al-mīrāth*, 4:11–12). Sit with what that does. The answer comes in two layers: Allah rules *now*, in this fresh fatwa — *and* the ruling was *already* revealed and recited to you. You are not waiting on a verdict that hasn't come. You have been quietly disregarding one that is already on the books. "What is recited to you" is not a footnote to the fatwa; it *is* part of the fatwa. The fixed share was named long before you asked.

The people thought they were asking about a technicality. They were told: *this matter has My personal attention.*

Let that be the first thing we carry. A single shift — from "they seek a ruling from *you*" to "*Allah* rules" — has told the orphan girl who could not even enter the room that her case is being heard by the Sovereign of all things.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: Two words, one shape — the seekers and the "deemed-weak"

Now I want to show you something that I find almost unbearably beautiful, and it only appears when you hold the verse's vocabulary side by side.

Remember that Form X mold — the *seeking* shape — *ista-*? The verse opened with it: *yasta-ftūnaka*, "they seek a ruling."

Travel down the verse a little further and you reach this word:

> وَٱلْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ — *wal-mustaḍʿafīn*

The root here is **ḍ-ʿ-f**, which simply means *weakness*. And it has been poured into — listen — the **same Form X mold**. *Musta-ḍʿaf.*

Now, Form X usually means "to seek." But it has a second, quieter function: *to deem something to be a certain way, to treat it as such, to count it as such.* So *mustaḍʿaf* does **not** simply mean "the weak one." It carries the sense of **"the one who is deemed weak — treated as weak — counted as nothing — by others."** Their weakness isn't just a fact about their bodies. It is a status *imposed on them* by the strong. They were *made* small.

Now put the two words next to each other, because the verse did:

- **يَسْتَفْتُونَكَ** — the ones with enough standing to *seek* a verdict.
- **ٱلْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ** — the ones the world has *deemed too small* to matter.

[PAUSE]

Same grammatical shape. Opposite ends of every human room. One group reaches out and asks. The other group has been pushed down and silenced. And the staggering thing is the direction of the verse's *care*. The seekers asked a narrow question about their own affairs. And Allah, in answering, reaches all the way down to the people on the *other* end of that grammatical mirror — the *deemed-weak*, the children no one would think to consult — and pulls them, unasked, into the ruling.

The ones who could ask, asked.
The One who answers brought up the ones who *couldn't.*

This is not decoration. This is the architecture of divine justice showing up in the choice of word-patterns. The verse literally contains, in two echoing words, the full social spectrum — and tilts toward the bottom of it.

And notice the phrase attached: *mina l-wildān* — **"among the children."** The plural of the very young. The most defenceless human being there is. The one who cannot ask, cannot fight, cannot even understand that they are being wronged. *That* is who God names without being asked.

Let this be the second thing we carry. In a verse triggered by the questions of the strong, the grammar itself bends toward the *mustaḍʿafīn* — those whom others have decided do not count.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: What was *written* — and the One who *knew*

There is a third thread, and it runs from the middle of the verse to its very last word, and once you see it, the whole ayah locks into place.

Look at the accusation buried in the middle:

> ٱلَّـٰتِى لَا تُؤْتُونَهُنَّ مَا كُتِبَ لَهُنَّ — *allātī lā tuʾtūnahunna mā kutiba lahunn*
>
> "...to whom **you do not give** what **was written** for them."

Two things to slow down on.

First — *lā tuʾtūnahunna*, "you do not give them." This is an absence. A withholding. The verse does not describe an act of cruelty as much as a *failure to hand over*. The wrong here is in what is **not** done. Their right just... stays in the guardian's hand. He simply doesn't pass it on. And the Quran, with terrible precision, calls that out as oppression. Not taking by force — merely *not giving* — is named here as the crime.

Second, and this is the heart of it — what is it they are not being given? *Mā kutiba lahunn* — **"what was written for them."** The root is **k-t-b**, the root of *kitāb*, the Book, of *writing*, of *decree*. And it's in the passive: *kutiba* — it **was written**. By whom? The passive leaves the Writer unnamed precisely because everyone knows who does the writing of decrees. **Allah wrote it.** Their share, their dowry, their right — it is not a social custom you may revise. It is *recorded.* It is on the ledger of the One who keeps the only ledger that finally matters.

So the guardian thinks the transaction is between him and a powerless girl who can't complain. But the verse has just informed him: **what you are withholding was written down by God.** You are not skimming from an orphan. You are intercepting something the Lord of the worlds entered into the record.

Now follow that thread to the last word of the entire verse:

> وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا۟ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ بِهِۦ عَلِيمًا
>
> "And whatever good you do — indeed, Allah is **ever Knowing** of it."

The root is **ʿ-l-m** — *knowledge.* *ʿAlīm* — the intensely, perfectly Knowing. And notice the verb before it: *kāna* — "was," "has ever been." Not just "Allah knows it now," but **Allah was, is, and has always been Knowing of it.** His knowledge is not a reaction to your deed. It precedes it, surrounds it, outlasts it. Al-Muyassar puts it plainly: *nothing of it, nor of anything else, is hidden from Him.*

Now see the bracket the verse has built.

It opens its accusation with a word from the root of **writing** — *kutiba*, what God recorded as their due.
It closes the whole matter with a word from the root of **knowing** — *ʿalīm*, the God who sees every good you do or fail to do.

[PAUSE]

**Their right was written. And the Witness is watching to see whether you hand it over.**

The guardian's entire scheme depended on the assumption that *no one is really watching* — that an orphan girl has no advocate, no auditor, no one to whom she can appeal. The verse demolishes that assumption from both ends. At one end: her right is *written*, in a Book she didn't draft and you can't edit. At the other end: the Writer is *ever-Knowing*, present at every moment you choose to give or to withhold.

There is nowhere in this transaction where God is absent.

Now — the bridge. We have watched three things in the language: a question lifted from a man to God Himself; a grammar that tilts toward the deemed-weak; and a right that is *written* and *witnessed* from both ends. Each of these is about words. **Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.**

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When you cannot speak for yourself, Allah speaks for you

Let me start where you actually live.

Have you ever been in a situation where decisions were being made *about* you, in a room you weren't allowed into?

Maybe it was a child in a custody battle, listening to the muffled voices of adults deciding their whole future on the other side of a door. Maybe it's the patient, sedated, while doctors and family debate over the bed. Maybe it was you — younger, smaller, at the mercy of someone bigger — and the grown-ups talked over your head as if you weren't a person yet. Maybe it's an employee whose fate is settled in a meeting they'll never see the minutes of.

There is a specific, cold kind of powerlessness in that. It's not the pain of being hurt. It's the pain of being *unrepresented*. Of having no voice at the table where your life is being decided. Of needing — desperately — for *someone* in that room to say, on your behalf, *"And what about them? What about what's owed to them?"*

[PAUSE]

Now go back to our verse.

The strong came to the Prophet ﷺ with their question — confident, articulate, able to frame the issue however suited them. They were *in the room*. And the orphan girl whose dowry they were quietly skimming? The small child whose inheritance was being held back? They were nowhere near that conversation. They didn't know it was happening. They couldn't have walked up if they'd tried.

And Allah — unasked — said: *And what about them?*

He named the orphan girls. He named the deemed-weak children. He did not wait for them to file a complaint. He did not require them to retain an advocate. **He became the advocate.** The voiceless got the most powerful voice in existence to speak for them, in a ruling they never requested, against people far stronger than they were.

Let this land on you in two directions.

First, if you are the one who has been pushed out of the room — if your rights are being decided by people who hold all the cards, and you feel utterly unrepresented — understand what this verse is telling you about your Lord. **You do not need to be able to ask.** The orphan girls in this verse never asked. The children never asked. Allah does not wait for the articulate, the powerful, the ones who know how to frame a question. He hears the case that no one filed. He speaks for the one who cannot speak. *Your inability to advocate for yourself is not the end of your case — it may be exactly the condition under which the Highest Court takes it up directly.*

And second — and this is the harder direction — *you are sometimes the one in the room.* You are sometimes the strong one, holding what is "written" for someone weaker, in a transaction where they cannot stop you. The verse is asking you: when you are in the room and they are not, are you the kind of person who says, unprompted, *"and what is owed to them?"* — or are you counting on the fact that they'll never know?

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge: **look for the person not in the room.** In every arrangement you're part of — at work, in the family, in money, in marriage — there is usually someone whose interests are being decided in their absence. The believer who has absorbed this verse trains themselves to be the one who raises their case before God has to. Be the voice for the one with no voice, *before* you are asked. Because you have seen Who does exactly that.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Justice toward those who can never make you pay for it

Here is a question that quietly measures the whole of a person's character.

*How do you treat the people who can never do anything back?*

Think about it honestly. We are all decent to the people who can reward us or punish us. We're polite to the boss, generous to the useful contact, careful with the friend who could embarrass us. That's not character. That's *bookkeeping*. That's behaving well because there's a watching eye and a possible consequence.

The real test is the person who can never repay you and never report you. The waiter you'll never see again. The junior who has no power over you. The elderly parent who has forgotten what you did yesterday. The child too young to remember. **The orphan with no father to come asking why his daughter was shortchanged.**

That last one is exactly the situation of our verse. Why was the orphan girl's dowry so easy to skim? Because *there was no father.* That's what "orphan" means here — al-Muyassar defines the *yatāmā* precisely: those whose fathers have died while they are still below the age of puberty. The protector is gone, and the ward is too young to fend for herself. There is no one to come knocking, no one to demand an accounting, no social consequence for the guardian who quietly keeps what was written for her. She is, in worldly terms, the safest person in the world to cheat.

And it is *precisely* into that situation that the verse drops its command:

> وَأَن تَقُومُوا۟ لِلْيَتَـٰمَىٰ بِٱلْقِسْطِ
>
> "...and that you **stand** for orphans **in justice**."

The word is *taqūmū* — from **q-w-m**, *to stand, to rise, to take up a matter and hold it upright.* It's not "be fair to orphans" in some passive sense. It's *stand up* for them. *Maintain* their cause. Take it onto your own shoulders and hold it level — *bil-qisṭ*, in the balance, in scrupulous fairness — for the very people who have no power to make you do it. Al-Muyassar reads it as the *obligation* to rise for the orphans in justice and to abandon every form of *jawr* — every tilt, every quiet wronging — in their rights.

[PAUSE]

And then comes the seal — the last words we examined:

> وَمَا تَفْعَلُوا۟ مِنْ خَيْرٍ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ بِهِۦ عَلِيمًا
>
> "And whatever good you do — indeed, Allah is ever Knowing of it."

Do you see why it ends here, and not on a threat?

A weaker verse would have ended: *and whatever wrong you do, Allah is watching.* That would have worked on fear. But that's not where it lands. It lands on: **whatever *good* you do — Allah is, and has always been, *Knowing* of it.**

Here is what that does. The orphan can't see your kindness. The child can't appreciate the fairness. The dead father can't thank you. The whole worldly economy of "be good and get credit for it" is *missing* in this transaction. There is no audience. There is no applause. There is no one to know.

Except.

**Allah was ever Knowing of it.**

The verse takes the one act that, in worldly terms, is utterly unwitnessed — kindness to a powerless orphan who can do nothing for you — and tells you it is recorded with perfect knowledge by the only Witness who finally matters. The thing you did when *no one was looking* is precisely the thing that is *most* fully seen. Your private justice toward the powerless is not lost in the dark. It is the most clearly known thing you do, because it is the truest.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the paradox. The strong man cheating the orphan believed his act was *unseen* — that was the whole premise of the crime. And the believer doing right by the orphan might *feel* her good is unseen — unthanked, unrewarded, invisible. The verse tells both of them the same thing from opposite directions: **there is no unseen here.** The withheld right was written; the quiet kindness is known. The God who recorded what was owed is the God who knows what was given.

What does this change about tomorrow? Find the person in your life who can never pay you back and never report you — and treat that relationship as your *real* report card. Give the full measure where you could easily give less. Be scrupulous in the dealing no one is auditing. And do it not for the thanks you won't get from them, but in the quiet certainty that the One who matters *was, is, and always has been* Knowing of it. That is what it means to live as though God's knowledge is more real to you than the world's applause.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and look at the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with astonishing care.

It **opens** with the powerful, the seekers — *yastaftūnaka*, those with the standing to ask.

It **lifts** the answer past the Prophet ﷺ to Allah Himself — *Allāhu yuftīkum* — the highest possible authority taking the case.

It then **descends**, deliberately, down the social ladder: from the women, to the orphan girls being cheated, to the *mustaḍʿafīn* — the deemed-weak — to the *wildān*, the small children. Lower and lower toward the most defenceless human being there is.

And at the bottom of that descent, it issues its command: *stand for orphans in justice.*

And then it **rises** back up to God — *fa-inna-llāha kāna bihī ʿalīman* — "Allah is ever Knowing."

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The verse begins with God as the Authority who rules, and ends with God as the Witness who knows. And in between, it travels all the way down to the weakest person in the room. **God's authority at the top, God's knowledge at the bottom — and the powerless held safe between the two.** The orphan girl is bracketed, above and below, by God Himself.

That is the architecture of mercy.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one single insight seen from two angles.

The grammar told us: the verse contains both the *seekers* and the *deemed-weak* — the same Form X mold, opposite ends of the room — and divine care tilts toward the bottom. The themes told us: Allah advocates for those who cannot speak, and watches over the justice no one can compel.

These are the same truth. **The reason the deemed-weak can be sure of justice is that their right is *written* by God and *known* by God** — the very thing the language showed us bracketing the verse from *kutiba* to *ʿalīm*. The voiceless have a voice because the Voiceless One's God writes their rights down and never stops watching to see them honored.

### Questions to carry

- Who, in my life right now, is having their interests decided in a room they're not allowed into — and have I been speaking for them, or counting on their silence?
- Where am I the strong one, holding what is "written" for someone weaker, in a deal they cannot force me to honor?
- How differently do I treat the people who can reward or report me, versus the people who never can — and what does that gap reveal about Who I'm really living for?
- Is there a kindness I've withheld because "no one would ever know"? Who told me no one would know?
- If Allah was *ever Knowing* of every good I've done in the dark — what is the truest thing on that record, and what is missing from it?

### One-sentence distillation

When the powerful bring their questions to Allah He answers them — and then, unasked, He takes up the case of those too weak to bring any question at all, because every right He wrote down, He also watches to see fulfilled.

### The closing invitation

You will be in a room this week where someone weaker than you is not present. You always are. The invitation of this verse is simple and quiet: be the one who says *"and what is owed to them?"* before anyone makes you — and give the full measure where no one is watching, because you have learned that the One who matters always is.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who took up the case of the orphan girl no one would defend — make us advocates for the voiceless and not exploiters of the silent. You wrote down their rights and You know our every deed; let us be among those whose record, in the dark where only You can see, is filled with the good we did for those who could never repay us. *Indeed, You are ever Knowing of it.*
