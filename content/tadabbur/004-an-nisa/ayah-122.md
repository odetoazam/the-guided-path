# The Promise That Can Actually Be Kept

> [ayah:4:122] وَٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ سَنُدْخِلُهُمْ جَنَّـٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ خَـٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا ۖ وَعْدَ ٱللَّهِ حَقًّا ۚ وَمَنْ أَصْدَقُ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ قِيلًا
>
> *Wa-lladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāti sa-nudkhiluhum jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā abadā. Waʿda llāhi ḥaqqā. Wa-man aṣdaqu mina llāhi qīlā.*
>
> "And those who believe and do righteous deeds — We will admit them into Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding therein forever. The promise of Allah, in truth. And who is more truthful than Allah in speech?"

---

## INTRODUCTION: Two Voices Making the Same Offer

I want you to begin somewhere very ordinary. Somewhere you have been a hundred times.

You are standing in front of a decision, and two voices are speaking to you. They are both, in a sense, making you the *same* offer. They are both saying: *trust me, and it will be good for you.* One voice is loud, immediate, dressed beautifully. It promises you something you can almost taste right now. The other voice is quieter, asks you to wait, and promises something you cannot yet see.

Every single day of your life is built out of moments exactly like this. The voice of the advertisement. The voice of the shortcut. The voice of the appetite. And underneath them all, the voice of your own deepest conscience asking you to hold out for something better.

Here is what you need to understand before we even open this ayah: **the Quran does not introduce this verse cold.** Just two ayahs earlier, in this same surah, Allah describes one of those two voices with chilling precision. He says of Shaytan:

> *"He promises them and arouses desire in them. But Satan does not promise them except delusion."* (4:120)

That word at the end — *ghurūr* — means a deception that works by appearance. A *ghurūr* is something that looks full and turns out to be empty. It is the beautiful storefront with nothing behind the glass. The voice in 4:120 *promises*. The Arabic literally uses the verb "to promise" — *yaʿiduhum*. Shaytan is, in the most precise grammatical sense, **a promiser.**

[PAUSE]

And then, two ayahs later, Allah makes His own promise. He uses related language. And He ends it not with the word *ghurūr* — delusion — but with its exact opposite: *ḥaqq* — truth. And then He does something stunning. He turns to you and asks a question.

Here is the hook. Here is what I want you hungry for. **The difference between these two voices is not in the offer. It is not even, on the surface, in the words. The difference is in one thing only — and the entire ayah is built to make you feel exactly what that one thing is.** Both voices say "I promise." Only one of them can keep it.

Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Letter That Carries the Future — سَنُدْخِلُهُمْ

Let me teach you something small before we use it.

In Arabic, as in English, you can talk about the future in more than one way. In English, "I will help you" and "I am going to help you" and "I help you" carry slightly different weights. The future is not one flat thing. It has texture. It has *nearness* and *farness*, *certainty* and *maybe*.

Arabic marks this with tiny prefixes attached to the front of a verb. There is *sawfa* — a fuller word that pushes the action out into a more distant, more drawn-out future. And there is its shortened cousin, a single letter: **سَـ**, *sa-*. This little *sīn* glued to the front of a verb says: *this is coming, and it is coming close, and it is sure.*

Now look at the verb in our ayah:

> **سَنُدْخِلُهُمْ** — *sa-nudkhiluhum* — "We will admit them."

Do you see the little *sīn* riding at the front? *Sa-.* The promise is not floated out into some vague, far-off someday. It is brought near. **It is coming, and it is close, and it is sure.**

Let's sit with this. Notice who is *speaking* and who is *acting*. The verb is *nudkhiluhum* — "**We** will admit **them**." Not "they will enter." **"We will admit them."**

This is from the root *dakhala*, "to enter." But the form here is not the simple "to enter." It is the causative form — *adkhala* — "to **cause** to enter," "to bring in," "to admit." Think of the difference between a guest walking through a door on his own, and a host taking the guest by the hand and walking him in. The first is *dakhala*. The second — the one Allah uses — is *adkhala*. **The host does the admitting.**

Stay with me, because this is where it opens. Go back to Shaytan in 4:120. Shaytan *promises*. But can Shaytan *deliver*? Can Shaytan take anyone by the hand and walk them into anything? Shaytan has no door to open. He has no Garden to give. His entire power, the Quran tells us elsewhere, is the power of *suggestion* — he whispers, he calls, and then, on the Day it all comes due, he says: *"I had no authority over you except that I called you, and you responded to me"* (14:22).

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. **Shaytan's verb is "promise." Allah's verb is "admit."** One is a mouth. The other is a hand on the door. Shaytan can describe the destination in glowing colors, but he stands outside the building with no key. Allah does not merely describe the Garden — **He says: We will bring you in.** The act belongs entirely to Him.

And notice one more thing, a beautiful gap. Look at what the believers *did*: "those who believe and do righteous deeds." Al-Ṭabarī unpacks both halves with a craftsman's care: to *believe* here is to have affirmed Allah and His Messenger and borne witness to His oneness and to the Prophet's ﷺ prophethood; and to *do righteous deeds* is, concretely, to have discharged the *farāʾiḍ* — the obligations Allah laid upon them. So the believers' part, though real and active, is human-sized and bounded: a witness held in the heart and a set of duties carried out with the hands. But the entering? **They do not enter themselves.** The grammar refuses to let them. It is not "they will enter the Gardens." It is "**We** will admit them." Even after a lifetime of righteous deeds, the final passage through the gate is not something you achieve. It is something you are *granted*. Al-Muyassar captures this exactly when it glosses the entering as *bi-faḍlihi* — "**by His grace**." You walk a lifetime toward the door. But the door is opened *for* you, *by* Him.

What does this single letter, this little *sīn*, reveal? **The believer leans forward into a future that someone with real power has guaranteed.** That is the whole posture of faith — living tilted toward a promise that is near, sure, and held in the hand of the One who alone can open the door.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Grammar of an Unbreakable Vow — وَعْدَ ٱللَّهِ حَقًّا

Now I need to teach you one of the most beautiful structures in the Arabic language, and I promise you it will change how you hear this verse.

In ordinary speech, we say a verb and move on: "He ran." But sometimes you want to *intensify* the action, to nail it down, to say "he ran — and I mean **really** ran." Arabic has a dedicated tool for this. You take the action and you repeat it as a noun right beside the verb, in a particular grammatical case. Grammarians call it the *mafʿūl muṭlaq* — the "absolute object" or cognate accusative.

Here is an everyday feel for it. Imagine someone doesn't just *thank* you — they say "I thank you **a thanking**." It sounds odd in English, but in Arabic it is the most natural way to say: *this is no casual thanks; this is the real, full, weighty thing.* The repeated noun slams the door shut on any doubt. **It removes the possibility that you meant it loosely.**

Now listen to the seam of our ayah:

> …خَـٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا ۖ **وَعْدَ ٱللَّهِ حَقًّا**
> "…abiding therein forever. **The promise of Allah, in truth.**"

After Allah describes the Garden, the eternity, the rivers — He stops the picture and stamps it. *Waʿda llāhi* — "the promise of Allah." *Ḥaqqā* — "in truth, truly, as a settled reality."

This is the *mafʿūl muṭlaq* at work — twice over. Al-Jalālayn, reading this very phrase, expands it for us: *waʿadahum dhālika wa-ḥaqqahu ḥaqqā* — "**He promised them that, and He made it true, a making-true.**" Do you hear the doubling? *Ḥaqqahu ḥaqqā* — He truthed it, a truthing. The grammar is not decorative. It is **a notarized seal.** It is Allah saying: *this is a promise — and I mean it as the most binding kind of promise there is.* Al-Muyassar reads the same seal and renders it as plainly as it can be said: this is *a promise from Allah — the One who does not break His promise* (*lā yukhlifu waʿdah*). The seal is not only that the promise is true; it is that the Promiser is constitutionally incapable of failing it.

Let's go deeper. Why does the ayah need this seal at all? Hasn't He just described paradise in luminous detail — gardens, rivers, forever? Why not simply let the beautiful image stand?

Here is the answer, and it sends us right back to Shaytan. **Because there was another promiser in the room.** And as Ibn Kathīr lays out the surrounding passage, that promiser had just finished *making his vows in the first person.* A few ayahs earlier (4:118–119), Shaytan swears his own oaths over the human race: *"I will surely take an appointed portion of Your servants, and I will mislead them, and I will arouse desires in them, and I will command them…"* — a whole speech of satanic guarantees. So when 4:120 then summarizes that *"Satan does not promise them except delusion,"* it is delivering the verdict on a speech of promises we just heard with our own ears. And the Quran sealed *that* promise too, with the very same kind of accusative emphasis: *wa mā yaʿiduhumu sh-shayṭānu illā **ghurūrā*** — "Satan does not promise them except **a deluding**" (4:120). Same grammatical architecture. A promise, stamped with a final accusative noun.

[PAUSE]

Now put them side by side and feel the chill of it:

- Shaytan's promise, sealed with: **غُرُورًا** — *ghurūrā* — "a deluding, an empty appearance."
- Allah's promise, sealed with: **حَقًّا** — *ḥaqqā* — "a truth, a settled reality."

**The structure is identical. The seal is the opposite.** Two promisers. Two final stamps. One stamp reads "this looks full but is empty." The other reads "this is the bedrock of what is real." The word *ḥaqq* does not just mean "true" as in "factually correct." It means *what is real, what has weight, what is owed and will be paid, what stands when everything else dissolves.* It is one of the names of Allah Himself — *al-Ḥaqq*, The Real.

So when the grammar repeats the seal, it is not Allah being verbose. It is Allah deliberately matching Shaytan's sentence — and overturning it. **Where the deceiver placed "delusion," the Real places "truth."** The believer is being handed a side-by-side comparison and asked: *you have heard two voices use the grammar of promising. Now look at what each promise is made of.*

What does this construction reveal? **A promise is only ever as good as the one who makes it — and the grammar is built to drag that question into the open.** Anyone can use the *words* of a promise. The accusative seal asks: but what is it made of? Air, or bedrock?

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Question With Only One Answer — وَمَنْ أَصْدَقُ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ قِيلًا

And now the ayah does the most daring thing of all. It turns around and asks *you* a question.

Let me first teach you what kind of question this is. In Arabic, as in every language, there are questions that seek information — "where is the masjid?" — and there are questions that are not really questions at all. They are statements wearing the clothing of a question, designed to make *you* arrive at the answer so that you own it. We call this the rhetorical interrogative — *al-istifhām al-inkārī*. When a parent asks a child, "and who left the door wide open?", they are not gathering data. They are making the child say the answer to themselves.

Here is the ayah's question:

> **وَمَنْ أَصْدَقُ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ قِيلًا**
> *Wa-man aṣdaqu mina llāhi qīlā.*
> "And **who** is more truthful than Allah in speech?"

The word *man* — "who" — opens it. And *aṣdaqu* is a comparative form, from the root *ṣ-d-q*, truthfulness — the same root as *ṣiddīq*, the utterly truthful one, the title given to Abū Bakr. *Aṣdaqu* means "more truthful," "truer in speech." And *qīlā* at the very end — "in speech, in saying" — is what grammarians call a *tamyīz*, a word of specification: it narrows the comparison to one precise arena. Al-Qurṭubī notes this precisely — *qīlā* comes "by way of clarification" (*ʿalā l-bayān*), and the meaning is simply: *there is no one truer than Allah.* More truthful **in what?** In *speech.* In the very act of saying.

Now feel the question land. *Who is more truthful than Allah in speech?* You are invited to scan the entire universe for a competitor. Search every speaker who has ever spoken. Search every authority, every expert, every promiser, every voice that has ever made you a guarantee. And the answer that rises in you — the answer the grammar *forces* you to say with your own inner voice — is: **no one. Not one. There is none.**

[PAUSE]

But stay with me, because there is something even deeper than "no one is more truthful."

Look at the precise word: *qīlā* — *speech, saying.* The ayah does not say "who is more truthful than Allah in *deeds*?" or "who keeps promises better?" It zeroes in on the *act of speaking itself*. And here is why that matters so much in this exact location. The entire passage, from 4:120, has been about two voices *speaking* to the human being. Shaytan **says** things. He whispers, he promises, he arouses desire — it is all *speech*. The disbeliever's whole tragedy is that he *listened* to a voice and *believed* a saying that turned out to be *ghurūr*.

So the ayah closes the loop with surgical precision. It does not ask "who acts better than Allah?" It asks **"who *speaks* truer than Allah?"** — because the whole drama was a drama of *whose speech you trusted.* You stood between two sayings. One saying was sealed *ghurūrā*. The other was sealed *ḥaqqā*. And now the ayah asks you, in the very category that the contest was fought in — **speech** — *whose word were you ever going to be safer betting your eternity on?*

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer, and it is the quietest and the heaviest. With every human promise, there is a gap between the *word* and the *reality*. A person can be utterly sincere and still fail to deliver — they meant it, but they got sick, they ran out of money, they died, the world changed. Their truthfulness in *speech* and their power in *deed* are two different things, and the gap between them is where every broken human promise lives.

But for Allah, **there is no gap.** His *qīl* — His speech — and the reality are the same thing. This is why al-Muyassar, glossing the closing question, refuses to leave it at *speech* alone: there is no one more truthful than Allah, it says, *in His statement and in His promise* (*fī qawlihi wa-waʿdih*) — fusing the two the way no human voice can, because for Him the saying and the keeping are one act. When He says *kun* — "be" — it *is*. His word does not point at a reality it hopes to reach; His word *is* the reality arriving. So to ask "who is more truthful than Allah in speech?" is to ask: *whose word has zero distance between the saying and the thing?* And the answer is: only His. Every other voice in existence — including the one in 4:120 — speaks across a gap. His speech alone has none.

What does this question reveal? **You have already been choosing whose word to live by, every single day. The ayah simply makes you say the answer out loud.**

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality Itself

Look at what these three pieces have shown us. The little *sīn* on *sa-nudkhiluhum* gave us a promiser with a hand on the door, not just words in the air. The doubled seal of *waʿda llāhi ḥaqqā* gave us a vow made of bedrock, deliberately set against a vow made of empty appearance. And the rhetorical question, *who is more truthful in speech*, made us scan all of existence and come back empty — there is no truer voice.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how you are actually living, right now, today.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Economy of Promises You Already Live In

Let me start where you actually live.

Your entire life runs on promises. Stop and notice how true this is. You swipe a card, trusting a promise that the bank will cover it. You take a job, trusting a promise of a paycheck. You board a plane, trusting a hundred strangers' promises that the bolts are tight and the fuel is real. You scroll past a thousand advertisements a day, each one a small promise: *buy this, and you will feel whole. Take this, and the emptiness will close.* You are, whether you notice it or not, a full-time evaluator of promises. You are constantly deciding: *whose word do I trust enough to act on?*

And here is the uncomfortable truth modern life has taught us so well: **most promises are partly* ghurūr.*** Most are at least a little bit storefront. The advertisement promises a feeling it cannot deliver. The shortcut promises a result it cannot sustain. The notification promises a connection it cannot provide. We have become, all of us, slightly numb — we half-expect every promise to be inflated, because so many of them are. We have learned to read the fine print, to assume there is a catch, to wait for the disappointment.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the ayah into this exhausted, promise-weary world you live in.

The Quran is not naïve about this. It is the *opposite* of naïve. Two ayahs before this one, it names the disease exactly: there is a promiser whose entire product is *ghurūr* — beautiful appearance over empty interior. The Quran agrees with your weariness. It says: *yes, you are right to be suspicious. Most of the voices calling to you are selling delusion. Shaytan is, quite literally, in the promise business, and his only inventory is the empty box.*

But then it does what nothing in your daily economy of promises ever does. **It introduces a promiser with no gap between the word and the goods.** Not a better advertiser. Not a more sincere salesman. A promiser whose speech *is* reality — *waʿda llāhi ḥaqqā* — and who asks you, point-blank, to compare His track record of truthfulness in speech against every other voice you have ever trusted.

And here is the movement I want you to feel. You have spent your whole life learning to discount promises — to assume the storefront is empty. That habit is wise in the dunya. But the ayah asks you to notice that you have let that wise habit metastasize into something that is now *robbing* you. **You discount Allah's promise with the same reflex you use to discount the advertisement.** You hear "Gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding forever," and some tired, over-marketed part of you files it under *too good to be true* — the same folder where you put every inflated claim. The ayah is reaching into that folder and pulling its promise out. *No*, it says. *That reflex is for the deluder. Do not aim it at the Real.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the specific, practical application. The next time you feel the pull of a *ghurūr* — and you will, probably within hours — the cheap promise of the screen, the appetite, the shortcut — I want you to do one concrete thing. **Name the seal on it.** Ask: *what is the final stamp on this promise — ḥaqq or ghurūr? Is there a real thing behind this storefront, or only the glass?* You already know how to do this. You do it with cars and contracts and reviews every day. The ayah is simply asking you to bring that same hard-nosed scrutiny to the spiritual transactions you currently make on autopilot — and to notice that, of all the promisers competing for your trust, exactly one has never once been *ghurūr.*

### Theme Two: Living Forward Into a Word You Can Stand On

Now let me take you somewhere more intimate. Not the marketplace of promises out there, but the quiet ground of your own heart.

There is a specific kind of human suffering that comes from having nothing solid to stand on about the future. The psychologists have a word for the opposite of it — they talk about a "secure base," the felt sense that something reliable is holding you, so that you can venture out into the world without terror. A child with a secure base explores boldly, because they *know* they can return to safety. A child without one clings, or freezes, or recklessly runs — because the ground beneath them is uncertain, and uncertainty about what holds you is one of the most corrosive feelings a human being can carry.

Most of us, if we are honest, carry a low-grade version of this insecurity about the largest question of all: *what is actually waiting for me?* We don't usually say it out loud. But it leaks out — in the anxiety that spikes at 3 a.m., in the frantic accumulation, in the inability to be still, in the way we numb ourselves rather than sit quietly with the open question of our own future. We are, many of us, future-insecure at the deepest level.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what that little *sīn* on *sa-nudkhiluhum* is doing for someone who carries that insecurity.

"**We will admit them.**" Near. Sure. Held in the hand of the One who opens the door. The ayah is not offering you a *philosophy* about the future. It is offering you a *secure base* for it. It is saying: *for the one who believes and does righteous deeds, the largest question — what is waiting for me — has an answer, and the answer is leaning toward you, close, and it is held by the only hand strong enough to open that particular door.*

And watch how the ayah builds the security in layers, each one removing a different fear:

First it removes the fear that it is *far*: the *sīn* makes it near.
Then it removes the fear that you must *earn the entry yourself*: "**We** will admit them" — the door is opened *for* you.
Then it removes the fear that it might *end*: *khālidīna fīhā abadā* — abiding therein **forever**. Not a long time. Not until something changes. *Abadā* — endlessly. The one thing that poisons every earthly joy is that it ends; you cannot fully rest in any happiness here because part of you knows it is temporary. This security has that poison removed.
Then it removes the fear that the *promiser might fail*: *waʿda llāhi ḥaqqā* — the promise is sealed with bedrock.
And finally it removes the last fear, the deepest one — *but can I trust the one who is telling me all this?* — with the question: *who is more truthful in speech than Allah?*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the ayah has done? **It has gone, fear by fear, and dismantled every reason your insecure heart had to refuse to rest.** It is the most thorough secure base ever offered. *It is near. You will be carried in. It never ends. The one promising has the power. And the one promising has never spoken a false word.*

Here is the paradox I want to leave sitting, unresolved, because it is meant to be lived rather than solved. The believer is asked to build their deepest security on something they **cannot yet see.** The rivers, the gardens — none of it is in front of you. By every standard of the dunya, that is the *least* secure thing to stand on: an unseen future. And yet the ayah insists it is the *most* secure thing of all — more solid than the bank, the job, the contract — precisely because of who is holding it. **The most reliable ground in your life is the one thing you cannot currently touch.** Faith is the willingness to shift your weight onto it anyway.

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical application, and it is quiet. The next time the future-fear rises — the 3 a.m. spike, the restless accumulation, the dread underneath the busyness — I want you to answer it with the structure of this ayah, not with a distraction. Don't numb the fear. **Place your weight on the promise instead.** Say it back to the fear in its own order: *it is near. I will be carried in. It does not end. He has the power. And He has never once lied.* You are not pretending the future is certain by your own calculation. You are leaning your weight onto the word of the only Speaker who has no gap between His saying and the real.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Sentence Built as a Mirror

Now that we have done the work, let me show you how the whole passage is built — because the structure is the proof.

This ayah is not a standalone verse. It is the second half of a deliberate diptych — a mirror. Set 4:120 and 4:122 side by side and watch the architecture appear:

**4:120 — The Deceiver's Promise**
- A promiser: Shaytan — *yaʿiduhum* ("he promises them")
- The content: desire aroused, appearance, storefront
- The seal: **ghurūrā** — "a deluding" (cognate accusative)
- The capacity behind it: *none* — only a voice, only suggestion

**4:122 — The Real Promise**
- A promiser: Allah — *waʿda llāhi* ("the promise of Allah")
- The content: Gardens whose rivers run beneath the palaces and the trees (al-Muyassar fills the picture in), eternity
- The seal: **ḥaqqā** — "a truth" (cognate accusative)
- The capacity behind it: *total* — "**We** will admit them," the hand on the door
- And then, the thing 4:120 cannot answer: **who is more truthful in speech?**

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The two ayahs are grammatically *rhymed*. Same verb of promising. Same final accusative seal. They are built to be held up against each other like two coins — and the moment you do, the entire point lands without anyone having to say it: **both voices use the word "promise." Only one of them is backed by the power to deliver and the truthfulness to mean it.** The architecture *is* the argument.

### Bringing It Together

Notice now that the linguistic discoveries and the lived themes were the *same insight* approached from two directions.

The little *sīn* and the hand-on-the-door verb were the grammar of a *promiser who can deliver* — and that became the lived reality of an economy of promises in which exactly one is not *ghurūr.* The doubled accusative seal, *waʿda llāhi ḥaqqā*, was the grammar of a *vow made of bedrock* — and that became the secure base your future-insecure heart has been searching for. And the rhetorical question, *who is more truthful in speech*, was the grammar of *a contest with only one possible winner* — and that became the daily, practical act of asking, of every promise that calls to you: *what is the seal on this — ḥaqq, or ghurūr?*

It is one truth, seen from two sides: **You are always trusting someone's word about your future. The only question is whose.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I caught myself filing Allah's promise in the same drawer as an advertisement — discounting it with the reflex I built for the deceiver?
2. What is one *ghurūr* — one empty-storefront promise — that I keep returning to, even though I already know there is nothing behind the glass?
3. The ayah dismantles my future-fear fear by fear. Which of those fears is the one that actually grips me — that it is far, that I must earn it, that it ends, that He lacks power, or that He might not be telling the truth?
4. If I truly lived as though *waʿda llāhi ḥaqqā* — that this promise is bedrock — what is the one thing I would stop doing tomorrow?
5. Whose word am I actually betting my eternity on — and have I ever consciously chosen, or have I just been listening to whichever voice was loudest?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Two voices offer you the same word — *I promise* — but only One has the power to open the door, the truthfulness to mean it, and no gap between His saying and the real; the ayah simply makes you say His name out loud.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what I would invite to shift — not as an obligation, but as a relief you have been needing. You have spent so much energy being wisely suspicious of promises. Keep that wisdom. But turn it the right way. Aim it at the *ghurūr* — and let it finally *rest* when it reaches the One whose speech has never, not once, been an empty storefront. You are allowed to stop bracing for disappointment from the only Speaker who has none to give. Let your weight down. The door is His, and He has said He will open it.

### Du'ā

*Yā Allāh, You whose word is the very bedrock of what is real — we have spent our lives trusting voices that promised and could not deliver. Pull our trust out of the drawer where we filed it next to every delusion, and let it rest on Your promise alone. You who are aṣdaq — truer in speech than any speaker in all Your creation — admit us, by Your grace and not by our deeds, into what You have promised, and let us live every remaining day leaning forward into Your word. Āmīn.*
