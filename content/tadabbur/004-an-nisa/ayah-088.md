# Two Parties Over the Two-Faced
### Surah An-Nisa, Ayah 88

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **فَمَا لَكُمْ فِى ٱلْمُنَـٰفِقِينَ فِئَتَيْنِ وَٱللَّهُ أَرْكَسَهُم بِمَا كَسَبُوٓاْ ۚ أَتُرِيدُونَ أَن تَهْدُواْ مَنْ أَضَلَّ ٱللَّهُ ۖ وَمَن يُضْلِلِ ٱللَّهُ فَلَن تَجِدَ لَهُۥ سَبِيلًا**

Transliterated, slowly:

> *Famā lakum fil-munāfiqīna fiʾatayn, wallāhu arkasahum bimā kasabū. A-turīdūna an tahdū man aḍalla-llāh? Wa man yuḍlili-llāhu falan tajida lahu sabīlā.*

And in plain English:

> *So what is the matter with you that you have become two parties concerning the hypocrites, while Allah has cast them back for what they earned? Do you wish to guide the one Allah has let go astray? And whoever Allah lets go astray — never will you find for him a way.*

Now let me rebuild the world this landed in.

The dust has barely settled on Uhud. This is the battle that began as a victory and turned, in an afternoon, into a wound the community would carry for years. Seventy of the best are dead. The Prophet ﷺ himself is bleeding. And woven through the trauma is a particular kind of betrayal: a body of men who marched out with the army and then, at the decisive moment, **turned around and went home.** Not killed. Not captured. Just — gone. Back in Madinah, walking the same streets, praying in the same rows, claiming the same faith.

And the believers cannot agree on what to do with them.

One group says: these are hypocrites, they abandoned us in the field, deal with them as enemies. Another group says: no — they say the *shahada*, they pray with us, they are still our brothers. Two camps. Hardening. Each certain. Arguing about men who are, by their very nature, **impossible to read.**

This is the moment Allah speaks into. And here is the first strange thing — the thing that should make you sit up.

He does not begin by talking about the hypocrites at all. He begins by turning to the *believers* and asking, almost the way a parent stops two arguing children: **"So what is the matter with *you*?"**

The argument was about *them*. The question is about *you*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that inversion. Because by the end of this ayah, Allah will have taken something out of your hands that you have probably been gripping your whole life without realizing it — and the loss of it, strangely, is going to feel like relief.

Let's earn that slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Fiʾatayn* — The Dual That Mirrors the Disease

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about Arabic that English simply does not have.

English has singular and plural. One book, many books. That's it. Arabic has a third category that sits *between* them: the **dual** — a special form, with its own ending, that means *exactly two.* Not "some." Not "a few." Two. The language has a dedicated grammatical shape reserved for twoness.

So when Arabic wants to say "you split into groups," it has options. It could use a plural — *fiʾāt*, factions, an open-ended scatter of opinion. But Allah does not say that.

He says:

> **فِئَتَيْنِ** — *fiʾatayn*

The dual. **Two** parties. Precisely two.

Now, why does that matter? On the surface it's just reporting a fact: there happened to be two camps. But notice *what they had split over.* They had split over the **munāfiqūn** — and the word *munāfiq*, the hypocrite, names a person who is himself **divided.** Two-faced. One face for the believers, one face for the disbelievers. A heart that says one thing and a tongue that says another. The hypocrite is, at his core, a *being-in-two.*

Let's sit with this.

The hypocrites carried a split *inside* themselves. And what did that internal split do once it entered the community? **It reproduced itself.** The believers, trying to figure out how to handle these divided men, became *divided men themselves.* Two parties. *Fiʾatayn.* The disease in the munāfiqūn's hearts — the twoness — leapt the gap and became twoness in the believers' ranks.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. This is one of the quietest things hypocrisy does, and one of the most dangerous. The hypocrite rarely defeats a community head-on. He doesn't have the strength. What he does instead is **introduce ambiguity** — and then he lets the community tear itself apart deciding what the ambiguity means. He doesn't have to fight you. He just has to be unreadable enough that *you start fighting each other about him.*

The dual is not decoration here. It is a diagnosis. Allah chose the one grammatical form in the language that means *exactly two* — to hold a mirror up to the believers and let them see: *the very shape of your division is the shape of their sickness. You have caught it.*

And then comes the framing of the whole question: **فَمَا لَكُمْ** — *famā lakum* — "what is the matter *with you*?" In Arabic this construction is the language of *diagnosis*, the phrase you use when something is *wrong* with someone. *Mā laka?* — what's wrong with you, what's gotten into you? The grammatical gaze that the believers had aimed at the hypocrites — *what's wrong with them?* — Allah gently rotates a hundred and eighty degrees and aims back: **what's wrong with you, that you let this happen to your unity?** al-Tabari and al-Jalalayn both gloss it just this plainly — *mā shaʾnukum*, "what is your affair, that you have *become* two factions over them?" — the verb placing the becoming squarely on the believers.

What this single word reveals: *the wound you are arguing about may already be inside the room.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Arkasahum* — The Verb That Sounds Like a Fall

Now I want to show you a word that even fluent Arabic speakers find heavy in the mouth.

> **أَرْكَسَهُمْ** — *arkasahum* — "He cast them back."

First, let me teach the concept underneath it.

The root here is **r-k-s** (ر-ك-س). In the classical lexicons, *raksa* means to overturn a thing, to flip it so its top becomes its bottom — and specifically, to *return something to its origin head-first*, to plunge it back down to where it began. Imagine setting something upright with great effort, and then it topples back to the exact heap it came from. That toppling-back, that head-over-heels reversion to the starting point — that is *raksa*.

And this is **Form IV** — *arkasa* — the form that takes a verb and makes it *transitive and causative*: not "they fell back" but *"He cast them back," "He flung them back down."* An agent reaches in and does the overturning. Here the agent is named explicitly: **wallāhu arkasahum** — *and Allah* cast them back.

Now — say the word out loud. *Ar-ka-sa.* Feel it.

The throat-catch at the start. The hard *kāf* striking against the *sīn.* It is a word with no softness in it. It does not flow; it *clatters.* It sounds like something tumbling down a staircase. And this is the second thing I want you to notice, the thing the methodology of the Qur'an does again and again: **the sound of the word enacts the meaning of the word.** A smooth reality gets a smooth word. A violent overturning gets a word that overturns in your mouth.

Here's what's remarkable when you hold this against the rest of the ayah.

The believers were arguing about what *they* should do to the hypocrites. Kill them? Spare them? And Allah's answer cuts underneath the entire debate: while you two parties stand here deliberating their fate — **Allah has already acted.** *Wallāhu arkasahum.* The decisive verb has already been spoken, in the perfect tense, *done.* He has already flipped them back to where they started. Your deliberation is happening on top of a reality that is already settled.

And the classical commentators tell us precisely *what* that settled reality was. al-Tabari and al-Jalalayn both unpack *arkasahum* with one plain word — *raddahum*, "He returned them" — and then spell out the return: Allah sent these men back to the *rulings* that govern those who rejected faith outright. al-Tabari is concrete to the point of bluntness: He returned them to the legal status of the people of shirk — their blood, and the capture of their offspring, made lawful again. Read that against the believers' argument and the floor drops out of it. The two parties were debating whether to treat these men as enemies or as brothers — and the verdict on exactly that question had *already been issued.* The thin covering of belief they had worn into Madinah was stripped, and they stood again, in the sight of the Law, where they had truly always stood. al-Tabari even reaches back for a line of old verse — Umayya ibn Abī al-Ṣalt using this very root to describe the damned *plunged back* into the Fire — proof that the Arab ear heard in *r-k-s* exactly this: a sending-back to one's origin.

And settled *justly* — because of the next two words: **بِمَا كَسَبُوا** — *bimā kasabū* — "for what they earned."

The root *k-s-b*, to earn, to acquire by one's own action. This is the Qur'an's vocabulary for things you *do to yourself.* He did not arbitrarily overturn them. He overturned them **by the weight of what their own hands gathered** — and al-Jalalayn names that earning exactly: *min al-kufr wa-l-maʿāṣī*, the disbelief and the disobedience they had been quietly accumulating beneath the surface. The reversal was not imposed on them from outside; it was the natural fall of a thing they had been building toward all along. They climbed up the appearance of faith, and the substance they had actually *earned* underneath dragged them back down to the bottom they came from. *Arkasahum bimā kasabū.* He let them fall — by means of their own earnings.

[PAUSE]

What this word reveals: *while human beings are still convening committees about a person, the real verdict has often already fallen — quietly, justly, by the person's own hand.*

### Element Three: The Address That Narrows, and the Way That Is Withheld

Watch the camera move in this ayah. This is the most delicate thing in it, and you can miss it entirely in translation.

It opens in the **plural**: *famā **lakum*** — "what is the matter with *you* (all)." *A-turīdūna an tahdū* — "do *you* (all) wish to guide." Allah is addressing the whole community, both factions, the entire arguing room.

And then, at the very end, the address **narrows to a single person**:

> **فَلَن تَجِدَ لَهُۥ سَبِيلًا** — *falan **tajida** lahu sabīlā* — "never will *you* (one person, singular) find for him a way."

This shift — Arabic rhetoricians call it *iltifāt*, the "turning" — is deliberate. Allah moves from addressing the crowd to addressing *one heart at a time.* Because a truth this heavy cannot be received by a committee. A crowd can nod at a general principle and walk away unchanged. But the closing verdict of this ayah is something *you*, singular, *you* alone, have to absorb in the privacy of your own chest. So He turns from the many to the one. From *all of you* to **you.**

And what is it He says to that single, isolated *you*?

He gives you a search, and then He empties it. **تَجِدَ** — *tajida*, from the root *w-j-d*, "to find" — is the verb of seeking and locating, the verb you use when you go looking for something expecting it to be there. And He attaches it to **سَبِيلًا** — *sabīlan*, "a way," "a path," "a route through."

But notice the absence. *Sabīlan* arrives **indefinite** — *a* way, *any* way — and it arrives drowned under the heaviest negation Arabic owns: **فَلَن** — *falan.* The particle *lan* negates the future with a kind of finality; it doesn't just say "you won't," it says "there is no future in which you will." *Falan tajida lahu sabīlā* — there is no road, anywhere, ever, that your searching will turn up, by which you could walk this person out of where Allah has placed him.

Read the absence carefully. Everywhere else in this surah and across the Qur'an, *sabīl* and its sister *ṣirāṭ* are things Allah *gives* — *the way*, *the straight path*, *the road of those He has favored.* The believer is forever being shown a *sabīl.* Here, for the one Allah has left astray, the *sabīl* is the thing that is **not there.** The word that usually marks divine generosity appears precisely as a void. And the void is not in the *path's* existence — it is in *your finding of it.* "You will not *find.*" The road out of someone else's misguidance is simply not an object available to your hands.

[PAUSE]

What this construction reveals: *Allah turns to you alone, hands you the verb of searching, and then — gently, finally — tells you the search has no object. Not so you despair. So you can finally put it down.*

---

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have built.

*Fiʾatayn* showed us a community catching the disease of the divided. *Arkasahum* showed us that the real verdict had already fallen, justly, by the person's own hand, before anyone finished arguing. And the narrowing address with its withheld *sabīl* turned, at the end, to each of us alone and quietly removed something from our grip.

Now let's ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about how we actually live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The People We Cannot Read — and the Wars We Fight Over Them

Let me start where you already live.

There is a person in almost everyone's life who is *hard to read.* The colleague who is warm to your face and you're never quite sure what they say when you leave the room. The family member who is in and out, devoted one month and gone the next. The friend who half-shows-up. The relative whose faith, or loyalty, or sincerity you genuinely cannot calibrate. They are not clearly an enemy. They are not clearly with you. They live in the maddening middle.

And here is what I want you to notice about those people: **how much conflict they generate among the people who care about them.**

Think about it honestly. The clearest fights you've had with people you *love* — siblings, spouses, close friends — how many of them were not about each other at all, but about *how to handle a third person nobody could read?* "We have to confront them." "No, give them space." "You're too soft." "You're too harsh." Two parties. Hardening. Each certain. *Fiʾatayn.*

This is exactly the scene of the ayah. The believers loved each other. They had just bled together at Uhud. And here they were, fracturing — not over an enemy in the field, but over how to categorize men in the middle. And the early commentators preserve the exact axis of that fracture: al-Muyassar and al-Jalalayn both record that one faction said *fight them, kill them* and the other said *do not* — the split was not abstract, it was over whether to draw the sword. The ambiguous person had become a wedge driven straight through the people who were supposed to be one.

Now let the ayah illuminate it. Allah's first move is not to resolve the ambiguity *for* them. He doesn't say "here is the correct policy on hypocrites." His first move is to **name what the argument is doing to them**: *famā lakum… fiʾatayn?* — what is happening to you, that you have become two? He makes them look up from the object of the fight and see the *shape* of the fight. You have become twoness. The thing you are arguing about is two-faced, and look — *so are you now.*

Here is the practical hinge, and it is sharp.

When you find yourself in a hardening conflict with people you love about a person you *can't read*, the Qur'anic instinct is to **suspect the ambiguity itself before you suspect each other.** The unreadable person may be doing nothing more aggressive than *being unreadable* — and the damage is being done not by them but by what their ambiguity is provoking in *us.* The hypocrite, in this ayah, throws no spear. He just stands in the middle, illegible, and lets the believers do to themselves what an enemy army could not do to them at Uhud: split them in half.

So the question to carry into tomorrow is not *"which faction is right about this person?"* The question is: **"What is my certainty about this unreadable person costing the people I'm actually bound to?"**

Because unity is not the prize you get *after* you finally classify everyone correctly. Sometimes the refusal to make the classification an emergency *is* the act of faith.

[PAUSE]

Let me re-enter through a human door before we go deeper. You have been the unreadable person too. To someone, at some point in your life, *you* were the one in the middle — the one whose sincerity others were arguing about behind a closed door. Hold both. The mercy you'd have wanted in that room is the mercy this ayah is quietly teaching you to extend.

### Theme Two: The Thing Allah Takes Out of Your Hands

Now the deepest current of this ayah. And I want to open it with the most exhausting experience a caring person knows.

There is someone you have been trying to *save.*

You know who they are the moment I say it. The sibling drifting from the deen. The friend destroying themselves and unable to see it. The parent you keep hoping will soften. The person you have explained, and pleaded, and prayed, and stayed up at night for — and they keep *reverting.* Every time you think you've gotten through, they flip back to exactly where they were. You set them upright with everything you have, and they topple back to the heap. *Arkasahum.* You have watched it happen so many times you've stopped trusting the good days.

And underneath the exhaustion is a quieter thing you might not have named: a kind of low-grade *guilt.* As if their misguidance is a problem **you failed to solve.** As if, had you found the right argument, the right tone, the right verse at the right moment, they would have walked the path. You carry their astray-ness as if it were an item on your own ledger.

Now hear what Allah asks. Not coldly — He asks it the way you'd take something heavy out of the hands of someone who's been carrying it too long:

> **أَتُرِيدُونَ أَن تَهْدُوا مَنْ أَضَلَّ ٱللَّهُ** — *Do you wish to guide the one Allah has let go astray?*

And the mufassirūn want you to hear the *shape* of that question before its content. al-Jalalayn marks it — along with its twin later in the verse — as *istifhām al-inkār*: a question asked not to gather an answer but to *deny and rebuke* the very thing it names. It is not "do you happen to want this?" It is "*how could you possibly* want this?" The form itself is a closed door.

Sit, too, with the verb **تَهْدُوا** — *tahdū*, "to guide." al-Jalalayn catches a fold here that is easy to miss: *an tahdū* can carry the sense of *to reckon them among the guided* — for the faction that rose to defend the hypocrites was, in effect, *enrolling* them in the company of the rightly-guided, vouching for hearts Allah had just cast back. So the rebuke lands twice: not only "do you imagine you can place a heart on the path," but "do you mean to *count among the rightly-guided* the very ones I have overturned?" The Qur'an uses *hidāya* in two very different senses, and confusing them is the source of all your exhaustion.

There is *hidāyat al-dalāla* — guidance as **showing the way.** Pointing. Conveying. Explaining the road. **This is yours.** This the Prophet ﷺ himself was commanded to do, and this you are commanded to do — *"Indeed, you guide to a straight path"* is said *to* the Prophet in one place.

And there is *hidāyat al-tawfīq* — guidance as **placing the heart upon the way and making it walk.** The inner turning. The opening of the chest. **This was never yours.** Of this same Prophet ﷺ Allah said elsewhere, *"Indeed, you do not guide whom you love."* You can show. You cannot turn. You can hold the door open with both arms until they ache. You cannot make a single soul walk through it.

[PAUSE]

This is the thing the ayah takes out of your hands. Not your *duty* to show the way — that stays. What it removes is the crushing, unspoken belief that the *outcome* was ever your responsibility. *A-turīdūna an tahdū man aḍalla-llāh* — do you really *want* to do the one thing in this universe that is reserved for Allah alone? Do you want to stand where only He stands?

And notice the mercy folded inside the rebuke. To say *"you cannot guide whom Allah left astray"* is also to say: **their straying was never a measure of your failure.** You did not lose them. You were never holding the rope that could keep them. The grief is real — but the *guilt* is a category error. You have been billing yourself for a transaction that was always between that soul and its Lord.

And here the mufassirūn guard us against a misreading, because *aḍalla* — "Allah led astray" — can land in the ear like a brute shove, as though Allah seized a willing seeker and flung him off the road. al-Muyassar unfolds it more carefully than that. Allah's *iḍlāl* of these men, he explains, was *ṣarf al-qalb ʿan dīnih* — the *turning of the heart away* from His religion — and *khidhlān*: the *forsaking* of one who had already forsaken Him, the withdrawal of the divine support without which no heart finds the road. They had turned; He turned them. They had let go; He let them go. The straying is not an arbitrary sentence imposed on the innocent — it is the dropping-away of a hand from one who had already pulled his own hand back. Read it beside *arkasahum bimā kasabū* and the two halves rhyme: in both, what Allah "does" to them is, at root, the ratifying of what they themselves had chosen.

Then comes the final, narrowing word — the one Allah turns and says to *you* alone: **wa man yuḍlili-llāhu falan tajida lahu sabīlā.** Whoever Allah lets go astray — *you will not find for him a way.* And we already walked through that absence: the road out of someone else's heart is simply not an object your hands can locate. al-Muyassar closes the verse with the same plainness al-Jalalayn does — *falā ṭarīqa lahu ilā al-hudā*, there is, for such a one, no road *to guidance* — which is exactly the *sabīl* the ayah names and then withholds from your searching hands.

Here is the paradox, and I'm going to let it sit without dissolving it, because the ayah does. This sounds like the bleakest sentence in the passage. *There is no way. Stop looking.* And yet for the person who has been breaking themselves trying to save someone — **it is the most merciful sentence in the passage.** Because it finally lets you set the weight down. You can keep showing the way — gently, faithfully, for the rest of your life. But you can stop trying to *be* the way. You can stop auditing your tone for the magic phrase that doesn't exist. You can return the outcome to the only One who was ever holding it.

So what changes tomorrow? Concretely: the next time you pray for that person — and keep praying, that's *yours* — change the verb. Stop praying *"let me find the way to guide them."* That road, the ayah tells you plainly, you will not find. Pray instead: *"Yā Allah, You who alone open and overturn hearts — open theirs. I will keep showing the way. The walking, I return to You."* You will feel the difference in your chest the same night. The exhaustion that lived there was never the weight of *daʿwah.* It was the weight of a job that was never on your desk.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this ayah turn out to be the same insight from opposite ends.

The **first half** turned to the community and said: *stop making a war out of the person you cannot read.* The **second half** turned to the individual and said: *stop carrying the outcome of a heart you cannot turn.* And both are the same lesson wearing two clothes:

**There is a line between what is yours and what is Allah's — and almost all our exhaustion lives in the places where we've put our hands across that line.**

Across the line was *judging the unreadable* into factions — Allah had already cast the true verdict, *arkasahum bimā kasabū,* before the argument began. Across the line was *guaranteeing the outcome* of another's guidance — *falan tajida lahu sabīlā,* that path was never findable by you. The community and the individual were reaching for the same forbidden thing from two directions: the believers wanted to *settle* the hypocrites' fate, the caring soul wanted to *secure* the lost one's heart. And to both, with one breath, Allah says: **that was Mine. Put it down.**

And the grammar proved it. The dual *fiʾatayn* showed the disease of division reproducing in us. The clattering *arkasahum* showed the verdict already fallen by their own hand. And the address that narrowed from *all of you* to *you alone* showed that this releasing is something each of us has to do in private — because no committee can set down a weight; only a single heart can.

### Questions to Carry

1. Who is the unreadable person in my life right now — and what is my need to *classify* them costing the people I'm actually bound to?
2. Whose guidance have I been carrying as if their straying were an entry on *my* ledger?
3. When I pray for someone lost, am I asking Allah to *open their heart* — or am I asking Him to hand *me* the key, which He has told me plainly I will not find?
4. Where, today, did I put my hands across the line between what He asked of me (to show the way) and what He kept for Himself (to turn the heart)?
5. Have I been, to someone, the unreadable one in the room — and what mercy would I have wanted from them?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you find yourselves split into camps over those whose own hearts are split, turn the question inward — for the verdict has already fallen by their hands, and the one thing your exhaustion keeps reaching for, the turning of a heart, was never an object your hands could find.

### The Closing Invitation

You came in, perhaps, gripping someone. An ambiguous person you've been fighting your family over. A lost person you've been failing to save. This ayah does not ask you to love them less or speak to them less. It asks you to **show the way with everything you have — and release the outcome with everything you are.** Keep your hands on the door. Take your hands off the heart. That is the entire teaching, and it is lighter than what you walked in carrying.

### A Brief Duʿā

*Yā Allah, You who alone overturn hearts and alone set them straight — keep me from making wars out of the people I cannot read, and lift from my chest the weight of outcomes that were never mine. Let me show the way faithfully and leave the walking to You; and the hearts I love that have wandered, including my own when it wanders — open them, for I have searched, and I have found no way but Yours.*

*Āmīn.*
