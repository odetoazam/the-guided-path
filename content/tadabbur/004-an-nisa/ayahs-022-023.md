# The Marriage of Abhorrence: How Guidance Seals the Past and Builds a Sanctuary

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch the meaning.

> وَلَا تَنكِحُوا۟ مَا نَكَحَ ءَابَآؤُكُم مِّنَ ٱلنِّسَآءِ إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ فَـٰحِشَةً وَمَقْتًا وَسَآءَ سَبِيلًا

*Wa lā tankiḥū mā nakaḥa ābāʾukum mina-n-nisāʾi illā mā qad salafa, innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa-maqtan wa-sāʾa sabīlā.*

**"And do not marry those women whom your fathers married, except what has already passed. Indeed, it was an immorality, and hateful, and an evil way."**

Now, on the surface, this looks like a legal verse. A rule. A line in a code of conduct about who you may and may not marry. And if we read it that way — as a regulation — we will walk past it. We will nod, agree that of course this is forbidden, and move on, never having let it touch us.

So let me ask you to do something harder. Let me ask you to walk into the world this verse fell into.

### Rebuild the world

Imagine a society where, when a man dies, his wives do not become free. They become inheritance. They are listed among his property — alongside his camels, his date palms, his swords. And the heir, often the eldest son, has a right to them. He could come to his father's widow — and understand, this is frequently not even his own mother, but a younger wife — and throw his cloak over her. That gesture meant: *you are mine now.* He could marry her himself, taking no dowry because she "came with the estate." He could marry her off to someone else and pocket her dowry. Or he could simply imprison her in widowhood, refusing to let her go, until she ransomed her freedom with whatever little she had.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that woman wakes up to. She has just buried her husband. And in the same breath, she discovers that the boy she may have helped raise, the young man of the household, now holds her like a deed. Her grief is not even allowed to be grief. It is immediately a transaction.

This was not a rare scandal at the edges of society. This was *normal*. This was the system.

Al-Tabari, gathering the reports of why this verse came down, describes these men with a single telling verb: they were a people who would *yakhlufūna ʿalā ḥalāʾil ābāʾihim* — who would "succeed to" their fathers' wives, stepping into the vacancy the way an heir steps into an inherited post. And notice the very word he reaches for: *ḥalāʾil* — "wives." It is the same word verse 23 will use only a breath later, in *ḥalāʾil abnāʾikum*, "the wives of your sons." The Jahili tongue had already filed the widow under a thing to be *succeeded to*; the Qur'an lifts that same category out of their hands and walls it off.

And there is a detail the classical sources preserve that I want you to hold onto. Al-Baghawi narrates that when a man named Abu Qays died — described as one of the righteous among the Anṣār — his son Qays proposed marriage to his father's widow. And she said something extraordinary. She said: *"I have taken you as a son, and you are among the best of your people — but let me go to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and ask."* She went. And this verse came down.

She felt, instinctively, that something was wrong. She had no scripture yet to point to. Just a flinch in the soul. And Allah descended a verse to vindicate that flinch.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to watch for as we go.

Allah could have simply said, "This is forbidden." Four words. Done. Instead, He does three things that no legal code does. He chooses one strange word where you expect another, and that single choice changes who this verse is even *about*. He pauses, mid-prohibition, to seal off the past with a mercy so quiet you could miss it. And then He reaches into the language of the very people He is correcting — and uses *their own word* for this practice against them.

[PAUSE]

By the end, I want you to see that this is not a verse that builds a fence. It is a verse that builds a home.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that should not be there — مَا instead of مَن

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we look at the verse, because without it, the most important word here will slide right past you.

In Arabic, when you refer to *who* — a person, a rational being, someone with a soul — you use the word **مَن** (*man*). And when you refer to *what* — a thing, an object, a category, something non-rational — you use **مَا** (*mā*). It's a basic distinction. *Man* for persons. *Mā* for things. A child learning Arabic learns this early.

Think of it in English. We do something similar. We say "the people *who* came" and "the things *that* came." We instinctively reserve "who" for persons. To say "the woman *that* my father married" instead of "*whom* my father married" — it lands slightly cold, slightly objectifying, doesn't it? "That" is the word for furniture.

Now hold that, and look at the verse.

The subject here is **women** — *an-nisāʾ*. Human beings. Rational souls. The most natural, expected word would be *man*: "do not marry **those whom** (man) your fathers married."

But that is not what Allah says.

He says: **وَلَا تَنكِحُوا۟ مَا نَكَحَ ءَابَآؤُكُم** — "do not marry **mā** — *that which* — your fathers married."

[PAUSE]

He uses the word for things.

Now, let's sit with this, because there are layers here, and I want to be careful and honest with you about what the grammar establishes and what it invites.

The grammarians note that *mā* in a construction like this points to the **category, the type, the kind** rather than to specific named individuals. It generalizes. So the first, plainest function is breadth: Allah is not forbidding one particular woman. He is forbidding *the entire class* of such unions — every father, every widow, every instance, no exceptions, no loopholes. *Mā* throws a net over the whole category.

And here I want to be scrupulous, because the classical commentators are not unanimous about how much weight that word can bear. Al-Jalalayn, glossing the verse, reads the *mā* here as simply standing in for *man* — *bi-maʿnā man* — as though the two were interchangeable in this context. And al-Muyassar, when it paraphrases the verse for a plain reader, reaches straight for *man*: "do not marry those whom your fathers married." So the established, conservative reading is that *mā* points to the women themselves as persons. The reading I'm about to draw out is a layer the *balāgha* — the rhetoric — opens up, not a meaning the bare grammar forces on you. Hold both honestly: the floor is solid, and the resonance above it is where the verse breathes.

That's the grammatical floor. Now let me show you what it invites us to consider — and I'll keep flagging this as interpretation built on the grammar, not the grammar itself.

When you choose the word for *things* to describe a kind of marriage, you are, in a sense, naming what that marriage *did* to the woman. In the world we just described, the stepmother had already been reduced to a *thing*. She was inheritance. She was estate. The Jahili system had already turned the *who* into a *what*. And the language of the verse holds up a mirror to that. It is as if Allah uses the word *mā* not to demean her — but to expose what *they* had done to her. *You treated her as a "what." You marry "what" your fathers married, as if she were a plot of land that passed down with the house.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the turn. Notice who is grammatically addressed in this verse. The command — *do not marry* — is spoken to the **sons**. The men who held the power. The widow herself is never addressed. She is spoken *about*, not *to*. And at first that might trouble us. But sit with it. The verse reaches *past* her, to the hands that held her, and it strikes the chain there. **The person with no power is protected by a command aimed at the person who held all of it.** She doesn't have to fight for her freedom. Allah pulls the deed out of his hands.

This is what a single substituted word does. *Man* to *mā*. The word for "who" replaced by the word for "what" — and in that swap, the whole tragedy of her objectification is named, and the whole machinery of it is dismantled.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Two: The mercy hidden in the middle — إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ

Now watch where Allah places His next move. Right in the middle of a prohibition. Mid-sentence.

He has just said: do not do this. And before He tells you *how bad* it is — before the condemnation — He stops and says: **إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ** — *"except what has already passed."*

Let me teach you the pieces.

**سَلَفَ** (*salafa*) comes from a root that means *to precede, to go ahead, to have gone before*. From this same root comes the word *salaf* — the predecessors, the ones who came before us, the early generations. The image inside the root is of something that has *moved on ahead of you* — it is down the road, around the bend, out of reach. You cannot call it back.

And in front of it sits the particle **قَدْ** (*qad*). When *qad* comes before a completed-tense verb like *salafa*, it does something specific: it *seals* it. It stamps it as **done, finished, fully completed**. *Qad salafa* — "it has, with finality, already gone."

So Allah is saying: as for what has *already happened* — the unions formed in the age of ignorance, before this guidance reached you — that is a sealed country. It has gone ahead. It is behind the bend. *I am not opening that door to walk back through it and hold you accountable.*

The mufassirūn are clear and gentle on this. Al-Jalalayn glosses it: *what has passed of your doing this — it is pardoned, maʿfuww ʿanhu.* Al-Muyassar: *what passed in the Jahiliyya — there is no taking-to-account for it, lā muʾākhadhata fīh.*

[PAUSE]

Now, I want you to feel the *placement* of this. Allah does not put the mercy at the end, as a footnote after the scolding. He puts it *before* the scolding. He forbids — then immediately, before He even tells you how ugly the thing was, He says: *but what's done is done; that's behind you.* He clears the weight off your back *first*, so that when the hard words come — *immorality, hatefulness, an evil way* — they do not crush you. You are already standing on cleared ground.

Think about how a wise, loving correction works versus a cruel one. The cruel correction says: *look at the terrible thing you did — and by the way, you'll pay for all of it.* The loving correction says: *that's behind you now, it's forgiven — now let me tell you why we never go back.* Allah corrects the way the most merciful parent corrects: He secures your heart *before* He shows you the wound.

This little phrase — *illā mā qad salafa* — is one of the great mercy-phrases of the Qur'an. And hold it, because we are going to see it again before we finish, in a way that will take your breath.

### Element Three: The triad of condemnation — and a word that sounds like what it means

Now comes the verdict. And Allah does not give one word. He gives three, and they escalate, and each one names a different dimension of the wrong.

> إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ فَـٰحِشَةً وَمَقْتًا وَسَآءَ سَبِيلًا

*"Indeed it was a **fāḥisha**, and a **maqt**, and **evil as a way**."*

Let's take them one at a time, because Allah did not stack them carelessly.

**فَـٰحِشَةً (fāḥisha)** — from a root meaning the *grossly excessive, that which exceeds all bounds of decency*. A *fāḥisha* is not just a small wrong; it is an act whose ugliness is *blatant, exposed, beyond dispute*. This is the same word the Qur'an uses for the gravest sexual transgressions. Al-Muyassar catches the intensity of the form when it paraphrases the verse: this was *amr qabīḥ yafḥushu wa yaʿẓumu qubḥuhu* — a foul thing whose ugliness *swells and magnifies*, a wrong that does not sit still but keeps growing more repulsive the longer you look at it. So the first dimension is: **the act itself is intrinsically ugly.** Look at the deed on its own, and it is repulsive.

**وَمَقْتًا (maqt)** — now we move from the act to the *response* it provokes. *Maqt* means not just hatred but **intense, settled, deep abhorrence** — the kind of loathing reserved for something genuinely vile. And here al-Jalalayn makes the point explicit: this is a cause of *maqt min Allah* — Allah's abhorrence — *ashadd al-bughḍ*, the most severe degree of detestation. So the second dimension is no longer about the act in the abstract; it is about **how it stands before Allah.** It draws down His abhorrence.

Now — I want you to *listen* to that word. **Maqt.** Say it. *Maqt.* It is short, hard, clipped. It closes on a *qāf* and a *tāʾ* — two of the most abrupt, percussive sounds in Arabic, slammed together with no soft vowel to cushion the landing. The word does not flow. It *stops*, like a door shut in your face. It sounds like contempt. It sounds like the thing it means. [PAUSE] Allah did not reach for a smooth, melodic word for this. He reached for one that *tastes* bitter in the mouth — because the reality it points to is bitter.

And here is the detail that makes the hair stand up. **The pre-Islamic Arabs themselves called this practice "nikāḥ al-maqt" — "the marriage of abhorrence."** Even in their ignorance, even while they *did* it, some part of them knew it was loathsome enough to name it after disgust. The children born of such unions were sometimes called *maqtī* — "the abhorred ones."

[PAUSE]

So when Allah calls it *maqt* — He is reaching into *their own vocabulary* and handing them back the word they already used. He is saying: *you already named this. You already knew. Your own tongue confessed what your hands kept doing.* There is no clearer way to strip away every excuse. He convicts them with their own language.

**وَسَآءَ سَبِيلًا (wa-sāʾa sabīlā)** — and the third dimension. *Sāʾa* is a verb of dispraise — "evil it is, wretched it is." And *sabīl* means a **path, a road, a way** — and al-Muyassar, glossing it, widens the frame still further: *biʾsa ṭarīqan wa manhajan* — wretched as a *road* and as a *methodology*, an entire system of living. So this is no longer about the single act, nor even about Allah's response to it. This is about **direction.** Where does this practice *lead*? What kind of society, what kind of family, what kind of human being does this road build?

Watch the architecture of the three:
- **Fāḥisha** — look *down* at the act. It is ugly in itself.
- **Maqt** — look *up* to Allah. It draws His abhorrence.
- **Sāʾa sabīlā** — look *forward* down the road. It leads somewhere wretched.

Down at the deed, up to the Creator, forward to the consequence. Three angles on one darkness, so that no matter which way you turn to find an excuse, the verdict is already standing there.

### Bridge

So look at what these three constructions have shown us. A single word — *mā* instead of *man* — that exposes how a woman was turned into property and then quietly snaps the chain that held her. A mercy-phrase tucked *before* the rebuke, sealing the past so the heart can stand. And a three-fold verdict that convicts the act from below, from above, and from up ahead — using the sinners' own word against them.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About your past. And about the house Allah wants you to live in.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The sealed country — what guidance does to your past

I want to start somewhere you already live.

There is a particular kind of pain that has nothing to do with the present. It is the pain of the *irreversible*. The thing you did before you knew better. The years you can't get back. The person you were — and the things that person did — that you would give almost anything to undo, and cannot.

Maybe it's a relationship you should never have been in. Maybe it's how you treated someone who is no longer alive to receive your apology. Maybe it's a whole *era* of your life — a way you used to live before your heart turned — that you now look back on the way you'd look at a stranger wearing your face. And the cruelest part is this: there is no door back into the past. You cannot go in and fix it. It has *gone ahead of you.* It has *salafa.*

[PAUSE]

For the person who comes to faith later in life — the convert, the one who reconnected after years away — this is not abstract. This is the central wound. *Yes, I'm here now. Yes, my heart has turned. But what about all of that? What about who I was? Does that just... hang on me forever?*

Now bring the verse back.

The very people Allah is speaking to here had, many of them, *already done this.* They were *already* married to women they now learned were forbidden. Imagine being in that gathering, hearing this verse, and your own marriage is one of the ones being named *fāḥisha, maqt, an evil way.* Your stomach drops. Your entire life feels suddenly condemned.

And in the same breath — *before* the condemnation even lands — Allah says: **إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ.** *Except what has already passed.*

He does not say the act wasn't wrong. He calls it three kinds of wrong. But He severs the *wrongness of the act* from *your accountability for it.* The thing was ugly. *And you are not going to be hauled to account for the part of it that happened before the light reached you.*

And al-Tabari draws the line with real precision. What the verse forbade these men, he explains, was *al-muqām ʿalayhinna* — *remaining* in those marriages from this day forward; what it pardoned was everything that had already passed in their days of ignorance and idolatry — *He did not take them to account for it* — and that pardon was fastened to a condition: that they now feared Allah in their Islam and obeyed Him. So the seal over the past is not a license to drift. The same motion that closes the door behind you turns you to face forward. *Qad salafa* is mercy for what is done — and, in the same breath, a summons to who you become next.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the deepest mercies in how Allah deals with us: **guidance resets the ledger.** The moment the light reaches you is a threshold, and Allah draws the line of accountability *at* that threshold, not behind it. This is the same principle He states plainly elsewhere: *"Say to those who disbelieve, if they cease, what has already passed will be forgiven them"* (8:38). The same root. The same mercy. The past, sealed.

Now — what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the weight you carry from *before* is a weight Allah has already set down. And carrying it anyway — dragging it through your prayers, letting it whisper *you're not really worthy of this* — is not humility. It is, in a quiet way, *arguing with Allah's mercy.* He said *qad salafa.* He sealed it. Who are you to break the seal and drag it back into the present?

Here is the practical turn. The shame over a sealed past is not a sign of piety. It is a chain. And Allah, in the very middle of a hard command, stopped to cut it for you — *before* He said anything else. Tomorrow, when that old memory rises to accuse you, answer it with the verse: *that has gone ahead. He sealed it. I live forward from here.*

### Theme Two: From a fence to a sanctuary — what verse 23 builds

Let me ask you to think about boundaries — but not the way we usually think about them.

We tend to experience rules as *restrictions*. Lines drawn to stop us from doing things we want. And if that's all a rule is, it feels like a cage. But there is another kind of boundary entirely. Think of the boundary around a sanctuary. A *ḥaram* — a sacred, protected space. The boundary there is not there to imprison the people inside. It is there to keep predators *out*. It is the wall that lets the people within finally *exhaust* — drop their guard, sleep without one eye open, be at peace.

Now watch what Allah does across these two verses.

Verse 22 abolishes *one* practice — the son and the father's widow. He could have stopped there. One cruelty, corrected. But He does not stop. Verse 23 *opens*, and out pours a whole catalogue:

> وَحُرِّمَتْ عَلَيْكُمْ أُمَّهَـٰتُكُمْ وَبَنَاتُكُمْ وَأَخَوَٰتُكُمْ...

*Forbidden to you are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your father's sisters, your mother's sisters, your brother's daughters, your sister's daughters...* — and it keeps going. The women who nursed you. Your milk-sisters. Your wives' mothers. The stepdaughters under your guardianship. The wives of your sons. Two sisters at once.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? Allah took the abolition of *one* wrong and used it as the doorway into the construction of an *entire architecture.* He is not just knocking down the practice of *nikāḥ al-maqt.* He is **drawing the floor plan of a safe home.**

And the floor plan reaches further than the surface words. The mufassirūn read these categories as principles, not a closed checklist. Al-Muyassar notes that "your mothers" draws in the grandmothers as well — on both your father's and your mother's side — and that "your daughters" reaches down to include your granddaughters. The list is *generative*: Allah names the nearest cases and lets the principle radiate up the lineage and down it, so that no one above you or below you is ever left standing outside the wall. The sanctuary is not a fixed perimeter of named individuals; it is a rule of safety that expands to cover everyone it should.

Think about what this list *means* from the inside of a household. It means: in this home, there is a category of relationships that is *categorically, permanently off the table.* Your daughter never has to wonder. Your sister never has to wonder. The woman who nursed you, your wife's mother, your son's wife — every one of them is placed, by divine decree, into a zone of guaranteed safety. The home becomes a place where a girl can grow up *without scanning the room.* Where vulnerability is not an opportunity.

[PAUSE]

And here is the unseen reality the physical list is pointing toward. The catalogue of names looks, on its surface, like dry law. But what it *is* — is the blueprint of a sanctuary. Every "forbidden" in that verse is a wall around the people you should be protecting. The prohibition is not aimed at *your* freedom. It is aimed at *their* safety. **Each *ḥurrimat* — "forbidden" — is a guarantee issued to the most vulnerable person in the room.**

This is why the universal human horror at incest exists at all. Across every culture, this is the deepest taboo — and we sometimes treat that as merely instinctive. But the Qur'an tells us it is *moral*: the family must be the one place on earth where certain people are *unreachable* in that way, so that the home can be a place of rest rather than a place of threat. Remove that, and the home — the one shelter a person has — becomes the most dangerous place they know.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It reframes every divine boundary in your life. The next time a limit Allah set feels like a wall pressing in on you, ask the better question: *who is this wall protecting?* Often — more often than we admit — the boundary that constrains *your* desire is the very thing that guarantees someone else's peace. The *ḥaram* that fences you in is the *ḥaram* that lets the vulnerable sleep. And to honor it is not to accept a cage. It is to become *trustworthy* — to become one of the walls of someone's sanctuary.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now that we have done the work, let me show you something I held back. Look at how these two verses *end* — and read them side by side.

Verse 22 closes on the act:
> إِنَّهُۥ **كَانَ** فَـٰحِشَةً وَمَقْتًا وَسَآءَ سَبِيلًا
> *"Indeed **it was** an immorality, and hateful, and an evil way."*

Verse 23 closes on Allah:
> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ **كَانَ** غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا
> *"Indeed **Allah is** ever Forgiving, Merciful."*

[PAUSE]

The same structure. *Innahu kāna* — *Inna-Llāha kāna.* "Indeed it was..." — "Indeed Allah is ever..." Two verses, two verdicts, mirror images of each other. The first verdict is on the **deed**: it was abhorrent. The second verdict is on the **Lord**: He is forgiving and merciful.

And what stands *between* those two verdicts? The whole list of forbidden relations — and, at its very end, the phrase we met before, returning like a refrain: **إِلَّا مَا قَدْ سَلَفَ** — *"except what has already passed."* It appears in verse 22. It appears again at the end of verse 23. It bookends the entire architecture.

So look at the full shape now. Allah names the evil. He lays out the entire protective structure of the family. He seals the past — twice. And then He closes not on the ugliness of the sin, but on the beauty of His own name: *Ghafūr, Raḥīm.* **The passage that began by naming the most abhorrent thing a family could do — ends on the most merciful thing Allah is.** That is the journey of the whole passage in miniature: from *maqt* to *raḥma.* From abhorrence to mercy. He will not leave you standing in the condemnation. He walks you out of it, into His forgiveness.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The *mā* instead of *man* showed us a woman reduced to a thing — and a chain broken by a command aimed at the powerful. That *is* the sanctuary: the boundary that protects the one with no power.

The *qad salafa* sealed the past in the grammar. That *is* the mercy: guidance resets the ledger, and the passage proves it by ending on *Ghafūr, Raḥīm.*

The triad — *fāḥisha, maqt, sāʾa sabīlā* — convicted the act from below, above, and ahead. And then the very next verse refused to leave us in that conviction, and built a home instead.

One movement: *He names the wound honestly, He seals what's behind you, and He builds you a place to live safely going forward.*

### Questions to carry

- What sealed thing from my past am I still dragging into my prayers — refusing the *qad salafa* that Allah already pronounced over it?
- Where in my life is a boundary I resent actually a wall protecting someone more vulnerable than me?
- Am I a *ḥaram* — a place of safety — for the people in my home? Can the vulnerable around me drop their guard, or do they have to scan the room?
- When Allah corrects me, do I let Him clear the ground first — or do I crush myself with the rebuke before I hear the mercy that came *before* it?
- Whose dignity have I quietly treated as a *what*, when Allah was waiting for me to see them as a *who*?

### One-sentence distillation

Allah abolishes a single inherited cruelty — and in doing so reveals that guidance seals the shameful past, names evil by its own bitter name, and rebuilds the home into a sanctuary whose every wall protects the one with the least power.

### The closing invitation

So here is what could shift. Stop reading Allah's boundaries as fences that hem *you* in, and start seeing them as walls that hold someone *safe.* And stop breaking the seal He placed over your past. He said *qad salafa* — it has gone ahead. Let it go ahead. Walk forward from the threshold of His mercy, and become, for the people entrusted to you, one of the walls of their sanctuary.
