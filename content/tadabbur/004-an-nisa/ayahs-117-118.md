# The Stripped-Bare One and His Appointed Share

### Surah An-Nisa, verses 117–118

> [ayah:4:117] إِن يَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ إِنَـٰثًا وَإِن يَدْعُونَ إِلَّا شَيْطَـٰنًا مَّرِيدًا
> [ayah:4:118] لَّعَنَهُ ٱللَّهُ ۘ وَقَالَ لَأَتَّخِذَنَّ مِنْ عِبَادِكَ نَصِيبًا مَّفْرُوضًا

*In yadʿūna min dūnihi illā ināthā — wa in yadʿūna illā shayṭānan marīdā. Laʿanahu llāh, wa qāla la-attakhidhanna min ʿibādika naṣīban mafrūḍā.*

> "They invoke, besides Him, nothing but female idols, and they invoke nothing but a rebellious satan — one whom Allah has cursed. And he said: 'I will surely take an appointed portion of Your servants.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Curtain and What Is Behind It

Let me ask you to do something before we read a single word closely.

Picture a man standing before a stone. He has carved it himself, or his grandfather carved it. He has given it a name. He brings it the first of his harvest. He whispers to it the fears he will tell no human being. When his child is sick, he comes here. When the caravan is late, he comes here. This is not a casual habit for him. This is the deepest gesture of his life — the lifting of his hands, the bending of his neck, the surrender of his heart to something he believes is listening.

Now I want you to hold a question in your mind, and I want you to feel how large it is.

**Who is on the other side of that prayer?**

He thinks he knows. He thinks there is a goddess there — a being of power who hears him, who can give and withhold, who deserves the trembling in his voice. That is the entire premise of what he is doing. Take away the listener, and the prayer collapses into a man talking to a rock.

These two verses are the moment the Quran walks up to that man, and to every version of that man that has ever lived — including the versions sitting in our own century, who would be insulted to be called idol-worshippers — and pulls back the curtain.

And what is behind the curtain is not what anyone expected. [PAUSE]

To feel why this lands the way it does, you have to know where we are in the surah. Surah An-Nisa is mostly a surah of *society* — inheritance shares counted to the fraction, the rights of orphans, the treatment of women, the laws of marriage, the conduct of war, the sickness of the hypocrites who pray with the believers and scheme against them in the dark. It is one of the most this-worldly surahs in the whole Quran. Page after page of human arrangement.

And then, in verse 116, the floor drops out. Allah says: *Indeed, Allah does not forgive that a partner be associated with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills.* Suddenly we are not talking about inheritance percentages anymore. We are talking about the one wound the universe does not heal on its own. The one debt that cannot be quietly written off.

And the moment Allah names that sin — shirk, associating partners with Him — He does something a courtroom does. He does not just name the crime. **He shows you the accused.** He turns the camera around to reveal who, exactly, is standing where God should be standing in the human heart.

Verses 117 and 118 are that reveal. And they are arranged in a descent — like a man going down a staircase into a cellar, each step darker than the last. First: what they call upon. Then: who is *really* behind what they call upon. Then: what that being said, in his own voice, about you and me.

Stay with me. We are going to go down those stairs slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Two Walls of "Nothing But"

Before we touch the verse, I want to teach you a single structure in Arabic, because once you feel it, this verse will never sound flat to you again.

In ordinary speech, when I want to tell you what someone is doing, I just say it. "He is eating bread." That sentence has open doors. He is eating bread — and maybe also some fruit, maybe some meat, the sentence doesn't slam any doors shut. It tells you one thing he's doing without ruling out everything else.

But Arabic has a way of *sealing the room.* You take a word of negation — a word that means "not" — and you place it at the very front. Then, later in the sentence, you place a word that means "except." And between those two walls, you trap exactly one thing.

"He is **not** eating **anything except** bread."

Feel the difference. The first sentence opened a door. This one bricks up every wall and leaves a single window. Bread. Only bread. There is nothing else in this room. The grammarians call this structure *ḥasr* or *qaṣr* — restriction, confinement. It doesn't just *tell* you something; it *fences* you in with it and removes every other possibility.

Now look at what Allah does. Not once. Twice.

> **إِن** يَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِهِۦٓ **إِلَّآ** إِنَـٰثًا
> *In* yadʿūna min dūnihi *illā* ināthā

That word at the front, **إِن** — *in* — here it is not the "if" you may have learned. The classical commentators are explicit on this. Al-Qurtubi states it plainly: *"In here is a negating particle, meaning ‘not.’"* It is *in nāfiya* — a wall of negation. And then comes **إِلَّآ** — *illā*, "except." The second wall.

So the sentence reads, with all its force: **"They do not call upon, besides Him, anything whatsoever — except females."**

The room is sealed. Everything these worshippers do, all their devotion, all their lifted hands across all their lifetimes — Allah confines it to one category and locks the door.

And then — [PAUSE] — He builds the *same* sealed room a second time, in the very same breath:

> **وَإِن** يَدْعُونَ **إِلَّا** شَيْطَـٰنًا مَّرِيدًا
> *wa in* yadʿūna *illā* shayṭānan marīdā

"And they do not call upon anything — except a rebellious satan."

Notice something, because this is the architecture of the whole passage. Allah did not say: *they call upon females and satans.* That would be a list — two items side by side, each a thing they worship. He built **two separate sealed rooms.** *They call upon nothing but females.* Full stop. *They call upon nothing but a satan.* Full stop.

Why two rooms instead of one list? [PAUSE]

Because the two statements are not two items. They are **two layers of the same act.** The first room is what the worship *looks like* from the outside — the worshipper sees a female deity, a goddess with a name. The second room is what the worship *is* from the inside — underneath the goddess, there is a will, and the will is not divine. It is a stripped-bare rebel.

This is precisely how al-Jalalayn reads the move from the first room to the second. The satan they "call upon," he says, is Iblīs himself — *wa huwa Iblīs* — and they invoke him *by* the very worship they pour into the idols, "because in that worship they are obeying him" (*li-ṭāʿatihim lahu fīhā*). That is the hinge. The worshipper never decides to worship a satan; he would recoil from the thought. He decides to worship a goddess. But the command to bow before the stone came from somewhere, and to obey that command is to serve the one who issued it. The mask is the goddess. The obedience underneath runs, unseen, straight to him.

He is telling you: peel the first sealed room, and you do not find a goddess. You find the second sealed room. And in the second room is something with a face you were never shown.

Let that be the first thing we carry forward. The grammar itself is doing the unmasking. *Nothing but females* — that is the mask. *Nothing but a satan* — that is the face. The verse hands you the mask and then, with the identical structure, peels it off.

### Element Two: Why "Females"? — Reading the Word They Were Stripped Down To

Now we have to sit with that first sealed room, because the word Allah chose to put inside it is strange, and the strangeness is the point.

He did not say *they call upon nothing but idols.* He did not say *stones,* or *statues,* or even *false gods.* He said **إِنَـٰثًا** — *ināthā* — **"females."** The feminine ones.

What is happening here?

The commentators give us the historical ground, and we should stand on it. Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both explain: the idols of Mecca bore feminine names. Al-Lāt. Al-ʿUzzā. Manāt. And beyond the famous three, al-Qurtubi tells us, *every tribe had its idol, and they would say, "the female of the tribe of so-and-so."* They feminized their gods. They literally called each idol *"the lady of the Banū such-and-such."*

It is worth knowing that al-Tabari opens his comment on this very word by noting that *the early exegetes differed* — *ikhtilāf ahl al-ta'wīl* — over precisely what *ināth* names here. The reading he documents first is exactly the one we are building on: that Allah named al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt "females" because the idolaters themselves had named them with feminine names — *kulluhā mu'annath,* "every one of them feminine," as the early authority Abū Mālik put it. So this is not a stray or fringe reading; it is the most direct of the classical understandings. But the fact that the *mufassirūn* paused here and disagreed is itself a signal worth heeding — the word is doing more than the surface shows, and that is exactly the door we are about to walk through.

So at the first level, Allah is simply quoting them back to themselves. *You call them females? Fine. Let the word stand. You worship — females.* And said plainly like that, stripped of all the incense and ceremony, it sounds exactly as hollow as it is. There is a quiet devastation in just *repeating someone's own words back without the costume.*

But stay with the word, because the Arabic root reaches deeper than the historical fact.

The root أنث — *a-n-th* — is the root of *unthā,* the female. But the lexicographers connect this family of meaning to softness, to pliancy, to that which yields and has no hardness of its own. From the same conceptual field comes the description of iron that is soft and workable, of a thing that bends. The feminine, in the raw lexical sense the Arabs heard, carried the shadow-meaning of *the receptive, the moldable, the thing acted upon rather than the thing that acts.*

Now — be careful here, and I want to be precise, because this is not a statement about women. It is a statement about *stone.* What Allah is exposing is the absurdity at the heart of idolatry: **the worshipper has taken something utterly passive — something carved, shaped, named, and positioned entirely by his own hands — and then bowed to it as though it had power over him.** He fashioned a thing that cannot move, cannot answer, cannot act — the very definition of the acted-upon — and then he made it his god. He worships his own product. He kneels to the clay he himself molded.

That is why "females" — in this ancient sense of *the shaped, the inert, the receptive* — is a more cutting word than "idols" would have been. "Idol" still grants the thing a kind of dark dignity, a false power. *Ināth,* heard this way, strips even that away. It says: you are not even worshipping a powerful false god. You are worshipping *something that gets done to.* Something with no agency at all. And this is exactly where al-Muyassar lands the plain sense of the verse: they worship *awthān lā tanfaʿ wa-lā taḍurr* — idols that "can neither benefit nor harm." Not weak gods. Not minor gods. Things with no power to move the world in either direction — which is the same indictment the root delivers from a different angle. [PAUSE]

And here is the absence I want you to notice — read what is *missing*. Allah does not name a single one of the idols in this verse. Not al-Lāt, not al-ʿUzzā, not Manāt. He had every opportunity. The worshippers knew these names; the names were their pride. And He withholds every one of them.

Why erase the names?

Because a name is a kind of honor. To name a thing is to grant it a place in the record. And Allah refuses. He reduces three named, storied, ancient goddesses — objects of devotion for generations — to a single dismissive category: *females. Inert things. The acted-upon.* He does to the idols, in His speech, exactly what idolatry does to the truth: He flattens them. Except when He flattens them, He is flattening a lie back into its true shape. **He denies them even the dignity of being mentioned by name.**

Hold these two together — the sealed room that says *nothing but,* and the word *females* that says *and even that "nothing" is a passive nothing, a shaped nothing, a nothing you made yourself.* The first verse, before we even reach the satan, has already emptied the idol completely. There is no one home.

Which is exactly what makes the next step down the stairs so terrifying. Because if there is no one home in the idol — then who has the worshipper actually been talking to all this time? [PAUSE]

### Element Three: مَّرِيدًا — The One Scraped Bare

The verse answers. Behind the empty idol, in the second sealed room, is **شَيْطَـٰنًا مَّرِيدًا** — *a satan, marīd.*

And we have to slow all the way down for that second word, *marīd,* because it is one of those Quranic words where the *sound and shape of the word are themselves the meaning.* The translators usually give us "rebellious," and that is not wrong — but it is the smallest part of what is in there.

The root is مرد — *m-r-d.* Let me show you what this root *physically means* before it ever means anything moral, because the Arabs heard the physical meaning first and the physical meaning is the whole picture.

*Marada* — at its root — means **to be stripped bare. To be smooth. To have nothing on the surface.**

The lexicographers give the concrete images, and you should see them, one by one:

A *shajaratun mardāʾ* — a tree described from this root is a tree that has **shed every leaf,** standing bare against the sky, nothing left on its branches.

*Ramlatun marda'* — a dune of sand described from this root is sand that **grows nothing,** a smooth slope where not a single blade will take root.

And — this is the one that unlocks it — *al-amrad,* from the very same root, is **the beardless youth.** The face that has not a single hair on it. Smooth. Bare. Nothing growing.

Now feel what these images have in common. The bare tree. The barren dune. The hairless face. Every one of them is a surface from which *everything good that should grow has been scraped away.* The tree should have leaves — bare. The sand should hold a plant — barren. And from this — only from this — does the root climb up into its moral meaning: *al-mārid,* *al-marīd,* is the one who has been **scraped utterly bare of every good** — and who, having nothing of goodness left to lose, exceeds every limit, smooths himself past every boundary, rebels without restraint.

Do you see what Allah has done by choosing this exact word? [PAUSE]

He has not merely told us the satan is *disobedient.* "Disobedient" is a verdict. *Marīd* is a *portrait.* It shows you a being from which every leaf of mercy, every blade of softness, every hair of remaining good has been stripped off until nothing is left but smooth, bare, will-to-corrupt. Al-Muyassar captures the active edge of it: a satan *who has reached, in corruption and in corrupting others, an enormous extreme.* He is not just empty of good — his emptiness has become a verb. He does to others what was done to him: he strips.

And now look — *look* — at what Allah has set side by side, in two sealed rooms, two breaths apart.

In the first room: **ināth.** The soft, the shaped, the passive, the thing-with-nothing-of-its-own.

In the second room: **marīd.** The scraped-bare, the thing-stripped-of-everything-good.

The idol is empty because it never had anything. The satan is empty because everything was taken from him. **Both rooms are barren. The entire architecture of false worship is two kinds of emptiness, stacked.** The worshipper reaches past the living God — *al-Ḥayy, al-Qayyūm,* the Living, the Sustaining, the One overflowing with every name of mercy and power — and he reaches *into a void, behind which is a deeper void.* He passes the Fountain to drink from a painted picture of water, behind which crouches a being dying of the same thirst he is selling. [PAUSE — let this sit]

This is what tadabbur is for. The word "rebellious" slides right past you. But *marīd* — the bare tree, the barren dune, the beardless smoothness, the being scraped down to pure appetite — once you have seen it, you cannot unsee who is really standing behind the curtain.

### Element Four: The Cut Notch — *Naṣīban Mafrūḍā*

We have one step left on the staircase, and it is the darkest, because here the satan stops being described and **starts to speak.**

Verse 118 opens with the verdict: **لَّعَنَهُ ٱللَّهُ** — *laʿanahu llāh* — *Allah cursed him.* And we should pause on what *laʿana* means, because in Arabic it is not merely "to condemn." Its core sense is **to drive far away, to banish, to expel from all nearness.** The cursed one is *the cast-out one* — pushed to the farthest possible distance from mercy. Remember that, because of what he says next.

Al-Tabari unfolds the word exactly along these lines: *akhzāhu wa aqṣāhu wa abʿadahu* — "He disgraced him, drove him off, and put him far." And then he adds the phrase that quietly closes the circuit with the verse before it: Allah, he says, cursed him and *distanced him from every good* — *abʿadahu min kulli khayr.* Hear that against *marīd.* The portrait in verse 117 showed us a being scraped bare of every good; the sentence in verse 118 tells us *how he came to be that way.* The curse **is** the stripping. To be cursed, in al-Tabari's reading, is to be banished from every good — and a thing banished from every good is, by definition, *marīd.* The two verses are filming one event from two angles: 117 shows you the bareness, 118 names the sentence that produced it.

Then the grammar does something it almost never does in a passage of narration. The voice changes. Allah has been telling us *about* the satan in the third person — *they invoke him, Allah cursed him.* And then, mid-verse, the curtain doesn't just lift — **the figure behind it opens its mouth.** *Wa qāla* — "and he said." We are no longer hearing a description of the enemy. We are hearing the enemy's own voice, his own oath, quoted directly into the divine record:

> لَأَتَّخِذَنَّ مِنْ عِبَادِكَ نَصِيبًا مَّفْرُوضًا
> *La-attakhidhanna min ʿibādika naṣīban mafrūḍā*

Let us take this apart, because every word is a blade.

**لَأَتَّخِذَنَّ** — *la-attakhidhanna.* This is one verb so loaded with emphasis that Arabic can barely contain it. The verb is from أخذ, "to take" — but it is in Form VIII, *ittakhadha,* which is the *reflexive* form: not just "to take" but **"to take for oneself, to seize and make one's own."** And then it is wrapped in *two* separate markers of absolute certainty: the *lām* of emphasis bolted onto the front (*la-*), and the heavy *nūn* of swearing hammered onto the back (*-anna*). In English we have nothing this strong. The closest we get is: **"I will most certainly, I swear it, seize for myself —"** This is not a hope. It is not a plan. It is an *oath,* sworn by the cast-out one in the very presence of the One he was cast out from.

**مِنْ عِبَادِكَ** — *min ʿibādika* — "from **Your** servants." And here, notice the pronoun. *Ka.* "Your." The cursed one, the one just driven to the farthest distance from mercy, is speaking *directly to Allah.* He does not say "from the people," or "from humankind." He looks at the Lord who banished him and says, of God's own beloved servants: *I will take a cut of Yours.* There is an almost unbearable insolence in it — he names them as God's possession in the very act of vowing to steal them. He acknowledges the ownership and challenges it in the same breath.

But the word I most need you to hold is the last one. **نَصِيبًا مَّفْرُوضًا** — *naṣīban mafrūḍā* — "an appointed portion." [PAUSE]

*Naṣīb* is a share, a portion, a cut. But *mafrūḍ* — *mafrūḍ* is the word that should stop your breath.

The root is فرض — *f-r-ḍ.* And the physical, original meaning of *faraḍa,* before it meant anything else, is **to cut a notch. To make a precise incision.** Carpenters and archers used it: you *faraḍa* the bow by cutting an exact notch where the string sits. From that came the meaning of *a fixed, measured, marked-out amount* — and from *that* came its most familiar meaning in our religion: *farḍ.* **Obligation. The thing that is divinely fixed and mandatory.** The five prayers are *farḍ.* The fast of Ramadan is *farḍ.* The shares of inheritance — laid out earlier in this very surah — Allah called them *farīḍa,* an ordained, notched, non-negotiable measure.

And here the classical *mufassirūn* split the word open along its two seams, and we should keep both. Al-Tabari glosses *mafrūḍ* in this verse simply as *maʿlūm* — a **known, determined** amount, a measure already reckoned. But al-Jalalayn reaches past "known" for the older, more physical sense and glosses it *maqṭūʿan* — **cut off, severed, portioned out** — which is the very meaning of the notched incision we just traced with our own hands. So this is not a reading we are importing onto the verse from the outside; the tradition itself heard both senses ringing at once. The satan's share is *cut* — that is the notch — and it is *known* — that is the fixed measure. And al-Muyassar fills in what the determined portion is made of: a known share the satan takes *fī ighwā'ihim qawlan wa-ʿamalan* — "in leading them astray, in word and in deed." Hold both halves of that. The cut is not vague ill-will; it is a *measured, reckoned quantity.* And it is collected in two specific currencies — what he gets a person to **say** and what he gets a person to **do.** Every false word talked into a mouth, every wrong act steered into a hand — these are the coins in which the appointed portion is paid.

And al-Jalalayn lets the cursed one finish his own sentence. After the oath, he records the purpose the satan attaches to it: *adʿūhum ilā ṭāʿatī* — "**I will summon them to my obedience.**" Hear the word he chose for the prize: *ṭāʿa* — obedience. Now carry it back up the staircase. In verse 117, al-Jalalayn told us the idolaters invoke the satan *by* their idol-worship "because in that worship they are obeying him" — *li-ṭāʿatihim lahu fīhā.* The same word. *Ṭāʿa* on both ends of the passage, like a clasp closing. What looked, from the outside, like devotion lifted to a goddess, the cursed one names, in his own voice and to God's face, as *obedience rendered to me.* The worshipper thought he was giving worship. The satan files it as a payment of obedience — and that, precisely, is the cut he counts.

Now hear what the satan is saying with this word, and hear the horror of it.

He is using **the language of fixed, ordained, religious entitlement** to describe his claim on human beings. He is not saying "I hope to lead some astray." He is saying: *I will take a portion that is **notched out, fixed, marked, mine by appointment.*** He is claiming a *farḍ* — an *obligation owed to him.* He looks at humanity and sees not a battlefield with an uncertain outcome but a **harvest with a guaranteed yield.** A percentage. A cut that comes off the top, measured and certain, like a tax.

Feel the inversion. The same root that gives us our sacred duties to Allah, the cursed one steals to describe the souls he counts as his rightful due. Where Allah *notches out* what He has made obligatory for us out of mercy, the satan *notches out* what he intends to seize from us out of malice. He has built a counterfeit religion in a single word — with its own "obligations," its own fixed dues, its own appointed share. [PAUSE]

And here is what should both terrify and *steady* us. He said *naṣīban* — **a portion.** A cut. Not all. Not the whole. He did not vow to take everyone. He swore to take *a share.* In the parallel passage in Surah Sād, the boast is total — *I will mislead them all* — and then he carves out the exception with his own mouth: *except Your sincere, chosen servants.* The cursed one himself admits there is a portion he cannot touch.

So even inside his oath, even in his most arrogant moment, there is a boundary he cannot cross. There is a "but" he is forced to confess. The harvest he counts as guaranteed has a fence around it, and he knows it. The whole question of our lives — and we will come to this — is which side of that fence we are standing on.

We have reached the bottom of the staircase. Let us gather what we found at each step, because the linguistic descent has shown us something — but now let us ask what it reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: What Is Actually on the Other Side of Your Longing

Let me bring this out of seventh-century Arabia and into your apartment.

I want you to think about the thing you reach for when you are empty. Not the thing you'd write on a form. The real thing. For one person it's the phone — the screen lifted before the eyes are fully open, the thumb already moving. For another it's the relationship they cannot leave even though it is hollowing them out. For another it's the career, the number in the account, the next acquisition, the body remade in the mirror, the substance, the scroll, the constant low hum of *something is coming that will finally make me feel held.*

We are sophisticated people. We do not bow to stones. We would be insulted by the comparison. But ask the honest question — the question this verse asks: **when you lift your deepest hope toward that thing, who is on the other side of it?**

The Meccan worshipper believed there was a goddess behind the idol. He was wrong. There was no one home. And the verse told us why with that one cold word, *ināth* — the thing is *passive.* It is shaped. It is acted-upon. It is something *you* gave power to. It has none of its own.

And this is precisely the structure of every false god we serve. **The thing cannot answer you.** The phone does not love you back. The number in the account does not hold you when you are afraid at three in the morning. The career does not weep at your funeral. The body in the mirror is, like the idol, something you shaped — and it will age and fall no matter what you do. Every one of these is *ināth* in the deep sense: inert, receptive, given all its meaning by *your* devotion and possessing none of its own. It can neither benefit nor harm — *lā tanfaʿ wa-lā taḍurr,* as al-Muyassar said of the idols — and yet you are pouring the ocean of your heart's longing into a cup that was never alive. [PAUSE]

But the verse does not stop at "the idol is empty." That would only make us sad, or lonely. It does something far more serious. It says: *behind the empty thing is not nothing.* Behind it is a will. *In yadʿūna illā shayṭānan marīdā* — they call upon nothing but a stripped-bare rebel.

Here is the part our age desperately needs to hear. We tend to imagine our compulsions as *neutral.* The algorithm is just math. The craving is just chemistry. The empty pursuit is just a personal weakness. The verse refuses this comfort. It tells you that behind the inert object of your false worship, there is *intention* — there is something that *wants* your devotion to land there, something that profits from your hands being lifted in the wrong direction. The emptiness of the idol is not an accident. It is *curated.* Somebody wants you facing the bare tree so that your back is turned to the Living God.

And what is that somebody? *Marīd.* Scraped bare. A being that lost everything good and now exists to strip it from others. Think about what that means for the things that hollow you out. They do not give. They *take.* The defining motion of the *marīd* is subtraction. Notice how every false god operates exactly this way: the more you feed it, the emptier you get. The scroll promises connection and leaves you lonelier. The acquisition promises fullness and leaves you wanting the next thing within the hour. **You are feeding something that can only strip, and calling it a source.**

So what does this change about tomorrow? It is brutally practical. The next time you feel that pull — the reach toward the thing — do not fight it as a neutral habit. Name it. Ask the verse's question: *Who is on the other side of this? Is it the Living, who answers, who holds, who gives? Or is it the bare tree, and behind the bare tree, the one who strips?* The naming itself breaks the spell. Idolatry survives only as long as the curtain stays down. The moment you see that there is no goddess there — only inert clay, and behind it a thief — the gesture of worship dies in your hands. You cannot bow to a bare tree once you have seen that it is bare. [PAUSE]

And then — turn. This is the mercy buried in the structure. Verse 116, just before all this, told us *Allah forgives what is less than shirk for whom He wills, and does not forgive the partner set up beside Him.* The whole point of unmasking the false god is not to shame you for having served it. It is to free your hands so they can lift toward the One who is actually there. The Living. The One who is, by every name, the opposite of *marīd* — not scraped bare but overflowing, not taking but giving, not absent behind a curtain but *closer than the jugular vein.* The verse empties the idol so that you will finally turn around and find that the One you were really thirsting for was behind you the whole time, never a curtain between you, waiting.

### Theme Two: The Appointed Portion — Living Outside the Cut

Now I want to take you inside the cursed one's business plan, because verse 118 lets us read it in his own handwriting, and there is something there that will either frighten you or fortify you depending entirely on where you choose to stand.

Here is a thing every honest person eventually notices about temptation: **it is not random.** It does not feel like a fair fight where anything could happen. It feels *engineered.* It feels like something studied you. The pull always seems to come at the exact hour you are weakest — the lonely night, the bruised ego, the moment right after a small success when your guard drops, the moment right after a failure when you think *what does it matter now.* It is targeted. It has a funnel. It knows the conversion rate.

The verse tells you this is not your imagination. *La-attakhidhanna min ʿibādika naṣīban mafrūḍā* — "I will most certainly seize for myself an **appointed portion** of Your servants." That word *mafrūḍ* — the cut notch, the fixed measure, the *farḍ* — tells you the cursed one operates like an actuary, not a gambler. He is not hoping. He is *counting.* He has run the numbers on human weakness across every century, and he speaks of his harvest the way a tax collector speaks of revenue: a fixed percentage, marked out in advance, certain to come in. And remember the currency al-Muyassar named — the cut is collected *qawlan wa-ʿamalan,* in word and in deed, with the cursed one's own stated aim being to *summon them to his obedience.* He is not after a vague gloom; he is after specific words in your mouth and specific acts in your hands, each one logged as obedience paid to him. [PAUSE]

This should sober us in a particular way. We tend to think of our slips as private dramas — *my* struggle, *my* weakness, *my* bad night. The verse reframes it. From his side, you are not a drama. You are a *statistic in a forecast.* He has budgeted for you. The mechanisms that catch you — the timing, the rationalization that appears right on cue, the "just this once" that has appeared a thousand times before — these are not new each time. They are the *mafrūḍ,* the fixed notch, the same trap re-set with the same precision because it works on the same predictable percentage of people in the same predictable moments.

And if that were the whole story, it would be despair. But it is not the whole story, and the rescue is hidden in the very word he chose.

He said *naṣīban.* **A portion.** A cut. Not the whole. [PAUSE]

Sit with the enormous weight of that limit. The cursed one is sworn, oath-bound, in the presence of God, to take the maximum he can possibly take — and the maximum he can name is *a share.* In the parallel oath in Surah Sād and in Surah Isrā', he is forced to draw the boundary with his own mouth: *except Your sincere servants* — *I will have no authority over them.* The greatest claim the enemy of mankind can make, at the height of his arrogance, **already contains the fence he cannot climb.** There is a portion of humanity his actuarial table cannot touch. He knows it. He admits it. He budgets *around* them.

So the entire question of a human life narrows to this: **Are you inside the cut, or outside it?**

And here is the mercy — the thing that should make you sit up. *You* have a say in which side of the fence you stand on. The ones he cannot touch are not a sealed, secret elite. The Quran names the quality that puts a soul beyond his reach: *ikhlāṣ* — sincerity. The *mukhlaṣīn,* the ones made pure, the ones who do their deeds *for Allah alone* with no idol — no *ināth,* no bare tree — sharing the throne of their heart. The fence around the protected portion is not made of status or knowledge or lineage. It is made of *to whom you turn.* The more entirely your worship belongs to the One who is truly there, the more completely you step out of the appointed cut. [PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow? Two things, very concretely.

First, **stop being surprised.** The targeting is real and it is predictable. Then know your own notches. Every one of us has the two or three fixed moments where the cut is taken — you know yours. The hour, the mood, the trigger. The enemy has them in his ledger; you should have them in yours. Name your weakest three hours of the week and build a wall there *before* you arrive — a planned dhikr, a call to a brother, a physical removal of the temptation from reach. You are not fighting a fair fight against a gambler. You are dismantling a trap set by an actuary. Take the notch off his table.

Second, and deeper: **the way out of the portion is not mainly resistance — it is sincerity.** You do not escape the cut by white-knuckling against a thousand individual temptations. You escape it by becoming someone whose heart belongs so wholly to Allah that there is no longer a doorway for the thief. The *mukhlaṣ* is not the one who fights the hardest. He is the one with no idol left to exploit. Empty the room of every *ināth,* and the *marīd* finds nothing to grab. The cursed one took an oath to seize an appointed portion — and then, in the same breath, confessed that the sincere are not for sale. **Make yourself someone he was forced to write off in advance.** [PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the staircase whole, because the two verses are built with a precision we could only feel step by step.

It descends in four movements, and each strips away a layer:

**First** — the sealed room of the idol: *nothing but females.* The mask. What worship looks like from the outside.

**Second** — the word *ināth* itself empties even the mask: the thing is inert, shaped, passive, made by the worshipper's own hands. No one is home.

**Third** — the second sealed room, identical in structure to the first, peels the mask off: *nothing but a stripped-bare satan.* Not a goddess behind the idol — a *marīd,* a being scraped of every good, behind the curtain.

**Fourth** — the cursed one speaks, and we hear the business model in his own voice: *an appointed portion,* a notched, fixed, religious-language cut of God's servants, sworn by oath — yet a *portion,* with a fence around it he himself confesses.

Two sealed rooms of emptiness, then a voice from the deeper dark, then — at the very bottom of the descent — a limit. The structure takes you all the way down into the cellar and then, in its last word, shows you the door. *Naṣīban.* A share. Not all. There is a way out, and it was named at the lowest point.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes are the same insight approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us *emptiness behind emptiness* — *ināth* and *marīd,* two barren rooms. The first theme lived inside that: every false god you serve is an inert thing with a thief behind it, and the cure is to turn toward the One who is actually, fully, overflowingly *there.*

The grammar then showed us the cursed one's *fixed notch* — *mafrūḍ,* the actuarial cut. The second theme lived inside that: temptation is engineered and predictable, but it is a *portion,* fenced, and sincerity puts you outside the fence.

And both arrive at one place. **Idolatry is reaching past the Living, who is near, to grasp at the bare, behind which crouches the one who strips.** The whole rescue is a single turn — emptying the heart of every shaped idol so completely that you belong to Allah alone, and the thief, by his own sworn admission, has nothing left in you to take.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you reach for the thing that empties you, and you ask *who is on the other side of this longing* — what answer do you actually find?
2. What is the *ināth* in your life — the inert thing you yourself shaped and then handed your heart to as though it had power over you?
3. You know the two or three fixed hours when the *cut* is taken from you. What wall could you build there *before* you arrive — not in the moment, but now?
4. The fence around the protected portion is made of sincerity, not status. In your worship this week, who is it really *for*?
5. If the cursed one were forced to read his ledger aloud — would your name be in the appointed portion, or among the ones he was made to write off?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Behind every false god is not a power but a void, and behind the void a stripped-bare thief who has sworn to take his fixed cut of humanity — yet he himself confesses there is a portion, sealed by sincerity, that he can never touch.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to fight a thousand temptations one by one. That is the long war, and it exhausts the soul. There is a shorter way, and these verses point straight at it: **empty the room.** Turn from the bare tree and face the Living. Make your worship so wholly His that when the thief comes with his ledger and his notched, certain cut — he finds the door bricked up and no idol inside to seize. Not because you fought him at the door, but because you were already, entirely, Somewhere Else.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who are truly there when every idol is empty — pull back the curtain on every false thing I have lifted my heart toward, and let me see the bareness behind it before I waste another prayer on it. Make my worship so purely Yours that the cursed one is forced to write me off in advance — count me, by Your mercy, among the sincere whom he confessed he could never take.* Ameen.
