# The Sheep and the Greeting

### *Surah An-Nisa, Ayah 94*

---

## INTRODUCTION — Standing Where the Blood Was Spilled

Let me give you the words first, the way they came down.

> **يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا ضَرَبْتُمْ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ فَتَبَيَّنُوا وَلَا تَقُولُوا لِمَنْ أَلْقَىٰ إِلَيْكُمُ السَّلَامَ لَسْتَ مُؤْمِنًا تَبْتَغُونَ عَرَضَ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū, idhā ḍarabtum fī sabīli-llāhi fa-tabayyanū, wa lā taqūlū liman alqā ilaykumu-s-salāma lasta mu'minan, tabtaghūna ʿaraḍa-l-ḥayāti-d-dunyā...*

> *O you who believe! When you go forth in the way of Allah, seek clarity, and do not say to one who offers you a greeting of peace, "You are not a believer," seeking the fleeting goods of worldly life — for with Allah are abundant gains. You were like that before, then Allah conferred favor upon you; so seek clarity. Indeed Allah is ever, of what you do, Aware.*

Now let me build the world this verse walked into.

There is a man alone on a hillside. The classical commentators preserve more than one account of who he was — al-Baghawi names him as Mirdās ibn Nahīk, a man of Fadak, the only one of his people to accept Islam; in the account behind al-Jalālayn he is an unnamed man of Banū Sulaym. But the shape of the story holds across the narrations. **A single believer, surrounded by people who do not believe, carrying his faith quietly, alone.**

He is herding his sheep. His *ghanam.* Hold onto that little word — *ghanam,* the sheep — because in a moment Allah is going to pick it up and turn it into the hinge of the entire verse.

A detachment of Muslim soldiers crests the hill. They are out **fī sabīli-llāh** — in the path of Allah. They see him. And he, seeing armed believers, does the one thing a frightened believer would do to signal he is one of them: **he gives them the greeting. He says the salām. In some narrations, he speaks the words of the testimony itself.**

And they look at him. And they look at the sheep.

And one of them says: *He only said that out of fear. He's not really one of us. He's just trying to save his neck.* And they kill him. And they drive his sheep home as spoils.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. These were not hypocrites. These were companions, soldiers of the Prophet ﷺ, out in the path of God. **How does a sincere person, doing a holy thing, end up killing a brother for a flock of sheep — and feeling justified the whole time?**

Because if you think that machinery lives only in 7th-century soldiers, you have not yet met yourself. This verse is not about a battlefield. It is about the moment your desire whispers a verdict about someone's heart — and dresses that verdict up as discernment.

Let us go slowly. There is something stunning buried in the *vocabulary* of this ayah, and we are going to dig it out word by word.

---

# PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Ḍarabtum* — "When You Strike Out"

Let me teach you a small thing about Arabic before we touch the word.

In English, when we travel, we have soft words for it. We *go.* We *set out.* We *journey.* The motion sounds gentle. But Arabic, for the act of setting out into open country, often reaches for a verb that is anything but gentle.

The word here is **ضَرَبْتُمْ** — *ḍarabtum* — from the root *ḍ-r-b.* And the first, the most basic, the most physical meaning of *ḍaraba* is: **to strike. To hit. To beat.**

This is the same root the Arabs used in the expression *ḍaraba fī-l-arḍ* — literally *"to strike in the earth"* — meaning to travel through it. Al-Qurtubi notes this directly: *aḍ-ḍarbu: as-sayru fī-l-arḍ* — striking is the journeying through the land. The traveler doesn't drift across the earth. **He strikes it.** His feet hit it, his pace beats it, he hammers his way forward.

Now sit with what Allah has done by choosing this word.

The verse is about soldiers who went out to *strike* in the path of Allah. The whole frame of their day is *striking.* They are primed for impact. Their hands, their minds, their entire posture is set toward the blow. And into that posture — that cocked, forward-leaning, ready-to-strike posture — a man walks offering peace.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the danger Allah is naming? **When your whole being is configured to strike, everything in front of you starts to look like a target.** The man who has decided he is at war reads a greeting as a trick, because a hammer cannot help seeing nails. Allah opens the verse by naming the exact mindset most likely to commit the error — *idhā ḍarabtum,* when you are in strike-mode — and then immediately, in the very next word, jams a brake into the machine.

That next word is its own universe.

---

## Element Two: *Fa-tabayyanū* — "Then Make It Clear To Yourselves" (and He Says It Twice)

Here is the brake. **فَتَبَيَّنُوا** — *fa-tabayyanū.*

Let me teach you the form before the meaning, because the *form* is the whole point.

The root is *b-y-n,* which carries the idea of *clarity, distinctness, separation* — *bayān* is clear eloquent speech; *bayyin* is something obvious. But Allah does not use the simple form. He uses **Form V** — *tafaʿʿala,* the reflexive pattern. In Form V, the action turns back on the doer. *ʿallama* means "he taught"; *taʿallama* means "he taught himself, he learned." The reflexive *t-* folds the verb back into the person.

So *tabayyana* is not merely "investigate the facts out there." It is closer to: **"make the matter become clear to yourself. Generate clarity inside you. Do the inner work until the fog lifts."**

Do you see why the form matters? The error in the story was not a lack of external information — the man was *standing right there speaking their language of faith.* The information was abundant. The failure was internal. **Their desire had already darkened the glass before any fact could reach them.** So Allah does not say "gather evidence." He says *tabayyanū* — clear the fog *in yourselves,* because the distortion was never in the world. It was in the wanting.

And there is a second reading worth knowing. Alongside *fa-tabayyanū,* al-Jalālayn records a variant recitation — *fa-tathabbatū* — and notes that it occurs in *both* places, exactly where *tabayyanū* does. *Tathabbatū* comes from the root *th-b-t:* firmness, steadiness, the act of making something stand still and sure. Hold the two readings together and you have the whole counsel in stereo: *tabayyanū* says *clear the fog;* *tathabbatū* says *plant your feet — do not be moved by the first thing your hunger tells you.* One word treats the haze in the eye, the other the haste in the hand. Against a strike-posture that is all motion and momentum, the Quran answers with a verb of stillness.

Now — here is what I want you to notice, and it is easy to miss.

**He says it twice.**

Go back to the verse. *Fa-tabayyanū* appears early — "when you go forth, seek clarity." And then, after the entire middle section about greed and mercy and memory, the command returns: *fa-tabayyanū* — "so seek clarity" — a second time, just before the end.

[PAUSE]

Why repeat a command in a single short verse? Arabic does not waste breath, and the Quran least of all.

Stay with me. The first *tabayyanū* comes **before** the diagnosis. It is the cold instruction: *check yourself.* But then Allah does something to you in the middle of the verse. He exposes your motive — *you're doing this for sheep.* He humbles you — *you were once exactly like him.* He reminds you of His favor — *Allah was generous to you.* And **only after He has softened the heart, broken the certainty, melted the strike-posture** — only then does He repeat the command.

The second *tabayyanū* is not the same as the first. The first was given to a hard heart. The second is given to a heart that has just been shown its own reflection. **It is the same word landing on completely different soil.** The first time you hear "seek clarity," you hear a rule. The second time, after He has dismantled your self-righteousness, you hear it as *mercy* — *now that you see yourself, look again at him.*

The repetition is not repetition. It is the *before* and the *after* of a heart being changed mid-sentence.

---

## Element Three: *Alqā ilaykumu-s-salām* — "He Cast Peace Toward You"

Now look at how Allah describes what the man did. He did not "say" peace. He did not "offer" it in some neutral way. The verb is **أَلْقَىٰ** — *alqā* — Form IV of *l-q-y,* and its core meaning is **to throw, to cast, to fling toward.**

*Alqā ilaykumu-s-salām* — **he cast the peace toward you.** He threw it across the gap.

Feel the physical picture. *Alqā* is the verb you would use for tossing something to someone — you cast it through the air and it lands in their hands. So Allah is not describing a polite murmur. He is describing **a man flinging his greeting across open ground toward armed strangers,** the way you would throw your only rope to someone on the far side of a chasm. It is an act of exposure. To throw something is to let go of it, to send it out where you can no longer protect it.

And what did he throw? **As-salām.** The greeting. Peace.

Here the lexicon opens a second door, and the classical commentators walk straight through it. The root *s-l-m* is the root of *salām* (peace), of *taslīm* (surrender), and of *islām* itself. So al-Jalālayn glosses *alqā ilaykumu-s-salām* in two registers at once: it is *at-taḥiyya* — the greeting — **or** it is *al-inqiyād bi-kalimati-sh-shahāda* — submission through the word of testimony. The man may have thrown them "peace upon you," or he may have thrown them the whole creed: *I surrender. I am one of you.*

And this double meaning is not a stretch — it is anchored in the recitation itself. Al-Jalālayn notes the word is read two ways, *with the alif or without it:* *as-salām,* the spoken greeting, or *as-salam,* surrender and the laying-down of arms. Both readings point the same direction — a man signalling *I am no threat; I am with you.* Al-Muyassar sharpens the picture: the one before you has shown some mark of Islam *and is not fighting you* — *wa lam yuqātilkum* — for it may well be that he is a believer concealing his faith. The very things the soldiers had in front of them — a greeting, a lowered weapon — were, in the reading of the commentators, evidence already submitted. They overruled it.

[PAUSE]

Now hold the two images together. A man takes the most defenseless thing he has — his declaration of faith, his peace, his *I-am-with-you* — and he *throws it across the gap* into the hands of men in strike-posture.

And they let it fall. They look past the thing he threw, down to the sheep behind him.

There is something almost unbearable here. To *cast* peace is to make yourself vulnerable. He didn't hold his faith close where it was safe. He flung it out toward the very people most likely to misread it. And the verse, by choosing *alqā,* makes us watch the greeting travel through the air — so that we feel, with full weight, what it meant for them to ignore where it landed.

---

## Element Four: The Sheep Become the Spoils — *Ghanam* and *Maghānim*

And now we arrive at the buried treasure. This is the moment the whole verse was built around, and you will only see it if you have been holding that little word from the introduction.

The man was herding his **ghanam** — his sheep. The soldiers killed him and seized them as spoils. And what does Allah call the thing they were chasing, and the thing He offers instead?

> *fa-ʿinda-llāhi* **مَغَانِمُ** *kathīrah* — *for with Allah are abundant* **maghānim.**

**Maghānim.** War-spoils, gains, plunder. And its root — confirmed, undisputed — is *gh-n-m.* **The very same root as *ghanam,* the sheep.**

[PAUSE — let this one land]

Do you see it now? The thing they killed a believer for — *ghanam,* a handful of sheep — and the thing Allah is dangling in front of them — *maghānim kathīrah,* abundant spoils — **are the same word.** Allah has reached into the crime scene, picked up the literal animals that were the price of a man's blood, and held them up against an infinite version of themselves.

It is as if He says: *You spilled a soul for ghanam? You wanted gain? Fine. I am the Lord of gain. With Me are maghānim kathīrah — gain without limit, gain without blood, gain you never have to murder your brother to receive.*

The wordplay is not decoration. It is the entire moral argument compressed into a single shared root. **Greed could not even imagine a number large enough to make the trade worth it, because the small ghanam in your hand and the abundant ghanam with Allah are made of the same letters — and only one of them costs a life.**

And notice the precision of the contrast on the *other* side. What did they gain in the world? Allah calls it **ʿaraḍ** — *ʿaraḍa-l-ḥayāti-d-dunyā.* The root *ʿ-r-ḍ* gives us the idea of something that merely *passes by,* something incidental, a fleeting accident with no substance. In the language of the philosophers who came later, *ʿaraḍ* is the opposite of *jawhar* — accident versus essence, the surface that flickers and vanishes versus the thing that abides.

So look at the trade Allah lays bare:
- On one side: **ʿaraḍ** — singular, fleeting, an accident that passes — of **al-ḥayāti-d-dunyā,** *the lower life* (and *dunyā,* from *d-n-w,* literally means **the nearer, the lower** — the cheap thing close at hand).
- On the other side: **maghānim kathīrah** — abundant, plural, overflowing, *ʿinda-llāh,* in the keeping of the Eternal.

A passing scrap of the low and near. Against limitless gain held by the Most High. **And they chose the scrap, and paid for it with a man's life.**

## Bridge — From the Words to the Wound

Look at what these four words have built.

*Ḍarabtum* — the strike-posture that turns everyone into a target. *Tabayyanū* — the inner clarity demanded, and demanded twice. *Alqā* — the peace flung across the gap and let fall. *Ghanam* into *maghānim* — the murder committed for a fistful of the very thing God was offering for free.

This is not a battlefield procedure. It is an x-ray of the human heart in the instant it sells out. **Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about us. About the thing inside you that has done a smaller version of this, this week.**

# PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Verdict You Pass to Get What You Want

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, at some point, decided that a person was a certain way — and you decided it precisely *because* deciding it gave you something. The coworker you concluded was "lazy" right around the time you wanted his project. The relative you decided was "manipulative" right when you wanted to stop calling. The ex you reclassified as "never really loved me" because that version let you keep the furniture and the friends.

We do not usually experience this as dishonesty. **We experience it as insight.** That is the terrifying part. The mind does not announce, *I am about to slander this person so I can have what's theirs.* It says, *I'm just being realistic about who they are.*

This is exactly the machinery in the verse. The soldiers did not think: *Let's murder an innocent man for his sheep.* They thought: *He's faking. He's not really one of us.* The greed never appeared as greed. **It appeared as a verdict about someone else's heart.** *Lasta mu'minan* — "you are not a believer." A judgment about *his* sincerity that conveniently unlocked *their* desire.

And Allah names the mechanism with surgical exactness. Right after the false verdict comes the diagnosis: **tabtaghūna ʿaraḍa-l-ḥayāti-d-dunyā** — *seeking the fleeting goods of the world.* He does not say "you were mistaken." He does not say "you misread the situation." He goes underneath the verdict to the want that authored it. **The judgment about his faith was a story your desire wrote to get the sheep.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the blade of it. Notice the word the verb *tabtaghūna* comes from — *b-gh-y* — to seek, to crave, the same root that gives *baghy,* transgression, overreach. The very act of *wanting* the world too much *is itself* the thing that tips into injustice. Your craving doesn't just precede the wrong verdict. It *is* the engine that manufactures it.

So the practical question is not "am I a good person?" It is much sharper: **When I find myself certain that someone is bad — fake, selfish, faithless — what do I stand to gain from that certainty being true?** Because if the verdict pays you, you are not a judge. You are a buyer who has disguised his price as a moral fact.

And here is the mercy in how Allah corrects it. He does not demand that you become naïve, that you believe everyone. He demands only **tabayyanū** — *seek clarity.* Slow down. Make the matter clear inside yourself before your hunger makes it clear for you. The whole law is: *do not let your wanting be the witness.*

**What changes tomorrow:** the next time you feel a hard, satisfying certainty snap into place about another person's character, run one check before you act on it — *what would I lose if I were wrong about them?* If the honest answer is "something I wanted," you have not reached a conclusion. You have been bribed by yourself.

## Theme Two: *Kadhālika Kuntum* — "You Were Just Like Him"

Now Allah turns the mirror around, and this is the most merciful sentence in the verse.

> **كَذَٰلِكَ كُنتُم مِّن قَبْلُ فَمَنَّ اللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ**
> *Kadhālika kuntum min qabl, fa-manna-llāhu ʿalaykum.*
> *You were like that before, then Allah conferred His favor upon you.*

Like *that.* Like *whom?*

**Like the man you just killed.** Al-Muyassar makes it explicit: *just as you, in the beginning of Islam, used to hide your faith from your idol-worshipping people — then Allah favored you and made you strong with faith.* There was a day — in Makkah, under threat — when *you* were the lone believer in a hostile crowd. When *you* whispered your faith and hid it, when *you* would have flung a greeting across a gap and prayed the armed men would believe you.

The man on the hillside, the solitary believer the soldiers couldn't read — **he is a photograph of their own past.** And they killed their own beginning.

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest medicine the Quran offers against contempt: **memory.** Almost every harsh judgment we pass on another person depends on a kind of amnesia — a forgetting that we were once where they are. We despise the struggling because we have forgotten our own struggle. We are impatient with the new, the weak, the wavering, because the favor that carried *us* past that stage now feels like something we always had, something we earned, something that proves we are a different species than the people still down there.

Allah destroys that amnesia in three words. *Kadhālika kuntum.* You were like that.

And then the gentlest word in the verse: **fa-manna-llāhu ʿalaykum** — *then Allah conferred favor upon you.* The root is *m-n-n,* which is *minnah* — an unearned gift, a grace bestowed freely. He does not say *"then you became worthy."* He does not say *"then you earned your faith."* He says *Allah favored you.* **Your faith — the very thing you were about to use as a weapon to declare him faithless — was never your achievement. It was a gift. It was handed to you the same way it was being handed to him.**

Do you feel the full circle? You stood over a man and said *lasta mu'minan,* "you are no believer," as if faith were a credential you possessed and he lacked. And Allah says: the faith you're so proud of, the faith in whose name you're killing — *I gave it to you.* As a gift. When you were just like him. **How do you sneer at a beggar for being hungry while you eat bread that was placed in your hands?**

[PAUSE]

And then — only now — the second *tabayyanū.* Now you understand why the command had to be said twice. The first time, "seek clarity" was a rule for a soldier. The second time, after *kadhālika kuntum,* after the mirror and the memory and the gift — "seek clarity" means *look at him and see yourself. Look at him and see the favor you were given. Look slowly, the way you would want to be looked at on your worst hidden day.*

**What changes tomorrow:** before you write someone off — the struggling believer, the person whose practice looks thin, the one whose sincerity you doubt — finish this sentence honestly: *"I, too, was once ______."* Name the version of yourself that needed mercy and got it for free. You cannot hold contempt and that memory in the same hand. One of them always drops.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture — What Was Hidden in the Frame

Step back now and see the whole shape, because the verse is built like a chamber that closes around you.

It **opens** by calling you *alladhīna āmanū* — "O you who *believe.*" Your faith is the first thing named.

The **crime** at the center is saying to another *lasta mu'minan* — "you are not a *believer.*" Denying his faith.

And the verse **closes** on the name **khabīr** — *innallāha kāna bimā taʿmalūna khabīrā* — "Allah is ever, of what you do, *Aware.*" *Khabīr,* from *kh-b-r,* is the One who knows the inner reality of things, the hidden grain beneath the surface.

[PAUSE]

Look at what that frame does. **You** were addressed by your faith. **You** dared to rule on the absence of his faith. And the verse seals shut on the reminder that there is only One who can actually read the contents of a heart — and it is not you. You said *lasta mu'minan,* "I know what you are," and Allah answers, across the whole verse, **khabīr** — *I am the only One who knows what anyone is.* The presumption to read a heart was always a theft of a name that belongs to God alone.

The narrations preserved with this verse give that truth a face. When a Companion killed a man who had professed faith — excusing himself with the very same reasoning as the soldiers, *he only said it out of fear of the sword* — the Prophet ﷺ pressed him with a question that has never stopped echoing: *did you split open his heart, that you should know what was inside it?* No one had. No one could. That is exactly the wall the name *khabīr* names. The inside of a chest is sealed to every created eye; only its Maker reads it. To pronounce *lasta mu'minan* is to claim you have done the one thing the Prophet ﷺ said could not be done — opened a heart and looked in. The greeting is treated, in the words of the commentators, as a *sign* of Islam precisely because the sign is all you are ever given. The thing the sign points to is God's to see.

And between the opening and the closing sits the mirror — *kadhālika kuntum* — so that the very faith you were named by in the first word is revealed, in the middle, as a gift you were given when you were no different from the man you condemned.

## Two Roads to One Truth

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery approached from two sides.

The words showed us: a strike-posture (*ḍarabtum*) that turns everyone into a target, a peace flung across a gap (*alqā*) and let fall, a murder committed for *ghanam* identical in its very letters to the *maghānim* God was offering for free, and a command to seek clarity (*tabayyanū*) repeated on either side of a softened heart.

The themes showed us: a verdict authored by desire, and a contempt cured only by memory.

They meet in one place. **You cannot see another person's heart. You can only see your own want. So when you find yourself certain about someone's inner state, you have learned nothing about them — and everything about what you are hungry for.**

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I called something *discernment* that was really *appetite* wearing a disguise?
2. Who have I quietly filed under "not sincere," "not really practicing," "not one of us" — and what do I gain from that file staying closed?
3. When I think of my own faith, do I experience it as something I *achieved* — or as *minnah,* a gift placed in empty hands?
4. Is there a person in front of me right now who is *kadhālika kuntum* — exactly where I once was? And am I treating them the way I was treated, or the way the soldiers treated the man with the sheep?
5. If the only true *Khabīr* — the only Knower of hearts — is Allah, how much of my certainty about other people is a name I have stolen?

## The Distillation

*Do not let your hunger pass a verdict on another person's heart — because the faith you would condemn him for lacking was a gift you yourself were given when you were exactly where he stands now.*

## The Invitation

Nothing here asks you to become gullible, to trust everyone, to switch off your judgment. The verse never says *believe him.* It says *tabayyanū* — slow down long enough for the fog of your own wanting to lift. That is all. The shift this verse invites is small and total: **a pause between the desire and the verdict.** One breath in which you ask whether you are seeing a person, or seeing a thing you want. In that single breath lives the entire difference between the soldier and the saint.

## A Closing Du'a

*O Allah, You are al-Khabīr, the only One who reads what is hidden in any chest — so do not let me steal that throne. When my wanting begins to write a story about someone's heart, halt my tongue before it says* lasta mu'minan. *Remind me, in that moment, that I was once* kadhālika — *just like them — and that the faith I carry was never my wage but Your gift, Your* minnah, *placed in my empty hands. Make me one who casts peace across the gap, and one who catches it when it is cast to me. Āmīn.*
