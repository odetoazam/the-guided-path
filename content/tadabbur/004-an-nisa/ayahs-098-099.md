# Except Those Who Could Not

## The Anatomy of Helplessness and the "Perhaps" of God

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Door That Was Just Slammed

Let me give you the ayahs first, and then let me put you in the room where they were revealed.

> إِلَّا ٱلْمُسْتَضْعَفِينَ مِنَ ٱلرِّجَالِ وَٱلنِّسَآءِ وَٱلْوِلْدَٰنِ لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ حِيلَةً وَلَا يَهْتَدُونَ سَبِيلًا
>
> فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ عَسَى ٱللَّهُ أَن يَعْفُوَ عَنْهُمْ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَفُوًّا غَفُورًا

*Illā l-mustaḍʿafīna mina r-rijāli wa-n-nisāʾi wa-l-wildāni lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan wa-lā yahtadūna sabīlā. Fa-ulāʾika ʿasā llāhu an yaʿfuwa ʿanhum, wa-kāna llāhu ʿafuwwan ghafūrā.*

"Except for the oppressed among men, women, and children who cannot devise a way out, nor are they guided to any road — for those, perhaps Allah will pardon them. And Allah is ever Pardoning, Forgiving."

Now — that word **"except."** *Illā.* You don't begin a sentence with "except." An exception is carved out of something. So before we can stand inside this ayah, we have to feel the wall it was cut into.

### Rebuild the world

Picture Makkah in the years before the migration. A small community of believers, surrounded, watched, pressured. Some of them found a way to leave — to make the journey to Madinah, to a place where they could pray without hiding, where being Muslim did not mean being broken down. That journey, that *hijra*, was the great test of the moment.

But some stayed. And among those who stayed were people who **could have gone and didn't** — they had the means, they had the strength, they had the road, but they were too comfortable, too attached, too unwilling to lose what they had built. They told themselves a story: *we were weak, we were stuck, what could we have done?*

The ayah right before ours — verse 97 — does something almost frightening. It shows you the moment of death for exactly those people. The angels come to take the souls of those who *ẓalamū anfusahum* — who wronged their own selves. And the angels ask one question: **"Fīma kuntum?"** — "What were you in? What state were you in?" And the people answer with the exact excuse they had rehearsed their whole lives: *"Kunnā mustaḍʿafīna fī l-arḍ"* — "We were weak, we were oppressed, in the land."

And the angels reply with a question that closes every exit: *"A lam takun arḍu llāhi wāsiʿatan fa-tuhājirū fīhā?"* — "Was not Allah's earth spacious enough for you to emigrate in it?" [PAUSE]

And then: *fa-ulāʾika maʾwāhum jahannam, wa sāʾat maṣīrā.* "Their refuge is Hell — and what an evil destination."

That is the wall. That is the door slammed shut. The excuse "I was weak" was placed on trial and rejected.

### The Hook

And then — *immediately*, in the very next breath — Allah says: **"Except."** *Illā l-mustaḍʿafīn.* "Except the ones who were truly weak."

Stay with the strangeness of this. The people in verse 97 *also* called themselves *mustaḍʿafīn* — weak, oppressed. They used the exact same word. And their claim was thrown out. Now verse 98 uses that same word — *mustaḍʿafīn* — and accepts it, and opens mercy for it.

Same word. Opposite verdict.

So the entire weight of these two ayahs hangs on a single question: **What is the difference between the person who only *says* "I couldn't" — and the person who genuinely could not?** Who gets to claim weakness? And who decides?

That is where we are going. Let's walk in slowly.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *al-Mustaḍʿafīn* — The Word That Locates the Blame

Before we touch the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs are *shaped*.

Arabic builds meaning not just from roots, but from the *pattern* you pour the root into. Take any three-letter root and you can stretch it into different "forms," and each form does a specific job. One of those — the grammarians call it **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern, the one that starts with the sound *ista-* — has a particular flavor. Often it means **to seek** something (*istaghfara* = to *seek* forgiveness). And just as often it means **to deem, to count, to treat something as** a certain way (*istaḥsana* = to *consider* something good; *istaṣghara* = to *treat* something as small).

Hold that: Form X can mean *to treat someone as X.* Not that they *are* X — that someone *decided to treat them* as X.

Now the word.

The root is *ḍād–ʿayn–fāʾ* — **ḌʿF** — and on its own it simply means *weakness*. If Allah had wanted to say "the weak people," there is a ready, simple word for that: *ḍuʿafāʾ*. The naturally weak. The frail by constitution.

But that is **not** the word He chose.

He chose **al-mustaḍʿafīn.** Form X. Pour the root for "weakness" into the *ista-* pattern, and you do not get *"the weak."* You get **"those who were *made* weak. Those who were *treated* as weak. Those whom the powerful *counted* as nothing and ground down."**

Let that land. [PAUSE]

The weakness in this word is **not a property of the person. It is something done *to* them.** The grammar itself points a finger away from the victim and toward the oppressor. A *ḍaʿīf* is someone who happens to be weak. A *mustaḍʿaf* is someone who was **weakened** — there is a hand pressing down on him in the very structure of the word. And the commentators describe these very people in exactly those terms: as those who *cannot repel the coercion and the injustice forced upon them* — *lā yaqdirūna ʿalā dafʿi l-qahri wa-ẓ-ẓulmi ʿanhum.* That word *qahr* — being forcibly overpowered, dominated, held down — is the same picture the morphology paints: not a weakness chosen, but a domination *imposed*, and impossible to push off.

Here is what is remarkable. Notice what this does to the false claimant of verse 97. When the comfortable man who could have emigrated says *"kunnā mustaḍʿafīn,"* he is not even using an accurate word for himself. He is reaching for the vocabulary of the genuinely crushed and draping it over his own choices. He calls himself "one who was forced down" when in fact no hand was on him. The word he steals is the very word that exposes him — because a *mustaḍʿaf*, by the meaning of the form, is someone with a *force on top of them.* The man who had a clear road and a full purse had no such force.

And now feel the *sound* of the word. Say it: *al-mus-taḍ-ʿa-fīn.* It is heavy. It is clustered and effortful in the mouth, and at its center sits the *ḍād* — one of the heaviest, most pressed-down letters in the entire Arabic sound-system, a letter pronounced by flattening the tongue against the roof of the mouth under pressure. The word *sounds* like a weight bearing down. The very act of saying it is laborious — as the lives it describes were laborious. The texture of the word *is* the burden it names.

So before Allah tells us anything about mercy, He has already, in one word, done two things: He has **located the cause of this weakness outside the person** — and He has **disqualified the impostor** who borrows the language of the oppressed without the reality of it.

One word. *Al-mustaḍʿafīn.* [PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Two Negations — *Lā Yastaṭīʿūna Ḥīlatan wa-lā Yahtadūna Sabīlā*

But Allah does not leave "weak" undefined. Because "I'm weak" is exactly the slippery claim that needs pinning down. So He follows the word with a precise, two-part X-ray of what real helplessness actually looks like.

Watch how surgically He does it. He gives **two** negations, not one. And they are not the same negation repeated for emphasis — they name two completely different things a trapped person lacks.

**The first:** *lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan* — "they have no *ḥīla.*"

That word *ḥīla* — root *ḤWL*, the same root as *ḥawl*, meaning to *turn*, to *shift*, to *maneuver* — is the **inner** resource. A *ḥīla* is a clever turn, a stratagem, a contrivance, the resourcefulness to *engineer* a way out of a tight spot. It is the human capacity to scheme an escape. (And notice — it is the very *ḥawl* we deny ourselves in *lā ḥawla wa lā quwwata illā billāh*: "there is no power and no strength except by Allah.") The *mustaḍʿaf* has no *ḥawl* of his own. No inner device. No mental or material means to plot the road out.

Al-Qurtubī notes that *ḥīla* here is a deliberately **general** word — it covers *every kind* of means of deliverance. Not one specific trick is denied them. *Every* avenue of self-rescue is closed.

Other commentators read it more concretely. Al-Jalālayn glosses *lā yastaṭīʿūna ḥīlatan* as having *no strength* for the journey and *no provision* to fund it — *lā quwwata lahum ʿalā l-hijra wa-lā nafaqa.* So the classical readings range from the most general (no means of any kind, al-Qurtubī) to the most concrete (no bodily strength and no money in hand, al-Jalālayn) — and the word is spacious enough to hold both. What every reading shares is the bare fact the morphology already insisted on: the means is not withheld by choice; it is simply *absent.*

**The second:** *wa-lā yahtadūna sabīlā* — "nor are they guided to a *sabīl.*"

The verb *yahtadūn* is Form VIII — *ihtadā* — "to find one's way, to be guided onto a path." And *sabīl* — root *SBL* — is the **outer** thing: the actual road. The physical route. Mujāhid, as Imam al-Baghawī and al-Qurtubī both record, took *sabīl* here to mean the literal road to Madinah — they didn't even know the way. Al-Jalālayn reads it exactly so: *ṭarīqan ilā arḍi l-hijra,* "a road to the land of emigration." Al-Qurtubī then widens it: the truest reading is that it means *any* path at all.

Now put the two together and see the precision. [PAUSE]

To escape a trap, a human being needs **two** things: the **inner means** to figure out how (*ḥīla*), and an **outer road** to actually walk (*sabīl*). Allah did not simply say "those who can't leave." He took apart the act of escape into its two components — the resourcefulness *and* the route — and declared **both** of them absent. No device *and* no door. They cannot think their way out, and even if they could, there is no road beneath their feet.

And one more thing, easy to miss. Both *ḥīlatan* and *sabīlan* are **indefinite** — *ḥīla*-tan, *sabīl*-an, with that final *-an* that in Arabic, under a negation, means *total generality.* He did not say they lack *the* particular plan or *the* particular road. He said they lack **any** plan, **any** road — *not a single one.* The negation is swept clean to the floor.

This is why the impostor of verse 97 cannot slip through. The bar Allah sets for genuine *mustaḍʿaf* status is **astonishingly high.** You must have *no* inner resource *whatsoever* and *no* outer path *whatsoever.* The man with a camel and a clear road to Madinah fails on both counts. The widow with small children, no money, no protection, and no knowledge even of which direction the city lies — she fails on neither. She *is* the exception.

Allah did not define weakness loosely so that anyone could claim it. He defined it with a jeweler's precision — two absences, both total — so that only the truly trapped would fit. [PAUSE]

### Element Three: *ʿAsā Allāhu* — When the All-Knowing Says "Perhaps"

Now we arrive at the word that should stop us in our tracks.

We have established who these people are: the genuinely crushed, with no means and no road. Surely now — *now* — comes the guarantee. Surely Allah says: *these I will certainly forgive.* They are blameless; they were trapped; justice itself demands their pardon.

Read what He actually says: *fa-ulāʾika **ʿasā** llāhu an yaʿfuwa ʿanhum.* "For those — **perhaps** Allah will pardon them."

***Perhaps.*** [PAUSE]

Sit with how strange that is. *ʿAsā* is the Arabic word of hope, of expectation, of "maybe, it could be." It is the word a human being uses precisely *because* a human being does not know the outcome. I say "perhaps it will rain" because I cannot command the sky. But this is **Allah** speaking — the One who already knows, with total certainty, exactly what He will do with every one of these souls. He does not wonder. He does not hope. Why, then, would the One who knows the end from the beginning phrase His own decision as *"perhaps"*?

Now, the classical commentators give us one key: they note that when *ʿasā* — "perhaps" — comes **from Allah**, it carries the force of a *realized* thing, a *taḥqīq*. When the Lord of the worlds says "perhaps," the servant may take it as good as done. So in one sense, the mercy is firm.

But that only deepens the question. If He *means* it as certain — why *say* it as "perhaps"? He could have said *sa-yaʿfū* — "He **will** pardon." He chose the softer, suspended word on purpose. What is the wisdom in dressing a certainty in the clothing of a maybe?

Here is what this invites us to consider. Three things, and they all converge.

**First** — the "perhaps" keeps the heart suspended between fear and hope, even for the excused. The moment a servant hears "you are *guaranteed* forgiven," something in the soul relaxes its grip, grows casual, begins to presume. Allah will not give even His most legitimately excused servants a certificate they can wave around. He keeps them *looking up,* hands still open, eyes still raised.

**Second** — and this is the heart of it — the "perhaps" teaches that pardon is **never owed.** Think about the logic the trapped person might fall into: *I had no choice, therefore I am owed acquittal. My innocence obligates God.* And Allah, with one word, gently dissolves that entire frame. Even you — even the blameless, even the one with no means and no road — **even your pardon is a gift, not a wage.** It is not extracted from Allah by your circumstances. It descends from Allah by His grace. *ʿAsā* strips away every trace of entitlement, even the most reasonable-sounding kind, and returns the whole thing to where it belongs: pure, unowed, divine generosity.

**Third** — for the one trembling, the "perhaps" is not cold. It is the opening of a door that was, just one verse ago, slammed shut. Coming directly after *"their refuge is Hell, what an evil destination,"* the word *"perhaps Allah will pardon"* is not a withholding — it is a **rescue.** In context, *ʿasā* reads as the warmest possible word: a lifeline thrown to exactly the people the world forgot.

And then — to make sure the trembling heart does not mistake the "perhaps" for stinginess — Allah seals the two ayahs with two of His own names: **ʿafuwwan ghafūrā.**

These are not synonyms padded together. *ʿAfw* — its root sense is **to efface, to wipe out** until no trace remains; the same root carries the idea of something growing *abundant,* given freely and in plenty. *Ghafr* — its root means **to cover, to conceal**; from the same root comes *mighfar,* the helmet that covers and shields the head. So in two words Allah names two distinct mercies: He **erases** the sin as though it were never written — *and* He **covers** it so it is never exposed. Effacement *and* concealment. He removes the stain, and He draws a veil over the place where it was.

And both come in the intensive pattern — *ʿafuww,* not merely *ʿāfin*; *ghafūr,* not merely *ghāfir* — the form Arabic uses for *much, again and again, by His very nature.* This is not a one-time act He might perform. It is **who He is.** *Wa kāna llāhu* — "And Allah has *ever* been" — *ʿafuwwan ghafūrā.* [PAUSE]

So the "perhaps" is held inside the names. The suspense of *ʿasā* rests in the lap of *al-ʿAfuww, al-Ghafūr.* You are kept humble by the maybe — and reassured by the One making it.

### Bridge

See what these three words, working together, have built. *Al-mustaḍʿafīn* told us the weakness was done *to* them, not chosen *by* them — and disqualified every impostor. The two negations defined that weakness with surgical exactness, so high a bar that only the truly trapped could meet it. And *ʿasā* — "perhaps" — refused to let even those rescued souls walk away thinking they had *earned* their rescue.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how mercy and justice actually *work,* and about the stories we tell ourselves to survive.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Excuse — Who Gets to Say "I Couldn't"

Let me start somewhere you already live.

Every one of us has a defense attorney living in our chest. Its entire job is to explain why the thing we didn't do — wasn't our fault. *I couldn't call her back, I was so busy. I couldn't pray on time, the day got away from me. I couldn't stand up for what was right, what could I have done, I'm just one person.* The phrase is always the same, and it is always reassuring: **"I had no choice."**

Psychologists have a name for one version of this — *learned helplessness.* You train a person, through repeated experience, to believe that nothing they do matters, and eventually they stop trying *even when escape becomes possible.* The cage door opens and they sit still, because somewhere inside, "I can't" has hardened into an identity. And here is the trap within the trap: from the *inside,* learned helplessness feels **exactly like** real helplessness. The person who *won't* and the person who *can't* both say, with total sincerity, "I had no choice."

Now look at what verses 97 and 98 are doing. They place these two people **side by side** — and they use the **same word for both.** The man in verse 97 says *kunnā mustaḍʿafīn.* The woman in verse 98 *is mustaḍʿafa.* Same word. From the *outside,* from any human courtroom, you could not tell them apart. Both pleas sound identical.

And that is precisely the point that should both terrify us and comfort us.

**It should comfort us** — because it means there exists a Judge who can tell the difference. No human tribunal can reliably separate the genuinely crushed from the merely unwilling; the words are the same, the tears can be the same. But Allah does not judge by the plea. He judges by the reality *behind* the plea — and verse 98 shows Him doing exactly that, reaching *past* the identical words to the truth underneath, and ruling: *this* one was truly trapped. The commentators put their finger on precisely this when they explain *why* pardon is held out to these and withheld from the others: it is *li-ʿilmihi bi-ḥaqīqati amrihim* — "because of His knowledge of the **reality** of their affair." Not the reality of their *words* — anyone can produce the words. The reality of their *state.* Which means: **no real powerlessness of yours ever goes unseen.** The single mother nobody believed, the abused child no one protected, the worker with no leverage and no exit — the world may dismiss their helplessness as an excuse, but the One who shaped the word *mustaḍʿaf* to mean *"a force is pressing on them"* sees the force, and sees that they could not move it. Your genuine *"I couldn't"* is registered in the highest court with perfect accuracy.

**And it should terrify us** — gently, usefully — because the same precision that vindicates the truly trapped *exposes* the one who is only borrowing their language. I can fool every human around me with "I had no choice." I can half-fool *myself.* I cannot fool the One who can tell, to a certainty, whether there was a *ḥīla* I refused to use, a *sabīl* I declined to walk. The two negations of verse 98 are, quietly, a test I will one day be measured against: *Did you really have no means? Was there truly no road?* [PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow? It asks us to retire the defense attorney for one honest hour and audit our "I couldn'ts." Take the recurring one — the prayer you keep missing, the relationship you keep neglecting, the wrong you keep staying silent about — and ask the two questions Allah asks: *Is there genuinely no ḥīla — no means within my reach? Is there genuinely no sabīl — no road I could take?* Most of the time, if we are honest, we will find we are closer to verse 97 than to verse 98 — we *have* a road; we simply prefer the comfort of staying. And the mercy of realizing that **now** is that the door of *ʿafuww* and *ghafr* is still open to us, in this life, where excuses can still be exchanged for action.

### Theme Two: "Perhaps" — Living Between the Bill and the Gift

Here is a second place you already live.

There are two completely different ways to receive something good. One is the way you receive a **paycheck.** You worked; you are owed; the money is *yours;* when it arrives you feel not gratitude but *settlement* — "finally, what I'm due." The other is the way you receive an unexpected **gift** — something you did not earn and could not demand, handed to you freely. The paycheck produces entitlement. The gift produces gratitude. And the difference between a heart that lives in the first mode and a heart that lives in the second is, quite literally, the difference between a miserable life and a thankful one.

Now watch where Allah places the rescued souls of verse 98 — the *blameless* ones, the ones with the most airtight excuse imaginable. If anyone ever had grounds to receive forgiveness as a *paycheck* — as something *owed* to their innocence — it is these people. *I was trapped, I had no means, no road; my acquittal is simply justice.*

And Allah will not let even *them* file the invoice. *ʿAsā* — "perhaps." [PAUSE]

Feel the precision of that mercy. He is not denying them the gift. He is refusing to let them mistake it for a wage. Because the moment forgiveness becomes something *owed,* it stops being mercy at all — a wage paid is not generosity, it is obligation discharged. By keeping it at *"perhaps,"* Allah keeps it a **gift** — and in doing so He gives them something *better* than a guarantee. He gives them a relationship in which they remain forever the recipients of grace rather than the collectors of a debt.

This is the secret architecture of the believer's heart that the scholars call living *between fear and hope* — *bayna l-khawf wa-r-rajāʾ.* The "perhaps" of *ʿasā* is the engineering that holds those two in balance. Lean fully into certainty of pardon, and the soul grows lazy and presumptuous — it has its receipt, why strive? Lean fully into fear of doom, and the soul collapses into despair — and despair, the Qur'an warns elsewhere, is itself a kind of disbelief in His mercy. The single word *"perhaps,"* wrapped inside the two names *al-ʿAfuww, al-Ghafūr,* holds the heart in the only posture that keeps it alive: **hoping hard, never presuming; fearing truly, never despairing.** Hands open, eyes up.

And notice the tenderness of the order. He could have ended on the suspense — *"perhaps Allah will pardon them"* — and left the heart dangling. He did not. He followed the maybe with the certainty of *who He is:* *wa kāna llāhu ʿafuwwan ghafūrā* — "and Allah has **ever** been Pardoning, Forgiving." The outcome for *you* is held at "perhaps"; the *nature* of the One deciding is fixed at "ever-Pardoning." So you are kept humble about your case — and absolutely reassured about your Judge. [PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes how you ask. Watch yourself the next time you make duʿāʾ for forgiveness, and notice whether you are presenting a *bill* — *I prayed, I fasted, I held back from that sin, so I'm owed* — or extending an *open hand* — *I have nothing that obligates You; I come only because You have ever been al-ʿAfuww.* The first is the posture of verse 97, the man who thought his circumstances entitled him. The second is the posture verse 99 trains into us. Ask as one receiving a gift, never as one cashing a check — and you will find both your humility and your gratitude grow in the same breath.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole passage — because the three verses, 97 through 99, are built as a single descending-then-rising movement, and the pivot is one tiny word.

Verse 97 **falls.** It moves from the self-wrongers, to the angels' interrogation, to the rejected excuse, down to the lowest point in the passage: *maʾwāhum jahannam, wa sāʾat maṣīrā* — "their refuge is Hell, and what an evil destination." The floor.

Then comes the hinge: **إِلَّا** — *illā,* "except." A single exceptive particle, and the entire trajectory bends.

Verse 98 carves out the exception and, crucially, **names** who stands in it: *ar-rijāl, wa-n-nisāʾ, wa-l-wildān* — the men, and **the women, and the children.** Remember which surah we are in: *An-Nisāʾ,* "The Women." In the surah named for the vulnerable, the exception to the verdict of doom explicitly gathers up the most vulnerable — the women, the children, the powerless — and shelters them by name.

And verse 99 **rises** back up, all the way to the ceiling: *ʿasā llāhu an yaʿfuwa ʿanhum, wa kāna llāhu ʿafuwwan ghafūrā* — from "Hell, an evil destination" to "Pardoning, Forgiving."

So the passage is bracketed: the **worst possible end** on one side (*sāʾa maṣīrā*), the **two names of mercy** on the other (*ʿafuwwan ghafūrā*) — and standing in between, on the hinge of *"except,"* are the truly crushed, the ones whose genuine helplessness was enough to swing the whole arc from doom to grace.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the same insight seen from two directions.

The word *al-mustaḍʿafīn* and the two negations are the **machinery of a perfectly precise mercy** — a mercy exacting enough to tell the trapped from the merely unwilling, generous enough to shelter the truly trapped by name. And *ʿasā* — "perhaps" — is the **guardrail on that mercy** that keeps even the rescued from ever mistaking it for a wage. Precision and humility. He sees *exactly* who could not — *and* He gives even them their pardon as a gift, never a debt. The same God who is too just to be fooled by a false "I couldn't" is too generous to be reduced to a creditor by a true one.

### Questions to Carry

1. Which of my recurring "I couldn'ts" would survive Allah's two questions — *was there truly no means, and truly no road* — and which are really "I wouldn'ts" wearing a borrowed word?
2. When I ask Allah for forgiveness, am I presenting a bill or extending an open hand?
3. Whose genuine helplessness around me does the world dismiss as an excuse — and how would I treat them differently if I believed, as this ayah says, that Allah sees the *force pressing on them*?
4. Can I hold "perhaps" without sliding into either presumption or despair — can I live with hands open and eyes up?
5. If even the blameless receive "perhaps," what does that dissolve in me about everything I think I am *owed* by God?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you genuinely cannot — no inner means, no outer road — the only One who can tell the trapped from the merely unwilling leans His effacing, concealing mercy toward you, yet still says *"perhaps,"* so that even the rescued never mistake grace for a wage.

### The Closing Invitation

Let two things shift. First, retire the defense attorney for the cases where you do, in fact, have a *ḥīla* and a *sabīl* — and walk the road while the door of *al-ʿAfuww* is still open to you. Second, when you turn to Him with the cases where you truly had no road, come not as a creditor but as a guest at a table you did not set — and let the relief of being *seen* by the One who knows the difference be enough.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who have ever been al-ʿAfuww, al-Ghafūr — You see the force pressing on every crushed soul, and You are not fooled by the excuses of the comfortable. Where I am truly weak, shelter me as You sheltered the men, the women, and the children of this ayah. And where I only call myself weak, give me the honesty to find the road and the strength to walk it — before the door of "perhaps" becomes the door of certainty.* Āmīn.
