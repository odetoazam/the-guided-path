# Some of the Deeds, None of the Reward

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first, and then let me tell you what room we are standing in when it arrives.

**وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ مِن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَأُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ يَدْخُلُونَ ٱلْجَنَّةَ وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًا**

*Wa man yaʿmal mina ṣ-ṣāliḥāti min dhakarin aw unthā wa huwa muʾminun fa-ulāʾika yadkhulūna l-jannata wa lā yuẓlamūna naqīrā.*

"And whoever does righteous deeds — whether male or female — while being a believer, those will enter Paradise, and they will not be wronged so much as the speck on a date-seed."

### Rebuild the room

To feel this verse you have to hear the argument it walked into.

Just one verse earlier, in 4:123, something sharp had been said. There were communities in Madinah and around it who carried a certain confidence about the next life — and that confidence was built not on what they *did* but on who they *were*. The People of the Book, some of them, said of themselves what the Qur'an records elsewhere: *naḥnu abnāʾu Llāhi wa aḥibbāʾuh* — "we are the sons of God and His beloved ones." The lineage was the ticket. The membership was the guarantee.

And on the other side, the Quraysh of Makkah had their own version. Al-Qurtubi tells us plainly: they leaned on their service of the Kaʿba — they fed the pilgrims, they gave water, they hosted the guests of the sacred house, they kept the rituals running. Surely, they reasoned, *that* counts. Surely the caretakers of the holy place have their seat reserved.

Two different communities. The same disease. Both believed that **belonging was salvation.** That identity, inheritance, and institutional role were enough — that you could be saved by your résumé.

And then 4:123 lands like cold water: *laysa bi-amāniyyikum wa lā amāniyyi ahli l-kitāb* — "It will not be by your wishes, nor the wishes of the People of the Book. Whoever does evil will be recompensed for it." The wishful thinking is cut off at the root. No lineage. No title. No inherited seat.

Now imagine the listener at that moment. The floor has just dropped out. If it is not my people, not my pedigree, not my place in the sacred order — then *what?* What is left? Is the door now impossibly narrow? Have I just been told that almost no one gets in?

[PAUSE]

And into that fear — that exact fear — comes verse 124. It is the answer to the panic that 123 created. And the astonishing thing about the answer is its *shape*. Because in the same breath, this one verse does two opposite-seeming things at once. It makes the entrance **wider than you feared** — and the accounting **finer than you imagined.**

### The Hook

There are two small words in this verse that most translations cannot carry. One of them quietly lowers the bar at the door so that you — ordinary, inconsistent, failing-and-trying you — can walk through. The other raises the precision of the reward to a resolution so fine that not one unseen act of yours could ever be lost.

Watch how Allah, in a single sentence answering the proud, manages to be more generous in what He *asks* and more exact in what He *gives* than anyone in that room expected.

Let's go slowly.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The little word *min* — "some of"

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you one feature of Arabic, because the whole first half of this ayah turns on it.

In Arabic there is a particle, **مِن** (*min*), that usually we translate as "from" or "of." But *min* has a quiet second job. Grammarians call it **min al-tabʿīḍiyya** — the *min* of partition. The *min* that means "some of," "a portion of," "part of the whole."

Here is an everyday example. If I say in English, "He ate the cake," you picture the cake gone. But if I say, "He ate *of* the cake," you picture a slice, a portion — some, not all. That little "of" changes the whole scale of the sentence. It turns a totality into a sample.

Now hold that, and look at what Allah actually says. We tend to translate the opening as "Whoever does righteous deeds." But the Arabic is:

**وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ**

*Wa man yaʿmal **min** aṣ-ṣāliḥāt.*

"And whoever does **of** the righteous deeds." Whoever does **some** of the righteous deeds.

Let's sit with this.

Allah did not say *wa man yaʿmal aṣ-ṣāliḥāt* — "whoever does the righteous deeds," the whole category, the full set. He did not say *whoever performs all righteousness.* He placed a partitive *min* at the door. **Whoever does even a portion of the good.**

Notice something. The verse arrives right after the door of false hope was slammed shut. You might have braced for the new condition to be crushing — *now you must be perfect, now you must complete the entire ledger of good.* And instead the very first move Allah makes is to put a *min* of partition in front of the deeds. The standard is not totality. The standard is *participation in the good.*

Here is what is remarkable. The same grammar that demolished the lazy hope of the boastful — *it's not by your wishes* — refuses to replace it with despair. It does not swing from "you're saved by birth" to "you're saved only by flawless perfection." It lands somewhere far more merciful and far more serious: bring *some* genuine good, rooted in faith, and you are not turned away.

Think about who this rescues. The person who prays but misses prayers. The person who gives charity but not always. The one whose record is patchy, streaked with failure, a portion of light inside a lot of ordinary gray. The partitive *min* is Allah saying: **I am not waiting for the complete set. I am looking for the real portion.**

And there is a discipline in it too — because *min al-ṣāliḥāt* still means *of the righteous deeds.* It must be a portion *of the good*, genuine good, not a portion of anything you please. The door is wide, but it opens onto *righteousness*. Wide — but not arbitrary.

One word. *Min.* And the entrance just became survivable for a human being.

### Element Two: *dhakarin aw unthā* — and the one condition that is not optional

Now watch what Allah does next, because He immediately closes off the very loophole the proud had been standing in.

**مِن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ**

"…whether male (*dhakar*) or female (*unthā*), while he is a believer (*muʾmin*)."

Let me teach the rhetorical move first. In Arabic, as in any language, you can establish a rule and then immediately specify *who it applies to.* And the way you specify tells the listener what you were worried they'd assume. When Allah explicitly says "male or female," He is reaching out to pre-empt an assumption — the assumption that the spiritual economy is gendered, that one half of humanity earns and the other half merely belongs to the earners.

Read the absence here as carefully as the presence. The boasters of 4:123 traded in *categories of belonging* — the right lineage, the right tribe, the right custodial role. And Allah answers category-pride by naming the two most basic human categories there are — *dhakar, unthā* — and flattening any difference between them at the door of Paradise. Not "the noble or the common." Not "the Arab or the rest." Not "the custodian or the outsider." **Male or female** — the one division that runs through *every* family, tribe, and nation — and then: *it makes no difference to the reward.*

Now notice the asymmetry that *is* there, the one thing Allah did *not* make optional.

He let the *deeds* be partial — *min* the righteous deeds. He let the *gender* be either — male or female. He let the quantity flex and the category flex. But there is exactly one word with no flexibility around it, no *min*, no *aw*, no "either-or":

**وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ** — "**while** he is a believer."

This is the hinge of the whole verse, and it is the precise answer to the room. The root of *muʾmin* is **أمن** (*amn*) — security, safety, trust. The same root from which *amān* (safe-conduct) and *amāna* (trust) come. To be a *muʾmin* is to be one who has entered into security with Allah — who trusts Him and is, in turn, made safe. Al-Qurtubi draws the line exactly here: the condition of *īmān* is stated *because* the polytheists were pointing to their service of the Kaʿba and their feeding of pilgrims, and the People of the Book to their ancestry — and Allah is declaring that **beautiful deeds are not accepted without faith.**

And al-Tabari fills in what that faith concretely *is*, which sharpens the confrontation even further. The believer the verse describes, he explains, is one who is "believing in Me and in My Messenger Muhammad, affirming My oneness and the prophethood of Muhammad ﷺ and what he brought from Me." That is not a vague spiritual warmth — it is a specific content: *tawḥīd*, and the acknowledgment of the Messenger. And notice how exactly that lands in this room. The polytheists were not merely lineage-proud; they *denied the Messenger.* The People of the Book leaned on ancestry instead of affirming what was newly revealed. Al-Muyassar names this second edge plainly — the believer is one who believes "in Allah and in what was revealed of truth." So *īmān* here is not a feeling that anyone in the room could claim; it is precisely the thing the boasters were refusing. Al-Tabari even reads the verse as Allah turning to them directly: *do not covet* what is reserved for those who actually believe. The door is wide, but it is not open to those still standing outside the one truth that gives a deed its life.

Stay with that. The Quraysh genuinely *did* feed the hungry pilgrim. That is, on its face, a *ṣāliḥa*, a good deed. The verse does not deny the action happened. It says the action, severed from faith, has no root to draw from. The deed without *īmān* is a cut flower — real, even beautiful, for a moment — but with nothing underneath it to keep it alive.

So look at the architecture of the conditions Allah set:
- The *amount* of good — flexible. *Min* the righteous deeds.
- The *gender* of the doer — irrelevant. Male or female.
- The *faith* of the heart — **non-negotiable.**

He was generous everywhere He could be generous, and immovable on the one thing that gives the deed its direction. Because a good deed is an arrow, and *īmān* is what points it at God.

### Element Three: *naqīran* — the speck on the date-seed

Now we arrive at the word that I want you to carry out of this reflection. It is the final word of the verse, and it is doing something almost no other word could do.

**وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًا**

"…and they will not be wronged a *naqīr*."

What is a *naqīr*? Here is where you need to hold a date-seed in your mind. Take the hard pit of a date, the stone in the center. Turn it over. On the back of that seed there is a tiny dimple — a small pit, a little notch, a speck-sized hollow. *That* is the *naqīr*. Al-Baghawi defines it for us precisely: *al-nuqra allatī takūnu fī ẓahri n-nawāh* — "the little pit that is on the back of the date-stone." Al-Jalalayn: *qadra naqratin-nawāh* — the measure of that tiny notch in the seed.

And listen to the word itself. **نقير** — *naqīr*. From the root **نقر** (*naqr*), which carries the sense of pecking, boring, notching — the way a bird's beak *pecks* a tiny hole. Even the sound of it is small and sharp and precise. This is not a word for "a little." This is a word for *the smallest carved point you can imagine* — the dent a beak leaves, the speck on a seed.

Now hear the full sentence: *wa lā yuẓlamūna naqīrā* — "they will not be wronged by even a *naqīr*."

Let's sit with the scale of this. Allah is not saying "you will be rewarded generously." Generosity we might expect. He is saying something about **precision.** He is saying that the resolution of His justice is finer than the speck on a date-seed. That if the entire weight of your life's reward were laid out, and from it there were subtracted the volume of that one tiny dimple — *that* much would be an injustice, and it will not happen. Not by *that* much.

Here is what is remarkable, and you have to feel the two halves of the verse together to feel it. The *first* half lowered the bar — *some* of the good is enough to get you in. You might worry: then is this a loose accounting? A rough, approximate mercy that waves the small stuff through and also, perhaps, loses the small stuff? And the *last* word answers: no. The entrance is wide, but **the ledger inside is exact to the speck.** Mercy at the door does not mean carelessness in the reckoning. The same God who does not demand all of the good *also does not lose any of yours.*

There is a beautiful detail in the surah itself here. Just seventy-five verses earlier, in 4:49, Allah used a *different* tiny measure: *wa lā yuẓlamūna **fatīlā*** — "they will not be wronged by a *fatīl*," the thin thread or fiber in the long groove of that same date-stone. And here, in 4:124, He uses the *naqīr*, the speck on its back. Two different microscopic parts of the very same date-seed, in the very same surah, in the very same phrase — *lā yuẓlamūna.* It is as though Allah turns the seed over in His hand and says: not by the thread along its face, and not by the speck on its back. He has measured His justice against the smallest things His servants could ever point to.

### A bridge between the words

Look at what these three words have built.

*Min* — He asks for **some** of the good.
*Wa huwa muʾmin* — He requires that it be **rooted** in faith.
*Naqīran* — and He promises that not even a **speck** of its reward will be lost.

The construction has shown us a God who is wide at the entrance and exact at the scale. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about grammar — but about how we live, and how we fear, and what we are actually afraid of when we are afraid we are not enough.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The fear that your unseen good is being lost

Let me start where you already live.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from doing good that no one sees. You stayed up with a sick child and no one will ever know how many times you got up. You were patient with someone who will never thank you. You held your tongue when you had every right to speak, and the holding cost you something, and no human being recorded it. You sent money quietly. You forgave privately. You did the small, unglamorous, invisible labor of being decent — and a quiet voice asks: *does any of this even count? Is it just evaporating?*

We live in systems that constantly lose our small efforts. The work email no one read. The extra hour that didn't show up in the metrics. The kindness that got no credit because the loud person took it. We have been *trained* by the world to expect that the small good is the good that gets lost. That only the visible, the large, the documented survives. And so we either stop doing the small good — or we do it bitterly, bracing for it to disappear.

Now bring the verse to that exhaustion.

*Wa lā yuẓlamūna naqīrā.*

Allah is describing a ledger with **infinite resolution.** Not "the big deeds count." Not "the visible deeds count." The *naqīr* — the speck, the thing so small it has no name in your language and barely a name in Arabic, the dimple on the back of a seed — even *that* is not lost. Which means the thing you did that no human eye saw, the thing too small to seem to matter, the patience that cost you and earned you nothing here — it is not floating away. It is on a record kept to a precision finer than any instrument you own. Al-Muyassar puts it in exactly these terms: they "will not be diminished from the reward of their deeds by anything — even if it were the measure of the *nuqra* on the back of the date-stone." Read the verb it chooses: not merely *un-wronged*, but *un-diminished* — *lā yunqaṣūna.* The reward of your good is not even *subtracted* from. Not shaved. Not rounded down. And al-Muyassar names the destination it is kept for: *dār al-naʿīm al-muqīm* — "the abode of lasting bliss." The good you thought was evaporating is being held for a home that does not end.

Let's go deeper, because there is a turn here. The verse does not say "you will be *rewarded* a *naqīr*." It says "you will not be **wronged** a *naqīr*" — *lā yuẓlamūna.* The frame is *justice*, not just generosity. And that is even stronger. Because to be generous is optional; one can choose how generous to be. But *ẓulm* — wrongdoing, injustice — is a thing Allah has forbidden upon Himself. To withhold from you the reward of even your speck-sized good would be *ẓulm*, and Allah does not do *ẓulm*. So your invisible good is not protected by His mood. It is protected by His **justice** — the most reliable thing in existence.

And then sit with the paradox the whole verse holds. On one side: *min al-ṣāliḥāt* — you don't have to bring all of it. On the other side: *naqīran* — and none of what you bring is lost. The mercy and the justice are not in tension. They are the same hand. **A God merciful enough not to demand everything, and just enough never to lose anything.** Most of us secretly believe in a god who is the opposite — who demands everything and yet somehow still loses our good. The verse dismantles that god completely.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means you can do the small good *without bracing for its loss.* The whispered duʿāʾ for someone who'll never know. The two extra rakʿahs at night. The dollar given where no one saw. You are not throwing these into a void. You are entering them into the one ledger in existence that loses nothing — not even the speck on a seed. So do the small good *freely.* Stop weighing whether it's "worth it." On this scale, it always is.

### Theme Two: When belonging is mistaken for transformation

Let me take you back into the room, because the second theme is the disease the verse was sent to cure — and it is *our* disease too.

The Quraysh and the People of the Book had made the same quiet substitution. They had traded *being transformed* for *belonging*. "I serve the Kaʿba." "I am descended from the prophets." "I am inside the right community, the right family, the right institution." The identity had become the salvation. The membership card had replaced the journey.

And before we shake our heads at them — notice how *deeply* modern this is. We do it constantly. "I'm a good person." "I come from a religious family." "I went to the Islamic school." "I'm part of the right movement, I follow the right scholars, I'm on the right side." We can build an entire spiritual identity out of *affiliation* and never once let it touch the heart. We can be culturally drenched in religion and inwardly untouched by it. We can know the vocabulary, wear the markers, belong to the group — and have a heart that has never actually *trusted* God, never actually been transformed.

The verse walks straight into this. Watch the order of its demands again, because the order is the teaching. It does not say "whoever *belongs*." It does not say "whoever is born to the right people" or "whoever serves the right institution." It says *whoever **does** — while **being a believer.*** A verb of action, *yaʿmal.* And a state of the heart, *muʾmin.* **Doing and trusting.** Neither one is your tribe. Neither one is your inheritance. Neither one can be performed for a crowd.

Here is the turn. Notice that the verse *honors the deed* — the Quraysh really did feed the pilgrims, and feeding the hungry is genuinely *ṣāliḥ*. The verse doesn't sneer at the action. It re-roots it. It says: that beautiful act needs to grow from *īmān*, from the security and trust of a heart turned to God, or it has no soil. Faith is not a *replacement* for the deed; it is the **soil** the deed must grow in. The boasters had the flowers and no soil. They were holding cut blossoms and calling it a garden.

And feel the mercy hidden even in this severity. Because if belonging *could* save you, then the convert with no lineage, the lonely believer with no community, the person estranged from every "right" group — they would all be lost. By making the condition *faith and action* rather than *belonging*, Allah made Paradise reachable for the person who has **nothing but their own heart and their own hands.** No tribe. No pedigree. No institution. Just a believing heart and a portion of real good. The same word that humbled the proud *liberated* the outsider.

Sit with the paradox one more breath. The verse is simultaneously the *most demanding* thing — you cannot coast on who you are — and the *most accessible* thing — you do not *need* to be anyone in particular. It strips away every external advantage and, in doing so, opens the door to everyone.

What does this change tomorrow? It asks you to audit the difference between your *affiliation* and your *transformation.* Not "what group am I in?" but "what did my faith actually move me to *do* this week, and was my heart actually *turned to God* when I did it?" Take one thing you've been doing on autopilot — a prayer, a habit of charity, a religious marker — and re-root it. Do it once, deliberately, *as a muʾmin* — from trust, toward God — and feel the difference between performing belonging and living faith.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and see the whole verse as one designed object, because its shape *is* its message.

> **وَمَن يَعْمَلْ** — whoever *does* (action, not affiliation)
> **مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ** — *some* of the good (the bar is survivable)
> **مِن ذَكَرٍ أَوْ أُنثَىٰ** — male or female (the door is for everyone)
> **وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ** — while believing (the one non-negotiable: the root)
> **فَأُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ يَدْخُلُونَ ٱلْجَنَّةَ** — those will enter the Garden
> **وَلَا يُظْلَمُونَ نَقِيرًا** — and not be wronged a speck

See the symmetry. The verse opens by *widening* — some of the good, either gender, anyone — and closes by *tightening* — exact to the speck. Generous in the demand, precise in the reward. And right at the center, holding the two halves together, sits the one fixed point: *wa huwa muʾmin.* Faith is the hinge on which both the width and the precision swing.

There is one more detail worth seeing. On the final verb, the reciters differ — and the difference is a gift. Abu ʿAmr and Ibn Kathir read it *yudkhalūna l-jannah* — passive: "they **will be made to enter** the Garden." The majority read *yadkhulūna* — active: "they **will enter** the Garden." Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both preserve both readings. And both are true at once: you *enter* by your own striving — your real, partial, faith-rooted good — *and* you are *ushered in* by Allah's grace, brought through a door you could never have forced open by works alone. You walk, and you are welcomed. Effort and grace, in a single word with two readings.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two sides.

The partitive *min* and the fear of lost good are one truth: **God's mercy is wide enough to accept your portion, and His justice is fine enough to lose none of it.**

The condition *wa huwa muʾmin* and the disease of belonging are one truth: **what saves you is not who you belong to but whether your heart has truly turned — and from that turning, whether your hands have moved.**

A God generous in what He asks. Exact in what He gives. Reachable by anyone. Foolable by no one.

### Questions to carry

1. What good have I been doing *bitterly* — bracing for it to be lost — that I could now do *freely*, knowing it is recorded to the speck?
2. Where in my life have I quietly substituted *belonging* for *transformation* — leaning on my family, my community, my labels instead of my heart and my hands?
3. If Allah lowered the bar to *some* of the good — what is the *some* I am actually bringing? Is there a portion, or have I brought nothing and trusted my affiliation?
4. When I do a good deed, is my heart turned to God in that moment — am I doing it *as a muʾmin* — or am I performing it for the room?
5. Do I secretly believe in a god who demands everything and still loses my good? What would change if I believed instead in the One of this verse?

### One-sentence distillation

Allah asks only a portion of the good but rooted in real faith, opens that door to every soul regardless of who they are, and weighs the reward with a justice so exact that not even the speck on a date-seed of your good is ever lost.

### The closing invitation

You walked into this verse, perhaps, afraid that you are not enough — not consistent enough, not impressive enough, not *born into* enough. Let the invitation be this: bring your portion. Bring it from a heart that trusts Him. Stop measuring whether the small good is worth it — on this scale, it always is. And let go of the résumé, the lineage, the labels you've been leaning on, because the only thing you actually need is the one thing no one can give you and no one can take: a believing heart, and hands that *do.*

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who lose nothing — not the thread in the seed, not the speck on its back — do not let our small, unseen good be wasted, and do not let us lean on anything but You. Make our hearts truly *muʾmin*, root our deeds in that faith, and by Your mercy usher us through a door we could never force open on our own. *Āmīn.*
