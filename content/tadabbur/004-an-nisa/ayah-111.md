# The Earning That Turns Against You

### Surah An-Nisa, Ayah 111

> وَمَن يَكْسِبْ إِثْمًا فَإِنَّمَا يَكْسِبُهُۥ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

*Wa man yaksib ithman fa-innamā yaksibuhu ʿalā nafsih. Wa kāna-llāhu ʿalīman ḥakīmā.*

**"And whoever earns a sin only earns it against his own soul. And Allah is ever All-Knowing, All-Wise."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Verse

Let me take you back to a quiet scandal in Madinah.

There was a man among the believers — the early sources around this passage name him Tuʿma ibn Ubayriq — who stole. And when the theft was about to come to light, he did something we recognize instantly, because human beings have been doing it since the first sons of Adam: he tried to make it someone else's problem. He hid the stolen goods, and when the trail led toward them, he pointed his finger at an innocent man — an outsider, someone whose word would carry less weight — and said, in effect, *He did it. Not me.*

And here is the part that should make us uncomfortable, because it is so familiar: some of the man's own people, his tribe, his community, were ready to stand up and **defend him.** Not because they were certain he was innocent. But because he was *one of us.* Because defending him felt like loyalty. Because the alternative — admitting that one of our own had done something shameful — felt unbearable.

So you have a perfect little ecosystem of sin here. A thief who steals. A schemer who frames the innocent. And a crowd ready to launder the whole thing through the language of loyalty.

[PAUSE]

Into this, Allah reveals a passage that does something remarkable. It does not just say *don't steal.* It does not just say *don't lie.* It reaches past the act, past the cover-up, past the social machinery of excuse-making — and it makes a single, quiet statement about the **physics of sin itself.**

Listen to it again:

*Wa man yaksib ithman fa-innamā yaksibuhu ʿalā nafsih.*

"Whoever earns a sin only earns it against his own soul."

Here is the hook I want you to carry. Tuʿma thought he was doing something *to someone else.* He thought sin was a thing you could aim outward — that you could steal from a victim, frame an innocent, offload the cost onto another body. And Allah, in eleven words, turns the entire arrow around.

What if the deepest truth about every wrong you have ever done is that it never actually left you? That it could not? That the moment you committed it, it was already filed — not against the person you thought you were hurting — but **against you?**

Let's sit with that. Let's earn it slowly.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yaksib* — Sin as Something You *Earn*

I want to start with a word that should stop us in our tracks, but usually slips right past, because the translation makes it sound ordinary.

The verb is **يَكْسِبُ — *yaksib.*** From the root **ك-س-ب (k-s-b).**

Now, before I tell you what it means, let me teach you the concept, because Arabic has more than one way to say "to do."

There is a generic verb for doing — *faʿala.* You did something. Neutral. And there is a verb for making — *ṣanaʿa.* But *kasaba* is none of these. *Kasaba* is a **commercial word. An economic word.** It means *to earn. To acquire. To gain. To draw something toward yourself through your own effort.*

This is the word a merchant uses for profit. It is the word for wages, for the thing your labor brings home to you. Al-Qurtubi, commenting on this very ayah, gives us the precise definition: **al-kasb** is "what a person draws to himself of benefit, or repels from himself of harm." It is the language of acquisition. Of the ledger. Of *what you take home at the end of the day.*

And notice — Qurtubi adds something striking — this is *why,* he says, the act of the Lord is never called *kasb.* Because *kasb* belongs to a being who works to acquire what he does not yet have. It is the verb of the needy. The verb of the one who labors and gains.

[PAUSE]

So now look at what Allah has done. He did not say "whoever *commits* a sin." He said whoever **earns** a sin. *Yaksib ithman.*

Stay with this. He has taken the most intimate, lossy, shameful human act — sin — and dressed it in the vocabulary of *enterprise.* Of profit-seeking. As if to say: you think you are gaining something. You think this transgression is an acquisition. You did the math. You decided there was a profit in it — a pleasure, an advantage, a thing taken, a rival defeated. **You showed up to work, and you earned.**

Here is what is remarkable. When Tuʿma stole that armor, in his own mind he was *gaining.* He came home richer. The ledger, he thought, was in the black. And Allah uses the exact word he would use for honest profit — *kasaba* — and lets it sit there, glittering, before turning it into the most catastrophic loss a soul can record.

Because what is the thing he actually earned? Not the armor.

**Ithman.** Sin. He went to work, he labored, he schemed, he acquired — and the wage that came home with him, the thing now sitting in his account, was *sin itself.*

This is the first turn of the screw. The Quran does not let you pretend sin is something that happens *to* you, a stumble, an accident, a slip. *Kasaba* says: you went and got it. You worked for it. **It is your earnings.**

And the classical commentators sharpen exactly this point. al-Tabari glosses the phrase as *whoever comes to a sin deliberately and with knowledge of it* — *ʿalā ʿamdin minhu lah, wa maʿrifatin bih.* Not the foot that slips, but the hand that reaches, eyes open. al-Muyassar reads it the same way: the one *who sets out, intending,* to commit the sin. *Kasb* is the verb of the chooser. Which is why it lands so heavily on Tuʿma — no one frames an innocent man by accident. The whole scheme was deliberate from the first breath, and *kasaba* is the word that refuses to let it be called anything softer.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Fa-innamā* — The Word That Closes Every Exit

Now watch the second word, because this is where the arrow turns around.

**فَإِنَّمَا — *fa-innamā.***

Let me teach you this particle, because it is one of the most powerful tools in the Arabic language and most of us walk past it.

*Innamā* is the particle of **restriction** — what the grammarians call *ḥaṣr* or *qaṣr.* It means *only. None but. Nothing other than.* It takes a statement and slams every door except one. It is exclusivity made into a single word.

If I say in Arabic, "He is a messenger," that is information. But if I say *innamā* he is a messenger — I am saying he is *only* a messenger, *nothing more,* I have shut the door on every other possibility.

So *innamā* does not add information. It **subtracts alternatives.** It takes the open field of possibilities and fences it down to one.

Now place it in the ayah:

*Fa-innamā yaksibuhu **ʿalā nafsih.***

"He earns it — **only** — against his own soul."

Feel what *innamā* just did. It went through every other address this sin might have been delivered to — *the victim, the innocent man framed, the community, the world* — and it crossed them all out. One by one. *Not there. Not there. Not there.* And it left exactly one name on the package.

**His own soul.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see how surgical this is in context? Tuʿma's entire scheme was an attempt to *change the address* on his sin. To take the package marked "thief" and re-label it with an innocent man's name. The whole machinery of his lie was a postal fraud — a forging of the destination.

And *innamā* is Allah's reply. **You cannot change the address.** It does not matter how clever the lie, how loyal the crowd defending you, how completely you fool the court of Madinah. The restriction particle has already sealed it: *innamā ʿalā nafsih.* Only against yourself. The package will be delivered. It was always addressed to you.

And watch what the Quran does in the *very next breath.* The verse that immediately follows describes Tuʿma's exact move — *whoever earns a fault or a sin and then casts it upon an innocent person* — and tells you what that attempt accomplishes: *fa-qadi-ḥtamala buhtānan wa-ithman mubīnā,* "he has only **burdened himself** with a slander and a manifest sin" (4:112). Look at the verb Ibn Kathir lingers on: *iḥtamala* — *he loaded onto himself,* hoisted onto his own back. Trying to forward the parcel to an innocent man does not lighten the load. It doubles it. He set out to offload one sin and ended up carrying two — the original theft, plus the *buhtān,* the slander he forged to move it. The two verses are one motion: the sin lands on the self (111), and the cover-up only piles more onto the self (112). Every attempt to re-address it is itself a new package, addressed once again to you.

This is not a threat tacked on at the end. This is a statement about the *nature of moral reality.* Sin is non-transferable. You can fool every human witness. You cannot re-route the consequence. The one word *innamā* makes the soul's ledger **fraud-proof.**

And this is exactly the conclusion al-Tabari draws on the spot. Having stated the law — the earner reaps the consequence upon himself *dūna ghayrihi,* and no one else of God's creation — he turns the verse directly against the very crowd we met in the introduction: *fa-lā tujādilū ayyuhā-lladhīna tujādilūna ʿan hāʾulāʾi-l-khawana* — "so do not argue, O you who argue on behalf of these traitors." And then the line that should land on every one of us who has ever closed ranks around our own: *fa-innakum wa-in kuntum lahum ʿashīratan wa-qarābah* — "for even though you are their kin and their close relatives," your blood-loyalty cannot carry an ounce of what they earned. Tribal solidarity, in the end, is just one more forged address. You can stand *beside* the guilty; you cannot stand *under* their sin. It was never deliverable to you. So the warmth that says *defend him, he is one of us* is not loyalty at all — it is a parcel you are trying to sign for that the universe will never let you accept.

### Element Three: *ʿAlā* — The Preposition That Betrays Him

And now the smallest word in the verse, which carries the heaviest weight. One preposition.

**عَلَىٰ — *ʿalā.***

In Arabic, when you earn something *for* your own benefit, the natural preposition is **لِ — *li***. *Kasaba li-nafsihi* — he earned *for* himself. That is the language of gain, of profit that accrues to your account.

But that is **not** what Allah said.

He said *yaksibuhu **ʿalā** nafsih.*

And *ʿalā* means **upon. Against. On top of.** It is the preposition of *burden.* Of a weight pressing down. Of liability. When something is *ʿalā* you in Arabic, it is *on* you — a debt is *ʿalā* the debtor, a duty is *ʿalā* the one obligated.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the heart of the whole verse.

The ayah opened with the vocabulary of *profit* — *yaksib,* the merchant's word, the word of earning *for* yourself. Every instinct of the language is now expecting the preposition of gain. *Li-nafsih* — for himself. The sentence is leaning toward the language of reward.

And at the last possible moment — Allah swaps the preposition. Not *li.* **ʿalā.**

He earns it — *against* himself. *Upon* himself. As a *weight* on himself.

This is the entire deception of sin compressed into a single grammatical swerve. **It comes wearing the face of *li* — profit, for-you, gain — and it lands as *ʿalā* — burden, against-you, debt.** The verb promised you the language of earning. The preposition delivered the reality of loss.

Every sinner in history has made the same miscalculation. They read the transaction as *li-nafsih* — *this is for me, this is my gain.* And the ayah says: no. Check the preposition. It was always *ʿalā.* You did not add to your account. **You loaded your own back.**

And al-Tabari refuses to let *the sin* stay an abstraction — he itemizes the cargo that actually arrives on the soul. What the sinner earns upon himself, he says, is the *wabāl* of the deed (its ruinous consequence), its *ḍarar* (its harm), its *khizy* (its disgrace), and its *ʿār* (its shame). Four crates, one address — *dūna ghayrihi min sāʾiri khalqi-llāh,* "and no one else of all God's creation." The merchant thought he was carrying home armor. The manifest itemizes ruin, harm, disgrace, and shame, every line of it billed *ʿalā nafsih.*

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words have built, stacked on top of each other.

*Yaksib* — you went to work and *earned* it, like wages.
*Innamā* — and it can be delivered to *one address only.*
*ʿalā* — and that delivery is not a credit to your account. It is a weight placed *upon* you.

Three words. A complete theology of sin. You labor for it, it cannot be re-routed, and what feels like profit is in fact a burden you have strapped to your own soul.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words — but about the reality you and I live inside every single day.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Boomerang You Mistake for a Weapon

Let me begin where you actually live.

There is a feeling — and you know it, because every human being knows it — that wrongdoing is a kind of *power.* When you cut a corner no one will see, when you take what isn't yours, when you say the cruel thing that lands, when you cheat and win — there is a rush, and the rush feels like *gain.* It feels like you have moved the cost *off of yourself and onto the world.* You got away with something. The universe paid, and you pocketed.

This is the oldest illusion there is. It is the exact illusion Tuʿma was living inside. *I steal, the victim loses. I lie, the innocent man takes the fall. The cost lands on them. I walk free and richer.*

And modern life has a hundred names for this. We call it "looking out for number one." We call it "playing the game." We talk about getting away with things as if "getting away" were a real destination you could arrive at.

Now here is what the people who study the human mind have begun to document — and it is nothing the Quran did not say first. There is a wound psychologists now call **moral injury.** It is the damage a person sustains not from what is done *to* them, but from what they *do* — from acting against their own deepest sense of right. Soldiers who committed acts in war they could not live with. People who betrayed someone who trusted them. The finding is consistent and haunting: **the perpetrator carries a wound.** Not metaphorically. A measurable, corrosive, often lifelong wound — to sleep, to peace, to the capacity to look at oneself.

In other words: the cost did not land on the victim alone. **Something came back.** The arrow you fired outward described a long, quiet arc and lodged itself in *you.*

[PAUSE]

This is *yaksibuhu ʿalā nafsih.* Fourteen hundred years before the clinical term, the verse named the mechanism. The sin you thought was a weapon aimed outward is a boomerang. It *will* return. And it does not return to the address you wrote on it. It returns — *innamā* — to one address only. **Yours.**

Let's go one layer deeper, because there is a paradox here that I do not want to resolve too quickly.

If sin only harms the self — then what about the innocent man? What about Tuʿma's victim? Did he not suffer? al-Jalalayn presses the difficulty to its sharpest edge, glossing the verse: *li-anna wabālahu ʿalayhā wa-lā yaḍurru ghayrah* — "because its evil consequence is upon the soul, and it does not harm anyone else." Read flatly, that collides with everything we know: of course the framed man suffered. The Quran does not deny that wrongdoing scatters pain into the world. People *are* hurt by the sins of others; that is real, and the same surah goes to great lengths to defend the wronged.

So what does the verse mean? It means this: the harm that scatters outward into the world is **temporary, partial, and avengeable** — Allah is the defender of the wronged, and the victim's account will be settled in his favor. But the harm that lands on the *sinner's own soul* — that is the **essential, permanent, defining** transaction. The victim's wound will be healed and recompensed. The sinner's *kasb* — his earning — is filed in his own ledger, and unless he turns back, it stays.

The world feels the splash. The soul takes the wound. *ʿAlā nafsih.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the next time you feel the pull of a small wrong — the convenient lie, the corner cut, the unkind word that would feel so satisfying to say — I want you to do one thing. **Check the preposition.** Your nafs is whispering *li* — *this is for you, this is a gain, this is a win.* And the ayah has already told you the true grammar of the transaction. It is *ʿalā.* Against you. On you. You are not about to *take.* You are about to *carry.*

The most practical fruit of this verse is a single re-translation you perform in the heat of temptation: *this is not profit. This is freight I am about to load onto my own back.*

### Theme Two: The Two Names That Close Every Loophole

Now let's turn to the way the ayah ends, because Allah does not leave us with the law of sin alone. He signs it. And the signature is two names:

*Wa kāna-llāhu **ʿalīman ḥakīmā.*** "And Allah is ever All-Knowing, All-Wise."

Begin, again, where you live.

Think about the strange relationship every human being has with *being seen.* On one hand, we spend enormous energy *hiding* — curating, editing, presenting a self to the world while keeping the real ledger private. On the other hand, there is a deep, aching human need to be *fully known* — to have even one being who sees all of you and does not turn away. We are split creatures: terrified of exposure, starving for it.

Now — Tuʿma's entire crime was an exercise in *not being seen rightly.* He banked everything on managing perception. On the court of Madinah seeing a version of events that was false. The whole scheme assumes one thing: **that reality is whatever people can be made to believe.**

And the two names at the end of this verse are the demolition of that assumption.

**ʿAlīm** — All-Knowing. The intensive form, from the root *ʿ-l-m.* Not merely "one who knows," but knowing as a settled, total, inexhaustible attribute. al-Muyassar renders the name in exactly this register: *ʿAlīman bi-ḥaqīqati amri ʿibādih* — Knowing **the reality of the affair of His servants.** Not the version filed in Madinah's court. Not the story the tribe agreed to tell. The *reality.* And the classical commentators apply it straight to the case at hand: He is the One who knows who actually stole — not who the crowd blamed, not who the court convicted, but the *actual thief.* The verse, with one name, walks past every layer of Tuʿma's deception and reads the original fact underneath.

Sit with what that does to the illusion of *getting away with it.* "Getting away" requires a universe in which, if no human knows, then *no one* knows — and a fact that no one knows is a fact that, functionally, did not happen. **ʿAlīm abolishes that universe.** There is no such thing as a fact that no one knows. The deed was witnessed in the instant it was earned. The cover-up changed the human record and left the real one untouched.

[PAUSE]

But knowing alone is not yet justice. A witness who knows but cannot act changes nothing. So Allah pairs it with the second name.

**Ḥakīm** — All-Wise. From the root *ḥ-k-m,* and here is something beautiful: this same root gives us the word *ḥukm* — **judgment, verdict, the binding decision of a judge.** The Ḥakīm is not only the one with wisdom; He is the one whose wisdom *issues as a verdict.* al-Muyassar catches precisely this judicial sense: *Ḥakīman fī-mā yaqḍī bihi bayna khalqih* — Wise **in what He decrees between His creation.** Not wisdom in the abstract, but wisdom that settles disputes between people — which is exactly Tuʿma's situation: a contest between a guilty man and the innocent one he accused. The same root that means *to be wise* means *to pass sentence,* and the verse fuses the two: the One whose wisdom is itself the ruling upon the thief.

So look at the two names together, and see how perfectly they answer the crime.

Tuʿma's sin had two organs. First, **concealment** — hiding the truth of who did it. Second, **manipulation** — bending the verdict toward the innocent. And Allah's two names are an exact, two-part reply.

*ʿAlīm* answers the concealment: **you cannot hide the fact.**
*Ḥakīm* answers the manipulation: **you cannot bend the verdict.**

The knowing closes the escape. The wisdom guarantees the sentence. Between them, there is no crack to slip through. [PAUSE]

And notice the verb that carries them: *wa **kāna**-llāh* — "And Allah *has ever been*..." The perfect-tense *kāna* here does not mean Allah *was* once these things in the past. The grammarians read this construction as expressing a permanent, timeless attribute — He is, eternally and unchangingly, the Knowing and the Wise. This is not news Allah received about Tuʿma. **It is who He has always been,** before the crime, during it, and at the final accounting. The thief was never operating in the dark. He only imagined he was.

[PAUSE]

Now let me turn this from a verse about a thief into a verse about you, because that is where it is aimed.

That same split inside you — the terror of being seen, the hunger to be seen — *ʿAlīm* and *Ḥakīm* speak to both halves at once. To the part of you that hides: **there is no hiding; you have always been fully known.** That should be sobering. But to the part of you that aches to be known and judged *rightly* — by someone who sees the whole context, the pressure you were under, the intention beneath the act, the wound that drove it — *Ḥakīm* is the most consoling word in the language. **You will not be judged by the crowd of Madinah.** You will not be judged by the rumor, the half-story, the worst-day-of-your-life snapshot that others hold of you. You will be judged by the One who knows *everything* and weighs it with *perfect wisdom.*

For the wrongdoer who will not turn back, that pairing is a sealed verdict. But for the one who is wronged and unseen — and for the sinner who repents and fears his record is sealed — that same pairing is mercy. The One who knows the true thief also knows the true penitent. The wisdom that convicts the unrepentant is the wisdom that receives the one who returns.

And the door of return is not something we have to infer; the passage *opens* with it. The verse immediately before ours sets the whole sequence in motion: *wa man yaʿmal sūʾan aw yaẓlim nafsahu thumma yastaghfiri-llāha yajidi-llāha ghafūran raḥīmā* — "whoever does evil, or wrongs his own soul, then seeks Allah's forgiveness, will find Allah Forgiving and Merciful" (4:110). Notice what Ibn Kathir lets us see by reading the three verses as a single motion, each one bending around the *nafs:* the soul that **wrongs itself** and may yet seek pardon (110), the soul that **earns against itself** (111), the soul that **loads still more onto itself** by framing the innocent (112). And see where the arc *begins* — not with the threat, but with the open hand. Before Allah tells you the sin is non-transferable, He has already told you it is forgivable. The same *nafs* that the sin is filed against is the very *nafs* invited, one verse earlier, to come back and have the file closed.

So the practical fruit of this second theme: stop managing the human record so anxiously. Stop spending your soul's energy on perception. There is exactly one audience whose verdict is final, and that audience already knows everything. Live *toward* that knowing — not in dread of exposure, but in the relief of being already, fully, and accurately seen.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look now at how the whole ayah is built, because the architecture *is* the argument.

It opens with **a human being at work** — *yaksib,* earning, laboring, acquiring. It traces where the earnings land — *innamā ʿalā nafsih,* against his own soul, one address, no re-routing. And then it lifts up and signs the whole thing with **two divine names** — *ʿAlīm, Ḥakīm.*

Do you see the shape? **Human action, wrapped in divine attribute.** The little ledger of the human heart, opened up — and then placed inside the infinite ledger of the One who knows and judges. The first half of the verse tells you *where the sin goes.* The second half tells you *who is watching it go there.* And the two halves are the same truth from two directions: the linguistic discovery — *you cannot change the address of your sin* — and the theological discovery — *the One who knows the true address is ʿAlīm and Ḥakīm.* The sin is non-transferable *because* the Bookkeeper is unfoolable.

The merchant's word *kasaba* opened the verse. And by the end, you understand the real economy: every soul keeps an account, the entries cannot be forged, and the Auditor has been present, knowing and wise, since before the first transaction.

### Questions to Carry

Let these stay open. Don't rush to answer them.

1. Where in my life am I reading a sin as *li* — "this is for me, this is a gain" — when the true grammar is *ʿalā* — a weight I am loading onto myself?

2. Whose verdict am I actually living for? Whose "record" of me am I anxiously managing — and have I confused the crowd's record with the only one that is true?

3. When I have been wronged and no one saw it rightly, can I rest in *ʿAlīm* — that there is no such thing as an unwitnessed injustice?

4. If every sin returns to my own soul, what does that make repentance — not a plea to an outside court, but a settling of my *own* account before the books are closed?

5. What would change today if I truly believed I have *always been fully and accurately known?*

### One Sentence

**Every sin you commit is wages you earn against your own soul — non-transferable, fraud-proof, and recorded in full by the One who has always known the truth and judges it with perfect wisdom.**

### The Closing Invitation

You came in thinking, perhaps, that this was a verse about a thief in old Madinah. It is a verse about the ledger you are keeping right now. The invitation is not to fear — it is to *honesty.* To stop the exhausting work of re-addressing your packages, of managing perceptions, of pretending the cost lands elsewhere. It all comes home. It was always coming home. And the One it comes home to is not waiting to ambush you — He is *ʿAlīm,* who already knows, and *Ḥakīm,* whose wisdom leaves the door of return open for as long as you draw breath. Settle the account now, while the settling is mercy.

### A Du'a

*O Allah, You are ʿAlīm — You know what I have earned against my own soul better than I know it myself. And You are Ḥakīm — wise enough to receive me when I turn back. Do not let me mistake the burden for a profit. And before my book is sealed, let me bring every entry home to You in repentance, while returning is still open.* Ameen.
