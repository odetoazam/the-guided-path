# The Wish for Sameness: When Loyalty Becomes the Whole Test

---

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the world they fell into.

> **وَدُّوا۟ لَوْ تَكْفُرُونَ كَمَا كَفَرُوا۟ فَتَكُونُونَ سَوَآءً**
>
> *Waddū law takfurūna kamā kafarū fatakūnūna sawā'ā*
>
> "They wish that you would disbelieve as they disbelieved, so that you would all be **alike**."

There is a clause later in this ayah that has made people stop reading and start arguing. *Seize them and kill them wherever you find them.* We are going to walk all the way to that clause. I am not going to step around it. But I am going to ask you to do something difficult first: I am going to ask you to not start there. Because if you start at the sword, you will misread the whole verse — and worse, you will miss the strange, quiet, almost intimate thing Allah does at the very beginning of it.

**Rebuild the world.**

Madinah, after the battle of Uhud. The young Muslim community has just bled. And inside the city, and around it, there is a category of people who are not the open enemy and not the firm believer — they are something harder to name. They say the words of faith with their tongues. They pray when watched. But when the community marches out to defend itself, they melt away. When the enemy gains, they lean toward the enemy. When the believers gain, they lean back.

The companions did not know what to do with these people. Were they brothers? Were they enemies? Some of the believers said, *"They prayed with us — they are with us."* Others said, *"Look at where they actually stand — they are not."* The community had **split into two camps over how to read these people** — and Allah names that split in the verse just before this one: *"What is the matter with you that you are two groups concerning the hypocrites?"* (4:88).

So this ayah arrives into a real argument among sincere people who are confused. And here is the hook — hold onto this question for the next hour:

**What if the confusion came from looking at the wrong thing entirely? What if these people were never asking to be judged by their words — and Allah is about to move the entire test somewhere the tongue cannot reach?**

[PAUSE]

Let's begin with a single word — and it is not a harsh word. It is, of all things, a word of love.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: وَدُّوا — The Tenderest Root, Bent Toward Your Ruin

Before we touch the word, let me hand you the idea behind it.

In Arabic there is a family of words built from three letters — **و د د** (*w-d-d*). From this root comes *wudd*: not cold wishing, but **warm longing**. It is affection, fondness, the pull of the heart toward something it loves. One of Allah's own names is built from it — *Al-Wadūd*, **the Loving**, the One whose love reaches toward His servants. When the Qur'an wants to describe a love so deep it is woven into the marriage bond, it uses this root: *He placed between you* **mawadda** *and mercy* (30:21). When the believers' hearts are knit together in love, this is the root.

So *w-d-d* is the vocabulary of the heart's tenderness.

Now look at how this ayah opens:

> **وَدُّوا۟ — *waddū*** — "They long, they fondly wish..."

It is a verb in the past tense from that same root of love. And what is it that they so warmly, so achingly long for?

> **...لَوْ تَكْفُرُونَ — *law takfurūn*** — "...that **you** would disbelieve."

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Allah did not say *"they want you to disbelieve."* He chose the verb of *longing*. He chose the root that carries warmth, affection, desire. And He pointed all that warmth at the wish that *you* — the believer they smile at, pray beside, share bread with — would lose the one thing that makes your life worth living. Al-Ṭabarī is precise about *what* they ache for you to lose: that you would "deny the oneness of your Lord, and the truth of your Prophet Muhammad ﷺ" — not some vague slippage, but the two pillars on which your whole life stands. And al-Muyassar sharpens it further into an exchange of hearts: they long for you to "deny the reality of what your hearts believed in, just as they denied it in their own hearts." Heart against heart. They want yours emptied the way theirs already is.

Notice something almost unbearable here. This is not the hatred of a stranger. The root *w-d-d* is the language of people who are *close* to you. The grammar is telling you this is not the open enemy on the battlefield, snarling from a distance. This is someone near enough to wish *upon* you — and what they wish, with the very heart-word reserved for love, is that you would fall. And al-Ṭabarī will not let us read the "they" loosely: the ones doing the longing, he says, are precisely *these hypocrites — the very people concerning whom you, O believers, have split into two factions.* The fond wisher of the previous verse and the fond wisher of this one are the same person. The argument in 4:88 was about *them*; now Allah turns from the argument to the longing inside *their* hearts.

Why would a heart *long* for your unbelief? Stay with me, because the rest of the clause answers it:

> **...كَمَا كَفَرُوا۟ فَتَكُونُونَ سَوَآءً**
>
> "...**as they disbelieved**, so that you would be **alike**."

Here is what is remarkable. Their longing is not really for your destruction as an end in itself. Their longing is for **symmetry**. They want you down here *with them*. The clause is not "they wish you would disbelieve" — full stop. It is "they wish you would disbelieve *the way they did* — *so that you would be the same.*"

Their wound is not that you believe. Their wound is that you believe **and they do not** — and that asymmetry is intolerable to them. So the heart's longing, the *wudd*, is bent toward one outcome: *level him. Pull him down to me. Make us the same.*

What this single word reveals is this: **there is a kind of love that is really a wish for sameness, and it longs for your fall the way true love longs for your rising.** Allah opens the verse here, with the heart-word, so that you understand — before any talk of swords — that the danger is not loud. It is fond. It is near. It smiles.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: تَتَّخِذُوا — The Verb of Taking-Into-Yourself

Now Allah gives the command. And the command turns on a single verb that most translations flatten into the little English word "take."

> **فَلَا تَتَّخِذُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءَ**
>
> "So do not **take** from among them allies."

Let me teach the structure before we use it, because everything hangs on it.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — call them gears — and each gear changes the *kind* of action. The simple gear of "to take" is *akhadha* (أَخَذَ): to grasp, to receive. But the word here is not that simple gear. It is a heavier, reflexive gear — what grammarians call **Form VIII**, *ittakhadha* (ٱتَّخَذَ). And Form VIII bends the action *back onto the doer*. It does not mean merely "to take." It means **to take for oneself, to adopt, to appropriate, to make a thing one's own.**

Think of the difference in your own life. There is a difference between *taking* a pen off a desk and *taking* someone as your spouse. There is a difference between *receiving* a stranger's coat and *adopting* a child. Form VIII is the second kind every time. It is taking something *into* yourself, into your circle, claiming it as belonging to you.

So Allah is not saying *"have no contact with them."* He is not even saying *"do not deal with them."* The Qur'an elsewhere commands kindness and justice toward those who do not fight you. The word here is sharper and narrower than "contact." It is:

> *Do not* **adopt them into yourself** *as awliyā'.*

And what is a *waliyy* (root **و ل ي**, *w-l-y*)? It is the one whose side you are on — the protector, the intimate, the ally to whom you turn your back in trust and your face in loyalty. The *wali* is the person you let *inside the wall*. Al-Muyassar reaches for an even more intimate word to gloss *awliyā'* here: *aṣfiyā'* — your *chosen* ones, the purest, closest companions, the handful you have filtered down to and kept. So the command, read in its true gear, is precise: **do not take the one who fondly wishes your ruin and install him inside your innermost circle of loyalty.**

And al-Jalālayn adds the detail that turns the screw: *do not befriend them as allies* — **and not even if they make a show of faith** (*wa in aẓharū al-īmān*). The outward display is exactly the trap. The verse anticipates that these people *will* say the right things, perform the prayer, profess the creed — and it tells you that none of that, by itself, earns the keys. The display is not the proof. We are about to learn what is.

[PAUSE]

Do you see why the gear matters? Because the believers in Madinah were confused on exactly this point. They thought the question was *"Are these people in or out?"* — a question of category, of label. And Allah answers with a *verb of action you control*: do not *ittakhidh* — do not perform the act of *taking them in*. The danger was never their label. The danger is the act *you* might do — pulling the fond wisher across the threshold and handing him the keys to your loyalty.

This is the second discovery: **the verse moves the test from what they are to what you do.** You cannot always see a heart. But you can always govern your own act of adoption. *Whom are you taking in?*

### Element Three: حَتَّىٰ يُهَاجِرُوا — The Open Door, and What the Verse Refuses to Close

Now watch what Allah does next, because this is the hinge of the whole verse, and it is almost always missed.

> **...حَتَّىٰ يُهَاجِرُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ**
>
> "...**until** they emigrate in the way of Allah."

Notice the word **حَتَّىٰ — *ḥattā*** — "until." The prohibition is not a wall. It is a **door with a condition on it.** *Do not take them as allies — until.* The moment you hear "until," you know the sentence is not a final verdict on these people. It is a gate, and Allah has just told you what opens it.

And what opens it? **يُهَاجِرُوا — *yuhājirū*** — "they emigrate." The root is **ه ج ر** (*h-j-r*), and the verb sits in another gear — **Form III**, *hājara* — the gear of *directed, effortful, sustained action*, action aimed at a target and carried through against resistance. *Hijra* is not a stroll. It is the wrenching act of tearing yourself out of one belonging and crossing — with your feet, your home, your safety on the line — into another.

Here is what is remarkable, and it answers the confusion of the whole passage. The believers wanted to read these people by their **words**. Allah says: do not read them by their words at all. Read them by **the most expensive action a human being can take** — leaving everything, *fī sabīlillāh*, on the road toward Allah. Words are cheap; the tongue can say anything. *Hijra* cannot be faked. You cannot half-emigrate. Your body is either on the road or it is not.

And this is exactly how the classical commentators read the gate. Al-Muyassar says they are to emigrate "as a *burhān* — a proof — of the truthfulness of their faith." Al-Jalālayn calls it a *hijra ṣaḥīḥa* — a *sound, valid* emigration that "realizes," that *makes real*, their belief. Catch the precision of that word: faith here is not something they merely *assert*; it is something the journey *actualizes*. The display of belief (which al-Jalālayn already warned us about) is the claim; the *hijra* is the proof that turns the claim into a fact. Allah is not asking for louder words. He is asking for the one act that converts a profession into a reality.

So Allah has not condemned these people. **He has handed them a way out — and made the way out an action no hypocrite would ever pay for.** That is the genius of the gate. It does not interrogate the heart. It simply waits to see whose feet move.

[PAUSE]

Now — and only now — we reach the clause that frightens people. And I want you to notice how much Allah has already done before He says it.

> **فَإِن تَوَلَّوْا۟ فَخُذُوهُمْ وَٱقْتُلُوهُمْ حَيْثُ وَجَدتُّمُوهُمْ**
>
> "**But if they turn back**, then seize them and kill them wherever you find them."

Read the absence here as carefully as the presence. **What is withheld is finality. What is offered, first, is the door.** The sword clause does not stand alone — it stands *behind a condition*, and the condition is **فَإِن تَوَلَّوْا۟ — *fa-in tawallaw*** — "if they turn away."

And look at the verb *tawallaw*. Its root is **و ل ي** — the **same root** as the *awliyā'* you were told not to adopt, the same root as the *waliyy* at the end of the verse. It is **Form V**, *tawallā*, the reflexive gear: *to turn one's own self away, to turn one's back.* To *tawallā* is, almost literally, to **turn your loyalty around** — to take the *walāya*, the allegiance, that you claimed to give Allah and swing it back toward the enemy camp.

So the killing is not commanded against people for *believing wrongly*, nor for *staying home*, nor for *being confused*. The classical commentators are careful here. **Al-Jalālayn** glosses *tawallaw* as *"and they persisted on what they were upon"* — active continuation in hostility. **Al-Muyassar** reads it as *"if they turn away from what they were called to"* and links the whole command to a battlefield context. **Al-Qurṭubī** anchors the verse in the law of *active combatants and traitors* — those who took up arms with the enemy after the door of *hijra* was held open and refused. This is wartime law concerning **those who turned from feigned allegiance into open warfare against the community** — not a standing license against anyone of another faith, and not against the peaceable, whom the very next ayah (4:90) explicitly exempts: *"except those who reach a people between whom and you is a treaty..."*

And the scholars do not even agree on *who* these people were. **Al-Qurṭubī** records more than one tradition — some say a group who left Makkah professing faith and then slid back; some say bedouins around Madinah; others a faction connected to Uhud. **Al-Baghawī** likewise reports *"those who turned back from the religion."* The honest reading holds this *ikhtilāf* open rather than flattening it into one neat story. What every reading agrees on is the *structure*: **an offered exit, and a sword reserved only for the one who answers the open door by turning his loyalty into a weapon.**

[PAUSE]

**The bridge.** We have found three things in the architecture of the words. A *longing* (*waddū*) bent toward your sameness. A *taking-in* (*ittakhidhū*) that you, not they, control. And a *turning* (*tawallaw*) of loyalty that is the real fault line — gated behind an open door of *hijra*. Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about a group in Madinah — but about reality itself, and about you.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Gravity of Sameness — Why People Long to Pull You Down

Let me start where you already live.

You decided to change something. Maybe you started praying again. Maybe you quit drinking. Maybe you began taking your deen seriously, or you walked away from a way of speaking, a way of spending, a way of being that everyone around you shared. And do you remember what happened? Not from your enemies — from the people *close* to you. The half-joke. *"Look who's gotten so holy."* The little tug. *"Come on, just this once, you used to be fun."* The quiet relief on their faces when you slipped.

You felt it as friction. Allah names it as *wudd* — a *longing*. The people around a person who rises often feel a pull, fond and unspoken, for that person to come back down. Not always because they hate you. Often because your rising is a mirror, and the mirror is unbearable, and the easiest way to break a mirror is to make the one holding it the same as you again.

This is the deepest psychology in the verse: **the wish for sameness is not neutral.** *Fatakūnūna sawā'ā* — "so you would be alike." There is a leveling instinct in the human heart that cannot tolerate someone standing on higher ground, and would rather drag them down to the flat than climb up to meet them. It dresses itself as friendship. It speaks in the voice of *w-d-d*, the love-word. And that disguise is exactly why it is dangerous — because you will not defend yourself against affection.

Now let the ayah illuminate it. Allah does not tell you to hate these people back. He does not tell you to rage. He tells you to do one cold, clear, structural thing: **do not hand them the keys.** *Lā tattakhidhū min-hum awliyā'.* Do not *adopt* the voice that longs for your sameness into the inner circle that steers your life. You may know them. You may be kind to them. But the people whose loyalty you *take into yourself* — the ones whose opinion governs your decisions, whose approval you organize your days around — those cannot be the people who are fondly waiting for you to fall.

[PAUSE]

And here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing to fix it: **the test of who belongs in your inner circle is not what they say about your faith — it is what they do with their own feet.** *Ḥattā yuhājirū.* The friend who is also climbing, also moving toward Allah, also paying the price — *take them in.* The one who only talks, who claims the same direction but never moves, who is warm to your face and longs for your sameness in their heart — keep the gate closed until the feet move.

What does this change about tomorrow? Tonight, name your inner circle honestly. Not your acquaintances — your *awliyā'*, the handful whose voices actually steer you. And ask of each one: *Is this person moving toward Allah, or fondly waiting for me to stop?* You do not have to cut anyone off. You have to decide who holds the keys.

### Theme Two: You Become Whom You Take As Your Wali

Here is the second place you already live.

Think about who you have become over the last five years. Now think about *whom you were loyal to* over those five years — whose approval you chased, whose group you wanted in, whose worldview you absorbed without noticing. I would wager the two answers are nearly the same. **We do not become who we admire from a distance. We become whom we take as our *wali* — the ones we let inside, the ones whose side we are on.**

The Qur'an knows this about us. That is why this single ayah uses the root *w-l-y* — the root of allegiance — **four times**, weaving it through the whole verse like a spine. *Do not take them as* **awliyā'** *(allies)... if they* **tawallaw** *(turn their loyalty away)... take no* **waliyy** *(intimate ally)... and no* **naṣīr** *(helper).* The entire verse, beneath its surface, is asking one question over and over: **whose side are you on, and to whom have you given the keys?**

And look at how it ends — with two words, not one:

> **وَلَا تَتَّخِذُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ وَلِيًّۭا وَلَا نَصِيرًا**
>
> "...and take not from among them any **waliyy** nor any **naṣīr**."

A *waliyy* is the ally of *loyalty* — the one whose side you are on in calm and in trust. A *naṣīr* (root **ن ص ر**, *n-ṣ-r*) is the ally of *the crisis* — the one you reach for in the moment you are losing, the helper in the hour of need. Both al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar pin the word down to its sharpest sense: the *naṣīr* is the one "through whom you seek victory against your enemy" — not a comforter, but a battle-ally, the hand you grip when the fight is going against you. Allah closes both doors deliberately. **Do not adopt them in your calm, and do not run to them in your panic.** Because the deepest betrayal of loyalty does not happen in calm — it happens in the crisis, when fear makes us grab the nearest hand. And the verse says: not even then. Not the hand of the one who longs for your sameness. Even when you are afraid. *Even then.*

[PAUSE]

Let the ayah turn once more. Why does it matter so much who your *wali* is? Because of what came three words earlier: *fī sabīli **llāh*** — *in the way of* **Allah.** The hijra that opens the gate is not migration toward a city; it is migration *toward Allah*. And the whole architecture of loyalty in this verse exists to protect one thing: that **your ultimate *wali* is Allah Himself.** This is the same root in His name *al-Walīy* — *the Protecting Ally.* Every lesser loyalty in your life is being measured against that one. The danger of adopting the fond enemy as your *wali* is not social — it is that you will slowly, warmly, by the gravity of sameness, be pulled off the road that leads to *the* Wali.

What does this change about tomorrow? When the crisis comes — and it will — notice the hand you instinctively reach for. That reflex tells you who your real *wali* is, far more honestly than your words do. Train the reflex now. Build the habit, in calm, of reaching first for Allah — *yā Walīy, yā Naṣīr* — so that when the fear comes, your hand already knows the way.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Beneath the Verse

Now let me show you the structure that was there the whole time.

This ayah is built on **two roots, repeated, that hold the entire meaning between them.** Read the spine:

- It opens with **w-d-d** — *waddū* — a **longing**, the heart-word, bent toward your sameness.
- It then sounds the root **w-l-y** — *awliyā'* — the loyalty you must not give.
- It turns on the same root again — *tawallaw* — the loyalty *they* turn away.
- And it closes on that root a final time — *waliyy* — the loyalty sealed shut.

The verse begins with a *feeling* (a fond wish) and ends with an *allegiance* (a closed door) — and the whole movement between them is Allah teaching you that **a wish must never be allowed to become an allegiance.** The danger starts as warmth (*wudd*). It becomes deadly only when warmth is granted the keys (*walāya*). The entire verse is the road from the one to the other — and the command is: *do not walk it.*

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

From the words: a *longing* for sameness (*waddū*), a *taking-in* you control (*ittakhidhū*), a *turning* of loyalty that is the real fault (*tawallaw*) — gated behind an *open door* no pretender will pay for (*yuhājirū*).

From the heart: people who cannot bear your rising will fondly wish you down; the gate of your inner circle is yours to guard; you become whom you take as your *wali*; and the only loyalty that will never long for your fall is your loyalty to Allah, *al-Walīy, al-Wadūd* — the One whose *wudd* truly wants you to rise.

The frightening clause, seen from the end, is not the center of the verse. It is the *last resort* behind an open door — and even it is built from the root of loyalty, because the whole verse was never about violence. It was about **allegiance, and the door Allah holds open before He ever closes one.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Who are my *awliyā'* — not my acquaintances, but the handful whose voices actually steer my decisions? Are they moving toward Allah, or fondly waiting for me to stop?
2. When I rose — in faith, in discipline, in becoming someone new — who pulled, and who climbed with me? What did I do with each?
3. In my last real crisis, whose hand did I reach for first? What does that reflex tell me about who my true *wali* is?
4. Is there a "fond wish for sameness" operating in me — toward someone whose rising I quietly cannot bear?
5. Have I been judging people, and myself, by *words* — when Allah keeps pointing me to *feet*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Those who cannot bear your faith will lovingly long to pull you down to their level — so guard the gate of your loyalty, give your innermost allegiance only to Allah and to those who prove their direction with their feet, and never adopt as your protector the one who has turned his back on the road.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to harden your heart against the world after this verse. You need to do one quiet, clear thing: **decide who holds the keys.** Be kind to many. Be just to all. But take *into yourself* — as the voices that steer you, the hands you reach for in the dark — only those who are walking the same road toward the same Wali. That is not coldness. It is the most loving thing you can do for the self Allah is trying to raise.

### Du'ā

*O Allah, You are al-Walīy and al-Wadūd — the Protecting Ally and the Loving. Let my truest loyalty rest in You alone, so that no fond wish for my falling ever finds the keys to my heart. Surround me with those whose feet move toward You, and when fear comes, let my hand reach first for Your help, O Naṣīr.* Āmīn.
