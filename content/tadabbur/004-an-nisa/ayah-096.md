# The Reward That Outruns Its Own Verb

## Surah An-Nisa, Ayah 96

> دَرَجَٰتٍۢ مِّنْهُ وَمَغْفِرَةًۭ وَرَحْمَةًۭ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًۭا رَّحِيمًۭا

*"Darajātin minhu wa maghfiratan wa rahmah. Wa kāna-Llāhu Ghafūran Rahīmā."*

**"Ranks from Him, and forgiveness, and mercy. And Allah is ever Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sentence Before This Sentence

Before we can stand inside this ayah, we have to hear the sentence that ends just before it begins.

In the ayah just before — 4:95 — Allah has been drawing a line between two kinds of believer. There are those who go out and strive, who pour their wealth and their bodies into the path of Allah, who put something real on the line. And there are those who sit back — not the ones with a genuine excuse, the verse is careful about them — but those who simply stay seated when they could have risen.

And Allah says something about the first group that should make us lean in. He says He has *preferred* them. He has given them a degree over those who sat. And then He ends 4:95 with a phrase that is deliberately, almost painfully, vague:

**"Ajran ʿaẓīmā"** — *a tremendous reward.*

A tremendous reward.

Now stop. Feel what that phrase does to you. It opens a door and then doesn't show you the room. *A tremendous reward* — how tremendous? Made of what? You strained, you gave, you went out into the heat and the fear and the loss — and the verse names your wage only as *great*, and then the sentence ends.

[PAUSE]

That hunger you feel right there — *yes, but what is it, what do I actually get* — that hunger is not an accident. Allah created it in you with the last word of 4:95. And ayah 96 exists to answer it.

Here is what you need to know before we go a single step further: **4:96 has almost no new grammar of its own.** It does not start a fresh sentence. It does not introduce a new verb and a new subject striding onto the stage. It simply leans back into the previous ayah and unfolds it. The "tremendous reward" was the closed fist. Ayah 96 is the fingers opening, one at a time, to show you what was inside.

And what falls out of that opening hand is not what most of us would predict.

You strove. You expected, at the summit, a medal. A rank. A *you earned this.*

What you find at the summit instead — and this is the thing I want us to spend the next hour discovering — is **forgiveness.** And then **mercy.** As if the one who climbed highest arrives at the top only to be *covered* and *embraced*, rather than decorated.

Why would the reward for striving be *forgiveness*? You forgive someone who did wrong. What does the striver in the path of Allah need forgiven?

Hold that question. Don't answer it yet. We have to earn it.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Verb That Isn't There

### First, a small thing about how language works

Let me show you something about your own speech before we touch the Arabic.

Imagine a friend asks you, "So what did you get for your birthday?" And imagine you had a wonderful birthday. How do you answer?

You don't usually say: *"I received a watch, and I received a book, and I was also given a trip."* That's the grammar of a tax form. That's slow. That's a full verb dragged out in front of every single gift.

When you're genuinely delighted, you drop the verb. You just say: **"A watch! A book! And — a trip!"** The gifts come tumbling out with no verb holding them up, because you're too full to slow down and conjugate. The joy outruns the grammar.

Now — hold that ordinary human moment in your mind, because Allah does *exactly this* in our ayah, and it is not an accident.

### Now look at what Allah actually says

In Arabic, when a word is the object of a verb, it usually takes a specific ending — the accusative, often a little *-an* sound. And here is the strange and beautiful thing about 4:96. Look at the three gifts:

**دَرَجَٰتٍ** — *darajāt* (ranks)
**مَغْفِرَةً** — *maghfiratan* (forgiveness)
**رَحْمَةً** — *rahmatan* (mercy)

Every one of them is wearing the accusative — the ending that says *I am the object of a verb.* They are all dressed as the objects of an action.

But **where is the verb?**

[PAUSE]

There is no verb in front of them. Grammatically, these three words are reaching back, expecting an action to govern them — and the action is *not written*. It has been left out.

This is not me speculating. The classical mufassir of *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* notes it directly: *maghfiratan* and *rahmatan*, he says, are accusative **بفعلهما المقدر** — *governed by their implied, estimated verb.* A verb the listener must supply. A verb Allah deliberately did not place on the page.

So the structure is, in feel: *"...He gives them — ranks from Him! And forgiveness! And mercy!"*

The verb is dropped **exactly the way you drop it when the gift is too full to wait for grammar.**

And we are not left to guess what that dropped verb was carrying. Al-Muyassar, paraphrasing the ayah, simply fills the gap in — and notice *with what.* It opens its entire gloss by naming the whole thing: *hādhā al-thawāb al-jazīl* — **"this abundant reward [is]:** lofty stations in the Gardens, forgiveness for their sins, and a vast mercy." The mufassir supplies precisely the verb al-Jalālayn told us was missing — and the word he reaches for to supply it, *al-thawāb,* *the reward,* is the very *ajran ʿaẓīmā* left undefined one ayah earlier. So the grammar's silence here and the vagueness back in 4:95 turn out to be the *same withheld word.* The "tremendous reward" was held back at the end of one verse precisely so the implied verb of the next could be the hand that finally gives it.

### Stay with this, because it's doing real work

Why does this matter? Why not just say "We give them ranks and forgiveness and mercy" in a tidy full sentence?

Because a stated verb *measures* the giving. A verb has a tense, a subject, a frame. It puts a container around the act. *"We gave"* — past, finished, bounded.

By dropping the verb and letting the three accusatives spill out unsupported, the giving is left **unmeasured.** It is not framed. It does not finish. The gifts simply pour: *darajāt — minhu — wa maghfiratan — wa rahmah.* Ranks from Him, and forgiveness, and mercy.

Notice what comes between the first gift and the rest: **مِّنْهُ — *minhu* — "from Him."** The ranks are not just ranks. They are ranks *from Him.* The source is named before the list even continues, as if Allah cannot let you receive the first gift without telling you Whose hand it left.

This is the grammar of overflow. The "tremendous reward" of 4:95 was a closed fist precisely *so that* 4:96 could open without a verb — could just *spill.* The vagueness in one ayah created the room for the flood in the next.

**What this single feature reveals:** the reward for the striver is described not as a transaction that was completed, but as a giving that is still falling, unbounded by any verb that could measure it.

## Element Two: *Darajāt* — The Word Built Like a Staircase

### The idea first

We are obsessed, all of us, with *levels.*

Think of how much of modern life is built on the ladder. The video game gives you experience points and you *level up.* The app gives you a streak and a tier — bronze, silver, gold, platinum. The company gives you a title, and above your title is another title, and above that another. The whole social internet is a leaderboard with a number next to every name.

We did not invent this hunger. We just keep building machines to feed it. The craving to *rise* — to be at a level, and then a higher level — is one of the deepest engines in the human chest.

Hold that. Allah is about to meet that exact hunger head-on.

### The word

The first gift is **دَرَجَٰت — *darajāt.***

Its root, *d-r-j*, is one of those Arabic roots whose physical meaning you can almost climb. A *daraja* is a **step** — a single stair in a staircase. The root carries the sense of moving *gradually, by stages,* one rung at a time. From this same root comes *tadarruj* — the idea of progressing step by step — and *adraja* — to fold something up in successive layers, roll by roll.

So when Allah names the reward *darajāt,* He is not handing the striver a flat prize. He is handing him a **staircase.** Plural. Levels rising above levels.

The classical commentary makes the physical image vivid. *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* glosses *darajāt* as **منازل بعضها فوق بعض** — *stations, one above another* — and adds the crucial qualifier *mina-l-karāma,* *of honoring:* these are not bare positions on a chart but dignities Allah confers. Al-Ṭabarī reaches for the same note, reading *darajātin minhu* as *faḍāʾil* — *favors* from Him — "stations drawn from the stations of *karāma,*" of His own honoring. Al-Muyassar then locates them precisely: *manāzil ʿāliya fī l-jannāt,* lofty stations *within the Gardens,* set apart for *khāṣṣat ʿibādih* — the innermost, special circle of the servants who strove. And al-Baghawī preserves a striking report from Ibn Muḥayrīz: that these are *seventy* degrees, and between every two of them is the distance a swift, well-bred horse would gallop for seventy years.

You feel the vertigo of that. The reward isn't a room. It's an ascent so vast that the gap between two of its steps is a lifetime of galloping.

### Now notice the deeper thing

Here is what I want you to see — and it answers the hunger we started with.

The word *darajāt* is **indefinite and plural.** Not "the ranks." Just — *ranks.* Unnumbered. Unbounded. The very vagueness we noticed in *ajran ʿaẓīmā* now lives inside this word too. Allah confirms that hierarchy is *real* — that there genuinely are higher and lower, that the one who strove is genuinely raised above the one who sat. He does not flatten everyone into sameness. **The ladder is real.**

But look at *which way the ladder points.* Every worldly ladder points sideways — toward other people. Your level only means something *measured against theirs.* The leaderboard is the point. You rise *over* someone.

The *darajāt* of this ayah point a different direction. They are **مِّنْهُ — from Him.** The staircase does not lead to a position above other servants. It leads *upward toward Allah.* The measure is not the person on the rung below you. The measure is your nearness to the One at the top of the stairs.

[PAUSE]

So the ayah does something with our hunger for levels that nothing in this world can do. It does not shame the hunger. It *redeems* it. It says: yes — you were built to climb. You were right to want to rise. You were just climbing the wrong staircase, in the wrong direction, measuring against the wrong thing.

**What this word reveals:** the desire to ascend is not a flaw to be crushed. It is a compass that was meant to point at God, and the whole tragedy of a wasted life is a staircase-shaped longing spent climbing toward people instead of toward Him.

## Element Three: The Three Gifts, The Two Names — And The One That's Missing

### The idea first

Let me give you a small picture.

A great king rewards a soldier. He grants him three things: a title, a pardon for old debts, and his personal friendship. And then, as the soldier leaves, the king signs the decree — and beneath his signature he writes two words describing *himself*: **"The Pardoner. The Loving."**

Now ask: why did the king seal the decree with *two* of the three gifts, and not the third? Why did he write "I am the Pardoner, I am the Loving" — but *not* "I am the Giver of Titles"?

Because two of those gifts came from *who he is.* And one of them came only from *what the soldier did.*

Hold that distinction. It is the whole secret of the end of this ayah.

### Look at the architecture

Watch the shape of the ayah. First, the three gifts:

1. **دَرَجَٰتٍ** — ranks
2. **مَغْفِرَةً** — forgiveness
3. **رَحْمَةً** — mercy

Then the seal — *"wa kāna-Llāhu..."* — *"And Allah is ever..."* — followed by two names:

- **غَفُورًا** — *Ghafūr* — ever-Forgiving (from the same root, *gh-f-r*, as gift number two, *maghfira*)
- **رَّحِيمًا** — *Rahīm* — ever-Merciful (from the same root, *r-ḥ-m*, as gift number three, *rahma*)

Do you see it?

The seal names the *forgiveness* again — as an eternal attribute. The seal names the *mercy* again — as an eternal attribute. **But the seal does not name the ranks.**

[PAUSE]

There is no closing name "*wa kāna-Llāhu Dhā darajāt*" — "and Allah is ever the Lord of Ranks." The first gift is given — and then *dropped* from the seal. Only the last two are lifted up and pressed into the eternal names of God.

This is the asymmetry I want us to live inside for the rest of our time together. **Read the silence as carefully as the speech.** Two gifts get echoed in the divine names. One does not. That gap is not empty. It is teaching.

### What the gap is teaching

Why would *darajāt* be left out of the seal?

Because the *darajāt* are tied to *you.* They are the gift that answers your striving. They are *minhu* — from Him — but they correspond to what you did. The seventy degrees, the ranks one above another: these track the effort of the strivers. In fact the classical commentators differed over what the *darajāt* even *are* — al-Ṭabarī notes plainly that "the people of interpretation disagreed" — and the disagreement is itself instructive. He preserves the report of Qatāda that the degrees are the *deeds themselves,* named and ranked: *al-Islām* a degree, *al-hijra* within Islam a degree, *al-jihād* a degree above that — stations the strivers *climbed* by striving (al-Baghawī echoes this, carrying the ascent up to martyrdom). Others — al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar — read them instead as ready-made *stations in Paradise,* one above another, awaiting the one who earned them. But whichever reading you take, the conclusion is the same: the *darajāt* are bound up with what the servant *did.* Ranks are, in part, *response.*

But *maghfira* and *rahma* are not a response to you at all. Look how Allah grounds them: **وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ — "and Allah *is ever*."** That word *kāna* in this construction does not mean "was" in the past. It means *has always been and unceasingly is.* Forgiving-ness and Mercy are not things Allah *did* because you strove. They are what He **was before you existed, is now, and will be when striving is over.**

So the architecture says something almost unbearable in its tenderness:

The ranks are *for what you did.*
The forgiveness and the mercy are *for who He is.*

And by sealing the ayah with only the second kind, Allah tells the striver — the one who climbed highest, the one who has the most "earning" to point to — **do not rest your weight on your ranks. Rest it on My names.**

### Now the question from the very beginning finally opens

Remember what we couldn't answer at the start: *why is forgiveness part of the reward for striving? What does the striver need forgiven?*

Here is the answer the architecture has been building toward.

The one who strove the hardest is *also* the one most aware of how flawed his striving was. He knows the moment of showing-off that slipped into his sincerity. He knows the fear he didn't fully conquer, the resentment he carried, the intention that wasn't pure all the way down. **The closer you climb toward Allah, the more clearly you see the dust on your own hands.**

So Allah, in His wisdom, does not reward the striver with a clean "well done" that would leave him standing on his own deeds. He rewards him with **forgiveness** — which quietly admits that even the best deed needed *covering* — the root *gh-f-r* literally means *to cover over.* Al-Muyassar does not soften this: the forgiveness here, it says, is *li-dhunūbihim* — *for their sins.* Even the *mujāhid* — even the servant Allah has just called *khāṣṣat ʿibādih,* the innermost circle of His own — arrives carrying wrong that needs covering. And then with **mercy** — *rahma* — which goes beyond covering the wrong, to *embracing* the servant in a tenderness he never earned at all. Al-Muyassar calls it *rahma wāsiʿa* — a *vast,* wide-open mercy "in which they delight": not a narrow pardon issued and filed away, but a field of grace they are given to *live inside.*

[PAUSE]

The highest rank, it turns out, is not reached by perfection. It is reached by a striver who is *forgiven for the imperfection of his striving* and then *embraced* beyond it. You climb with everything you have — and at the summit you discover that what carried you the last and highest step was never your climbing. It was Him.

**What this architecture reveals:** Allah lets you keep your ranks, but He will not let you stand on them. He seals the reward with His own names so that the very best of His servants arrives at the top held up by mercy, not by merit.

[PAUSE — major]

## Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what these three features have shown us, standing together.

The **missing verb** told us the giving is unmeasured — it pours rather than transacts.

The **staircase-word** told us our hunger to rise is real and was meant to point at God.

The **asymmetric seal** told us that even the one who climbs highest is carried, at the summit, by forgiveness and mercy rather than by his own deeds.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of reality itself, and about the two things every human heart is secretly trying to reconcile.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Ladder and the Lap

### Start where you already live

There are two deep cravings inside you, and most of your life is the war between them.

The first is the craving to **matter through achievement.** To climb. To be *more* tomorrow than you are today. To have it *count.* This is the ladder. When you're on it, you feel alive and purposeful — and also anxious, because the ladder never ends and someone is always above you.

The second is the craving to be **loved without having to earn it.** To be held simply because you are *you,* not because of your rank. This is the lap — the way a small child, who has accomplished nothing, is gathered up and embraced for no reason but love. When you're in it, you feel safe — but a restless part of you whispers that love you didn't earn doesn't really *count.*

Almost every person you know has chosen a side, and is quietly miserable about the half they gave up. The achievers are exhausted and unheld. The ones who long to just be loved feel like they never amounted to anything.

The wisdom of our age says you must choose. *Either* you are someone who climbs *or* you are someone who is simply loved.

### Now let the ayah illuminate it

Read the ayah again with those two cravings in your hands:

**Darajātin minhu** — *ranks from Him.* There is your ladder. Real. Confirmed. Allah Himself raises the striver. He does not say "everyone is the same, don't bother climbing." He says *climb — and I will lift you, rung over rung, seventy degrees of it, a galloping lifetime between each step.* Your hunger to *matter through striving* is not a sin to repent of. It is **answered.**

**Wa maghfiratan wa rahmah** — *and forgiveness, and mercy.* And there is your lap. The covering you didn't earn. The embrace beyond your deeds. Sealed into His eternal names so you can never lose it to a bad day or a failed climb.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the ayah just did? **It refused to make you choose.** In eight words, it handed the same servant *both* the ladder *and* the lap. You climb — *and* you are held. You strive for real ranks — *and* you are forgiven and embraced regardless. The achievement is real and the unearned love is real, and they are sitting in the same sentence, given to the same person, in the same breath.

This is what no leaderboard on earth can do. The leaderboard gives you the ladder and tells you that you are *only* your rank — fall, and you are nothing. The world's softer voices give you the lap and tell you ranks are an illusion — so your striving means nothing. Allah alone says: **your striving genuinely raises you, and you are genuinely held no matter how the striving goes.**

### Build it further

And notice the *order.* The ranks come first — *darajāt.* Then forgiveness and mercy. The lap comes *after* the ladder, not instead of it.

This is the deepest pastoral wisdom. Allah does not say "don't bother climbing, I'll love you anyway" — which would rot the soul into laziness. And He does not say "climb, and your love depends on the climb" — which would crush the soul into despair. He says: **climb with everything you have — and know that when you reach the top of your effort, you will be met not with a scoreboard but with forgiveness and an embrace.**

You are freed to strive *hard,* precisely because your striving is not what holds you. The mercy at the summit is what lets you climb without terror.

### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical thing. Tomorrow you will feel both cravings again. You will want to achieve, and you will want to be held.

Stop treating them as enemies. **Pour yourself into the climb** — the prayer offered with effort, the wealth given when it's hard, the self pushed into the path of Allah — because the ranks are *real* and He will lift you. **And refuse to let your standing rest on the climb** — because the seal of the ayah is not your rank, it is His mercy. Strive like everything depends on your deeds; rest like everything depends on His names. That is the life this ayah is describing.

## Theme Two: The Names That Were True Before You Tried

### Start where you already live

Think of the most insecure relationship you've ever been in — maybe a job, maybe a friendship, maybe something closer.

The defining feature of an insecure relationship is this: **the love is a response.** It exists *because* of what you provide. Stop performing, and you feel it cool. So you never stop performing. You are always, quietly, auditioning to keep something you are terrified is conditional. You can never fully rest, because the love began the day you started being useful, and you suspect it could end the day you stop.

Now here is the question that secretly haunts a striving believer: *Is my relationship with God like that?* Is His mercy a *response* to my deeds? If I have a bad month, a cold heart, a season where I can't climb — does the love cool? Am I auditioning before Allah the way I've auditioned before everyone else?

### Now let the ayah answer

Look at the single most important word in the seal: **كَانَ — *kāna.***

*"Wa **kāna**-Llāhu Ghafūran Rahīmā."* — *"And Allah **is ever** Forgiving, Most Merciful."*

In this construction, *kāna* does not report a past event. It declares a **standing, timeless reality.** The grammarians understood it as continuity without beginning or end: Allah *has always been* and *unceasingly is* Forgiving and Merciful.

Now feel the weight of where this sits. It comes *after* the striving has been described. After the ranks. After the reward. And it reaches *backward past all of it* to say: these names — *Ghafūr, Rahīm* — were not *generated* by your striving. **They were true before you ever lifted a finger.**

[PAUSE]

Allah was Forgiving before you sinned, so there would be forgiveness ready the moment you did.
Allah was Merciful before you were created, so there would be mercy waiting the moment you arrived.

His mercy is **not a response.** It is not the cooling, auditioning, conditional love you have known from people. It is an *eternal attribute* that pre-exists your performance entirely. You are not auditioning. You never were. The love did not begin the day you became useful, and it does not end the day you fail.

### Sit with the paradox

But hold on — doesn't that collapse the striving? If His mercy was true before I did anything, why climb at all?

Here is where the ayah is wiser than the paradox. Look again at the *full* shape: there are still **ranks.** The unconditional names do not erase the real ladder. The mercy that was true before you tried does not make your trying meaningless — it makes your trying *safe.*

Think of the most secure earthly love you can imagine — a parent who would love the child no matter what. Does that unconditional love make the child *less* eager to make the parent proud? No — it makes him *more* eager, and *free* while doing it. The child who knows he is loved unconditionally is the one who strives most joyfully, because he strives *out of* the love instead of *for* it.

That is exactly the architecture of our ayah. *Ghafūr* and *Rahīm* were true before you climbed — so you climb the *darajāt* not to *purchase* a love you fear is conditional, but as the natural overflow of a love you already have. **The names came first so the striving could be free.**

And notice how the classical commentators read these two eternal names: not as floating in the abstract, but as already *leaning toward this striver.* Al-Jalālayn glosses *Ghafūr* as forgiving *li-awliyāʾih* — *to His friends* — and *Rahīm* as merciful *to the people of His obedience.* Al-Muyassar reads them as forgiving *to whoever turns back to Him in repentance and returns,* and merciful *to the obedient who strive in His path.* See what that does. The attribute is timeless and unconditioned *in itself* — *kāna,* always and ever — and yet in the very same breath it bends toward the one who climbed: His friend, the one who repents, the one who strives. The love was true before you tried; and it was waiting, all along, precisely for the one who would.

### What this changes tomorrow

So tomorrow, when you fail — and you will, because the striver fails more visibly than the sitter — do not hear the old voice that says *the love just cooled, you blew the audition.*

Hear *kāna.* He *is ever* Forgiving. The attribute did not flicker because you stumbled. It was there before your stumble, it is there in the middle of it, and it will be there after.

And then — this is the practical turn — **let that security send you back to the climb,** not away from it. Insecure love makes you either frantic or paralyzed. Secure love makes you *generous.* The believer who has truly heard *kāna-Llāhu Ghafūran Rahīmā* is not the one who relaxes into doing nothing. He is the one who strives the hardest, because for the first time he is striving *from* a love instead of *for* one.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Now That We Can See It

Let's stand back and look at the whole shape, now that we've done the work to read it.

The ayah has a deliberate structure:

**Three gifts** — *darajāt, maghfira, rahma* — poured out with no verb to hold them, spilling from the closed fist of "a tremendous reward" in the ayah before.

**Two names** — *Ghafūr, Rahīm* — sealing the ayah, echoing the *second* and *third* gifts but pointedly **not** the first.

That missing echo — the absence of any name about *ranks* in the seal — is the entire theology of the verse drawn in negative space. The ladder is given but not enshrined; the mercy is given *and* eternalized. Allah lets you have your ranks. He will not let you stand on them. He moves your weight, in the final two words, off your striving and onto His names.

## The Two Discoveries Were One Discovery

The **linguistic** journey found a verb deliberately dropped so the giving could pour unmeasured, a word built like a staircase pointing up at God, and a seal that keeps the mercy and quietly releases the rank.

The **thematic** journey found a heart torn between the ladder and the lap, and a love it feared was a conditional audition.

These are the same discovery from two directions. The *grammar* drops the verb so the gift can overflow; the *heart* is told its striving need not earn the love, because the love overflowed first. The *seal* keeps mercy and releases rank; the *heart* is taught to climb hard and rest soft. **What the words are doing to the sentence is exactly what Allah is doing to the soul of the one who strives.**

## Questions to Carry

1. Which staircase have I actually been climbing — the one that points sideways at other people, or the one that points *minhu*, up toward Him?

2. When I fail, do I hear an audition cooling, or do I hear *kāna* — He *is ever* Forgiving?

3. Have I been forcing myself to choose between *achieving* and *being held* — when the ayah hands both to the same servant in one breath?

4. If even the highest striver arrives at the summit needing *forgiveness*, what does that say about the dust on my own best deeds — and can I let it be *covered* instead of denied?

5. Am I striving *for* a love I'm afraid is conditional, or *from* a love I finally believe was true before I ever tried?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah answers the human hunger to rise by granting real, ascending ranks — yet seals the reward not with His rank-giving but with His own eternal names of Forgiveness and Mercy, so that the one who climbs highest arrives carried by what he could never earn.**

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to choose between the ladder and the lap anymore. That war you've been fighting — *do I matter because I achieve, or am I loved without earning it* — the ayah ends it in eight words.

Climb. The ranks are real and He will lift you, rung over rung. And when you reach the top of your effort, let yourself be met by *Ghafūr, Rahīm* — covered, and embraced, by names that were true before you took your first step. Strive like it all depends on your deeds. Rest like it all depends on Him. Because, in the seal of this ayah, it does.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who are ever Forgiving, ever Merciful — raise us by the ranks that come from You, and do not let us stand on them. Cover what was flawed in our striving with Your forgiveness, and gather what remains into Your mercy. Let us climb toward You with everything we have, and rest, the whole way up, in names that were true before we ever tried. Āmīn.*
