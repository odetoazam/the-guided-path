# The Pillar of Intent: When a Single Choice Prepares Its Own Exile

## Introduction

Let us begin by listening to the words themselves.

> **وَمَن يَقْتُلْ مُؤْمِنًا مُّتَعَمِّدًا فَجَزَآؤُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ خَـٰلِدًا فِيهَا وَغَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَلَعَنَهُۥ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُۥ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا**

*Wa man yaqtul mu'minan muta'ammidan fa-jazā'uhu jahannamu khālidan fīhā, wa ghadiba Allāhu 'alayhi wa la'anahu wa a'adda lahu 'adhāban 'azīmā.*

"And whoever kills a believer intentionally — his recompense is Hell, abiding therein; and the wrath of Allah is upon him, and He has cursed him, and has prepared for him a tremendous punishment."

I want you to feel where you are standing when these words arrive.

### Rebuilding the world

You are in Madinah. The community is new, fragile, knit together out of people who, only a few years ago, belonged to tribes that raided and avenged and counted blood the way merchants count coin. In the world they came from, a life had a *price* — so many camels, a feud settled, a score evened. Killing was not unthinkable. It was *currency*.

And now, just one verse earlier — in 4:92 — Allah has been speaking, at length, about what happens when a believer kills another believer *by mistake*. Read that verse and you will be struck by how much **care** is in it. Free a believing slave. Pay the blood-money to the family. If you cannot find a slave to free, then fast two consecutive months as a turning back to Allah. Layer upon layer of repair. The whole tone of 4:92 is: *here is how the wound is healed, here is how the soul is mended, here is the way back.*

[PAUSE]

And then comes verse 93. Same act — one believer, one life ended. But change a single thing — the *intention* — and the entire universe of the verse changes with it. The repair vanishes. The blood-money vanishes. The path back is not mentioned. Instead: Hell. Abiding. Wrath. The curse. A tremendous punishment prepared.

Four hammer blows, where a moment ago there was only mending.

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us in our tracks. The *act* in verse 92 and the *act* in verse 93 are, on the outside, identical. A body falls. A soul leaves. The same blood is on the ground. What is the one thing that stands between the gentle ladder of repair and the avalanche of divine wrath?

It is something invisible. Something that lives entirely inside a human chest. A single word in this verse names it — and that word, in its very grammar, is built out of the Arabic word for a *pillar*.

What is a pillar doing inside the verse on murder? Stay with me. Because once you see it, you will never read the difference between an accident and a sin the same way again.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: مُّتَعَمِّدًا — the deliberate setting-up of intent

#### Teaching the structure first

Before we touch the word, let me hand you a small tool from Arabic grammar — not as a technicality, but because you cannot see what is hidden in this word without it.

Arabic builds its verbs in patterns, called *forms*. Take a simple three-letter root and you can pour it into ten different molds, and each mold *adds* a layer of meaning. One of these molds is what scholars call **Form V** — the *tafa'ʿala* pattern. And Form V has a very particular flavor: it is **reflexive and deliberate**. It means to take an action *upon yourself*, to deliberately bring a state *into being for your own self*, to *cultivate* something within you.

Here is an everyday example. The root *'-l-m* means "to know." In Form V it becomes *ta'allama* — "to learn." Do you feel the difference? Knowledge can just *happen* to you — you overhear something, you stumble onto a fact. But *ta'allama*, learning, is something you **do to yourself, on purpose, with effort, over time.** You sit down. You repeat. You build the knowledge into yourself, brick by brick. That is the fingerprint of Form V: *deliberate self-construction.*

Hold that.

#### Opening the word

Now look at the fourth word of the verse:

**مُّتَعَمِّدًا** — *muta'ammidan.*

Our translations render it, flatly, "intentionally." A small adverb. Easy to skip over. But it is not a small word. It is a Form V active participle — the *exact* deliberate-self-construction pattern we just met — built on the root *'-m-d*.

#### Deepening

Let us sit with that root, *'-m-d*, because it is doing quiet, enormous work.

The classical lexicons gather a family of meanings under *'-m-d*. From it comes *'amada* — to prop something up, to support it. From it comes **'amūd** — a *pillar*, a *column*, the upright post that holds a tent or a roof in place. And from it comes *'amd* — **intent, deliberate purpose.**

Notice how those meanings hold hands. To intend something, in the deep logic of this root, is to **erect a pillar in your will.** It is to take a thing and make it *stand firm* inside you, to prop it up so it does not fall, to give it structural support in your soul. An idle thought drifts through the mind and is gone. But an *'amd* — a deliberate intent — is something you have raised up, braced, and decided to *hold standing*.

Now put the Form V mold back on top of it. *Muta'ammid* is not merely someone to whom a killing happened. It is someone who has **deliberately raised the pillar of this act within himself.** Who has taken the intent, propped it up, braced it, and built his action upon that foundation — knowingly, on purpose, as an act of inner construction.

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why this single word is the entire hinge of the verse?

In 4:92, the killing had no pillar. It collapsed *onto* the person — an accident, a thing that *fell*, unsupported by any intent. And so Allah's response is to help him rebuild: free a soul, mend the family, fast and return.

But in 4:93, the person has built. He has erected, with his own hands, inside his own chest, a structure whose entire purpose was to end a believing life. And the verse that follows is not about a thing that *fell* — it is about a thing that was *raised*. The punishment answers the architecture.

#### The composite of the scholars

The classical commentators circle this word *muta'ammidan* and ask: what exactly makes a killing deliberate? They are not disagreeing so much as photographing the same act from different angles.

Al-Qurtubi reports the view of 'Aṭā', al-Nakha'ī and others: the deliberate killer is the one who strikes with something *made for cutting and killing* — a sword, a spear-point, a blade — or with something he *knows* will kill, like a heavy stone. The deliberateness shows in the **instrument**: you do not pick up a sword by accident.

Al-Ṭabarī glosses the word from inside the will itself. He renders *muta'ammidan* with two phrases laid one on the other: *'āmidan qatlahu* — "deliberate in his killing" — and then, deeper, *murīdan itlāfa nafsihi* — "**willing the destruction of his very soul.**" Sit with that second phrase. The deliberateness is not merely choosing to act; it is *wanting the annihilation* of a living person. The intent does not stop at the hand or the blade — it reaches all the way to the desired end: that this soul should *cease.*

Al-Jalālayn captures it from inside the mind: *bi-an yaqṣida qatlahu bi-mā yaqtulu ghāliban 'āliman bi-īmānihi* — "that he *intends* his killing, by a means that usually kills, *knowing* that he is a believer." Three things stacked: the aim, the lethal means, and the knowledge of who stands before him. And al-Muyassar adds the frame that the *fuqahā'* never let fall: this is a killing *'an 'amdin bi-ghayri ḥaqq* — "deliberately, **and without right.**" The verse is not speaking of the lawful taking of a life — the just retribution, the defense — but of *i'tidā'*, aggression: a soul ended deliberately *and* unjustly. Both conditions stand in the word.

Lay those photographs over one another and the composite is exactly what *muta'ammid* already told us: a person who has raised the pillar of intent, chosen the lethal tool, willed the very undoing of the soul before him, and known — *known* — that the one before him carries faith, and that no right gave him license. Every angle confirms the morphology. The deliberateness is not a vague mood. It is a built thing.

**What this single word reveals: the entire difference between divine repair and divine wrath is not in the hand that strikes, but in the pillar the soul chose to raise before the hand ever moved.**

### Element Two: The architecture of the verdict — a nominal sentence, then four falling verbs

#### Teaching the structure first

A second tool, and then we will watch the verse do something extraordinary with it.

Arabic can make a statement in two very different ways. It can use a **verbal sentence** — built on a verb — which tends to convey *an event*, something that *happens*, unfolds in time. Or it can use a **nominal sentence** — built on nouns, with no verb at the core — which conveys *a settled fact*, a fixed and standing reality. "The sky rained" is an event. "The sky is blue" is a state of affairs. One moves; the other simply *is.*

Watch what the verse does with these two registers.

#### Opening the construction

The apodosis — the "then" half of the sentence — begins:

**فَجَزَآؤُهُۥ جَهَنَّمُ** — *fa-jazā'uhu jahannam* — "then his recompense is Hell."

There is no verb here. No "will be," no "becomes." It is a pure nominal sentence: *his-recompense — Hell.* Two nouns set side by side, and between them, silence. Grammatically, this is the register of the **settled, standing fact.** Not "Hell will happen to him" — but "his recompense *is* Hell," fixed, as plain and immovable as "the sky is blue." The very absence of a verb makes it feel like a verdict already entered into the record.

And notice the *word* chosen for the consequence. *Jazā'* is not merely "punishment" — it is *requital*, what is paid back, the wage an act earns. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it exactly so: *thawābuhu min qatlihi iyyāhu jahannam* — "his **wage** for killing him is Hell." It is the same root used, elsewhere, for the reward of the righteous; here it is turned, with terrible precision, upon the murderer. The Fire is not an external penalty bolted onto the crime — it is what the deed *itself* is worth, returned to its author. The pillar he raised is paid back in kind.

And then — having stated the fact — the verse releases four things in sequence:

1. **خَالِدًا فِيهَا** — *khālidan fīhā* — abiding therein
2. **وَغَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ** — *wa ghadiba Allāhu 'alayhi* — and Allah's wrath fell upon him
3. **وَلَعَنَهُۥ** — *wa la'anahu* — and He cursed him
4. **وَأَعَدَّ لَهُۥ عَذَابًا عَظِيمًا** — *wa a'adda lahu 'adhāban 'azīman* — and prepared for him a tremendous punishment

#### Deepening

Now notice something about those four. Two of them — *ghadiba* (was wrathful) and *la'ana* (cursed) — are in the **perfect tense.** The perfect, in Arabic, is the tense of the *completed*, the *already-done*. Not "Allah will be angry" — but, grammatically, **"Allah's wrath has fallen."** Not "He will curse" — but **"He has cursed."** The verse speaks of these as though they are *finished facts*, already inscribed, the moment the pillar of intent went up.

[PAUSE]

Let that land. The killer may still be walking the earth. The sun still rises on him. And yet the grammar has already closed the book: the wrath *has* descended, the curse *has* been spoken. He is living inside a verdict that, on the level of reality, is already complete.

And then the fourth blow. *Wa a'adda* — "and He has **prepared**."

This is a Form IV verb — *a'adda*, from the root *'-d-d*, whose Form I, *'adda*, means "to count, to number." In Form IV it becomes transitive in the sense of **making something ready, setting it in order, having it standing by.** It is the word you would use for a host who has *prepared* the table before the guest arrives. The food is already laid out. The seat is already set.

Feel the chill in that. The punishment is not improvised at the moment of judgment. It has been **made ready in advance.** It is *waiting.* The grammar of preparation tells you that the consequence pre-exists the arrival of the one who earned it — the table is already laid.

#### The shape of the four

And look at the *shape* of these four together, because the order is not random. *Jahannam* — the place. *Khulūd* — the duration, abiding within it. *Ghadab* — the wrath, the inner reality of how Allah meets him. *La'na* — the curse, which we will see in a moment means *distance*. And *'adhāb 'azīm* — the magnitude, sealed with the adjective *'azīm*, "tremendous."

Place, duration, relationship, distance, magnitude. The verse builds a complete world of consequence — a where, a how-long, a how-He-meets-you, a how-far-from-Him, and a how-great. Against verse 92's ladder of repair, here is a wall, built in five courses of stone.

**What this construction reveals: the deliberate act does not earn a future punishment that *might* come — it steps into a verdict the grammar treats as already entered, already prepared, already standing.**

### Element Three: لَعَنَهُۥ — the curse as distance

#### Opening the word

Among those four blows, I want to slow down on one, because we use its English translation so carelessly that we have lost what it actually means.

**وَلَعَنَهُۥ** — *wa la'anahu* — "and He cursed him."

In English, "curse" carries a tangle of associations — a witch's spell, foul language, bad luck. Clear all of that away. The Arabic root *l-'-n* has one core, lexically attested meaning, and the classical lexicons are unanimous on it.

#### Deepening

*La'n* means **distancing. Driving away. Expulsion from a presence.** Al-Jalālayn, glossing this very word, says it plainly: *ab'adahu min raḥmatihi* — "He removed him far from His mercy." And al-Muyassar, reaching independently for the same image, renders the curse as *ṭarduhu min raḥmatihi* — His *driving him out* from His mercy. Two commentators, the same gesture: not a hex laid upon him, but a *door closed behind him*. He is **distanced**. The curse is *exile.*

Now hold this against the whole shape of the verse, and a terrible symmetry appears.

What did this person do? He ended the life of a **mu'min** — a *believer*, one connected to Allah, one within the circle of faith and mercy. He severed someone from the nearness of life and faith.

And what is the divine response, named with the root *l-'-n*? He is **distanced.** Severed. Put *outside* the circle of mercy. The one who cut another off from the nearness of life is himself cut off from the nearness of the All-Merciful.

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest stepping-stone in the verse. *Ghadab* — wrath — we picture as heat, as fire, a physical boiling-over. *La'n* — curse — we picture as words, as a spell. But strip away the physical images and what is the unseen reality underneath both? **Separation.** Distance from Allah. The fire of *jahannam*, the heat of *ghadab*, the exile of *la'n* — they are all pointing at one single unseen thing: to be *far* from the One whose nearness is the only true life. Hell, at its root, is *distance.* And the deliberate killer, the verse tells us, has earned the deepest distance — because he himself dealt in severance.

**What this word reveals: the punishment is not arbitrary cruelty answering a crime — it is the crime's own shape, reflected back. He who severs is severed. He who exiles a soul from life is exiled from the Source of life.**

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things. A single word, *muta'ammid*, that locates the entire verdict not in the hand but in the *pillar of intent* the soul deliberately raised. A grammar of judgment that treats the consequence as **already entered, already prepared, standing and waiting.** And a curse, *la'n*, that turns out to mean *distance* — the act's own severance reflected back upon the one who committed it.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because a verse this severe raises two questions that live in every honest heart: *What does my intention actually weigh?* And — *if the verdict is this absolute, where on earth is the door of mercy?*

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The invisible thing that restructures the world

Let me start where you already live.

Every legal system humanity has ever built draws a line that you, too, draw instinctively. Imagine two people, and in both cases someone has died at their hands. The first was driving carefully, within the speed limit, when a child ran out from between two parked cars; he braked, he swerved, and still — a death. The second spent a week watching his target, learned his routine, waited in the dark, and struck.

Both stand before you with the same fact on their record: *a person is dead because of me.* And yet — you do not, for one second, hold them the same. Your whole soul recoils at treating them alike. We have separate words for them: *accident* and *murder*. *Manslaughter* and *premeditation.* The courts will send one home to grieve and the other away for life.

Now ask yourself: *what* did the two people do differently? On the level of physics — of bodies and cars and falling and blood — sometimes almost nothing. The difference is **entirely invisible.** It happened inside a chest, where no camera can reach. It is the *pillar of intent.* And we — every human society, the moment we sit down to weigh justice — discover that this invisible thing is the most *real* thing in the entire event. It restructures the whole moral universe of the act.

[PAUSE]

This is exactly what 4:92 and 4:93 are doing, side by side. Same death. Same blood. And Allah draws *precisely* the line your conscience draws — except He draws it with perfect sight. Where your courts must *guess* at intent, sifting evidence, He sees the pillar directly. The whole pairing of these two verses is a revelation that **the thing you cannot see is the thing that is weighed.**

And here is where it turns from courtroom to mirror. We tend to think we are judged on our *behavior* — the visible record, the deeds others can witness. But these two verses tell us the deepest variable is the one *no one* sees: the orientation of the heart, the *pillar* you chose to raise or not raise. The Prophet ﷺ said it directly — *innamā al-a'mālu bi'n-niyyāt*, "actions are but by intentions." This verse is that hadith written into law with the most extreme example possible: the *same* killing, sorted into accident or atrocity by intent alone.

So the question this theme leaves on your tongue is not "what have I done?" It is gentler and far more searching: **what have I been building?** What pillars have I quietly raised inside myself — pillars of resentment I prop up daily, of a grudge I brace and refuse to let fall, of a plan I am slowly constructing in the dark of my own chest? The verse warns us that the inner architecture is not private decoration. It is the realest thing about us. It is what is weighed.

**The change for tomorrow:** Tonight, before sleep, do not only review what your hands did today. Walk through your *chest.* Ask of any standing resentment or quiet intention: *am I propping this up? Am I bracing it so it does not fall?* And if it is something Allah would not have you build — let it collapse. Withdraw the pillar. That is an act of worship no one will ever see, and it is the very thing the verse tells us is weighed.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Living between the wall and the door

Now to the harder question — the one this verse has provoked in sincere hearts for fourteen centuries.

Begin, again, where you live. Think of how a parent speaks to a child standing at the edge of a busy road. The parent does not murmur, "Now sweetheart, there is a small statistical risk." The parent's voice cracks like a whip: *"If you EVER run into that road—!"* The threat is total, absolute, no exceptions named. And the *reason* it has no exceptions is not because the parent's love has exceptions — it is because the love is so *complete* that it refuses, in that instant, to dilute the warning with a single qualifier. The severity *is* the love, weaponized to keep the child alive.

Hold that, and come back to our verse. Because the great tension here is plain: 4:93 names *no door.* It says Hell, abiding, wrath, curse, tremendous punishment — and stops. No "unless he repents." No "except whom Allah forgives." The wall is built to the sky with no gate drawn in it.

And yet — elsewhere in the very same Book — Allah says: *"O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, despair not of the mercy of Allah; indeed Allah forgives **all** sins"* (39:53). And He says of the gravest sins, including unjust killing, that the one who *repents and believes and does righteous deeds* — Allah will **transform** their evil deeds into good (25:68–70). Two voices in one revelation. How do they meet?

This is precisely where the classical scholars stood, and they did not flinch. Let me give you their positions honestly, as an *ikhtilāf*, a real spectrum of understanding — not a tidy single answer.

One reading, reported by al-Jalālayn from **Ibn 'Abbās**, takes the verse at its surface (*'alā ẓāhirihā*) and treats it as decisive — going so far as to hold that it **abrogates** (*nāsikha*) the other verses that promise forgiveness for the one who deliberately murders a believer. This is the most severe position in the spectrum: the wall is the wall, and no later gentleness was meant to soften it.

A second reading, also preserved in al-Jalālayn, understands *khulūd* — "abiding" — here as referring to **one who deems the killing lawful** (*istiḥlāl*) — for to consider the murder of a believer *permissible* is itself a departure from faith, and so eternity follows from that, not merely from the act.

A third reading, which al-Jalālayn states plainly: this is the recompense **if Allah chooses to requite him** — *in jūziya* — and it does not contradict Allah's promise to forgive what is less than *shirk* for whom He wills, because Allah does not break a *promise* of reward, but He may, out of mercy, *not carry out* a threatened punishment. Withholding a threatened punishment is not a flaw — it is generosity.

And al-Jalālayn anchors this last reading in the Book itself, pointing to the verse of *al-Baqara* (2:178) as having *clarified* the matter — for there, addressing the very one who killed deliberately, Allah still calls him the *brother* of the slain man's heir: *"but if any remission is made to him by his brother…"* If the deliberate killer is named, in the Qur'an's own diction, a **brother** to the family of the one he killed, then the bond of faith has not been severed at the root — and a believer who remains, however gravely fallen, a *brother* in faith is not handed the unending abiding of the one who left faith entirely. The severe *khulūd* of 4:93, on this reading, is the wage of the act's full enormity, not a decree that the believing soul is lost forever.

And al-Muyassar makes the mercy door explicit, adding after the warning: *but He, Glorified, pardons and shows grace to the people of faith, and does not requite them with eternity in Hell.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the scholars are *doing*? They are holding the parent's whip-crack warning and the parent's bottomless love **at the same time**, without letting either one cancel the other. The severity of 4:93 is real — it is meant to make the blood of a believer feel as heavy as it actually is, to stop your hand before it ever moves. And the mercy of 39:53 is real — the door of sincere repentance is never, ever bolted while breath remains.

So how does a believing heart *live* between them? Not by choosing one verse and discarding the other. The fear and the hope are two wings, and a bird with one wing cannot fly. You take the wall of 4:93 and you let it do its work: you let it make sin *terrifying*, so that you flee from it. And you take the door of 39:53 and you let *it* do its work: you let it make *despair* impossible, so that no matter how far you have run, you can always turn back.

The disease is to reverse them — to take mercy as a license for sin ("Allah will forgive, so why not?") and to take severity as a reason for despair ("I have done too much, there is no point"). Both are the whisper of the same enemy. The verse before us is the wall *precisely so that* you never have to test the door.

**The change for tomorrow:** Notice which wing your soul is short of. If you are someone who treats sin lightly, who has grown casual — read 4:93 slowly, three times, and let the wall rise. Let yourself feel the weight. But if you are someone crushed under what you have already done, convinced the gate is shut — hear al-Muyassar and 39:53: *the door of sincere repentance is open while you breathe.* The same God who built this wall is the One who calls you, in the same Book, by the tender name *"My servants,"* and tells you not to despair. Run to Him, not from Him.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing it together

Stand back now and see how the two halves of this reflection were one insight, approached from two directions.

The **language** showed us that the entire verdict turns on *muta'ammid* — the *pillar of intent*, an invisible thing raised inside the chest. The **theme** of intention showed us that this invisible thing is, in fact, the realest thing we possess, the thing the whole moral universe — and Allah Himself — actually weighs.

The **language** showed us that *la'n* means *distance*, and that the punishment is the crime's own shape reflected back — the severer, severed. The **theme** of the wall and the door showed us that this severity exists *for our protection*, a parent's absolute warning born of an absolute love — and that the same Book which builds this wall draws, just verses and surahs away, a door that is never bolted.

The severity and the mercy are not two different Gods. They are the two hands of the One who values a single believing soul so highly that He would warn you with thunder to keep you from harming it — and value *your* soul so highly that He would never close the door of return, no matter what you have done.

### A note on where this verse was born

One major tradition, reported by al-Baghawī, places the revelation of this verse around the incident of **Miqyas ibn Ṣubāba**, who found his brother killed, received the rightful blood-money for him from the Prophet ﷺ — and then, his heart unsatisfied with justice, deliberately killed the man who had paid it, and turned away from faith. If that occasion is correct, then the verse was born exactly at the spot where a man chose *vengeance over justice*, where he raised the pillar of deliberate killing even after right had been done by him. The setting itself preaches the verse. (Other narrations and concerns are preserved by the commentators; this is one major reported occasion, not the only word said about it.)

### Questions to carry

1. What pillars am I quietly propping up inside my chest right now — and would I want them *standing* on the Day I am shown my own heart?
2. Where in my life have I been judging others by their visible deeds, while excusing myself by my invisible intentions — when Allah sees both with equal clarity?
3. Which wing is my soul missing — the fear that flees from sin, or the hope that refuses to despair? And how would today look if I grew the one I lack?
4. The killer in this verse *severed* a soul from life and was *severed* from mercy. In what smaller ways do I cut people off — and what might that be doing to my own nearness to Allah?
5. If a single believing life weighs *this* much in the sight of Allah — what does that tell me about the weight of my own soul, and the souls of every person I will meet tomorrow?

### One-sentence distillation

When a person deliberately raises within himself the pillar of destroying a believing soul, he constructs, in that one invisible choice, his own exile from the very Mercy he was killing — and the only thing that can pull down that wall is the door of sincere return, which the same God keeps open while breath remains.

### The closing invitation

Let this verse make two things heavier in you at once. Let it make the harming of any believing soul — by hand, by tongue, by the slow severance of a grudge — feel as weighty as it truly is. And let it make *despair* feel as foolish as it truly is. Walk away not crushed, and not careless, but *awake*: aware that what you build in the dark of your chest is weighed in the light, and aware that the One who weighs it is calling you, by name, *not to run from Him but toward Him.*

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You who see the pillars we raise in secret — let us build nothing within our chests that would shame us before You, and pull down whatever standing thing in us severs us from Your mercy. You who built this wall out of love for the souls You made — keep us far from the deliberate harm of any soul, and keep open for us, until our last breath, the door of return. *Āmīn.*
