# When the Good Itself Is Withdrawn

## Surah An-Nisa, Ayah 160

> فَبِظُلْمٍ مِّنَ ٱلَّذِينَ هَادُوا۟ حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ طَيِّبَـٰتٍ أُحِلَّتْ لَهُمْ وَبِصَدِّهِمْ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ كَثِيرًا

*Fa-bi-ẓulmin mina-lladhīna hādū ḥarramnā ʿalayhim ṭayyibātin uḥillat lahum, wa-bi-ṣaddihim ʿan sabīli-llāhi kathīrā.*

**"So for the wrongdoing of those who became Jews, We made unlawful for them good things that had been lawful for them — and for their hindering many from the way of Allah."**

---

## Introduction: The Verdict After the Trial

Let's begin by placing ourselves inside the moment this verse arrives.

For nearly ten verses now, Allah has been recounting a history. Not a triumphant history. A history of breach. Read what comes just before: they broke the covenant. They disbelieved in the signs of Allah. They killed prophets without right. They said, "Our hearts are wrapped." They demanded to see Allah with their own eyes in the open. They took the calf. They slandered a pure woman, Maryam, with a monstrous lie. And they boasted — *we killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary.*

It is a long indictment. Verse after verse, the charges accumulate like documents stacking on a judge's bench.

And then comes our verse. It opens with a single letter — **فَ**, *fa* — "so," "therefore," "and as a result."

This is the verdict.

Now, here is what I want you to feel before we go a single word further. When you imagine a divine consequence for sins this severe — killing prophets, worshipping idols, slandering the chaste — what do you expect? You expect fire. You expect the earth to swallow. You expect plague, flood, thunder. The dramatic punishments of the old narratives.

But that is not what this verse announces.

[PAUSE]

The consequence Allah names here is quieter. Stranger. More intimate. He says: *good things that were lawful for you — We made them unlawful.*

Not "We destroyed you." Not "We struck you down." But: **the circle of good things you were permitted to enjoy — We made it smaller.**

Why would *that* be the headline consequence? Why, out of everything Allah could have named, does He name the contraction of the permitted? What does it mean that the punishment for wrongdoing is that *good things get taken off the table* — not as a deprivation imposed from outside, but as something the wrongdoing itself produced?

That is the question we are going to live inside today. Stay with me. Because by the end, I think you will see that this verse is describing something happening in your own life right now — something far closer to home than the history of a distant nation.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Comes First — *fa-bi-ẓulmin*

Let me teach you something about Arabic word order before we touch this verse.

In English, we have a fairly fixed sentence order: subject, verb, object. "The man hit the ball." If I move the words around — "the ball, the man hit" — it sounds broken, like poetry or Yoda.

Arabic is different. Arabic can place words in their normal order, *or* it can deliberately move a word to the front of the sentence. And when a word is moved forward — when something that would normally come later is pushed to the very front — the Arabs understood this as a signal. The fronted word is the one being *emphasized*. It is the word the whole sentence is really *about*. Grammarians call this **taqdīm** — "putting forward."

Think of it like a spotlight on a dark stage. Everything else is in shadow; the fronted word stands in the light.

Now look at how our verse opens:

> فَبِظُلْمٍ ... حَرَّمْنَا
>
> *fa-bi-ẓulmin ... ḥarramnā*
>
> "So because of **wrongdoing** ... We made unlawful."

The natural order would be: "We made unlawful for them good things — because of their wrongdoing." Verb first, then the reason. But that is not what Allah did. He took the *reason* — the wrongdoing — and pushed it all the way to the front, before the verb, before everything.

The classical commentator al-Qurtubī noticed exactly this. He writes that the wrongdoing was placed first — *qaddama-ẓ-ẓulma ʿalā at-taḥrīm* — "because it is the very point that was meant to be conveyed: that it is the *cause* of the prohibition."

Let that land. The whole purpose of the sentence is to make one thing unmistakable: **this consequence has a cause, and the cause is yours.** Allah does not let the prohibition appear first, where you might read it as arbitrary — as God simply deciding to make life harder. No. He puts the *ẓulm* in the spotlight, at the front, so you cannot miss the chain of causation. *You did this. This is why.*

[PAUSE]

But there's more in this opening, and it's hiding in something most translations cannot carry.

Look at the word ظُلْمٍ — *ẓulmin*. In Arabic, nouns can be definite ("the wrongdoing") or indefinite ("a wrongdoing," "some wrongdoing"). This word is **indefinite** — it carries that little *-in* ending, the *tanwīn*. And here is a feature of classical Arabic that takes time to feel: an indefinite noun in a context like this is not vague. It is *magnifying*. It means something like: *because of wrongdoing — and what wrongdoing!* A wrongdoing so vast it doesn't even need naming; its enormity is built into the very looseness of the word.

So the opening is not a flat "because they did wrong." It is closer to: **"So — because of a wrongdoing of staggering proportion, from those who became Jews — We made unlawful..."**

The grammar itself is recoiling at the scale of it. And the classical reading hears exactly this. Al-Muyassar, summarizing the plain sense, does not render *ẓulm* as a mild fault but as *adh-dhunūb al-ʿaẓīma* — "grave, enormous sins." The magnification we feel in the bare indefinite noun is precisely how the tradition has always understood it: not a single slip, but a vast and accumulated wrongdoing.

And here is something worth noticing before we move on. The verse leaves the *ẓulm* unnamed — grammatically open, magnified by its very vagueness. But the Qur'an does not leave it unnamed for long. The very next breath, the verse that immediately follows (4:161), reaches in and itemizes what this "wrongdoing" concretely contained: *"and for their taking of usury, though they were forbidden it, and their devouring people's wealth wrongfully."* Ibn Kathīr draws the line directly: the *ẓulm* of our verse opens out, in the next, into riba taken after it had been forbidden, and the swallowing of others' property *bil-bāṭil* — by falsehood. So the structure is deliberate: first the word is left huge and unspecified, *and what wrongdoing!* — and then, having let its enormity register, the Qur'an names representative instances of it. Generalize to stagger you; specify so you cannot pretend it was abstract.

And notice the structure of cause and effect now fully exposed:

- The *cause*: ẓulm — wrongdoing.
- The *effect*: ḥarramnā — the forbidding of good things.

One clear statement of what this single fronted word reveals: **the verse is built, from its very first letter, to destroy the illusion that hardship in life is random.** The constriction came *from* something. And the something was not God's stinginess. It was their own wrongdoing, spotlit at the front of the sentence so no one could look away from the link.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Two Verbs Facing Each Other — *ḥarramnā* and *uḥillat*

Now we come to the heart of the verse, and I want to slow us right down, because Allah has done something here with two verbs that, once you see it, you cannot un-see.

First, let me teach the underlying tool.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — "forms." The simplest form just describes an action. But there is a form — grammarians call it **Form II**, made by doubling the middle letter — that takes a thing and *declares it to be so*, or *renders it so*. From the root *Ḥ-R-M*, which means sacredness, forbiddenness, something set apart and off-limits, Form II gives us **ḥarrama** — "to render forbidden," "to declare ḥarām." It doesn't just describe; it *establishes* the new reality.

And there is another form — **Form IV** — that does something similar from the opposite root. From *Ḥ-L-L*, which carries the sense of something untied, loosened, released, freely available — Form IV gives us **aḥalla** — "to render lawful," "to set free for use," "to declare ḥalāl."

Now watch Allah place these two opposites in a single breath:

> حَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ طَيِّبَـٰتٍ أُحِلَّتْ لَهُمْ
>
> *ḥarramnā ʿalayhim ṭayyibātin uḥillat lahum*
>
> "**We rendered forbidden** to them good things that **had been rendered lawful** for them."

Sit with the architecture of this. The *same* good things appear twice — once as forbidden, once as lawful. They didn't change. The food, the provision, the good things — they were the same good things. What changed was their *status before God*. What was once *uḥillat* — opened, loosened, freely available — became *ḥurrimat* — sealed, set off-limits, forbidden.

[PAUSE]

And look at the small but devastating detail in the second verb. أُحِلَّتْ — *uḥillat* — is in the **passive**, and it points to the *past*: these things "had been made lawful." There was an earlier era of openness. A time when the table was wide. When the good things of the earth were spread before them and the word over all of it was: *ḥalāl, ḥalāl, ḥalāl — go ahead, this is yours.*

The verse makes you stand in the *after* and look back at the *before*.

This is the grief inside the grammar. It is not the grief of someone who never had. It is the grief of someone who *had it, and lost it — by their own hand.* The good was real. The permission was real. And then, brick by brick, the wrongdoing built a wall across the very provisions that had once been open.

Now — what were these "good things"? Here the classical scholars are in agreement, and they point us across the Qur'an. Al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, and al-Qurṭubī all direct us to Surah al-Anʿām (6:146), where Allah specifies: *"And to those who became Jews We forbade every animal with claws, and of cattle and sheep We forbade them their fat..."* Al-Jalālayn pins it to that exact phrase — *ḥarramnā kulla dhī ẓufur*, "every animal with claws." These were genuine goods — wholesome provision — withdrawn.

But I want you to notice the theological gear-shift the scholars make here, because it matters enormously. Earlier in the Qur'an, when other foods were forbidden to the Children of Israel, the framing was sometimes that *they themselves* had forbidden things upon themselves, or that certain prohibitions predated revelation. But *here*, the framing is unambiguous and it comes from Allah's own voice: **ḥarramnā — "WE made it forbidden."** This was not their legislation. This was a divine response. A prohibition issued *by God, in consequence of conduct.*

And when you read this verse beside the one that follows it — as Ibn Kathīr does — the response reveals a terrible symmetry. Part of their *ẓulm*, the next verse says, was reaching for wealth that had been made *forbidden* to them: riba they had been told not to touch, and the property of others swallowed *bil-bāṭil*, by falsehood. They grasped at the unlawful. And so the lawful was made unlawful *to* them. The very *ḥalāl* they once enjoyed was sealed shut — measured out to fit the crime. They forced open doors God had closed; God closed doors He had once left open. The punishment is cut from the same cloth as the sin.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable, and I want to make sure we don't rush past it.

We usually think of God's commands — the ḥalāl and the ḥarām — as a fixed map handed down once, the same for everyone, everywhere, forever. And in their foundations, the great principles are. But this verse reveals something more dynamic and more sobering: **the boundaries of the permitted can themselves move in response to a people's conduct.** Persistent, large-scale wrongdoing can cause the circle of the good to *contract.* What was open can be closed. Not as cruelty — as consequence.

And notice *who* speaks the verbs. **ḥarramnā** — "*We* forbade." This is not nature taking its course. This is not karma, an impersonal mechanism. This is the direct act of a Lord who was *responding*. The "We" is intimate and it is deliberate. The contraction of the good was not an accident of history. It was a sentence, pronounced.

One clear statement of what these two facing verbs reveal: **sin does not only earn a punishment stored up for later — it can reach back into the texture of your daily permitted life and start closing doors that were once wide open.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Second Cause, and the Word Left Hanging — *wa-bi-ṣaddihim ... kathīrā*

The verse gives us a *first* cause: *bi-ẓulmin*, wrongdoing in general. But then it singles out a *second*, specific cause, as if to say: of all the wrongs, this one in particular earned the verdict.

> وَبِصَدِّهِمْ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ كَثِيرًا
>
> *wa-bi-ṣaddihim ʿan sabīli-llāhi kathīrā*
>
> "and for their **hindering** from the way of Allah — **many**."

The word is صَدّ — *ṣadd*. Its root means to turn away, to block, to obstruct, to bar a door. And the classical commentators open this word in a way that doubles its weight. Al-Baghawī explains *ṣaddihim* as *ṣarfihim anfusahum wa-ghayrahum* — "their turning away **themselves and others**." Al-Muyassar says the same: *ṣaddihim anfusahum wa-ghayrahum ʿan dīni-llāh* — "their hindering of themselves and others from the religion of Allah."

So *ṣadd* here runs in two directions at once. There is the inward block — turning *yourself* away from the path. And there is the outward block — standing in the doorway so that *others* cannot pass through to it. The same root holds both, because they are the same disease facing two directions: a heart that has refused the path cannot bear to see anyone else walk it.

Now I want to teach one more piece of Arabic before we open the final word, because it is the most haunting thing in the verse.

In Arabic, an adjective or a quantity-word usually attaches to a specific noun. "Many *what*?" we want to ask. And the verse ends with exactly such a word left **hanging**:

> ...كَثِيرًا
>
> *...kathīrā*
>
> "...much / many."

Many *what*? The verse does not say. And the grammarians and commentators read it in more than one way — and rather than choose, let us hold them together as a single picture, because each is a camera angle on the same shadow.

**One reading:** *kathīrā* describes the *people*. "Their hindering of **many people** from the way of Allah." A great multitude turned away at the door. Not one soul, not ten — a crowd, a generation, who might have walked the path and were blocked from it.

**Another reading:** *kathīrā* describes the *hindering itself*. "Their hindering — a **great deal** of hindering." Not an occasional obstruction but a way of life. Persistent. Industrial. Hindering as a vocation. This is the reading al-Jalālayn leans into: he supplies the unspoken verbal noun, reading the phrase as *ṣaddan kathīran* — *a great hindering* — so that *kathīrā* attaches to the act of blocking, not only to the blocked. And note how al-Jalālayn opens the phrase *sabīl Allāh* itself: the "way of Allah" here, he says, is *dīnuhu* — His religion. So what was being blocked was not a road in the desert but the road *to God* — access to the dīn itself.

Hold both at once and feel the full darkness of it: **a great deal of blocking, that turned away a great many.**

[PAUSE]

And now look at what this word does to the *shape* of the consequence. The verse never tells us, in the second half, what the punishment for the *ṣadd* was. It names the cause — the hindering, the *much* — and then it *stops.* The sentence ends on *kathīrā*, on the open wound of the word "many," with the consequence trailing off into silence.

Read that silence carefully, because absence in the Qur'an speaks as loudly as presence. The first cause got its explicit verdict — *We forbade the good things.* The second cause, the *ṣadd*, is left with its consequence unstated, hanging in the air after that terrible word *kathīrā.* It is as though the verse is saying: *the harm of turning many away from God is so vast that We will not even try to itemize its cost. Let it hang. Let it be felt.*

One clear statement of what this final hanging word reveals: **the gravest wrong named here is not what they did to themselves — it is what they did to others' access to God; and the very grammar refuses to close the account, because that account does not close.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. A spotlight thrown on *wrongdoing* at the very front, so the chain of cause and effect could not be denied. Two verbs facing each other — *forbidden* and *had-been-lawful* — so we could watch a wide-open table narrow into prohibition. And a final word, *many*, left hanging over a silence, where the cost of blocking others from God is too large to write down.

Now let's ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About how sin actually *works* in a human life. Because I promised you at the start that this verse is describing something happening in your life right now. Let me show you where.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How Wrongdoing Shrinks Your World

Let me start somewhere you already live.

Think about a person who tells one lie. Just one, to cover a small mistake at work. Now, to protect that lie, there are suddenly conversations they can no longer have freely. A colleague they must avoid. A topic that makes them tense. A document they hope no one opens. The lie didn't just sit there as an isolated event. **It started closing doors.** A week ago they could speak freely in any meeting; now there are rooms in their own life they cannot fully enter.

Or think about a person who lets resentment grow against a family member. At first it's one relationship that's strained. But resentment is rarely tidy. Soon there are family gatherings they dread, then avoid. Mutual friends become awkward. A whole wing of their social world quietly shuts down — not because anyone locked the door from outside, but because *what they were carrying inside made those rooms unbearable.*

This is something we all know in our bones but rarely name: **wrongdoing contracts the world of the wrongdoer.** It makes the available smaller. The free becomes fraught. The open becomes closed.

Now hear the verse again with that in your chest:

> "So for the wrongdoing... We made unlawful for them good things that had been lawful for them."

[PAUSE]

Do you see it now? The verse is not describing an exotic ancient punishment. It is naming, at the level of an entire nation and with God Himself as the actor, the *exact same mechanism* you feel in the small lie and the quiet resentment. **Sin shrinks the circle of the good you may enjoy.**

And here is the movement I want us to make together — from the physical good things, to the unseen reality they point toward.

The "good things" forbidden in al-Anʿām were literal: certain meats, fats, provisions. Tangible. You could point to them on a plate. But the physical prohibition is a *stepping stone* to something the verse wants us to see about every soul that persists in wrongdoing: **the world itself stops yielding its goodness to you.** The same blessings remain in existence — they're just no longer *for you.* The food is on the table; it is simply no longer yours to take. The good is still good; it has been sealed off.

Have you ever met a person who has everything and can enjoy none of it? Wealth that brings no rest. A home that brings no peace. Relationships that are present in form and empty in substance? That is *ṭayyibāt uḥillat lahum* turned into *ḥurrimat* — good things that were lawful, now somehow forbidden to the heart even while they sit fully within reach of the hand.

[PAUSE]

Let the paradox sit, because the verse doesn't rush to soften it: **the most frightening punishment is not the loss of the good thing. It is keeping the good thing and losing your access to its goodness.** To have the food and not be allowed to be nourished. To have the blessing with the *barakah* drained out of it.

So here is the practical turn — what this changes about tomorrow.

When you find a part of your life that has gone strangely barren — a relationship that used to be warm and is now cold, a worship that used to be sweet and is now mechanical, a provision that used to satisfy and now just sits there — resist the first instinct, which is to blame the *thing.* The verse trains a different instinct. It teaches you to ask, gently and honestly: **is there a ẓulm of mine standing at the front of this sentence?** Is there a wrongdoing I am holding onto that has quietly reached into this good thing and sealed it?

Because if the contraction came from a *ẓulm*, then no amount of chasing the good thing will reopen it. Only one thing reopens a door that wrongdoing closed: turning back. The same Lord who said *ḥarramnā* — "We forbade" — is the One whose mercy reopens what repentance unseals.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Particular Weight of Standing in the Doorway

Now to the second cause, the *ṣadd* — the hindering. And again, let's begin where you live.

There is a specific kind of harm in this world that is worse than ordinary harm. Imagine a man who is himself lost — fine, that is his tragedy, and it is real. But now imagine a man who is lost *and stands at the trailhead, turning back everyone who tries to find the way.* He doesn't just fail to walk the path. He plants himself in the gate. Every person who approaches, he sends away.

These are not the same crime. The first man harmed himself. The second man harmed himself *and a multitude.*

This is why the verse, after naming wrongdoing in general, reaches in and pulls out *this one* by name: **wa-bi-ṣaddihim ʿan sabīli-llāh — and for their hindering from the way of Allah.** Of all the wrongs, the obstruction of others' access to God gets singled out for special mention beside the general *ẓulm.* Because there is something uniquely grave about becoming an *obstacle between people and their Lord.*

[PAUSE]

And remember what the commentators taught us — that *ṣadd* runs in two directions: turning away *yourself*, and turning away *others.* These are not separate sins; they grow from one root. The heart that has refused the path develops, almost automatically, a discomfort with anyone else finding it. Their faith is an accusation against your refusal. So you minimize it. You mock it. You "warn" people away from it. You become, perhaps without ever naming it to yourself, a doorway-stander.

Let me bring this very close to home, because this is not only an ancient sin.

You can hinder from the way of Allah with a sentence. A sarcastic comment when a younger sibling starts praying more. An eye-roll when a friend wants to take their faith seriously. A "don't become one of those people" to a spouse who is reaching for God. You did not write a book of disbelief. You did not lead an army. But you stood in someone's doorway for a moment — and that is the *family* of sin the verse names. And the word that hangs over it all is *kathīrā* — *many.* Because the doorway-stander rarely turns away just one.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the weight of that silence we found in the grammar. The verse names the *ṣadd* and then will not even write down its full consequence. It ends on *kathīrā* and goes quiet. Why?

Because the cost of turning a soul away from God *cannot be totaled.* If you turn one person away, and that person would have prayed, and taught their children to pray, and those children theirs — you have not blocked one soul. You have blocked a *river* of worship that would have flowed for generations. The account does not close. The verse leaves it open because *it is open.* This is *kathīr* in the deepest sense — not "many people" as a number, but a "muchness" that runs out past where any ledger can follow.

So the practical turn, and this one is sharper than the first.

The first theme asked you to look *inward* — what wrongdoing is shrinking my world? This one asks you to look at the *doorways you stand near.* Because most of us will never consciously try to lead someone away from God. But many of us, without thinking, stand near the doors of people who are *reaching* — and what we do in that moment matters more than we know.

When someone near you turns, even clumsily, toward Allah — a friend who wants to start praying, a relative who's reading Qur'an for the first time, a child taking it seriously — you are standing in a doorway. And in that exact moment you become one of two people. **The one who widens the door, or the one who narrows it.** This verse is the most serious warning in the Qur'an against being the second. So tomorrow, when you find yourself beside someone's reaching — *get out of the doorway. Better: hold it open.* Say the encouraging word. Make the path look beautiful, because it is.

The opposite of *ṣadd* is to become a *dāʿī* — one who *opens* the way. And the One who recorded the *muchness* of those who block will, with equal precision, record the *muchness* of those who help others through.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing the Two Directions Together

Look at what we have found, from two directions, and see that it is one truth.

The linguistic journey showed us a sentence engineered to expose cause and effect: *wrongdoing* spotlit at the front, two verbs revealing a wide table narrowed to a forbidden one, and a final word *— many —* left hanging over an uncountable harm.

The thematic depths showed us that this is not ancient history but present mechanics: **wrongdoing contracts the world of the wrongdoer, and the gravest wrongdoing of all reaches past the self to block others' road to God.**

And here is where the two directions meet. The structure of the verse mirrors the structure of the soul's decline. First comes *ẓulm* — a private wrongdoing — and the good in *your own* life begins to seal shut. But left unrepented, the disease faces outward; the heart that has closed itself to the path begins to *close the path to others.* The verse moves from *ḥarramnā ʿalayhim* — what was done *to them* — to *bi-ṣaddihim* — what they did *to others.* From the shrinking of your own world to the blocking of everyone else's. **Inward contraction becomes outward obstruction.** That is the whole arc of the verse, and it is the whole arc of a hardening heart.

### Questions to Carry

1. Is there a place in my life that has gone barren — a blessing I still hold but can no longer taste? And have I had the courage to ask whether a *ẓulm* of mine is standing at the front of that sentence?

2. What good things were once "lawful" and sweet to me — a prayer, a relationship, a Qur'an I used to love — and what closed the door on them?

3. Whose doorway am I standing near right now? Is there someone reaching toward Allah in my life, and am I widening their path or narrowing it?

4. When I have discouraged someone's turning toward God — even with a small comment, a joke, an eye-roll — did I understand that I was touching the gravest family of sin this verse names?

5. The verse left *kathīrā* hanging because the account does not close. What account am I leaving open — for harm, or, God willing, for good?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Wrongdoing seals shut the good things that were once open to you, and the wrongdoing that blocks others from God carries a cost so vast that even the Qur'an leaves its ledger unclosed.**

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not, in the end, a verse of despair. It is a verse of *causation* — and causation is the most hopeful word in the world, because it means *the door that closed can be reopened by reversing what closed it.* The same Hand that wrote *ḥarramnā* in response to *ẓulm* writes *aḥalla* — restoration, opening, release — in response to turning back. If sin can narrow the world, then *tawbah* can widen it again. And if standing in the doorway is the gravest harm, then holding it open for others may be among the most luminous of deeds. Let this verse send you not into fear, but to the two doorways: the one inside you, and the one beside you. Open them both.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who once opened the good things and have the power to seal them — let no wrongdoing of mine close the doors of Your goodness in my life, and reopen by Your mercy whatever I have already shut. And O Allah, make me one who *widens* the way to You for others, never one who stands in the doorway; and write me, in Your precise and merciful ledger, among those who turned *many* toward You — not away. Āmīn.
