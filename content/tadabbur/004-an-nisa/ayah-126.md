## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:4:126] وَلِلَّهِ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ مُّحِيطًا

**Transliteration:**
*Wa-lillāhi mā fi al-samāwāti wa-mā fi al-arḍi wa-kāna Allāhu bi-kulli shay'in muḥīṭā.*

**Translation:**
*And to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth. And Allah is ever, of all things, Encompassing.*

### The World We Must Inhabit First

Surah An-Nisa — the surah named after women — is not principally about gender. It is about the vulnerable. Read it whole and you feel the weight of a society in which the weak can be forgotten: orphans whose property is being devoured, women whose inheritance rights are being ignored, those at the margins whose suffering no one is officially counting. The surah opens with a command to fear Allah — and then immediately: *"Indeed Allah is ever, over you, a Watcher."* (4:1) Someone is watching what you do to the vulnerable. The whole surah breathes with that atmosphere.

Then near the end of the surah, something shifts. Four verses from the end of a section, Allah names Ibrahim — peace be upon him — as His khalīl. His intimate friend. *"And Allah took Ibrahim as a khalīl."* (4:125) That word, khalīl, carries an extraordinary weight in Arabic. It comes from khulla — a friendship so deep that it penetrates (takhalluṣ) into the innermost layers of the heart. Not a business associate. Not a patron. A friend at the level of the soul.

And then, immediately, without pause, comes this ayah.

*To Allah belongs everything in the heavens and the earth. And Allah is — eternally — Encompassing of all things.*

Why here? What is this sovereignty declaration doing immediately after the most intimate name Allah gives any human being?

Stay with that question. Because the answer will change how you understand both divine love and divine encompassing. And everything hinges on one word at the end — a single active participle in Form IV Arabic morphology — that contains more than most of us have ever extracted from it.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: وَكَانَ — The Grammar of Eternity

Before we read this word in the ayah, we need to understand something about Arabic verbs.

The verb **كَانَ** (kāna) is the Arabic equivalent of "to be" — existence, state, identity. In ordinary Arabic sentences, كَانَ in the past tense means "was." *Kāna al-waladu ṭawīlan* — "The boy was tall." He used to be. Now perhaps he has grown, or changed, or we're simply speaking of a past moment.

This is how we use kāna in everyday speech — it anchors a state in time.

But Arabic grammarians recognized something unusual: when kāna appears in the Quran to describe Allah's eternal attributes, it does not behave the same way. The scholars named this usage **كَانَ الدَّائِمَة** — the perpetual kān. Not "was at some point." But "is, and has always been, and always will be." It is a grammatical marker of timeless constancy — a way of saying that the attribute being described has no beginning and no end, that it precedes creation itself, that it is woven into the nature of reality rather than into any particular moment.

Now look at what this does to our ayah.

**وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُّحِيطًا**

A naive reading hears: "And Allah *was* Encompassing of all things." As though, at some point in the past, Allah encompassed everything — and perhaps by implication, that state has concluded, or was completed, or was true only of a prior moment.

But the perpetual kān refuses that reading entirely.

What it says is: *Allah has always been, and is, and will always be, Encompassing of all things.* There is no moment in which the encompassing began. There is no edge in time where it starts. It preceded the creation of the heavens and the earth that are named in the first half of this same ayah. It preceded the creation of Ibrahim. It preceded the creation of the pain you are carrying right now.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment.

Before you were born — the encompassing was already there. Before the circumstances that have tried you were set in motion — the encompassing was already there. There is no time in which you were outside it. There is no chapter of your life that falls before the encompassing began. The perpetual kān closes that door completely.

This is not a trivial grammatical observation. It is the difference between a God who watches from a fixed point in time and a God whose knowing and surrounding precede every moment you have ever inhabited.

**وَكَانَ اللَّهُ** — the eternal I AM. The Encompassing that has no start date.

### Element Two: مُّحِيطًا — The Root, the Form, and the Image

Now we arrive at the word everything builds toward.

First, a brief lesson in how Arabic morphology works — because this word is doing something specific, and we need to be able to see it.

Arabic verbs come in forms, sometimes called binyanim or awzān — patterns of intensification and extension that modify the base meaning of a root. The base form is Form I. Form II doubles the middle letter of a three-letter root, usually intensifying or extending the action. Form IV adds a letter at the beginning — أَفْعَلَ — and typically takes the action and applies it to something beyond the actor. Form IV has multiple functions; in any given instance you have to look at the lexical context to see what the verb is actually doing.

Here's the root underneath مُّحِيطًا: **ح-و-ط** (Ḥ-W-Ṭ).

Form I from this root: حَاطَ (ḥāṭa). To go around something. To put a fence around it. To circle it. It is the verb of a watchman who patrols the perimeter of a settlement, going around and around, making sure the boundary is maintained.

Form IV: أَحَاطَ (aḥāṭa). The action pushed to its intensive completion. Not to merely go around — but to fully surround. To encompass from every direction with no gap. Not a patrol that might miss a corner. A surrounding that is complete.

The active participle of Form IV: **مُحِيط** (muḥīṭ) — the one who fully encompasses.

In our ayah, it appears as **مُّحِيطًا** (muḥīṭan) — in the indefinite accusative, serving as the complement of the verb كَانَ. *Allah has always been muḥīṭ. Allah has always been the complete encompasser.*

Now — notice something remarkable about what the text does and does not say.

The classical commentators — al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī — all specify that the encompassing refers to Allah's **knowledge** ('ilm) and His **power** (qudra). Al-Jalālayn writes: "*muḥīṭan* — in knowledge and power." Al-Qurṭubī: "*aḥāṭa 'ilmuhu bi-jamīʿ al-ashyāʾ* — His knowledge has encompassed all things." Al-Baghawī says the same.

Their reading is sound and grounded in the tradition.

But here is what should strike you: the text itself does not say this. The text says simply: *وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُّحِيطًا* — "And Allah has always been encompassing of every thing." No specified instrument. No "with His knowledge" or "with His power." The encompassing is asserted absolutely, without qualification or restriction.

The scholars specify — because they are being careful. But the text leaves it open — because it is being total.

The absence is not an accident. When Arabic wants to restrict a claim, it restricts it. When it wants a claim to be absolute, it leaves it unqualified. The *muḥīṭ* here does not say "Allah encompasses with one of His attributes." It says: **Allah encompasses**. The encompassing is total. It is everything He is — knowing, powerful, aware, present, surrounding — applied to every single thing that exists, with no gap.

[PAUSE]

Here's an image that might help carry this.

If you have ever stood at the edge of the sea and looked at the horizon — you know that the horizon is always a complete circle around you. Wherever you stand on the surface of the earth, you are at the center of a circle. You can walk toward the horizon your whole life and never reach the edge. It is not that the horizon is far away. It is that the horizon has no edge. It moves with you. You are always inside it.

Allah's encompassing is like that — but infinitely more real. There is no direction in which His encompassing stops. There is no corner of your inner life or your outer circumstance that falls outside the circle. No edge where His knowing ends and the unseeable begins. The circle has no gap.

This is what مُّحِيطًا means. Not surveillance. Not monitoring. **Encompassing** — with no gap, no edge, no moment before or after.

### Element Three: The Placement — Why Here, After Ibrahim?

Now let us read the structure of the passage as a whole — because placement in the Quran is itself a form of language.

**4:124** — Whoever does righteous deeds, male or female, while believing — they will enter paradise.

**4:125** — And who is better in religion than one who submits his face to Allah while being a doer of good, following the religion of Ibrahim? And Allah took Ibrahim as a khalīl — an intimate friend.

**4:126** — And to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth. And Allah has always been Encompassing of all things.

Al-Ṭabarī saw it clearly. Reading the two verses together, he explains that Allah took Ibrahim as His khalīl *for his obedience to his Lord, his sincerity of worship toward Him, and his racing toward His pleasure and His love* — not from any need of Ibrahim or of his friendship. And then he turns the logic into a question: how could Allah possibly need him and his khulla, when everything in the heavens and the earth — *little and much (qalīl wa-kathīr)* — is His possession? The owner, al-Ṭabarī observes, is the one whom his possession needs; the possession is not the one the owner needs. The direction of dependence runs one way only. **"And to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth."** He owns everything. He lacks nothing.

Notice what al-Ṭabarī's reading adds. The friendship was not arbitrary — Ibrahim was *chosen for something*: his obedience, his sincerity, his eagerness toward Allah. And yet that something added nothing to Allah, completed nothing in Him, supplied no lack. Ibrahim's devotion was real and was honored — but the honoring flowed downhill, from the One who owns everything to the servant who owns nothing. The later commentators sharpen the same point: Allah chose Ibrahim not to increase Himself through the association, not because He lacked something Ibrahim provided.

There is something here about the nature of divine love that should land very slowly.

When a human being forms a deep friendship, there is usually — underneath the love, underneath the genuine care — some element of need. We need people who understand us. We need companionship against loneliness. We need witnesses to our existence. The most honest relationships acknowledge this mutual need as part of what makes them beautiful.

Allah chose Ibrahim as khalīl from a position of absolute sovereignty and complete sufficiency. He needs nothing Ibrahim has. He lacks nothing Ibrahim could provide. He owns everything in the heavens and the earth. His encompassing already covers everything that exists.

And yet — He still chose Ibrahim. He still called him khalīl. He still took him as an intimate friend.

This is the most extraordinary kind of love: love that comes entirely from abundance, with no element of need. The verse immediately after the most intimate name Allah gives any human being is a declaration of the completeness from which that intimacy proceeds.

**The circle has no gap — and yet He still chooses.**

### Bridge to the Themes

The linguistic work has shown us three things:

A verb form that makes the encompassing eternal — with no start date.

An active participle from Form IV that makes the encompassing complete — with no gap.

A structural placement that roots the encompassing in sovereign abundance — not need.

What we have been looking at are words. Now let us ask what these words reveal about reality itself — about suffering, about trust, about what it means to be surrounded by the One who owns everything.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Terror of the Unwitnessed

There is a particular kind of pain that researchers have documented across cultures and across centuries — a pain that is different in kind from physical suffering, from loss, from grief.

It is the pain of being unseen.

Not just the pain of what happened. But the pain of what happened *and nobody knows.* The injustice that was never acknowledged. The wound that was minimized. The cry that was never answered. The version of events that exists only inside you, contradicted by every official account.

Psychologists who work with trauma have found that one of the most important elements in healing is not the processing of the event itself — it is the presence of a *witness.* Someone who says: I see what was done. I believe you. It was real. The pain without a witness has a particular depth that pain with a witness does not have — because the unwitnessed suffering contains, underneath itself, a second wound: *did it even happen? Does it count? Does anyone know?*

Now come back to Surah An-Nisa.

This is the surah of the orphan whose guardian is eating his inheritance. The widow who has been pressured to give back her dowry before she can leave a bad marriage. The poor man cheated by someone more powerful. Throughout this surah, Allah addresses these people and keeps saying, in different ways: *I see. I know. I am aware.* "Indeed Allah is ever, over you, a Watcher." (4:1) "Allah knows what you intend." "Allah is ever Knowing of all things."

And then 4:126 comes — not as one more reassurance in the series, but as its theological foundation.

*Allah has always been Encompassing of all things.*

This is not "Allah will eventually see what was done." This is: **there has never been a moment when it was unseen.** The encompassing is eternal. It has no start date. Before the injustice was committed, the encompassing already held it. While it was being committed — in the worst of it, in the moment when you were most alone — you were inside the circle. The circle never had a gap.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this does to the second wound.

The second wound of unwitnessed suffering is: *maybe it doesn't count. Maybe no one knows.* Allah's eternal, gapless encompassing closes that wound at its root. It cannot be unseen — because the encompassing precedes the moment of seeing. He was already there. Al-Muyassar renders the meaning of the encompassing in exactly these terms: *lā yakhfā ʿalayhi shayʾun min umūri khalqihi* — not one of the affairs of His creation is hidden from Him. Not the smallest detail of what was done to you, or by you, or around you. The comprehensiveness of مُّحِيطًا means that knowledge of it is not waiting on Allah to turn His attention your way. The encompassing is total, all-directional, without a moment's gap.

"*Aḥāṭa ʿilmuhu bi-jamīʿ al-ashyāʾ*" — as al-Qurṭubī wrote. His knowledge has encompassed all things. Not "will encompass." Not "sometimes encompasses." **Has encompassed, does encompass, will encompass** — because the perpetual kān has no tense.

The orphan in An-Nisa. The woman who could not defend herself. You, in whatever situation has made you feel invisible.

The circle was already there. It never had a gap.

[PAUSE]

Let this sit without rushing to its implication. Just let the image be real for a moment. A circle with no gap, surrounding everything you have been through, with no start date. Already encompassing before it began.

What is the shape of your relief when that lands?

### Theme Two: The Freedom of Being Encompassed

There is a paradox in this word that needs careful holding.

In almost every human context, being surrounded is threatening. A city under siege. An animal trapped. A child cornered in an argument. The instinct when surrounded is to fight outward — to find the gap, to break through, to reach the open. We are wired for the anxiety of enclosure.

And yet the word at the end of this ayah — مُّحِيطًا — describes a surrounding with no gap.

How does that become good news?

The answer is not a trick. It requires thinking carefully about *who* is surrounding and *from what* they surround you.

A prison and a walled garden look identical from a satellite image. Both are enclosed. Both have walls. What changes everything is what is inside the walls and who built them. A prison separates you from what you need. A garden provides everything you need, within the walls. The same structure — enclosure — is liberation or captivity depending entirely on the nature of the One who encompasses.

Allah owns everything in the heavens and the earth. He encompasses every single thing with complete knowledge and power. That means: the surrounding is by the One who lacks nothing, who sees everything, who controls everything — and who, as the verse immediately before this one established, takes His servants as intimate friends not out of need but out of sovereign love.

You are not trapped. You are *held.*

[PAUSE]

Ibrahim understood this with a fullness that very few people have reached.

When Nimrūd's people gathered the wood and prepared the fire and threw him in — Ibrahim was inside the circle. When he left Hājar and the infant Ismāʿīl in a valley with no water and turned to walk away — he was inside the circle. When the knife was at his son's throat and his hand was raised — he was inside the circle.

How does a human being act with that degree of trust?

The Arabic concept is tawakkul — often translated as "reliance on Allah" or "trust in Allah." But tawakkul without a foundation is not trust; it is wishful thinking. You cannot genuinely rely on someone whose awareness of your situation you are not sure about. You cannot genuinely trust an outcome to someone who might miss what is happening to you.

The *muḥīṭ* is the foundation of tawakkul.

Because if the encompassing is total — if there is literally no corner of your situation that falls outside Allah's knowledge and power — then what exactly are you afraid of? Not that it won't be seen. Not that it won't be accounted for. Not that it will fall into some gap between His attention now and His attention later.

Ibrahim walked into the fire not because he was brave in the ordinary sense. He walked in because he knew something about the structure of the world. He knew he was inside a circle with no gap, encompassed by the One who owns everything. The fire could not change that. The fire was also inside the circle. **كُلِّ شَيْءٍ** — every thing. Including the fire.

[PAUSE]

Notice something subtle in the classical commentators' reading.

Al-Jalālayn specifies: *muḥīṭan bi-kulli shay'in — ʿilman wa-qudra.* Encompassing with knowledge and power. Two attributes named together deliberately — because the terror in a gap is not only "no one saw" but also "no one could do anything even if they saw." We have all experienced the compassionate but helpless witness: the friend who knew exactly what was happening to you but had no power to stop it. Knowing without power is a kind of agony for both the witness and the witnessed.

Allah's encompassing is knowledge *and* power together. Not one without the other. This is why tawakkul can be something other than passive resignation — because the encompassing is not merely observational. It is active.

*Aḥāṭa* in Form IV means to fully surround. A surrounding with knowledge and power is not just watching. It is surrounding the way a river levee surrounds the city it protects — not just present but structurally operative.

The encompassing is holding what it encompasses.

[PAUSE — major]

Here is the practical movement this asks of us.

We exhaust ourselves trying to see the full picture. We lie awake calculating outcomes, tracing contingencies, trying to encompass the situation ourselves — to see all sides, all possibilities, all threats. The anxiety of incomplete information. The desperate attempt to achieve a comprehensiveness that only belongs to Allah.

وَكَانَ اللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ مُّحِيطًا — this is an invitation to put down the calculation. Not because the situation isn't real. Not because the difficulties aren't present. But because the encompassing of every single thing by the One who owns everything is not contingent on your ability to also encompass it.

Ibrahim couldn't see the angel. Couldn't see the knife being lifted from his son's throat by divine command. Couldn't see the water that would spring from the valley for Hājar. The situation did not require his seeing to be held within the encompassing.

You are not the circle. You are held *inside* the circle. That distinction changes everything.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Look at the ayah once more — its two halves:

*And to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth —*

*And Allah has always been encompassing of all things.*

The first half is ownership. The second is encompassing.

What is the difference?

**Ownership** says: it belongs to Me. **Encompassing** says: and I surround it completely.

And al-Jalālayn pauses on that first word — *belongs* — to show it is not one relationship but three. Everything in the heavens and the earth is Allah's *mulkan wa-khalqan wa-ʿabīdan*: His by **possession**, His by **creation**, and His by **servitude**. He owns it, He made it, and it stands before Him as His servant. A human owner might possess what he did not make, or rule servants he did not create. Allah's ownership folds all three together — there is no thing in existence He does not own, did not bring into being, and does not hold in service. The claim is total before the second half of the verse ever names the encompassing.

A landlord owns his properties but may not know what is happening inside each one at every moment. A parent owns their responsibility to their child but cannot always see what their child is going through. Ownership and encompassing are different categories.

Allah owns everything in the heavens and the earth — and He encompasses it all. The ownership is the *what.* The encompassing is the *how.* He possesses it the way a circle possesses its interior: **completely, with no gap, with no edge, with no blind spot.**

The two halves are not separate statements. They are one statement made from two directions. He owns it — and nothing is unseen within what He owns. He encompasses it — because He is the sovereign owner of every single thing within the encompassing.

### What the Linguistic and Thematic Work Reveals Together

The perpetual kān showed us: the encompassing has no start date. It is eternal.

The Form IV participle مُّحِيطًا showed us: the encompassing has no gap. It is complete.

The placement after khalīl showed us: the encompassing comes from abundance, not need. Allah did not choose Ibrahim because He was incomplete. He encompassed Ibrahim, like everything else, from a position of infinite sovereignty.

And the two themes showed us that these are not theological abstractions. They are answers to the two most urgent human anxieties:

The anxiety of the unwitnessed: *Is my suffering seen?* — answered by the eternal, gapless muḥīṭ.

The anxiety of the unsupported: *Can the One who sees also act?* — answered by *ʿilm wa-qudra* together, by knowledge and power inseparable within the encompassing.

The ayah is short. Twelve words. But it closes both wounds.

### Questions to Carry

Here are five questions to take with you — not rhetorical, genuinely open. Let them work on you over the coming days:

1. When you last felt unseen in your suffering — what would it have changed, in the moment itself, to truly inhabit the reality that you were inside the circle and always had been?

2. Is there a situation you are currently trying to encompass yourself — to see all sides of, to calculate, to control? What would it mean to name it explicitly: *I cannot be the muḥīṭ here. Only Allah is.*

3. Ibrahim is named khalīlullāh in the verse just before this one — and the verse immediately following is this declaration of divine sovereignty and encompassing. How does it change your understanding of what a relationship with Allah looks like when you know it comes from His abundance rather than His need?

4. The text does not specify *how* Allah encompasses — it simply says He does. What does the absence of that qualification open for you?

5. **وَكَانَ** — the perpetual kān — means the encompassing of every chapter of your life preceded your birth. Before you knew what you would face, it was already surrounded. What is the first specific memory or situation that comes to mind when you hear that?

### One-Sentence Distillation

*Allah's encompassing of all things is eternal and without gap — which means no suffering has ever gone unseen, no faithfulness has ever gone uncounted, and there is no corner of existence that falls outside His knowledge and power.*

### The Closing Invitation

After this reflection, one practice: when you feel the pull toward anxious calculation — the need to encompass the situation yourself, to see all sides, to find the gap before it finds you — pause and return to this word. مُّحِيطًا. Say it slowly. Let the image settle: a circle with no gap, surrounding everything, with no start date and no edge. Not as a magic formula, but as an orientation. *I am inside the circle. I cannot fall outside it. The One who encompasses is the One who owns everything. The calculation is already done.*

Not escape from the difficulty. Presence *within* it — but from inside the encompassing, rather than as though the encompassing were something you still need to find.

This is what Ibrahim walked into the fire knowing. This is what the surah of the vulnerable has been building toward from its first verse. You were never outside the circle. You are not outside it now.
