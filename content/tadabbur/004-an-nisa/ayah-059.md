# The Obedience That Was Never Said Three Times

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first, and then I want to ask you to forget, for a little while, that you already know what they mean.

> **يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓاْ أَطِيعُواْ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُواْ ٱلرَّسُولَ وَأُوْلِى ٱلْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ ۖ فَإِن تَنَـٰزَعْتُمْ فِى شَىْءٍ فَرُدُّوهُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَٱلرَّسُولِ إِن كُنتُمْ تُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْءَاخِرِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَحْسَنُ تَأْوِيلًا**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū aṭīʿū Allāha wa-aṭīʿū ar-rasūla wa-ulī al-amri minkum. Fa-in tanāzaʿtum fī shayʾin fa-ruddūhu ilā Allāhi wa-r-rasūli in kuntum tuʾminūna billāhi wa-l-yawmi al-ākhir. Dhālika khayrun wa-aḥsanu taʾwīlā.*

"O you who believe, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. Then if you disagree over anything, refer it back to Allah and the Messenger, if you believe in Allah and the Last Day. That is best, and finest in its outcome."

Most of us have heard this ayah quoted in two very different rooms. In one room, it is read to keep people in line — *obey those in authority, the Quran commands it.* In another room, it is read as a charter of conscience — *here is exactly where authority ends.* The same twenty-eight words. Two opposite uses.

That should make us suspicious. Not of the ayah — of how quickly we reach for it before we have actually *read* it.

### Rebuilding the world

Step back into the moment this was revealed. Madinah is no longer a loose collection of households. It is becoming something the Arabs had never built before — a community under a single revealed law, where a Jewish farmer, a former Quraysh aristocrat, a freed slave, and a desert convert are all suddenly subject to the same court, the same commands, the same Messenger ﷺ. People who a few years earlier would have settled a quarrel with a sword are now being asked to settle it with a *ruling*.

And the verse just before this one — 4:58 — has just finished speaking to the people who *hold* that power. Allah told them: deliver the trusts back to those they belong to, and when you judge between people, judge with justice. Al-Qurtubi notes this sequence precisely: first Allah addressed the rulers and commanded them to be just; *then*, in our verse, He turns and addresses the governed.

So picture it as two halves of one sentence spoken to a young society. To the powerful: *here is the weight on your shoulders.* To the people: *here is what you owe — and here is exactly what you do not owe.*

### The hook

Now here is what I want you to carry into everything that follows. In the first half of this ayah, Allah names three parties you are to obey: **Allah, the Messenger, and those in authority among you.** Three.

But the command "obey" — *aṭīʿū* — is not said three times.

It is said twice.

[PAUSE]

That gap — one missing word that you would never notice reading quickly — is not an accident of style. It is the entire argument of the verse, hidden in plain sight. By the time we are done, I want you to see that almost everything people fight about when they quote this ayah was already answered by Allah *in what He chose not to repeat.*

Let's earn it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The word that was said twice, not three times

Before we touch the ayah, a small piece of how Arabic — and frankly, how any careful speech — works.

When you give someone a list of instructions, the way you attach your verbs tells the listener how *independent* each item is. Imagine I say to my children: "Listen to your mother, and listen to your teacher, and your grandparents." Notice what happened. I said "listen to" twice — for the mother and the teacher — and then for the grandparents I just *attached* them to the end without repeating the verb. Any Arabic-trained ear, and honestly any careful English ear, picks up something: the grandparents got folded onto the teacher. Their authority is riding on the back of the verb that came before. It is real — but it is *attached*, not freestanding.

Now hold that, and look at the ayah.

> أَطِيعُواْ ٱللَّهَ — *aṭīʿū Allāh* — Obey Allah.
>
> وَأَطِيعُواْ ٱلرَّسُولَ — *wa-aṭīʿū ar-rasūl* — and obey the Messenger.
>
> وَأُوْلِى ٱلْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ — *wa-ulī al-amri minkum* — and those in authority among you.

The verb **أَطِيعُواْ — "obey"** — is a Form IV imperative from the root *ṭ-w-ʿ* (ط-و-ع), which carries the sense of *willing compliance*, of yielding readily rather than being dragged. Allah uses it, and then He uses it *again* — a fresh, full, independent command — for the Messenger ﷺ.

And then, for those in authority, the verb stops. They are simply *joined on*: "**and** those in authority among you." No third *aṭīʿū*.

Let's sit with this, because the early scholars sat with it.

What does a fresh, repeated verb signal? **Independent obedience.** When Allah says "obey" and then says "obey" again for the Messenger, He is establishing that obedience to the Messenger is its own absolute axis — not a derivative of anything, not conditional on agreeing with him, owed in full. This is why elsewhere the Quran can say *"whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah"* (4:80). Two separate "obeys" — two absolutes that happen to point the same direction.

Now notice what *not* repeating the verb does for those in authority. Their obedience was never given its own axis. **It was grammatically tethered to the Messenger's.** It rides on his verb. And a thing that rides on another thing only stands as long as the thing it rides on stands.

Here is what is remarkable. The grammar is not a side-detail that happens to agree with the theology. The grammar *is* the theology. Allah did not need to add a sentence saying "but only obey them within limits." He encoded the limit in the architecture of the verb itself. The missing third *aṭīʿū* **is** the limit.

[PAUSE]

And this is exactly what the classical commentators draw out — not from outside the verse, but from inside it. Al-Jalālayn glosses "those in authority among you" with a quiet, decisive condition: *idhā amarūkum bi-ṭāʿati Llāhi wa-rasūlih* — "**when** they command you toward obedience to Allah and His Messenger." Al-Muyassar says it even more plainly: obey those who hold authority over you *fī ghayri maʿṣiyati Llāh* — "**in other than** disobedience to Allah." The condition the scholars insert is not an addition to the verse. It is them *reading the silence where the third verb should have been.*

There is a sound, well-known statement of the Prophet ﷺ that says the same thing in a single breath: obedience is owed *only in what is right* — and there is no obedience to any created being in disobedience to the Creator. Read against our ayah, that hadith is almost a commentary on a missing word.

So what does this one absent verb reveal? **Authority in Islam is real, but it is never sovereign.** It is always borrowing. The moment a human command unhooks itself from "obey Allah and obey the Messenger," it loses the very verb it was standing on — and it falls.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: "Those of the amr — from among you"

Stay with that phrase, because two more words inside it are doing quiet work: **أُوْلِى ٱلْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ — *ulī al-amri minkum*.**

First, *al-amr* — from the root *ʾ-m-r* (أ-م-ر), the same root as "command" and "affair." *Ulū al-amr* literally means "the possessors of the command / the people of the affair." It is deliberately broad. It does not say "the kings." It does not say "the army." It says: those who carry the *amr* — the responsibility of the community's affairs.

Now, who are they? Here the classical scholars genuinely differed, and the methodology of honest tadabbur is not to pick one and hide the rest, but to lay the camera angles side by side. Al-Baghawī preserves the *ikhtilāf* openly:

- Ibn ʿAbbās and Jābir — and with them al-Ḥasan, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, and Mujāhid — read *ulū al-amr* as **the scholars and jurists**, those who teach people the landmarks of their religion. Their evidence is later in this very surah, 4:83: *"had they referred it to the Messenger and to those of authority among them, those who could draw out its meaning would have known it"* — a verse about people of *knowledge*.
- Abū Hurayra and others read it as **the rulers and governors** — those who hold executive power.
- Other reports add the commanders of military expeditions and the Companions.

Don't rush to resolve this. Let the breadth sit, because the breadth is the point. *Ulū al-amr* is wide enough to cover **anyone who carries a piece of the community's affair** — the scholar who carries its knowledge, the ruler who carries its order, the one entrusted with its decisions. The verse is not naming a single office. It is naming a *function*: whoever legitimately bears the weight of the *amr*, among you.

And now the word that quietly governs all of it: **مِنكُمْ — *minkum* — "from among you."**

Not *ʿalaykum*, "over you." *Minkum* — "from you, of you, part of you." Let's notice something. The authority the Quran asks you to obey is not an alien power imposed from outside the community of faith. It is *of* the same body — sharing the same *qiblah*, the same Book, the same accountability before the same Allah. The one who carries the *amr* is your brother in the very thing that limits him. He is *minkum* — bound by the same Lord you are bound by.

That single preposition does two things at once. It dignifies legitimate authority — *these are your own, not your enemies.* And it *fences* it — because the authority you obey is itself "from among you," it is itself under the two absolutes named first. *Minkum* means *no one stands outside the circle of "obey Allah and obey the Messenger," least of all the ones who lead inside it.*

[PAUSE]

So the first half of the ayah, read slowly, has already built a complete structure of authority: two absolutes given their own verb, and a derived, tethered, *minkum*-bound authority that holds only so long as it stays hooked to them.

Which raises the obvious human question. *Fine — but what do I do at the exact moment the hook seems to slip? When I genuinely don't know whether to follow? When we don't agree?*

Allah answers that next. And the way He answers it is the most beautiful word in the verse.

### Element Three: The tug-of-war, and the return

> فَإِن تَنَـٰزَعْتُمْ فِى شَىْءٍ — *fa-in tanāzaʿtum fī shayʾin* — "Then if you disagree over anything..."

Here is a piece of Arabic worth carrying with you forever. Arabic verbs come in patterns, and one pattern — **Form VI**, the *tafāʿala* pattern — almost always means **a mutual, back-and-forth action between two or more parties.** *Kātaba* is to write; *takātaba* is to write *to each other*, to correspond. *Qātala* is to fight; *taqātala* is to fight *one another*. Form VI is the grammar of reciprocity — it always takes (at least) two, and it implies both sides are *doing* the action to each other.

Now the root underneath this particular Form VI verb. **تَنَـٰزَعْتُمْ — *tanāzaʿtum*** — comes from *n-z-ʿ* (ن-ز-ع), and the bare meaning of *nazaʿa* is **to pull, to tug, to yank something away.** It is the word for drawing a bucket up from a well, for pulling an arrow from a quiver, for wrenching a thing loose.

Put the pattern and the root together and *feel* what Allah actually called your disagreement. He did not call it "if you have differing opinions." He called it — literally — **"if you *pull against each other*."** A tug-of-war. Two people with their hands on the same rope, each hauling it toward himself. Listen to the word even in your mouth: *ta-nā-za-ʿtum* — it stretches, it strains, it pulls.

That image is doing real work, so let's not let go of it. When you and I disagree and dig in, what are we actually doing? We are each trying to drag "the truth of this matter" onto *our* side of the line. The thing in dispute — *fī shayʾin*, "over *any*thing," Allah leaves it wide open — becomes a rope, and we are pulling. And anyone who has held a rope in a tug-of-war knows: as long as both sides pull, *nothing moves and everyone is exhausted.* The harder each side pulls, the more locked the whole thing becomes.

So what does Allah command us to do with the rope?

> فَرُدُّوهُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَٱلرَّسُولِ — *fa-ruddūhu ilā Allāhi wa-r-rasūl* — "refer it back to Allah and the Messenger."

**رُدُّوهُ — *ruddūhu*** — from *r-d-d* (ر-د-د), *radda*: to return, to send back, to give back to where it came from. And hear the sound of it — that doubled, firm *dd*, *rud-dū* — it lands like a decision, like setting something down. It is the opposite sound to the straining *tanāzaʿtum*. One word pulls; the other releases and returns.

Here is the move, and it is exquisite. In a tug-of-war, the solution is *not* for one side to pull harder and "win." The solution is for **both sides to let go of pulling toward themselves and hand the rope to a third point above them both.** *Ruddūhu* — return it. Stop hauling the matter toward your ego and *send it back up* to Allah and His Messenger — which al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar specify as His Book and the Sunnah of His Messenger ﷺ. Not "let the stronger personality win." Not "split the difference." **Return the rope to a point neither of you owns.**

Notice what this does to the disagreement. The instant both hands let go and the matter goes *up*, the contest is over — not because someone was crushed, but because the rope was never really yours to win. It belonged to Allah the whole time. You were just holding it.

[PAUSE]

And now look at what is *missing* from this second command — because we have trained our eyes to read absence.

When Allah told you whom to **obey**, He named three: Allah, the Messenger, and those in authority. When Allah tells you where to **return a dispute**, He names only **two**: "refer it back to **Allah and the Messenger.**"

*Ulū al-amr have disappeared.*

The very authority you were commanded to obey is *not* the place you return your disputes to. Why? Because of everything we already discovered. Their authority was always tethered — it rode on the Messenger's verb. So at the precise moment of genuine dispute, you do not refer the rope to human authority; **you return it above human authority, to the source that human authority was itself borrowing from.** The missing *ulū al-amr* in the second clause is the *same silence* as the missing third *aṭīʿū* in the first. Allah is saying the identical thing twice, both times by leaving something out.

[PAUSE]

One word remains, and it closes a circle so quietly you could miss it. The verse ends:

> ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ وَأَحْسَنُ تَأْوِيلًا — *dhālika khayrun wa-aḥsanu taʾwīlā* — "That is best, and finest in **taʾwīl**."

**تَأْوِيلًا — *taʾwīl*** — comes from the root *ʾ-w-l* (أ-و-ل), and at its heart *āla* means **to return, to come back to an origin or final destination.** *Taʾwīl* is the place a thing ultimately *returns to* — its final outcome, its end-result, what it comes home to.

Do you feel it? The ayah told you to *ruddū* — **return** the dispute to Allah — and then it tells you that doing so is *aḥsanu taʾwīlan* — the **best returning**, the most beautiful homecoming, of the whole affair. The verb you were commanded with (*radd*, return) and the reward you were promised (*taʾwīl*, the place of return) are *the same gesture*. Return the matter to Allah now, and the *matter itself* will return to the best possible end. The act and its reward are one motion. Al-Jalālayn captures the practical edge of it: this referral is *khayr* — better for you — than *tanāzuʿ* and ruling by mere *raʾy*, naked opinion, each of us pulling the rope toward our own desire.

That is the linguistic architecture. Now let's ask what it reveals not just about words — but about how to actually *live* inside authority and disagreement, which is to say, about how to live with other human beings at all.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The freedom hidden inside the limit on obedience

Let me start where you already live.

Every single one of us is inside a chain of command right now. You have a manager, or a board, or a client. You grew up under parents. You belong to a country with laws, a community with leaders, maybe a religious institution with figures you are told to defer to. From the time we are small, the deepest social training we receive is: *fit in, comply, don't make trouble, obey.* And there is a real fear underneath it — the fear that if you ever question the authority over you, you are being arrogant, divisive, a bad employee, a bad citizen, a bad Muslim.

So here is the question almost everyone is quietly carrying: *Where is the line? When is my obedience a virtue, and when is it cowardice wearing the mask of humility?*

The modern world answers this badly in both directions. One answer says: authority is oppression, trust nothing, defer to no one, the autonomous self is king. The other answer says: submit completely, the institution is always right, your discomfort is your flaw. One produces people who can't belong to anything. The other produces people who can be led into anything.

Now let the ayah illuminate it.

We discovered that Allah said "obey" twice, not three times — that the authority of *ulū al-amr* is real but tethered, riding on the Messenger's verb. Sit with what that actually gives you. **Allah Himself drew the line you were afraid to draw.** Your discomfort, at the exact point where a human command pulls against Allah's command, is not arrogance. It is the verse working in you. The missing third *aṭīʿū* lives in your conscience.

This is not a license for chaos — notice that carefully. The ayah does not say "obey only when you feel like it." It commands obedience to legitimate authority *firmly and first*; al-Qurtubi reads the whole verse as an obligation laid on the governed, *fully*. The believer is not an anarchist suspicious of every leader. The default is obedience, ease, *ṭawʿ* — willing compliance, the very root of the verb. The line is not "do I agree?" The line is one place and one place only: **does this command unhook me from obeying Allah and His Messenger?** Everywhere else, you yield, and yielding is your worship.

Do you see the freedom in that? It is a *terribly narrow* exit and a *vastly wide* room. The room — where you obey your parents, your manager, your scholars, the law — is enormous, and inside it your obedience is not weakness; it is *minkum*, belonging, the strength of a person who can be part of something. The exit is a single hairline crack: the moment obedience to a creature would cost you obedience to the Creator. And that crack is not yours to invent on a whim — it is defined by revelation, not by your mood.

Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit rather than smooth it over. **The very thing that limits your obedience is what makes your obedience trustworthy.** A person who *cannot* refuse cannot really be said to obey — he is just incapable of anything else. But a person who knows exactly where his obedience would stop, and who therefore obeys *freely* everywhere short of that line — *his* obedience means something. The limit doesn't weaken his loyalty. It is what makes his loyalty a gift rather than a chain.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means the next time you feel the friction — when the boss, the parent, the leader, the institution asks for something — you have a precise instrument instead of a vague anxiety. You stop asking the paralyzing question, "Am I allowed to disagree?" and you ask the verse's question: "Is this command still hooked to obeying Allah, or has it slipped the hook?" If it's hooked — even if it's inconvenient, even if you'd have done it differently — you obey, and you find peace in the obeying. If it has truly slipped the hook into clear disobedience of Allah, then your refusal is not rebellion. It is the *deepest* obedience — to the One whose verb was never tethered to anyone.

### Theme Two: What faith looks like at the exact moment you're sure you're right

Now the second human door — and this one is even closer to home.

Think about the last real disagreement you were in. Not a debate about something abstract — a real one, where you were *sure* you were right and the other person was *sure* they were right, and the certainty on both sides only grew the longer it went. A family rupture over an inheritance. Two friends who stopped speaking over politics. A community split into camps, each convinced the other had abandoned the truth. Spouses locked over the same five-year-old grievance.

Notice what these have in common: nobody is *moving*. The more each side pulls, the more locked it gets. That is not a metaphor I am adding — that is the word Allah chose: *tanāzaʿtum*, **you pulled against each other.** He named the tug-of-war fourteen centuries before you found yourself in one.

Now watch where Allah places this. The first half of the ayah opened by calling you **"O you who believe."** Then, right at the moment of dispute, He says it *again* in a different form: *in kuntum tuʾminūna billāhi wa-l-yawmi al-ākhir* — **"if you believe in Allah and the Last Day."** Your faith is named at the start, and then your faith is put *on the line* precisely at the point of conflict.

Let that land. Allah did not make the test of your faith your prayer in the mosque, where everyone is calm and facing the same direction. He located one decisive test of your faith **inside your fights** — at the one moment your *nafs* is most inflamed, most certain, most desperate to win. He is saying: *anyone can look like a believer when they agree with the room. Show Me your faith when you are convinced you are right and someone is pulling the rope the other way.*

And the proof He asks for is not that you *win*. It is not even that you *concede*. It is that you **let go of the rope and send it up** — *fa-ruddūhu ilā Allāhi wa-r-rasūl.* Refer it back. The believer is not the one who pulls hardest, and not the one who is too weak to pull. The believer is the one who, in the heat of being *sure*, can still unclench his hands and say, "this was never mine to win — let's return it to the Book and the Sunnah."

Here is the movement that transforms the whole thing. When two people both reach for *raʾy* — their own opinion, their own desire, as al-Jalālayn names it — the rope can never resolve, because each is its own anchor, and there are now two anchors pulling apart. The dispute is structurally *unwinnable*; that is the nature of a tug-of-war between equals. The *only* thing that ends it without crushing someone is a point **above them both** that neither one owns. Faith, in this verse, is precisely the willingness to admit such a point exists — and to return the matter to it.

And notice the mercy folded into the ending: *dhālika khayrun wa-aḥsanu taʾwīlā* — "that is **better** for you, and the **best outcome.**" Better *for you.* Allah is not just laying down a procedure; He is telling you that letting go of the rope is *in your own interest.* The exhausting thing, the thing that wrecks families and communities, is the pulling. The relief — the *aḥsanu taʾwīlan*, the beautiful homecoming — is the returning. Anyone who has finally let a long grudge go knows that the release felt better than any victory would have.

What does this change about tomorrow? It gives you something to *do* the next time you are locked in a tug-of-war and you can feel both of you pulling harder. Before you reach for the next argument, you ask one question that changes the whole posture: *"Is there a point above us both that we could return this to?"* For a fiqh disagreement between believers, that point is literally the Book and the Sunnah — *ruddūhu*, refer it to what Allah and His Messenger ﷺ actually said, and to those with the knowledge to draw it out (4:83). For an ordinary human quarrel, the posture itself is the gift: stop asking "how do I win this rope," start asking "what does the truth of this matter actually belong to, above the two of us?" The believer is the one willing to ask that question while still holding strong convictions — because deep down he knows the rope was always Allah's.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse at once, because the structure has been hiding in front of us the entire time.

In the **command to obey**, Allah names **three**: Allah, the Messenger, those in authority — but gives the verb "obey" only **twice**, so the third party is tethered, not free-standing.

In the **command to return disputes**, Allah names only **two**: Allah and the Messenger — and *ulū al-amr* simply **vanish**.

Two absences. The missing third *aṭīʿū*, and the missing *ulū al-amr* in the referral. And they are not two facts — **they are one fact, stated twice.** Both silences say: *human authority is real, dignified, and binding — and it is also borrowed, tethered, and never the final court.* The first half builds the structure of authority; the second half shows you what that structure *collapses back into* the instant there is genuine dispute — it collapses up, to the two whose verb was never tethered to anyone.

The whole ayah is one shape: **everything rises to Allah and His Messenger, and everything returns to Allah and His Messenger.** *Aṭīʿū* points up. *Ruddūhu* sends back up. And *taʾwīl* — the homecoming — *is* up. Authority flows down from the two absolutes and disputes flow back to them. It is a closed, breathing circuit of a just society, and it is the literal answer to the verse before it (4:58), where Allah told the powerful to return trusts to their owners. The powerful *return* trusts; the people *return* disputes. The whole community is built on one verb: *radd* — give it back to where it belongs.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discovery and the lived themes are the same insight seen from two sides.

The grammar showed us: obedience to creation is always tethered to obedience to the Creator. The life showed us: that tether is your freedom — it is the line Allah drew so you would never have to choose between belonging and your soul, and it is the point above every tug-of-war that lets a believer let go of the rope without being crushed.

True faith, this verse says, is not measured when you agree. It is measured in two hard places: the moment a human command pulls against Allah's, and the moment you are *sure* you are right and someone is pulling the other way. In both, the believer does the same single thing — **he returns the matter, above himself, to the One it always belonged to.**

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life am I obeying out of fear and calling it faith — and where am I disobeying out of ego and calling it conscience? How would the question *"is this command still hooked to obeying Allah?"* sort the two?
2. When was the last time I let go of a rope I was sure I should win? What did the release feel like compared to the pulling?
3. In my disagreements with other believers, do I actually *return* the matter to the Book and the Sunnah — or do I reach for those sources only when they happen to back the side I had already chosen?
4. Is there a person whose authority over me is legitimate and *minkum*, whom I resist simply because submitting is uncomfortable — not because they ever unhooked from Allah?
5. What would change in my home, my work, my community if everyone treated disputes as ropes to *return upward* rather than ropes to *win*?

### One-sentence distillation

Obey fully, but never blindly — because the authority you owe is always borrowing from the One you owe absolutely; and prove your faith not when you agree, but in the moment you are sure you are right, by letting go of the rope and returning the matter to Allah and His Messenger.

### The closing invitation

You will be inside a chain of command again today, and you will be inside a disagreement again this week. This verse is not asking you to become rebellious or to become a doormat. It is offering you a third way that is harder and freer than both: to obey willingly in the vast room Allah opened, to refuse only at the hairline where a creature would cost you the Creator, and — when the rope is in your hands and your certainty is at its peak — to be the kind of believer who can still unclench, and send it home. Not because you are weak. Because you finally believe it was never yours to win.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You whose command was never tethered to any other — make our obedience to You free and full, and our obedience to others true only as long as it returns to You. When we are pulled into dispute and certain we are right, give our hands the strength to let go of the rope and return the matter to Your Book and the Sunnah of Your Messenger ﷺ. And make that returning, for us, the most beautiful homecoming — *aḥsanu taʾwīlā*. Āmīn.
