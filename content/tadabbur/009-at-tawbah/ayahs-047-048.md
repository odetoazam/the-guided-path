# Allah Sometimes Protects the Believers by Keeping Certain People Out

## Surah At-Tawbah (9:47-48)

[ayah:9:47] لَوْ خَرَجُوا۟ فِيكُم مَّا زَادُوكُمْ إِلَّا خَبَالًا وَلَأَوْضَعُوا۟ خِلَـٰلَكُمْ يَبْغُونَكُمُ ٱلْفِتْنَةَ وَفِيكُمْ سَمَّـٰعُونَ لَهُمْ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌۢ بِٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

[ayah:9:48] لَقَدِ ٱبْتَغَوُا۟ ٱلْفِتْنَةَ مِن قَبْلُ وَقَلَّبُوا۟ لَكَ ٱلْأُمُورَ حَتَّىٰ جَآءَ ٱلْحَقُّ وَظَهَرَ أَمْرُ ٱللَّهِ وَهُمْ كَـٰرِهُونَ

*Law kharaju fikum ma zadukum illa khabalan wa la-awda'u khilalakum yabghunakumu al-fitnah wa fikum samma'una lahum. Wa Allahu 'alimun bi-al-zalimin.*

*Laqadi ibtaghawu al-fitnata min qablu wa qallabu laka al-umura hatta ja'a al-haqqu wa zahara amru Allahi wa hum karihun.*

**Had they gone out among you, they would have increased you in nothing but corruption, and they would have rushed about in your midst seeking fitnah for you; and among you are listeners for them. And Allah fully knows the wrongdoers. They had already sought fitnah before and kept turning matters upside down for you until the truth came and Allah's command became manifest while they were averse.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

We often assume that more people automatically means more strength.

These ayahs shatter that illusion. The hypocrites being kept back was not only exposure of them. It was also protection for the believers. If they had gone out, they would not have increased the community in resolve, clarity, or strength. They would have increased it only in **khabal**: confusion, corruption, internal spoilage, and destabilization.

[PAUSE]

Before going further, it is worth being clear about who is being described and when. The setting is the expedition to Tabuk, a long and difficult march in heat and hardship, and the passage sits immediately after the verse that says Allah disliked their being sent out and so held them back. The people described here are a specific set of individuals in Madinah at a specific moment, whose behaviour the Qur'an itself is exposing. Al-Tabari, commenting on these two verses, names the pattern with a concrete example from that period: the withdrawal at Uhud. These verses are a divine disclosure about named actors in a known history. They are not a lens the Qur'an hands us for scanning the faces around us. Everything in this reflection stays on that side of the line, and the self-examination it asks for is aimed inward.

[PAUSE]

Then the ayah gets even more precise. They would have moved rapidly through the ranks seeking fitnah. This means their threat was not passive. They were not just weak links who might fail under pressure. They were active agents of agitation, spreading vulnerability from inside the body. And the verse adds something even more uncomfortable: among you are listeners for them. So the danger is not only the whisperer, but the ear available to whisper.

Notice, too, that the whole of verse 47 is a description of something that never happened. It opens with **law** — *had they*, *if only they had* — the particle Arabic uses for a condition understood to be contrary to fact. Allah is not narrating a disaster the believers survived. He is narrating a disaster they were never made to face. The damage listed here is entirely hypothetical, which is exactly what makes it a mercy: the community is being shown the bill it did not have to pay.

The second ayah makes clear that none of this is new. They had been chasing fitnah before. They had already been flipping matters over, trying to complicate, churn, and destabilize until the truth arrived and Allah's command stood clear despite their dislike. The surah is teaching the believers to recognize that some people do not merely arrive late to obedience. They carry a longstanding appetite for disorder.

The hook of the passage is this: **At-Tawbah teaches that not every subtraction from a community is a loss, because Allah may be protecting the believers by removing people whose presence would only multiply confusion from within.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Law kharaju fikum**: Not *With* You — *Inside* You

Start with the opening clause:

**law kharaju fikum**.

Had they gone out among you.

[PAUSE]

The preposition matters. The verse does not say *ma'akum*, "along with you," which would picture them travelling alongside the army as a separate block. It says **fikum** — *in* you, *among* you, inside the body. That single letter sets up everything that follows. The problem was never going to be an extra column marching at the rear. It was going to be a presence distributed through the ranks, mixed in, indistinguishable, positioned wherever the seams were.

And the verb is **kharaju**, from the root خرج, the same root the surah has been using for this whole march: *going out*, the physical act of leaving Madinah for the campaign. The word carries no suspicion in itself. Going out was the good thing. That is what makes this verse so unsettling — the very act that would have looked like obedience is the one Allah says would have cost the believers.

### 2. **Ma zadukum illa khabalan**: More Can Mean Worse

Verse 47 says:

**ma zadukum illa khabalan**.

They would have increased you in nothing but corruption.

[PAUSE]

This is a profound correction to our instinctive trust in numbers. Increase is not always benefit. Addition can be diseased. A person may enlarge the group outwardly while rotting it inwardly.

Look at how tightly the sentence is built. It is a restriction: *ma* ... *illa*, "nothing except." Arabic uses this structure to slam a door. Every other possible outcome is denied first, and then exactly one is let through. The verse does not say they would have added little. It says the entire content of their contribution — the whole of it, without remainder — would have been **khabal**.

And notice that the verb is still an *increase* verb. **Zadukum** concedes the arithmetic. They really would have been an addition. The numbers really would have gone up. The verse simply refuses to let the numbers mean what the community assumed they meant. Growth was on offer. It was growth of the wrong substance.

Al-Tabari glosses **khabal** here plainly: *fasad* and *darr* — spoilage and harm. The word covers the kind of damage that is not a clean break but a rot: disorder in the mind, unsoundness in the body, a thing coming apart from the inside rather than being struck from the outside. Al-Muyassar renders the effect as disturbance running through the ranks. This is not the vocabulary of defeat. It is the vocabulary of decay.

### 3. **La-awda'u khilalakum**: They Would Have Ridden Hard Through Your Gaps

Then:

**wa la-awda'u khilalakum**.

They would have hurried about through your midst.

[PAUSE]

The picture is not static. They are moving inside the lines, passing through the spaces between people, carrying agitation through the body. The threat here is internal motion, not only external absence.

But the image is sharper than "moving." **Awda'u** comes from the root وضع, and in this usage it is a riding word: al-Tabari explains it as driving their mounts at speed between you. *Idā'* is the quick, urgent pace of a camel being pushed. So the verse is not describing people drifting through a camp. It is describing riders working a column at speed — the same mounts, the same road, the same expedition as everyone else, but the energy is pointed sideways instead of forward. On a campaign where every animal and every hour was needed for the march, their speed would have been spent going *across* the ranks rather than *toward* the destination.

**Khilalakum** completes the picture. The root خلل means the gaps, the intervals, the spaces between things — the seams in a formation rather than the formation itself. They would not have travelled the road. They would have travelled the gaps. Every place where two people are only loosely joined, every crack in a community's cohesion, is precisely where this kind of movement finds room.

And al-Muyassar tells us what they would have been carrying at that speed: **namimah** and **baghda'** — talebearing, and the stoking of mutual dislike. That is the freight. Words moved from one person to another, fast, through the seams, until people who were marching together no longer trusted each other. An army can survive a hard road. It cannot easily survive that.

### 4. **Yabghunakumu al-fitnah**: Their Goal Was Your Trial

Then:

**yabghunakumu al-fitnah**.

They seek fitnah for you.

[PAUSE]

This gives motive, not just effect. They are not accidentally demoralizing the believers. They are seeking the fracture. That is why the previous withholding was mercy.

The tense is worth noticing. **Yabghuna** is the present-tense form — ongoing, unfinished, in-progress. Even inside a sentence about something that never happened, the seeking is rendered as continuous activity. Had they gone out, this is not something they would have done once and stopped. It is what they would have been doing the whole way.

The commentators are also concrete about what the sought-after fitnah actually was. Al-Muyassar identifies it as **tathbit** — the work of discouragement, sapping the believers' will to go forward. Al-Tabari, on the following verse, describes the same aim reaching further: turning believers away from their religion, wearing down their resolve until they slid back. So "fitnah" here is not vague trouble. It is a specific sequence: first the courage goes, then the commitment.

And the root of **yabghuna** is بغي — *seeking, pursuing, wanting*. Hold onto that root. The next verse will use it again, and the repetition is the hinge of the whole passage.

### 5. **Wa fikum samma'una lahum**: Fitnah Needs Receptive Ears

Then:

**wa fikum samma'una lahum**.

Among you are listeners for them.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most searching lines in the passage. The Qur'an does not only warn us against corrupt speakers. It warns us about becoming the kind of ears through which corruption travels.

The word itself is intensive. **Samma'un** is not the ordinary word for someone who hears; it is built on the *fa''al* pattern, the form Arabic uses for habit, repetition, and appetite — the pattern behind words like *ghaffar* and *'allam*. A *samma'* is not a person who happened to overhear something. It is a person whose listening is a settled disposition. The problem is not one careless conversation. It is a standing readiness to receive.

Here classical commentary splits, and the split turns on one small word: **lahum**, "for them" or "to them." Both readings are held by respected commentators, and the Arabic genuinely carries both.

On the first reading, the *lam* means *to*: among you are people who listen *to* them — ears that take in what the agitators say, absorb it, and are moved by it. On this reading the danger is credulity. The community's weak point is the person who enjoys hearing the corrosive thing.

On the second reading, the *lam* means *for* or *on behalf of*: among you are people who listen *for* them. Al-Muyassar takes this line explicitly, glossing *samma'un lahum* as **'uyun lahum** — "eyes for them," people who pick up the believers' news and pass it back. On this reading the danger is not credulity but conveyance: an information channel running out of the camp.

[PAUSE]

The verse does not settle between them, and there is no need to force it to. What both readings share is the structural point: the speech of a few only becomes a danger to many when there is something inside the community willing to carry it. A whisper with nowhere to land is just a whisper.

### 6. **Wa Allahu 'alimun bi-al-zalimin**: Who Actually Has the Intelligence

The verse closes:

**wa Allahu 'alimun bi-al-zalimin**.

And Allah fully knows the wrongdoers.

[PAUSE]

Read this against the line that just came before it. A verse that has just described a listening network — people gathering what is said and moving it elsewhere — ends by naming the One whose knowledge is total. The community has just been told it contains ears it cannot identify. It is not then told how to find them. It is told Who already knows.

Notice also what they are called. Not "the hypocrites" here, but **al-zalimin**, the wrongdoers — the root ظلم, which carries the sense of putting a thing where it does not belong. The label is placed on the act, not on a demographic. And al-Muyassar adds the natural completion: that this knowledge is not merely a record but a reckoning; He will requite them for it.

This ending does something important to the whole verse. It closes off the direction the passage could otherwise be dragged in. A community that has just heard "among you are listeners" could turn that into a hunt. The verse forbids it by relocating the knowledge: the identification of wrongdoers belongs to Allah, who does it perfectly, and not to a community that would do it badly.

### 7. **Laqadi ibtaghawu al-fitnata min qablu**: The Same Verb, Already Spent

Verse 48 says:

**laqadi ibtaghawu al-fitnata min qablu**.

They had already sought fitnah before.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hinge. Verse 47 said **yabghunakum al-fitnah** — they *would be* seeking fitnah for you. Verse 48 says **ibtaghawu al-fitnata** — they *already have*. Same root, بغي. Same object, **al-fitnah**. Only the time changes, from a future that was prevented to a past that actually happened.

This is what makes verse 47 more than a suspicion. Allah had just described what these people would do if allowed out. A reasonable listener might ask how anyone could know that in advance. Verse 48 answers by pointing at the record: this is not a prediction about strangers, it is a description of people whose behaviour has already been observed. The hypothetical of verse 47 rests on the history of verse 48.

The verb has also grown. **Yabghuna** in verse 47 is the simple form. **Ibtaghaw** is Form VIII, the pattern that adds effort and acquisition to a root — not merely wanting a thing but going after it, working at it, spending yourself to get it. And the sentence is stamped with **laqad**, the double emphasis of the *lam* plus *qad*, which Arabic uses to make an assertion unarguable. The verse is not floating a possibility. It is putting something on the record.

**Min qablu** — "before" — is left deliberately open in the Arabic, and the commentators fill it from the known history of that period. Al-Muyassar places it before the Tabuk expedition and points to the day of Uhud and the day of al-Khandaq. Al-Tabari cites the same Uhud episode, the withdrawal of Ibn Ubayy and those who followed him partway to the battle. Ibn Kathir stretches the window back further still, to the years soon after the migration to Madinah. So "before" is not a vague past. It is a documented stretch of years, with specific incidents attached, involving specific people the community could name.

### 8. **Qallabu laka al-umura**: They Have Been Turning Matters Over for a Long Time

Then:

**wa qallabu laka al-umura**.

They turned matters over for you.

[PAUSE]

This is sustained destabilization. They kept flipping matters, muddying clarity, complicating what should have been received with submission.

**Qallabu** is Form II of the root قلب — to turn something over. The doubled middle letter is Arabic's way of intensifying a verb: not one turn, but turning and turning, working a thing over from every side. Al-Muyassar glosses the purpose precisely: they turned matters this way and that in order to nullify what the Prophet had brought. The aim was not to argue against the message directly. It was to keep every matter in motion so that nothing could settle. Where there is no settled ground, there is no obedience to give.

And a pronoun shifts here that is easy to walk past. Verse 47 spoke entirely in the plural *you*: **fikum**, **zadukum**, **khilalakum**, **yabghunakum** — the whole community addressed together. Verse 48 turns and says **laka**: singular, to you, Prophet. The damage in verse 47 was aimed at the body; the churn in verse 48 was aimed at the man carrying the message. The Qur'an does not blur these into one grievance. It shows a campaign that worked on two levels at once — fracture the community, and exhaust its Messenger.

### 9. **Hatta ja'a al-haqqu wa zahara amru Allahi**: The Endpoint They Did Not Set

Then the ayah resolves:

**hatta ja'a al-haqqu wa zahara amru Allahi**.

Until the truth came and Allah's command became manifest.

Truth did not win because their opposition was weak. It won because Allah's command finally stood over all their churn.

[PAUSE]

The particle **hatta** is doing quiet work here. It marks a limit — the point at which an ongoing action stops. All that turning over of matters, sustained for years, has a terminus written into the sentence, and the terminus is not their exhaustion. They did not run out of energy. Something arrived.

And look at the verb chosen for that arrival: **zahara**, from ظهر, which means to become outward, visible, uppermost — the opposite pole from everything the previous clauses described. Their whole method was covert: talebearing through the gaps, ears that pass things along quietly, matters kept turning so nothing could be pinned down. Against all of that, the verse does not say Allah's command *defeated* them. It says it became **visible**. What ends concealment is not a louder concealment. It is daylight.

There is one more thing here that only shows in the Arabic. They turned over **al-umur** — matters, plural, the small business of a community, from the root أمر. And what became manifest was **amru Allah** — the matter of Allah, singular, from that same root. They spent years working over the *amrs*. The *amr* that stood at the end was not one of theirs. Al-Muyassar reads this manifestation concretely as the help that came from Allah, honouring His soldiers and giving His religion the upper hand.

### 10. **Wa hum karihun**: A Dislike That Changed Nothing

The passage ends:

**wa hum karihun**.

While they were averse.

[PAUSE]

This is a circumstantial clause — in Arabic, a *hal* — and it is built as a noun sentence rather than a verb, which gives it the sense of a settled state rather than a passing reaction. **Karihun** is an active participle: not "they disliked it" as a single event, but "they were, and remained, disliking." Allah's command became visible *while this was true of them* and stayed true of them. Their aversion did not delay it, dent it, or shorten it by anything at all. It was simply the weather in which the command arrived.

And there is a symmetry in the passage that this closing word completes. Just two verses earlier, in the ayah that sets up this whole unit, the same root كره is used of Allah: He disliked their being sent out, and so held them back. Here the root comes back on the other side — they dislike what Allah made manifest. Two dislikes face each other across the passage, and only one of them determines what happens.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Internal Corruption Is Often More Dangerous Than External Threat

A community usually knows how to recognize the outside enemy. The inside disturber is harder. He speaks familiar language, moves among familiar people, and corrodes cohesion from within.

[PAUSE]

That is why these ayahs are so vital. They teach that some of the gravest threats to collective faith do not arrive at the gate. They circulate in the rows.

The vocabulary of the verse carries this by itself. Al-Tabari's gloss of **khabal** is *fasad* — spoilage. Not a wound, a rot. An army defeated in the field knows it has been defeated and can regroup. A community coming apart at the seams often has no single moment it can point to. It only notices, later, that people who used to trust each other no longer do. The verse names the mechanism precisely: motion through the **khilal**, the gaps, carrying **namimah**, talebearing. Nothing dramatic. Just a great many small transfers of speech, at speed, through the spaces where a body is only loosely joined.

### Theme 2: Protecting the Ummah Requires Guarding Both Tongues and Ears

The mention of listeners is deeply contemporary. Fitnah survives not only because someone speaks it, but because others want the thrill, suspicion, or emotional charge of receiving it.

[PAUSE]

The believing community must learn to become hard ground for destabilizing whispers.

The intensive form of **samma'un** sharpens this. The verse does not describe a lapse; it describes a habit. Someone who has become a *samma'* has developed a taste. And whichever of the two classical readings we take — the ear that absorbs, or the ear that relays — the practical safeguard is the same, because both readings describe a person who has made himself useful to the wrong traffic. One takes the corrosive thing in. The other passes it on. Both are needed for a whisper to become a fracture, and neither requires the whisperer to be present.

There is something merciful in the fact that this is the one part of the passage a reader can actually act on. We are not given the power to identify who the agitators are. We are given complete access to what kind of ear we have become.

### Theme 3: The Community's Real Intelligence Is Allah's Knowledge

Verse 47 tells the believers that their ranks contain listeners, and then immediately tells them that Allah knows the wrongdoers.

[PAUSE]

Those two statements sit next to each other for a reason. The first creates a problem the community cannot solve — you cannot see who is listening. The second says the problem is already solved, by Someone else. The verse deliberately does not hand the believers a method of detection, and that omission is the point. It closes the door on the response that would have destroyed them faster than any hypocrite could: mutual suspicion, everyone auditing everyone, a community that consumes itself trying to find the enemy inside.

This is also why the passage is safe to read and dangerous to misuse. Allah reserves this knowledge to Himself and, in the specific case of the Tabuk generation, disclosed part of it directly through revelation to His Messenger. That disclosure has no successor. The classical commentators, discussing these verses, stay with the actors of that period — al-Tabari with the episode at Uhud, al-Muyassar with Uhud and al-Khandaq before Tabuk, Ibn Kathir with the years after the migration to Madinah. They describe a closed history. The verse gives us its diagnosis of that history and, for ourselves, gives us a mirror rather than a searchlight.

### Theme 4: A Prevented Harm Is Still a Mercy, Even Though You Never Feel It

The strangest quality of verse 47 is that everything in it is unreal. The corruption, the riders through the gaps, the sought-after fitnah — none of it occurred. It is the description of a road not taken.

[PAUSE]

That is a difficult kind of mercy to be grateful for, because it leaves no evidence. The believers marched to Tabuk and experienced a hard journey, not a fractured one. Nothing visibly good happened; something invisible simply failed to happen. Had Allah not told them, they would never have known what the withholding of those men had spared them.

Which raises the honest question underneath this verse. Most of what we are protected from, we never learn about. The subtraction feels like a loss at the time — fewer people, thinner ranks, a smaller turnout than hoped for. The verse is one of the rare places where the curtain is lifted and the arithmetic is shown from the other side.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs explain that if the hypocrites had gone out with the believers, they would have brought only corruption, frantic internal agitation, and fitnah-seeking. They also expose that some within the community were ready listeners for them. Then the surah reminds us that this appetite for disorder was old: they had sought fitnah before and kept turning matters over until the truth came and Allah's command became manifest against their dislike.

[PAUSE]

Notice how the two verses are joined. The first is entirely hypothetical, built on **law**; the second is entirely factual, stamped with **laqad**. The same root — بغي, seeking — runs through both, once as what they would have done and once as what they had already done. The prevented future is guaranteed by the recorded past. That is the whole argument of the passage in miniature, and it explains why the withholding described a verse earlier was not arbitrary. Allah did not hold back people who might have caused harm. He held back people whose record was complete.

And the passage ends by relocating the outcome. They worked the **umur**, the matters, over and over. What finally stood was the **amr** of Allah — not argued into place, not won by a stronger counter-campaign, but simply made **zahir**: visible, out in the open, above everything. Years of covert churn were not answered in kind. They were ended by daylight.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: do I still imagine that every extra voice, every extra body, and every apparent increase is automatically a blessing? In the communities I belong to, do I recognize the damage of internal agitation early enough? And am I the kind of ear through which corrosive speech finds easy passage?

[PAUSE]

And one thing to be careful about. This passage hands us a diagnosis, not a warrant. The verse's own conclusion — *and Allah fully knows the wrongdoers* — is precisely the sentence that keeps this knowledge out of our hands. Allah exposed a specific set of people at a specific moment through revelation, and the commentators keep that material where it belongs, in the years between the migration and Tabuk. What is left for us is the one part we can actually see: our own speech, and our own appetite for listening.

[PAUSE]

The architecture of this unit lands here: **Allah may protect a believing body not only by giving it strength, but by denying entrance to those whose presence would multiply fitnah from within its own ranks.**
