# When Asking Permission Reveals What the Heart Already Decided

## Surah At-Tawbah (9:45-46)

[ayah:9:45] إِنَّمَا يَسْتَـْٔذِنُكَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ وَٱرْتَابَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ فَهُمْ فِى رَيْبِهِمْ يَتَرَدَّدُونَ

[ayah:9:46] ۞ وَلَوْ أَرَادُوا۟ ٱلْخُرُوجَ لَأَعَدُّوا۟ لَهُۥ عُدَّةً وَلَـٰكِن كَرِهَ ٱللَّهُ ٱنۢبِعَاثَهُمْ فَثَبَّطَهُمْ وَقِيلَ ٱقْعُدُوا۟ مَعَ ٱلْقَـٰعِدِينَ

*Innama yasta'dhinuka alladhina la yu'minuna bi-Allahi wa al-yawmi al-akhiri wa irtabat qulubuhum fa-hum fi raybihim yataraddadun.*

*Wa law aradu al-khuruj la-a'addu lahu 'uddatan wa lakin kariha Allahu inbi'athahum fa-thabbatahum wa qila uq'udu ma'a al-qa'idin.*

**Only those ask your permission who do not truly believe in Allah and the Last Day and whose hearts have fallen into doubt, so they keep wavering in their doubt. Had they truly wanted to go out, they would have prepared for it a proper preparation. But Allah disliked their rising, so He held them back, and it was said: sit with those who sit behind.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

These ayahs teach us that sometimes the request itself is the revelation.

Outwardly, someone is only asking permission. It can sound polite, respectful, procedural. But the Qur'an refuses to treat this moment as neutral. In this context, the ones repeatedly asking for leave are not described as cautious believers trying to do the right thing. They are described as people whose faith is compromised, whose hearts have entered doubt, and whose souls keep circling inside indecision.

[PAUSE]

Before going further, it is worth being precise about the setting and about who is described, because a verse like this can be badly misused. The march is Tabuk: a long expedition in heat and hardship, and one that the Qur'an itself is commenting on as it happens. The people here are a specific set of individuals in Madinah at a specific moment. And the commentators put a limit on the description that the English translation can hide. Al-Tabari, explaining this ayah, says the people meant are those who sought leave to stay behind after the Prophet and to abandon the striving with him — and then he adds four words that do a great deal of work: *without a clear excuse*. The verse is not describing everyone who did not march. It is describing those who had no reason and asked anyway. Keeping that limit visible is not softening the verse; it is reading it as the commentators read it. Everything below stays on that side of the line, and the self-examination the passage asks for is aimed inward, at the reader's own heart and no one else's.

[PAUSE]

Then the next verse removes any remaining ambiguity. If they had truly wanted to go out, they would have prepared. Real intention leaves material traces. A heart that actually means to obey starts arranging itself toward obedience before the final moment arrives. It gathers what is needed. It gets ready. It leans forward. So when no such preparation exists, the excuse spoken at the end is only the audible form of a refusal made much earlier inside the heart.

The commentators keep this refreshingly concrete. Al-Muyassar says that had they meant to go, they would have got ready with **zad** and **rahilah** — provisions and a mount. Al-Tabari widens it slightly: they would have made ready the equipment for the journey and the equipment for meeting the enemy, both. This is not an abstract test of sincerity. It is a question about food, water, and an animal. The evidence the verse asks for is the kind you could have pointed at in a courtyard.

And then comes one of the most sobering lines in the passage: Allah disliked their rising. So He held them back. This means there are states in which joining the sacred effort is not an honor automatically granted to whoever presents himself at the surface. A diseased heart can reach the point where its participation is itself unwelcome before Allah.

One clarification has to be made immediately, because the verse can be read as though it excuses them. It does not. Al-Muyassar states the distinction plainly: Allah disliked their going out, so the going out was made heavy upon them *by decree and destiny*, even though He had commanded it of them *in the Law*. Two things are true at once. What Allah decreed came to pass, and what Allah commanded still stood over them. Their sitting was still their sin. The verse describes what happened to them, not a permission slip handed to them.

The hook of the passage is this: **At-Tawbah teaches that repeated excuse-seeking often reveals not a last-minute difficulty but a heart that had already withdrawn from obedience long before it asked permission to stay behind.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Innama yasta'dhinuka**: The Restriction Is Itself an Exposure

Verse 45 opens:

**innama yasta'dhinuka**.

Only those ask your permission.

[PAUSE]

The restricting force of **innama** matters. The ayah is not merely saying that these people asked. It is identifying a class of people through the act. In this setting, the habit of seeking exemption becomes diagnostic.

Look at the shape of the verb itself. **Yasta'dhinu** is Form X of the root أذن, the pattern Arabic reserves for *seeking* a thing — the same pattern behind *istaghfara*, to seek forgiveness, and *ista'ana*, to seek help. The root by itself means permission. The form turns it into a request. So the word is not "they were absent"; it is "they come looking for the word that would make absence acceptable." And it is in the present tense, the tense of ongoing and repeated action. This is not one man with one problem on one morning. It is a stream of requests arriving at the same door.

And that door is named. **Yasta'dhinu-ka** — they seek permission *from you*, singular, the Prophet himself. Al-Tabari fills in what exactly was being asked for: leave to remain behind after him and to give up the striving alongside him — and, he specifies, without a clear excuse. Notice the word he uses for staying behind, *khilafaka*: it means both *after you* and *contrary to you*. In this setting, being left behind and being at odds are the same position seen from two angles.

### 2. **Alladhina la yu'minuna bi-Allahi wa al-yawmi al-akhir**: What Was Actually Missing

Then the verse names them:

**alladhina la yu'minuna bi-Allahi wa al-yawmi al-akhir**.

Those who do not believe in Allah and the Last Day.

[PAUSE]

This is a much deeper charge than cowardice, and it is worth slowing down for. Al-Tabari glosses the phrase as those who do not affirm Allah as true and do not acknowledge His oneness. Al-Muyassar adds a third element to the list: nor do they do righteous work. So the diagnosis is not "they were afraid of the desert." It is that the foundation was never laid.

And notice which two things are paired. Allah, and the Last Day. The beginning of accountability and its end. Those two beliefs are exactly what makes a hard, hot, unprofitable march make sense. If the Last Day is real, then everything spent on this road is stored somewhere and the loss is temporary. If it is not real, then the road is pure cost with nothing at the other end, and asking to stay home is simply good arithmetic. The verse is telling us that the men asking for leave were not doing bad arithmetic. They were doing correct arithmetic on the wrong ledger.

### 3. **Wa irtabat qulubuhum**: The Problem Is Located in the Heart

Then:

**wa irtabat qulubuhum**.

Their hearts fell into doubt.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an does not first blame logistics, weather, or inconvenience. It locates the fracture in the inner organ that should have steadied them. Once the heart enters **rayb**, outward hesitation multiplies naturally.

The tense is doing something precise here. **Irtabat** is a past-tense verb, and a Form VIII one at that — the pattern that turns a root inward, describing something the subject has taken into itself. The doubt is presented as a finished event. It already happened; the hearts already went over. What follows in the next clause will be in the present tense, ongoing and unfinished. So the verse hands us a completed cause and an unfinished symptom, in that order. Something closed in the past is still producing motion in the present.

The commentators are also specific about what, exactly, the doubt was about — and it is not what a modern reader would guess. Al-Tabari lists three objects: the reality of Allah's oneness, the reward waiting for those who obey Him, and the punishment waiting for those who disobey Him. Al-Muyassar names a fourth: doubt about the soundness of what the Prophet had brought, of Islam and its laws. None of these is doubt about the expedition. The uncertainty was never about whether the campaign was wise. It was about whether any of it was true.

That reframes the whole passage. The excuse-making at the surface is downstream of a creed problem, not a courage problem. The men in this verse did not lack nerve. They lacked conviction that there was anything on the other side of the march worth having.

### 4. **Fa-hum fi raybihim yataraddadun**: Doubt Produces Perpetual Wavering

Then:

**fa-hum fi raybihim yataraddadun**.

So they keep wavering in their doubt.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the clearest descriptions of spiritually paralyzed indecision. They are not described as carefully discerning. They are circling. Doubt becomes not a question they move through, but an atmosphere they keep living inside.

The word is built to say exactly that. **Yataraddadun** comes from the root ردد, whose basic meaning is *to send back, to return a thing to where it came from*. The root already has a reversal inside it. Then the verb is put in Form V, the reflexive-intensive pattern, which turns the action back on the doer and makes it repeat. Put those together and you get people who keep sending themselves back — arriving at a decision and returning to the start, over and over, on their own steam. **Taraddud** in Arabic is precisely this: going back and forth without arriving.

And al-Tabari does not translate it as thinking. He glosses it as **mutahayyirun** — bewildered, at a loss. That is a different state from careful deliberation. A person weighing a matter is moving toward something. A person in *hayrah* has lost the direction in which movement would even count.

Then there is the small word **fi**. They are not troubled *by* their doubt, as though it were an obstacle standing in front of them. They are **fi raybihim** — *inside* their doubt. It is not the thing they are facing. It is the space they are moving around in. That is why the wavering never resolves: you cannot walk out of a room by pacing it.

Notice also the **fa-** that opens the clause. It is the particle of immediate consequence — *and so*, *therefore*. The wavering is not an additional fault listed alongside the doubting heart. It is what a doubting heart automatically produces. The verse gives us a mechanism, not a list.

### 5. **Law aradu al-khuruj la-a'addu lahu 'uddah**: Real Intention Prepares

Verse 46 says:

**wa law aradu al-khuruj la-a'addu lahu 'uddah**.

Had they wanted to go out, they would have prepared for it a preparation.

[PAUSE]

This is devastating because it reads behind the spoken excuse into the practical truth of the person. Intention is not invisible in the absolute sense. It shows itself in what we begin arranging before the decisive hour arrives.

The sentence opens with **law**, the particle Arabic uses for a condition understood to be untrue — *had they wanted to*, with the unspoken completion, *but they did not*. So the verse is not raising a possibility. It is closing one, and using the closure as evidence.

The vocabulary is chosen with unusual care. The verb for wanting is **arada**, from the root رود, and that root belongs to the world of travel: the *ra'id* is the scout a travelling group sends ahead to look for water and pasture before the party moves. Wanting, in this root, already leans forward. It looks down the road. It is not a feeling; it is a reconnaissance. So the verse is saying that if the *irada* had been real, it would have already gone ahead of them.

And then the word for preparation. **A'addu ... 'uddatan** comes from the root عدد, whose primary meaning is *counting, number, enumeration*. The Arabic word for getting ready is built on the word for counting things out. **'Uddah** is the gear, the kit — items you can total. That is the standard the verse applies: not "did they feel committed?" but "what was counted out and set aside?" And the construction doubles down. The verb takes its own verbal noun as its object — *they would have prepared for it a preparation* — a pattern Arabic uses to make a claim unarguable, closing the gap between the act and its full measure.

The commentators keep this at ground level. Al-Muyassar: they would have got ready with provisions and a mount. Al-Tabari: they would have made ready what the journey required and what the enemy required, both — travel gear and battle gear. Two lists, neither of them assembled. And in a town where an expedition was being outfitted in the open, the absence of two such lists was not a hidden thing. Their hearts were private. Their storerooms were not.

### 6. **Kariha Allahu inbi'athahum**: Divine Withholding Can Be Judgment

Then:

**wa lakin kariha Allahu inbi'athahum**.

But Allah disliked their rising.

[PAUSE]

This is frightening and clarifying. Not every closed door means a servant was wronged. Sometimes being held back is itself a form of divine judgment on a diseased inner state. Ibn Kathir puts it without cushioning: Allah hated that they should go out with him.

The word for their going is worth stopping on. It is not *khuruj*, "going out," which is what verse 46 used a moment ago. It is **inbi'ath**, from the root بعث — the root of *sending forth* and of *ba'th*, the raising of the dead. It is the vocabulary of being roused, lifted from a settled position, set in motion by something outside yourself. And it is Form VII, the pattern of yielding to that motion rather than generating it. So the word already contains the picture the passage ends with: people who are sitting, and the question of whether they will be raised.

That is the axis this whole verse turns on. Rising and sitting. **Inbi'athahum**, their being raised up — and then, at the end, **uq'udu** and **al-qa'idin**, sit and the sitters, the root قعد struck twice in four words. What Allah declines to grant them is the rising. What they are left with is the sitting, named twice so it cannot be mistaken for a temporary posture.

One quiet detail: the verb here is **kariha**, from the root كره. Two verses later the same root returns, but pointed the other way — they are described as *karihun*, averse, while Allah's command stands manifest. Two dislikes face each other across this passage, and only one of them decides anything.

### 7. **Fa-thabbatahum**: Made Heavy, So That Sitting Felt Light

Then:

**fa-thabbatahum**.

So He held them back.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari's explanation of this single word is the most illuminating line in the whole report. He says: *He made going out heavy upon them, until sitting in their houses felt light to them.* That is a description of an inner weather change, not a locked gate. Nobody chained them. The exact same road, the same heat, the same distance that a believer was carrying with a light heart became, for them, unbearable — while staying behind, which should have been the shameful option, started to feel reasonable, even comfortable.

This is what makes the punishment so exact. It matches the crime in kind. They wanted sitting to be acceptable. It was made to feel acceptable. Nothing was taken from them except the ability to find the right thing attractive.

And the whole surah has been working this scale of weight. Earlier it asked why, when they were told to march, they clung heavily to the earth — *iththaqaltum ila al-ard*. Then it commanded: go forth *khifafan wa thiqalan*, light and heavy, whichever you are. Weight is not the disqualification. Here, the weight is put in the one place that stops a person entirely: not on the body, on the willingness.

Then the **fa-** again, the particle of immediate consequence: *He disliked their rising, so He held them back*. The holding back is not a separate decision taken later. It follows the dislike the way the wavering followed the doubt.

And this is precisely where the theological guardrail belongs, because a reader can easily draw the wrong conclusion — that they were prevented, and therefore not accountable. Al-Muyassar blocks that reading directly: Allah disliked their going out, so it was made heavy upon them *by decree and destiny* — even though He had commanded them to it *in the sacred Law*. Two different senses of Allah's will are in play, and the commentators keep them apart. What He decrees is what actually happens. What He commands is what is owed. These men were not released from the second by the operation of the first.

### 8. **Wa qila uq'udu ma'a al-qa'idin**: The Company You Are Assigned

Then comes:

**wa qila uq'udu ma'a al-qa'idin**.

And it was said: sit with those who sit behind.

[PAUSE]

The spiritual station they chose inwardly becomes the company they are assigned outwardly.

Notice first that the verb is passive. **Qila** — *it was said*. The speaker is not named. The verse does not tell us whether this was spoken aloud by someone in Madinah, or spoken to them by the state of things itself, or spoken over them in the unseen. It simply arrives as a sentence with no visible mouth behind it, which makes it feel less like an instruction and more like a verdict being read out.

And then the sting, which is easy to miss without the commentary. Who are *al-qa'idin*, the ones already sitting? Al-Muyassar identifies them: the sick, the weak, the women, and the children. These are the people the sacred Law itself excused — people whose sitting is no fault at all. So the men in this verse are not sent to a category of shame. They are sent to a category of mercy that does not belong to them. They are seated among those who have a real reason, while having none.

That is a heavier sentence than exile would have been. They wanted their absence to look like the absence of the excused. They are given exactly that appearance, permanently, and the Qur'an publishes the difference.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Excuses Often Arrive at the End of a Much Older Withdrawal

These ayahs help us read ourselves more honestly. We often imagine the key spiritual battle happens at the final moment of decision. But many decisions are already largely made before that point. The last-minute excuse is often only the visible fruit of long-term spiritual cooling.

[PAUSE]

That means one of the best protections against hypocrisy is to notice what we are preparing for when no one is yet forcing the issue.

The verse gives this a usable shape by choosing a counting word. **'Uddah** is gear you can total; provisions and a mount, in al-Muyassar's reading. Sincerity is being tested against inventory. That is uncomfortable, but it is also fair in a way that feelings never are — nobody can audit your enthusiasm, and everybody can see whether the bags were packed. Whatever the obedience is that we keep saying we intend, the honest question is not how strongly we intend it. It is what has been counted out and set aside for it, and when.

### Theme 2: Some Doors Close Because Allah Knows What Our Participation Would Carry

The line about Allah disliking their rising should make us tremble. We usually think only in terms of wanting opportunities. The Qur'an teaches that an opportunity can be withheld because the inner state coming toward it is corrupt.

[PAUSE]

So the real plea is not merely "O Allah, open the door," but "O Allah, make me fit for the door You open."

But this theme has to be handled carefully, or it curdles. The verse does not license anyone to read their own closed doors, or anyone else's, as a verdict of divine displeasure. What made this a verdict was that Allah announced it. Revelation named the state of these particular hearts; nothing in the passage suggests we can run the same inference from the outside. Doors close for a hundred reasons, and the Qur'an elsewhere shows the beloved of Allah being kept from what they want. The right use of this verse is not diagnosis. It is fear — a fear that keeps a person checking the condition in which they are approaching the door, since that is the only part of the arrangement they can actually see.

### Theme 3: Being Prevented Is Not the Same as Being Excused

The most theologically important sentence in the commentary on this passage is al-Muyassar's, and it answers the objection every honest reader will feel. If Allah disliked their rising and made the going out heavy on them, how can they be blamed for sitting?

[PAUSE]

The answer the commentators give is that two different things are being spoken of. Allah's decree is what comes to pass; Allah's command is what is owed. He made the march heavy on them by decree and destiny, *even though He had commanded them to it in the sacred Law*. The decree describes; the command obliges. Being carried by the first has never cancelled the second.

This matters far beyond these two verses, because it is the standard shape of every excuse built on destiny. A person does the thing, then points at the fact that it happened as though the happening authorized it. The Qur'an keeps the two ledgers separate. What Allah allowed to occur and what Allah asked of you are answered in different courts, and only one of them is your business.

### Theme 4: The Verse Names a Disease, Not a Category of People

It matters enormously how this passage is read, because a verse that diagnoses hidden motives is the easiest verse in the Qur'an to turn into a weapon.

[PAUSE]

The text builds in its own limits, and they are worth naming plainly. Al-Tabari restricts the condemnation to those who sought leave *without a clear excuse*. Al-Muyassar tells us the ones already sitting were the sick, the weak, the women, and the children — people whose sitting Allah Himself accepted. So the passage is not against staying behind. It is against a specific inner state: hearts that had gone over into doubt about Allah, the Last Day, and the truth of the message, and then dressed that state in a request for permission.

And the diagnosis was made by revelation, addressed to a Prophet who was receiving it. That instrument is not in our hands. The classical commentators discussing these ayahs stay entirely inside that history — Tabuk, the men of that moment, the request made at that door. Nothing here transfers to a suspicion we may form about a fellow Muslim whose reasons we cannot see. What does transfer is the one heart we have actual access to.

## Closing Synthesis

This passage identifies the repeated request for permission as a sign of weak faith and doubting hearts, describes such people as wavering inside their own uncertainty, exposes their lack of preparation as proof that they never truly meant to go, and then reveals that Allah Himself disliked their rising and held them back among those who stay behind.

[PAUSE]

Notice how tightly the two verses are wired to each other. Verse 45 works entirely on the inside: a heart that has already gone over into doubt, and a man circling in it without arriving. Verse 46 works entirely on the outside: gear that was never counted out, provisions never gathered, a mount never readied. One verse gives the condition, the other gives the evidence, and the Qur'an does not ask us to take the first on trust — it points at the empty storeroom.

And the sequence of verbs at the end is its own small architecture. He disliked their **rising** — the word from the root of being raised up. So He made the road heavy, until sitting felt light. And then the last word of the passage seats them: sit, with the sitters. The unit begins with men standing at a door asking a question, and ends with them assigned a posture.

One way to sit with these ayahs is to ask: what have I been calling hesitation that is actually doubt left untreated? Where in my life am I waiting until the last moment to speak an excuse for an obedience I never truly prepared for? And if Allah were to read my hidden intentions through the preparations of my life, what would those preparations say about me?

[PAUSE]

And one thing to be careful about. This passage hands us a mirror, not a searchlight. Allah exposed the hearts of specific men at a specific moment by revealing it, and the commentators keep the material exactly there — at Tabuk, among those who asked for leave without a reason, while the sick and the weak and the women and the children sat with Allah's own permission. The verse never invites us to run its diagnosis on someone whose reasons we cannot see, and every safeguard the commentators add points the same way. Al-Tabari's *without a clear excuse* is a fence. The only person on the other side of it, for us, is ourselves.

[PAUSE]

The architecture of this unit lands here: **when the heart has already withdrawn from Allah's call, excuse-seeking becomes its public language, and even being left behind can become a sign that Allah knew the rising itself was unworthy.**
