# Until They Pay — The Terminal Limit on War and the Grammar of Just Order

> **قَـٰتِلُوا۟ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَلَا بِٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ وَلَا يُحَرِّمُونَ مَا حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُۥ وَلَا يَدِينُونَ دِينَ ٱلْحَقِّ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ حَتَّىٰ يُعْطُوا۟ ٱلْجِزْيَةَ عَن يَدٍ وَهُمْ صَـٰغِرُونَ**
>
> *Qātilū alladhīna lā yuʾminūna billāhi wa lā bil-yawmil-ākhiri wa lā yuḥarrimūna mā ḥarrama'llāhu wa rasūluhu wa lā yadīnūna dīnal-ḥaqqi mina'lladhīna ūtū'l-kitāba ḥattā yuʿṭū'l-jizyata ʿan yadin wa hum ṣāghirūn.*
>
> "Fight those — from among the people given the Scripture — who do not believe in Allah or the Last Day, do not treat as forbidden what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden, and do not adhere to the religion of truth, until they pay the jizyah out of hand, having been brought low."

## Introduction — The Verse Everyone Quotes and Almost No One Reads in Place

There are perhaps five or six ayahs in the entire Quran that are quoted more often by people who do not love this Book than by people who do. This is one of them.

It is quoted in Islamophobic blog posts as proof of permanent enmity. It is quoted by extremist propagandists as license for unbounded war. It is quoted in sober academic papers as evidence of "religious supremacism." It is rarely quoted by ordinary Muslims, who tend to skip past it quickly because they have been told it is "complicated" and they are not sure what to do with it.

[PAUSE]

So we have to do something unusual here. We have to slow down on a verse most people read fast. We have to read it inside its own surah, inside its own historical hour, inside its own grammar — not yanked out of all three.

**The hour:** Year 9 of the Hijra. The young Muslim community in Madinah has received credible intelligence that Byzantine Roman forces have massed at the northern frontier near the Syrian border. This is the largest non-Arab military power on earth at the time. The Prophet ﷺ calls for the most arduous mobilization of his life — the Tabuk expedition — through the punishing summer heat. Surah At-Tawbah is, in significant measure, the surah of that mobilization. The classical commentators locate the sabab of this ayah in that frontier crisis, reading *qātilū* as a command addressed to a specific army — al-Ṭabarī opens his commentary by glossing it as Allah's address "to the believers among the Companions of His Messenger ﷺ." This is not a lecture about humanity in general. It is a battlefield ruling delivered to a specific army facing a specific threat.

**The surah:** At-Tawbah opens by terminating treaties that have already been broken by the other side. Then it differentiates — sharply — between treaty-violators and treaty-keepers (vv. 4, 7). Then it commands the believers to grant safe-conduct to any of the polytheists who simply *asks for it*, and to escort him to safety (v. 6). Then it turns, in our ayah, to the question of belligerent powers among the People of the Book. The whole surah is making distinctions, not collapsing them.

**The hook:** The most important word in this entire ayah is a single particle — two letters of Arabic — that almost no critic and almost no extremist quotes. It changes the verse from "fight forever" into "fight until a specific political endpoint." It is the word **ḥattā** — *until*. And it is the door through which classical Islamic law walked when it built the entire jurisprudence of war and peace.

So tonight we are going to do three things. We are going to learn what kind of war this ayah authorizes. We are going to learn where Allah commanded it to *stop*. And we are going to learn what the four descriptions in the middle are actually doing — because they are not insults. They are diagnostics.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The Partitive *min*: مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, the small preposition **مِنْ** (*min*) — usually translated "from" — has several distinct grammatical jobs. One of them, the one classical grammarians call **min al-tabʿīḍiyyah** (*partitive min*), means "from *among*" — pointing to a *part* of a larger whole, not to the whole itself.

In English, think of the difference between "I spoke to the doctors" and "I spoke to some of the doctors." The second sentence narrows. It does not gather everyone in white coats; it isolates a subset within them. That narrowing function is exactly what partitive *min* does in Arabic.

#### Opening the word

Now hear the ayah's structure with this in mind. The command is **qātilū** — "fight." The object is described first by four characteristics. Then comes the qualifier:

**مِنَ الَّذِينَ أُوتُوا الْكِتَابَ** — "*from among* those who have been given the Scripture."

#### Deepening

Notice something. Allah did not say *qātilū alladhīna ūtū'l-kitāb* — "fight those who were given the Scripture." That construction would have made the People of the Book, *as a whole*, the target. Allah did not say that.

What Allah said is: fight *those who* — *from among* the People of the Book — meet these four conditions. The grammatical structure first describes a group by what they do (or fail to do). Then it tells you where to *find* that group: among the recipients of earlier scripture. The People of the Book is the *pool*. The four descriptions are the *filter*.

Stay with this. Classical exegetes were not naive about this construction. The grammarians who treated this *min* as partitive read it as meaning the verse is not a blanket ruling on all Jews and Christians; later commentators in the *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb* and *Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* traditions link it to the specific belligerents at the northern frontier. Ibn Kathīr ties the four descriptions tightly to that historical group whose hostility had become open and military. Even al-Ṭabarī, who reads the target broadly, frames the ayah around *what this group does* — their failure to affirm what God revealed — rather than around their bare identity as scriptuaries.

This is why the historical record is what it is. Within the lifetime of the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions, **Jewish and Christian communities lived under formal covenants of protection** in Madinah, in Najrān, in Khaybar after the campaign, in the conquered Levant. The Pact of Najrān — preserved in the historical record — explicitly guaranteed Christians the protection of their churches, their crosses, their bishops, and their internal religious life. None of that would have been possible if this ayah were a universal command to fight every Christian and Jew on earth.

[PAUSE]

The partitive *min* is the door through which all of that historical practice walked. **The ayah filters; it does not gather.**

#### What this single word reveals

A two-letter particle is doing the work of distinguishing between *belligerent powers* and *neighboring communities*. Read without it, the ayah looks like a sweeping civilizational verdict. Read *with* it, the ayah looks like exactly what it is — a rule of engagement against a specific category of hostile actor inside a much larger population that is otherwise to be lived with under covenant.

### Element Two — The Terminal *ḥattā*: حَتَّىٰ يُعْطُوا الْجِزْيَةَ

#### Teaching the concept first

Arabic has several words for "until." The one Allah uses here, **حَتَّىٰ** (*ḥattā*), is what classical grammarians call a **ghāyah** — a *terminus*, an *endpoint*. It marks the *cessation* of the verb that came before it.

When you say in Arabic, "I walked *ḥattā* I reached the door," the word *ḥattā* tells you two things at once. It tells you the walking *had a destination*. And it tells you the walking *stopped* when that destination was reached. The verb does not continue past the *ḥattā*.

This is the most important grammatical fact in the entire ayah.

#### Opening the word

The verb at the start is **qātilū** — "fight." The terminus is:

**حَتَّىٰ يُعْطُوا الْجِزْيَةَ عَن يَدٍ وَهُمْ صَاغِرُونَ** — "*until* they pay the jizyah, out of hand, having been brought low."

#### Deepening

Let's sit with what this means. Allah did *not* command: *fight them until they convert*. Allah did *not* command: *fight them until they are destroyed*. Allah did *not* command: *fight them until they are gone from the earth*.

Allah commanded: fight them **until they pay**. Until a *political settlement*. Until a *transfer of fiscal-political relationship* from belligerent enemy to protected non-Muslim subject inside an Islamic legal order.

Notice what kind of endpoint this is. It is a *non-coercive religious endpoint*. The jizyah is precisely the alternative to forced conversion. It is the formal recognition that this person *is not going to become a Muslim* — and that, in exchange for a defined tax that replaces the zakāt that Muslims pay and the military service that Muslims are conscripted for, they will be **protected** by the state, their religion preserved, their churches and synagogues guaranteed.

This is why the classical jurists — across the four Sunni madhāhib and the Jaʿfarī school — universally derived from this ayah a terminal limit, not a permanent license. Once the jizyah is offered, fighting must cease. Once a non-Muslim community accepts the dhimma covenant, their blood, their wealth, and their honor become as inviolable as a Muslim's.

The Prophet ﷺ said it in a hadith preserved in Abū Dāwūd: *"Whoever harms a dhimmī, I will be his adversary on the Day of Judgment."* That hadith is unintelligible if you have read this ayah as a license for permanent war.

[PAUSE]

The *ḥattā* converts what looks like an open-ended command into a **bounded one**. It tells the army when to sheathe the sword. It is the difference between a war of conquest with a stopping point and a war of annihilation with no stopping point. Islam, here, is choosing the former and refusing the latter.

#### And then the difficult phrase

We have to address the tail end honestly: **عَن يَدٍ وَهُمْ صَاغِرُونَ** — *ʿan yadin wa hum ṣāghirūn*.

This is one of the most contested clauses in the Quran. We are not going to flatten it into a single reading; we are going to lay out what the classical scholars actually said.

**The phrase ʿan yad** — literally "from a hand" or "out of hand" — has been read by classical exegetes in several converging ways. Some read it as describing the *manner* of payment: paid in person, hand-to-hand, not by remote intermediary, signaling that this is an actual political-civic recognition, not a faceless transaction. Some read *yad* as a metonymy for *power* or *capacity*, meaning paid out of one's own ability, not under such crushing duress that the community is destroyed. Some read it as a contrast with charity received "from on high" — the jizyah is paid *upward*, from subject to state, marking the political relationship clearly.

**The phrase wa hum ṣāghirūn** — "while they are *ṣāghirūn*" — comes from the root *ṣ-gh-r*, which carries the sense of being made small, being humbled, being *subordinated*. This is the most uncomfortable word in the verse for the modern ear. The concise modern *Tafsīr al-Muyassar* renders it plainly as paying "while submissive and lowly" (*khāḍiʿīn adhillāʾ*) — which already shows that the word is not being glossed as *destruction* or *abuse*, but as a posture of submission to authority.

The classical readings differ. One major strand — represented by al-Shāfiʿī and many of his school — read it as describing the **political subordination** itself: the simple fact that this community has accepted a non-sovereign status under the Islamic state is the *ṣaghār*. There is nothing more to it. They are no longer a sovereign belligerent power; they are a protected community within someone else's polity. That structural fact *is* the humbling.

Another strand — and we have to be honest that this strand existed — read it as authorizing some specific ritual humiliation at the moment of payment. Many later Ḥanafī jurists explicitly *rejected* this reading and held that no such ritual is required by the verse; the *ṣaghār* is simply the acceptance of dhimmī status. Ibn Qudāmah, the great Ḥanbalī jurist, in his *al-Mughnī*, similarly resists adding humiliations not specified in the text.

What we can say with grammatical certainty is this: **the verse describes a political relationship in which a defeated belligerent community accepts non-sovereign status inside an Islamic legal order**. What the verse does *not* do — by its own grammar — is authorize the abuse, killing, conversion, or expulsion of that community after that acceptance. The *ḥattā* has closed the door on those.

Sit with the asymmetry of these two readings, because both are part of the tradition. The most demanding reading restricts itself to political subordination. The historical record — when actually examined — shows centuries in which non-Muslims under Islamic rule preserved their faith communities, languages, scriptures, internal courts, and senior administrative roles, often *better* than minorities preserved theirs under contemporaneous Christian Europe. That historical record is the strongest evidence for which reading the *ummah* actually lived.

#### What these two words reveal

The *ḥattā* tells us that this is **war with a destination, not war as a destination**. The destination is a political settlement, not an erasure. The destination is a covenant of protection, not a graveyard. Read with the *ḥattā* held firmly in view, the ayah is an act of *limitation* on what war is allowed to do.

### Bridge — From the Words to the Reality

The partitive *min* tells us *who* is the target — a specific belligerent subset, not a civilization.

The terminal *ḥattā* tells us *when* it stops — at political settlement, not at extermination or conversion.

Now let's ask what this construction reveals not just about words on a page — but about the moral architecture of just war that Allah is building inside this surah.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — War With a Terminus: The Refusal of Unbounded Violence

Begin where you live. You have probably watched, in your own lifetime, wars that had no terminus. You have watched a "war on terror" that lasted twenty years and produced more terror than it eliminated. You have watched cycles of violence in which one atrocity is answered by another atrocity, which is answered by another, and no one — no one — ever defines what *enough* would look like. You have watched the language of "until victory" used in such open-ended ways that it becomes synonymous with permanent war.

[PAUSE]

What Allah does in this ayah, what is so easy to miss because we are scandalized by the verb at the start, is that He **refuses** to authorize that kind of war for the believers.

He gives them a verb: *qātilū*. And then, in the same breath, He gives them a stop sign: *ḥattā yuʿṭū'l-jizyah*. Until they pay. Until political settlement. Until the relationship between you and them is regularized into one of protection and tribute, not blood.

This is structurally the same thing Allah did in Surah al-Baqarah, ayah 193: *"Fight them until there is no more fitnah and the religion is for Allah; but if they desist, then there is no aggression except against the wrongdoers."* Same architecture. Verb of fighting, then *ḥattā*, then stopping condition, then explicit cessation when the condition is met.

Sit with what this says about the Quranic moral imagination. War is not a value. War is an *instrument*. Instruments have purposes, and when the purpose is achieved, the instrument is set down. To continue the instrument past its purpose is itself the *aggression* the verse warns against.

This has shattering implications for how Muslims are supposed to *think* about violence — not just authorize it.

A war that has no defined endpoint is, in this Quranic logic, not a Muslim war. It is something else, dressed up in Muslim language. The classical jurists understood this so deeply that they built an entire jurisprudence — *fiqh al-jihad* — around the question: *when must hostilities cease?* When the enemy offers terms (8:61). When the enemy asks for safe-conduct (9:6). When the enemy accepts dhimmī covenant (here, 9:29). When the enemy converts. There are *exit ramps*. The roads are *not* one-way.

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?** It changes what I will applaud. It changes whose rhetoric of permanent enemies — whether spoken in a Muslim accent or a Western one — I will refuse to absorb. It teaches me that the moral test of any war I am asked to support is: *does the person asking me to support it have a vision of when it ends?* If not, my Quran is telling me to be deeply suspicious.

### Theme Two — The Four Descriptions as Moral Diagnosis, Not Insult

Begin again with where you live. Imagine you are a doctor. A patient comes in. You do not say "I diagnose you because I hate you." You diagnose because the diagnosis is the *first step toward the right relationship* — what treatment, what dosage, what referral, what prognosis.

Now read the four descriptions in our ayah with that ear:

- *Lā yuʾminūna billāhi wa lā bi'l-yawm il-ākhir* — they do not believe in Allah, nor in the Last Day.
- *Wa lā yuḥarrimūna mā ḥarrama'llāhu wa rasūluh* — they do not treat as forbidden what Allah and His Messenger have forbidden.
- *Wa lā yadīnūna dīna'l-ḥaqq* — they do not adhere to the religion of truth.

Now, this is delicate. These descriptions are made of People of the Book — people who *have* prophetic scripture, who have *records* of revelation, who are *not* pagans without any access to divine speech.

So what does it mean to say of them that they "do not believe in Allah" or "the Last Day" or "do not forbid what Allah forbade"?

Classical exegetes understood this not as a denial that these communities had any concept of God or judgment, but as a diagnosis that **the specific belligerent group in question had functionally departed from what their own scriptures taught**. Al-Ṭabarī makes the second description concrete: to "not believe in the Last Day" is glossed as *lā yuṣaddiqūna bi-jannatin wa lā nār* — "they do not affirm a Paradise or a Fire" — that is, they have hollowed out the very accountability their prophets preached. And the fourth description, *wa lā yadīnūna dīna'l-ḥaqq*, al-Muyassar reads not as some abstract heresy but as a refusal to *abide by the rulings of the revealed law* — they "do not commit to the ḥukm" that God's messengers delivered. The way they conceived God, the way they treated the resurrection, the way they consumed what their own prophets forbade, the way they refused the *dīn al-ḥaqq* — the truth-religion — that successive prophets in their *own* lineage had brought: by all four measures, this group had broken with the very tradition they claimed.

This is not Islam saying "you are not Christian enough" or "you are not Jewish enough." This is Allah saying: *the group you are about to fight has detached itself even from what its own prophets gave it. They are not fighting you because they are loyal to Moses or Jesus. They are fighting you out of imperial ambition, against the residual moral protests of their own scriptures.*

Stay with this. What this transforms is the moral status of the *war itself*. The believers are not being asked to fight Christians *because they are Christians*. They are being asked to fight a specific imperial actor whose claim to a scriptural mandate is, by Allah's diagnosis, false. The four descriptions are *de-legitimating* the imperial claim, not the religion of revelation.

This is why, when actual scriptural Christians and Jews lived among Muslims and remained loyal to their own scriptures and lived peaceably, they were treated entirely differently — under Surah Mumtaḥanah 60:8: *"Allah does not forbid you, regarding those who have not fought you for your religion and have not driven you from your homes, that you should treat them justly and equitably; indeed, Allah loves those who act equitably."*

[PAUSE]

The diagnosis in 9:29 is *meant to identify a specific belligerent class* — those whose actions have proved that, whatever they call themselves, they have departed from the substance of revealed religion. The treatment varies; the diagnosis is the first step toward the right treatment.

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?** It teaches me to read description as diagnosis. When the Quran calls a group out, it is doing two things at once: it is naming a real spiritual condition, and it is also, simultaneously, *limiting the scope* of that naming to those who actually fit the description. I am not invited to extend the description further than Allah did. And I am not invited to treat the description as license to despise human beings whom Allah has already, elsewhere in His Book, commanded me to treat justly and equitably.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the ayah whole, and notice its structure.

It begins with a **verb** of action: *qātilū*.
Then comes the **diagnosis** — four descriptions of who, specifically, fits this ruling.
Then comes the **filter** — *min al-ladhīna ūtū al-kitāb* — restricting the diagnosis to a subset within a larger population.
Then comes the **terminus** — *ḥattā yuʿṭū'l-jizyah* — the political endpoint at which the verb of action must cease.
Then comes the **modality** — the manner in which that political settlement is to be enacted.

Five movements. A verb, a diagnosis, a filter, a terminus, a modality. This is not the architecture of a verse of unbounded violence. This is the architecture of a **constrained, conditioned, terminated, regulated** rule of engagement. Every part of the verse is a *limit* on what the verb at the start is allowed to do.

The verse, read whole, is *narrower* than it looks read in fragments. The fragments are how it is weaponized. The whole is how it must actually be understood.

### Bringing the Linguistic and the Thematic Together

The partitive *min* and the terminal *ḥattā* — those are the linguistic anchors.

The refusal of unbounded war and the four descriptions as diagnosis — those are the thematic depths.

These are the same insight from two directions. Allah, in this verse, is teaching the believers that war must be **named correctly** (the diagnosis), **scoped correctly** (the partitive), **terminated correctly** (the *ḥattā*), and **transformed correctly** (the political settlement that follows). To skip any of those is to misuse the verse. And the misuse is not a small misuse. It is the difference between a community of just order and a community of permanent grievance.

### Questions to Carry

1. When have I quoted a verse — or heard a verse quoted — without its *ḥattā*, without its *min*, without its specific historical hour? What did that quotation produce?

2. Where in my own moral life do I authorize wars — wars of words, wars of grudges, wars of family — that have no defined endpoint? What would it mean to insert a *ḥattā* into them?

3. The classical scholars resisted reading *ṣāghirūn* as license for petty humiliation, even though the word is uncomfortable. They read it structurally. Where in my own reading of difficult verses do I reach for the most punitive interpretation when the tradition reaches for the most structural one?

4. When have I treated a description — of a person, a community, a group — as license to act, instead of as a diagnosis that limits the scope of what I can say about whom?

5. Is there a part of me that secretly *prefers* the inflamed reading of this verse — because it lets me feel either besieged or triumphant? What would it cost me to put that preference down?

### One-Sentence Distillation

This ayah authorizes a bounded, conditioned, terminated military engagement against a specific belligerent class within the People of the Book — not a war on humanity — and the entire grammar of the verse is built to *prevent* the very weaponizations it has so often suffered.

### The Closing Invitation

If this is one of those ayahs you have been quietly avoiding — read it now, slowly, with the *min* and the *ḥattā* held firmly in front of you. Notice that Allah has written limits into His own command. Notice that the verse does *more* work to constrain violence than it does to authorize it. Notice that fourteen centuries of Muslims lived alongside Christian and Jewish neighbors *because of*, not in spite of, how the classical tradition read this verse.

And then ask yourself: if Allah Himself wrote limits into the very command, what does it mean to be His servant in the way I read His Book? Does my reading make His words *narrower* than He made them, or *wider* than He made them? Either error is a kind of trespass.

The straight reading — the *terminated* reading, the *filtered* reading, the *historically situated* reading — is not the apologetic reading. It is the reading the classical tradition actually built. It is the reading that produced the centuries of dhimma covenants, the protection of Najrān's churches, the safety of Cordoba's synagogues, the offices held by Christians and Jews in Abbasid Baghdad and Fatimid Cairo. That history is not a coincidence. It is what happens when this verse is read with its grammar intact.
