# The Nearest Enemy and the Constraint of Taqwa

**Surah At-Tawbah, ayah 123**

> [ayah:9:123] يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ قَـٰتِلُوا۟ ٱلَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم مِّنَ ٱلْكُفَّارِ وَلْيَجِدُوا۟ فِيكُمْ غِلْظَةً ۚ وَٱعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ مَعَ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ
>
> *Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū qātilū alladhīna yalūnakum mina al-kuffāri wal-yajidū fīkum ghilẓah, wa-'lamū anna Allāha ma'a al-muttaqīn.*
>
> **"O you who have believed — fight those of the disbelievers who are nearest to you, and let them find in you harshness. And know that Allah is with those of taqwa."**

---

## Introduction — A Surah's Parting Instruction

To understand why this ayah lands where it lands, we have to feel where in At-Tawbah we are.

This is one of the last ayahs in a long, heavy surah. The surah opened with **بَرَاءَةٌ** — a renunciation, an annulment of treaties that had been violated by the other party. It moved through the strange and difficult moment of the Year of the Deputations. It walked us through Tabuk — that exhausting summer expedition where the hypocrites finally exposed themselves by staying behind. It put the *munāfiqūn* on trial through ayah after ayah of unmasking. It told us about the mosque built to harm. And then, near its end, it took us to one of the most tender moments in the entire Quran: the three who were left behind. **حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا ضَاقَتۡ عَلَيۡهِمُ ٱلۡأَرۡضُ** — until the earth, in all its vastness, became too narrow for them. And then Allah turned to them so that they could turn back.

So when we arrive here — at ayah 123 — we are not arriving in a vacuum. We are arriving at the closing instructions of a community that has just been put through the fire. The wheat has been separated from the chaff. The hypocrites have been named. The penitent have been embraced. And now Allah hands the community a parting rule — a method for how to carry itself outward into a still-hostile world.

Read the ayah again with that in mind:

> *"O you who have believed — fight those of the disbelievers who are nearest to you, and let them find in you harshness. And know that Allah is with those of taqwa."*

Notice immediately what this ayah does. It gives a command. It gives a quality the command should produce. And then it closes with a declaration — "and know that Allah is with the muttaqīn." Classical commentators read that closing clause in two related registers. Al-Muyassar hears it as a *promise* — *"بتأييده ونصره,"* through His support and victory — divine companionship awarded to those who fight with taqwa. A complementary reading, held alongside this, takes the clause as simultaneously *conditional*: the harshness being commanded is not raw harshness, not unbounded severity, but severity held within taqwa — and the divine company tracks that condition. The Arabic *maʿa al-muttaqīn* does not force a choice between promise and condition; both registers live in the grammar together. We will return to this tension at the close of the linguistic journey. For now, mark what both readings agree on: the ayah does not end with the command — it ends with a moral anchor, and that anchor is the declaration that Allah walks alongside a particular kind of person.

This is what we are going to sit with for the next hour. Not the rhetoric of war. Not the polemic that the surah is sometimes reduced to. But the architecture of an ayah that sequences engagement, qualifies harshness, and binds divine company to a single moral condition.

Here is the question I want us to hold from the beginning:

**Why does Allah, when commanding the most physically difficult thing a community can be commanded to do, frame it through the language of nearness, density, and constraint — rather than through the language of zeal, victory, or domination?**

Stay with that question. We are going to earn the answer.

## A Word Before We Enter

A note about how we handle this kind of ayah at AyahGuide.

The classical jurists understood ayah 123 to be a prescriptive command in the context of formally declared, just-cause armed conflict — specifically against actively hostile combatants who had broken treaties or initiated aggression. They did not read it as a license for general civilian harm; the inherited rules of engagement (no killing of non-combatants, no destruction of crops or wells without necessity, honoring of treaties, no compulsion in religion as established in *lā ikrāha fī al-dīn*) all sit *on top of* this ayah, not in tension with it. The four classical madhāhib differ in detail on what "those nearest" requires by way of sequence and exception, and contemporary scholars differ further on how this ayah applies in a world of nation-states, international law, and standing armies.

We are not here to do fiqh. AyahGuide is not the place for rulings.

We are here to do *tadabbur* — to receive the ayah's structure, its language, its psychology, and the spiritual law it contains. That spiritual law applies to every believer in every era, regardless of whether the original prescriptive context applies to them. The ayah teaches us *how* a believing community is supposed to carry opposition, *with what disposition*, and *under what constraint*. Those teachings are universal.

So let's enter.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Strange Choice of *yalūnakum*

Look at the verb Allah uses to describe who is to be fought.

> **ٱلَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم**
>
> *alladhīna yalūnakum* — "those who are next to you, those who border you, those who are nearest to you."

It is a small word. Three letters of root: **و - ل - ي**. And here is the first thing that should arrest us: this is the same root from which the Quran builds some of its most beautiful and intimate vocabulary.

**ولي** (*walī*) — close ally, intimate protector. Allah is the *Walī* of those who believe.
**موالاة** (*muwālāh*) — alliance, loyalty.
**أولياء** (*awliyā'*) — the inner circle, the friends-of-Allah.

The root **و-ل-ي** in Arabic carries one core meaning: **immediate adjacency without anything between**. Two things that are *walī* to each other are touching. Nothing intervenes. The lexicographers of classical Arabic — the compilers of *Lisān al-ʿArab*, of *Mufradāt al-Rāghib*, of *Tāj al-ʿArūs* — agree on this: the root denotes proximity so close that there is no gap. From that core meaning, the language extends outward in two directions. In the direction of relationship, you get *walī*: the one so close to you that there is no distance between you. In the direction of position, you get *yalī*: the one whose body is next to yours, whose territory borders yours.

Now consider what Allah has just done.

When commanding the fight against hostile disbelievers, Allah could have used any of a dozen Arabic verbs for "near" or "close." There is **قرب** (*qaraba*), the most general word for nearness. There is **دنا** (*danā*), to draw close. There is **لاصق** (*lāṣaq*), to be physically attached. The Quran uses all of these elsewhere. But here, for *this* command, Allah reaches for **يَلُونَكُم** — *yalūnakum* — built on the same root as **walī**.

And let's sit with this. Because this is doing something.

The word **walī** in the Quran most often describes intimate spiritual closeness — Allah as your protector, the believers as one another's allies. It is the language of the inner circle. And here, that same root is being used to describe your geographic neighbor who happens to be hostile to you.

Why?

There is a teaching here about what *walāyah* — closeness, allegiance, adjacency — actually is. **Walāyah is not a feeling. Walāyah is a position.** The person whose territory borders yours is, in the literal sense of the Arabic word, in a *walī*-relationship to you. They are not your friend. They may be your enemy. But they are *next to you*. And being next to you means something. It creates obligation. It demands attention. It cannot be ignored the way a distant abstraction can be ignored.

This is the first stunning thing about the verb choice. By describing the hostile near-neighbor with a verb from the *walī*-root, Allah is signaling that **proximity itself is a form of relationship — even when the proximity is hostile**. You do not get to pretend they are not there. You do not get to fixate on the enemy three thousand miles away while ignoring the one whose border touches yours. The very fact of their adjacency makes them your concern.

Notice something else. The verb is in the **present-tense imperfect** form — *yalūnakum*, "they are continually being adjacent to you." Not *walū* (past tense, "they were adjacent") and not *sayalūn* (future, "they will be adjacent"). The form Allah chose denotes *ongoing, present, continuous adjacency*. As if to say: this is not a temporary geographic accident. This is the standing condition. As long as they are next to you, this command applies.

And here is the third layer. The grammatical object of *yalūna* — what they are adjacent to — is **you**, *kum*, second-person plural. They are adjacent to *the community of believers*. Allah does not say "those who are adjacent to the land" or "those who are adjacent to the territory." He says: **those who are adjacent to** ***you***. The community itself is the reference point. Wherever the believers are, those nearest to them are the ones to be addressed first.

This is a portable principle. It moves with the community. It is not tied to a particular geography. Wherever you are — *who is closest to you* is who you must address first.

Al-Ṭabarī, in his exegesis of this ayah, does not leave the principle abstract. He names who *yalūnakum* referred to at the moment of revelation: the Byzantines — *al-Rūm* — who then occupied Greater Syria, the territory geographically closest to Medina. Al-Ṭabarī is explicit in his paraphrase: *"ابدأوا بقتال الأقرب فالأقرب إليكم دارًا، دون الأبعد فالأبعد"* — begin with those nearest to you in dwelling, not the farthest. What arrests attention is that he does not identify some minor tribal raider as the "nearest enemy." He identifies the dominant Christian empire of the eastern Mediterranean. The principle of nearest-first was thus applied at scale — it was not a concession to ease but a discipline of sequence. You engage what is immediately before you, however formidable, before you reach for what is distant. Ibn Kathīr similarly traces how the Prophet ﷺ himself embodied this logic: beginning with the idolators of the Arabian Peninsula, consolidating that sphere, and only then turning outward toward the Roman and Persian frontiers. The *sīrah* becomes an illustration of the ayah's grammar — concentric, expanding, proximate first.

So what has the language of *yalūnakum* given us?

It has given us a principle of **concentric engagement**. The ayah does not say "fight all disbelievers." It does not say "fight the strongest disbelievers." It does not say "fight the most ideologically distant." It says: **fight those who are nearest to you**. Begin with the immediate. Address the adjacent. Do not skip over what is in front of you to chase what is far away.

And it does this using a verb from the same root as *walī* — making sure we hear, beneath the command, that even an adversary who borders you is in a kind of relationship with you. They are your *waliyy* in the geographical sense, even if not in the spiritual sense. You owe them — at minimum — your attention. You cannot pretend they are not there.

Let's hold that finding and turn to the next word.

### Element Two: The Density of *ghilẓah*

> **وَلۡيَجِدُواْ فِيكُمۡ غِلۡظَةً**
>
> *wal-yajidū fīkum ghilẓah* — "and let them find in you harshness."

The word **غِلْظَة** — *ghilẓah* — is one of those Arabic words that English cannot hold in a single word. Translators reach for "harshness," "firmness," "severity," "sternness," "toughness," "hardness." Each captures a slice. None captures the whole.

Let's open the root.

**غ - ل - ظ** in classical Arabic denotes **density, thickness, the quality of being substantial and not yielding easily**. The opposite of *ghilẓah* is *riqqah* — thinness, fineness, delicacy. *Ghilẓah* is what you feel when you grip something solid, dense, not hollow. The Arabs used the root to describe:

- A garment whose weave is so thick the wind cannot pass through it.
- A tree trunk whose wood is so dense the axe meets resistance.
- Soil that is heavy and packed, not loose and sandy.
- A bond — *mīthāq ghalīẓ* — so weighty in its commitment that it cannot be casually broken.

Yes — that exact word, *ghalīẓ*, is what the Quran uses to describe the marriage covenant in *Surah An-Nisā* (4:21): **مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا**, "a weighty pact." It is also used for the covenants Allah took from the Prophets in *Surah Al-Aḥzāb* (33:7). *Ghilẓah* is the quality of something that holds — that does not give way under pressure.

Now hear the command again with that meaning loaded:

> *"And let them find in you* ***density***. *Let them find in you* ***thickness***. *Let them find in you a quality of being unmoved when pressed."*

But here we must pause at the boundary of etymology and usage — because classical tafsir corrects any temptation to rest at "density" alone. Al-Muyassar, in glossing this ayah, does not say *ghilẓah* and move on. It pairs the word explicitly: *"غِلْظة وشدة"* — *ghilẓah* **and** *shiddah* — harshness and severity together. The pairing of *shiddah* is not ornamental. *Shiddah* means hardness, force, difficulty — a quality that bears down on what encounters it. The classical tafsir tradition is telling us that while the root of the word describes density, the word as deployed in this context carries a harder edge than "density" or "firmness" alone conveys. Standard translations of the Quran are consistent on this: the word here is harshness, toughness, a quality the adversary encounters as genuinely formidable. The etymological *structure* of the word is density; the classical *weight* of the word — in tafsir usage — is severity.

This is not — and I want to be precise here — this is not the language of cruelty. The Arabic word for cruelty is **قسوة** (*qaswah*), and *qaswah* is built on a root that means *hardness of heart*. The Quran condemns *qaswah* repeatedly. **ثُمَّ قَسَتۡ قُلُوبُكُم** — "then your hearts hardened" (2:74) — that is *qaswah*, and that is condemnation.

*Ghilẓah* is different. *Ghilẓah* is not hardness of heart; it is **density of presence and severity of bearing**. It is not aggression; it is **the quality of not yielding under pressure — a resistance that is real, solid, and formidable**. A wall has *ghilẓah*. A trustworthy promise has *ghilẓah*. A trained muscle has *ghilẓah*. *Ghilẓah* is what the enemy of a community *encounters* when they try to push it around — it is not what the community *exports* indiscriminately. The word describes a constitutional quality, not a disposition of hostility.

And notice the grammatical construction. Allah does not say *aghliẓū 'alayhim* — "be harsh upon them." He says **وَلۡيَجِدُواْ فِيكُمۡ غِلۡظَة** — *"let them find in you harshness."*

Three things to mark in that construction.

**First**, the verb is *yajidū* — "let them find." The action is one of *encountering* the quality, not of the believers *unleashing* it. The *ghilẓah* is something they walk into when they push. It is not something the community projects outward indiscriminately. The aggressor *finds* it. The peaceful neighbor does not.

**Second**, the preposition is **فِيكُمۡ** — *fīkum*, "*in* you." Not *minkum* ("from you") or *'alaykum* ("upon you"). The *ghilẓah* lives *inside* the community. It is a constitutional quality, an interior solidity. The aggressor encounters it because the community *contains* it — not because the community is firing it outward.
