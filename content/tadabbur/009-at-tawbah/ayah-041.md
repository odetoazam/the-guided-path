# Light and Heavy: The Merism That Refuses Every Excuse

## Surah At-Tawbah, Ayah 41

> ٱنفِرُوا۟ خِفَافًۭا وَثِقَالًۭا وَجَـٰهِدُوا۟ بِأَمْوَٰلِكُمْ وَأَنفُسِكُمْ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌۭ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

> *Infirū khifāfan wa thiqālan wa jāhidū bi-amwālikum wa anfusikum fī sabīli'llāh. Dhālikum khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūn.*

> "Go forth — light and heavy — and strive with your wealth and your selves in the path of Allah. That is better for you, if only you knew."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

Picture a community in the ninth year after the Hijra. The summer is brutal. The kind of heat that makes the air shimmer above the date palms. The crops are ripening. Harvest is days away — the year's food, the year's income, the year's security, all hanging on those bunches that need to be cut down and stored before they spoil.

And in this moment, the Prophet ﷺ stands and announces that everyone — every able person — is to march north. Not a short march. Not a defensive skirmish near Madinah. He is calling them to Tabuk, on the edge of the Byzantine frontier, weeks of travel through scorching desert, against an empire whose war-machine had been the most feared on earth for centuries.

Stay with that for a moment. Try to feel what was being asked.

The harvest you've been working all year for? Leave it.
The shade of your home in the worst heat of the year? Leave it.
The Roman legions waiting somewhere on the horizon? March toward them.

This was among the most difficult calls to mobilization in the entire Sirah. *Ghazwat al-ʿUsra* — they would later call it. The expedition of hardship. The campaign of difficulty. The verses of At-Tawbah are saturated with this moment. Page after page, the surah is examining one question: **who marched, and who hung back?**

Some marched. They are remembered.
Some hung back and made excuses. They are also remembered — in passages that read like courtroom transcripts, with each excuse named and dismissed in Allah's own voice.

And in the middle of this long examination, in the heart of this sacred reckoning over who showed up and who didn't, comes this ayah.

### The Hook

Look at this verse and notice something unusual. Allah does not say *"Go forth — those who can."* He does not say *"Go forth, except for the elderly and the sick and the indebted."* He does not list categories of who is excused.

Instead, He says something stranger. Something that, when you sit with it, becomes one of the most carefully engineered phrases in the entire Quran.

He names **two opposite states** — and commands the going forth in *both* of them.

*Light. And heavy.*

Two words. Two adjectives. And in those two words is hidden a divine refusal of every excuse a human being has ever made or ever will make. Not because Allah lists the excuses and rejects them one by one. Something far more elegant.

He uses a structure that *encompasses every excuse before it can be spoken.*

Let's see how.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Merism — *Khifāfan wa Thiqālan*

#### Teaching the structure first

Before we open Allah's words, let's understand a structure that exists in every human language — including yours.

When I say to you: **"I love you, in good times and bad."**

What have I done? I have not given an exhaustive list. I have not named every possible scenario. I haven't said: *"I love you when you're employed, and when you're unemployed; when you're well, and when you're sick; when we agree, and when we don't; when you're gentle, and when you're frustrated."*

I named two opposite poles — *good and bad* — and by doing so, I covered every state in between, and every state outside of them too. The pair becomes a totality. The two ends, named together, gather everything.

This is called a **merism**. It is one of the most ancient and powerful structures in human speech. It works by naming the extremes and letting them imply the whole.

*"Search high and low"* — every direction.
*"Heaven and earth"* — all of creation.
*"Day and night"* — every hour.
*"From dust to dust"* — the entire span of a life.

The merism doesn't list. It encompasses. It says *I will not give you the loophole of an unmentioned middle. I have closed the door from both ends.*

Hold that in your mind. Now let us return to the ayah.

#### The pair Allah chose

Allah did not say: *"Go forth, young and old."*
He did not say: *"Go forth, mounted and on foot."*
He did not say: *"Go forth, healthy and sick."*

He could have used any of those merisms. Each would have worked. Each would have closed off one set of excuses. But each would have left other excuses standing.

What if you were young AND sick? *"Mounted and on foot"* doesn't close that door.
What if you were old AND healthy? *"Young and old"* lets you slip through.
What if you were neither well-equipped nor poorly-equipped, but somewhere in the middle? Each of these specific merisms leaves cracks.

So Allah chose a pair so abstract, so encompassing, that **no condition of any human being could fall outside it.**

**خِفَافًا** — *khifāfan*. The accusative plural of *khafīf*, meaning **light**. From the root *kh-f-f*, which carries the meaning of being unweighted, easy to lift, easy to move, unburdened, swift.

**ثِقَالًا** — *thiqālan*. The accusative plural of *thaqīl*, meaning **heavy**. From the root *th-q-l*, which carries the meaning of being weighted, slow to move, encumbered, burdened, freighted.

These are not categories. They are *states of being.*

Notice something. Listen to the phonetics for a moment. *Khifāf* — the *kh* and the *f* are airy sounds, sounds your mouth makes with breath barely meeting resistance. The word itself feels light. *Thiqāl* — the *th* and the heavy *q* are stop-sounds, sounds where the airflow is interrupted, weighted, halted. The word itself feels heavy.

The Arabic is sonically performing what it means. **Even before you parse the meaning, your mouth tells you what these words are.**

#### What does the pair actually cover?

The classical commentators differed over precisely what *light and heavy* meant — and that very disagreement is illuminating. Al-Ṭabarī records the *ikhtilāf* openly: some of the early authorities read the pair concretely as **the young and the old** — al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī glossed it *shuyūkhan wa shubbānan*, old men and young men, youth being light on its feet and age heavy with years. Others widened it to the eager and the reluctant, the well-resourced and the hard-pressed. Al-Muyassar gathers the readings into a single sweep: *go forth, young and old, in hardship and in ease, in whatever state you are.* The commentators were not so much contradicting one another as circling a word built to hold all of their readings at once. List them together and the abstraction reasserts itself:

- **Young and old** — youth is light on its feet; age is heavy with years. (al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, in al-Ṭabarī)
- **Hardship and ease** — *al-ʿusr wa al-yusr*: when resources are strained and when they flow freely (al-Muyassar).
- **Healthy and sick** — health is light in the body; illness is heavy.
- **Eager and reluctant** — eagerness is light in the heart; reluctance drags.
- **Wealthy and poor** — abundance can travel light; poverty is encumbered with anxiety.
- **Single and family-burdened** — alone is light; with dependents is heavy.
- **Well-equipped and poorly-equipped** — armor and supplies make some heavy with readiness, others heavy with the weight of insufficient gear.
- **Free of cares and full of them** — some hearts move easily; others drag every step.

Each of these is a true reading. None of them exhausts the meaning. That the scholars reached for so many different glosses is itself the proof: the pair is wide enough that no single condition can claim it. And notice the shape of al-Muyassar's own paraphrase — after listing the concrete pairs (*young and old, in hardship and ease*) it widens to *ʿalā ayy ḥālin kuntum*: **in whatever state you are.** Even the mufassir, having offered his specifics, returns to the totality. The verse keeps refusing to be narrowed.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. Because this is what's stunning.

If you are *young and healthy and eager and unburdened and well-equipped* — Allah named you. *Khifāfan.* Light. Go.

If you are *old and sick and reluctant and burdened and poorly-equipped* — Allah named you. *Thiqālan.* Heavy. Go.

And if you are anywhere in between, in any combination — you are still somewhere on the spectrum that runs from *khifāf* to *thiqāl*. You are still inside the pair. **The merism leaves no gap.**

This is what the structure does. By naming the two poles, Allah preemptively named **every condition you might claim**. He has made it impossible for you to invent a state that falls outside the command.

And this was not an abstract worry. Ibn Kathīr preserves a report that some people in Madinah had begun telling themselves *they would incur no sin if they lagged behind the army — because they were ill, or old, or otherwise excused.* It was into the teeth of that exact reasoning that this verse came down. Ibn Kathīr even records, on the authority of Abū al-Ḍuḥā (Muslim ibn Ṣubayḥ), that *"March forth, light and heavy"* was **the first portion of Sūrat Barāʾah to be revealed** — the opening salvo of the entire surah's reckoning over who showed up. The merism, then, is not a poet's flourish. It is a door deliberately shut on a real excuse that real people were, in that very season, preparing to walk through. Ibn Kathīr titles the passage with the principle it establishes: *jihad is required in all conditions* — the verse is read by the tradition not merely as a command for one campaign but as a standing rule that the obligation does not wait for the comfortable state.

And yet — and this matters for reading the verse honestly — the tradition never took this universality to crush the genuinely incapable. The same revelation that commands the able to march, *khifāfan wa thiqālan*, sets apart in a sister verse *ghayr ulī al-ḍarar* — "except those who have a disabling harm" (4:95) — when it distinguishes the strivers from those who stay behind. Al-Muyassar, glossing that verse, is careful to name those exempted *aṣḥāb al-aʿdhār*, the people of valid excuse, and to record that Allah has promised them the very same Garden as the fighters, *for what they gave and sacrificed in the path of truth*. So the door this merism slams shut is not the door of the truly unable; it is the door of the *able* who manufacture an incapacity they do not have — the precise people Ibn Kathīr describes, telling themselves they would "incur no sin." The pair refuses the *invented* excuse. The real one it honors.

#### The order matters: light first, heavy second

Notice which one Allah names first.

He could have said *thiqālan wa khifāfan* — heavy and light. The Arabic permits it. The meaning would be the same in encompassment.

But He says *khifāfan wa thiqālan* — **light first**.

Why?

Here is what this invites us to consider. The natural human pattern, when called to something demanding, is to **wait for the light state**. *I'll go when I'm rested. I'll do this when the kids are older. I'll begin when my finances stabilize. I'll commit when my health returns. I'll start when work is less busy. I'll be there when the heat dies down.*

We treat *khifāf* — the state of being unburdened — as the **prerequisite** for response. We tell ourselves that lightness is the precondition of going forth.

So Allah names it first — **and then immediately follows it with its opposite**, joined by *wa* (and), not *aw* (or). Not *light or heavy*. **Light AND heavy.**

The conjunction *wa* refuses the choice. It refuses to let *khifāf* be a precondition. It says: *yes, when light. AND yes, when heavy. The lightness was not the gate. There is no gate.*

Allah names the state we want to wait for — and then closes the waiting room by adding the state we'd otherwise use as our reason not to come.

The order is a refusal of the human bargain.

[PAUSE]

This is one word — *khifāfan*. One word — *thiqālan*. Joined by one letter — *wa*. And in this tiny construction, Allah has done what no list of exceptions could do: He has refused every excuse that has ever been or ever will be made, by naming the two ends of the spectrum that contains them all.

### Element Two: *Jāhidū* — Form III, the Sustained Struggle

#### Teaching the form first

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns called *forms* — there are ten of them. The base form, **Form I**, is the simple action. *Kataba* — he wrote. *Naṣara* — he helped. *Ḍaraba* — he struck.

But Arabic also has **Form III**, which has a very specific texture. Form III is built by adding a long *ā* after the first root letter. *Kāṭaba* — to correspond with (sustained writing back and forth, between two parties). *Nāṣara* — to support over time, in mutual aid. *Ḍāraba* — to strike each other, to engage in mutual blows.

What does Form III do? It takes the base meaning and adds **two textures**: *mutuality* (the action goes both ways) and *sustained engagement* (the action continues over time, not in a single moment).

A Form I verb is a single act.
A Form III verb is a relationship over time — between an actor and what they are acting upon.

Hold that in your mind. Now let's open Allah's word.

#### The verb in the ayah

After Allah commands *infirū* — go forth — He says **وَجَاهِدُوا** — *wa jāhidū*. **And strive.**

*Jāhidū* is from the root *j-h-d*, which means to exert effort, to struggle, to strain to capacity. The base form, *jahada*, would mean simply *he struggled*. A single act. An effort made.

But Allah does not use Form I. He uses Form III. **Jāhidū**.

This is not *make an effort*. This is *engage in sustained mutual struggle*. The struggle is not a moment. It is a state. It continues. It is a relationship that runs across time — between the believer and everything in the world that pushes back against the path. And because Form III carries that texture of *mutuality*, the verb already concedes that the path will resist you: there is something on the other side of the exertion, straining back. The going-forth of *infirū* is the first step; *jāhidū* is what the rest of the road asks.

And notice what Allah immediately attaches the striving to: **بِأَمْوَٰلِكُمْ وَأَنفُسِكُمْ** — *with your wealth and your selves.* Al-Muyassar reads the pairing plainly: *spend your wealth in the path of Allah, and fight with your own hands to raise the word of Allah* — *li-iʿlāʾi kalimati'llāh.* The striving is costed. It is not a feeling in the heart that the verse asks for; it is the two most concrete things a person owns — what is in their hands and what is in their chest — laid down on the road. And here too the structure refuses the bargain: not *wealth OR selves* for those who can spare only one, but *wealth AND selves*, the whole person mobilized, just as *khifāfan wa thiqālan* mobilized the whole spectrum of states.
