# The Man Who Did His Math

### Surah Al-Humazah, Ayah 3

> **يَحْسَبُ أَنَّ مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ**
>
> *Yaḥsabu anna mālahu akhladah.*
>
> **"He thinks that his wealth has made him immortal."**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

I want you to picture a man. Not a cartoon villain. A man you might actually know.

He is successful. By every measure the world hands out, he has won. And the surah just before this ayah has told us two things about him. In ayah 1, that he is a **humaza, lumaza** — someone who tears people apart with his tongue, who mocks behind their backs and to their faces, who has made cutting others down into a kind of sport. And in ayah 2, that he is a man who **gathered wealth and counted it** — *jamaʿa mālan wa ʿaddadah* — counting it over and over, the way some people scroll a banking app at red lights, not because the number changed, but because looking at it does something to them.

So we have a portrait. A man with money and a mean streak. And here is the thing the original listeners in Makkah would have felt immediately, the thing we have to work to feel: **these two traits are not separate.** The mockery and the money are connected. The man who feels secure enough to belittle everyone around him feels that security from *somewhere*. And the Quran is about to reach in and show us the foundation underneath both — the load-bearing wall in this man's entire psychology.

[PAUSE]

Makkah at the time of this revelation was a city being reorganized around wealth. The old tribal bonds — the ones that said *you protect the orphan because she is one of us* — were being quietly replaced by a new logic: you matter as much as you own. Trade had made certain families enormously rich, and that wealth had started to feel like more than wealth. It had started to feel like **protection.** Like standing. Like a kind of permanence.

And into that world comes this ayah. Four words. One of the shortest verses in the Quran. And it does not attack the man's behavior. It does not list his sins. It goes underneath all of that, to the single sentence he has never once said out loud — the bet he has placed his whole life on without ever admitting he placed it.

Here is the hook. The verb at the center of this ayah, the word for what this man is *doing* in his head — it comes from a root that also means **to calculate. To do accounting. To reckon a sum.** This is a man who has spent his life counting. And the Quran is about to tell us that the one calculation he was sure of is the one he got catastrophically wrong.

Stay with me. Because the miscalculation in this ayah is not just his. It might be ours.

## PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yaḥsabu* — The Verb That Indicts the Accountant

Let me teach you something simple first, before we touch the ayah.

In Arabic, many words grow from a three-letter root, and that root carries a core idea that branches into related meanings. Think of a root like a seed and the words as different fruits from the same tree — different, but tasting of the same thing.

The root here is **Ḥā–Sīn–Bā (ح-س-ب).** And this root has two great branches.

The first branch means **to suppose, to think, to assume** — to hold something in your mind as probably true. That is the meaning operating in our ayah: *yaḥsabu*, "he thinks," "he supposes."

The second branch of the very same root means **to count, to calculate, to do the accounting.** From this branch comes the word **ḥisāb** — a reckoning, a tally, a final account. It is the word the Quran uses for **the Day of Judgment** itself: *Yawm al-Ḥisāb*, the Day of Reckoning. The day the books are opened and the sum is finally settled.

[PAUSE]

Now look again. Look at what Allah has done by selecting *this* verb for *this* man.

The man in ayah 2 was *counting* — *ʿaddadah*. He is, in his own mind, a man of numbers. Precise. Careful. A man who knows exactly what he has down to the last coin. He audits everything.

And the Quran describes his great belief — his belief that money has made him deathless — with a verb from the root of **calculation.** *Yaḥsabu.* As if to say: here is your great sum. Here is the one calculation you were most confident about. **And it is wrong.**

Notice something. The Arabic does not say *he knows* his wealth made him immortal. It does not say *he is certain.* It says *yaḥsabu* — he **supposes.** He reckons. It is a guess wearing the costume of a fact. This man, who would never let a single dirham go unaccounted for, has built his entire life on an assumption he never once audited. He checked his bank balance ten thousand times. He never checked *this.*

Here is what's remarkable. The same root that names his delusion — *yaḥsabu* — names the day that will undo it — *al-ḥisāb*. The accountant who never saw that he himself would be **accounted for.** He thought he was the one holding the ledger. He never understood he was an entry in someone else's.

[PAUSE — let this land]

And there is a tenderness hidden in the harshness here, if you look for it. The Quran does not call this man a liar. A liar knows the truth and speaks against it. This man *yaḥsabu* — he genuinely **believes** his own math. He has fooled himself first. He is not evil so much as he is **mistaken** — mistaken in a way that feels, from the inside, exactly like being right. That is the most dangerous kind of wrong there is. The wrong that feels like certainty.

What this single word reveals: **the most precise man in the city never ran the only number that mattered.**

### Element Two: *Akhladahu* — Immortality in the Past Tense

Now the second word. And this one is strange in a way that, once you see it, you cannot unsee.

The root is **Khā–Lām–Dāl (خ-ل-د).** Its core meaning is **to remain, to endure, to last without end.** From it comes **khuld** — eternity, perpetuity. This is not an ordinary word for "a long time." This is the word the Quran reserves for the deepest permanences in all of existence: **Jannat al-Khuld**, the Garden of Eternity. The *khālidūn* — those who abide forever, whether in mercy or in fire. *Khuld* is the vocabulary of the afterlife. It is the word for what only Allah can grant and only eternity can hold.

And this man has attached that word — the word for *forever* — to **his money.**

The form of the verb here, *akhlada*, is what grammarians call **Form IV.** Without getting technical, Form IV often takes a state and makes someone *cause* it, *bring it about*, *render it so.* So *khalada* (to endure) becomes *akhlada* — **to make endure, to render eternal, to immortalize.** The man believes his wealth has *akhladahu* — has rendered *him* eternal. Has performed on him the one act that belongs to God alone.

[PAUSE]

But here is the thing I cannot get past. Listen to the **tense.**

*Akhladahu.* This is **past tense.** Not "my wealth *will* make me immortal." Not "my wealth *is making* me immortal." **"My wealth *has already made* me immortal."** Done. Completed. Accomplished fact.

Sit with how insane that is — and how familiar.

Immortality is, by definition, a thing that can only be *proven* at the end. You cannot know you are deathless until death has failed to take you, and death does not fail. So the only honest grammar for a claim of immortality is the future, and a desperate, uncertain future at that. But this man does not speak in the uncertain future. In his heart he has filed it under **already done.** The bet is not a bet anymore. In his mind it has paid out. He walks around a man who, as far as he is concerned, **has already won the argument with the grave.**

The classical commentators caught this. Al-Qurtubi notes the reading that this past-tense verb carries a *future* meaning — *māḍin bi-maʿnā al-mustaqbal*, a past form pointing at a thing not yet come. And he gives the most chilling everyday example of how Arabs used this construction. They would say of a man who had done something ruinous: *"By God, so-and-so has perished and entered the Fire"* — using the **past tense** for a man still walking around alive. Why past tense? Because the outcome is **so certain** that to speak of it as future would be to pretend there is still doubt. It is *as good as done.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the terrible symmetry?

He uses the past tense for his immortality because in his delusion it feels **as good as done.**

And the Arabs used the past tense for the doomed man because his ruin was **as good as done.**

Same grammar. Opposite truths. He has filed *eternal life* under *already accomplished* — and the reality the surah is racing toward is that something else entirely has been *already accomplished* about him. Read four words further into the surah — *kallā, la-yunbadhanna fi'l-ḥuṭamah* — "No! He will surely be hurled into the Crusher." The certainty he attached to his immortality, Allah peels off and attaches to his ruin.

What this word reveals: **he conjugated his immortality in the past tense, and reality conjugated his destruction in the same.**

### Element Three: The Word He Never Says — Reading the Absence

We have looked at two words that are present. Now I want to do something the Quran constantly invites and we constantly forget: I want to look at a word that is **absent.**

Here is the absence. **The man never speaks.**

This entire ayah is about the most important belief in his life — and it is reported to us in the third person, from the outside, as something happening *inside his head*. *Yaḥsabu* — "he thinks." Not *qāla* — "he said." Not a quotation. Not a creed he stood and declared. The Quran does not give us a single word from his own mouth on the subject.

Why does that matter? Teach yourself the principle: in the Quran, when a belief is quoted in someone's own speech, it is usually a belief they have *examined enough to defend.* The disbelievers who argue against resurrection — Allah quotes them: *"Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?"* They at least *made the argument.* They put it into words and stood behind it.

This man never does. His most foundational belief — that money has bought him out of death — is something he has **never once said out loud, even to himself.** It lives entirely below the surface, in the *yaḥsabu*, in the silent arithmetic running in the back of the mind. It was never a decision. It was an **assumption.** It seeped in. He absorbed it the way you absorb the temperature of a room — without ever choosing it, until one day it is simply the air you breathe.

[PAUSE]

This is why the ayah is so frightening, and why it is not really about a rich villain at all.

A belief you *declared* you can *examine.* You can hold it up, turn it over, argue with it, repent of it. But a belief you never put into words — a belief that lives only as a *feeling of security*, a *reflex of the heart* — that one is almost impossible to catch. You cannot repent of a sentence you have never admitted you are saying.

The man's deepest error was not that he believed something false. It was that he believed it **without ever knowing he believed it.** And that is the most human thing in this entire surah. Because the silent assumption — the one running in the dark, the one we have never put into words — is exactly where each of us is most exposed.

What this absence reveals: **the most dangerous beliefs are the ones we never said out loud — including the ones we are running right now.**

### Bridge

So look at what these four words have shown us. A verb of *calculation* used to name a man's *miscalculation*. A verb of *eternity* placed, in the *past tense*, onto a pile of *coins*. And a belief so deep the man never even *said* it. The grammar itself is a portrait of self-deception — precise, certain, and silent.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about us. About reality itself. Because that silent calculation in the back of his mind — I want to show you that it is running in the back of ours too, just wearing different clothes.

## PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Immortality Project

Let me start where you actually live, far from any pile of gold.

A man works late his whole life. He misses dinners, recitals, ordinary Tuesday evenings, to build something — a company, a portfolio, a name. Ask him why and he will say *security.* He wants to be secure. He wants his family secure. And that is good and real and Islam honors it.

But notice what "secure" quietly expands to mean. Secure against what? Against a bad month, yes. Against illness, against old age. And then — without anyone deciding it — secure against *everything.* Against vulnerability itself. Against the one thing no portfolio has ever held back from a single human being who ever lived.

A psychologist named Ernest Becker spent a whole career on one idea, and he was not a Muslim, but he saw something the Quran named fourteen centuries before him. He said: most of human achievement is an **immortality project.** Underneath our ambition is a terror of death so large we cannot look at it directly, so we build things that will outlast us and we pour ourselves into them, hoping that if the *thing* does not die, some part of *us* will not either. The empire. The fortune. The name carved over the door.

[PAUSE]

That is *yaḥsabu anna mālahu akhladah* in modern dress. The man in the ayah is not exotic. He is every one of us who has ever felt, just for a moment, that a large enough number on a screen would make us *safe* in a way that goes deeper than money could ever actually reach. He took a feeling we all have and let it harden into a belief he never examined. *My wealth has made me immortal.*

And here is the cruelty the Quran is exposing, the part Becker also saw: **it does not work, and it cannot work, and the harder you push it the emptier it gets.** This is why the man in ayah 2 *counts and recounts.* Why does he keep counting? Because counting is the ritual of a faith that is not delivering. If the money had truly made him secure, he could look at it once and rest. He cannot rest. He counts again. And again. The recounting is not pride — it is **anxiety wearing the mask of pride.** It is a man going back to the well again and again because the water keeps not satisfying the thirst, never letting himself realize *this is the wrong well.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It asks you to find your *own* version of the silent calculation. Not the rich man's — yours. For one person it really is money. For another it is *status* — if enough people respect me, I am safe. For another it is *health* — if I optimize my body perfectly, I can hold death at arm's length. For another it is even *children* — if I live on through them, I have not really died. Every one of these is a real and often beautiful thing that has been quietly asked to do a job it can never do: to make you *khālid.* To make you last forever.

The practical application is almost startlingly simple. **Say it out loud.** Take the silent sentence — *"I will be okay if only I have enough ____"* — and finally put it into words, because the moment it becomes a sentence, you can finally see it is not true. The thing in that blank is a gift from Allah, good and worth pursuing in its place. It was just never going to be a god. It was never going to make you *akhlad.*

### Theme Two: The Reckoning That Reckons the Reckoner

Now I want to take you back to that root — Ḥā-Sīn-Bā — because there is a mercy hidden inside the warning, and I do not want you to leave without it.

Start, again, somewhere human. Have you ever known you were avoiding a number? The credit card statement you do not open. The scale you step around. The medical result sitting unread in the inbox. There is a very specific kind of dread in *the sum you will not face* — because as long as you do not look, you get to keep believing it might be fine.

That is this man's entire spiritual life. He is a master at facing one ledger — his wealth — precisely so he never has to face the other. The counting in ayah 2 is not just anxiety. It is **avoidance.** As long as he is buried in *his* numbers, he never has to think about the day when *he* becomes the number. He audits his coins so he never has to audit his soul.

[PAUSE]

And here is the turn. The Quran tells him — tells us — that there is a *Ḥisāb* coming. A reckoning. And to the man in this ayah, *Yawm al-Ḥisāb* sounds like nothing but a threat. The day his bluff is called. The day the past-tense immortality he filed away is exposed as the worst calculation a man ever made.

But listen to what the reckoning actually *is*, underneath the fear of it. A reckoning is the universe's promise that **the sum matters.** That your life is not noise. That the unkind word — the *humaza, lumaza*, the mockery the surah opened with — is not free, and neither is the kind one. That nothing you do drops into a void. *Yawm al-Ḥisāb* means there is an accounting *because there is a meaning.* The man's tragedy is that he heard "you will be counted" and felt only terror, when buried inside it was the most dignifying news a human being can receive: **you are being taken seriously. Down to the atom.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see how the two readings of the root finally meet?

He spent his life on *yaḥsabu* — supposing, assuming, running silent uncalculated bets and calling them certainties. And what awaits is *al-ḥisāb* — the real calculation, the one done by the One who actually knows the value of things. The whole movement of this ayah is from a **false math we do in the dark to a true accounting done in the light.** And the invitation — the entire invitation of the surah — is to stop being the man who assumes and start being the person who reckons honestly, *now*, while the books are still open and a single tear of repentance can still rewrite the bottom line.

The Prophet ﷺ pointed exactly here. As reported in the well-known tradition, he taught that the feet of a servant will not move on the Day of Judgment until he is asked about four things — among them **his wealth: how he earned it and how he spent it.** The very thing the man in our ayah thought was his *security* turns out to be the thing he will be *questioned about.* The shield becomes the subject of the exam.

So what changes tomorrow? **Do your own ḥisāb before it is done for you.** The early Muslims called this *muḥāsabat al-nafs* — taking your own soul to account. . Sit, once, deliberately, and reckon: not your bank balance — your *actual* balance. What did this day cost someone? What did it give them? Where is my wealth going, and what is it quietly promising me that only Allah can deliver? Run the calculation while running it is still a mercy and not yet a verdict. The man in the ayah was destroyed not because he was rich, but because he refused to be his own accountant — until the day the accounting was taken out of his hands.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two paths we walked are the same path.

The **linguistic discovery** was that a man of *calculation* (Ḥ-S-B) made the great *miscalculation* — assuming (*yaḥsabu*) rather than knowing, and filing his immortality (*akhlada*) under *already done* in a past tense he had no right to, in a belief he never even said aloud.

The **thematic discovery** was that this is the universal *immortality project* — the silent bet every human heart is tempted to place, that *enough of something* will make us *khālid* — and that the cure is the very thing the man fled: a real *ḥisāb*, an honest reckoning we do *now*, in the light, by our own hand.

They are one insight from two directions. **The man who will not account for himself has, in that refusal, already made the only calculation that will define him.** *Yaḥsabu* and *Ḥisāb* — the supposing and the reckoning — are the same root because they are the same choice, faced twice: once in the dark by guessing, and once in the light by truth. He chose the dark. The surah is begging us to choose the light while choosing is still possible.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the sentence I have never said out loud — *"I will finally be safe when I have enough ____"* — and what is in that blank?
2. What do I count and recount, return to and return to, that never quite delivers the peace I keep going back to it for?
3. If wealth is something I will be *questioned about* rather than *protected by*, does that change a single decision I am making this week?
4. Where in my life am I being the precise accountant of small things so that I never have to face the reckoning of the large ones?
5. If I did my own *ḥisāb* tonight — honestly, gently, before Allah — what is the first entry I already know is wrong?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The most careful counter in the city placed his whole life on a calculation he never once examined — that money could buy what only Allah can grant — and the word for his self-deception is the same word for the Day that will undo it.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to be afraid of this ayah. It is not pointing at a rich villain over there. It is offering you a mirror and a mercy in the same hand. The mercy is this: the *ḥisāb* the man dreaded is still, for you, in the future — which means it is still, for you, *open.* The books are not closed. The silent calculation can still be said out loud, examined, and corrected. You can still be the one who reckons honestly with himself, rather than the one reckoned with. That is not a threat. That is *time*, which is the most generous gift Allah gives the living.

So let the silent sentence become a spoken one tonight. Let the thing you have asked to make you immortal go back to being merely a blessing. And let the only One who grants *khuld* be the only One you ask for it.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You are *Al-Bāqī*, the Everlasting, and everything I own will pass through my hands and be gone. Save me from the silent bet I have never dared examine — the belief that anything besides You could make me safe. Let me do my own reckoning, gently and honestly, before the day my reckoning is done for me, and let me find, when the books are opened, that I faced the sum while there was still mercy in facing it. *Āmīn.*
