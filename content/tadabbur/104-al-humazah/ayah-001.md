# Woe to the One Who Has Become the Jab

## Introduction: A Verdict Before the Crime

Listen to it first in Arabic. Let it land before you understand it.

**وَيْلٌ لِّكُلِّ هُمَزَةٍ لُّمَزَةٍ**

*Waylun li-kulli humazatin lumazah.*

"Woe to every backbiter, every faultfinder."

Four words. That's the whole ayah. And I want you to notice something strange about it before we do anything else.

A verdict has been handed down — *woe*, ruin, a sentence of punishment — and we don't yet know what the crime is. Usually a case is built first. The evidence is laid out, the act described, and *then* the judgment falls. Here it's reversed. The gavel comes down in the very first word. **وَيْلٌ.** Woe. And only afterward are we told who it falls upon.

That reversal is not an accident. Allah wants the sentence to ring in your ears before you have time to distance yourself from the accused. Because the moment you hear *who* this is about, your mind will start its quiet work of self-exemption: *Oh, that's not me. That's the cruel ones, the obvious mockers, the bullies.* So He drops the woe on you first — and then, slowly, shows you the face of the one it lands on. And by the end, you may find that face is more familiar than you wanted it to be.

### Rebuild the world

Picture Makkah in those years. A small, mocked community of believers — poor ones, freed slaves, the socially invisible — walking through streets owned by men of enormous standing. And as they pass, something happens that is almost beneath description because it is so small. A wealthy man tilts his head. A wink passes between two notables. A muttered word as the Prophet ﷺ walks by. A gesture with the hand. A sound made in the throat.

Nothing you could take to court. Nothing you could point to and say *he struck me.* Just the steady, daily drip of being made small. Being made into a joke. Being prodded — not with a fist, but with a glance, a snort, a whisper that you were meant to half-hear.

The traditions name some of them — men like al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and Umayya ibn Khalaf are mentioned in the classical commentary as among those whose behavior this described. But notice that Allah did **not** name them in the ayah. He could have. Instead He left it open. And we'll see, by the end, that the openness is the entire point.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to hold onto as we begin. The Arabic of this ayah does something that the English translation simply cannot carry. Two of these four words **sound like the very thing they condemn.** And the *shape* of two of these words — their grammatical form — tells us that Allah is not condemning an action at all.

He is condemning a *person who has become an action.*

Stay with me, because once you hear it, you will never read this ayah the same way again. And you may never scroll through a comment section the same way again either.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Shape That Turns a Verb Into a Self

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah — a small thing about *word shapes* that will unlock everything.

In English, we sometimes turn an action into an identity by adding a suffix. You *help*, occasionally — that's an action. But if I call you a *helper*, I've made it part of who you are. And we have an even stronger move. There's a difference between "you joke sometimes" and calling someone "a *joker*" — and a difference again between "a joker" and "he's a total *clown.*" Each step, the behavior climbs deeper into the person. By the end it isn't something they *do*. It's something they *are.*

Arabic has a precise tool for exactly this. It's a word-shape — a pattern called **فُعَلَة** (*fuʿalah*). When you pour a root into this mold, it stops describing an occasional act and starts describing a *habitual character.* It means: this is not what he did once. This is what he is *made of.* This is his second nature, his reflex, the thing that happens when no one is making him do anything at all.

Think of the difference between a man who told a lie under pressure and a man you'd call *a liar.* The first is an event. The second is a diagnosis of character.

Now look at the two key words in our ayah:

**هُمَزَةٍ** — *humazah*
**لُّمَزَةٍ** — *lumazah*

Both of them are poured into that exact mold. **فُعَلَة.**

Do you feel what just happened? Allah did not say *woe to the one who backbit.* He did not even say *woe to the backbiter* in some neutral, occasional sense. He chose the form that means: **woe to the one whose entire character is the jab. The one for whom belittling others is not an act but an appetite. Not a slip, but a reflex.**

Let's sit with this. The classical lexicons note that this pattern carries *mubālagha* — intensity, habituality, exaggeration of the trait. It's the same family of forms that gives us a word for someone who doesn't just laugh, but is *forever* laughing *at* people; someone who doesn't just mock once, but is a fountain of mockery. The form itself is the verdict. Before Allah tells you what's wrong with this person, the *shape* of the word has already told you: **the disease has gone all the way in. It's not on their tongue anymore. It's in their bones.**

Here's why this matters so much for us. We have a defense mechanism, all of us, and it sounds like this: *"I'm not a bad person. I just made a comment."* And Allah, with a single word-shape, dismantles the comfort of that sentence. Because the question this ayah asks is not *did you commit the act?* The question is: **has the act become you?** Have you done it so often, so easily, with so little resistance, that it no longer feels like a choice? That's the moment a behavior crosses over and becomes a character. That's the moment the فُعَلَة form is describing.

[PAUSE]

What this single word-shape reveals: **Allah is not warning us off a sin. He is warning us about who we are quietly becoming, one effortless jab at a time.**

### Element Two: The Words That Sound Like the Wound

Now I want you to do something unusual. I want you to *listen* to the two words — not for their meaning, but for their sound.

**هَمْز.** **لَمْز.** *Hamz. Lamz.*

Say them. Feel where they happen in your mouth. There's a sharpness to them — a quick, biting closure. The *h* and the *l* leading into that buzzing *z* that cuts off hard. *Hamz. Lamz.* They almost *prick.* They are short, jabbing, stinging little sounds.

This is not a coincidence, and it's not a pretty literary decoration I'm adding. The Quran does this deliberately, and here it's doing something profound. Because look at what these roots *literally* mean underneath their religious meaning.

The root of **هَمْز** (*hamz*) — in the old language, before it ever meant backbiting — meant *to push, to prod, to poke, to shove.* A physical jab. You *hamaza* something when you pressed into it, drove into it, poked it. (It's the same root behind the little mark in Arabic writing, the *hamza* — a sharp glottal *push* in the throat.)

And the root of **لَمْز** (*lamz*) carried the sense of *jabbing with a gesture* — a wink of the eye, a curl of the lip, a movement of the hand or eyebrow that says *look at him* without a single word spoken.

Do you see what Allah has done?

He has taken two words that originally described **physical violence** — poking, shoving, jabbing — and used them for what we do with our tongues and our eyes. And He has chosen words whose very *sound* enacts the jab. The form pricks. The meaning pricks. The history of the word is a finger driven into someone's side.

Here's what's remarkable. We have built an entire civilization of comfort around the idea that **words and looks are not violence.** "It was just a joke." "I didn't *touch* anyone." "Sticks and stones." We draw a bright moral line between the fist and the whisper, and we live on the safe side of it.

And Allah, in His choice of these two roots, *erases that line.*

Because the root says: backbiting **is** a shove. Mockery **is** a poke. The wink across the room that turns a human being into a punchline — that **is** a jab to the body. The unseen reality these physical words are pointing toward is this: **contempt is violence that leaves no mark on the skin and a deep one on the soul.** The victim cannot show you the bruise. There is no bruise. That is precisely what makes it so easy, and so deadly, and so beloved of the *humazah lumazah* — because you can wound a person all day long and never be charged with a single blow.

Notice something. The person who is jabbed with a fist knows they've been attacked, and so do the witnesses. But the person who is jabbed with a glance, a tone, a "just kidding" — they often can't even prove to *themselves* that something happened. They walk away smaller and can't say why. That is a more cowardly violence, and the sound of these words — *hamz, lamz* — captures exactly its quick, deniable sting.

[PAUSE]

What these two roots reveal: **There is no such thing as "only words." Allah classified the jab of the tongue and the eye as an act of harm — and chose words that bite as you say them, so you could never again pretend the wound isn't real.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Word "Every," and the Door Left Open

Now look at one small word we skipped: **لِّكُلِّ** — *li-kulli* — "to **every**."

And look again at that opening word, **وَيْلٌ** — *waylun* — which sits there indefinite, with its tanwīn, deliberately *unbounded*. Not "the woe" — just *woe*, vast and unspecified, the kind of dread that has no edges.

Let me teach the concept quickly. In Arabic, as in English, you can address a sin in two completely different ways. You can name and shame: *woe to al-Walīd, woe to Umayya* — and the moment you do, every other person in the room exhales, because the spotlight has a name on it and the name isn't theirs. Or you can universalize: *woe to* ***every*** *one who does this* — and suddenly there is nowhere to stand. The spotlight has no name on it. Which means it has *your* name on it, and mine, and everyone's.

Allah chose the second. **لِّكُلِّ هُمَزَةٍ لُّمَزَةٍ.** *Every.*

This is the door I told you He left open at the beginning. The traditions tell us specific men prompted this revelation — but Allah refused to lock the verdict to them. He swung the door wide. *Every* backbiter. *Every* faultfinder. Across every century, every culture, every comment thread not yet invented.

Let's sit with the discomfort of this, because the discomfort is the mercy.

If this ayah were about *those people* — the obvious cruel ones, the historical villains — it would be a comfort. We'd nod along, agree that mockery is bad, and feel ourselves on the right side of it. But the word **كُلّ** reaches across fourteen centuries and taps *us* on the shoulder. It asks the question we do not want asked: *And you? In your group chat. In your subtweet. In the way you describe your coworker, your in-law, your "friend." In the laugh you share at someone's expense the moment they leave the room — are you* ***sometimes*** *the humazah lumazah?*

And here the two earlier discoveries come crashing back together. Remember the فُعَلَة form — it's about a sin that has become a *character.* And remember that the verdict is universal — *every* one of these. Put them side by side and you get the most searching question of all:

**Not "have I ever mocked someone?" — of course I have. But: "Have I done it so easily, so often, that I have quietly drifted from a person who slipped into a person who** ***is*** **the jab — and never once noticed the crossing?"**

[PAUSE]

What this word reveals: **By refusing to name names, Allah made sure no one could escape — and turned a verdict on the few into a mirror for us all.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these four words have built. The *shape* of the words tells us this is about character, not incident. The *sound and history* of the words tell us this is real violence, not "only words." And the word *every* tells us there is no safe distance from this — it is about us.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself. Because no one wakes up and *decides* to become a humazah lumazah. So how does it happen? What is actually going on inside a person who has become the jab?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Machine That Manufactures Contempt

Let me start where you live.

You are holding your phone. You're tired, maybe a little bored, maybe a little anxious about your own life. And your thumb finds the comment section. The dunk. The quote-tweet that exists only to humiliate. The video where a stranger is filmed at their worst so that millions can feel, for a few seconds, *better than them.* And something in you relaxes. Something in you feels — for just a moment — taller.

We have built, in our generation, the most efficient *humazah lumazah* machine in human history. A machine that pays you — in attention, in likes, in that little hit of superiority — every single time you jab. It has taken the wink across the room that this ayah condemned and given it a global audience and a reward system. The mockery that once cost you a guilty glance now earns you a following.

So here is the question this surah forces us to ask, and I want us to go slowly: **Why does belittling another person feel good?**

Sit inside the experience honestly. When you mock someone, what are you actually reaching for? It isn't really *them.* The person you're tearing down usually has nothing you want. So why do it?

Here's the first movement of the answer. **Contempt is a borrowed altitude.** When I cannot make myself taller, I can still feel taller by making you smaller. The distance between us grows either way. The mockery isn't really about you at all — it's a tool I'm using to manage *my own* sense of smallness. The jab points outward, but the wound it's treating is mine.

Now go deeper. *Why am I feeling small in the first place?* And this is where the surah's own structure answers us — because the very next ayahs describe a man **counting his wealth, imagining it has made him eternal.** The humazah lumazah of this surah is not a random nasty person. He is a man who has built his entire sense of worth on what he has *accumulated* — and a worth built on accumulation is a worth that can never rest. Someone always has more. So he must constantly re-establish his rank, and the cheapest way to climb is to push others down. **The mockery is the symptom. The disease is a soul that has located its value outside of itself — in status, in having-more-than — and so must endlessly defend a rank that nothing can secure.**

Do you feel the trap closing? The person who is *secure* in their worth before Allah has no need to belittle anyone. They have nothing to prove and no rank to defend. It is precisely the *insecure* heart — the one that has staked itself on being above others — that becomes a manufacturer of contempt. **The humazah is not strong. He is terrified. Every jab is a man grabbing at a ledge.**

And now the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing to fix it: **the very act he uses to feel bigger is the act that makes him smallest of all in the eyes of the only One whose measure is real.** He spends his life climbing a ladder that is, in the sight of Allah, driving him straight down into the deepest pit. The surah will literally end with that pit — *al-ḥuṭamah*, the crushing fire. The one who spent his life *crushing* others is named by the very fire that will *crush* him. The punishment is not arbitrary. It is the act, finally turned around to face its author.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is one concrete thing. The next time you feel the pull to belittle someone — in a chat, in your head, out loud at dinner — pause and ask the diagnostic question: ***What am I feeling small about right now?*** Because the urge to jab is almost never about the other person. It is a smoke alarm going off in your own soul. Treat it as information. The contempt rising in you is *telling you* where your own sense of worth has gone fragile. Don't act on it — *read* it. That single redirection — from "what's wrong with them" to "what's frightened in me" — is the beginning of being freed from this surah's verdict.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Sins That Leave No Fingerprints

Let me ask you to remember the last time you genuinely repented for something. What comes to mind?

For most of us, it's the big, *loud* sins. The ones with edges. The ones we can name. We rarely lie awake over a glance. We almost never repent for a tone. We don't keep a ledger of the eye-rolls, the "I'm just saying," the times we made someone the joke in a story to make ourselves the hero of it.

And this is the deepest danger the ayah is pointing at — deeper even than the cruelty itself. It is the *invisibility* of this sin **to the one committing it.**

Go back to the roots one more time. *Hamz* is a poke. *Lamz* is a wink. These are, by design, the most *deniable* acts a human being can perform. There is no weapon to recover, no blood, no broken thing. The humazah can stand before you — can stand before *himself* — and say with total sincerity: *"What did I do? I didn't touch anyone. It was a joke. You're too sensitive."* He has wounded people his entire life and kept his hands, in his own eyes, perfectly clean.

This is why I believe Allah opened the surah with the verdict *before* the description. **وَيْلٌ** first. Because this is the one sin the sinner will never see coming. The murderer knows what he is. The thief knows what he is. But the humazah lumazah has so thoroughly normalized his jabbing — it's "just his sense of humor," it's "just how he is," it's "just honesty" — that he will arrive on the Day of Judgment genuinely surprised. So Allah front-loads the warning. *Before you explain it away — woe.*

Let's go deeper into the mechanism, because it's beautiful and terrifying at once. Remember the فُعَلَة form: the sin that has become a *character.* How does a behavior become a character? **Through repetition so small it never triggers the alarm.** No single eye-roll feels like a sin. No single "did you SEE what she was wearing" feels like backbiting. Each one is *under the threshold* of conscience. But the form is telling us: do the under-the-threshold thing ten thousand times, and you don't have ten thousand small sins. You have **become a different kind of human being.** The drift was invisible because every individual step was invisible. And one day the word that describes you is no longer a verb you do but a noun you are.

Connect this to the universal struggle. This is how *most* of us will be undone — not by the dramatic sin we'd never commit, but by the tiny one we commit so constantly we stopped counting. The Prophet ﷺ warned us against exactly this category — the small sins we belittle, that pile silently until they destroy. The tongue, he taught, is what casts most people face-first into the Fire. Not the dramatic crimes. The *casual* ones. The ones that leave no fingerprints.

And here, finally, is the mercy hidden inside the severity. **An invisible sin, once made visible, is one of the easiest to abandon** — because it costs you almost nothing to stop. You don't have to return stolen wealth or repair a shattered thing. You simply have to *see it* — and then close your mouth, lower your eyes, and refuse the jab. The whole battle is the *seeing.* That is what this ayah is: Allah dragging an invisible sin into the light so that you finally have the chance to fight it.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the concrete practice. For one day — just one — keep a quiet count, not of others' faults, but of your *own* jabs. Every wink, every "just saying," every story where someone is the punchline, every silent eye-roll. Don't try to stop them yet. Just *count* them. Make the invisible visible. Most people are stunned by the number. And the stun is the cure beginning — because the فُعَلَة character can only be unmade the same way it was made: one noticed, refused jab at a time.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this reflection are really one truth approached from two sides.

The **linguistics** told us: the *form* makes this about character, not incident; the *sound and roots* make this real violence, not "only words"; the *word "every"* makes this about us, not them.

The **themes** told us: contempt is a borrowed altitude for an insecure soul, and its deadliest feature is that it leaves no fingerprints — so it becomes our character without our ever noticing the crossing.

These are the same insight from two directions. The grammar said *this sin becomes who you are* — and the psychology showed us *exactly how*: through ten thousand invisible, deniable, threshold-evading little jabs, each one a man grabbing at a ledge. The grammar said *woe to every one* — and the psychology showed us *why none of us is safe*: because the machine that manufactures contempt runs on the universal fuel of our own smallness.

### The Architecture of the Whole Surah

And now stand back and see what this single opening verse is the doorway into. The surah moves: the *jabber* (ayah 1) → the *accumulator counting his wealth* (ayahs 2–3) → the *crushing fire, al-ḥuṭamah* (ayahs 4 onward). The man who **jabbed and crushed** others is delivered to the fire that **crushes.** The diagnosis and the sentence rhyme. The whole surah is a single sentence handed down at the start — **وَيْلٌ** — and then slowly, mercilessly, justly explained.

### Questions to Carry

- When I feel the urge to belittle someone, what am I actually feeling small about in myself?
- Where have I let "it's just a joke" or "I'm just honest" become a license to wound without consequence?
- If I counted my jabs for a single day the way I count others' faults, what number would frighten me?
- Has any of this drifted, in me, from something I *do* into something I *am*?
- Whose worth have I quietly tried to lower so that my own would feel, for a moment, higher?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah pronounces ruin not on those who slip into mockery but on those who have *become* it — and by choosing words that bite as you speak them and a verdict that names no one, He made certain that none of us could read this and think it was about someone else.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to become silent. It is inviting you to become *secure* — so secure in your worth before Allah that you no longer need anyone beneath you to feel that you are someone. The person who has stopped climbing on others has not lost anything. They have finally put down a weight they were never meant to carry. Let that be the shift: not "I will bite my tongue," but "I will heal the smallness that made me want to bite at all."

### Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who see the jab that leaves no mark and hear the whisper meant to wound — purify our tongues, our glances, and the frightened smallness in our hearts that reaches for contempt. Make us secure enough in Your sight that we never again need anyone beneath us, and let us not arrive before You as people who became, without ever noticing, the very thing You warned us against. Āmīn.
