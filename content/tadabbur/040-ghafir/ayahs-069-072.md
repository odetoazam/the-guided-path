# The Twisted Rope and the Collar It Becomes

## INTRODUCTION

### The world you have to stand in first

Surah Ghafir has one word it cannot let go of.

Listen to the surah's fourth ayah — nearly its opening line: *none dispute the signs of Allah except those who disbelieve; so do not be deceived by their movement through the lands.* The word is **جِدَال** — *jidāl*, disputing — and it becomes the surah's pulse. It returns in ayah 5: every nation *disputed* with falsehood to demolish the truth. It returns in ayah 35, and the surah pauses there to tell you where the disease lives. And in ayah 56 it names the organ: *those who dispute the signs of Allah without any warrant that has come to them — there is nothing in their chests but* ***kibr*** — swollen greatness — *which they will never reach.*

Five times this surah returns to the disputer. No other surah circles him like this. And in between those returns, Ghafir tells the story of a man who is the disputer's perfect opposite: the believer of Pharaoh's own household, the man who **hid faith in his chest** in the most dangerous building on earth — and still found a way to speak it, gently, reasonably, at the risk of his life.

Two men. Two chests. One carries hidden *īmān* and speaks truth. The other carries hidden *kibr* and speaks arguments.

By ayah 69 the surah is in its final descent, and it turns to the Prophet ﷺ — a man who by now had heard every objection Makkah's cleverest tongues could produce — and asks him, almost softly: *a-lam tara* — **do you not see them?** Do you see what is actually happening to the ones who argue?

And then, in four short ayahs, it answers — not with a counter-argument, but with a camera that simply follows the arguer all the way down.

### The hook

Here is what to watch for. The man in this passage begins as the most *active* person imaginable — arguing, gripping, pressing his case. And across four ayahs the grammar itself is going to strip that activity away from him, verb by verb, until the only action left in his future is the one he spent his whole life refusing to perform.

Count the verbs with me as we go. The passage is a machine, and it has exactly five moving parts.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — يُجَٰدِلُونَ: the rope in the word

Before the ayah, a word about rope.

The classical Arabic lexicons record that the root **ج-د-ل** belongs, at its base, to the world of *twisting*: to twist a cord hard and tight until it holds. A *jadīl* is a firmly twisted rope. From that physical picture the language built its word for disputation — **جِدَال** — because that is what a hardened argument is: strands of position wound around each other, pulled tight, built to resist.

Feel how different that is from every other picture of conversation. Dialogue is an open hand. Inquiry is an open door. *Jidāl* is a rope under tension — and the man holding it is not trying to learn anything. He is trying to *win the pull*.

Now the verb as the ayah shapes it: **يُجَٰدِلُونَ** — *yujādilūna* — and the mould matters. This is **Form III**, the pattern Arabic uses for actions directed *against* a counterpart — the mould of contending, of doing something *at* someone who is doing it back. Wrestling is Form III thinking. So is a tug-of-war. The verb does not describe a man examining the signs of Allah. It describes a man **braced against them**, feet dug in, rope wrapped around his fist.

And it is imperfect tense — ongoing. He is not a man who once objected. Disputing is his *tense*. His continuous present.

Then comes the question that shatters the picture: **أَنَّىٰ يُصْرَفُونَ** — *annā yuṣrafūn* — "how are they **turned away**?"

Stop. Look at what the grammar just did.

*Yuṣrafūn* is **passive**. The man who was all grip and effort and directed force — the Form III contender — is, in the same breath, revealed to be a thing *being turned*. He experiences himself as the most active person in the room; the ayah photographs him from above and shows a man being rotated by something he cannot see. The arguments were never steering. Something else — the swollen thing in the chest, named back in ayah 56 — has its hand on the wheel.

He grips the rope. And the rope, all along, was around *him*.

### Element Two — three passives and the verb they are marching toward

Now the machine of the passage, because its five verbs are arranged with terrifying care.

Verb one, we have seen: *yujādilūna* — they dispute. Active, Form III, his own choice, his pride.

Verb two: **كَذَّبُوا** — *kadhdhabū* — they denied. Still active — and in **Form II**, the doubled, intensive mould: not "they doubted" but they denied *thoroughly*, as a settled practice. This is the last verb in the passage that the disputers get to *perform*.

Because now watch the voice of every remaining verb they appear in:

**يُصْرَفُونَ** — they *are turned*. Passive.
**يُسْحَبُونَ** — they *are dragged*. Passive.
**يُسْجَرُونَ** — they *are fed as fuel* to the Fire. Passive.

Three passives in a row, and the grammar is the theology. A man spends a lifetime insisting on his own agency — *my* reasoning, *my* objections, *my* right to withhold assent — and the Quran grants him exactly two active verbs, the arguing and the denying, and then removes his hands from every verb that follows. He turned every discussion into a contest of wills; now he is an object in every clause. The dragging verb, *yusḥab*, is the word for hauling a dead weight across the ground — a thing that no longer moves itself.

And between the second and third verbs stands the hinge of the whole passage: **فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ** — *fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn* — "so they *are going to know*."

There is the fifth verb: **يَعْلَمُونَ** — they will know. And notice — it is *active*. Grammatically, knowing is the one thing left that they will genuinely *do*. The man refused, all his life, the single act the signs were asking of him: to know. To let the clarity in. So the sentence is almost unbearably precise: the knowing he would not do freely, he will do under compulsion — with *sawfa*, the particle of the patient future, sitting in front of it like a signed appointment. The appointment was never in doubt; only the attendance was.

### Element Three — ٱلْأَغْلَٰلُ: the collar whose name is a grudge

One more word.

The shackles of ayah 71: **ٱلْأَغْلَٰلُ** — *al-aghlāl* — the iron collars locked around the neck. A concrete, physical instrument of the punishment.

Now open the corpus of Quranic morphology and look at how that word is filed. Its root is **غ-ل-ل**, and its dictionary headword — the base noun the plural is built on — is **غِلّ**, *ghill*.

If you know the Quran, that word just made you flinch. Because *ghill* is not primarily a word for iron. It is the Quran's word for the **hidden constriction of the chest** — rancor, resentment, the tight coil of ill-will a person carries inside. It is the thing Allah promises to *remove from the chests* of the people of Paradise: *wa nazaʿnā mā fī ṣudūrihim min ghillin* — "We will strip away whatever ghill was in their chests" — so they can finally sit as brothers, face to face.

One root. Two objects. The invisible knot carried *in* the chest, and the iron ring locked *around* the neck. The language itself is testifying that they are the same thing at two stages of its life — the inner constriction, given enough years and enough refusals, hardening into something you wear.

And where does the surah say the disputer's problem lives? Ayah 56: *in their chests is nothing but kibr.* The chest again. The passage does not need to spell out the transfer; the vocabulary performs it. What was carried in the chest arrives, in the end, at the throat.

Hear the last ayah do its work with pure sound: *fī-l-ḥamīmi thumma fī-n-nāri **yusjarūn**.* The ḥamīm — water at the far edge of boiling, from the root of intense heat — and then *yusjarūn*, they are made the Fire's own kindling. The mouth drags through the words the way the verbs drag their objects.

[PAUSE]

The grammar has drawn the full arc: a rope-verb of contention aimed at the signs; an unseen turning already underway; agency stripped clause by clause until only a compelled knowing remains; and a collar whose very name says it was being forged, quietly, inside the chest all along. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about a life.

## PART 2: WHAT THE PASSAGE IS REALLY SAYING

Let me pull back now and try to see the shape of everything we have opened.

We began with a man who argues. He is arguing about the signs of God — specifically, the classical commentators note, about the *Qur'an itself*, the Book that has been placed before him. He does not engage the Book. He wrestles with it. He twists the rope of his position tight against it. He is, in his own experience, the most active person in the room.

And from this point forward, the Qur'an does a very precise thing. It does not argue back. It does not refute his specific objections. It does not engage him in the Form III of his own disputing. It simply *follows him.* It traces the arc of where his posture leads, step by step, without editorial comment, without melodrama. Just the verbs.

*Turned away. Shackled. Dragged. Used as fuel. Knowing.*

The classical commentators fill in only what the verbs leave bare, and it is worth hearing. The collars — *al-aghlāl* — clamp at the neck; the chains — *al-salāsil* — bind at the feet; and the dragging is not metaphor but is done by hands, the *zabāniya*, the angels set over the punishment. He is hauled first through *al-ḥamīm* — water taken to the far edge of its boiling — and then into the Fire, where the last verb, *yusjarūn*, does not merely burn him but makes him fuel: the kindling the fire is fed with. The man who would not be moved by a clear sign is, in the end, moved by force. The one who would not carry the Book is carried.

And in those five stages — the trajectory of a life made visible — the whole diagnosis is complete.

The *jidāl* was not innocent curiosity that went wrong. The root told us that from the first moment. The grip was always the point. The *kibr* of ayah 56 was always present — swollen in the chest, invisible even to the man himself, driving the argument while he believed the argument was something he had chosen freely. And then what was invisible inside — the *ghill*, the inner constriction — became what is iron outside — the *ghull*, the collar at the neck. The interior made exterior. The hidden made manifest.

The structure of the whole surah reinforces this. Between the man of Pharaoh's household — who hid his faith in his chest under the most dangerous conditions on earth and still found a way to *speak* it — and the disputers who never stopped speaking but said nothing — the contrast is between two kinds of interiority. One man carried *īmān* in his chest; the disputers carried *kibr* in theirs. Both things invisible. Both things carried quietly through a life. Both things, eventually, made manifest — in the next world's physics, one as light, the other as iron.

And that, perhaps, is the deepest thing these four ayahs are saying: **what you carry in your chest does not stay in your chest.**

## FOR THE ONE SITTING WITH THIS NOW

If you have made it this far, I want to offer you something that is not about the disputers of Mecca.

It is about the disputer in the room.

There are arguments we carry — positions we hold not because we have examined them freshly but because we have had them for so long that the rope is tightly twisted and nothing can get in. There are things we have already decided we will not let move us. And we dress this in the language of discernment, of being careful, of not being naïve.

The Qur'an is not naïve about how sophisticated this can be. The disputers in Surah Ghafir are not fools. They are articulate. They have objections. Some of their objections are probably, by the standards of worldly rhetoric, quite good. The Form III of *jidāl* is not the grammar of stupidity. It is the grammar of someone who has studied the terrain and knows where to press.

And the Qur'an says: it does not matter. The grip is the problem, not the quality of the grip. The twisting is the problem, not the elegance of what has been twisted. *Annā yuṣrafūn* — how are they turned away? Not by the power of their arguments. By something prior to the arguments. By something the arguments exist to protect. The commentators are precise about the direction of that turning: *yuṣrafūn* is being turned away *from īmān* — and al-Muyassar catches a second question folded inside the first. *Annā* asks not only *how* they are turned, but *toward what?* After clarity this total — *al-bayān al-tām* — where is there even left to go?

The question the passage leaves us with is not rhetorical. It is diagnostic. *What am I gripping so tightly that truth cannot get a finger in?* What position has sat in my chest long enough to have begun, without my knowing it, to take the shape of a collar?

And the passage answers a question we might not have thought to ask: what becomes of the thing we were gripping? A few ayahs on, the disputers are asked where their partners are now — *ayna mā kuntum tushrikūn* — and their reply is among the emptiest words in the Qur'an: *ḍallū ʿannā*, they have vanished from us; in truth, we never called on anything at all. Whatever they held so tightly that no light could get in — when they finally turn to it, it is not there. The position defended nothing. The thing protected was never a thing.

*Fasawfa yaʿlamūn.* They will know. And there is in that phrase the quiet warning that knowledge deferred is not knowledge escaped. The clarity the signs offered — *wāḍiḥat al-dalāla* — does not disappear because we argue against it. It waits. With the patience of *sawfa*. With the certainty of iron already taking shape.

The surah is named *Ghāfir* — the Forgiver. That name is not an afterthought. It is the other side of this passage. The forgiveness is open. The believing man of Pharaoh's household — who was in far more danger than any of us — found a way to carry *īmān* in his chest under Pharaoh's own roof and let it speak.

The question is only what we are carrying in ours.

## Questions to Carry

- Which of your positions have been held so long that the rope is now twisted too tight for anything to get in — and when did you last genuinely test one?
- The disputer felt like the most active man in the room while being turned. Where in your life might your sense of agency be exactly backwards?
- *Ghill* and the collar share one name. What is coiled in your chest right now that you would not want to meet, one day, in iron?
- The one act he refused freely — knowing — was the one act left to him under compulsion. What are you currently refusing to know?
- The believer of Pharaoh's house and the disputers both carried something hidden in the chest. Which carrier are you closer to tonight — honestly?

## One sentence to carry

**The argument you grip is a rope, the rope is being wound by what lives in your chest, and what lives in your chest does not stay there — so hold your positions loosely enough that the truth can still get a finger in.**

## A du'a

*O Allah, Forgiver — Ghāfir — before whom every rope unwinds: loosen our grip on every position we hold against Your signs, and let clarity in before the knowing becomes compulsory.*

*Strip the ghill from our chests in this life, as You have promised to strip it from the people of the Garden — and let nothing we carry within ever take shape around our necks.*

*Make us like the one who hid faith under Pharaoh's roof and still spoke: soft in method, unmovable in truth, safe in Your keeping.*
