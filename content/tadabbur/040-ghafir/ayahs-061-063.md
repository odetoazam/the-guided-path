# The God You Turn From at Dawn

## Introduction: The Most Ordinary Miracle

Let me put the words in front of you first.

> **ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِى جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلَّيْلَ لِتَسْكُنُوا۟ فِيهِ وَٱلنَّهَارَ مُبْصِرًا ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَذُو فَضْلٍ عَلَى ٱلنَّاسِ وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ ٱلنَّاسِ لَا يَشْكُرُونَ**

*Allāhu alladhī jaʿala lakumu-l-layla litaskunū fīhi wa-n-nahāra mubṣirā. Inna-llāha la-dhū faḍlin ʿalā-n-nāsi wa-lākinna aktthara-n-nāsi lā yashkurūn.*

**"It is Allah who made for you the night that you may rest in it, and the day giving visibility. Indeed, Allah is full of bounty to the people — but most of the people do not give thanks."**

And then, immediately:

> **ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ خَـٰلِقُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ لَّآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ ۝ كَذَٰلِكَ يُؤْفَكُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَانُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ**

*Dhālikumu-llāhu rabbukum khāliqu kulli shayʾin lā ilāha illā huwa fa-annā tuʾfakūn. Kadhālika yuʾfaku-lladhīna kānū bi-āyāti-llāhi yajḥadūn.*

**"That is Allah, your Lord, Creator of all things; there is no god but Him — so how are you turned away? Thus are those turned away who used to reject the signs of Allah."**

### Rebuild the world

Imagine you are sitting in Makkah, in the years of mounting pressure. The believer who spoke up in Pharaoh's court — that long, brave speech earlier in this very surah — has fallen silent. The discourse has turned to warn of a Day when hearts climb into throats, when no friend can help a friend. The air is heavy with the language of accountability.

And then, into that heaviness, comes this. Not thunder. Not the trembling of the earth. **The night.** The thing that happened to you last night and will happen again tonight. The day. The thing happening to you right now as you read these words in its light.

This is the Quran's method at its most disarming. It has just spoken of the most terrifying unseen reality — the Day of Judgment — and now it grounds your gaze on the most familiar seen reality you possess: the rhythm you live inside every twenty-four hours and have stopped noticing entirely.

Here is why that matters. The people being addressed were not, for the most part, atheists. Ask them *who made the night and the day*, and the Quran tells us elsewhere they would answer: *Allah.* They knew. The problem was never information. The problem was something stranger — a turning away from what they already knew to be true.

### The hook

So hold two things in your mind as we begin.

The first: this passage names the single most universal gift you have ever received — and then says that *most people do not give thanks for it.* Not some. **Most.**

The second: in the very next breath, it asks a question that should stop us cold. Not *why don't you believe?* The deniers half-believed. The question is sharper than that. **فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ** — *so how are you turned away?* As if to say: the evidence is wrapped around you like the night itself. You are sleeping inside the proof. **So by what mechanism, exactly, does a person look at this and turn?**

That is what we are going to sit with. Not whether the proof exists. But how the human heart manages to turn from a proof it is literally lying down inside of.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Verb That Means *to Bring Into Being*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In English, we have one verb — "to make" — and it does a lot of jobs. *I made a chair* (I created it). *I made him angry* (I caused a state in him). *I made him captain* (I assigned him a role). One word, three very different actions.

Classical Arabic is more precise. The verb **جَعَلَ** *(jaʿala)* lives in this same family — "to make" — but the grammarians noticed something about how it behaves. Al-Qurtubi states it plainly in his commentary on this exact verse: the Arabs *distinguish* between **جَعَلَ when it means خَلَقَ — to create, to bring into being from nothing** — and **جَعَلَ when it means to render, to assign, to turn one thing into another.**

And here is the tell, the grammatical fingerprint: when **جَعَلَ** means *to create*, it takes **only one object.** When it means *to render X into Y*, it takes **two objects** — *I rendered the clay [object one] a vessel [object two].*

Now look at our verse.

> **جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلَّيْلَ**
> *He jaʿala for you the night.*

One object: *the night.* No second object. No "He rendered the night *into* something." Just — the night, brought into being, *for you.*

Al-Qurtubi's conclusion is precise: **جعل هنا بمعنى خلق** — *jaʿala here carries the meaning of khalaqa, to create.*

Let's sit with what that does.

This is not God *organizing* a night that already existed. This is not management of pre-existing material. **The night itself is a created thing.** The darkness that descends, the lowering of the world's volume, the slowing of your own pulse — these are not the absence of something. They are the *presence* of something God deliberately brought into being. The night is not what is left over when the day leaves. **The night is a gift that was made.**

Al-Ṭabarī's commentary gives this precision its full depth: what the night gives rest from is specifically "التصرّف والاضطراب" — the movement and *iḍṭirāb* of seeking one's livelihood. That word *iḍṭirāb* — agitation, turbulence, the restless anxiety of a creature in motion without settled ground — is the exact interior condition the night addresses. God looked at the being He fashioned, saw the iḍṭirāb that the daily pursuit of provision naturally generates, and built a daily cure for it into the architecture of time itself. The night is not a pause in your life. **It is the treatment God prescribed for the condition your life naturally creates.**

Notice something else. We tend to think of darkness as a subtraction — the light goes, and what remains is night. But the Arabic refuses that. *Jaʿala lakumu-l-layl.* He *created* for you the night. It is an addition, not a subtraction. A thing fashioned. A mercy authored.

And then comes the smallest, most easily-skipped word: **لَكُمُ** — *for you.* Not *He created the night.* He created the night **for you.** The lām here is a lām of benefit, of belonging-to. The night is not a cosmic accident you happen to live under. It is addressed to you. It has your name on it.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this single verb reveals: **the rest you take tonight is not a gap in your life. It is a thing God made, deliberately, and handed to you with your name on it — to interrupt the agitation your days produce, before you are consumed by it.**

## Element Two: The Strange Asymmetry of Night and Day

Now I want you to watch the architecture of the sentence itself, because the Quran is about to do something it does *not* announce.

Look at how the night is described and how the day is described. Watch the shape.

> **ٱلَّيْلَ لِتَسْكُنُوا۟ فِيهِ**
> the night — *so that you may rest in it*

> **وَٱلنَّهَارَ مُبْصِرًا**
> and the day — *giving sight*

Do you feel the imbalance? Stay with it, because this is the kind of asymmetry that the methodology of deep reflection teaches us to read as carefully as any presence.

For the **night**, the Quran gives a *purpose clause centered on you*: **لِتَسْكُنُوا** — "so that *you* may find stillness." The verb is yours. You are the subject. The night's whole reason is bent toward your inner state.

For the **day**, the Quran gives no such clause. No "so that you may work." No "so that you may seek your provision." Just one word, an adjective: **مُبْصِرًا** — *mubṣir*, "sight-giving," "made for seeing."

Why the asymmetry? Why does the night get a tender purpose-clause about *your* rest, while the day gets a single, brisk descriptive word?

Let me teach the word **مُبْصِرًا** first, because it hides a marvel.

It comes from the root **ب-ص-ر** *(b-ṣ-r)* — sight, perception, vision. But notice its *form*. This is **Form IV**, the *mubṣir* pattern — the active participle of *abṣara*. Form IV here is transitive-causative in flavor: not "the day that sees," but **"the day that makes-see," the day that enables sight, the sight-giver.**

And here the classical commentators catch something exquisite. Al-Jalālayn notes: **إسناد الإبصار إليه مجازيّ** — *attributing "seeing" to the day is metaphorical (majāzī)* — **لأنه يبصر فيه** — *because seeing happens* **in** *it,* not *by* it.

Sit with this. The day does not literally see. *You* see — and you see *during* the day, *because* of the day's light. But the Arabic does not say "the day in which you see." It collapses the whole relationship into one word and hands the seeing **to the day itself.** The day becomes *the one who makes sight possible.* The light is so generous, so total in its gift of vision, that the language transfers your very seeing onto it. The day is credited with your sight.

[PAUSE]

Now — back to the asymmetry, because now it opens.

Why does the night get *"so that you may rest"* and the day get *"sight-giving"*?

Because **rest is something that happens inside you, and sight is something that comes to you from outside.** The night's gift is *interior* — sukūn, stillness, the settling of the soul. So the verse points inward: *litaskunū*, "that **you** may grow still." The day's gift is *exterior* — it floods the world with the means to perceive it. So the verse points outward, to the day as the sight-bringer: *mubṣir.*

The grammar is not careless. It is mapping two completely different kinds of mercy. **One mercy reaches into you and quiets you. The other mercy opens the world up before you.** And the sentence shapes itself differently for each because they are *felt* differently.

Al-Ṭabarī makes the day's function equally precise: the day is given to "the one who is in agitation (*iḍṭirāb*) for his livelihood" so that he may move about for his needs — and al-Muyassar renders this: the day is given "so that you may direct (*taṣarrifū*) the affairs of your livelihood." Read those two classical glosses together and the mercy of the verse fully opens. Night quiets the iḍṭirāb. Day enables the taṣarruf. One mercy stills the restless creature; the other opens the world before it so it can move through provision purposefully. **God designed the rhythm of your exhaustion and your energy, your rest and your striving, as a single act of mercy in two movements — each calibrated to a different need of the same creature.**

There is one more thread here, and it is the root **س-ك-ن** *(s-k-n)* in **لِتَسْكُنُوا.** This is the root of *sukūn* — stillness — but also of *sakīnah*, the tranquillity God sends down into believing hearts, and of *maskan*, a dwelling, a home. The night is described with the very word-family of *home* and *peace of heart.* The night is meant to be a *dwelling* you enter — not just darkness, but a maskan for the exhausted soul. **God built you a house out of time itself and called it night.**

What this construction reveals: **the night and the day are not two halves of a neutral cycle. They are two distinct acts of tenderness — one that comes to still the iḍṭirāb, one that comes to open the world for taṣarruf — and the language bends to fit each gift exactly because they address entirely different needs of the same creature.**

## Element Three: The Passive Voice With No Hand Behind It

Now we cross from verse 61 into 62 and 63, and the tone shifts. Watch one word — and watch what is *missing* from it.

After establishing the night, the day, the bounty, verse 62 declares the conclusion: **ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ** — *That is Allah, your Lord* — **خَـٰلِقُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ** — *Creator of all things* — **لَّآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ** — *no god but Him.* And then the question lands:

> **فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ**
> *So how — by what path — are you turned away?*

The word is **تُؤْفَكُونَ** *(tuʾfakūn)*, from the root **أ-ف-ك** *(ʾ-f-k)*. Let me give you the root before the grammar, because the root is heavy.

**Afaka** means *to turn a thing away from its rightful direction* — to divert, to pervert a course. From this same root comes **الإفك** *(al-ifk)* — *fabrication, slander, the great lie.* It is the word the Quran uses for the slander against the household of the Prophet ﷺ. And from the same root come **المؤتفكات** — *the overturned cities*, the towns of Lūt that were flipped upside-down. The root carries the image of something **turned from its true position into its opposite.** A lie is truth turned over. A perverted course is a road turned from its destination. An overturned city is a place turned upside-down.

So *tuʾfakūn* is not a gentle "how are you mistaken?" It is: **how are you flipped? How are you turned clean away from a truth this obvious, the way a lie turns a fact upside-down?**

Now — here is the grammatical marvel, and it lives in what is *absent.*

**تُؤْفَكُونَ is passive.** Not *"how do you turn away?"* — that would be active, that would name you as the one doing the turning. The Quran says *"how are you turned away?"* — passive. **And it does not say by whom.**

Read that absence carefully. In Arabic, the passive voice — *al-majhūl*, literally "the unknown" — is used precisely when the doer is left unnamed. Someone, or something, is doing the turning. But the verse does not say who.

Why withhold the agent?

[PAUSE]

Because **that is exactly how delusion feels from the inside.** No one who has been turned from the truth experiences it as *being turned.* They experience their drift as their own reasonable conclusion. The hand that turns them — their desires, their pride, the whisperer, the crowd — that hand is invisible *to them.* The passive voice is doing psychological work. It captures the lived experience of the deluded: *I wasn't turned. I just... ended up here.* The agent is hidden in the grammar because the agent is hidden from the one being turned.

And al-Ṭabarī reads *fa-annā tuʾfakūn* with a spatial force that the translation alone cannot fully carry. He glosses it: "to what direction do you go, and where do you depart from Him, that you worship other than Him?" The word *annā* in Arabic holds both the modal question — *how* — and the directional question — *to where.* And here both senses are fully operative. Not only: *how do you manage to be deluded?* But: *where exactly are you going?* In what direction does the turned-away person think they are heading, when every direction in creation is authored by the One they are turning from? The night they sleep under — His. The day they move through — His. The faculty of reason they deploy to reject Him — His. There is no elsewhere. **The turned-away person is trying to find a door in a house whose walls, floor, and ceiling are all the same Author — and wondering why they keep ending up in the same room.**

Then verse 63 answers the question 62 asked. *How are you turned away?* **كَذَٰلِكَ يُؤْفَكُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَانُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ يَجْحَدُونَ** — *Thus are turned away those who used to reject the signs of Allah.*

One last word, because it is the key to the whole passage: **يَجْحَدُونَ** *(yajḥadūn)*, from **ج-ح-د** *(j-ḥ-d)*. This is one of the most precise words in the Quran's vocabulary of disbelief. **Juḥūd is not ignorance. Juḥūd is denial of what you know.**

Denial of what you already know to be true. The grammarians are precise on this: juḥūd specifically presupposes prior knowledge. You cannot make juḥūd of something you have never encountered. The verb arrives in its characteristic form — *yajḥadūna*, continuous present, habitual action — meaning this is not a single act of rejection but a sustained posture. The evidence came: night after night, day after day. The proof descended, and they pressed against it, continuously, deliberately. Each morning the day arrived as sight-giver, and each morning there was another *no.*

This is what makes the passive *tuʾfakūn* land so hard alongside it. You are being asked: how does a person who *knows* end up turned away? And the answer, embedded in the grammar and the root, is: gradually, through sustained rejection of what keeps being re-presented, until the *no* hardens into a posture so habitual the person no longer notices the evidence is still arriving.

[PAUSE]

What these two verses together reveal: **the mechanism of the turn is not intellectual failure. It is volitional accumulation. The iḍṭirāb of chasing the world fills the spaces God made for sukūn; the signs become noise; and the passive voice captures the result — a person who cannot even identify the hand that turned them, because the turning happened so gradually it felt like their own conclusion.**

# Part 2: The Weight of What You Already Know

The passage has now given us three things. A mercy. A verdict. A question.

The mercy: God made the night for your sukūn and the day for your sight. He made them *for you*, with your specific need written into the design of time itself — one mercy to quiet your iḍṭirāb, one to open the world for your taṣarruf.

The verdict: most people do not give thanks.

The question: *how* are you turned away? *To where* do you go?

But there is something the classical commentators make explicit about the verdict that changes its weight entirely. Al-Muyassar defines what *lā yashkurūn* actually requires: the failure of shukr is not a failure of feeling or of words. It is a failure of **"taʿah wa-ikhlāṣ al-ʿibādah"** — obedience and sincere worship. To give thanks for the night is not to say *alhamdulillāh* as a habit before sleep. It is to *orient the whole person toward the One who gave it* — to let the gift produce an acknowledgment so deep it bends your life in the direction of the Giver. Shukr, on this reading, is a total posture. It restructures everything downstream of itself.

And al-Jalālayn sharpens this to its theological edge. His gloss on *lā yashkurūna-llāha* is four words: **فَلا يُؤْمِنُونَ** — *and therefore they do not believe.* The causal arrow runs from ingratitude to disbelief. Not: "they don't believe, and therefore they don't give thanks." The order is reversed. Ingratitude is structurally prior. The person who genuinely receives the night as a *gift from a specific Giver* cannot remain in disbelief. Reception of the gift implies recognition of the Giver. And recognition of the Giver is the root of iman.

This means the passage is not describing two separate failures — ingratitude and disbelief — running in parallel. It is describing one failure from two angles. The heart that has learned to experience the night as a bare meteorological fact, an astronomical regularity, a thing that simply happens — that heart has already closed the door through which iman enters. **Lā yashkurūna fa-lā yuʾminūn: the ingratitude is not a symptom of the disbelief. It is its cause.**

[PAUSE]

Think about what this means practically.

You have been sleeping inside the proof since the day you were born. The night came for you as an infant and quieted your first iḍṭirāb. The day came and showed you your mother's face. Every night since then has been a *created thing*, a gift with your name on it, an authored mercy whose lām of purpose points straight at you: *litaskunū fīhi* — *so that you, specifically, may find stillness in it.* And every day has been a sight-giver, opening the world before you moment by moment, enabling the taṣarruf of a creature who needs both provision and the light to pursue it.

The question is not whether you notice this. You do, sometimes, in flashes — when the early light comes through a window and you feel, briefly, that the world is too generous to be an accident. But the question the passage is pressing on is: what do you do with that flash? Do you receive it as evidence, follow it toward its Source, let it briefly restructure the orientation of your day? Or does the iḍṭirāb of pursuit — the press of what you haven't resolved, the hunger for what you haven't secured, the anxiety of what might slip away — fill in behind the flash and close it over?

Because that is the mechanism. Not dramatic apostasy. Not a deliberate turning of the back. Just: the iḍṭirāb fills the spaces where the evidence was. And the evidence keeps coming — night after night, day after day — but the posture of reception has quietly closed, slowly, the way a hand closes without the person deciding to close it.

[PAUSE]

There is a reason this passage appears where it does in Surah Ghafir.

The believer who spoke up in Pharaoh's court — that long, brave speech in the surah's earlier movement — has fallen silent. The discourse has warned of the Day when hearts climb into throats, when no intercession saves and no friend helps a friend. The air is saturated with the language of final accountability.

And then, into that heaviness, comes *this.* Not a new argument. Not a fresh revelation of the unseen. The night. The day. Things that were already there, already arriving, already carrying the full weight of proof — for any heart whose posture of reception had not yet closed.

Al-Ṭabarī captures the theological logic here exactly. The One who offers these verses is الله الذي لا تصلح الألوهة إلا له — "Allah, the One for whom alone divinity is fitting, and for whom alone worship is appropriate." The passage is not making a new case for this claim. It is pointing to the case that has been made every morning and every evening since the person's first breath. The night came. The day came. Creator of all things, no god but Him. *So where are you going?*

The surah is making a pastoral argument as much as a theological one. You do not need more evidence to believe. You are sleeping inside adequate evidence. What you need is not information but orientation — the willingness to receive what has been arriving every twenty-four hours as *gift*, to feel the lām of purpose in *litaskunū*, to sense the *faḍl* that is so abundant most people swim in it without tasting it.

And the passage closes with both a warning and a key. The warning is *kadhālika yuʾfaku* — this is how turning happens. Not in a single act but in a sustained posture of rejection, continuous present tense, night after night and day after day pressing against what keeps being offered. The key is hidden in its opposite: **the path back is also a sustained posture.** Not a single act of belief but a daily, nightly practice of reception. Of letting the night do what it was made to do — still you, interrupt the iḍṭirāb, bring you back to the ground of a creature held in care. Of letting the day do what it was made to do — show you the world, open the field of taṣarruf, give you the faculty of sight as a gift rather than a given. Of letting the rhythm God authored in time itself become the rhythm your inner life inhabits — until the faḍl you have been swimming in finally reaches your tongue.

[PAUSE]

So when you wake tomorrow, before the iḍṭirāb of the day fills the space behind your eyes: notice it. The light. The fact of another day, sight given once more. The fact that you rested — or tried to — inside a darkness that was *authored* for that purpose, that has your name written into its lām. The fact that the same One who warned of the Day of accountability also built you a nightly maskan out of time itself, and a daily lamp, and called them both *faḍl* — bounty — in the same breath.

This is what *lā yashkurūn* means, in reverse. **Shukr is the practice of noticing the gift before the day fills in over it. Of staying, for a moment, in the full awareness of the Giver. Of letting that awareness do what it always does when honestly held — bend the whole of you toward the only direction that exists in this house whose walls, floor, and ceiling are all the same One.**

فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ

*So where — where exactly — are you going?*
