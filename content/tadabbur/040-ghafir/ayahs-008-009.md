# Carried In: The Angels' Prayer That Gathers a Family Into Eden

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first. Sit with the sound before we touch a single meaning.

> [ayah:40:8] رَبَّنَا وَأَدْخِلْهُمْ جَنَّـٰتِ عَدْنٍ ٱلَّتِى وَعَدتَّهُمْ وَمَن صَلَحَ مِنْ ءَابَآئِهِمْ وَأَزْوَٰجِهِمْ وَذُرِّيَّـٰتِهِمْ ۚ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ
>
> *Rabbanā wa-adkhilhum jannāti ʿadnin allatī waʿadtahum wa-man ṣalaḥa min ābāʾihim wa-azwājihim wa-dhurriyyātihim — innaka anta-l-ʿazīzu-l-ḥakīm.*
>
> **"Our Lord, admit them into Gardens of perpetual residence which You have promised them — along with whoever was righteous among their fathers, their spouses, and their descendants. Indeed, it is You who are the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."**

And then, immediately after:

> [ayah:40:9] وَقِهِمُ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ ۚ وَمَن تَقِ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ يَوْمَئِذٍ فَقَدْ رَحِمْتَهُۥ ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ
>
> **"And protect them from the evil consequences. And whoever You protect from evil consequences that Day — You will surely have shown him mercy. And that is the supreme triumph."**

[PAUSE]

### Rebuild the world

Here is what you have to see before this ayah can land.

The surah is called *Ghafir* — "The Forgiver." It opens in the middle of a war of words. People are arguing about the signs of Allah, *yujādilu fī āyāti-llāh*. There is heat in these opening verses. There is denial, there is mockery, there is the long history of nations who called their messengers liars and were seized for it. The atmosphere is dense, earthbound, contentious. Voices raised. Hearts hardened.

And then — without warning — verse 7 lifts the camera straight up.

Past the arguing crowds. Past the roofs of Makkah. Past the clouds. Past the sky itself. Up to a place no human eye has seen: **the bearers of the Throne.** *Alladhīna yaḥmilūna-l-ʿarsh.* The greatest creatures in all of creation. The ones positioned closest to the Throne of the Most Merciful. Beings of such magnitude that the descriptions of them in the prophetic reports stagger the imagination.

What are these colossal beings doing?

You might expect them to be glorifying. And they are — *yusabbiḥūna bi-ḥamdi rabbihim.* But the ayah tells us something you would never have guessed. These immense beings, wrapped in light, encircling the Throne — **they are praying. For you.** For the believers. The small, struggling, sinning, doubting believers down here on the surface of a planet they will never visit.

[PAUSE]

The bearers of the Throne look down through all of creation and they make *istighfār* — they beg forgiveness — for the people of faith on earth. Verse 7 is them asking for forgiveness. Verse 8, the one we are sitting with, is the second movement of that same prayer. Having asked that the believers be *forgiven*, they now ask that the believers be *brought in.*

### The Hook

And here is what should stop you cold.

When these angels — who have never disobeyed, who do not eat or sleep or sin, who carry the very Throne — finally open their mouths to ask Allah for the highest thing they can ask for the believers, they do not stop at the believer.

They reach past the believer.

They reach for the believer's *father.* For the believer's *spouse.* For the believer's *children.* They ask Allah to gather a whole family into the Garden, and the condition they attach to that gathering is so gentle, so merciful, so unlike the strict ledger we imagine Judgment to be, that scholars have wept over the single word they used.

What is that word? And why did the angels — of all beings — choose it?

Stay with me. Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *adkhilhum* — the verb that "makes" you enter

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we open the word, because it will change how you hear the whole ayah.

In English, we have separate words for an action and for *causing* that action. We "enter" a room. But to make someone else enter, we need a whole new verb — "admit," "usher in," "bring in." Arabic does this differently. Arabic can take a simple verb and, by reshaping it internally — by adding a sound, doubling a letter, stretching a vowel — turn it into a *causative.* The same root, now meaning "to cause this to happen."

The simple verb here is *dakhala* — to enter. Plain, intransitive. You enter. You walk in on your own two feet.

Now look at what the angels say:

> **وَأَدْخِلْهُمْ** — *wa-adkhilhum*

This is not *dakhala*. This is the fourth form — *adkhala.* And here that fourth form is doing exactly the work it most naturally does: it is **causative.** Not "let them enter." Not "may they enter." But: ***You* bring them in. *You* admit them. *You* be the one who causes their entering.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something, and let it sink in.

The angels do not pray, "O Allah, let the believers walk into the Garden." They do not picture the believer arriving under his own power, presenting his record at the gate, earning his way across the threshold. The grammar will not allow it. They make Allah the *agent* of the entering. The believer is the object — *hum*, "them" — the one acted upon. The hand that opens the door, that lifts, that carries across the threshold, is Allah's hand.

Here's what's remarkable about that. The greatest worshippers in existence, when they imagine the believer's salvation, do not imagine it as an achievement. They imagine it as an *admission.* A being brought in by Another.

And if the angels — who never sinned — frame the believer's entry as something Allah must *do to* the believer rather than something the believer *does* — then how much more should we drop the fantasy that we will stride into Paradise on the strength of our own deeds?

You do not enter Jannah. **You are admitted.** The verb itself is the theology.

Let that be the first stone under our feet. Now watch where it leads.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *jannāti ʿadnin* — the Garden whose name means "it will not move"

The angels name the destination: *jannāti ʿadnin.* We translate it "Gardens of Eden," and the English smuggles in a whole accidental history — orchards, the first couple, a lost paradise behind us. Strip all that away. Listen to the Arabic word on its own terms.

*ʿAdn.* The root is ʿayn-dāl-nūn.

In the classical lexicons — in Lisān al-ʿArab — this root carries a single, stubborn idea: **to stay. To remain fixed. To abide in a place and not leave it.** *ʿAdana bil-makān* — he stayed put in the place, settled, did not move on.

And here is the detail that opens it. From this very same root comes the Arabic word *maʿdin* — which means a **mine.** A mineral vein. The place in the earth where gold or iron *sits* — fixed, embedded, permanent, the spot it has occupied since the mountains were laid down. A *maʿdin* is where a precious thing *lives in the ground and does not budge.*

[PAUSE]

So feel what the angels are actually asking.

They are not asking that the believers *visit* a beautiful place. They are asking that the believers be embedded in it the way gold is embedded in the earth — *jannāti ʿadn*, Gardens of the unmovable abode. The name of the destination contains, inside its own letters, the promise that no one will ever be asked to leave.

Why does this matter? Because every joy you have ever known on this earth came with an expiration printed on it. Every embrace ended. Every good evening with the people you love slid toward bedtime and then toward goodbye. The deepest grief of being human is not that good things don't happen — it's that they don't *stay.* The root of all your homesickness is *change.*

And the angels, in choosing this word, are praying against the one thing that has poisoned every joy you've ever had. They are asking for a garden whose name is, essentially, **"It will not be taken from you."**

[PAUSE]

And look — there's an even quieter detail. *ʿAdnin* is **indefinite.** Not "*the* Garden of Eden," some single named location you could pin on a map. The indefiniteness lets it open outward — a *kind* of garden, a *quality* of dwelling, vast and unbounded, defined not by where it is but by what it does: it holds you and does not let go.

So now we have two stones. You are *brought in* — that was the first verb. And what you are brought into is, by its very name, **permanent.** The entering is by His hand; the staying is forever.

But neither of those is the word the scholars wept over. That word is next.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *wa-man ṣalaḥa* — the word so gentle it gathers a whole family

Now the heart of it.

Let me first show you the grammar, because the grammar is where the mercy hides.

The angels say: *wa-adkhilhum* — "admit **them**." That *hum*, "them," is the believers. Then immediately:

> **وَمَن صَلَحَ** — *wa-man ṣalaḥa* — "and whoever was righteous"

That *wa-man*, "and whoever," is grammatically **joined** — *maʿṭūf* — onto the "them." The classical commentators are explicit about this. Al-Jalālayn says *wa-man ṣalaḥa* is *ʿaṭf* — a conjunction — onto the *hum* in *wa-adkhilhum.* Al-Qurtubi says the same: *man* is in the accusative position, conjoined onto "the *hā* and the *mīm*" — onto the "-them."

So read it as one continuous request: "Admit *them* — and admit *whoever was righteous* from among their fathers, their spouses, and their offspring." The same divine hand that brings in the believer reaches over and brings in the believer's family.

[PAUSE]

Now — the word. *Ṣalaḥa.*

Watch this carefully, because this is the whole point.

The root ṣ-l-ḥ gives us *ṣāliḥ* (righteous), *iṣlāḥ* (to set right, to reform), *ṣulḥ* (reconciliation), *maṣlaḥa* (benefit). It is the root of *being sound, being in good order, being whole.*

But notice the *form* the angels chose. They did not say *aṣlaḥa* — the fourth form, the intense, active, reforming form, "the one who set things right and rectified and laboured at his religion." They did not pile on adjectives — "the devout, the foremost, the ones racing ahead." They used the plainest possible form of the verb. The first form. *Ṣalaḥa.*

**To simply be sound. To merely be in good order. To be okay.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the size of what just happened?

The angels are asking for the believer's father, his wife, his children — and the bar they set for that family member is not "was a saint." Not "matched the believer in worship." Not "raced ahead in faith." The bar is *ṣalaḥa* — **was, in some real but humble way, sound.** Had faith. Was, at the root, *alright.*

This is exactly where the classical commentary takes us, and it is one of the most moving narrations in all of tafsir. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr — preserved by al-Baghawī and pointed to by al-Qurtubī — describes the scene:

The believer enters the Garden. And he looks around and he says, *"Where is my father? Where is my mother? Where is my child? Where is my spouse?"* And it is said to him: *"They did not work the way you worked."* Their deeds did not reach your level. And the believer says — listen to this — **"I was working for myself *and for them.*"** *Innī kuntu aʿmalu lī wa-lahum.* My whole striving had them inside it. And then the command comes: *adkhilūhum-l-jannah* — **"Admit them into the Garden."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that.

The merely-sound family member — the one who was decent, who believed, who was *okay* but no spiritual hero — is lifted into the Garden on the strength of someone who loved them and carried them in their intention. The angels' word *ṣalaḥa* is the door through which that mercy walks. They did not raise the bar. They lowered it to "sound" — and then let love do the carrying.

And read the absence here, because it speaks. The angels do *not* say "whoever *believed exactly as much*," do not say "whoever *earned it equally.*" They leave the standard deliberately, mercifully low. The gap between *ṣalaḥa* — "was sound" — and *aṣlaḥa* — "was a reformer" — is precisely the gap that your imperfect mother, your distracted brother, your ordinary-but-faithful spouse fits through.

[PAUSE]

That is the word the scholars wept over.

Not because it is grand. Because it is *small enough to fit the people we love.*

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have built, standing one on top of the other.

*Adkhilhum* — you do not enter; **you are brought in.**
*ʿAdn* — and what you are brought into **will never be taken away.**
*Ṣalaḥa* — and the door is set **low enough that your merely-sound loved ones come in with you.**

Three words. A complete theology of how a soul — and a family — arrives home.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You are being carried by prayers you cannot hear

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a quiet loneliness that runs underneath modern life, and it has a specific shape. It is the feeling that you are *on your own.* That your soul is a private project. That whether you make it — spiritually, morally, ultimately — comes down to you, your willpower, your discipline, your performance, alone in a room with your own conscience. We have absorbed an entire culture of the self-made individual, and we have quietly applied it to our religion too: *I* must fix myself. *I* must earn it. If I fall, it is *my* failure, mine alone, unwitnessed and uncarried.

Hold that feeling up against this ayah.

[PAUSE]

Right now — as you sit here — the bearers of the Throne are praying for you. Not for you by name, perhaps, but for you by *category*: for *alladhīna āmanū*, for the people who believed. The largest, most luminous beings in creation are turned in supplication toward the believers, asking forgiveness for them, asking that they be admitted, asking that they be protected. **You have never been on your own. Not for one second.**

You did not earn that prayer. You don't even feel it. It is happening above the ceiling of everything you can perceive. The angels are not waiting for you to qualify. They are interceding *now*, mid-struggle, while you are still failing, still distracted, still you.

Let that dismantle the loneliness for a moment. The universe is not indifferent to your soul. The very architecture of creation — the throne-bearers at the summit of it — is bent toward your rescue.

[PAUSE]

And then the ayah deepens the point, and makes it unbearably personal.

Because it is not only the angels who carry you. *You* carry others, and others carry *you.*

Go back to Saʿīd ibn Jubayr's narration. The believer enters and pulls his family in behind him — *"I was working for myself and for them."* Now flip it around and stand in the other position. Somewhere in your life there is a person whose prayers are carrying *you* and you do not know it. A grandmother who said your name in the last third of the night for years. A parent who, in some prayer you never witnessed, folded you into their hope for Jannah. You are, right now, possibly being carried toward the Garden on an intention that someone formed about you while you slept.

This is what the ayah unveils: **salvation is relational.** It is not a sealed, private transaction between you and God conducted in isolation. It runs along the lines of love. The believer's worship has a *gravity* to it — it pulls the people he loves into its orbit. Faith is not a solo climb. It is a rope team. The strong pull up the merely-sound, and the merely-sound were never asked to climb alone.

[PAUSE — major pause]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Two things, very concretely.

First: **start making the intention.** When you pray, when you fast, when you give — widen it. *"O Allah, this is for me and for my parents, my spouse, my children, the ones who fell short."* The believer in the narration did not pull his family in by accident; he pulled them in because his striving *had them inside it.* Build that into your worship deliberately. Make your good deeds a vehicle with empty seats, and put the names of the people you love in those seats.

Second: **stop carrying your soul alone.** The loneliness of the self-made spiritual project is a lie. You are surrounded — by angels above you and by the prayers of those who love you around you. Let yourself be carried. And then turn and carry someone else.

### Theme Two: The mercy that reaches past the ledger

Now the harder, more wondrous theme. Let me build to it carefully.

We carry inside us a picture of Judgment, and it is essentially an *accounting* picture. A scale. A ledger. Good deeds on one side, bad on the other, a precise weighing, and an outcome that follows strictly from the math. We even have the imagery for it in the Qur'an — the *mīzān*, the balance. And there is truth in that picture. Justice is real. The weighing is real.

But here is the trap we fall into. We take that just, exact, ledger-picture and we apply it to *the people we love* — and it terrifies us. Because when you run the people you love through a strict ledger, you start doing the math on their behalf, and the math frightens you. *My father prayed, but he had his flaws. My brother believes, but he barely practices. My spouse is good, but is "good" enough?* You lie awake doing arithmetic on the souls of the people you cannot bear to lose, and the arithmetic does not comfort you.

[PAUSE]

Now bring that fear to this ayah, and watch what it does to your math.

The merely-sound family member in Saʿīd ibn Jubayr's narration — run *him* through the strict ledger. What happens? *"They did not work the way you worked."* By the strict accounting, he does not reach the believer's station. The math is honest about that. The ledger says: lower rank, lesser deeds, falls short.

And then something enters the equation that is not on the ledger at all.

Love. Intention. The longing of one believer for another. *"I was working for myself and for them."* And on the strength of that — on the strength of a *relationship* — the command descends: **admit them.**

[PAUSE]

This is mercy that *reaches past the ledger.* Not mercy that erases justice — the man was *ṣalaḥa*, he was genuinely sound, he had real faith; this is not a free pass for those who turned away. But mercy that, *given* a foundation of soundness, lifts the merely-okay to the station of the excellent, because they were loved by someone whom Allah was pleased with.

And now look at how the angels *seal* this prayer. This is the architecture. They close with two of Allah's names — and pay attention to which two, because the pairing is the proof.

> **إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ** — *innaka anta-l-ʿazīzu-l-ḥakīm*

First, notice the emphasis. *Innaka anta* — "Indeed *You*, *You* alone." There's a doubled insistence here: *innaka*, "indeed You," and then the separate pronoun *anta*, "You," laid down again like a second stamp. The Arabic structure restricts it — *it is You and none but You* who are these things. The angels are not describing Allah loosely. They are pinning the whole prayer onto two specific attributes.

[PAUSE]

*Al-ʿAzīz* — the All-Mighty, the Unassailable. The root ʿ-z-z is strength that cannot be overcome, a might that no one can resist or negotiate down.

*Al-Ḥakīm* — the All-Wise. The root ḥ-k-m is wisdom, but also *judgment*, putting each thing precisely in its right place.

Why these two? Why close a prayer about gathering merely-sound families into Eden with *Might* and *Wisdom*?

Here is what I think the angels are doing. Feel the two fears that should attack this prayer.

Fear one: *"Can it even be done? Can a merely-sound soul really be lifted to the rank of the excellent? Can a whole family be reunited across the gulf of their unequal deeds?"* — Answer: **You are al-ʿAzīz.** The All-Mighty. There is no force in existence that can resist Your decree to bring them in. If You will it, *nothing* — not the ledger, not the gulf in their deeds, not death itself — can stand in the way. Your might *guarantees* the reunion.

[PAUSE]

Fear two: *"But is it* right*? Is it just? Doesn't lifting the lesser to the rank of the greater break the fairness of the whole system?"* — Answer: **You are al-Ḥakīm.** The All-Wise. This mercy is not a loophole, not a sentimental exception that cheats justice. It is *wisdom* — every soul placed in precisely the station Your perfect judgment assigns. The believer is not robbed by his family's elevation; the family is not unjustly inflated. It is all *exactly right.* The reunion is not a flaw in the system. It *is* the system, working perfectly.

Do you see it? **Al-ʿAzīz guarantees the mercy can't be stopped. Al-Ḥakīm guarantees the mercy isn't a mistake.** Might makes the reunion *possible*; Wisdom makes it *just.* The angels chose the only two names that answer both fears at once.

[PAUSE]

And then verse 9 lands the final word, and it reframes everything.

> **وَقِهِمُ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ** — *wa-qihimu-s-sayyiʾāt* — "And protect them from the evil consequences."

Look at that command — *qi.* Stripped down to almost nothing. From the root w-q-y, *to shield, to guard.* And it is the same root — sit with this — as *taqwā.* The God-consciousness you are asked to build your whole life around — *taqwā* — literally means *making a shield.* All your life, *you* are asked to build the shield: *ittaqū-llāh*, shield yourselves. And here, at the end, the angels ask Allah to do for the believer the very thing the believer spent his life trying to do for himself: *qihim* — **You shield them.** The lifelong striving of *taqwā* is met, at the last, by the divine *wiqāya* — His protection.

And then the verdict:

> **وَمَن تَقِ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ يَوْمَئِذٍ فَقَدْ رَحِمْتَهُ** — "And whoever You shield from evil consequences that Day — You will *surely* have shown him mercy."

Notice: it does not say "whoever You shield, You will reward him," or "credit him," or "rank him." It says **You will have shown him mercy.** *Raḥimtahu.* At the absolute summit of the matter, the final word is not *justice.* It is not *merit.* It is *raḥmah* — mercy. The whole towering structure — admission, permanence, the gathered family, Might and Wisdom — all of it resolves into one word: He had mercy on him.

And *that* — *dhālika huwa-l-fawzu-l-ʿaẓīm* — **that, that right there, is the supreme triumph.** Not wealth. Not status. Not even the deeds themselves. The triumph is to be *shielded by His mercy* on the Day it is the only thing that will matter.

[PAUSE — major pause]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you *grieve* over the people you love who fall short — and how you grieve over your own falling short. Stop running the strict ledger on the souls you love and despairing at the math. The math is real, but the math is not the last word. *Mercy reaches past the ledger* — for the merely-sound, for the okay, for the ordinary believer who simply *was, in some real way, alright.* Your job is not to perfect your loved ones into spiritual heroes. Your job is to *be the believer whose worship carries them*, to keep their faith *sound*, and to entrust the gulf between "sound" and "excellent" to the One who is *al-ʿAzīz* — strong enough to close it — and *al-Ḥakīm* — wise enough to close it justly.

And for yourself: stop building your hope on your performance and start building it on His *raḥmah.* You will not *out-deed* your way into the Garden. You will be *shielded* into it. The final word over you, if you are sound, is *raḥimtahu* — He had mercy on him. Live like someone betting on that mercy, not auditing your own ledger in the dark.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at what the angels built, because the structure itself is the argument.

Watch the prayer move:

1. **Admit them** — *adkhilhum* — they don't walk in; they are *brought* in. (Salvation is *received*, not earned.)
2. **Into Gardens of permanence** — *ʿadn* — and once brought in, never moved out. (The gift, once given, is *kept*.)
3. **Along with whoever was sound** — *man ṣalaḥa* — and the door is low enough for the family. (The gift is *shared* down the lines of love.)
4. **You, the Mighty, the Wise** — *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — and this reunion is both *unstoppable* and *just*.
5. **And shield them** — *qihim* — protected, by the same root as the *taqwā* they spent life building.
6. **You will have had mercy on him — that is the triumph** — *raḥimtahu... al-fawzu-l-ʿaẓīm* — and it all resolves into *mercy*, named as the supreme victory.

It begins with a divine hand opening a door and it ends with the word *mercy.* Everything between — the permanence, the gathered family, the Might, the Wisdom, the shield — is the explanation of *how* that mercy operates. The whole prayer is an arch, and the keystone at the very top is *raḥimtahu*: **He had mercy on him.**

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

From the *words*, we learned: you are *brought in* (you don't earn the entry), into a place that is *permanent* (you don't lose the gift), with the door set *low enough for the merely-sound* (your loved ones come too).

From the *themes*, we learned: you are *carried* — by angels above you, by the prayers of those who love you — and the final accounting is not a cold ledger but a *mercy* that reaches past the math to gather the people you cannot bear to lose.

They are one truth. **Salvation is not a solo achievement weighed on a strict scale. It is a gift, received by a hand not your own, kept forever, and shared along the lines of love — guaranteed by His Might, made just by His Wisdom, and named, at the end, as Mercy.**

### Questions to carry

- Whose prayer might be carrying *you* right now — and have you ever thanked them, or carried them back?
- When you do your good deeds, are there empty seats in them? Whose names could you put in those seats?
- When you imagine the people you love at the Judgment, do you run the strict ledger and despair — or do you trust the mercy that reaches past it?
- You spend your life trying to build the shield of *taqwā.* What would change if you truly believed that, in the end, *He* would be the one shielding *you*?
- If the final word over a sound soul is *raḥimtahu* — "He had mercy on him" — are you living like someone betting on His mercy, or like someone auditing your own deeds in the dark?

### One-sentence distillation

The angels who carry the Throne pray not that the believers *earn* their way into eternal Gardens but that Allah *bring them in* — and gather their merely-sound loved ones with them — revealing a salvation that is received, permanent, and relational, sealed by a Might that makes the reunion unstoppable and a Wisdom that makes it just, and resolving, at the last, into one word: mercy.

### The closing invitation

So here is what I would invite to shift.

Loosen your grip on the ledger. Not because justice isn't real — it is — but because you were never meant to face it alone, and neither were the people you love. Let yourself be carried. Be someone who carries. Widen the intention behind every prayer until it has room for your father, your spouse, your children, the ones who are merely *alright.* And when the fear rises about whether the people you love will make it — answer that fear the way the angels did: *He is al-ʿAzīz, strong enough to bring them in; al-Ḥakīm, wise enough to do it rightly.*

You do not enter the Garden. **You are admitted.** Live as someone who is being carried home.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who are *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — bring us in by Your hand, for we cannot walk in by our own. And do not bring us in alone: gather with us our fathers and our mothers, our spouses and our children, everyone among them who was sound, even the ones who only ever managed to be *alright.* Shield us with Your *wiqāya* on the Day our own striving runs out, and let the last word over us be the one the angels prayed for — *raḥimtahu* — that You had mercy on us. *Āmīn.*
