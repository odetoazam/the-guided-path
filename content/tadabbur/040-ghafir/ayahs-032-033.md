# The Day of Mutual Calling
### A Warning Spoken by One Who Loved His Enemies

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the man who said them — because you cannot feel these two ayat until you know whose mouth they came out of and what it cost him to open it.

> [ayah:40:32] وَيَـٰقَوْمِ إِنِّىٓ أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلتَّنَادِ
> *wa yā qawmi innī akhāfu ʿalaykum yawma at-tanād*
> **"And O my people, I fear for you the Day of Mutual Calling —"**

> [ayah:40:33] يَوْمَ تُوَلُّونَ مُدْبِرِينَ مَا لَكُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ مِنْ عَاصِمٍ ۗ وَمَن يُضْلِلِ ٱللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُۥ مِنْ هَادٍ
> *yawma tuwallūna mudbirīn, mā lakum mina-llāhi min ʿāṣim, wa man yuḍlili-llāhu fa-mā lahū min hād*
> **"— the Day you will turn and flee, with no one to shield you from Allah. And whomever Allah leaves to stray, for him there is no guide at all."**

### Rebuild the world

Picture the room.

It is the throne room of the most powerful man on earth. Pharaoh has just declared, in front of his court, his intention to murder Musa. *"Leave me to kill Musa,"* he says a few verses earlier, *"and let him call upon his Lord."* The air in that room is the air of a sentence already passed. Everyone there knows which way the wind blows. To agree with Pharaoh is safety. To hesitate is suspicion. To object is death.

And in that room there is a man.

He is not an outsider. He is not one of the Banī Isrāʾīl. The Qurʾān tells us he is **مِّنْ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ** — *from the family of Pharaoh.* An insider. A man of the court. A man who, for years, had carried a secret heavier than anyone around him could guess: **يَكْتُمُ إِيمَانَهُ** — *he had been concealing his faith.*

For years he had said nothing. For years he had watched and worshipped in silence and survived. And now, at the exact moment when silence would have been safest — when the machinery of the state has turned to kill the Messenger — this man stands up and begins to speak. He speaks the longest single speech of any believer recorded in the Qurʾān outside the Prophets themselves. The surah is *named after him.* Not after Pharaoh, who dominates its scenes. **Sūrat Ghāfir** — also called **Sūrat al-Muʾmin**, *The Believer.*

[PAUSE]

And somewhere in the middle of that speech, after he has argued with them about logic, about history, about the fate of the nations before them, he arrives at these two ayat. And something in his voice changes. He stops arguing *about* them and starts pleading *for* them.

Listen to the smallness of the gap between him and the men who would kill him: he does not say *"O you people."* He says **يَا قَوْمِ** — *"O my people."* My people. With the possessive. These men — these idol-worshipping courtiers of a tyrant who calls himself a god — he claims them. *Mine.* I belong to you and you belong to me, and that is exactly why I cannot stay quiet while you walk off a cliff.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to sit with before we go a single word further.

Of all the names this man could have used for the Day of Judgment — and the Qurʾān gives it dozens of names — he reached for one strange, specific phrase: **يَوْمَ التَّنَادِ**, *the Day of Mutual Calling.* The Day of calling back and forth.

Why that name? Why, of all the things he could have warned them about — the Fire, the Scale, the Reckoning — does he choose to frighten them with *the sound of people calling to each other?*

And there is something even stranger. Look at the very last word of ayah 32 in the Arabic: **التَّنَادِ**. A word about *calling out* — and yet the word itself has had its ending cut off. It is missing a letter. It is, quite literally, a call that doesn't reach its end.

Hold those two questions. We are going to earn the answers slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: يَوْمَ التَّنَادِ — The Day of *Mutual* Calling

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small piece of Arabic, because the whole insight lives inside it.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — *forms* — and each form bends the basic meaning of a root in a specific direction. There is one form, what scholars call **Form VI**, the *tafāʿul* pattern, whose entire job is to express **reciprocity** — an action that goes *back and forth between two or more parties.*

You already know this intuitively. Think of the difference in English between *"to fight"* and *"to fight one another."* Between *"to write"* and *"to correspond."* One is an action you do. The other is an action that bounces between people — each one doing it to the other. *Takātaba* — they wrote *to each other.* *Tashāwara* — they consulted *one another.* The shape of the word tells you: this is not one person calling. This is a whole field of people calling, and being called, all at once.

Now the word.

The root is **ن-د-و / ن-د-ي** — *to call out, to summon, to cry to.* From it comes *nidāʾ*, a call. From it comes *munādī*, a caller, a herald. And from it, in **Form VI**, comes **التَّنَادِ** — *at-tanād*: the calling that goes back and forth. Not a call. **A crossfire of calls.**

So let's open what the man actually said. He did not say *"the Day of the Trumpet"* or *"the Day of Reckoning."* He said: the Day when **everyone is calling to everyone.**

Now — what is all that calling?

Here the classical commentators paint the scene, and I want to give it to you not as a list of opinions but as one widening picture, each scholar adding a brushstroke. **Al-Baghawī** fills the air with voices: the people of the Garden call out to the people of the Fire, and the people of the Fire call back to the people of the Garden. The people of the heights — *aṣḥāb al-aʿrāf* — call out. A herald announces, by name, *so-and-so, son of so-and-so, has attained a happiness he will never lose,* and *so-and-so has fallen into a misery from which he will never rise.* And at the very end, when death itself is brought forth and slaughtered, a voice calls: *O people of the Garden — eternity, no death. O people of the Fire — eternity, no death.* **Al-Muyassar** adds the simplest and most human stroke: people call to one another **مِنْ هَوْلِ الْمَوْقِف** — *out of the sheer terror of standing there.* The calling of people who have lost each other in a crowd and are screaming names into the noise.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with me, because here is what I think this man was doing, and it is almost unbearably poignant.

*He is standing in a room, calling out to people who will not answer him.*

That is the scene right now. He is the lone voice in Pharaoh's court, calling — *yā qawmi, yā qawmi,* O my people, O my people — and they are turning their faces away. He is *living* a one-sided call. His nidāʾ is going out and nothing is coming back.

And so when he warns them, he reaches for the name **يَوْمَ التَّنَادِ** — the Day when *calling becomes mutual at last.* The Day when the silence finally breaks and everyone is calling. But by then the calling is useless. By then the call you ignored becomes the call you scream. **The voice you would not answer in the throne room becomes the voice you cannot stop hearing in the fire.**

Today there is one caller and no answer. On that Day there will be nothing *but* answer — frantic, too-late, crossing answer — and no one to help.

### The cut-off call

Now look again at the word's *shape*, because the Qurʾān's spelling is doing something here.

The full word for *the calling* would be **التَّنَادِي** — ending in a *yāʾ*. But in the most widespread recitation it is written and read **التَّنَادِ** — the final *yāʾ* dropped, leaving a bare, suspended *kasra*. **Al-Qurṭubī** and the commentators on recitation note both readings — *bi-ithbāt al-yāʾ wa ḥadhfihā*, with the *yāʾ* kept and with it deleted.

I am not going to over-read a point of orthography — the deletion follows known patterns of the language, and I want to be honest about that. But I will say what it *invites us to feel:* the word for *calling* arrives with its tail cut off. The call does not get to finish. It hangs in the air, unresolved, on a vowel that points somewhere it never reaches.

A call that doesn't complete. On the Day named for calling.

[PAUSE]

That is the first thing this single phrase reveals: **the warning and the warned-about are the same shape.** A man calling out to people who won't listen, naming a Day when calling out is all there is — and helps no one.

### Element Two: تُوَلُّونَ مُدْبِرِينَ — The Doubled Turning

Watch how ayah 33 opens. He has named the Day. Now he tells you what your *body* will do on it.

**يَوْمَ تُوَلُّونَ مُدْبِرِينَ** — *the Day you turn, fleeing.*

Let me teach the structure first, because it looks redundant and the redundancy is the point.

**تُوَلُّونَ** — from the root *w-l-y* — means *to turn, to turn away, to turn your face from something.* That alone would be a complete statement: *the Day you turn away.*

But he does not stop there. He adds **مُدْبِرِينَ** — from the root *d-b-r*, which is the root of *the back, the rear, what is behind.* *Mudbirīn* is an active participle: *ones who are showing their backs, ones in flight.* Grammatically it is a **ḥāl** — a description of the *state* in which the turning happens.

So Arabic has just said the same physical motion *twice,* from two angles. You turn (*tuwallūna*) — and the way you turn is *back-first, in flight* (*mudbirīn*). It is like saying *"you flee, running."* One word would have carried the meaning. He used two.

Why?

Because there are two different things happening in a single act, and he wants you to feel both.

**تُوَلُّونَ** is the *turning of the face* — the refusal, the *I-don't-want-to-see-this.* It is the same gesture they are making at him *right now,* in the room, as he speaks. They are *turning away* from his words. The Qurʾān uses this very verb again and again for people turning their backs on the truth in *this* life.

**مُدْبِرِينَ** is the *flight of the body* — the legs running, the back exposed, the animal panic of *get me out of here.*

Put them together and the man is telling his people something terrifying and precise: **the turning-away you are doing now, with your faces, you will one day do with your whole body — and there will be nowhere for the body to go.**

Consider the gesture itself. Turning your back is the most ancient signal of two opposite things: *contempt* and *terror.* You turn your back on someone you despise. And you turn your back on something you are running from in fear. In the court, they turn their backs on him in **contempt.** On that Day, they will turn their backs in **terror.** Same motion. The contempt curdles into panic. The dismissive shoulder becomes the fleeing one.

[PAUSE]

And here is where the doubling does its deepest work. The intensification — *turning, fleeing* — paints a picture of frantic, total escape. Maximum motion. And the very next words pull the floor out from under all that motion:

**مَا لَكُم مِّنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ عَاصِمٍ** — *there is no one to shield you from Allah.*

The more frantically the image runs, the more absolute the next clause makes the futility. He gives you the most violent verb of escape — and then tells you escape does not exist. **It is the running of someone in a sealed room.** All that energy, all that flight, and not one inch of distance gained. That is the feeling these two words, side by side, are built to produce.

### Element Three: مِنْ عَاصِمٍ … مِنْ هَادٍ — The Symmetry of What Is Withheld

Now we come to the architecture, and I want you to see two clauses standing like twin pillars at the end of the passage.

**مَا لَكُم مِّنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ عَاصِمٍ**
*there is, for you, from Allah, not a single shield.*

**فَمَا لَهُ مِنْ هَادٍ**
*there is, for him, not a single guide.*

Look at how they rhyme in structure. Both begin with the negation **مَا**. Both use that small, devastating particle **مِنْ** before the final noun — **مِنْ عَاصِمٍ**, **مِنْ هَادٍ**.

Let me teach you that little **مِنْ**, because most translations cannot carry it. When *min* is placed before an indefinite noun inside a negation like this, grammarians call it **min al-istighrāqiyya** — the *min* of total exhaustion. It does not just say *"no protector."* It says *"not even one — not a trace — not a single conceivable protector of any kind, anywhere."* It slams every door at once. English has to reach for clumsy emphasis: *"no protector whatsoever,"* *"none at all."* Arabic does it with two letters.

So now read both pillars with that force restored:

*Not one shield. Not anywhere. Not of any kind.*
*Not one guide. Not anywhere. Not of any kind.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel the two words those *mins* are crushing.

**عَاصِم** — active participle from *ʿ-ṣ-m*, *to protect, to shield, to hold something back from harm, to grant immunity.* It is the root behind *ʿiṣma* — the divine protection. A *ʿāṣim* is the one who steps between you and the falling blow. And here is a thread worth pulling: this exact word appears in one of the most heartbreaking scenes in the Qurʾān. The son of Nūḥ, refusing the ark as the water rises, cries that he will *"take refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water."* And his father answers: **لَا عَاصِمَ الْيَوْمَ مِنْ أَمْرِ اللَّهِ إِلَّا مَن رَّحِمَ** — *"There is no protector today from the command of Allah — except for the one He has mercy upon."* The same word. *No ʿāṣim.* The mountain is no ʿāṣim. The court is no ʿāṣim. Pharaoh — who these men believe is their lord and protector — is no ʿāṣim.

And notice the absence: **no protector is named.** He does not say *"Pharaoh cannot protect you"* or *"the idols cannot protect you."* He leaves the *ʿāṣim* utterly unspecified — *min ʿāṣim,* any protector at all — because to name one would be to grant it the dignity of being a candidate. There is no candidate. The slot is empty. The silence where a protector's name should be *is* the message.

**هَادٍ** — active participle from *h-d-y*, *to guide.* The one who shows the way.

And here the symmetry turns the screw. Watch what is withheld from each clause:

> No **ʿāṣim** — no one to *shield* you when the blow comes.
> No **hādī** — no one to *show you the way* before the blow comes.

One is rescue *in* the danger. The other is direction *out of* it. He has sealed both exits. There is no one to save you when you arrive, and — for the one Allah leaves astray — there was no one to keep you from arriving in the first place.

### The clause that reframes everything

But look closely at the *second* pillar, because it is not parallel by accident. The first clause is about *them* — *mā lakum,* "for **you**." The second clause suddenly widens into a universal law:

**وَمَن يُضْلِلِ اللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُ مِنْ هَادٍ**
*And whomever Allah causes to stray, for him there is no guide at all.*

The verb **يُضْلِل** is from *ḍ-l-l*, *to go astray, to lose the path.* In **Form IV** here it is causative — *to leave astray, to let go astray, to cause to wander.* And the man places it inside a conditional: *whoever Allah leaves to stray…*

This is no longer just a forecast of their flight. This is the *engine underneath it.* He is telling them: your fleeing on that Day, your finding no shield — these are not the disaster. They are the *symptom.* The disaster already happened, quietly, here, now, in this room — the disaster is **being left without a guide.** Misguidance is not the punishment that comes after. **Misguidance is the punishment.** To be a person for whom *there is no hādī* — that is the fire before the Fire.

[PAUSE]

So here is what these twin pillars reveal when you stand them side by side: **the absence of a guide today and the absence of a shield tomorrow are the same sentence, written twice.** What you lose at the end, you lost at the beginning. The man is begging them to notice that the catastrophe is not in the future. It is in the turning-away they are doing *right now.*

---

### The bridge

So look at what these three pieces of language have built.

A word for *calling* — given to people who won't answer a call.
A doubled verb of *fleeing* — given to people who have nowhere to run.
A twin negation of *shield* and *guide* — sealing both the exit and the entrance.

This is not a man describing the weather of the next world. This is a man holding up a mirror and saying: *look at what you are already doing. You are already turning away. You are already refusing the call. And the only thing missing is the moment when you find out it was real.*

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart, and about what it means to be loved by someone willing to tell you the truth.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Fear of Love — *"I fear FOR you"*

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There are two kinds of people in your life. There is the person who tells you what you want to hear — and there is the person who tells you what you *need* to hear. And here is the cruel thing about the human heart: in the moment, the flatterer feels like the friend, and the one who warns you feels like the enemy.

Think of the friend who watched you walk into something destructive — the relationship everyone could see was wrong, the habit that was eating you alive, the decision made in anger — and who *said something.* Who risked the friendship to say it. In the moment, you may have pulled away from them. You may have thought, *who are you to judge me?* And only later — sometimes years later — did you understand that of everyone around you, that person loved you the most. Because the others were comfortable letting you fall. Only that one cared enough to make you uncomfortable.

Now bring that into the throne room.

Look at the *exact* words this man chose: **إِنِّي أَخَافُ عَلَيْكُمْ** — *"I fear FOR you."*

Sit with that little preposition: **عَلَيْكُمْ** — *ʿalaykum,* "for you," "over you," "on your behalf." He did *not* say *akhāfu-kum* — *"I fear you."* He had every reason to fear *them*; they were about to become murderers and he was outing himself as a dissident. The rational fear, the survival fear, would have been *fear of them.*

But that is not the fear he names. The fear he names points the *opposite direction.* His fear is not crouched behind his own ribs, protecting himself. His fear is *standing out in front of them,* like a man throwing his arms wide to stop a crowd from walking off a ledge. **He is more afraid of what will happen to them than of what they will do to him.**

[PAUSE]

That is the precise anatomy of love. Not the warm feeling. The *direction of the fear.* Fear that has left your own body and gone to live in someone else's future.

And **al-Qurṭubī** captures the courage of it exactly: he says the man *increased in his exhortation and his warning, and made his faith plain* — either as one who had surrendered himself and made peace with being killed, or as one trusting that they would not actually harm him. Either way, the words came *out.* The secret of years broke open — not to save himself, but to warn the very people most likely to kill him for it.

Now turn the mirror around, because this is the part that should reach into your own life.

**Who in your life has feared *for* you — and you mistook it for them being against you?**

The parent whose worry felt like control. The teacher whose hard grade felt like cruelty. The friend whose question felt like an accusation. And — let us name the deepest one — **Allah Himself,** whose every prohibition is a *ʿalaykum,* a fear-for-you built into the shape of the command. Every *do not* in the Qurʾān is a man in a throne room throwing his arms wide. We experience the boundary as a restriction *on* us. It is, in truth, a fear *for* us. The same preposition.

There is a sound in this world that we are trained to flinch from: the sound of someone telling us a truth we would rather not hear. And these ayat are asking us to retrain that flinch. To learn to hear, underneath the warning, the *ʿalaykum.* To ask, when someone's words make us defensive: *is this person against me — or are they afraid for me?*

**What it changes about tomorrow:** The next time someone's honesty stings you — pause before you turn your back on them in contempt. Ask which preposition they are using. And the next time *you* love someone enough to be afraid for them — find the courage of this man. Say the hard thing. Say it as *yā qawmi* — *my people* — not from above them, but from inside the love. The flatterer is comfortable. The one who warns is brave. Be brave.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Nowhere to Run, No One to Shield — and the Mercy Hidden in the Terror

Let me start, again, in your own body.

Every human being is wired with a survival sequence. Threat appears, and before you can even think, your nervous system makes a decision: *fight, flight, or freeze.* And of those three, the oldest, the most animal, the most automatic, is **flight.** Run. Put distance between you and the danger. Your whole body is engineered around the assumption that *somewhere out there is a place the danger isn't.*

Almost every fear you have ever felt has had an exit built into it. The fear comes *with* a direction to run. That is what makes fear survivable — the body believes in escape.

Now read the ayah again: **يَوْمَ تُوَلُّونَ مُدْبِرِينَ** — *the Day you turn and flee.*

The instinct fires. Exactly as designed. The body does the only thing it has ever known to do with terror: it runs. And then — **مَا لَكُم مِّنَ اللَّهِ مِنْ عَاصِمٍ** — *there is no one to shield you from Allah.*

[PAUSE]

For the first time in the existence of that soul, the flight instinct meets a universe with **no exit.** The most basic promise the body ever made you — *there is always somewhere to run* — is revealed to have been, all along, only true of small dangers. The one Danger that matters has no *away.* You cannot flee *Allah,* because there is no location that is not in His grip. The running itself becomes the proof of the trap.

This is the terror these ayat hold up. And I do not want to soften it too quickly, because the man who said it did not soften it. He let his people feel the sealed room. He let them feel the stripping away of every security they had stacked their lives on — the protector, Pharaoh; the guide, the priesthood; the crowd, the court. *None of it is a ʿāṣim. None of it is a hādī.* Everything you outsourced your safety to — gone in a sentence.

But now — *now* — let me show you the mercy folded inside the terror, because it is the whole reason he is speaking.

He is describing this room with no exit **while the door is still open.**

He is telling them about the Day with no *ʿāṣim* and no *hādī* **at the exact moment when a *hādī* is standing in front of them, calling.** Don't you see it? *He* is the guide. His warning *is* the guidance. The terror of *fa-mā lahū min hād* — "there is no guide for him" — is being delivered *by a guide,* to people who still have time to follow one.

[PAUSE]

That is the secret architecture of this whole passage. The description of hopelessness is itself an act of hope. The map of the sealed room is being handed to you *before* you are inside it. A warning about a Day when warnings are useless — given on a day when it is still useful. **The very fact that these words can frighten you is the proof that it is not yet too late.** The fear of the sealed room, felt *now,* is the one thing that can keep you out of it.

So the stripping-away is real, but it has a question hidden inside it, and the question is mercy: *if the protector and the guide you have been leaning on cannot shield you from Allah — then who can? Who is the only One who can shield you from Allah?*

And the answer is the door this entire surah opens with. The surah is not named *al-ʿAdhāb,* the Punishment. It is named **Ghāfir** — *The Forgiver.* It opens: **غَافِرِ الذَّنبِ وَقَابِلِ التَّوْبِ** — *Forgiver of sin, Acceptor of repentance.* The only shelter from Allah **is Allah.** The only *ʿāṣim* from His justice is His mercy. The man is not driving them toward despair. He is driving them through the terror of every false shelter — so that they will run, at last, in the only direction where running *works:* not *away* from Allah, but *toward* Him.

**What it changes about tomorrow:** Make an honest inventory of your *ʿāṣims.* What are you actually trusting to shield you — your money, your status, your cleverness, the people who would vouch for you, your sheer ability to manage and control outcomes? Name them. Then ask of each one the ayah's question: *can this shield me from Allah?* Let the honest *no* do its work. Not to terrify you into paralysis — but to relocate your trust to the one Shelter that holds. Run today, while running still arrives somewhere.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at what these two ayat actually are, structurally — because the shape is the argument.

The passage moves in a perfect, tightening sequence:

> **A name** — *Yawm al-Tanād*, the Day of Mutual Calling. (The *sound* of that Day: voices.)
> **A motion** — *tuwallūna mudbirīn*, you turn and flee. (The *body* on that Day: running.)
> **A wall** — *mā lakum min ʿāṣim,* no shield. (The body's running hits the sealed room.)
> **A root cause** — *wa man yuḍlili-llāhu fa-mā lahū min hād,* and whoever He leaves astray has no guide. (Why the room was sealed: there was no guide.)

It runs *backward in time as it moves forward in the sentence.* It starts at the end — the calling, the fleeing — and walks you back to the beginning: *the missing guide.* It shows you the wreckage, then points to the cause. And the cause is the one thing you can still change today: whether or not you let a guide guide you.

### Bringing it together

See now how the language and the themes are one insight from two directions.

The **linguistics** showed us a man living the meaning of his own words: *calling* with no answer, watching people *turn away,* offering himself as a *shield* and a *guide* that they were free to refuse. The grammar of *ʿalaykum* — "for you" — was the grammar of love. The doubled *fleeing* was the futility of escape. The exhausting *min* of "not one shield, not one guide" sealed the room.

The **themes** showed us why he bothered. Because love points its fear outward — *I fear for you.* And because the description of a hopeless Day, spoken while hope remains, is the most merciful thing one human can do for another.

It is the same insight: **the one who loves you tells you the truth about the sealed room — while you are still standing in the doorway.**

### Questions to carry

- Who, in my life, has feared *for* me — and did I mistake their fear for an attack?
- When honesty stings me, do I ask whether the person is *against* me or *afraid for* me — or do I just turn my back?
- What am I currently trusting to be my *ʿāṣim* — my shield from consequence? Can it actually shield me from Allah?
- Is there someone *I* love enough to be afraid for — and have I been silent out of comfort, when I should be brave?
- The fact that this warning can still move me — am I grateful for what that proves: that the door is still open?

### One-sentence distillation

A man who loved his enemies enough to risk his life warned them of a Day when everyone will be calling and no one answering, when the instinct to flee meets a universe with no exit and no shield — and he did it while a guide still stood before them, because to be left without a guide is not the punishment that comes later; it is the punishment itself.

### The closing invitation

Do not let these ayat leave you only frightened. The fear is real, but it is not the point — it is the *means.* The point is the open door behind the fear. Let the terror of *no shield, no guide* do exactly one thing: send you running. Not away — there is no *away.* Send you running toward the only One named at the head of this very surah — *Ghāfir al-dhanb,* the Forgiver of sin. The voice warning you is, itself, the mercy. Answer it now, while the call still reaches its end.

### A brief duʿāʾ

*O Allah, Forgiver of sin and Acceptor of repentance — on the Day of Mutual Calling, when there is no shield but Your mercy and no guide but Your guidance, do not leave us to ourselves for the blink of an eye. You are the only ʿĀṣim; shelter us in You. You are the only Hādī; do not let us be among those who turn and flee. Make us hear, today, the voices that fear for us — and make us the kind who are brave enough to warn the ones we love.* Āmīn.
