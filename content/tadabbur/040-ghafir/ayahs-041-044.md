# The Man Who Feared for His Enemies

## Introduction: Stand in the Court

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand somewhere.

You are in a throne room in ancient Egypt. The most powerful man on earth sits before you. He has just said, out loud, in front of his assembled court, that he intends to kill Mūsā. *"Leave me to kill Mūsā,"* he says. The air is thick. To speak now is to mark yourself. To object is to die.

And in that room there is a man. We are told earlier in this very surah — ayah 28 — that he is *a believing man from the family of Pharaoh, who concealed his faith.* Read that again. **From the family of Pharaoh.** This is not an outsider. This is an insider — a man of rank, of access, of comfort. He has been a Muslim in secret, carrying his faith folded up inside him for who knows how long, surviving by silence.

And now the silence breaks.

He steps forward. And once he begins to speak, something extraordinary happens: he does not stop. His speech runs for verse after verse. He argues history. He argues logic. He warns them of the fate of ʿĀd and Thamūd. And then he arrives at the four verses we are sitting with today — and here the speech turns. It stops being an argument *at* them and becomes something almost tender. Something that breaks the heart.

[PAUSE]

Here is the thing I want you to notice before we even begin — the thing that should unsettle you.

**He is the one in danger.** He is the cornered one. He is the man whose next sentence could be his last. Every instinct of self-preservation should have his mind racing toward his own survival.

And what is he worried about?

*Them.*

> [ayah:40:41] ۞ وَيَـٰقَوْمِ مَا لِىٓ أَدْعُوكُمْ إِلَى ٱلنَّجَوٰةِ وَتَدْعُونَنِىٓ إِلَى ٱلنَّارِ
>
> *Wa yā qawmi mā lī adʿūkum ilā n-najāti wa tadʿūnanī ilā n-nār*
>
> "And O my people, how is it that I invite you to salvation while you invite me to the Fire?"

The man who should be afraid *for himself* is afraid *for the people who want him dead.*

Hold that. We are going to spend the next hour understanding how four short verses carry the entire psychology of a soul that has stopped grasping at the world — and what that has to do with you, tonight, with your own fears and your own outcomes.

Let me tease what's coming, and then we'll earn it slowly. By the end, the most quoted phrase in this passage — *"I entrust my affair to Allah"* — is not going to sound like a religious slogan. It's going to sound like the only sane thing a free human being can say.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Two Invitations — *adʿūkum* and *tadʿūnanī*

### First, a word about calling

Before we touch the verse, let me hand you the concept it's built on.

In Arabic there is a root — **dāl-ʿayn-wāw**, *daʿā* — and it is one of the most quietly important words in the entire Qur'an. We usually translate it "to call" or "to invite," but it's wider than both. *Daʿā* is what you do when you call out to someone. It's what you do when you invite a guest. It's what you do when you make duʿā' — when you call upon Allah. And it's what an ideology does when it *summons* you toward a way of life.

So hold this in your mind: *daʿwah* is a **pull**. It is a force exerted on a person to move them in a direction. Every *daʿwah* has a caller, a called, and a destination.

Now — here is a truth about your life that this root quietly exposes. **You are never not being called.** Right now, in this moment, a dozen forces are calling you somewhere. The phone in your pocket is a *daʿwah*. The ambition in your chest is a *daʿwah*. The crowd you want to belong to is a *daʿwah*. The lower self whispering "just this once" is a *daʿwah*. To be a human being is to stand at the center of competing invitations, all of them pulling.

Keep that. Now let's open the verse.

### Now look at what he says

Look at the grammar, because the grammar *is* the message.

> أَدْعُوكُمْ ... وَتَدْعُونَنِىٓ
>
> *adʿū-kum* ... *wa tadʿūna-nī*
>
> "I call **you** ... while you call **me**"

Two verbs from the same root, *daʿā*. But look at the pronouns. In the first, *I* am the caller and *you* are the called: **adʿū-kum**. In the second, the arrow reverses completely: *you* are the caller and *I* am the called: **tadʿūna-nī**.

Notice something. This isn't a man preaching from above. This is a man describing a **collision**. Two invitations traveling in opposite directions, passing each other in mid-air. He is being pulled by them in exactly the same instant that he is pulling at them.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's remarkable. The Arabic builds a perfect mirror — what the rhetoricians call *muqābalah*, balanced opposition:

- *adʿūkum* — I call you — **ilā n-najāh** — to salvation
- *tadʿūnanī* — you call me — **ilā n-nār** — to the Fire

Two callers. Two destinations. Let's sit with those destinations, because the choice of words is doing something we'd miss in English.

### The destination he offers them: *al-najāh*

The root is **nūn-jīm-wāw**, *najā* — to be saved, to escape, to be delivered. *Najāh* is not "paradise" here. He doesn't say *jannah*. He says **salvation** — *escape*. Rescue. Being pulled out of something.

Why does that word matter? Because *najāh* presumes danger. You only need rescue if you are in peril. By choosing this word, he is quietly telling them: **you are already in the water.** You are already drowning. I am not offering you a luxury. I am throwing you a rope.

### The destination they offer him: *al-nār*

And the root they're pulling him toward — **nūn-wāw-rā**, *al-nār* — the Fire.

Now listen to the two words side by side, in Arabic, because the Qur'an is making them rhyme on purpose:

**na-jāh** ... **nā-r**.

Both open with that same soft *nūn*. They almost sound like siblings. And that is the horror of it — that the path to rescue and the path to ruin can *sound so alike* at the entrance. The two invitations arrive in nearly the same packaging. The voice calling you to the Fire does not announce itself as fire. It, too, sounds like *na-*. It, too, promises something. The difference is not in the volume of the call. **The difference is in where it ends.**

[PAUSE]

This is the first thing this single mirrored line reveals: that the most consequential question of a human life is not *"Am I being called?"* — you always are — but *"Who is calling me, and where does this end?"*

A major pause here.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: "That of which I have no knowledge" — *mā laysa lī bihi ʿilm*

### Teach the idea first

Let me give you a concept before the verse hands it to you.

There is a difference between **belief** and **knowledge**. I can believe something with total emotional certainty and have zero warrant for it. People do this constantly. We inherit convictions. We absorb them from the room we grew up in. We hold them because everyone around us holds them, and the holding feels like knowing — but it isn't. It's *conjecture wearing the costume of certainty.*

The Qur'an has a precise vocabulary for this distinction. **ʿIlm** — knowledge, from ʿayn-lām-mīm — is warranted. It rests on evidence. Its opposite in this domain is *ẓann*, conjecture, and *hawā*, desire dressed as conviction.

Now watch how surgically the believing man uses this.

### Look at how he describes their invitation

He could have just said, "You're calling me to shirk." Instead, look at how he unfolds it:

> تَدْعُونَنِى لِأَكْفُرَ بِٱللَّهِ وَأُشْرِكَ بِهِۦ **مَا لَيْسَ لِى بِهِۦ عِلْمٌ**
>
> "You call me to disbelieve in Allah and to associate with Him **that of which I have no knowledge**."

Stay with that final phrase: *mā laysa lī bihi ʿilm* — "that for which I have no knowledge."

He does not refute their gods by listing their flaws. He does something far more devastating. He simply points at the **absence**. He says: you are asking me to stake my eternity on something I have *no evidence for whatsoever.* You want me to bow to a claim that has no ground under it.

Read the absence carefully — because the absence is the argument. He is not saying "your gods are weak." He is saying "**there is nothing here to even know.**" The object of their worship is so empty that the most precise thing you can say about it is that there is no knowledge of it to be had.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does. It reframes the entire encounter. They imagine themselves as the establishment — the sane majority, the inherited tradition, the safe consensus. And he, with five words, exposes the foundation under their feet as *air.* You, the majority, are the ones operating on conjecture. I, the lone man you want to kill, am the one standing on knowledge.

### Now the contrast lands

And against that emptiness, watch what he sets:

> وَأَنَا۠ أَدْعُوكُمْ إِلَى **ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْغَفَّٰرِ**
>
> "while I call you to **al-ʿAzīz, al-Ghaffār** — the Mighty, the All-Forgiving."

He doesn't call them to "a god." He calls them to a God he can *name* — and the two names he chooses are not random. Let's sit with them, because this pairing is the secret heart of the whole passage.

### Why *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* — and why these two together?

**Al-ʿAzīz** — from ʿayn-zāy-zāy — carries the sense of might, of unassailable strength, of one who cannot be overcome. **Al-Ghaffār** — from ghayn-fā-rā — is an intensive form (the *faʿʿāl* pattern, which Arabic uses for *abundance* and *repetition*): not merely the Forgiver, but the One who forgives *again and again and again,* whose forgiveness is a flood, not a trickle. *(This intensive sense of the faʿʿāl pattern is a standard, lexically attested feature of Arabic morphology.)*

Now ask: why pair these two, here, in this room?

Think about who he's talking to. He's talking to people drunk on the wrong kind of power — the power of Pharaoh, who has just claimed the right to kill. To people who worship *might*, he says: you want might? **Al-ʿAzīz.** Real might. The only Might that was never borrowed and can never be taken.

But here's the tenderness. He doesn't leave them with Might alone. He immediately pairs it with **al-Ghaffār.** And this is the genius of it. Because a person being *called* away from a lifetime of disbelief — a person standing in Pharaoh's court, hands stained with complicity — that person's deepest fear is not *"Is God strong?"* Their deepest fear is *"Even if I turned, would there be any way back for someone like me?"*

[PAUSE]

Look at the order. **Might, then Mercy.** He shows them a God strong enough that resistance is futile — *and then,* in the same breath, opens a door so wide that no past, however dark, is locked out. He is saying: the One I call you to is powerful enough to crush you, *and chooses instead to forgive you over and over.* There is no safer combination in existence than overwhelming power wedded to overwhelming mercy. **That** is who I'm inviting you to. And you — you want me to trade *Him* for something I can't even claim to know exists.

What this single pairing reveals: the difference between the two invitations is not the difference between two religions. It is the difference between **a named, knowable, forgiving Power** and **a nameless absence dressed up as tradition.**

Major pause.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Final Handover — *ufawwiḍu amrī ilā Allah*

### Teach the gesture first

There is a kind of action that is the hardest action a human being ever performs — and paradoxically it looks like *not* acting.

Imagine you've done everything. You've studied for the exam, sat it, handed in the paper. You've had the hard conversation. You've made the case. You've thrown the rope. And now — it is *out of your hands.* The outcome belongs to forces you cannot touch. What do you do with that gap, that terrible space between *I have done all I can* and *I do not control what happens next*?

Most of us fill it with anxiety. We re-grip. We lie awake re-running it. We try, in our minds, to keep controlling a thing that has already left our hands.

The Qur'an names a different option. And the believing man reaches for it in the very last thing he says.

### Look at the verb

> وَأُفَوِّضُ أَمْرِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ
>
> *wa ufawwiḍu amrī ilā Allah*
>
> "and I entrust my affair to Allah."

The verb is **ufawwiḍu** — from the root fā-wāw-ḍād. It is a Form II verb, *fawwaḍa*. *(Form II, the doubled-middle-radical pattern, characteristically intensifies or makes deliberate the base meaning — a standard, attested function of the form.)* And its meaning is precise: **to delegate completely. To hand over authority. To commission someone else to act on your behalf and accept whatever they decide.**

This is not "I hope Allah helps." This is the language of a *full transfer of authority.* In ordinary Arabic, *tafwīḍ* is what a ruler does when he delegates power to a deputy: *you decide; your decision is mine.* The believing man takes that very word and turns it toward Allah.

[PAUSE]

Now notice *when* he says it. Look at the position of this phrase. It comes at the **very end** of his speech — after the argument, after the warning, after the naming of the names. He says it *last.* Why?

Because *tafwīḍ* is what you do **when there is nothing left to do.** It is not an excuse to stop trying — he tried with everything he had, for verse after verse. It is the seal you press onto your effort once the effort is complete. First you exhaust your striving. *Then* you release the outcome. He has thrown the rope as hard as a human can throw it. And now, with the rope in the water and Pharaoh's executioners at his back, he says the only free thing left to say: *I hand the rest to the One in charge.*

### Look at what he hands over

And read *what* he entrusts. Not "my safety." Not "the result." He says **amrī** — *my affair.* My whole matter. The entirety of my situation, my fate, my self.

He doesn't negotiate. He doesn't say "Allah, get me out of this room alive." He hands over the **whole file** — open, unconditional — and accepts in advance whatever the verdict is. That is the difference between *tawakkul* as a bargaining chip and *tafwīḍ* as a surrender. He is not asking for a specific outcome. He is declaring that he no longer needs to specify it, because the One holding the file is *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* — the very names he just spoke.

[PAUSE]

### And the seal on the seal

Listen to the very last words of the passage:

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بَصِيرٌۢ بِٱلْعِبَادِ
>
> "Indeed, Allah is ever-Seeing of His servants."

He hands over his affair — and then explains *why he can.* Because the One he's handing it to is **baṣīr** — *seeing.* Not distant. Not inattentive. **Seeing.** He entrusts his case to a Judge who has watched every frame of it. Who saw the years of concealed faith. Who saw the moment of courage. Who sees the executioners. Who sees the hearts of the men in the room.

You can only truly hand your affair to someone who *sees.* You would never delegate your life to a blind authority. He releases his grip precisely *because* the eyes that now hold his fate miss nothing.

This is what the final verb reveals: that the deepest act of strength available to a powerless human being is the willing, total release of the outcome into the hands of the One who sees.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

So look at what these three features have shown us. The **two invitations** revealed that life is a field of competing calls, and the only question is where each one ends. The **"no knowledge"** revealed that the confident majority can be standing on nothing, while the lone believer stands on warrant. And the **final handover** revealed that faith ends not in a clenched fist but in an open hand.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how *you* are living, right now.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: You Are Always Being Invited — The Question Is the Destination

Start where you actually live.

You woke up this morning and before your feet hit the floor, you were already being called. The notification was a call. The algorithm that decided what you'd see first was a call — a *daʿwah* — engineered by people who studied exactly which words, which colors, which timing would pull you. You moved through your day inside an invisible weather of invitations: be afraid, buy this, resent them, compare yourself, scroll a little more, become this kind of person.

We tell ourselves we are choosing freely. But mostly we are *responding to calls* — and we rarely stop to ask the believing man's question: *where does this one end?*

[PAUSE]

Here is what his verse does to that fog. It cuts through it with a single distinction. Not "good call vs. bad call." Not even "religious vs. secular." Just: **najāh or nār. Does this call end in rescue, or in ruin?**

And remember the sound — *na-jāh, nā-r.* The calls arrive sounding alike. The Fire never sends an invitation labeled *Fire.* It sends one labeled *freedom,* or *success,* or *just being normal,* or *you deserve this.* The packaging at the door tells you almost nothing. Only the destination tells you the truth.

Think of how the worst directions in a human life are entered. Almost no one walks into ruin through a door marked ruin. They walk in through a door marked *connection,* or *relief,* or *belonging.* The believing man's people genuinely believed they were inviting him to *safety* — to the sensible, established, survivable choice. Conform, and live. They thought they were throwing *him* the rope.

That's the trap. The most dangerous invitations in your life are the ones that feel like rescue.

[PAUSE]

So how do you tell them apart, if they sound the same at the door?

The verse answers in its own structure. The believing man can name his destination — *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* — a knowable, specifiable end. The other call, he says, is *mā laysa lī bihi ʿilm* — toward something he can't even know. **A call you can't trace to a clear, examined destination is a call you should not answer.** When you can't articulate where something is actually taking you — when the honest answer to "where does this end?" is a shrug or a vague feeling — that vagueness is itself the warning.

What does this change about tomorrow? Just this. Tomorrow, when you feel the familiar pull — toward the phone, toward the resentment, toward the easy compromise — pause for one second and ask the believing man's question. Not "do I want this?" Wanting is easy to manufacture; that's what the call is *for.* Ask instead: **najāh or nār? Where, honestly, does this end?** That single question, asked even half the time, slowly rebuilds a person.

## Theme Two: The Open Hand — Doing Everything, Then Releasing Everything

Let's begin, again, where you live — in the gap.

You know the gap. It's the night before the result. It's after you've sent the message and you're staring at the screen. It's the waiting room. It's the moment after you've raised the child as well as you could and now they're walking out into a world you can't follow them into. The gap between *I have done everything I can* and *I cannot control what happens next.*

We are, most of us, terrible at the gap. We treat it as a problem to be solved by more gripping. We re-run the scenario. We lie awake. We try to control, in our minds, a thing that has already left our hands. And it exhausts us — because we are spending energy on the one part of reality we were never given power over.

[PAUSE]

Now look at where the believing man stands. His gap is the most extreme gap imaginable. He has spoken truth to a tyrant who has just announced a killing. The "result" he's waiting on is whether he himself lives or dies — and the verdict belongs entirely to men who hate him. If anyone had a right to spend the night re-gripping, it's him.

And what does he do? *Ufawwiḍu amrī ilā Allah.* I hand my whole affair over.

Let's be precise about what this is and isn't, because this is where people misunderstand faith entirely.

It is **not** passivity. He didn't *start* with surrender. He started with everything he had — the argument, the history, the warning, the naming of God, the courage of standing up in a room where standing up could kill him. *Tafwīḍ* is the **last** word, not the first. The open hand only means something *after* the hand has done all its work. A student who surrenders the result *without studying* isn't practicing tawakkul; he's practicing negligence. The believing man earns his release by first emptying himself of effort.

[PAUSE]

And it is **not** a bargain. Look again — he doesn't say "and so Allah will save me." He says *I entrust my affair* — and falls silent on the outcome. He genuinely lets go of *which* outcome. He accepts in advance that the verdict — life or death — is the One who sees making a call he will not second-guess. (And history records the cost: many narrations hold that he did not walk out of that confrontation safely. His *tafwīḍ* was real because it was unconditional. He released the outcome and the outcome was hard, and the release was still right.)

This is the paradox the verse asks us to sit with, and not resolve too quickly: **the freest a human being ever becomes is the moment he stops trying to control what was never his to control.** The gripping feels like power. It is actually the opposite — it's the posture of someone who believes the whole weight rests on his fingers. The open hand looks like weakness. It is actually the posture of someone strong enough to admit there is a Power that sees, and great enough, to carry what he cannot.

[PAUSE]

And notice the foundation underneath the release — *innā Allāha baṣīrun bil-ʿibād.* He can only let go because the One he lets go *to* sees everything. This is the difference between *tafwīḍ* and despair. Despair says "it's out of my hands, so it's lost." *Tafwīḍ* says "it's out of my hands, and into the hands of the One who has watched every moment, who missed nothing, who knows the whole of my affair better than I do." You are not dropping your file into a void. You are placing it before the eyes of *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* — Might that can do anything, Mercy that wants to forgive.

What does this change about tomorrow? When you reach the edge of your effort — and you'll know the edge, it's the point where more thinking produces only more dread, not more action — practice the believing man's sequence. First, honestly ask: *have I done what was actually mine to do?* If not, the cure isn't surrender; it's effort. But if yes — if you've genuinely thrown the rope — then say his words and *mean the release in them.* "I entrust my affair to Allah." And then let your hand actually open. Not as a slogan. As a real handing-over to Eyes that see what yours cannot.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Now step back and look at the whole shape of what this man said, because the four verses are built like a single descending staircase, and we can only see it now that we've walked each step.

He begins with the **collision** — two invitations crossing in mid-air (v.41).
He sharpens it into the **content** — what each call actually contains: emptiness on one side, the Named God on the other (v.42).
He delivers the **verdict** — *lā jarama,* without a doubt, your call leads nowhere and ours leads to the Return (v.43).
And he ends in the **release** — having said everything, he opens his hand (v.44).

Argument → content → verdict → surrender. He talks himself, and us, all the way down from the noise of competing voices to the silence of a single trust. And the same two divine names hold up both ends: he *invites* them to *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* in verse 42, and he *entrusts himself* to that same seeing God in verse 44. The God he calls them toward is the God he falls back into. **The invitation and the surrender are the same act, pointed in two directions** — outward to them, upward to Allah.

## Bringing It Together

Our linguistic discoveries and our two themes are one insight seen from two sides.

The two invitations and the "no knowledge" taught us to *interrogate the calls* — to refuse any pull whose destination we cannot name. The final handover taught us to *release the outcome* — to stop gripping what was never ours. And these are not two lessons. They are one posture. **The person who has learned to ask "where does this end?" is the same person who has learned to open their hand** — because both come from the same root: a soul that has stopped pretending it is the one in charge, and has located the real Power, named Him, and entrusted itself to Him.

The man feared for his enemies, and not for himself, for exactly this reason. He had already handed his own affair to the One who sees. With his own fate released, the only thing left to fear *for* was them.

## Questions to Carry

1. Of all the invitations you answered today — the scrolls, the spends, the resentments, the small compromises — how many could you honestly trace to a destination you'd be willing to name out loud?

2. Where in your life are you confidently certain of something you have never actually examined — a *mā laysa laka bihi ʿilm* you've inherited and never tested?

3. What are you currently gripping that has already left your hands — re-running, re-worrying, trying to control after the effort is done?

4. Is your version of "trust in Allah" the believing man's — surrender *after* full effort — or is it sometimes surrender used as a substitute *for* effort?

5. If you truly believed that the One holding your hardest situation is *baṣīr* — that He has watched every frame and missed nothing — what would you be able to set down tonight?

## One-Sentence Distillation

When you have thrown the rope with everything in you and the outcome is no longer yours, the believer's last and freest act is not to grip harder but to name the One who sees and hand Him the whole of your affair.

## The Closing Invitation

You don't have to fix your whole relationship with control tonight. Start with one open hand. Take the single situation you've been gripping in the dark — the one you re-run when you should be sleeping — and try, just once, saying his words and *meaning the release in them*: *ufawwiḍu amrī ilā Allah.* Not to stop caring. Not to stop trying where trying is still yours to do. But to finally let down the part of the weight you were never built to carry — and to place it, deliberately, before Eyes that have never once looked away from you.

## A Brief Duʿā'

*O Allah — al-ʿAzīz, al-Ghaffār — You who are mighty enough to settle every affair and merciful enough to forgive us again and again: teach us to hear where each call is taking us before we answer it. And when we have done all that was ours to do, give us the believing man's open hand — let us entrust our whole affair to You, certain that You are Seeing of Your servants, and that nothing we surrender to You is ever lost.*

*Āmīn.*
