# Leave Me to Kill Him: The Fear Beneath the Throne and the Refuge Beyond It

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first to the words as they were spoken. Two men. One sentence each. Spoken across a throne room thick with tension.

> **وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ ذَرُونِىٓ أَقْتُلْ مُوسَىٰ وَلْيَدْعُ رَبَّهُۥٓ ۖ إِنِّىٓ أَخَافُ أَن يُبَدِّلَ دِينَكُمْ أَوْ أَن يُظْهِرَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ٱلْفَسَادَ**
>
> *Wa qāla Firʿawnu dharūnī aqtul Mūsā wal-yadʿu rabbah. Innī akhāfu an yubaddila dīnakum aw an yuẓhira fī-l-arḍi-l-fasād.*
>
> "And Pharaoh said, 'Leave me to kill Moses, and let him call upon his Lord. Indeed, I fear that he will change your religion or that he will cause corruption to appear in the land.'"

And then, immediately, the answer:

> **وَقَالَ مُوسَىٰٓ إِنِّى عُذْتُ بِرَبِّى وَرَبِّكُم مِّن كُلِّ مُتَكَبِّرٍ لَّا يُؤْمِنُ بِيَوْمِ ٱلْحِسَابِ**
>
> *Wa qāla Mūsā innī ʿudhtu bi-rabbī wa rabbikum min kulli mutakabbirin lā yuʾminu bi-yawmi-l-ḥisāb.*
>
> "And Moses said, 'Indeed, I have sought refuge in my Lord and your Lord from every arrogant one who does not believe in the Day of Account.'"

### Rebuild the world

Picture where we are. Egypt is the most organized power on earth. Pharaoh is not merely its king — he has told his people, in this very Quran, *anā rabbukumu-l-aʿlā*, "I am your lord, the highest" (79:24). He has built the architecture of a god: the temples, the obelisks, the court that bows. And to a man like this comes a former member of his own household — Moses — raised in the palace, fled into the desert, returned now with a staff and a message and a brother, saying: *there is a Lord above you, and you will answer to Him.*

By the time we reach these verses, the court has already made up its mind. They have called Moses a sorcerer, a liar. The decree to slaughter the sons of the believers has been given. And yet — and this is the strange thing you must hold onto — Moses is still alive. The man who claims to be the highest lord, who commands armies and rivers and the lives of infants, has not killed the one man who threatens him.

Why not? What is stopping the god-king from swatting away a single shepherd?

That question is the door into everything here.

### The Hook

Read Pharaoh's sentence again and notice something almost no one notices on the first hearing. The most powerful man alive opens his threat with a request for **permission**. *Leave me.* And he closes it with a confession: *I fear.*

The god is asking. The god is afraid.

Stay with that. Beneath the throne, beneath the title, beneath *anā rabbukumu-l-aʿlā* — there is a frightened man asking the people around him to let him do something. And in the very next breath, a man with nothing but his Lord answers a death threat not with a counter-threat, but with a single word that has protected the weak for all of human history.

Let's go slowly. There is more in these two sentences than in most chapters of most books.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Dharūnī* — "Leave me" — the god who must ask permission

Begin with the very first word out of Pharaoh's mouth: **ذَرُونِىٓ** — *dharūnī*.

Before we open it, let me teach you the shape of the word. In Arabic, when you want to command someone, you have a grammatical form for it — the imperative. *Iftaḥ* — open. *Ijlis* — sit. And there is a particular construction, an everyday one, where you command someone to *let you* do something. In English we do it too. A child reaching for a task says, "Let me." A frustrated worker says, "Just let me handle it." The phrase *let me* is fascinating, because it is a command wrapped around an admission. To say "let me" is to admit that you need someone else to step back before you can act. You cannot say "let me" to someone you have total power over. You only say it to someone whose cooperation, or whose restraint, you require.

Now — the word itself. *Dharūnī* comes from the root **w-dh-r** (و ذ ر), which carries the meaning *to leave alone, to let be, to step aside from*. It is in the imperative, addressed to a group — the *-ū* ending is plural, *you all* — and the *-nī* at the end is *me*. So the literal force is: *"You all, leave me [to it]."* Pharaoh is speaking to his court, his nobles, his inner circle.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the weight of this. The classical commentators — al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, and the others — stop precisely here and ask the obvious question: **why would Pharaoh need to say "let me" to anyone?** He is the supreme authority. He kills infants by decree. Who is stopping him from killing one grown man?

And their answer is stunning in its quiet realism. Al-Baghawi explains that *among Pharaoh's own close people there were those who restrained him from killing Moses, out of fear of destruction* — fear that harming Moses might bring down ruin. Al-Qurtubi preserves a tradition that someone had said to Pharaoh, in effect, *we are afraid that if you kill him, his Lord might answer his prayer against us.* In other words, even inside the god-king's own court, there were people more cautious than he was, people quietly holding his hand back.

So the supreme lord of Egypt opens his great threat with **a request to his own subordinates to please get out of his way.**

Notice what the grammar will not let Pharaoh hide. He cannot simply say *aqtulu Mūsā*, "I am killing Moses." He has to first negotiate the room. *Dharūnī* — let me. The word is a confession smuggled inside a command. The most powerful man on earth is not, in this moment, free. He is constrained — by the caution of his own advisors, by the unease in his own court, and, though he would never say it, by something he cannot name.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this single word reveals: **absolute power is a costume.** The man who has told everyone he is the highest lord cannot, in the decisive moment, simply act. He must ask. And the asking gives him away. A real god does not say *dharūnī*. Only a man does — a man surrounded by other men, dependent on their permission, performing a sovereignty he does not actually possess.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: *Innī akhāfu* — "Indeed, I fear" — the confession at the center of the throne

Now move to the middle of Pharaoh's sentence, to the words that should be impossible for him to say.

**إِنِّىٓ أَخَافُ** — *innī akhāfu* — "Indeed, I fear."

Let me first set the grammatical frame, because it matters. *Innī* opens with *inna*, the particle of emphasis — the Arabic way of underlining a statement, of saying *truly, indeed, without doubt*. It is the construction you reach for when you want to remove all hesitation from what comes next. And what comes next? *Akhāfu* — from the root **kh-w-f** (خ و ف), *fear*. It is in the present-tense form, the *muḍāriʿ*, which in Arabic carries the sense of something ongoing, continuous, not finished. Not "I felt a fear once." But *I fear* — right now, and continuing.

So put the two together: Pharaoh does not merely admit fear. He *emphasizes* it. He underlines it. **"Truly, I — I fear."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the impossibility of this. This is the same man who said *anā rabbukumu-l-aʿlā*, "I am your lord, the highest." A lord does not fear. A god does not fear. Fear is the condition of the creature — the small, the exposed, the contingent thing that can be harmed. To fear is to confess that there is something out there bigger than you, something that can reach you, something you do not control.

And Pharaoh says it of his own mouth, with emphasis: *innī akhāfu.*

Here is what is so devastating, and so true to life. Watch the *order* of Pharaoh's sentence. First the bravado — *leave me to kill Moses, and let him call his Lord* — the swagger, the mockery, the performance of supreme confidence. And then, immediately, the thing the swagger was covering: *indeed, I fear.* The threat and the fear are in the same breath. They are not two separate moods. **The threat is made of the fear.** He wants to kill Moses *because* he is afraid of Moses. The violence is not strength overflowing. The violence is fear, looking for an exit.

This is the anatomy of tyranny laid bare in two words. The tyrant is not a strong man indulging his power. The tyrant is a frightened man who has discovered that frightening others quiets, for a moment, the fear inside himself. *Innī akhāfu* is the most honest thing Pharaoh ever says. And it is the key to him. Pull on that thread and the whole god-costume unravels — the *dharūnī*, the death decree, the temples, all of it. It is all *innī akhāfu* wearing a crown.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Yubaddila... yuẓhira-l-fasād* — the corrupter calls the reformer corrupt

Now look at what, precisely, Pharaoh claims to fear. This is where the language turns the whole scene inside out.

He fears two things. First: **أَن يُبَدِّلَ دِينَكُمْ** — *an yubaddila dīnakum* — "that he will change your religion." The verb *yubaddila* is from the root **b-d-l** (ب د ل), *to substitute, to exchange one thing for another*. And it is in the intensive form — what the grammarians call Form II, where the doubling of the middle letter intensifies the action. Not a light adjustment. A thorough swapping-out, an exchange of one whole thing for another. Pharaoh fears Moses will *replace* the people's *dīn*.

Second: **أَوْ أَن يُظْهِرَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ٱلْفَسَادَ** — *aw an yuẓhira fī-l-arḍi-l-fasād* — "or that he will cause corruption to appear in the land." *Yuẓhira* is from the root **ẓ-h-r** (ظ ه ر), *to appear, to become manifest*, here in the causative form — *to make appear, to bring forth into the open*. And the thing he fears being brought forth is **الْفَسَادَ** — *al-fasād*, from **f-s-d** (ف س د) — corruption, ruin, the breaking-apart of what should hold together.

[PAUSE]

Now. Stop everything and hold this up against what you already know is true about Pharaoh, from the Quran's own testimony elsewhere. In Surah al-Qaṣaṣ, Allah's verdict on this very man is: *inna Firʿawna ʿalā fī-l-arḍ* — "Pharaoh exalted himself in the land" — *wa jaʿala ahlahā shiyaʿan* — "and made its people into factions, oppressing a group among them, slaughtering their sons" — and then the seal: *innahu kāna mina-l-mufsidīn* — **"indeed, he was among the corrupters"** (28:4). The same root. *Mufsidīn* — corrupters — from *fasād*.

Do you see what has happened in the language?

**The corrupter is standing in his own court, pointing at the one righteous man in the room, and accusing *him* of bringing corruption to the land.**

The actual *mufsid* — the one who divided the people, who enslaved a nation, who ordered the murder of infants — looks at Moses, who has come to free the enslaved and end the killing, and says: *I fear he will spread fasād.* The man drowning the land in corruption frames the man trying to drain it.

This is not a small rhetorical trick. This is one of the oldest and darkest patterns in the human heart, and the Quran captures it in a single verb. The wrongdoer does not merely do wrong. He must *rename* the wrong, and he renames it by **projecting it onto the one who opposes him.** The reformer becomes the radical. The truth-teller becomes the troublemaker. The one calling people back to their Lord becomes the one accused of "changing your religion" and "spreading corruption."

Notice, too, what Pharaoh calls the people's idolatry: *dīnakum* — *your religion*. He dresses the worship of himself in the language of tradition, heritage, stability — *the way of your fathers, the order of the land.* And he casts the worship of the One true God as the threat to all of it. The lie is total: corruption is renamed order, and the call to the Creator is renamed corruption.

[PAUSE]

Here is what these verbs reveal, taken together: **the tongue of the oppressor always inverts reality.** He does not say "I am afraid Moses will free the people I enslaved." He says "I fear he will corrupt the land *I* am corrupting." The accusation is a mirror, and the face in it is Pharaoh's own.

### The bridge

So look at what the language of Pharaoh's single sentence has shown us. A god who must say *let me*. A lord who confesses *I fear*. A corrupter who calls the reformer corrupt. The grammar has quietly dismantled the throne from the inside — revealed the frightened, dependent, self-deceiving man behind *anā rabbukumu-l-aʿlā*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about Pharaoh's words — but about the structure of reality itself. Because directly across from this frightened man stands another man, who answers a death threat with a single, ancient word. And the contrast between them is the whole point.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Fear is the engine of cruelty — and projection is its disguise

Let's begin where you live, not where Pharaoh lived.

You have met this dynamic. Maybe not in a throne room, but in an office, a family, a friendship. There is a particular kind of person who, the more insecure they feel, the more they need to control and diminish the people around them. The manager who feels his own incompetence and so humiliates his team. The parent terrified of losing authority who tightens every rule until the home cannot breathe. The friend who, threatened by your growth, quietly works to make you smaller. And here is the part that confuses everyone who has been on the receiving end of it: **the cruelty almost never admits it is fear.** It dresses itself as principle. "I'm doing this for your own good." "Someone has to maintain order." "You're the one causing problems here."

If you have ever been the target of that and walked away thinking *am I crazy? am I actually the problem?* — then you already understand Pharaoh's sentence from the inside. Because that confusion, that vertigo, is the *intended effect* of projection. The corrupter calls you the corrupter precisely so that you will doubt your own eyes.

Now let the ayah illuminate what you have lived.

The Quran does something here that is almost merciful in its honesty: it puts the fear and the cruelty in the same sentence, where Pharaoh himself tried to keep them apart. *Leave me to kill Moses* — and — *indeed, I fear.* The Quran refuses to let us see the threat without seeing its root. It is telling us: **whenever you see someone reaching to destroy, look underneath, and you will find a frightened person.** Not to excuse the cruelty — Pharaoh is fully condemned — but to *understand* it, and in understanding it, to stop being mystified by it.

This changes how you read power. The world teaches you to fear the strong and to read aggression as strength. The Quran teaches you to read aggression as *fear in motion*. The one who must dominate is the one who is terrified of what happens if he does not. The drowning man does not hold you under the water because he is strong. He holds you under because he is drowning.

[PAUSE]

And there is a deeper layer still. Why was Pharaoh afraid? Strip away the politics. At the very bottom, what did Moses threaten? Moses threatened Pharaoh with *the truth that Pharaoh was not God.* That is the whole fear. Every tyrant's deepest terror is the same: the exposure of the lie that he is more than he is. The temples, the armies, the death decrees — these are not the actions of a being who is secure in his divinity. They are the frantic labor of a man trying to silence the small voice that whispers: *you are a creature, you will die, you will be questioned.* Moses is the embodiment of that voice walking into the throne room. Of course Pharaoh wants to kill him. He is not trying to kill a man. He is trying to kill a truth.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change for you, tomorrow?

Two things. First, when you are the *target* of someone's projection — when you are being told you are the problem by the person who is the problem — let this ayah steady you. Reality is not decided by who speaks with the most authority in the room. Pharaoh had the throne, the court, the title, and he was lying. Moses had nothing and was telling the truth. Do not let volume and position rewrite what you can see. The corrupter calling you corrupt does not make you corrupt.

And second — the harder mirror — turn it on yourself. The next time *you* feel the urge to control, to diminish, to lash out, to "put someone in their place," stop and ask the Pharaoh question: *what am I afraid of?* Because almost always, underneath the urge to make someone smaller is a fear of being small ourselves. Naming the fear is how you refuse to become the thing in this ayah. Pharaoh could not say *I am afraid* without immediately reaching for a knife. The believer learns to say *I am afraid* — and reach for his Lord instead. Which is exactly what happens in the next breath.

### Theme Two: *ʿUdhtu* — refuge as the answer to the threat

Now we cross the room to Moses. And here is where these two verses become one of the most perfect pairings in the Quran.

Let me first place a word in your hand, a word you already half-know. **عُذْتُ** — *ʿudhtu* — "I have sought refuge." It comes from the root **ʿ-w-dh** (ع و ذ), and you have met this root your whole life, perhaps without realizing it. Every time you say *aʿūdhu billāh* — "I seek refuge in Allah" — you are using it. Every time you recite the last two surahs of the Quran, *Qul aʿūdhu bi-rabbi-l-falaq* and *Qul aʿūdhu bi-rabbi-n-nās* — "Say: I seek refuge in the Lord of the daybreak... the Lord of mankind" — you are standing exactly where Moses stands in this verse. *Istiʿādha* — seeking refuge — is the soul running to shelter.

Now teach yourself the everyday shape of it before you feel its weight here. To seek refuge is what a small animal does when the hawk's shadow crosses the field — it does not turn to fight the hawk, it *runs to the rock*, into the cleft where the hawk cannot follow. Refuge is not the strength of the threatened thing. Refuge is the strength of the *shelter* the threatened thing runs to. The rabbit is weak. The rock is not. And the whole safety of the rabbit is in knowing the difference and running.

[PAUSE]

Now feel three things in Moses's word, each one a small earthquake.

**First: the tense.** Moses does not say *I will seek refuge*, as if the threat has just begun and he is now scrambling for a plan. He says *ʿudhtu* — past tense — **"I have *already* taken refuge."** It is done. Before Pharaoh ever opened his mouth, before the threat was ever uttered, Moses was already inside the shelter. Pharaoh is still in the *dharūnī* — still negotiating with his court, still asking permission, still arranging the conditions under which he might act. Moses has nothing left to arrange. The refuge was taken long ago. While Pharaoh is gathering his courage, Moses has already arrived.

[PAUSE]

**Second: what Moses does *not* do.** A death threat has just been spoken across the room. *Leave me to kill Moses.* And Moses does not threaten back. He does not say "and I will call my Lord *against* you," even though Pharaoh practically dared him to — *wal-yadʿu rabbah*, "let him call his Lord," Pharaoh sneered, mockingly inviting Moses to try his prayers. Moses does not take the bait of the curse. He takes refuge instead. He turns away from the contest of threats entirely and turns toward his Lord. This is the believer's instinct in its purest form: **when threatened, the believer's first motion is not toward the enemy but toward Allah.** Not *what will I do to him* but *with Whom have I taken shelter.*

[PAUSE]

**Third — and this is the most beautiful thing in the whole pairing — listen to *whose* Lord Moses takes refuge in.** *ʿudhtu bi-rabbī **wa rabbikum*** — "I have sought refuge in my Lord **and your Lord.**"

Go back to Pharaoh's mockery one verse earlier. Pharaoh said *wal-yadʿu **rabbah*** — "let him call **his** Lord." Do you hear what Pharaoh did with that little pronoun? He made the Lord *Moses's* private possession — a far-off, foreign, personal deity, *his* God, nothing to do with us, let him go call on it and see if it answers. He pushed the Lord away to the edge of the room, made Him remote, made Him *somebody else's*.

And Moses, in his answer, reaches out and pulls that very pronoun back into the center. *My Lord **and yours.*** No, Pharaoh — He is not only my Lord. He is *your* Lord too. The One you mock as "his Lord" is the One who made you, the One who is right now keeping your heart beating, the One before whom you will stand. You can deny Him with your tongue and still be utterly inside His grip. Moses, facing death, takes the time to correct Pharaoh's theology — not out of debate, but out of mercy. He will not let Pharaoh's lie stand even in a pronoun.

[PAUSE]

And from *whom* does Moses seek refuge? Not "from you, Pharaoh." Listen: **مِن كُلِّ مُتَكَبِّرٍ** — *min kulli mutakabbir* — "from **every** arrogant one." Indefinite. Universal. Moses does not name Pharaoh. He describes a *type*. And the word he chooses is exact: *mutakabbir*, from the root **k-b-r** (ك ب ر), in the reflexive form — the form that means *to make oneself big.* A *mutakabbir* is not someone who is great. He is someone who *makes himself* great, who inflates himself, who claims a size he does not have. And who in all of history has more literally made himself big than the man who said *anā rabbukumu-l-aʿlā*, "I am your lord, the highest"?

Moses lands the description perfectly on Pharaoh's chest without ever saying his name — and by leaving it general, he also hands the verse to you and me, and to every soul that will ever face a *mutakabbir* of its own. The shelter Moses ran to is open to all of us, against all of them.

And finally, the root of the arrogance, named in the last words: **لَّا يُؤْمِنُ بِيَوْمِ ٱلْحِسَابِ** — *lā yuʾminu bi-yawmi-l-ḥisāb* — "who does not believe in the Day of Account." There it is. The disease beneath the swagger. The arrogant one is arrogant because, deep down, *he does not believe he will ever be questioned.* Strip away the belief in the Day of Reckoning, and there is nothing left to make a powerful man bow. Pharaoh can play god precisely because he has decided there is no Day when the accounts come due. Moses, taking refuge, names the cure in the same breath as the disease: there *is* a Day. There *is* a reckoning. And the One who runs it is your Lord, Pharaoh, whether you call Him "his Lord" or not.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change for you, tomorrow?

When the threat comes — and in some form it will: the diagnosis, the layoff, the cruelty of someone with power over you, the fear that arrives at three in the morning — notice your first motion. The world's instinct is to scramble, to strategize against the threat, to meet the curse with a curse. Moses teaches a different first motion: *ʿudhtu.* Run to the rock first. Take the refuge before you do anything else. And the most practical thing in this entire reflection is this: *you already have the words.* *Aʿūdhu billāh.* They are not a magic phrase to mutter. They are Moses's exact posture, handed to you — the past-tense, already-sheltered, turn-toward-the-Lord-first posture of a man who looked death in the eye and answered it with refuge instead of fear.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now stand back and look at the two verses as a built thing, because the architecture is the proof of everything we've seen.

Both verses open with the **identical verb**: *wa qāla* — "and he said." *Wa qāla Firʿawn.* *Wa qāla Mūsā.* Two men, the same doorway, two opposite worlds walking through it.

Watch how every element answers its mirror across the gap:

- Pharaoh must say **"leave me"** — he needs permission to act. Moses says **"I have taken refuge"** — he needs no one's permission; the deed is already done in his Lord.
- Pharaoh **"fears"** — *akhāfu* — and his fear drives him toward a *killing*. Moses faces a killing and is **unafraid**, because he has *already fled to safety*.
- Pharaoh says **"his Lord"** — *rabbah* — pushing God to the far edge as a foreign possession. Moses says **"my Lord and your Lord"** — *rabbī wa rabbikum* — pulling God back to the center as the shared Sovereign of them both.
- Pharaoh fears corruption **"in the land"** — *fī-l-arḍ* — his entire horizon ends at the earth, the now, the kingdom he can see. Moses points to **"the Day of Account"** — *yawmi-l-ḥisāb* — his horizon runs past the earth, past death, to the reckoning Pharaoh refuses to believe in.

Do you see it? **Pharaoh's whole world is bounded by *al-arḍ*, the earth. Moses's world opens onto *yawm al-ḥisāb*, the Day.** The tyrant's vision stops at the edge of what he can grip; that is *why* he grips so hard. The believer's vision passes through the grave to the Court beyond it; that is *why* he can let go. Pharaoh's last word is *al-fasād* — corruption, ruin, the breaking-apart. Moses's last word is *al-ḥisāb* — the accounting, the setting-right. Two men. Two final words. Two ends of the universe.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the same insight seen from two directions.

From the language: a god who says *let me*, a lord who says *I fear*, a corrupter who calls the reformer corrupt. From the themes: cruelty is fear in motion, and projection is its disguise; while refuge is the believer's first motion, taken in advance, in the shared Lord, against every self-inflated soul that has forgotten the reckoning.

They meet in one place. **Pharaoh's power was a performance over an abyss of fear, and Moses's apparent powerlessness was a fortress.** The man on the throne was the trapped one. The man under the threat was the free one. And the only difference between them — the entire difference — was *whom* each had taken as his Lord, and whether he believed in the Day he would have to answer for it.

### Questions to carry

1. When someone with power over me tells me I am the problem, do I test their claim against what I can actually see — or do I let their authority rewrite my own eyes?
2. The next time I feel the urge to control, to diminish, to "put someone in their place" — can I stop and ask Pharaoh's buried question: *what am I actually afraid of?*
3. When a threat arrives in my life, what is my *first* motion — toward the enemy, or toward my Lord?
4. Do I live as though there is a *yawm al-ḥisāb*, a Day of Account — or, in the parts of my life I keep hidden, do I quietly live like one who *lā yuʾminu bi-yawmi-l-ḥisāb*?
5. Is there a "his Lord" in my own speech — a place where I have pushed Allah to the edge, made Him distant, someone else's concern — that I need to pull back to the center as *my* Lord and *the* Lord?

### One-sentence distillation

The tyrant who claims to be a god must beg permission and confess his fear, while the man he threatens has already fled to the shared Lord of them both — proving that real power is not the throne you sit on but the Refuge you run to.

### The closing invitation

You will not meet Pharaoh. But you will meet his fear in others, and — if you are honest — its smaller cousins in yourself. And you will, certainly, meet moments when the shadow of the hawk crosses your field. Let these two verses teach you which man to be. Not the one negotiating his courage in a room full of advisors, eyes fixed on the earth. The one who has already taken refuge, eyes lifted to the Day. The words are already in your mouth. *Aʿūdhu billāh.* You do not have to invent the shelter. You only have to run to it — and, like Moses, run *first.*

### A brief duʿā

O Allah, my Lord and the Lord of all who deny You — I seek refuge in You, as Your servant Moses did, from every arrogant soul that has forgotten Your Day, and from the arrogance and the fear that hide in my own heart. Make my first motion when I am threatened a turning toward You, and let my horizon never stop at this earth, but open always onto the Day I will stand before You and account for it all. Āmīn.
