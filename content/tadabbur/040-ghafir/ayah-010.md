# The Two Callings: When the One Who Invited You Near Now Proclaims to You From Afar

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓاْ يُنَادَوْنَ لَمَقْتُ ٱللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ مِن مَّقْتِكُمْ أَنفُسَكُمْ إِذْ تُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلْإِيمَـٰنِ فَتَكْفُرُونَ
>
> *Inna alladhīna kafarū yunādawna lamaqtu-llāhi akbaru min maqtikum anfusakum idh tudʿawna ilā al-īmāni fa-takfurūn*
>
> "Indeed, those who disbelieved will be cried out to: *Allah's loathing of you was greater than your loathing now of yourselves — when you were called to faith, and you refused.*"

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

I want you to begin not in the Hereafter, but in a feeling you already know.

There is a particular kind of pain that has no external cause. No one is attacking you. Nothing has been taken from you. You are simply sitting with yourself — and you cannot stand the person you are sitting with. You replay a decision. You see, with a clarity that arrived too late, exactly what you should have done. And something inside you turns against you. You hate the choice. And then, quietly, you begin to hate the chooser.

We have many polite words for this. We call it regret. We call it self-criticism. The therapists call it shame. But strip the politeness away and you find something rawer underneath — a self turned against itself, a person who has become their own enemy.

Now hold that feeling. Because the ayah we are about to enter takes that feeling — the worst feeling a human being can have, the feeling of loathing your own self — and it does something almost unbearable with it.

[PAUSE]

Let me set the scene the Qur'an is painting.

Surah Ghafir — Surah 40 — opens with one of the most balanced portraits of Allah in the entire Qur'an. In the third ayah He names Himself: *Ghāfir al-dhanb* — the Forgiver of sin. *Qābil al-tawb* — the Accepter of repentance. *Shadīd al-ʿiqāb* — Severe in punishment. *Dhī al-ṭawl* — the Possessor of abundance. Forgiveness and severity, held in the same breath. This surah lives in the tension between the open hand and the closing door.

And then, just a few verses in, we are shown what happens when the open hand is refused for an entire lifetime.

The scene is the edge of the Fire. The people who spent their lives turning away have arrived. And the very first thing the Qur'an tells us they do is not scream at the angels, not curse their leaders — that comes later. The first thing is internal. *They loathe themselves.* The self-hatred we just described — the worst feeling a human can carry — they are now drowning in it, fully, with no distraction left to numb it.

And in that exact moment, a voice calls out to them.

Here is the hook. The voice does not say, "You deserve this." The voice does not say, "Now you understand." The voice says something far stranger, something that reaches all the way back across their entire life. It says: *whatever you are feeling right now — this self-loathing that is consuming you — it is the smaller of two loathings. There was a greater one. And it was happening the whole time you were being invited home.*

What was that greater loathing? Whose was it? And why are they only being told about it now?

Stay with me. Because the answer is hidden in two words for "calling" that most translations flatten into one — and in a single word for "hatred" that Arabic reserved for the most contemptible thing a human being can do.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Maqt* — The Hatred That Is Not Ordinary Hatred

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah.

Arabic does not have one word for hatred. It has a family of them, and they are not interchangeable. There is *kurh* — dislike, aversion, the thing you'd rather not do. There is *bughḍ* — the standard, general word for hatred, the opposite of love. These are common words. You hear them constantly.

And then there is a third word, a rarer and heavier word. *Maqt*.

Think of it this way. In English, "dislike," "hatred," and "disgust" are not the same temperature. You can hate a person and still respect them. But disgust — *revulsion* — carries something extra. It carries contempt. It says: this is not just something I am against; this is something *beneath* contempt, something that turns the stomach.

That is *maqt*.

[PAUSE]

The lexicons are precise here. *Maqt*, in classical Arabic, is the most intense form of hatred, and — this is the crucial part — it is hatred *directed at something blameworthy*. It is not arbitrary dislike. It is the loathing you feel toward an act so ugly, so contemptible, that the loathing is *deserved* by the thing itself.

Here is the proof from the language itself. Before Islam, the Arabs had a specific term: *nikāḥ al-maqt* — "the marriage of loathing." It referred to a man marrying his own father's widow. It was considered so repugnant, so beneath dignity, that the very word *maqt* became attached to it. The Qur'an itself uses this in Surah al-Nisāʾ: of that marriage Allah says *innahu kāna fāḥishatan wa-maqtan* — "it was an obscenity and a *maqt*" (4:22).

So when you hear *maqt*, do not hear "anger." Do not even hear "hatred." Hear: *revulsion toward something genuinely contemptible.* A loathing that the object has earned.

Now look at our ayah. The word appears not once but twice, side by side:

> **لَمَقْتُ ٱللَّهِ** أَكْبَرُ مِن **مَّقْتِكُمْ** أَنفُسَكُمْ
>
> *...the maqt of Allah was greater than your maqt of yourselves...*

Two loathings. The same heavy, contemptuous word used for both. And the Qur'an sets them against each other.

Notice something. The people in the Fire are not described, in this moment, as being *afraid*. Fear would be the obvious emotion. The Qur'an reaches past fear to something more devastating: they have become *disgusted with themselves*. Not "I am scared of what's coming" — but "I cannot bear what I am." That is *maqt* turned inward. Self-revulsion.

And listen to the word in your mouth. *Maqt.* The *qāf* sits deep in the throat, hard and constricted; the *tāʾ* clips it off short. There is no flow in this word, no openness — it catches and chokes. It is a word that sounds like the thing it names: the gag reflex of a soul that cannot stand itself.

[PAUSE]

Here is what stops me.

The self-loathing these people are feeling — this is the deepest human pain. We said at the start: to hate your own self is the worst place a person can be. There is no escape from it, because the thing you want to flee *is* the thing fleeing. It is the closest thing to hell that a living person can experience on earth.

And the ayah says: *this is the smaller maqt.*

> *lamaqtu-llāhi **akbaru** min maqtikum anfusakum*
>
> Allah's loathing was **greater** than your loathing of yourselves.

Let that land. The most unbearable feeling a human can have — the bottomless self-disgust of the person who has ruined themselves — the Qur'an presents it as the *lesser* of two quantities. There is a *greater* loathing. And it is not even theirs.

This is the first door the ayah opens. We will walk through it fully later. For now, hold the question it forces: *when* was Allah's greater loathing happening? The answer is in the next words, and it is the most merciful and most terrible detail in the entire verse.

### Element Two: The Two Callings — *Yunādawna* and *Tudʿawna*

Now I need to show you something that almost every English translation erases.

This ayah contains *two* acts of calling. They are not the same word. They are not even close. And the entire emotional architecture of the verse depends on the difference between them.

Let me teach the concept first.

In Arabic, there are two distinct roots for summoning a person.

The first is **daʿā** — root *dāl-ʿayn-wāw*. This is the *intimate* call. It is the root of *duʿāʾ*, the word for supplication itself — when *you* call upon Allah. *Daʿā* is the call of nearness: a host inviting a guest to dinner, a parent calling a child to come, a beloved calling a beloved by name. It assumes closeness. It assumes you are *within reach* and the caller *wants you to come.*

The second is **nādā** — root *nūn-dāl-wāw*. This is the call across *distance*. *Nidāʾ* is the raised voice, the proclamation, the shout that travels because the one being called is *far away*. You use *nādā* when someone is across the valley, on the other shore, beyond the reach of a normal voice. It is announcement, not invitation. It carries no intimacy. It carries distance.

Hold those two apart in your mind. *Daʿā* — near, inviting, warm. *Nādā* — far, proclaiming, distant.

[PAUSE]

Now watch what the ayah does with them.

When the people are at the Fire, the verb is **يُنَادَوْنَ** — *yunādawna*. From *nādā*. They are *called across a distance.* They are *proclaimed to* from far off. And notice it is passive — *yunādawna*, "they are cried out to" — the Qur'an does not even say who is calling. The classical commentators say it is the angels, the keepers of Hell (al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar). The point of the passive is the distance itself: a voice reaches them from somewhere beyond, and they are merely the recipients of it. They are far. They are addressed *from afar.*

And what does that distant voice remind them of? Look:

> *...idh **tudʿawna** ilā al-īmān...*
>
> "...back when you were **called** to faith..."

**تُدْعَوْنَ** — *tudʿawna*. From *daʿā*. The *intimate* call. The call of nearness.

[PAUSE — let this sit]

Do you see what the Qur'an has just done?

In the *dunyā* — in the world, in their lives — they were called with *daʿā*. The near call. The warm call. The invitation of a host who wants you at the table. *Tudʿawna ilā al-īmān* — "you were *invited* to faith." Allah did not shout at them across a chasm. He called them *near*. The door was open. The voice was intimate. *Come.*

And now, in the Hereafter, the calling has changed its very nature. Now it is *yunādawna* — the call across the abyss. The proclamation to people who are now, finally, *far*. The intimacy is gone. The nearness is gone. All that is left is a voice traveling across a distance that can no longer be closed.

The grammar of the relationship has inverted. **When He was near, they made themselves far. Now that they are far, He is no longer calling them to come — He is only telling them what they did.**

[PAUSE]

This is not decoration. This is the spine of the verse.

Think about the cruelest thing about the scene at the Fire. It is not the heat. The Qur'an, here, is not even talking about heat. The cruelest thing is the *change in the calling*. Once you were invited close. Now you are addressed from across a gulf. The same God, two callings — and the difference between them is the entire distance between a life that turned toward Him and a life that turned away.

And here is the detail that breaks the heart open. The distant call (*nidāʾ*) exists *only to point back at the near call* (*duʿāʾ*). The whole content of the proclamation from afar is: *remember when I called you near?* The far voice is made entirely of the memory of the near voice.

That is the most painful structure imaginable. To be reminded, from across an uncrossable distance, of every time you were invited home and did not come.

### Element Three: *Akbar* — A Loathing With No Stated Object

Let me show you one more thing — something hiding in plain sight, in what the ayah does *not* say.

We have two loathings:

> *lamaqtu-llāhi* — the maqt of **Allah**
>
> *maqtikum anfusakum* — **your** maqt **of yourselves**

Look carefully at the grammar. The second one is *complete*. *Maqtikum* — your loathing — *anfusakum* — of yourselves. Subject and object, both stated. We know exactly who is loathing whom: you, loathing you.

But the first one is *incomplete*. *Maqtu-llāhi* — the loathing of Allah. The subject is named — Allah. But the *object* is not stated. Allah's loathing *of... what? Of whom?*

[PAUSE]

Arabic grammar would normally complete it. We expect "Allah's loathing *of you*." The commentators supply it — *maqtu-llāhi iyyākum*, "Allah's loathing of you" (al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī all fill in *iyyākum*). The meaning is clear. But the *word* is not there. Allah left the object off.

Why would the object be dropped?

Here is what this invites us to consider — and I mark this as an interpretive reflection, not a grammatical claim. When you state an object, you contain the act. "I am disgusted *by this specific thing*" — the disgust has edges, it has a size, it stops where the object stops. But when you say "the loathing of Allah" and leave the object hanging — the loathing is left *unbounded*. It does not stop at a neat edge. It is presented as a magnitude, not a transaction.

And then the verse confirms exactly that by measuring it: *akbar* — **greater**. Root *kabura*: greatness, magnitude. The whole ayah turns into a comparison of *sizes*. Your self-loathing has a known size — you can feel its full extent right now, it is the size of you. But Allah's loathing is *akbar* — larger than that. Larger than the largest pain you are capable of feeling.

[PAUSE]

Now put the two grammatical facts together, because they were designed to work as a pair.

The self-loathing is **complete and present**: *maqtikum anfusakum* — happening now, fully felt, object and all.

The divine loathing is **unbounded and past**: *maqtu-llāh*, no fixed object, "*greater*," and located — by the next phrase — *idh tudʿawna*, "back when you were being called."

So the comparison is not between two feelings happening at the same time. It is across time. *The self-loathing you feel now* is being measured against *the loathing that was happening then* — in the very moments you were being invited and were refusing. While you thought nothing was happening — while you were comfortably turning the invitation down, again and again, year after year — *that* was when the greater loathing was occurring. Not as random divine anger. As the *maqt* word demands: as the deserved revulsion toward a genuinely contemptible act — the act of being called near by your Maker and turning your back.

That is the linguistic discovery. Now let us ask what it reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Invitation You Did Not Know Was an Invitation

Let me begin where you live.

Imagine you find out, years later, that someone loved you. Really loved you. And they tried to reach you — not dramatically, but steadily. A message here. An open door there. A "you're always welcome" that you half-heard and never answered because you were busy, distracted, sure there would be time. And then you learn that they have died, or moved beyond reach, and that the door is closed now. And the grief that hits you is not ordinary grief. It is grief braided with shame. Because you realize: *I was being invited the whole time, and I treated the invitation like noise.*

Almost everyone, by a certain age, carries one of these. A relationship we let lapse. A parent we kept meaning to call. A chance we assumed would wait. And the specific agony of it is this: the invitation never announced itself as important. It came disguised as ordinary. A normal Tuesday. A casual "let's catch up sometime." It did not arrive with trumpets. So we let it pass.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is telling us that the call to faith was exactly this kind of invitation — and that we are at risk of treating it exactly this way.

*Tudʿawna ilā al-īmān.* You were *invited* to faith. With the near call. The warm one.

How does that invitation actually come, in a life? Almost never with trumpets. It comes as an ayah you happen to hear on a Tuesday. A moment of unexpected stillness in a hard week. A death that makes you ask, for an afternoon, what any of this is for. A friend who lives with a peace you secretly envy. A pull toward prayer that you feel and then talk yourself out of. These are the *daʿā* — the near calls. They do not announce themselves as the most important moments of your life. They arrive disguised as ordinary.

And the danger the ayah exposes is that you can decline a *near* invitation so gently, so undramatically, that you never even register that you declined. You did not slam a door. You just... didn't walk through it. Not today. Maybe later. And "later" became a lifetime of laters.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement the ayah forces.

The people at the Fire are not being shown a list of their crimes. The verse does not say "remember when you murdered, when you stole." It says: *remember when you were **called**, and you refused.* Of all the things to throw back at them, the Qur'an chooses *the rejected invitation.* Because that, it turns out, is the deepest wound. Not what they did — what they *declined.* Not the doors they broke down — the door they left standing open and walked past, every single day.

And this reframes the divine *maqt* entirely. Why would Allah's loathing be *greatest at the moment of invitation*? Because that is the moment His nearness was most exposed and most refused. To reject a stranger costs little. To reject the One who is calling you *near*, by name, to your own salvation, with the warm call and not the cold one — that is the *nikāḥ al-maqt* of the soul, the genuinely contemptible thing, the act that earns the heaviest word in the language.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you do with the ordinary pulls. The next time you feel that small, quiet tug — toward a prayer, toward turning back, toward closing a sin, toward picking up the Qur'an you've been avoiding — recognize it for what it might be. *That is the near call.* That is *tudʿawna ilā al-īmān* arriving disguised as a Tuesday. The whole tragedy of this ayah is built out of unanswered small pulls. Answer one. Today. Do not let it become a *later.*

### Theme Two: Whose Verdict on You Is Larger Than Your Own?

Now the harder theme. And I have to handle it carefully, because it touches the most fragile part of a person.

We live in an age drowning in self-criticism. The inner critic. The shame spiral. The voice in your head that catalogues every failure. And the modern response — much of it wise — is to soften that voice. "Be gentle with yourself. You are your own harshest critic. No one judges you as harshly as you judge yourself."

And there is real mercy in that counsel. For the believer crushed by their own sins, the Qur'an's overwhelming message is hope — *do not despair of the mercy of Allah* (39:53). I want that held firmly in view before we go further. This ayah is not addressed to the trembling believer who fears they have failed. It is addressed, explicitly, to *alladhīna kafarū* — those who *refused*, finally and totally, the invitation itself.

But within that frame, this ayah says something that cuts directly against the easy version of "you're your own harshest critic." It says: *no. You are not.*

[PAUSE]

Look at the comparison again. The self-loathing of the people in the Fire is *real* and it is *total*. They are not under-rating themselves. In that moment they see themselves with perfect clarity and they are revolted, fully, completely. There is no exaggeration left in them, no distortion — only the naked truth of what they chose. *And even that maximal, perfectly accurate self-judgment is the smaller one.*

This tells us something staggering about the nature of self-assessment. Your judgment of yourself — even at its most severe — is operating with incomplete information. You are judging the choice. Allah was judging *the choice against the full weight of what was being refused.* You saw a Tuesday. He saw the entire reality you were turning down — Himself, His nearness, the salvation in your reach. You cannot loathe a refusal as accurately as the One refused can, because you never fully saw the size of what you were saying no to. *That* is why His *maqt* is *akbar*. Not because He is more cruel than you. Because He sees more than you.

[PAUSE]

Now, here is where I want to turn this from terror into something that can actually reshape a life — because the ayah, read rightly, is not just a threat. It is a map.

If the One whose verdict is *larger than your own* is the One whose loathing is provoked most by *the rejected invitation* — then the same logic runs in the opposite direction. The One whose *maqt* is greater than your self-hatred is the same One whose *love* and *mercy* are greater than your self-love. The surah told us this from its third verse: *Ghāfir al-dhanb, Qābil al-tawb* — Forgiver of sin, Accepter of repentance — named *before* "severe in punishment." His capacity on the side of mercy is, if anything, the dominant one.

So here is the reframe that should change how you live tomorrow. Stop letting your own verdict on yourself be the final court — in *either* direction. Do not let your self-loathing convince you that you are beyond reach; His mercy is *akbar* than your worst opinion of yourself. And do not let your self-satisfaction convince you that you are fine; His seeing is *akbar* than your most flattering opinion of yourself. The lesson of *akbar* is the same both ways: *there is a larger assessment than yours, and it belongs to the One who sees what you cannot.*

The practical shift: when the inner critic screams that you are worthless, answer it not with "I'm actually great" but with "my verdict is not the largest one — and the One whose is, called me *near*, and is still, while I breathe, the Forgiver of sin." And when the inner voice grows comfortable, complacent, sure you've done enough — remember the same thing. *His is the larger assessment.* Live toward His seeing, not your own.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how the verse is built. It has a symmetry that you could not have seen at the start.

The whole ayah is a structure of **two measurements across two times**, hinged on two callings:

- **Then** (the dunyā): the *near* call — *tudʿawna* (daʿā) — "you were invited to faith." And, hidden inside that same moment, the *greater maqt* — Allah's unbounded loathing of the refusal.
- **Now** (the Fire): the *far* call — *yunādawna* (nādā) — "you are proclaimed to across the distance." And, present in this moment, the *lesser maqt* — your complete, accurate, total self-loathing.

The verse moves from *now* backward to *then*: the distant voice reaches across the gulf only to point at the moment of nearness that was thrown away. The far call is made entirely of the memory of the near call. That is the architecture. That is the proof. The form of the verse *is* its meaning: distance remembering nearness, the greater loathing located precisely where the warmest invitation was refused.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight from two directions.

The *language* showed us: two words for calling (near and far), one word for loathing (the contemptuous *maqt*), and a comparison of *sizes* (*akbar*) across *time*.

The *themes* showed us: the invitation that arrives disguised as ordinary and is declined without drama, and the verdict on yourself that is real but never the largest one.

They meet here: **The deepest tragedy of a soul is not the sins it committed but the nearness it refused — and the truest measure of that refusal belongs not to you but to the One who was near.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What "near calls" — the quiet, ordinary pulls toward Allah — am I currently treating as background noise, telling myself I'll answer *later*?
2. When I judge myself harshly, am I judging the *choice*, or the choice against *the full weight of what I am refusing*? What is the difference between those two?
3. If His *maqt* is greater than my self-loathing, His mercy is greater than my self-love. Which of those two truths do I most need to hear today — and which am I avoiding?
4. Is there a door in my life that has been standing open so long I've stopped seeing it as a door?
5. When the calling changes from *near* to *far* — and one day it will — what will I wish I had answered while it was still near?

### One-Sentence Distillation

*The most contemptible thing a soul can do is refuse the God who calls it near — and on the Day the call comes only from afar, even your perfect, total self-loathing will be the smaller of the two reckonings.*

### The Closing Invitation

You are still being called with the *near* call. That is the whole mercy of being alive. The *yunādawna* — the distant proclamation — has not begun for you. You are still in the time of *tudʿawna*, the warm invitation, the open door, the host who wants you at the table. The entire weight of this ayah is meant to land on you *while the door is still open* — so that this scene is a warning you heed, not a memory you inherit.

So do not close this reflection and return to "later." Answer one near call today. One. The prayer you've been delaying. The sin you've been managing instead of leaving. The Qur'an you've been meaning to open. Walk through the door while it is still a door.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, You who call us near while we keep ourselves far — let us hear Your invitation in the ordinary moments before it becomes a proclamation across a distance we cannot cross. You whose mercy is greater than our love for ourselves, and whose seeing is greater than our judgment of ourselves: do not let us be among those reminded, too late, of the nearness we refused. Pull us toward You while the call is still warm, and answer it in us before we are only answered to. Āmīn.*
