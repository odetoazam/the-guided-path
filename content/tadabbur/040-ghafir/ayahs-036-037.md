# The Tower That Reached for Loss
## Pharaoh and the Architecture of a Rigged Question

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Building Permit Instead of an Answer

Let me put two sounds in the air before we begin.

> **وَقَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ يَـٰهَـٰمَـٰنُ ٱبْنِ لِى صَرْحًا لَّعَلِّىٓ أَبْلُغُ ٱلْأَسْبَـٰبَ**
>
> *Wa qāla Firʿawnu yā Hāmānu-bni lī ṣarḥan laʿallī ablughu-l-asbāb*
>
> "And Pharaoh said: O Haman, build for me a tower, that I may reach the ways —"

> **أَسْبَـٰبَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ فَأَطَّلِعَ إِلَىٰٓ إِلَـٰهِ مُوسَىٰ وَإِنِّى لَأَظُنُّهُۥ كَـٰذِبًا**
>
> *asbāba-s-samāwāti fa-aṭṭaliʿa ilā ilāhi Mūsā wa innī la-aẓunnuhu kādhiban*
>
> "— the ways into the heavens, so that I may look upon the God of Moses; and indeed I think him a liar."

I want you to feel where we are in the surah before we touch a single word.

For verses on end, one man has been speaking. The Quran calls him *muʾminun min āli Firʿawn* — a believing man from the household of Pharaoh himself. A man inside the palace. A man with everything to lose. And he has just delivered one of the most courageous speeches in the entire Quran — pleading with a tyrant, warning him of the day of mutual calling, reminding him of the nations that perished before, begging him: *yā qawmi-ttabiʿūni ahdikum sabīla-r-rashād* — "O my people, follow me, I will guide you to the path of right conduct."

The court has gone quiet. The warning has landed. Everyone is waiting to see how the god-king will respond to being told, to his face, that there is a Reality above him.

And here is his answer.

He doesn't argue. He doesn't refute. He turns to his minister and says: **build me a tower.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the strangeness of that. A man makes a claim about the unseen, about transcendence, about a Lord beyond the heavens — and Pharaoh's response is a *construction project.* He answers a spiritual claim with a building permit. He answers theology with masonry.

Why? Why does the most powerful man in the world, when confronted with God, reach not for a sword but for a *scaffold*?

That is the question this passage answers. And the answer is hidden — as it so often is — inside the very words Pharaoh chose. Because Pharaoh, in his arrogance, used a word for "tower" that secretly indicts him. He named the exact thing he was doing wrong, and he didn't even hear himself say it.

Stay with me. We are going to listen to a tyrant's vocabulary betray him.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: صَرْح — The Tower Whose Name Means "To Make Clear"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open this word, because without it you'll walk right past the miracle.

In Arabic, words grow out of roots the way trees grow out of seeds. A root is usually three consonants, and every word sprouting from that root carries some genetic memory of the others. The root is the family. The words are the cousins. And sometimes a cousin you'd never expect turns out to share blood with a word you thought you knew.

So when you meet a word in the Quran, one of the first questions a person of reflection asks is: *what family does this come from? Who are its cousins?*

Now. Pharaoh says: **ٱبْنِ لِى صَرْحًا** — *ibni lī ṣarḥan* — "build for me a **ṣarḥ**." A ṣarḥ is a tower, a high palace, a tall conspicuous edifice. The lexicographers tell us it is *al-bināʾ al-ẓāhir alladhī lā yakhfā ʿalā-n-nāẓir wa in baʿuda* — "the structure so prominent it cannot be hidden from the onlooker even from far away."

Good. A tall building. A skyscraper. Fine.

But now ask the question. *Who are this word's cousins?*

[PAUSE]

The root is **ṣ-r-ḥ** (صرح). And from this exact same root comes the word **تَصْرِيح** — *taṣrīḥ* — which means *to state something openly, to declare it explicitly, to make it manifest, to bring it out into the clear.* When you speak *ṣarīḥ*, you speak plainly, transparently, with nothing hidden. A *taṣrīḥ* in modern Arabic is an official public statement. The whole family means one thing: **to make the hidden visible. To bring into the open. To render manifest.**

Al-Baghawi, the classical commentator, catches this and says it directly: **وأصله من التصريح وهو الإظهار** — "the origin of the word *ṣarḥ* is from *taṣrīḥ*, which is *making-manifest.*"

Do you see what just happened?

[PAUSE]

The word for "tower" — the very word Pharaoh chose — is built from the idea of *making the hidden visible.*

And what is Pharaoh trying to do with the tower? He says it himself: **فَأَطَّلِعَ إِلَىٰٓ إِلَـٰهِ مُوسَىٰ** — "so that I may *look upon* the God of Moses." He is trying to take the most hidden Reality in all of existence — the Lord of the heavens, *al-Bāṭin*, the One whom no vision grasps — and *make Him manifest.* Make Him visible. Bring Him out into the open where Pharaoh can put eyes on Him.

He chose a building whose name is *making-visible* — to try to make visible the One who, by His very nature, is beyond the reach of sight.

The word is a confession. The tower's own name announces the fool's errand. He is using the architecture of *the manifest* to chase down *the Manifest-of-a-different-order* — the One who is manifest in everything precisely *because* He is not a thing among things you can build a tower up to.

Notice something. Pharaoh is not, in this moment, a crude atheist who says "there is no God." He is something subtler and, honestly, more modern. He says: *if there is a God, He must be a being located somewhere — up there — and if He is located somewhere, then with enough engineering I can get to where He is and have a look.* He has reduced the Lord of the worlds to an object at a coordinate. A thing at an address. And things at addresses can be reached by towers.

This is the deepest error the word exposes. Not that Pharaoh doubts God. That he has *shrunk* God down to the size of his own methods. He has assumed that the Real must fit inside the categories of the seeable, the locatable, the reachable — and then concluded that since he is the master of all reaching, he is the master of the question.

The ṣarḥ — the *make-it-visible* tower — is what materialism looks like when it has the budget to build. **One clear statement of what this single word reveals: Pharaoh's tower is the monument every age builds to the belief that whatever is real must be reachable by our instruments — and the word's own root whispers the verdict, that he was trying to drag into the visible the One whose glory is that He cannot be.**

### Element Two: ٱلْأَسْبَاب — The Ropes He Mistook for the Path

Now look at where the tower is supposed to take him: **لَّعَلِّىٓ أَبْلُغُ ٱلْأَسْبَـٰبَ** — "that I may reach **al-asbāb**." And then, in the second ayah, he specifies: **أَسْبَـٰبَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ** — "the *asbāb* of the heavens."

We translate *asbāb* as "the ways" or "the means." But let me give you the original picture, because the original picture is everything.

A **sabab** (سبب), in its root sense, is a **rope.** A cord. Specifically, the rope you lower into a well to draw water, or the cord you climb to reach something high. It is the physical connector between you-down-here and the-thing-up-there. From that concrete image — the rope that connects two levels — Arabic grew the abstract meaning we use today: a *means*, a *cause*, the link by which you get from where you are to where you want to be. Even now we say *sababiyyah* for causality. But underneath the abstraction, the rope is still hanging there.

So when Pharaoh says "I want to reach the *asbāb* of the heavens," hear the literal hunger in it: *I want to grab the ropes that connect the earth to the sky. I want to lay my hands on the cords of the cosmos and climb them.*

He thinks the heavens have *handles.* He thinks there are *ropes* you can seize, gateways with hardware, mechanisms a sufficiently tall ladder can dock with. He is imagining the sky as a ceiling with access points — and he wants the keys.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the thing I need you to hold very gently, because it is the quiet devastation of this passage.

Pharaoh spends both ayahs straining upward for the **asbāb** — the ropes, the means, the connectors to the heavens. He wants *more* connection, *more* means, *more* access. And then, in the very same breath, the Quran tells us what actually happened to him: **وَصُدَّ عَنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ** — "and he was turned away from **al-sabīl**."

*Al-sabīl.* The **path.** The way.

Listen to those two words sitting side by side. **As-sabab** — the rope he reached for. **As-sabīl** — the path he was cut off from. They even *sound* like cousins, both circling the idea of a way-to-somewhere.

And here is the heartbreak: **he was so busy grasping for the ropes to heaven that he lost the path to it.** He wanted the *asbāb* — the mechanical means, the engineering access, the technical connection — and in that very wanting he was *ṣudda ʿani-s-sabīl*, severed from the only road that ever actually led up.

The road to the heavens was never a rope you climb. It was never an *asbāb* problem. It was a *sabīl* — a path you *walk*, with your heart, in submission. The believing man had just named it: *sabīla-r-rashād*, the path of right guidance. It was standing right there in front of Pharaoh, offered, free, requiring no minister and no masonry. And Pharaoh looked straight past the *sabīl* that was being handed to him, and demanded an *asbāb* he could build.

[PAUSE]

This is what reflection on a single root can open. Pharaoh's tragedy is not that he failed to reach heaven. It is that he was looking for the *wrong kind of connection entirely.* He wanted a rope when he was being offered a road. He wanted hardware when he was being offered a heart. **And the man who spends his life reaching for the ropes will find, at the end, that he was turned away from the path the whole time — because the reaching itself was the turning away.**

Let that land. The asbāb he grasped for and the sabīl he lost are the same destination — heaven — approached two opposite ways. One you build toward. One you bow toward. He chose the build. And the building was the proof he had refused the bow.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: أَبْلُغُ — The Verb of "Arriving All the Way There"

One more word, and it's small, but it's the engine of the whole sentence: **أَبْلُغُ** — *ablughu* — "that I may **reach**."

The root is **b-l-gh** (بلغ). And *bulūgh* is not just "to get near" or "to head toward." It carries the meaning of *arriving at the utmost point, attaining the full extent, reaching all the way to the end of a thing.* It's the same root from which we get *bāligh* — a person who has reached full maturity — and *balāgha*, eloquence, language that *arrives* completely at its meaning with nothing falling short. *Bulūgh* is *completion of arrival.* Getting *all the way there.*

So Pharaoh is not saying "let me get closer to the heavens." He is saying "let me *arrive completely* at them. Let me *attain* them in full. Let me get *all the way up* to where the ropes are."

And the verb is in the imperfect — **أَبْلُغُ**, *ablughu* — the ongoing, the not-yet, the still-reaching tense. There is something almost unbearable in that. The whole man is captured in one verb form: forever *reaching*, never *arriving*. A perpetual *ablughu* that never becomes a completed *balaghtu*, "I have reached." He is the tense of striving with no fulfillment.

Because here is the secret the verb knows that Pharaoh doesn't: **you cannot *bulūgh* your way to God.** You cannot *arrive completely* at the One who has no place at which to arrive. The God of Moses is not at the *top* of the tower. He is not at the end of the asbāb. He is not a distance away that enough vertical feet will close. *Bulūgh* — total arrival — is exactly the category that does not apply to Him. You can reach the top of any structure you build. You will never, by reaching, reach Him. The closer you get to Him is never *up.* It is always *down* — down into the dust, in sajdah, which is the one direction Pharaoh's entire architecture refused to consider.

[PAUSE]

Notice the cruel comedy the three words make together when you line them up:

- He wants to **build** a *ṣarḥ* — a *make-it-visible* tower, to make visible the Invisible.
- He wants to **reach** the *asbāb* — the *ropes*, while losing the *sabīl*, the path.
- He wants to **arrive** — *ablughu* — *all the way at* the One at whom there is no arriving.

Three words. Three impossibilities. And every one of them is a word *Pharaoh himself chose,* in his confidence, to describe his master plan. The man wrote his own verdict into his own command and read it aloud at full volume to his court — and heard nothing.

**What this final verb reveals: the deepest unbelief is not a wrong answer. It is a wrong category — the conviction that the Real is something you *arrive at* by reaching, when reaching was never the road, and the road was never up.**

---

### The Bridge

Look at what the three words have built between them — not a tower, but a portrait. *Ṣarḥ*: trying to make the Hidden manifest by building. *Asbāb*: reaching for ropes while losing the road. *Ablugh*: straining to arrive at the One who is not a destination. Pharaoh's vocabulary, when you slow it down, is a complete anatomy of a particular soul-disease — the disease of a man who has confused *mastery of means* with *access to truth.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the very same disease still living, today, in us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: "Show Me God in a Test Tube" — The Materialist Reflex

Let me start where you already live.

You have heard the sentence. Maybe you've said a version of it. *"I'll believe in God when someone shows me the evidence. Real evidence. Something I can measure. Put it under the microscope. Run the experiment. Until then, I'm not buying it."*

It sounds so reasonable. It sounds, in fact, like the very height of intellectual honesty — *I only believe what can be demonstrated.* It is the unofficial creed of an entire age. *Show me. Prove it. Let me see it for myself.*

I want to suggest something to you that may be uncomfortable: **that sentence is Pharaoh's tower, rebuilt in the language of the laboratory.**

[PAUSE]

Look at what Pharaoh actually proposed. He did not propose to *think* about whether God exists. He did not propose to *reflect*, to *reason*, to weigh the believing man's argument. He proposed an *instrument.* A *device.* A piece of *apparatus* — the tallest one ever built — through which he would *look upon* the God of Moses with his own two eyes. *Fa-aṭṭaliʿa* — "so that I may peer up at Him, get a look at Him, observe Him."

This is the empirical reflex in its purest, oldest form: *the real is the seeable. If I cannot put my instrument on it and observe it, it does not count.* Pharaoh's tower and the demand "show me God in a test tube" are *the same move.* Both assume that the only valid evidence is the kind that fits inside a measuring device. Both assume that God, if He is anything, must be a *findable object* — and that the failure to find Him with the chosen instrument settles the matter.

And here is what the tower exposes about that whole reflex — gently, but completely:

**The instrument determines what you can find. And if you've chosen an instrument that can only detect things-among-things, you have *guaranteed in advance* that you will never detect the One who is not a thing among things.** A telescope can find a star. It cannot find the laws of physics that govern the star — not because the laws aren't real, but because *that's not the kind of thing a telescope detects.* A microscope can find a cell. It cannot find the mathematics the cell obeys. Pharaoh built the most powerful "God-detector" of the ancient world, and its very design — *look up, peer at, observe* — was incapable of detecting a God who is the ground of looking itself, the One in whom Pharaoh's own eyes and tower and breath were held at every instant.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the trap now? The materialist reflex feels like rigor. But it is actually a *decision, made before any evidence, about what kind of thing is allowed to be real.* It rules out the transcendent not by investigation but by *definition* — and then congratulates itself on having investigated.

The Quran's answer to the test-tube demand is not to scramble for a test tube. It is to do what the believing man did, what this whole surah does — to point at the *asbāb* themselves. To say: you want the rope to the heavens? Look at the rope *you are already holding.* Your own existence. The order in the sky you want to climb to. The fact that the cosmos is *legible* at all, that it answers to law, that your mind can reach it. *These* are the asbāb — not ropes you climb up, but signs you read where you stand. **The One you're building a tower to *see* is the One making the tower-builder *able to see at all.***

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the question you bring to God. Stop standing at the bottom of the tower demanding He climb down into your instrument. The whole posture — *prove yourself to my method* — is Pharaoh's posture, and it has never once reached the heavens. The believer's posture is the opposite: *not "show me," but "show me how to see."* Not "descend to my apparatus," but "open my eyes to the signs already flooding the apparatus of my own existence." The materialist builds upward and finds nothing. The believer looks at exactly the same sky and, because the question changed, finds the heavens *full.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: زُيِّنَ — How the Mind Dresses Up Its Worst Impulse as Noble Inquiry

Now I want to take you to the most psychologically devastating word in the passage — and it's not one of Pharaoh's words. It's God's word *about* Pharaoh.

But let me start, again, where you live.

You know the experience of *making a decision and then assembling the reasons.* Everyone does it; it's one of the most human things there is. You already wanted the thing — the purchase, the grudge, the exit, the indulgence — and *then* your mind, like a brilliant lawyer working for a client who has already confessed, goes and builds a gorgeous case for why it's actually the wise, principled, even *generous* choice. Psychologists call it *motivated reasoning.* The verdict comes first. The reasoning is hired afterward to make the verdict look like it came from reasoning.

The terrifying part is not that we do it. It's that *we cannot feel ourselves doing it.* From the inside, motivated reasoning feels *exactly* like honest reflection. The lawyer is so good that the client genuinely forgets he ever confessed.

Now watch Pharaoh, and watch the seam show.

He says: build a tower **so that I may look upon the God of Moses** — that's the noble inquiry, the open-minded investigation, the *let's examine this claim fairly* — and then, in the *same sentence,* without a breath between: **وَإِنِّى لَأَظُنُّهُۥ كَـٰذِبًا** — "and indeed **I already think him a liar.**"

[PAUSE]

Stop. Read that seam again. He announces the *experiment* and the *result* in one breath. "Let me build a tower to find out the truth — and by the way, I already know the answer." The verdict comes *before* the tower's foundation is poured. *La-aẓunnuhu kādhiban* — emphatic, doubled with the *lām* of certainty — "I most certainly reckon him a liar." This is not an investigation. **It is a verdict wearing the costume of an investigation.**

Al-Qurtubi, reading this passage, sees exactly the theater of it: Pharaoh, he says, *awhama annahu yamtaḥinu mā jāʾa bihi Mūsā* — "gave the *impression* that he was *testing* what Moses brought" — when really he feared the believer's words taking root in the people's hearts, and built the tower to keep them steady on their old religion. The tower wasn't an experiment. It was *public relations.* A stage set. Pharaoh needed his denial to *look like* due diligence.

And here is where God's word lands like a hammer: **وَكَذَٰلِكَ زُيِّنَ لِفِرْعَوْنَ سُوٓءُ عَمَلِهِ** — "and **thus was made fair-seeming** to Pharaoh the evil of his deed."

**زُيِّنَ** — *zuyyina.* "Was made beautiful. Was dressed up. Was made to appear attractive." His *sūʾ* — his *ugliness*, his evil — was *beautified to him.* The verb is passive: it does not say "Pharaoh thought his deed was good." It says his evil *was made* to look good — *to him.* He genuinely could not see the ugliness of what he was doing. From the inside, his rigged anti-God theater *felt like* fearless inquiry. The man who had already decided experienced himself as the one still investigating.

[PAUSE]

This is the most frightening thing the Quran tells us about the human heart: **that it can be turned, against itself, into a machine for making evil look beautiful — and the machine runs silently.** Pharaoh isn't twirling a mustache thinking "I am being evil now." He thinks he is being *rigorous.* He thinks he is being *responsible* — protecting his people from a dangerous lie. The *zuyyina* is the lawyer who has so dazzled his own client that the client now believes he was innocent all along.

And notice the absence — read the silence as carefully as the speech. Who *did* the beautifying? The verb is passive. *Zuyyina* — "it was made beautiful," by an unnamed hand. The Quran leaves the agent veiled, and the veiling is the point. Sometimes it is the *nafs.* Sometimes, elsewhere in the Quran, it is *al-Shayṭān* who *zayyana* deeds. And sometimes — most soberingly — it is described as a divine *leaving-him-to-it*: a man insists so long on choosing the ugly that God withdraws the light by which he might have seen its ugliness, and *seals* him inside his own preferred darkness. *Wa ṣudda ʿani-s-sabīl* — "and he was turned from the path." Passive again. He turned away so many times that, at last, *he was turned.*

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It plants one merciless, life-saving question in you: **"Did I decide this, and *then* find the reasons?"** When you feel most certain that you are being *objective*, most convinced that you are the calm, fair, evidence-following one in the room — that is *precisely* the moment to suspect the *zuyyina*. Because the *zuyyina* never announces itself. It feels like clarity. It feels like rigor. It felt, to Pharaoh, like science. The single most protective habit a believing heart can build is the willingness to ask: *Is this tower I'm building an honest search — or is it scaffolding I've erected around a verdict I reached before I started, to make my refusal look like research?*

The cure is the thing Pharaoh would not do: hand the verdict *back.* Pray *Allahumma arini-l-ḥaqqa ḥaqqan* — "O Allah, show me truth *as* truth, and let me follow it; show me falsehood *as* falsehood." That du'a is the demolition of the tower. It is the heart saying: *I will not pre-decide. Strip the beautifying paint off my deeds and let me see them in their real color, even if the color is ugly, even if it costs me.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Tower That Goes Up, A Man Who Goes Down

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole thing, because the two ayahs are built as a single descending staircase disguised as an ascending one.

Watch the direction of every verb Pharaoh speaks. **Build** — *up.* **Reach the asbāb** — *up.* **Reach the asbāb of the heavens** — *higher up.* **Peer at the God of Moses** — *up at the very top.* Four movements, each one climbing. The grammar itself ascends. Pharaoh's speech is a rocket.

And now watch what God's voice does the instant Pharaoh stops talking. **Zuyyina** — his evil was painted over (he can no longer *see* down to the ground of what he's doing). **Ṣudda ʿani-s-sabīl** — he was turned *off* the path (the road is lost). **Wa mā kaydu Firʿawna illā fī tabāb** — "and the scheme of Pharaoh was nothing but **in tabāb**" — in *ruin, loss, perishing, coming-to-nothing.*

[PAUSE]

There is the architecture, and there is the proof. The man builds the tallest *upward* structure in the world — and the passage ends with him in *tabāb*, the lowest word in the language for a thing that has crashed to nothing. The higher his tower climbed, the further he fell. *Ablughu* — "let me arrive *all the way up*" — answered by *tabāb*, "all the way *down* to ruin." The whole construction project, the entire vertical fantasy, lands in a single word that means *total collapse.* He reached for the asbāb of the heavens and arrived at the *tabāb* of the earth. Even the sounds rhyme the fall: *asbāb… tabāb.* He grasped at one and got the other.

The tower was never going up. It was a measure of how far he had to fall.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The **words** showed us a man using the vocabulary of *reaching, building, arriving, making-visible* — the vocabulary of *means and mastery* — to lay hands on the One who is not reached by means and not mastered by anyone. The **themes** showed us why: because the materialist reflex *(show me)* and the self-deceiving reflex *(zuyyina)* are two faces of one refusal — the refusal to approach God on God's terms instead of our own. Pharaoh wanted heaven on the terms of *his* instruments and *his* pre-decided verdict. And a heaven you can only accept on your own terms is a heaven you have already declined.

The believer was offered the *sabīl* — the path, walkable, free, requiring nothing but a bowed heart. Pharaoh demanded the *asbāb* — the ropes, the apparatus, the engineering. He got his tower. He lost the path. And he discovered, in *tabāb*, that the only direction that was ever *up* to God was the one direction his tower could not build: *down,* in surrender.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I say "I'll believe it when I see the evidence" — have I first asked whether my chosen instrument is even capable of detecting the kind of thing God is? Or have I, like Pharaoh, ruled Him out by the *design* of my method and called it open-mindedness?

2. Where in my life am I *building a tower* — assembling an elaborate, impressive case — around a verdict I actually reached before I started? What am I really protecting?

3. The most chilling word here is *zuyyina* — his evil was *beautified to him,* and he couldn't feel it. What deed of mine might be painted over right now, looking noble from the inside while it is something else entirely?

4. Am I reaching for *asbāb* — means, mechanisms, access, control — in a place where God is offering me a *sabīl,* a simple path that only asks me to walk it with a humbled heart?

5. Pharaoh's plan ended in *tabāb,* ruin. What am I climbing right now that, the higher it goes, only measures how far I have to fall?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The man who insists on *reaching up* to God by his own instruments and his own pre-decided verdict builds, in the end, only a monument to his refusal — for the One road to the heavens was never a rope to climb but a path to walk, and never *up* but *down,* in the surrender Pharaoh would not make.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will be tempted, many times, to build a tower. To demand that the Unseen present itself to your terms before you'll bow. To dress your reluctance up as rigor. This passage is not here to shame that impulse — it is here to *name* it, so that the next time you feel it rising, you recognize the architecture. And recognizing it, you might do the one thing Pharaoh never did: set down the blueprint, step off the scaffold, and let the path that was in front of you the whole time finally be walked.

Not a tower toward a God you'll inspect. A path toward a God who is already nearer to you than the very eyes you wanted to look at Him with.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You whom no tower reaches and no instrument contains — save us from the arrogance of demanding that You answer to our methods, and from the *zuyyina* that paints our worst deeds beautiful so we cannot see them. Do not let us reach for ropes while You are offering us the path. Show us the truth as truth, that we may follow it; show us falsehood as falsehood, that we may turn from it — and let us come to You the only way that ever arrived: not climbing, but bowing. *Āmīn.*
