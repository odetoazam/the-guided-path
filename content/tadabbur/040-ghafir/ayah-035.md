# When the Argument Swells, It Is the Self That Has Swollen

## Surah Ghafir, Ayah 35

> ٱلَّذِينَ يُجَٰدِلُونَ فِىٓ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ سُلْطَٰنٍ أَتَىٰهُمْ ۖ كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ وَعِندَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ يَطْبَعُ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ قَلْبِ مُتَكَبِّرٍ جَبَّارٍ

*alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyāti-llāhi bi-ghayri sulṭānin atāhum — kabura maqtan ʿinda-llāhi wa ʿinda-lladhīna āmanū — kadhālika yaṭbaʿu-llāhu ʿalā kulli qalbi mutakabbirin jabbār.*

> "Those who dispute concerning the signs of Allah without any authority having come to them — great is it in detestation in the sight of Allah and in the sight of those who believe. Thus does Allah set a seal over every arrogant, tyrannical heart."

---

## Introduction: The Lone Believer in the Tyrant's Court

Let me set the scene, because you cannot feel this ayah from the outside.

There is a man in Pharaoh's court. We do not know his name. The Qur'an calls him only *a believing man of the house of Pharaoh, who concealed his faith* — **rajulun mu'minun min āli firʿawna yaktumu īmānahu** (40:28). He has lived for years inside the most powerful, most God-denying regime on earth, keeping the deepest truth of his life hidden behind his teeth.

And then there comes a moment — the moment when Pharaoh resolves to kill Musa — when this man can no longer stay silent. He steps forward. He begins to speak. And the speech that pours out of him is one of the longest, most sustained appeals in the entire Qur'an. He warns them of the nations destroyed before — the people of Nuh, of ʿĀd, of Thamūd. He warns them of *the Day of Mutual Calling*, the Day when feet slip. He pleads with the logic of a man watching people walk off a cliff in slow motion.

And somewhere in the middle of that desperate plea, he stops *describing* the danger and starts *diagnosing* it. He asks, in effect: why won't it land? Why does the proof never reach them? What is actually wrong here?

[PAUSE]

That diagnosis is our ayah.

Here is what makes it stunning, and I want you to hold the question before I give you anything else. This man is standing in front of people who *argue*. They are not silent. They are not passive. They have responses, rebuttals, counter-points, sophisticated objections. By every outward measure, they are *engaging* with the truth. They are debating it.

And the ayah says: **that is exactly the disease.**

Not their silence. Their arguing. The Qur'an is about to tell us that there is a kind of arguing about God's signs that is not a search for truth at all — it is the precise opposite — and that this specific kind of arguing is among the most detested things in existence, and that it ends in a heart being sealed shut like a jar.

Why? What separates the arguing that opens a heart from the arguing that seals it? And how does a person cross from one to the other without ever noticing?

Stay with me. The answer is hidden in a single repeated sound.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Yujādilūna* — The Verb That Already Has an Opponent

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the whole ayah turns on a shape you can feel once you know it.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — moulds that the root is poured into, and each mould carries its own flavour of meaning. There is a particular mould called **Form III**, the *mufāʿala* pattern. And Form III has a signature: it is the form of an action *aimed at someone else*, an action that is **reciprocal, directed, relational** — done *against* or *toward* a counterpart.

You feel it in everyday words. *Kataba* means "he wrote." But *kātaba* — Form III — means "he corresponded *with* someone," wrote back and forth. *Qatala* means "he killed." *Qātala* — Form III — means "he fought, traded blows *with* an enemy." The shape itself smuggles in a second person standing opposite you. Form III is never alone in a room.

Now hold that, and look at the word the believer chooses.

> **يُجَٰدِلُونَ** — *yujādilūna*

The root is **jīm-dāl-lām (jdl)**. And the bare root, in the lexicon, has a startling literal image. *Jadala* is to *twist a rope tight*, to *plait it firmly* so the strands grip and won't come apart. From there it gives *al-ajdal*, the falcon, for the tightness and force of its grip.

And the verb is **Form III**.

[PAUSE]

So sit with what this word is actually built from. *Jidāl* — disputation — is, at its root, *the tight twisting of words*, and in Form III it is twisting them *against an opponent*. It is not "to think." It is not "to question." It is not even "to discuss." It is to take your position and twist it tight as rope and pull against the other side.

Notice what that means. **The verb already contains a winner and a loser before the first word is spoken.** The grammar of *yujādilūna* is the grammar of a contest. You cannot *jādil* alone, the way you can *reflect* alone or *wonder* alone. The form requires an adversary. The moment you are in Form III, you have already decided that the truth is a thing to be *defeated*, not a thing to be *found*.

And it is in the *imperfect* tense — *yujādilūna*, not *jādalū*. Not "those who argued, once." Those who *go on* arguing, habitually, as a settled mode of being. This is not a slip in a heated moment. This is a man's posture toward reality.

Here is the thing I want you to feel. Two people can say the *exact same sentence* about a verse of the Qur'an. One is twisting rope against an opponent. The other is reaching, open-handed, for something true. The words can be identical. The *form* is opposite. And the believer of Pharaoh's house, with the precision of a surgeon, names not the words but the form. **Alladhīna yujādilūn** — *those whose whole stance is the tight-twisted contest.*

What this single verb reveals: the problem was never the content of their objections. It was the shape of their hearts toward the truth. They had pre-decided to win.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Bi-ghayri Sulṭānin Atāhum* — The Authority That Has to *Come*

Now the believer adds something, and this is where the absence in the ayah begins to speak.

> **بِغَيْرِ سُلْطَٰنٍ أَتَىٰهُمْ** — *bi-ghayri sulṭānin atāhum* — "without any authority having come to them."

The word is **سُلْطَٰن** — *sulṭān*, root **sīn-lām-ṭāʾ (slT)**. You know this word even if you've never studied Arabic. It is the root of *sulṭa* — power, dominion — and of the title *Sultan*, the one who holds overpowering authority.

So *sulṭān* is not merely "evidence." A weak proof is not a *sulṭān*. *Sulṭān* is **proof that dominates, evidence with the force of authority over the mind** — a demonstration so commanding that it *overpowers* objection the way a king overpowers a subject. It is the kind of proof that *takes the throne* in your reasoning and makes resistance illegitimate.

And here is the first thing to notice. *Sulṭān* is **indefinite**: *bi-ghayri sulṭānin* — without *any* sulṭān, without a shred of dominating proof. They twist their rope of argument and there is *nothing* on the throne of their reasoning. No authority. Empty.

But now the second thing, and this is the one I want to live in. Look at the verb attached to *sulṭān*:

> **أَتَىٰهُمْ** — *atāhum* — "[that] came to them."

*Atā*, root *hamza-tāʾ-yāʾ*, perfect tense: it *came*. Authority, in this ayah, is something that **comes to you**. It *arrives*. It is *received* — handed down, granted from outside the self.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the quiet devastation in that?

A real *sulṭān* — a commanding proof — is not something you *manufacture*. It is something that *comes to you*, **atāhum**, the way revelation comes, the way a sign descends, the way truth is *given*. It originates outside you and arrives. You are its recipient, not its author.

So now line up the two halves of the picture the believer has drawn:

- The *jidāl* — Form III, generated *from inside the self*, twisted tight *by you*, manufactured, pushed outward against an opponent.
- The *sulṭān* — that which *comes to you*, **atāhum**, received from outside, granted.

And the disputers have all of the first and **none** of the second. They are producing argument, argument, argument — endlessly twisting rope — while the one thing that could give an argument legitimacy, the *sulṭān* that *comes*, is absent. They are loud with what they made and empty of what they were given.

This is the anatomy of a particular human posture, and you have met it. It is the person who has *concluded* and is now *working backward* — assembling reasons in defence of a verdict the self already issued. The reasons feel like reasoning. They are not. Real reasoning waits for the *sulṭān* to *come*. This kind generates argument precisely *because* no sulṭān came and the self refuses to wait empty-handed.

What this phrase reveals: the difference between a seeker and a disputer is the *direction of the flow*. The seeker stays open for the proof that **comes** to him. The disputer **produces** argument outward to defend a self that was never going to be moved.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: *Kabura* and *Mutakabbir* — The Root That Gives the Whole Game Away

Now I have to show you the thing that the whole ayah is secretly built around. It is hidden in plain sight, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

The ayah delivers its verdict on this arguing:

> **كَبُرَ مَقْتًا** — *kabura maqtan* — "it has become great in detestation."

*Kabura*, root **kāf-bāʾ-rāʾ (kbr)**, perfect tense: *it became big, it swelled to greatness*. The arguing, the believer says, has *swelled* — **kabura** — into a thing of enormous *maqt*.

Let me pause on *maqt*, because it is not a casual word. **مَقْت**, root *mīm-qāf-tāʾ (mqt)*: it is not ordinary dislike. *Bughḍ* is hatred. *Maqt* is **the most intense, most settled loathing** — the disgust you feel toward something contemptible, something that *deserves* to be despised. The Arabs used *maqt* for a specific shameful thing: a marriage so abhorrent it was called *nikāḥ al-maqt*. *Maqt* is revulsion at the morally repugnant. And this *jidāl* has *swelled* — **kabura** — into a thing of towering *maqt*, both *ʿinda-llāh*, with Allah, *and* — note this — *ʿinda-lladhīna āmanū*, with the believers. The believing hearts loathe what Allah loathes; they are aligned. The disputer makes himself repugnant to God and to every sound heart at once.

So far, so clear. But now watch the *end* of the ayah:

> **عَلَىٰ كُلِّ قَلْبِ مُتَكَبِّرٍ جَبَّارٍ** — *ʿalā kulli qalbi mutakabbirin jabbār* — "over every heart of an arrogant tyrant."

**مُتَكَبِّر** — *mutakabbir*. Look at the root. **kāf-bāʾ-rāʾ.** *kbr.*

[PAUSE]

It is the **same root** as *kabura*.

The argument **swelled** — *kabura* (kbr).
The self has **swelled itself** — *mutakabbir* (kbr).

This is not a coincidence of vocabulary. This is the believer of Pharaoh's house, by the deliberate placement of one root at the two ends of his sentence, *handing you the diagnosis*. Let me teach you the form so you feel the full weight.

*Mutakabbir* is **Form V** — the *tafaʿʿul* pattern. And Form V has a signature too: it is **reflexive**, the form of doing something *to yourself*, *making yourself* into something. *Taʿallama* — to *make oneself* learn. *Takabbara* — to **make oneself big**. The *mutakabbir* is not someone who *is* big. He is someone who *inflates himself*, who *claims* a bigness he does not possess. Greatness — *kibriyāʾ* — belongs to Allah alone. The *mutakabbir* is a creature wrapping himself in a robe sized for God.

Now read the ayah as a single mechanism:

The arguing **swelled big** — *kabura* — **because the self had swelled big** — *mutakabbir*. The bigness of the argument *is* the bigness of the ego. They are the same swelling, measured in two places. When you see a *jidāl* that has *kabura*, that has bloated into something towering and detestable, you are not looking at a big *argument*. **You are looking at a big self that has leaked out through its words.**

[PAUSE]

And then the final word: **جَبَّار** — *jabbār*, root *jīm-bāʾ-rāʾ (jbr)*. This is an *intensive* form, *faʿʿāl*, the pattern of someone who does a thing *constantly, by nature*. *Jabara* is to *force, to compel, to set a bone by force*. The *jabbār* is **the one who forces his will on others** — the tyrant, the coercer. (When *al-Jabbār* is a name of Allah, it means the One who mends what is broken and whose will none can resist; in a human, stripped of all right to it, it curdles into mere tyranny.)

So look at the pairing the ayah seals the heart of: *qalbi **mutakabbirin jabbār*** — the **arrogant tyrant's** heart. And notice these are the *two directions* of the same swollen self:

- **Mutakabbir** — the self swelling **upward, toward God**: refusing to bow, claiming a greatness that isn't its own.
- **Jabbār** — the self swelling **outward, toward people**: forcing, crushing, coercing what it cannot persuade.

Internal arrogance and external tyranny are not two diseases. They are one disease facing two ways. The man who will not lower himself before God will not stop forcing his fellow man. *Kibr* toward the Creator *always* becomes *jabr* toward the creation. Pharaoh, sitting in that very court, was the living portrait of both.

What this triad of roots reveals: the believer has shown you the entire causal chain in three words sharing two roots. The *self* inflates (*mutakabbir*), so the *argument* inflates (*kabura*), and the inflation turns *outward* into force (*jabbār*). Three stations of one swelling.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So the language has shown us a sequence, hasn't it. A verb whose very *shape* means *contest with an opponent* — *yujādilūna*. A missing thing, the *sulṭān* that *comes* and never came — *atāhum*. And a single root, *kbr*, planted at both ends to whisper that the swollen argument and the swollen self are *one thing*.

But this is not, in the end, a lesson about words. The believer of Pharaoh's house was not teaching grammar in a court that was about to kill a prophet. He was naming a mechanism in the human heart — one that operated in Pharaoh's palace and operates, right now, in yours and mine.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about how a heart actually closes.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Two Kinds of Arguing — And How You Cross From One to the Other Without Noticing

Let me start where you live, not where the ayah lives.

There is a thing that happens in your chest in the middle of a disagreement. You are making your point — about politics, about a decision at work, about something your spouse said — and somewhere in the exchange, *something shifts*. A moment earlier you were trying to figure out what was *true*. And now, without any announcement, you are trying to *win*. You feel it as a tightening. The other person stops being a partner in finding the answer and becomes an *obstacle to your being right*. And from that moment, every fact they offer, you are no longer *weighing* — you are *parrying*.

Modern psychology has a name for this. It is called **motivated reasoning**. The research is brutal and consistent: when we have a conclusion we are emotionally invested in, our minds do not reason *toward* a verdict — they reason *backward from* one. We become, the studies say, not scientists but *lawyers*. A lawyer does not ask "is my client guilty?" A lawyer has been *handed* the verdict and is *paid to defend it*. And the most chilling finding is this: the *smarter* a person is, the *better* they often are at it — because intelligence simply gives the lawyer better arguments. Cleverness does not protect you from motivated reasoning. It *weaponizes* it.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the ayah back.

This is *exactly* the line the believer of Pharaoh's house is drawing — fourteen centuries before a single psychology paper. He divides all arguing about God's signs into two kinds, and the dividing line is the **sulṭān atāhum**, the authority that *comes to you*.

The first kind of arguer is the *scientist of the soul*: he stays *open*, he *waits* for the *sulṭān* to arrive, and when it comes — **atāhum** — he yields to it. His arguing is a *reaching*. If the proof lands, he moves.

The second kind is the *lawyer of the ego*: he has *no sulṭān* — none came — and yet he argues anyway, *bi-ghayri sulṭānin atāhum*. His arguing is not a reaching. It is a *defending*. And what is he defending? Not a proof — he has none. He is defending **the self**. The *mutakabbir*. The swollen thing that has decided, in advance, that it will not be moved.

Here is the part that should make all of us go quiet. **From the outside, the two arguers look identical.** Both are speaking. Both have points. Both sound reasonable, even brilliant. The disputers in Pharaoh's court were not fools — they were the sophisticated elite of the most advanced civilization of their age. The difference between the two arguers is *invisible in the words*. It lives entirely in one question, and it is a question only *you* can answer about *yourself*:

**Am I still capable of being moved? Is there a sulṭān that, if it came, would change me — or have I already decided, and everything I now say is just rope twisted around a verdict I will never release?**

[PAUSE]

And here is how the crossing happens without you noticing — because this is the mercy and the terror of it. *Nobody decides to become a disputer.* No one wakes up and says, "today I will argue against the truth out of pure ego." The crossing is gradual. You begin as a seeker. You take a position. The position becomes *yours*. *Yours* becomes part of *you*. And now an attack on the position feels like an attack on *you* — and the self rises to defend itself, and you are *certain*, in the moment, that you are defending the *truth*, when you have quietly begun defending only your *self*. The *kabura* — the swelling of the argument — creeps up so slowly that you never feel the *mutakabbir* underneath it inflating.

What does this change about tomorrow? It gives you one diagnostic question to carry into every disagreement, sacred or ordinary: *If I imagine the strongest possible evidence against my position landing right now — do I feel myself lean toward it, or do I feel myself reach for a rebuttal?* The reach for the rebuttal *before* you have weighed the evidence — that tightening, that reflexive parry — **that** is the *mutakabbir* stirring. That is the moment to stop talking and make istighfār, before the rope twists one turn tighter. You cannot always tell a seeker from a disputer by their words. But you can *feel*, in your own chest, which one you are becoming.

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: The Sealed Heart — How a Choice Hardens Into a Cage

Now we come to the most frightening clause in the ayah, and I want to handle it gently, because it has frightened sincere people for a long time.

> **كَذَٰلِكَ يَطْبَعُ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ قَلْبِ مُتَكَبِّرٍ جَبَّارٍ**
> "Thus does Allah set a seal over every arrogant, tyrannical heart."

The word is **يَطْبَعُ** — *yaṭbaʿu*, root *ṭāʾ-bāʾ-ʿayn (TbE)*. And I want you to *see* the physical image, because the believer is using *istidlāl bil-maḥsūs* — pointing at the unseen through the seen.

A *ṭabʿ* is a **seal** — the signet ring a king pressed into hot wax or soft clay to close a letter. Picture it. While the wax is *warm*, it takes any impression — it is receptive, it can be shaped, it can be written upon. But once the seal is pressed and the wax *cools and hardens*, the letter is **closed**. Sealed. Nothing more goes in. Nothing comes out. The contents are fixed forever in the shape they had at the moment of sealing.

That is the image. A heart that was once *warm wax* — receptive, impressionable, able to take the imprint of truth — has been *sealed*. Whatever proof now arrives, however commanding, however much *sulṭān* it carries, **knocks against a closed surface and cannot enter.**

[PAUSE]

Now — the frightening question. Does Allah seal a heart *arbitrarily*? Does He reach down and close a heart that wanted to open, and then condemn it for being closed?

Look at the *grammar of the ayah itself*, because it answers this. The sealing does not come first. The sealing is the *last* clause, and it opens with **كَذَٰلِكَ** — *kadhālika*, "**thus**," "*in this way*," "*as a result of all this*." The seal is the *consequence* of everything that came before it. *Because* they argued without proof. *Because* they twisted the rope. *Because* the self swelled into *mutakabbir* and forced its way into *jabbār*. **Kadhālika** — *that* is how, *that* is why — the heart gets sealed.

And look at *which* heart. Allah does not say He seals "every heart." He says **kulli qalbi mutakabbirin jabbār** — every heart *of an arrogant tyrant*. The seal falls only on the heart that has *already made itself* a *mutakabbir*. The sealing is not the *cause* of the arrogance. **The arrogance is the cause of the sealing.**

This is the law the believer is revealing, and it is one of the most sobering laws in all of creation: **a sin repeated long enough stops being something you do and becomes something you are.** Every time you choose the swollen self over the *sulṭān* that came, you press the wax a little. You choose. And you choose. And you choose. And then — at a moment you will never be able to identify, because there is no announcement — *the wax cools.* The choice you made a thousand times becomes a *state* you can no longer escape. The door you closed by hand so many times finally *locks from the inside*, and now you could not open it even if you wished — except you no longer wish, because the wishing was sealed in too.

[PAUSE]

There is a beautiful detail in the classical reading here that deepens this. The reciters differ on two words: some read *qalbin* with *tanwīn* — "every *heart*, [itself] arrogant and tyrannical" — and some read *qalbi* in *iḍāfa* — "every heart *of* an arrogant tyrant." And al-Jalālayn notes something luminous about *why both readings are true at once*: **"when the heart becomes arrogant, its owner becomes arrogant — and when the owner becomes arrogant, his heart becomes arrogant."** The arrogance of the *heart* and the arrogance of the *person* feed each other in a closing loop. There is no daylight between the *qalb* and its *ṣāḥib*. The inner swelling and the outer self are *one sealed unit*. That is *why* both recitations carry the meaning: because in the *mutakabbir*, there is no longer any gap between the man and his hardened heart.

Now hear the mercy hidden inside the terror — because there is one, and it is the whole reason this ayah is a *gift* and not just a warning.

**The seal falls on the *mutakabbir jabbār*. It does not fall on the one who breaks.** It does not fall on the one who, in the middle of his arguing, feels the tightening and *stops*. It does not fall on the one who notices the rope twisting in his hands and *lets it go*. The wax seals only when it is *left* to cool in the shape of arrogance. **Every act of humility is heat returned to the wax.** Every "I might be wrong." Every "let me reconsider." Every yielding to a *sulṭān* that came even when it cost your pride — *that* keeps the wax warm. *That* keeps the letter of your heart open for one more imprint of guidance.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means the most spiritually urgent thing you can protect is not your *correctness* — it is your *malleability*. Your capacity to still be moved. The moment you notice you have become *unable* to imagine being wrong about something — about a person, a grievance, a reading of a verse, a verdict you passed on someone years ago — that is not strength. **That is the wax beginning to cool.** And the response is not to argue harder. It is to fall down in sujūd and beg the One who holds the seal: *Yā muqalliba-l-qulūb, thabbit qalbī ʿalā dīnik* — O Turner of hearts, keep mine *turning*, keep it warm, do not let it set.

[PAUSE — major pause]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: One Root Holds the Whole Ayah Together

Now stand back and look at what the believer of Pharaoh's house actually built, because the structure *is* the proof.

The ayah has three movements:

1. **The act** — *alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyāti-llāhi bi-ghayri sulṭānin atāhum* — those who *twist the rope* against God's signs with no *sulṭān* that *came*.
2. **The verdict** — *kabura maqtan ʿinda-llāhi wa ʿinda-lladhīna āmanū* — it has *swelled* into towering loathing, with God and with every believing heart.
3. **The consequence** — *kadhālika yaṭbaʿu-llāhu ʿalā kulli qalbi mutakabbirin jabbār* — *thus* the heart of the *swollen, forcing self* is sealed.

And the keystone that locks all three together is the root **kbr** — appearing at the *verdict* as **kabura** (the argument swelled) and at the *consequence* as **mutakabbir** (the self swelled). The believer placed the same root at the swelling of the *speech* and the swelling of the *soul* to tell you, structurally, that **they were never two things.** The towering, detestable *argument* in movement two and the sealed, arrogant *heart* in movement three are the same swelling, named twice. The architecture *is* the argument: when the *jidāl* gets big, look underneath it — you will find a *self* that got big first.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey have been the same insight approached from two doors.

The language showed us a verb that *means contest* (*yujādilūna*), a proof that has to *come from outside* (*atāhum*), and a root that *fuses* the big argument to the big self (*kbr*).

The themes showed us a human heart that crosses, unnoticed, from *seeking* to *defending* — and a wax that hardens, unnoticed, from *choice* to *cage*.

Put them together and you have one sentence of truth: **The disease was never a lack of evidence. It was a self too swollen to receive it — and the same swelling that inflates your arguments is the wax that, left to cool, seals your heart shut.**

## Questions to Carry

- In my disagreements — even the sacred ones — am I *reaching* for what is true, or *defending* what is mine? How would I honestly know the difference *in the moment*?
- Is there a *sulṭān*, a piece of evidence, that — if it came to me — would actually change my mind about something I hold tightly? Or have I quietly become unable to be moved?
- Where in my life have I crossed from *having* a position to *being* it — so that an attack on the idea now feels like an attack on me?
- *Kibr* toward God becomes *jabr* toward people. Where is my arrogance *upward* leaking out as harshness *outward* — toward a spouse, a child, an employee, a stranger online?
- Is there a place in my heart where the wax has *already cooled* — a grievance, a judgment, a refusal — that I have stopped even *trying* to reopen?

## One-Sentence Distillation

When you argue against the truth without any proof having come to you, it is not your logic at work but your swollen self — and the very same self-magnification that inflates the argument is the wax that, left to harden, seals the heart shut.

## The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to stop having convictions. The believer of Pharaoh's house had the deepest conviction of anyone in that court — he was about to risk his *life* for it. It is asking you to keep one thing soft while everything else in you grows firm: **your capacity to still be moved by what is true.** Guard your malleability the way you guard your prayer. Notice the tightening. Honor the *sulṭān* when it comes, even when it humbles you — *especially* when it humbles you. Keep the wax warm. That is the whole work.

## Du'ā'

O Allah, You who turn the hearts — keep mine *turning*. Do not let me twist my words into rope against Your signs; let me reach for them open-handed instead. And when the proof comes to me, *atāhum*, give me the humility to yield before the wax of my heart cools in the shape of my pride. *Yā muqalliba-l-qulūb, thabbit qalbī ʿalā dīnik.* Āmīn.
