# Be — And It Is: The Word With No Gap

> [ayah:40:68] هُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ ۖ فَإِذَا قَضَىٰٓ أَمْرًا فَإِنَّمَا يَقُولُ لَهُۥ كُن فَيَكُونُ
>
> *Huwa lladhī yuḥyī wa yumīt. Fa-idhā qaḍā amran fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu kun fa-yakūn.*
>
> **"He is the One who gives life and causes death. And when He decrees a matter, He only says to it, 'Be,' and it is."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Word

Let me put you somewhere before we begin.

You're in Makkah, and the air is thick with argument. This surah — Ghafir — has a recurring sound to it, a recurring complaint. Again and again it describes a certain kind of person: *alladhīna yujādilūna fī āyāti llāh* — "those who dispute concerning the signs of Allah." Not people who haven't heard. People who have heard, and who push back. Who fold their arms and say: *prove it.*

And what are they disputing, most of all? The thing that every messenger had to defend, the claim that sounds — to a certain kind of mind — like the most absurd thing a human being could say out loud: **that you will be brought back.** That after your body has gone into the earth and become indistinguishable from the earth, after the very atoms that were "you" have scattered into soil and root and rain — you will *be again.*

To the disputers, this was the giveaway that the whole thing was a fantasy. *Look at a graveyard,* they would say. *Look at bone gone to powder. And you tell me it stands up again? Who could possibly do that?*

[PAUSE]

Now notice what Allah does *not* do in response. He does not launch into a technical description of resurrection. He does not explain the mechanism. He reaches instead for something you have seen with your own eyes every single day of your life.

The ayah just before this one — ayah 67 — takes you by the hand and walks you, slowly, through the making of a human being. *He created you from dust.* Then *from a drop.* Then *from a clinging clot.* Then He *brings you out as an infant.* Then you *reach your maturity.* Then you *become old* — and some of you, He says, are taken before that. A whole human life, narrated as a sequence of stages. Slow. Gradual. Step. After step. After step.

And then — ayah 68. The pivot. The camera pulls back from the long, patient process of *you*, and lands on the One who was running it the whole time:

**"He is the One who gives life and causes death. And when He decrees a matter, He only says to it, 'Be,' and it is."**

Here is the hook. Hold onto it for the next hour, because everything turns on it.

In one breath, Allah shows you the slowest thing you know — the nine-month, sixty-year, dust-to-old-age unfolding of a human being. And in the very next breath He tells you that the Maker of all that needs no time at all. **"Be." And it is.**

So which is it? Is creation slow, or is it instant? Why would the One who can simply *say a word* take you the long way around — through the drop, and the clot, and the helpless infant, and the aching years?

That question is not a riddle to be solved and set aside. It is a door. Let's walk through it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Two Verbs That Never Stop — *yuḥyī wa yumīt*

Before we touch the Arabic, let me teach you something small about your own language, because Arabic is about to do something English cannot easily do.

In English, when we say "he gives life," we don't hear, inside the words, whether that's a one-time thing or a forever thing. "He gives" could mean *he gave, once* or *he is giving, always.* The verb itself is flat about it. You'd need extra words — "he keeps giving," "he is always giving" — to carry the difference.

Arabic carries it *inside the verb.* Arabic verbs come in two basic textures: the **completed** (the *māḍī*, the perfect — a thing finished, sealed, done) and the **ongoing** (the *muḍāriʿ*, the imperfect — a thing happening, unfolding, not yet sealed). The shape of the word *itself* tells you whether you're looking at a closed door or an open one.

Now look:

> **يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ** — *yuḥyī wa yumīt*

Both of these — "gives life" and "causes death" — are in the **ongoing** form. The imperfect. Not *aḥyā wa amāt*, "He gave life and caused death," a thing done. But **yuḥyī wa yumīt** — He *is giving* life, He *is causing* death. Right now. As you listen to these words.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that, because we carry a quiet assumption that the ayah is dismantling.

We tend to think of life and death as **events.** Life is the thing that happened to me at my birth — a one-time switch flipped on, decades ago. Death is the thing that will happen to me at my end — a one-time switch flipped off, somewhere in the future. Two dots on a timeline, far apart, and in between them: *me, on my own.*

The grammar refuses that picture. **Yuḥyī** — the giving of life — is not a dot behind you. It is a verb still running. Every heartbeat since your first one has been Him *giving life,* present tense, continuous. You did not receive life once and then own it. You are being given it, syllable by syllable, the way these very words are reaching you one after another. Stop the giving for a single second and there is no "you" to notice the gap.

And **yumīt** — the causing of death — is not only that final dot ahead of you. The same continuous form. The author of every ending is, *right now,* the One actively holding the power to end. Every person somewhere in the world crossing that threshold in this exact moment is crossing it under this same present-tense verb.

Here's what's remarkable. We experience these two as *opposites* — life and death as enemies, one stealing from the other. The ayah names them as **two ongoing actions of a single Hand.** *Yuḥyī wa yumīt* — He gives life *and* causes death, in one breath, one subject, one verb-pair. They are not a war. They are two motions of the same craftsman. The hand that opens is the hand that closes.

Notice, too, the **order.** Life is named first. *Yuḥyī*, then *yumīt.* Even here, in the most compressed possible statement of His power, the giving precedes the taking. That ordering is not nothing. It is the signature of the One whose mercy, He tells us elsewhere, *outruns* His wrath. Even in the grammar, life goes first.

So this is what one verb-pair reveals before we have moved a single step further: **you are not a being who was once given life and now possesses it. You are a being who is being given life, continuously, by the same One who continuously holds the power to take it.** There is no neutral ground in between where you stand alone. There never was.

Classical commentary adds one more layer worth holding. Al-Muyassar describes Allah in this verse as *al-mutafarrid* — the one who is *uniquely singular* in these two actions. Not the most powerful among those who give life and cause death. Not primary among a class of actors. But the *only* one for whom these are properly, essentially predicated. Every other apparent cause — the surgeon, the accident, the medicine, the food — is an instrument operating under permission, never in its own name. The *yuḥyī wa yumīt* of this verse names a category of one. There is no other in it.

### Element Two: The Word "Only" — *fa-innamā*

Now the ayah turns from life and death in general to the *mechanism* behind all of it. And it does something with one tiny particle that I want you to feel the weight of.

But first, a word on *qaḍā* — "decrees" — because this word carries more than it seems. Al-Jalalayn's commentary glosses *fa-idhā qaḍā amran* as *arāda ījāda shay'* — "when He *wills to bring something into existence.*" This is precise: *qaḍā* here is not a legal pronouncement that then waits for implementation, not a plan that still requires execution. It is the very act of willing into being. His decree and His will are the same motion. The moment He turns His will toward a thing, the decreeing is already done. This is the premise that makes everything that follows feel as vast as it does.

First, the concept. In every language, there is a small word whose entire job is to *fence off.* In English it's "only," or "just," or "all it takes is." Watch what it does:

"To open the vault, you need three keys, two passwords, and the manager's signature."
"To open the vault, you **only** need to say your name."

That word "only" — it doesn't add anything. It *removes.* It sweeps the whole table clear of everything you assumed was required, and leaves one thing standing. The power of "only" is the power of *subtraction.* It tells you: *all of that complication you were bracing for — none of it applies here.*

Arabic has a precise tool for this fencing-off. It is **إِنَّمَا** — *innamā* — a particle whose grammatical job is **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*, *qaṣr*). It draws a line and says: *this, and nothing besides this.* The classical grammarians describe it exactly so — *innamā* affirms one thing and negates everything else in one stroke.

Now read the ayah's turn:

> فَإِذَا قَضَىٰٓ أَمْرًا **فَإِنَّمَا** يَقُولُ لَهُۥ كُن
>
> "And when He decrees a matter, He **only** says to it, 'Be.'"

Feel what *innamā* is sweeping off the table.

When *you* decide to bring something into the world — a house, a meal, a healed wound, a child raised — between your decision and the result lies a vast, crowded field. Materials. Tools. Labor. Time. Cost. Other people's cooperation. Resistance. The thing fighting back. The possibility of failure. The decision is the easy part; the *gap* between decision and reality is where your entire life is spent. We even have a whole vocabulary for that gap: effort, struggle, "the grind," "putting in the work."

*Innamā* takes that entire field — every item on it — and sweeps it off.

When He decrees a matter, **only** — *innamā* — He says to it, "Be." No materials. No tools. No time. No resistance. No process. No risk of the thing not cooperating. The restriction particle strips it all away and leaves one thing standing on the empty table: **a word.**

[PAUSE]

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* working at the level of a single particle. You *know,* from the inside, the heaviness of the gap between wanting and getting. You live in that gap. *Innamā* is the linguistic act of telling you: *for Him, that gap that defines your whole existence — does not exist.* The word does not describe His power directly. It removes everything that would stand between His will and its fulfillment, and lets you feel the absence.

And notice — this matters — *innamā* here is not diminishing Allah. It is not saying "all He can manage is a word." It is saying the opposite. The smallness of what's required is the *measure* of the greatness of the One requiring it. The less a thing needs in order to act, the more total its power. You need scaffolding because you are weak. He needs nothing because He is *al-Qādir,* the Absolutely Able. The "only" is not a limit on Him. It is the signature of having no limits at all.

### Element Three: The Smallest Word for the Largest Reality — *kun fa-yakūn*

Now we arrive at the two words that the entire Quran returns to again and again — and I want to slow all the way down, because there is more packed into them than a lifetime of reflection can empty out.

> يَقُولُ لَهُۥ **كُن فَيَكُونُ**
>
> "He says to it, **'Be,' and it is.**"

Three things are happening here. Let's take them one at a time.

**First — the sheer size of *kun.***

كُن. *Kun.* Look at it. It is the imperative — the command form — of the verb "to be." And it is built from the smallest possible material: two letters. *Kāf.* *Nūn.* That is the entire utterance. Two letters, one syllable, the briefest sound a mouth can shape into a command.

And what does this two-letter sound do? It calls *everything* into existence. Galaxies. Oceans. The mountain that took your breath away. The child's first cry. The atom and the supernova. The thing you are most afraid of and the thing you most long for. All of it, every created reality that has ever been or will ever be, answers to these two letters.

The largest reality conceivable, summoned by the smallest utterance possible.

[PAUSE]

Why does that contrast matter so much? Because it shatters the assumption that *bigness of effect requires bigness of effort.* In your world, to move something large you need something large — a bigger engine, a longer lever, more hands, more time. The size of what you want to accomplish dictates the size of what you must spend. *Kun* tells you: not for Him. The effect can be infinite and the "expenditure" can be two letters, because for Him there is no expenditure at all. The word is not a *tool* that does the lifting. The word *is* the relationship between His will and reality, expressed so that you, a creature of words, can grasp it.

**Second — and this is the detail I most want you to catch — who is He talking to?**

Read it again, painfully literally:

> يَقُولُ **لَهُۥ** كُن — "He says **to it,** 'Be.'"

*To it.* *Lahu.* There is a pronoun there — "it" — pointing at the thing being commanded.

But here is the thing that should stop you cold: **when He says "Be" to it — it isn't there yet.** That is the entire point. He is decreeing a thing into existence; the thing does not yet exist. So the "it" that He addresses, the "it" that the pronoun *lahu* points to — is *nonexistence.* He is speaking to what is not.

Stay with me, because this is staggering and it is easy to rush past.

You and I can only command what already exists. I can tell a person to stand; the person is there to hear me. I can tell a dog to sit; the dog exists to obey. Command always presupposes a *someone* who can receive it. **But Allah issues His command to nothing.** He addresses the void. And the void's *first act of existence* — its very first motion of being — is its obedience. The thing does not first exist and *then* comply. **Its coming-into-being IS its compliance.** To hear "Be" and to *be* are, for the created thing, the same instant.

[PAUSE]

Let that reorder something in you. There has never been, and never could be, a created thing that disobeyed *this* command. *Kun* has a perfect record. Everything that exists, exists because it said "yes" to "Be" — and saying yes was identical with beginning to exist. Your own existence, right now, is the echo of your "yes." You are, at this moment, the ongoing answer to a word spoken to your own nonexistence.

Here I owe you honesty, because the scholars discuss this carefully and I will not overstate what it means when the ayah says "He *says.*" Classical commentary is precise on this point. Al-Jalalayn clarifies that the *qawl* — the "saying" — in this verse is to be understood as the divine will to bring something into existence, noting that the thing "comes into being immediately following the will, which is the meaning of the mentioned saying." This is not a retreat from the text. It is the text's own depth being honored. You and I are creatures of language; we receive everything through words. So we are given the form of a word — the most compressed, arresting word imaginable — as the vehicle for something that ultimately exceeds what words are. The "saying" is not what does the work. It is how the gaplessness of His power is communicated to beings who live entirely in the gap.

**Third — the *fa-* that leaves no room for delay: *fa-yakūn***

*Fa-yakūn.* "And it is."

The small letter *fa-* opening this word has a name in Arabic grammar: *fa' al-ta'qīb* — the *fa* of immediate sequential consequence. When two things follow each other with no interval between them, this is the particle you use. Not "and then," not "and afterward," but "and *immediately.*"

Al-Jalalayn is precise: the created thing comes into existence *'aqiba al-irāda* — immediately following the will. The moment of His willing and the moment of the thing's existence are not separated by anything — not an hour, not a breath, not the space of a syllable. The *fa-* is the bridge with no length.

One further detail worth pausing on: the scholars of Quranic recitation note that *fa-yakūn* carries two transmitted readings. In one: *fa-yakūnU* — with a ḍamma on the nūn, a plain indicative declaration, "and it *is.*" In the other: *fa-yakūnA* — with a fatḥa, implying the particle *an* before it, giving the clause the force of a purposive response: "so that it *may be,*" or "so that it *becomes.*" These two readings are not in competition. The first tells you what happens — existence follows immediately. The second tells you that this existence is willed and purposive, not accidental. Together they affirm: it *is,* because it was always meant to be.

## PART 2: THE ANSWER TO THE QUESTION

Now we can return to the door we opened in the introduction.

If He can call the entire universe into being with two letters — if His decree and His will are the same motion, if the gap between wanting and getting does not exist for Him — then why, in the verse immediately before this one, does He walk you through the slow, nine-stage unfolding of a human life? Dust. Drop. Clot. Infant. Child. Maturity. Old age. Some taken earlier. An appointed term.

Why the long way around?

[PAUSE]

The answer is not that He *had* to take the long way. The ayah you just spent an hour inside has demolished that idea completely. He is *al-mutafarrid* — uniquely singular — in giving life and causing death. *Kun* has never failed. There is no constraint on His side that makes gradualness necessary.

The gradualness is not for Him. It is **for you.**

Think about what you are given in those nine stages. You are not *deposited* into existence as a finished product. You are *grown into it.* You are given the experience of *becoming.* The infant does not know it is becoming a child; the child does not know it is becoming an adult. Each stage is its own world, complete in itself, with its own perceptions and relationships and discoveries. You are not denied the journey by being sent straight to the destination. **The journey is the gift.** The gradualness is mercy, dressed as process.

Al-Tabari's reading of this whole passage frames both ayahs together as *hujaj* — proofs, arguments, evidence deployed against those who dispute. The gradual creation of ayah 67 is not there as background scenery. It is itself an argument. *You have watched yourself become.* You experienced stages. You know what it is to be assembled over time by a will that is not your own. Every transition — from the clot to the infant, from the infant to the child — was a *kun* stretched across time, expressed in a form you could receive. The process you call "natural" is not the opposite of *kun fa-yakūn*. It is *kun fa-yakūn* given to a creature who can only receive the gift of being in increments.

And then ayah 68 delivers the punch of the proof. The One who grew you slowly *does not need slowness.* He took the long way out of care for your receiving, not out of any constraint on His giving.

[PAUSE]

Now the disputers' objection crumbles from the inside.

They said: *a body becomes dust. Dust cannot stand up again.* They were imagining resurrection as a long process — the same slow assembly, run in reverse and then forward again. They were measuring the Maker by the measurements of the made.

But *kun fa-yakūn* says: **the speed of your reassembly is the speed of His word.** The same two letters that brought you out of nothing the first time do not forget how. There is no second creation that is harder than the first, because the first was not hard either. It was a word. The second is a word. And as al-Muyassar closes its commentary on this ayah — plainly, finally — *lā rādda li-qaḍā'ihi*: **there is no one to repel His decree.** The disputers dispute. But disputation is not a *rādd* — not a force capable of pushing back against what He has decreed. They fold their arms; His word unfolds the worlds.

This is the answer the surah gives to the people of argument: not a lecture on the mechanics of resurrection, but a *redirection of scale.* You are not asking the right question. The question is not whether a scattered body can be reassembled. The question is whether the One who assembled it in the first place can do it again. And if you have stayed with these twelve words — *He is the One who gives life and causes death, and when He decrees a matter, He only says to it "Be," and it is* — you already know the answer.

## PART 3: TADABBUR

Here is what I want to leave you with.

You are, right now, in the middle of the long way. You are somewhere in the stages — perhaps past the infant, perhaps in the middle of the aching years, perhaps further along than you'd like. The world feels thick with process. With effort and resistance and the grind. With things that take longer than you need them to. With waiting that wears on you.

And somewhere in your life there is a thing you are waiting for. A door you knocked on that hasn't opened. A healing that hasn't come. A person you prayed for. A situation that has not resolved. You know the weight of the gap between wanting and getting. You have been living in it.

The ayah does not promise you that the gap will close on your timeline. It does something more fundamental. It tells you who it is that stands on the other side of the gap.

**He is the One who gives life and causes death.** Right now. Continuously. The verb is still running. Your next heartbeat is not a mechanism proceeding on its own momentum — it is Him, *yuḥyī*, giving life to you again, in this exact moment, because He chose to.

**And when He decrees a matter, He only says to it: "Be."**

That word — two letters, no waiting — has been spoken to everything you have ever loved or lost or hoped for. Every star above you. Every cell within you. Every person you have ever known. All of it answering to the same word, before you or they existed to hear it.

[PAUSE]

Your nonexistence was once addressed by that word, and it became *you.*

Whatever you are waiting for: if it is written in His decree, then it is already answered. The only question is when the *fa-yakūn* — the immediate consequence — lands in the dimension of time that you inhabit. But from His side, there is no gap. There never was. The *fa* of immediacy is already in place. The bridge has no length. The word has already been spoken.

What this calls for, from a creature like you who lives in time: not urgency that forgets who holds time. Not despair that mistakes the gap for absence. But the deep, unhurried *tawakkul* of someone who knows that the One behind the decree needs nothing to fulfill it — no scaffolding, no favorable conditions, no arrangement of circumstance — except the submission that releases the outcome back to Him.

*Kun.* And it is.

---

*Huwa lladhī yuḥyī wa yumīt. Fa-idhā qaḍā amran fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu kun fa-yakūn.*

He is the One. He is giving life. He is causing death. He is decreeing. He is saying. And it is.

Be — and it is.
