# The Judge Who Hears and Sees — and the gods Who Settle Nothing

## Surah Ghafir 40:20

> [ayah:40:20] وَٱللَّهُ يَقْضِى بِٱلْحَقِّ ۖ وَٱلَّذِينَ يَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِهِۦ لَا يَقْضُونَ بِشَىْءٍ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْبَصِيرُ

*wa-llāhu yaqḍī bi-l-ḥaqq, wa-lladhīna yadʿūna min dūnihi lā yaqḍūna bi-shayʾ, inna-llāha huwa s-samīʿu l-baṣīr*

> "And Allah judges with truth, while those they call upon besides Him judge nothing at all. Indeed, it is Allah who is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing."

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word, I want you to feel where we are standing.

Surah Ghafir is a Meccan surah, and it has the temperature of a courtroom. Not a lecture hall — a courtroom. The whole surah keeps pulling you toward one moment: the Day it calls **yawm al-āzifah**, the Day of the Approaching Calamity, the Day that creeps up close, when — as the ayah just before our passage says — *the hearts are at the throats, choking* (40:18). Picture that physically. The feeling of your heart climbing into your throat. The body's response to a verdict you cannot escape.

And then, right before our ayah, comes one of the most quietly terrifying statements in the Qur'an:

> *yaʿlamu khāʾinata l-aʿyuni wa mā tukhfī ṣ-ṣudūr*
> "He knows the **treachery of the eyes** and what the breasts conceal." (40:19)

Sit with that. Not your actions. Not even your words. **The treachery of the eyes.** The glance you stole and thought no one saw. The flicker of contempt you hid behind a polite face. The look of longing toward something forbidden, lasting half a second, that you yourself barely admitted to. Allah names it. He has it on record.

So now the world is built. We are standing in a courtroom presided over by a Judge who has surveillance footage of your *eyelids*. And in that courtroom, the disbelievers of Mecca have brought their lawyers — their idols, their intercessors, the gods they call upon "besides Him."

**Here is the question this ayah answers, and I want you to feel the hunger for it before we begin:** When the case is finally heard — whose verdict counts? You have spent your whole life appealing to tribunals. The court of public opinion. The approval of people. The gods of status and reputation. The ayah is about to ask you, very gently: *the things you keep taking your case to — can they actually rule on anything at all?*

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Verb That Appears Twice — *yaqḍī* / *lā yaqḍūn*

### First, the concept

In English, when we say a judge "judges," we mostly mean he *forms an opinion* — he weighs and decides. But the Arabic verb here, from the root **q-ḍ-y** (قضى), is much heavier than that. It is one of those words English has to chase with five different words and still not catch.

**Qaḍā** means: to decide — *and to settle the matter so it is finished*. To decree. To carry out. To bring to completion. When you "pay off" a debt in Arabic, you *qaḍā* it — you settle it, finish it, close the file. When a matter is **maqḍī**, it is done, sealed, over. This is why the prayer you make up after its time is called *qaḍāʾ* — you are *completing* what was left open.

So *qaḍā* is not a verb of opinion. It is a verb of **finality**. It is the gavel coming down and the case being closed forever.

Hold that, because everyday language hides it. When we hear "Allah judges," we imagine a deliberation. The Arabic says: **Allah closes the case. Permanently. With nothing left open.**

### Now open the words

Notice that this same root appears **twice** in our ayah, deliberately set against itself:

- About Allah: **يَقْضِي** — *yaqḍī* — "He decides-and-settles."
- About the false gods: **لَا يَقْضُونَ** — *lā yaqḍūn* — "they do **not** decide-and-settle."

Same verb. One affirmed, one negated. The ayah is staging a confrontation between the only One who can close a case and the things that cannot close anything.

Let's sit with this.

The grammatical structure here is a contrast built on a single repeated word, and that repetition is doing something a synonym could never do. If Allah had said "Allah judges, but the idols are *powerless*," you would have two separate ideas sitting side by side. Instead He uses the *exact same verb* for both — and then strips it from the idols with **lā**. It is like watching someone hold up a key and say, "This opens the lock. And this —" holding up an identical-looking key — "does *not* open the lock." The shock is in the sameness. They *look* like they should work. People have spent lifetimes inserting them and turning.

Here's what's remarkable. The classical commentators draw the logical sword out of this very repetition. Al-Jalālayn notes the unspoken argument buried in the contrast: *"so how could they be partners with Allah?"* The whole case for idolatry — for taking *anything* besides Allah as the thing you orient your life around — collapses on one fact. **A partner who cannot rule on anything is not a partner. It is furniture.**

[PAUSE]

What does this one repeated word reveal? That tawḥīd is not first a doctrine you *believe*. It is a fact about **who can actually close your case**. There is exactly one Being in existence whose verdict on your life is final and real. Everyone and everything else you have been appealing to has been issuing rulings that bind no one.

## Element Two: The Asymmetry — *bi-l-ḥaqq* versus *bi-shayʾ*

### First, the concept

Arabic, like every language, lets you measure the *weight* of a thing by the word you attach to it. Watch what each act of "judging" is paired with in this ayah:

- Allah judges **بِٱلْحَقِّ** — *bi-l-ḥaqq* — "with **the** truth, the real, the established right." Definite. Maximal. *Al-Ḥaqq* is itself one of Allah's names — the Real, the Truth that has no falsehood in it.
- The false gods judge **بِشَيْءٍ** — *bi-shayʾ* — "with **a** thing." Indefinite. Tiny. Vague. The smallest, most generic noun in the entire Arabic language. *Shayʾ* is the word you use when you cannot name what you mean: "give me *a thing*," "is there *anything*?"

### Now read what is present — and what is absent

This is where I need you to read an *absence* as carefully as a presence, because this ayah's deepest blow lands in something it refuses to say.

Look again. Allah judges **with the Truth** — the maximum. And the idols? You would expect the ayah to say, "and the false gods judge *with falsehood*." A clean opposite. Truth versus lies. Right verdict versus wrong verdict.

**But that is not what it says.**

It says they **lā yaqḍūna bi-shayʾ** — they do not judge with **a thing**. With *anything*. Not "they judge wrongly." **They do not judge at all.** Their gavel never even comes down. Stay with this — because it is more devastating than calling them liars.

To judge *wrongly*, you have to at least be a participant. A corrupt judge is still in the courtroom. A false verdict is still a verdict. But the ayah denies the idols even that. They are not bad judges. **They are not in the building.** They issue not a single ruling, not even a wrong one — not one *shayʾ*, not the smallest sliver of a decision.

[PAUSE]

Al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both put their finger on the *reason* embedded here, and it is breathtakingly simple: *"because they do not know anything, and have no power over anything, and own nothing"* — *lā taʿlamu shayʾan wa lā taqdiru ʿalā shayʾ*. Notice the word *shayʾ* echoing again. They cannot judge a *thing* because they do not know a *thing* and cannot do a *thing*. The emptiness is total and it is consistent all the way down.

Here's what's remarkable about the rhetoric. The ayah gives Allah the most weighted word in the language — *al-Ḥaqq* — and gives the rivals the most weightless — *shayʾ* — and then puts a *negation* even on that weightless word. It is a deliberate descent: from *the Truth itself*, down to *a thing*, down to *not even a thing*. The false gods are placed below the smallest possible unit of reality and then deleted from it.

### Let me bring this home

Think about who, or what, you actually take your case to.

When you have been wronged and you are aching to be vindicated — where does the appeal go? To the group chat, so people will rule in your favor. To the replay in your own head where you finally win the argument. To the metric, the follower count, the promotion, the parent's approval you are still chasing at forty. These are the tribunals of modern life, and we are *constantly* filing motions with them.

And this ayah says, with surgical calm: every one of those courts is empty. Not corrupt. **Empty.** The verdict you are desperate for from them — they cannot issue it, because they cannot *see* your case and cannot *do* anything about it. You have been mailing your appeals to a building with no judge inside. The letters pile up at the door. Nothing is ever ruled. Nothing is ever settled.

And so you stay un-vindicated, forever, because you took the case to a *shayʾ* instead of to **al-Ḥaqq**.

## Element Three: The Closing Names — *huwa s-Samīʿu l-Baṣīr*

### First, the concept: the pronoun that bars the door

The ayah ends: **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْبَصِيرُ** — *inna-llāha huwa s-samīʿu l-baṣīr.*

There is one tiny word here that English simply throws away, and it is carrying the whole weight of the ending: **هُوَ** — *huwa* — "He."

In Arabic, you do not need this word. "Indeed Allah is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing" is a complete sentence without it: *inna-llāha s-samīʿu l-baṣīr.* So why is *huwa* sitting there in the middle, doing apparently nothing?

This is a known structure the grammarians call **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — the "pronoun of separation," or the pronoun of exclusivity. When you drop this otherwise-unnecessary "He" between the subject and its description, it functions like a spotlight that narrows: it means **"He, and no one else."** It bars the door. It says: this quality belongs to *this One alone*.

So the ending does not merely say "Allah hears and sees." It says: **"It is Allah — He alone — who is THE Hearer, THE Seer."** The definite articles on *al-Samīʿ* and *al-Baṣīr* seal it further: not *a* hearer among hearers, but *The* Hearing, *The* Seeing, in the absolute.

### Now: why these two names, here, at this exact moment?

Names of Allah at the end of an ayah are never decoration. They are the *legal grounds* for everything the ayah just claimed. And these two — *al-Samīʿ*, *al-Baṣīr* — are the perfect, devastating proof of the whole verse. Watch how they snap the entire ayah shut.

**What does a judge actually require to judge?** Strip a courtroom down to its essentials. A real judge must be able to **hear** the testimony, and **see** the evidence. A judge who is deaf to the witnesses and blind to the proof is not a judge — he is a statue in a robe.

Now look at what the ayah has just told you about the false gods. Two of the roots in this very surah's argument — *samʿ* (hearing) and *baṣar* (seeing) — are precisely what idols, stone, status, and dead intercessors *lack*. They cannot hear your call. They cannot see your situation. **And that is exactly why they "judge nothing at all."** It is not an arbitrary failure. A thing that cannot hear and cannot see is structurally incapable of ruling on a case. The ending does not change the subject — **it explains the verdict.** Allah judges with truth *because* He alone truly hears and truly sees. The idols judge nothing *because* they do neither.

[PAUSE]

### And now look backward — the architecture reveals itself

Remember where we started. One ayah earlier:

> "He knows the **treachery of the eyes** (*khāʾinat al-aʿyun*) and **what the breasts conceal**." (40:19)

The eyes. What is hidden in the chest.

And now our ayah closes on **al-Baṣīr** — *the All-Seeing* — and **al-Samīʿ** — *the All-Hearing*.

Do you see the design? The passage that *began* by naming the stealthy glance of the eyes *ends* by naming Allah as **the One who Sees**. The treacherous look in 40:19 is caught precisely by *al-Baṣīr* in 40:20. And what the breast conceals — the unspoken vow, the whispered intention — is caught by *al-Samīʿ*, for Allah hears even what is never said aloud. This is why al-Qurṭubī reads *yaqḍī bi-l-ḥaqq* as: **He recompenses the one who lowered his gaze from the forbidden, and the one who let it linger.** The verdict in 40:20 is the gavel coming down on the eyes of 40:19.

The two ayahs are a single closing argument. *He sees the glance you hid (19). Therefore He, and He alone, can close your case in truth (20) — because He, and He alone, is The Seer and The Hearer.*

## Bridge

So the words have shown us a structure. A single verb, *qaḍā*, held up twice — once full, once emptied. An asymmetry that places the rivals beneath even the smallest *thing*. And a closing pair of names that turn out to be the legal ground for the whole verdict: only the One who hears and sees can judge, and He hears and sees *alone*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how you are living right now.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Tribunal You Actually Appeal To

Let me start where you live, not where the ayah lives.

There is a courtroom running in your head almost every waking hour, and you may never have noticed it. You replay conversations to win them. You imagine explaining yourself to someone whose opinion of you stings. You curate a photo, a sentence, a success, and some invisible jury nods or withholds its nod. You are, all day long, **filing for a verdict.** Human beings are vindication-seeking creatures. We cannot bear to be in the wrong in the eyes of the court that matters to us.

So the only real question is: *which court matters to you?* Because you will get your sense of being okay — or not okay — from whatever tribunal you have unconsciously made supreme.

And here is where the ayah walks in and quietly dismantles the whole thing. *Wa-lladhīna yadʿūna min dūnihi lā yaqḍūna bi-shayʾ.* The things you call upon besides Him **judge nothing at all.**

Think about what it means to spend your life seeking acquittal from a court that cannot rule. The approval of the crowd — it shifts every week; today's verdict is reversed tomorrow by the same crowd, because the crowd cannot *see* you, it only sees the fragment you showed it. The replay in your head where you win — it settles nothing, because you wake the next morning and the ache is still there; the case reopens. The parent whose approval you chased — even if you finally win it, even if they finally say it, something in you stays unsettled, because deep down you know **they did not see the whole of you.** They ruled on a fragment. And a ruling on a fragment cannot close a case that is about your whole self.

[PAUSE]

This is the agony of taking your case to a *shayʾ*. The verdict never lands, because the judge never truly saw the evidence. You can win every argument in the group chat and remain, at three in the morning, completely un-vindicated.

Now feel the other side. *Wa-llāhu yaqḍī bi-l-ḥaqq.* There exists **one** Judge who will rule on the *whole* of you — including the treachery of the eyes you never confessed, including the breast's concealment no one else ever heard — and rule with *al-Ḥaqq*, the Truth, with nothing missed and nothing distorted. For the one who has been wronged and never believed, never vindicated, never heard: **your case is not lost. It was simply filed in the wrong court.** There is a Judge who heard every word, saw every injustice done to you in the dark, and His gavel is the only one that actually comes down.

The believer's relief and the believer's trembling are the *same fact* seen from two sides. The very completeness that terrifies you — He sees *everything* — is the completeness that will finally vindicate you. The court that misses nothing against you is the only court that will also miss nothing *for* you.

**What changes tomorrow?** Catch yourself filing the appeal. The next time you feel that hot need to be proven right — to the group, the parent, the rival, the phone — pause and ask: *am I taking this to a court that can rule, or to a* shayʾ *that judges nothing?* Then re-file. Take the case where it can actually be heard. Make duʿāʾ — literally *call upon* (the very verb *yadʿūn* in the ayah, redirected) the One who hears. That is what it looks like to move your case from the empty courtroom to the real one.

## Theme Two: The Hunger to Be Truly Seen and Heard

Now let me take you somewhere even more tender.

Beneath the need for a verdict is a need even deeper — older than any argument. **The need to be witnessed.** To have one being in the universe who has actually seen the whole of your life and heard the whole of your heart. Psychologists circle this constantly: the deepest human wound is not being disliked; it is being *unseen*. To go through your days, your private struggles, your unspoken sacrifices, your quiet sins and quiet repentances — and feel that *no one knows.* That it all happened in a room with no witness.

We try to solve this with people. We tell our story, hoping to be understood. And people — even the ones who love us most — catch a fragment. They see the face we show. They hear the words we choose. Even your closest friend has never seen *khāʾinat al-aʿyun* — the treachery of your own eyes — and never heard *mā tukhfī ṣ-ṣudūr* — what your chest conceals. There is a chamber inside every human being that no other human has ever entered.

And the ayah ends by naming the One who lives in that chamber: **huwa s-Samīʿu l-Baṣīr.** He — and He alone — The Hearer, The Seer.

[PAUSE]

Let this turn slowly, because it has two faces and you need both.

**The first face is exposure.** Someone has seen the glance you hid. Someone heard the thing you never said aloud. There is no chamber He has not entered. For a moment this feels like nakedness, like the heart climbing to the throat in 40:18.

**But stay with me, because the second face is the gift** — and it is the thing your soul has been starving for without a name. *You have never, for one second of your existence, been unwitnessed.* The sacrifice no one thanked you for — He saw it. The kindness you did when no camera was running — He saw it. The tears you cried where no one could hear — He heard every one. The years of quiet faithfulness that no human ever clapped for — there was a Witness the entire time, *al-Samīʿ al-Baṣīr*, and not a single moment was lost on Him.

Do you understand what this does to loneliness? The terror of being unseen *cannot be true* for the believer. It is metaphysically impossible. The One whose seeing is so total that He logs the flicker of an eyelid is the same One who has been watching the good in you that the world overlooked.

And notice — this is *exactly why* He can judge with truth. A judge who only saw your fragments would rule on your fragments. But the One who saw the *whole* — the hidden glance *and* the hidden tear — is the only One who can give a verdict that is finally, completely **just to you.** Being fully seen is the precondition of being fully vindicated. You cannot have the second without the first.

**What changes tomorrow?** When you do something good and no one notices — and the small starved part of you wants to announce it, to get it onto the record of some human court — pause and let it be enough that *al-Baṣīr* saw it. Practice doing one thing each day purely for the Witness who already saw it. That is the cure for the performance that exhausts us: you do not need to broadcast your case to the empty courtrooms when the only Judge who matters was already in the room.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing it together

Look at how the two roads we walked are actually one road approached from opposite ends.

From the **words**, we found: a single verb *qaḍā* held up full and then emptied; the rivals placed beneath even the smallest *shayʾ*; and a closing *huwa* that hands the powers of hearing and seeing to **Allah alone** — the very two faculties a judge requires.

From the **themes**, we found: a human being filing endless appeals to courts that cannot rule, and aching to be seen by a witness who catches more than a fragment.

They meet here: **The reason the false gods judge nothing is the reason the human heart stays restless — they cannot hear and they cannot see.** And the reason Allah's judgment is *al-ḥaqq* is the reason the heart can finally rest — He alone hears all and sees all. The tawḥīd of the linguistic analysis and the peace of the thematic one are the same truth: *there is exactly one courtroom in existence with a Judge actually inside it, and He has heard and seen everything — against you, and for you.*

## Questions to carry

1. When I feel the burning need to be proven right, *which court* am I instinctively filing my appeal with — and can it actually rule on anything?
2. Is there a wound, an injustice done to me in the dark, that I have given up on ever being vindicated — and what changes if I believe *al-Samīʿ al-Baṣīr* recorded all of it?
3. What good have I done lately that I felt the itch to *publish* — and could I let it be enough that He saw it?
4. The "treachery of the eyes" — what is the small, hidden thing I assume no one witnessed? How does my life look if I truly believe it is on the record of *al-Baṣīr*?
5. Where, exactly, have I been mailing my appeals to a building with no judge inside?

## One-sentence distillation

There is only one Being in existence who can close the case of your life — because He alone truly hears it and truly sees it — and everything else you have been appealing to rules on nothing at all.

## The closing invitation

Let something shift. Not a new belief added to a list — a *redirection* of where you take your case. Every day you are filing appeals; the only question is the address. The invitation of this ayah is simply to stop mailing them to the empty courtrooms — the crowd, the metric, the replay, the approval you are still chasing — and to bring the whole of yourself, the seen and the hidden, before the only Judge who was ever actually in the room.

## A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, *al-Ḥaqq*, You judge with truth and Yours is the only verdict that is real — settle our case in Your truth, not in the empty courts we keep running to. O *al-Samīʿ*, You heard what we never said aloud; O *al-Baṣīr*, You saw the good no one clapped for and the glance we hid in shame — let us live as people who know we are never, for one moment, unwitnessed, and make the knowledge that You see us our peace and not our terror. Āmīn.
