# When the Followers Turn on Their Leaders

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic, then sound, then meaning.

> **وَإِذْ يَتَحَآجُّونَ فِى ٱلنَّارِ**
> *wa idh yataḥājjūna fi-n-nār*
> "And when they dispute with one another in the Fire..."

> **فَيَقُولُ ٱلضُّعَفَـٰٓؤُاْ لِلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوٓاْ إِنَّا كُنَّا لَكُمْ تَبَعًا**
> *fa-yaqūlu-ḍ-ḍuʿafāʾu lilladhīna-stakbarū innā kunnā lakum tabaʿā*
> "...and the weak will say to those who were arrogant: 'We were your followers...'"

> **فَهَلْ أَنتُم مُّغْنُونَ عَنَّا نَصِيبًا مِّنَ ٱلنَّارِ**
> *fa-hal antum mughnūna ʿannā naṣīban mina-n-nār*
> "'...so can you avert from us a portion of the Fire?'"

And then the answer comes back, and then the desperate turn to the angels, and then the door closes. Four verses. One scene. A conversation in Hell.

I want you to slow down before we enter it, because if we rush in, we will read it as a horror story about other people — the disbelievers, the tyrants, them — and we will miss that it is a mirror held up to something we do every single day.

**Rebuild the world for a moment.**

Think about the world the first listeners lived in. Mecca. A society built almost entirely on the authority of leaders — the chiefs of Quraysh, the elders, the men of wealth and lineage whose word organized everything. When a person in that world resisted Islam, very often the real reason was not an argument. It was a relationship. *I follow my people. I follow the chiefs. They know better. They carry the weight. If they're wrong, that's on them, not me.*

This was not a Meccan disease. It is the oldest arrangement in human history: the many give their loyalty to the few, and in exchange they hand over something precious — their responsibility. The leader thinks for them. The crowd decides for them. And there is a quiet, almost invisible contract underneath it: *if this goes wrong, you will carry me.*

Surah Ghafir is, from beginning to end, a surah about power. It is named after the Forgiver, but its great drama is Pharaoh — the supreme leader, the man who said *I am your lord, the highest* — and the one believer in his court who dared to speak. The whole surah is asking: *what is authority actually worth? Who can really carry whom?*

And then, right here, Allah does something stunning. He fast-forwards. He takes the leaders and the followers — still locked in the same relationship — and He drops them into the Fire together. And He lets us listen.

**Here is the hook.** [PAUSE]

In this world, the follower says to the leader: *I'm with you. Carry me.* And the leader, holding power, seems able to.

In the Fire, the follower says the exact same words — *I was your follower* — and waits for the contract to pay out.

What happens next is one of the most quietly devastating exchanges in the entire Quran. And the way Allah builds it — word by word — does not just *tell* us the alliance fails. It makes us *feel* the floor give way.

Let's listen carefully enough to hear it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yataḥājjūn* — the verb that turns them against each other

Before we touch the word, let me teach you a small piece of how Arabic works, because it is the entire key to this verse.

Arabic verbs come in *forms* — patterns layered on top of a root, each pattern bending the meaning in a specific direction. Imagine a root as a lump of clay, and the forms as different molds you press it into.

There is one form — grammarians call it **Form VI** — whose mold means *mutual, back-and-forth, reciprocal action.* Two or more parties doing something **to each other.**

Here's an everyday example. The root *k-t-b* means writing. Push it into Form VI and you get *takātaba* — "they corresponded **with each other**," letters flying both directions. The root *q-t-l* means killing. In Form VI it becomes *taqātala* — "they fought **one another**." The form itself tells you: this is not one person acting. This is a collision. Bodies turning toward each other.

Hold that.

Now the word Allah chose:

> **يَتَحَآجُّونَ** — *yataḥājjūn*

The root is *ḥ-j-j*. It is the root of *ḥujja* — a proof, an argument, the thing you marshal to win a case. (It is also the root of *ḥajj*, the pilgrimage — a word built on the idea of repeatedly heading toward something, advancing.) So at its heart this root is about **bringing an argument, pressing a case.**

And Allah did not put it in the simple form. He put it in **Form VI** — the reciprocal mold.

*Yataḥājjūn.* They **argue against one another.** They throw proofs back and forth. They press their cases **at each other.** Al-Qurtubī glosses it plainly: *yakhtaṣimūn* — they quarrel, they dispute. Al-Ṭabarī's reading is congruent: *yatakhāṣimūn.* Al-Muyassar widens it: the people of the Fire dispute, each blaming the other. And al-Ṭabarī frames the passage in a way that sharpens the scene further — he places this dispute directly after the verse of *yawm al-āzifa*, the Day of the Approaching, when hearts rise to the throats, strangled and choked, unable to release. The hearts that were throttled by dread in the moment of arrival now find their only outlet in mutual accusation. The constriction becomes a quarrel.

Let's sit with this. [PAUSE]

Notice **who** these people are. In the dunya, they were one bloc. The followers and the leaders were a single unit — same direction, same loyalty, same refusal. They stood shoulder to shoulder against the messengers. They were a *we.*

And here is the first thing the verse does, before a single sentence of dialogue: it tells you their grammatical relationship. The form chosen for the very first verb is the form of **turning against each other.** Not *yaḥtajjūn* (they argue, generally). *Yataḥājjūn* — they argue **mutually, reciprocally, face to face.**

The alliance is already gone. Allah encodes its collapse in the *shape of the verb* before anyone opens their mouth.

Notice something else — the sound of it. Say it: *ya-ta-ḤĀJ-jūn.* That heavy throat consonant, the *ḥā*, grinding into the doubled *jīm.* It is a guttural, clashing, abrasive sound. The word **sounds like quarreling.** The mouth itself has to scrape to produce it. This is not decoration; in the Quran's sound-world, the texture of the word and the texture of the reality are made to match. The reconciliation that was never real now grinds itself apart, and you can hear the grinding in the consonants.

Here's what's remarkable. In this world, what holds groups of people together in falsehood is almost never love. It is **usefulness.** *You're useful to me. I'm useful to you. As long as we're both useful, we're loyal.* It is a transaction wearing the costume of a bond.

And a transaction lasts exactly as long as the payout is possible.

The Fire is the place where no one can pay anyone. So the moment they arrive — *yataḥājjūn* — the costume comes off. There is nothing left to be loyal *for.* And the same people who marched together turn and press their arguments **into each other's faces.**

What this single word reveals: **falsehood has no glue.** What looked like brotherhood was only a shared escape route, and the instant the route closes, it curdles into accusation. The dispute in the Fire is not a new event. It is the *truth* about the alliance, finally allowed to surface.

### Element Two: *Tabaʿan* — the word in which a self disappears

Now the followers speak. And their very first description of themselves is a single word that should stop us cold.

> **إِنَّا كُنَّا لَكُمْ تَبَعًا**
> *innā kunnā lakum tabaʿā*
> "Indeed, we were, for you, *tabaʿan.*"

We translate it "followers." But look closely, because the *grammar* of this one word carries the whole tragedy.

Let me teach the device first. In Arabic, there is a difference between an **agent-noun** and a **verbal-noun** (a *maṣdar*).

An agent-noun names *the one who does the thing.* "A follower" — *tābiʿ.* It still has a person in it. There is a doer standing inside the word.

A verbal-noun names *the act itself,* in the abstract. Not "the runner" but "running." Not "the follower" but "the following." It is the action lifted away from any person, made into a bare thing.

Now — *tabaʿan.* The grammarians examined this word closely. Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī both record the discussion: the Basran scholars said *tabaʿ* is the **plural** of *tābiʿ* (a follower). But — and this is the point — they note its strange behavior: it is used for **one and for many alike**, without changing its form, *because it carries the sense of a maṣdar, a verbal noun.* The Kufan scholars went further: they said *tabaʿ* has **no singular at all** — it is a collective, like an abstract noun. *Had it been an ordinary plural,* al-Baghawī notes, *they would have said* aṭbāʿ.

Stay with what that means. [PAUSE]

The weak ones, in the Fire, looking for the leaders to save them, do not say *innā kunnā lakum tābiʿīn* — "we were your **followers**," plural people, agents, selves who chose. They say *innā kunnā lakum **tabaʿā*** — we were, for you, **following.** The bare act. The abstract noun. A word with no individual standing inside it.

Do you feel it? They describe themselves with a word **that has no singular.** A word where the persons have dissolved into the verb. They are not even *followers* anymore. They have become **the following itself.**

This is the most precise possible description of what happens to a human being who hands over their agency completely. You do not just *do* a lot of following. Slowly, the doing eats the doer. The self that was supposed to *choose* shrinks and shrinks until there is nothing left to point at — until the most honest word you have for yourself is not a person at all, but a function. *We were your following.*

Classical commentators saw in this scene something specific about the mechanism. Al-Muyassar calls them *al-atbāʿ al-muqallidūn* — the **blind imitating followers**, the ones whose following was *taqlīd*: imitation without understanding, deference without discernment. And then he adds a detail that changes the moral picture entirely. The leaders, he notes, were not merely unhelpful guides; they actively *zayyanu lahum ṭarīqa al-shaqāʾ* — **beautified for them the path of misery.** They dressed the catastrophic in the colors of the reasonable. They ornamented the wrong road until it gleamed.

This matters enormously for what comes next. The followers' self-defense is: *look how completely we surrendered ourselves to you.* But al-Muyassar's account of the dunya shows us that they were not passive victims — they were people who found the wrong path attractive, who let it be beautified for them, who did not push back against the ornamentation. You cannot present your own seduction as your innocence.

And here is the heartbreak buried in it. Why are they saying this? They are saying it to **make a claim.** They think their dissolved self is a *credential.* *Look how completely we gave ourselves to you. We kept nothing back. We were following, pure following — so now you owe us. Carry us.*

They present the erasure of their own agency as the reason they deserve to be rescued. *I made myself nothing for you — so save my nothing.*

What this single word reveals: **the deepest danger of following is not that you might follow the wrong leader. It is that you might follow so completely that there is no longer a "you" left to be saved.** The maṣdar with no singular is the grammar of a vanished self.

### Element Three: *Mughnūn ʿannā naṣīban* — the request that has already surrendered

Now hear what they actually ask for. Because they do not ask to be saved.

> **فَهَلْ أَنتُم مُّغْنُونَ عَنَّا نَصِيبًا مِّنَ ٱلنَّارِ**
> *fa-hal antum mughnūna ʿannā naṣīban mina-n-nār*
> "...so can you avail us — avert from us — *a portion* of the Fire?"

Two words here do quiet, devastating work.

The first is **مُّغْنُونَ** — *mughnūn.* Its root, *gh-n-y*, is the root of *ghinā* — **wealth, sufficiency, being free of need.** It is the root behind one of Allah's own names, *al-Ghaniyy*, the Self-Sufficient, the One who needs nothing and to whom all need turns. The verb-form here, *aghnā ʿan*, means **to avail someone, to suffice for them, to make them free of need** in some matter — to be the one who covers the cost so the other doesn't have to.

Al-Jalālayn adds a sharper gloss: *dāfiʿūna* — **those who push away, ward off, deflect.** Not the soft sense of "availing" in the abstract but the active image of standing between someone and an oncoming force, pressing it back. The followers are asking: *can you push the Fire off us?* They want a seawall. They are asking whether the powerful — who once looked like seawalls — can still function as one.

Feel the irony built into the root. In the dunya, these leaders were the people of *ghinā* — wealth, sufficiency, resource. They were precisely the ones who *seemed* able to make you free of need. *Follow me and you'll never have to worry. I'll cover it.* The followers are now reaching for exactly that promise: *you were the sufficient ones — so be sufficient for us now. Push this back.* They are calling in the one thing the powerful always sell: the illusion that they can absorb your costs.

The second word is **نَصِيبًا** — *naṣīban.* "A portion. A share." Its root, *n-ṣ-b*, carries the sense of something **fixed, set up, allotted** — a stake driven into the ground, a measured allocation. A *naṣīb* is your assigned share, your fixed cut. Al-Jalālayn reads it here as *jazāʾ* — **recompense, what is owed.** Not a random slice of suffering but the portion of punishment that belongs to each person by the logic of reckoning.

Now read the request with both words alive: *Can you push away — can you ward off — even the portion of what we are owed?*

And here is where we must **read the absence.** [PAUSE]

Listen to what they do *not* ask for.

They do not ask, *save us.*
They do not ask, *get us out.*
They do not ask, *take all of it.*

They ask: *can you ward off a **portion**? A **share**? A cut of our recompense?*

Even in their plea, they have already conceded that they will burn. The request has surrendered before it is spoken. The most they dare to hope for is that some slice of an eternal sentence might be transferred to someone else — to shoulders stronger than theirs. Not escape. A *portion.* And even that concedes the ledger is real: they call it *naṣīb*, their *due*, their *recompense* — they are not arguing innocence. They are asking someone to absorb a debt they know belongs to them.

The grammar of the request has already conceded the prison. It is only bargaining for a slightly smaller cell.

### Element Four: *Kullun fīhā* — the leaders' two truths

> [ayah:40:48] قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوٓا۟ إِنَّا كُلٌّ فِيهَآ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ حَكَمَ بَيْنَ ٱلْعِبَادِ
> *qāla alladhīna-stakbarū innā kullun fīhā inna Allāha qad ḥakama bayna al-ʿibād*
> "Those who were arrogant will say: 'Indeed, all of us are in it. Indeed, Allah has judged between the servants.'"

The leaders respond. And what they offer is not cruelty, not a counter-argument, not deflection. What they offer is the most stripped-down acknowledgment of reality in the entire scene. Two sentences. Both true. Neither of any help.

Look at *kullun.* In Arabic, *kull* can be fused to what it describes — attached, bound into the noun phrase. But here it stands alone with *tanwīn*, the grammatical nunation that marks independence: *kullun*, cut loose, self-contained. Grammarians read this construction as emphatic severance — not "all of us together" but "**every single one**, each separately, each in their own column." The tanwīn disaggregates. It is the word that announces the group is over. From here, each soul stands in its own account.

Al-Muyassar reads the leaders' response as *mubayyanūna ʿajzahum* — a **formal declaration of their own helplessness.** They are not being callous. They are being, for the first time, honest. *We cannot push anything off you. We cannot bear any of it. We are as trapped as you are. The power we had — we have it no longer.* Everything the followers thought the leaders possessed — and the leaders allowed them to believe they possessed — is announced void in a single clause.

But the second sentence is the more significant one. *Inna Allāha qad ḥakama bayna al-ʿibād.* "Indeed, Allah has already judged between the servants." Al-Muyassar gives the full weight: "Allah has apportioned the punishment among us according to what each deserves by His just decree." The word is *ḥakama* — not merely *qaḍā* (decreed) but *ḥukm*, a **ruling that stands, a verdict already handed down.** And the leaders invoke it not as a complaint but as a statement of final closure: *the verdict is rendered. There is no appeal. The scales have been set.*

Here is the quiet devastation. In the dunya, these were the people who claimed *access.* The ones who could make arrangements, navigate the powerful, broker what ordinary people could not. Now, at the moment of ultimate crisis, they do something they never did in the world: they *concede divine authority absolutely.* They acknowledge a ruling they cannot contest, a verdict that precedes any intercession they could offer. The intermediary power they once traded in — they now explicitly renounce it, and in renouncing it, they confirm the very argument the messengers made in the dunya: there is a reckoning, it is precise, and no human alliance can absorb what Allah has written for each soul.

There is a brutal irony in this. The leaders who made their followers feel safe from accountability now invoke accountability itself as the reason they cannot help. They have, at last, become honest witnesses to the truth they spent their lives evading.

### Element Five: The keepers of Jahannam and the closing door

> [ayah:40:49] وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ فِى ٱلنَّارِ لِخَزَنَةِ جَهَنَّمَ ٱدْعُوا۟ رَبَّكُمْ يُخَفِّفْ عَنَّا يَوْمًا مِّنَ ٱلْعَذَابِ
> *wa qāla alladhīna fi-n-nār li-khazanati jahannama udʿū rabbakum yukhaffif ʿannā yawman mina al-ʿadhāb*
> "And those in the Fire will say to the keepers of Hell: 'Call upon your Lord to lighten for us a day of the punishment.'"

When the leaders cannot help, the people of the Fire try one more move. Notice the grammar shifts: it is no longer *the followers* addressing *the leaders.* Now it is *alladhīna fi-n-nār* — **all those in the Fire**, the full company together, addressing the *khazana.*

The *khazana* are the angels appointed as custodians over Jahannam — its keepers, its wardens. They are a third party, entirely outside the mutual recrimination of leaders and followers. When the horizontal network of human alliance fails completely, the inhabitants of the Fire attempt a vertical move: find someone with standing before God and ask them to carry the request upward.

And what they ask for tells us how far hope has collapsed. *Yukhaffif ʿannā yawman mina al-ʿadhāb.* "Lighten for us even **a day** of the punishment." Not release. Not pardon. Not even a reprieve. One day — *yawman*, indefinite, singular — slightly less intense, from an eternal sentence.

Watch the arc of the requests across this passage. They started by asking the leaders to *avert a portion* of the Fire. The leaders couldn't. Now they are asking the keepers to ask *God* simply to *lighten a single day.* Each request smaller than the last. Each one a further concession that the punishment is real, permanent, and belongs to them. The contraction of hope across these four verses is one of the most methodically precise movements in the Quran — not arbitrary darkness but a careful, step-by-step closing of every door, in order.

But now comes the most unexpected turn in the scene.

The *khazana* do not pray. They do not refuse. They **ask a question.**

> **قَالُوٓاْ أَوَلَمْ تَكُ تَأْتِيكُمْ رُسُلُكُم بِٱلْبَيِّنَـٰتِ**
> "Did not your messengers come to you with clear proofs?"

This is a *recall before refusal.* Before the door closes, it opens just a crack — not to let them out, but to let memory in. The keepers' question is not cruelty. It is the question that makes everything that follows *coherent.* The punishment is not arbitrary. The keepers are ensuring that the people in the Fire understand why they are there, and that they understand that they already knew.

Did the messengers come? Were the proofs clear?

And the people of the Fire answer: **بَلَىٰ** — *balā.*

One word. Yes.

In Arabic, *balā* is the response to a negatively-framed question — "did they *not* come?" — and it means: *yes, they did come, contrary to any claim otherwise.* It is an affirmation against the negative. And in this context, its brevity is its full weight. No qualification. No defense. No "but." Just: yes.

Yes, the messengers came.
Yes, the proofs were clear.
Yes, we knew.

In one syllable, the entire case closes. Every evasion the followers tried in the scene before — *we were just following, someone else was responsible* — dissolves against this single word. The confession is complete, voluntary, immediate. The truth surfaces on its own, with no pressure.

Then the keepers say: *fa-dʿū* — **"then call."**

Not *we will call for you.* Not *we will intercede on your behalf.* Just: you call. The invitation to pray is returned to them — and in being returned, it is simultaneously withdrawn. Because what follows is the final sentence of the passage.

*Wa mā duʿāʾu al-kāfirīna illā fī ḍalāl.*

"And the prayer of the disbelievers is only in **ḍalāl.**"

The root *ḍ-l-l* carries the sense of wandering, going astray, being utterly lost — the classical Arabic of something that cannot find its way, that disappears into directionlessness. The prayer does not fail in the sense of being heard and rejected. It does not bounce back denied. It **wanders. It vanishes.** It enters *ḍalāl* — going nowhere, reaching nothing, dissolving in the emptiness between the suppliant and the Lord they once refused.

Hold *ḍalāl* for a moment. [PAUSE]

In the Quran, *ḍalāl* — going astray, being lost — is the word used in the dunya for what happens to a person who refuses guidance. *Al-Fātiḥa* itself asks Allah to save us from those who are in *ḍalāl.* The path of *ḍalāl* is the path that looks like a path but leads nowhere — a road that consumes the traveler without arriving. And here, in the Fire, it is not merely their *lives* that were in *ḍalāl.* Their very *prayers* enter it. The condition of their dunya — lost, straying, wandering from the truth — has become the condition of their *supplication.* Ḍalāl follows them in.

The symmetry is exact and devastating. In the world, *ḍalāl* was the name the messengers gave to the path these people chose. In the Fire, *ḍalāl* is the name for what their prayers become. The wandering that defined their lives now swallows even the last thing they attempt.

## THE MIRROR

Let me tie all of this back to us. Because this scene is not about strangers.

The followers in the Fire were not evil people, in the conventional sense. They were people who made a very ordinary choice: they let someone else do their thinking. They attached themselves to the powerful. They said, *I am with them.* And they handed over, piece by piece, the one thing that cannot be handed over — the *self* that will stand before Allah alone.

The leaders in the Fire were not monsters. They were people who offered something genuinely attractive: certainty, belonging, a plan. And then — al-Muyassar's phrase lodges itself here like a splinter — they *zayyanu lahum ṭarīqa al-shaqāʾ*: they beautified the path of misery. They made the catastrophic look reasonable. They dressed the wrong road in the right colors, and the followers walked it gladly. Which is why the followers' final argument — *we were just following* — cannot hold. You cannot present your own seduction as your innocence. The beautified path still required your feet.

Now, in the Fire, what the leaders most feared in the dunya — accountability — is the only thing on the table. And what the followers most craved — to have someone else carry the weight — is the thing most clearly, permanently impossible. The leaders cite divine justice. The keepers ask a question that expects only one answer. And the one answer — *balā*, yes — collapses every remaining defense in a single breath.

The scene ends with *ḍalāl.* Their prayer is lost.

But we are not yet in that scene. We are here. In the dunya. In the moment before the verse.

There is a question that runs through all four of these ayahs like a thread: *what will you do with your agency before it is too late to use it?* Not in the abstract — now. Today. In the specific place where you are inclined to say *I follow what they say* or *it's not my call* or *someone else is responsible for the direction this is going.*

The word the followers used for themselves in the Fire — *tabaʿan*, dissolving into the bare act of following, a word with no singular — does not have to be the word for you. There is still a singular self in you. There is still a *tābiʿ* — a person who follows — rather than a *tabaʿ* that has dissolved into the function. There is still a you who can read and think and weigh and choose. The maṣdar with no singular is not yet your grammar.

And when the keepers of Jahannam ask — and they will ask, one way or another — *did the messengers not come to you with clear proofs?*

The question is whether your answer will only be *balā.*

Or whether you will have done something with the yes.
