# The Monument and the Ruin Are the Same Word

## INTRODUCTION

Let us begin by simply hearing it.

> أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَيَنظُرُوا۟ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَـٰقِبَةُ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ ۚ كَانُوٓا۟ أَكْثَرَ مِنْهُمْ وَأَشَدَّ قُوَّةً وَءَاثَارًا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَمَآ أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ

*Afalam yasīrū fī-l-arḍi fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu-lladhīna min qablihim — kānū akt_hara minhum wa-ashadda quwwatan wa-āthāran fī-l-arḍi, fa-mā aghnā ʿanhum mā kānū yaksibūn.*

"Have they not traveled through the land and seen how was the end of those before them? They were more numerous than them, and mightier in strength, and greater in the impressions they left upon the land — yet all that they used to earn availed them nothing."

### Rebuild the world

Picture the man hearing this for the first time. He is in Makkah, or somewhere on the trade roads that radiate out from it. And he is not a man without eyes. He has walked these roads. He knows the great caravan routes north toward Syria and south toward Yemen. And every merchant who walked those routes passed something.

They passed ruins.

The Arabian peninsula and its edges are scattered with the bones of dead civilizations. The rock-cut tombs and façades of a people who carved their houses into the mountains. The remains of cities swallowed by sand. The traveler did not need an archaeologist or a textbook. He walked past the evidence with his own feet. He camped in the shadow of monuments built by hands far stronger than his own, by people far more numerous, far wealthier, far more advanced in the arts of building and carving and engineering than the small tribes of his own time.

And here is the thing about ruins. They are silent. You can walk past a ruin a hundred times and feel nothing — just a backdrop, just the scenery of the journey, a strange shape on the horizon that you stopped noticing years ago.

This is the world the ayah enters. Not a world of people who have never seen the dead cities. A world of people who walk past them every season — and have learned to not see them.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah opens with *afalam* — "have they not...?" — He is not informing them of something new. He is asking why what is already in front of their eyes has never traveled the short distance from the eye to the heart. The data is in. The evidence has been collected for them, for free, scattered across the land they cross every year. Why has it never been *read*?

### The Hook

There is a single word in this ayah that I want you to hold in your hand for the next hour. It is the word Allah uses for the greatness of these vanished peoples — the monuments, the constructions, the marks they pressed into the earth that were so vast our man can still see them centuries later.

It is the word **آثَار** — *āthār*.

And here is what is going to undo us by the end of this reflection: the word Allah chose to describe the *peak of their civilization* — their monuments, their achievement, the proof of their might — is the very same word, from the very same root, that means **the trace something leaves behind after it is gone**. A footprint in sand. A scar. A vestige.

The word for their glory and the word for their ruin are one word.

Stay with me. Allah has buried the entire fate of these people inside the very word He uses to praise their power. The collapse is hidden inside the achievement. Let us go and dig it out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Fa-yanẓurū* — the difference between looking and seeing

Before we touch the word, let me ask you about something from your own life.

You have driven a route so many times you cannot remember the drive. You arrive home and realize you have no memory of the last fifteen minutes. Your eyes were open. Light entered them. Your eyes "saw" the road, the signs, the other cars. And yet — did you *see* anything? Nothing registered. Nothing was contemplated. The eye worked perfectly and the mind was somewhere else entirely.

Arabic has a precise gift here that English blurs. English uses "see" for both: the eye that catches light, and the mind that grasps meaning. Arabic separates them. There is *baṣar* — the physical catching of light by the eyeball. And there is *naẓar* — to direct the gaze with attention, to consider, to look *into* a thing in order to draw a conclusion from it. *Naẓar* is the looking that thinks. It is the root, in fact, behind the Arabic words for "theory" and for "consideration" — *naẓariyyah*, *munāẓarah*. It is looking that reasons.

Now hear the ayah again:

> أَفَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَيَنظُرُوا۟
> "Have they not traveled through the land and then *yanẓurū*..."

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say "have they not traveled and *seen*" in the sense of *baṣar*. He chose **naẓar** — the looking that reasons, the gaze that draws a conclusion.

[PAUSE]

And notice the small letter that joins the two verbs — the *fa* in *fa-yanẓurū*. In Arabic grammar this little *fa* is doing real work. It is not a flat "and," a mere addition. It carries the sense of consequence — *and so*, *in order that*, *which would then lead to*. The travel is supposed to *produce* the seeing. The journey is the cause; the reflective gaze is the intended effect.

Let us sit with what this implies. Allah is not commanding tourism. He is not impressed that they have *been* to the ruins. Standing in front of the dead city changes nothing. Millions stand in front of the great ruins of the earth every year. They take their photograph. They buy something at the gift shop. Their *baṣar* is flooded with the monument — and they leave exactly as they came. The eye was full. The heart was untouched.

The ayah locates the failure precisely. It is not that they did not *travel* — *afalam yasīrū*, of course they traveled, they were merchants and nomads, the most traveled people on earth. The failure is that the travel never completed itself into *naẓar*. The *fa* never fired. The cause never produced its effect. They walked across the largest open-air sermon in the world and never once let it preach to them.

Here is what this single grammatical choice reveals: **information about the dead is not the same as reflection upon the dead.** You can know every fact about a fallen empire and have learned nothing. The knowing is *baṣar*. The learning is *naẓar*. And most of us, most of the time, are living in *baṣar* — eyes full, heart asleep — driving the route home with no memory of the road.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: *ʿĀqibah* — what the gaze is told to land on

So the gaze is supposed to fire. But fire *at what*? Allah does not leave the target vague. He tells us exactly where to point the reflecting eye:

> فَيَنظُرُوا۟ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَـٰقِبَةُ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ
> "...and see *how was the end* of those before them."

Look at where the gaze is sent. Not "look at *those before them*." Not "look at their cities," or "their power," or "their works." The grammatical object the eye is told to fix upon is **ʿāqibah** — *the end, the outcome, the final result*.

The root here, *ʿayn-qāf-bā*, is the root of *ʿaqib* — the **heel** of the foot. The part of you that comes last, that lands last, that touches the ground when everything else has already moved forward. From the heel comes *ʿāqibah*: the part of a matter that comes at the very end, the consequence, what it all finally amounts to when the motion stops.

This is a stunning instruction about *where to look*. Because when we look at a great civilization, our eye is naturally drawn to its *peak* — the height of its glory, the moment of its strength, the photograph in the textbook of the empire at its widest. We are dazzled by the middle of the story.

Allah redirects the gaze to the *heel*. To the end. *Kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu* — not "how great were they," but "how did it *end*."

Stay with this, because it is a complete philosophy of how to measure anything. A thing is not measured by its peak. It is measured by its *ʿāqibah* — by what it amounts to when the motion stops. The height of a building tells you nothing if you have not seen whether it stood or fell. And these people, whose ruins our traveler walks past — Allah is saying: do not be hypnotized by how high they built. Walk to the end of their story and read the heel.

[PAUSE]

And there is something quietly devastating in this for us, sitting here. Because we, too, are inside a story whose middle dazzles us. We are inside our careers, our acquisitions, our reputations — the peak, or the climb toward it. The ayah is teaching the gaze a discipline: do not evaluate your life from inside its middle. Walk to its *ʿāqibah*. Stand at the heel. Look back at the whole thing from its end — which, for every single one of us, is coming — and ask *kayfa kāna*: how did it actually turn out?

### Element Three: *Āthār* — the monument and the ruin in one word

Now we come to the word I asked you to hold.

Allah describes the might of these vanished people in a rising stack of three. Listen to it climb:

> كَانُوٓا۟ أَكْثَرَ مِنْهُمْ — *kānū akthara minhum* — "they were **more numerous** than them"
> وَأَشَدَّ قُوَّةً — *wa-ashadda quwwatan* — "and **mightier in strength**"
> وَءَاثَارًا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ — *wa-āthāran fī-l-arḍi* — "and **greater in the āthār** they left in the land"

Feel the ascent. First quantity — there were simply more of them. Then quality of force — they were stronger, harder, more intense in power; *ashadd* is the word for severity, for hardness, for the intensity of a thing. And then the climax, the highest rung: their **āthār** in the land.

The classical commentators — al-Jalalayn, al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, al-Muyassar — are in agreement on what these *āthār* concretely were: their *maṣāniʿ* and their *quṣūr* — their great works, their factories and fortresses, their palaces and constructions and irrigation works and carved cities. The monuments. The things that outlast a generation. The achievement so solid it presses a permanent mark into the surface of the earth.

So *āthār* here is unmistakably a word of *praise*. It is the top of the stack. It names them at their greatest.

Now let me open the root.

*Āthār* comes from *alif-thā-rā*, *a-th-r*. And the core meaning of this root in the Arabic language is: **the mark or trace that something leaves behind after it has passed.** The *athar* is the footprint left in the sand after the foot has lifted and gone. It is the scar left after the wound has healed and the event is over. It is the track of an animal that tells you it *was here* — and is no longer. The grammarians of Arabic even use *ism al-athar* — the noun of trace — for the residue an action leaves behind.

Do you feel it yet?

[PAUSE]

The word for what they *built* and the word for what is *left of them* are the same word.

When Allah says they were greater in *āthār*, He is naming their monuments. And the monument *is*, by the deepest meaning of its own name, a **trace left behind by something that has passed**. The pyramid is an *athar* not only because it is grand — but because it is, by definition, the mark of a hand that is no longer here. Every monument is, in the root of the very word that praises it, already a tombstone.

Let this land fully. These people poured their strength into building *āthār* — traces — precisely *because* they wanted to defeat the trace. They built to *not* be forgotten. They carved their names into stone, raised palaces, cut their houses into the living rock, so that long after they were gone, something would remain. They were trying to outrun erasure.

And the word for their solution *is the word for their disappearance.* The Arabic does not let you separate them. The thing you build to prove you were here is, in its very name, the proof that you have gone. Our traveler walks across the desert and sees their *āthār* — and the *āthār* are simultaneously the loudest possible declaration of their greatness *and* the silent, total confirmation that not one of them remains alive to enjoy it.

[PAUSE — major]

And now stand back and see the whole stack collapse. *Akthar* — more numerous: every one of those numbers is now dead. *Ashadd quwwah* — mightier in strength: that strength is now dust and a heel-print. *Āthār* — their monuments: standing empty, owned by no one, a trace of a vanished hand. Allah built a ladder of their greatness, three rungs high — and every rung, looked at through *naẓar* rather than *baṣar*, is already a gravestone. The traveler was meant to climb the ladder of their glory and arrive, at the top, at their grave.

This is the *āthār*. One word, holding the monument and the ruin together, refusing to let you have one without the other.

### Element Four: *Mā aghnā* — when richness cannot make you rich

And then the ayah delivers the verdict. After the whole towering stack of their greatness, one short clause brings it all to nothing:

> فَمَآ أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ
> "...yet all that they used to earn **availed them nothing**."

The verb is *aghnā*, from the root *gh-n-y*. And you must hear what lives in this root, because the irony is exquisite and deliberate. *Ghinā* means **richness, wealth, sufficiency, the state of needing nothing**. From it comes *al-Ghanī* — The Self-Sufficient — one of the names of Allah, the One who is utterly free of all need, who needs nothing and no one.

So the verb *aghnā* literally means *to make rich, to render sufficient, to free from need*. And the ayah says: *fa-mā aghnā ʿanhum* — all their wealth, all their earning, **did not make them rich.**

Read that again slowly. The people who were the richest, the most numerous, the mightiest — *their richness did not make them rich.* The thing that defines *ghinā* failed to produce *ghinā*. At the one moment that mattered, when the decree of Allah arrived, all their sufficiency rendered them not-one-bit sufficient. Their independence left them utterly dependent. The wealthy died as the most bankrupt beings imaginable, because the only Being who is truly *Ghanī* — free of need — is Allah, and against Him no created richness is richness at all.

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars note a beautiful ambiguity here, and it is worth seeing — not as two competing answers, but as one truth viewed from two angles. The word *mā* in *fa-mā aghnā* can be read two ways. Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both record them.

Read one way, *mā* is a flat negation — *mā aghnā*: "it did **not** avail them." A closed, final statement. Their earning did nothing for them.

Read the other way, *mā* is a question — *mā aghnā ʿanhum?*: "**What** did their earning avail them?" A question flung across the ruins, expecting the obvious answer: *nothing. Look around. What did it get them?*

And here is the gift: both readings arrive at the identical place. Whether you state it as a fact — *it availed them nothing* — or fling it as a question across the empty palaces — *what exactly did it get them?* — the answer the ruins give back is the same silence. The grammar itself refuses to let the meaning escape. Statement or question, the verdict is *nothing*.

And then the final word: *yaksibūn*, from *kasb* — to earn, to acquire, to gain by effort. This is the everyday word for *what you work for*. Your earnings. Your acquisitions. The fruit of your striving. Hold this word — we are going to walk straight into it, because it is the word that turns this ayah from a story about *them* into a question about *you*.

### Bridge

So look at what these four words have shown us, woven into one verse. *Naẓar*: there is a seeing that thinks, and the dead nations are its assigned text. *ʿĀqibah*: a thing is measured at its heel, its end, not its dazzling middle. *Āthār*: the monument and the ruin are one word, and everything built to defeat forgetting is, in its own name, the mark of the forgotten. *Aghnā*: richness that cannot make you rich, because true sufficiency belongs to Allah alone.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about ancient words, but about the life you are living right now, this week, with the body you currently inhabit.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You are already building your āthār

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, somewhere in you, a quiet hope about being remembered. Almost everyone does, and it wears a thousand respectable disguises. For one person it is the portfolio — the body of work, the thing they will leave behind. For another it is the building they want their name on, or the company that will outlast them, or the wealth passed to children, or simply the photographs and the posts, the digital footprint, the carefully curated trail of a life that says *I was here, I mattered, I existed.*

This is not, in itself, a sin. The drive to leave a mark is woven deep into us. But I want you to notice what it *is*. Every one of these is an *athar*. A trace. We are all, every day, building our monuments — most of us in small, modern, invisible ways. The promotion is an *athar*. The renovated house is an *athar*. The follower count, the savings account, the reputation among colleagues — *āthār*, all of them. Marks we are pressing into the surface of our short lives in the hope they outlast us.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the ayah down onto your own monuments.

The people in this verse did not build half-measures. They were *ashadd quwwatan* — mightier than you. They were *akthar* — more numerous, more resourced, more advanced than you. Their *āthār* are still standing, centuries after every modern thing you own will have crumbled into landfill. If sheer scale of monument could defeat erasure, *they* would have defeated it. They built in granite and carved into mountains. You are building in drywall and pixels.

And *mā aghnā ʿanhum* — it availed them nothing.

This is not a verse that says *do not build*. Read it again with *naẓar*. It says: do not be deceived about what the building can *do*. The athar can preserve your name on a wall. It cannot preserve *you*. The monument and the ruin are one word — which means the very thing you are pouring your life into to escape being forgotten is, in its essence, already a marker of the forgotten. You are working overtime to carve a tombstone and calling it a legacy.

So what does the ayah ask of us? Not to stop building — but to relocate the *purpose* of the building. There is a kind of *athar* the ruins of this surah cannot mock. The Prophet ﷺ taught of deeds whose reward continues after death — knowledge that benefits, ongoing charity, a righteous child who prays. Look at what those have in common: they are *āthār* whose value is registered not in this earth, where every monument becomes a ruin, but with the One who is *Ghanī*, where nothing decays. They are traces pressed not into sand, but into the record of Allah.

**Tomorrow this changes one thing:** before you pour your hours into building some monument to yourself this week — the project, the acquisition, the image — pause and ask the *naẓar* question. *Is this an athar that will avail me at my ʿāqibah, or one that will stand empty in the sand while I stand before my Lord?* You do not have to abandon the work. You have to re-aim it. The same hours, pressed into a trace that lasts where lasting is real.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The richness that cannot make you rich

Here is a feeling you know.

You hit a number you once thought would be *enough*. The salary, the savings, the title. And for a strange, short moment — it is enough. And then the floor of "enough" silently rises, and you are standing below it again, reaching for the next number. You have more than you have ever had, and you feel, somehow, exactly as un-sufficient as before. The wealth grew. The *richness* — the felt state of needing nothing — never arrived.

The ancients in our ayah lived this at civilizational scale. *Akthar, ashadd, āthār* — they had *more* in every measurable way. And the ayah's verdict, in the cruelest precision of the Arabic, is that *all their richness did not make them rich.* *Mā aghnā ʿanhum.* The wealth was real. The *ghinā* — the true sufficiency, the freedom from need — never came, because it was never theirs to manufacture.

This is the secret the root *gh-n-y* keeps. There is only One who is *al-Ghanī* — truly, intrinsically free of need. Everything else in existence is *faqīr* — needy, dependent, leaking — by its very nature. So when a created being chases sufficiency through accumulation, it is chasing a quality that does not live in created things at all. You can stack *kathrah* — quantity — to the sky, and it will never convert into *ghinā* — sufficiency — because they are different substances. You are trying to build water out of stone.

[PAUSE]

And notice *which word* the ayah uses for what they had: *mā kānū yaksibūn* — what they *used to earn*. *Kasb*. Their acquisitions, the fruit of their striving, the thing they could point to and say *I made this, I earned this, this is mine.* And it is precisely this — the proudest category, the self-made gain — that the verb *aghnā* negates. The harder you believe a thing is *your* earning, *your* doing, *your* security, the more total the shock when *mā aghnā ʿanhum* arrives and the earning simply... is not there. It does not show up. The account you trusted does not pay out at the one transaction that matters.

There is a quiet mercy buried in this, though. If *ghinā* cannot be earned, then it can only be *given*. And the One who gives it gives it freely, to the poor and the rich alike, and it does not rise and fall with your bank balance. The companions of the Prophet ﷺ included some who owned almost nothing — and they walked the earth with a sufficiency that the kings of the *āthār*-builders never tasted, because their hearts were anchored to *al-Ghanī* and not to the leaking vessel of *kasb*. *Ghinā an-nafs* — the richness of the soul, the contented heart — was theirs. And that is the only *ghinā* that survives the *ʿāqibah*.

**Tomorrow this changes one thing:** notice the next time the floor of "enough" rises under you — the next time more arrives and sufficiency does not. Instead of reaching for the next rung, name what is happening: *I am asking my kasb to make me ghanī, and kasb cannot do that.* Then turn, even for thirty seconds, to *al-Ghanī*, and ask Him for the sufficiency of heart that no number on earth has ever delivered to anyone. That is the only place the request has ever been answered.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bring it together

See now how the four words and the two themes were one insight, approached from different doors.

*Naẓar* taught us there is a seeing that thinks — and that the ruins of the dead are its assigned text, walked past unread by everyone living in mere *baṣar*. *ʿĀqibah* taught us to send that thinking-gaze not to the dazzling peak of a story but to its heel, its end — because that is where the truth of a thing is finally told. *Āthār* gave us the single word that holds monument and ruin together, and so dismantled forever the hope that building bigger could defeat being forgotten. And *aghnā* delivered the verdict that even total richness cannot manufacture sufficiency, because *ghinā* belongs to *al-Ghanī* alone and was never on sale in the marketplace of *kasb*.

The two themes are the same verdict turned toward you. *You are building āthār* — so aim them where lasting is real. *You are chasing ghinā through kasb* — so turn instead to the only One who has it to give. Both themes are the ayah refusing to let you measure your life from inside its middle, and walking you firmly to the heel — to your *ʿāqibah* — and asking you to look back at everything you are accumulating from *there*.

And here is the architecture of the whole verse, now that we have earned it. It is built as a rise and a fall. It rises: *more numerous — mightier in strength — greater in monuments.* Three rungs up, into the sky. And then it falls in a single clause: *availed them nothing.* The entire grammar of the ayah is the shape of a civilization — the long climb, and the one-line collapse. Allah did not just *tell* you that worldly greatness ends. He *built the sentence in the shape of its ending.*

### Questions to carry

- When I last stood before something ancient and ruined, was I in *baṣar* or *naẓar* — did my eye merely fill, or did my heart read the sermon?
- What *āthār* am I pouring my hours into this season — and will any of them avail me at my *ʿāqibah*, or will they stand empty in the sand?
- Where in my life have I been asking my *kasb* — my earning — to make me *ghanī*, to make me feel sufficient, when sufficiency has never once come from there for anyone?
- If I walked to the heel of my own story right now and looked back, *kayfa kāna* — how is it turning out?
- Who is the *al-Ghanī* I have been ignoring while I tried to manufacture richness with my own hands?

### One-sentence distillation

The people who built the mightiest monuments on earth left us a single word — *āthār* — that means both *their glory* and *their grave*, and the ayah commands us to walk to that word with a seeing-that-thinks, so that we measure our own lives not by the dazzling middle but by the heel, where all earning is revealed as powerless and only the truly Self-Sufficient remains.

### The closing invitation

You do not need to travel to a dead city to obey this verse. The *naẓar* it asks for can be done from where you sit. Walk, in your mind, to your own *ʿāqibah* — the end that is coming for you as surely as it came for them. Stand at your heel and look back at the monuments you are building and the richness you are chasing. And let just one thing re-aim itself. Not abandoned — re-aimed. The same hours, the same striving, the same drive to leave a mark — turned toward an *athar* that lasts where lasting is real, and toward the only *Ghinā* that was ever yours to receive.

### Brief duʿā'

*O Allah, You are al-Ghanī and we are the needy; teach our eyes to read the ruins before we become them. Do not let us pour our lives into āthār that will avail us nothing at our ʿāqibah, and grant us the richness of the heart that no earning has ever bought. Make the last of our story better than its middle, and let what we leave behind be a trace that pleases You — not a tombstone in the sand.* Āmīn.
