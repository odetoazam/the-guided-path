# The Two Descents and the One Who Turns

> [ayah:40:13] هُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُرِيكُمْ ءَايَـٰتِهِۦ وَيُنَزِّلُ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ رِزْقًا ۚ وَمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ إِلَّا مَن يُنِيبُ
>
> *Huwa alladhī yurīkum āyātihi wa-yunazzilu lakum mina-s-samā'i rizqā. Wa-mā yatadhakkaru illā man yunīb.*
>
> **"It is He who shows you His signs and sends down provision for you from the sky. Yet none takes heed except the one who turns back to Him."**
>
> [ayah:40:14] فَٱدْعُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ
>
> *Fa-dʿū-llāha mukhliṣīna lahu-d-dīna wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn.*
>
> **"So call upon Allah, making the religion sincerely His, even though the disbelievers dislike it."**

## INTRODUCTION: The Surah of the Disputed Sign

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

We are in Surah Ghafir — the fortieth surah, and the first of the seven great surahs that open with the letters **Ḥā Mīm**. If you read these surahs together, you start to feel a single weather front moving across all of them. And the storm at the center of *this* surah has a name. It is **al-jidāl** — disputation. Arguing. Quarreling about the signs of Allah.

Listen to how the surah opens, just a few ayahs before ours: *"None disputes concerning the signs of Allah except those who disbelieve, so let not their fluctuation through the land deceive you"* (40:4). The whole surah pulses with this tension — there are signs everywhere, and there are people standing in front of those signs, arms folded, arguing.

And then, a little further on, the surah will give us one of the most extraordinary human portraits in the entire Quran: **the believing man of Pharaoh's own household** — a man who hid his faith inside the most hostile court on earth, and who finally stood up in the middle of that court and spoke the truth about God while the most powerful tyrant in history glared at him.

[PAUSE]

So hold that frame. This surah is about a person who *sees* what others refuse to see, and who *says* it out loud even when everyone around him hates it.

Now — right before the surah dives fully into that drama, Allah lays down two short ayahs. Almost like He is setting the foundation stones of a building before raising the walls. And these two ayahs answer two questions that the rest of the surah will live inside:

**First** — if the signs of God are everywhere, why do some people see them and others, looking at the exact same thing, see nothing?

**Second** — once you *have* seen, what are you supposed to *do* with it, especially when the people around you despise it?

Here is the stunning part. Allah does not answer the first question by talking about the signs. He answers it by talking about *the one who sees them*. He shifts the camera. He tells you the signs are sent to everyone — and then He quietly removes almost everyone from the room.

We are going to find out who is left.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Yurīkum* — He Does Not Just Show Signs. He Makes You See.

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we open the word, because it is the whole key.

In Arabic, a simple root can be reshaped to change *who is doing what to whom*. Take the root that means "to see" — **رأى**, *ra'ā*, "he saw." That's the simple form. Now Arabic has a way of taking a verb and adding a layer that means **"to cause"** — to make the action happen to someone else. We call this Form IV. When you pour the verb "to see" into that Form IV mold, you do not get "he saw." You get **أرى** — *arā* — **"he made [someone] see."** "He showed."

Think of the difference in your own life. There is a difference between a window and a guide. A window merely exists; the light is there whether you look or not. But a guide takes your face in his hands and turns it toward the thing — *look, there, do you see it now?* That second one — that turning of your face — that is Form IV. That is causation.

Now open the ayah:

> هُوَ ٱلَّذِى **يُرِيكُمْ** ءَايَـٰتِهِۦ
>
> *Huwa alladhī **yurīkum** āyātihi*
>
> **"It is He who *makes you see* His signs."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. Allah did not say, *"He who creates His signs."* He did not say, *"He who places His signs around you."* Both of those would be true. But He chose the verb that puts His hand on your face and turns it. **Yurīkum.** *He makes you see.*

Notice what this quietly tells you. The seeing itself is a gift from Him. The sunrise is His. But so is the *eye that catches the sunrise and gasps.* So is the moment something cracks open in your chest and you suddenly understand. We tend to think the miracle is the sign out there. This verb says: the miracle is also the seeing in here — and both are coming from the same Hand.

There's something even more tender folded into the form of the word. It is not *arākum* — past tense, "He showed you," done, finished. It is **yurīkum** — present-tense, ongoing, unfinished. *He keeps showing you. He is showing you right now.* Every single day this verb is still running. The signs did not stop arriving in the seventh century. He is making you see, today, in whatever just crossed your field of vision before you opened this reflection.

And then — **āyātihi**, "*His* signs." Not "the signs." *His.* Everything you are about to be shown has His fingerprints on the back of it. The word **āyah** itself is worth pausing on: in Arabic an *āyah* is a sign that points beyond itself. A road sign is not the destination; it is an arrow toward the destination. So Allah is not asking you to stop and worship the sunrise. He is making you see the sunrise *so that your eye travels up the arrow* to the One who made it. The sign is a stepping stone. He never meant for you to stand on the stone forever.

What this one word reveals: **the signs of God are not waiting passively for clever people to discover them. God Himself is the one actively turning your face toward them.** Which means if you have ever truly *seen* — that itself was already a mercy.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Yunazzilu* — The Provision That Comes Down in Portions

Now watch what He pairs with the seeing.

> وَ**يُنَزِّلُ** لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ رِزْقًا
>
> *wa-**yunazzilu** lakum mina-s-samā'i rizqā*
>
> **"and sends down for you, from the sky, provision."**

Again, let me teach the shape first. Arabic has a Form II — you build it by doubling the middle letter of the root — and one of the things Form II does is take a simple action and give it a sense of **doing it repeatedly, gradually, portion by portion.** The simple verb *anzala* (Form IV) means "to send down" — often all at once, a single descent. But *nazzala*, the form Allah uses here — *yunazzilu* — carries the texture of **sending down again and again, in installments.**

Here is an everyday picture. There is a difference between a man who hands you a year's salary in a single envelope, and a father who deposits something into your account every month for your whole life. The first is generous once. The second is *present* — over and over, on a rhythm you can lean your life against. **Yunazzilu** is the second kind.

[PAUSE]

Look out the window the next time it rains. Allah did not pile the year's water into the sky and tip it over your head in one flood that would drown you. He sends it down — *yunazzilu* — a shower here, a shower next week, a season and then another season. The very *form of the verb* matches the *form of the rain.* The grammar drizzles. This is what we mean when we say the Quran's sound and shape do the work the meaning needs done.

And **min as-samā'** — "from the sky." The classical scholars note that the rizq here is the rain, *because the rain is the cause from which the provisions grow.* Al-Baghawi says it plainly: *"the rain, which is the cause of all provisions."* But notice the direction. **Down.** It comes *down.* You did not climb up and seize it. It descended onto you from above, the way a gift is lowered into open hands.

Now here is where I want you to slow all the way down, because the deepest insight in this ayah is not in any single word. **It is in the pairing.**

Al-Qurtubi catches it beautifully. He asks: why did Allah join these two things — *showing the signs* and *sending the provision* — in one single breath? And he answers:

> **"Because by the signs, religions are sustained; and by the provision, bodies are sustained."**
>
> *bi-l-āyāti qawāmu-l-adyān, wa-bi-r-rizqi qawāmu-l-abdān.*

[PAUSE]

Feel the symmetry of that. Two descents. Two sustenances. **The signs come down to feed the soul. The provision comes down to feed the body.** And Allah mentions them in one breath because He is the One source of both. The same Hand that keeps your heart alive with meaning keeps your body alive with bread. You are not two beings — a hungry animal and a separate searching spirit, fed by two different gods. You are one creature, and **one Lord is feeding both halves of you, from above, continuously.**

Most of us split these. We thank God for our food and we look for meaning somewhere else — in books, in achievement, in other people. This ayah refuses the split. The rain that grows your wheat and the sign that grows your faith *come down the same sky.*

What these two words reveal together: **God's care is not abstract. It descends. It feeds the part of you that eats and the part of you that wonders, and it never stops sending.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: *Wa-mā Yatadhakkaru illā Man Yunīb* — The Room Empties

And now the ayah does something that should stop us cold.

It has just told us that the signs are shown to **you** — *yurīkum*, plural, all of you. The provision is sent for **you** — *lakum*, plural, all of you. Everything so far has been wide open, universal, sent to the whole human crowd without exception.

And then, in five words, Allah empties the room.

> وَمَا يَتَذَكَّرُ إِلَّا مَن يُنِيبُ
>
> *wa-mā **yatadhakkaru** illā man **yunīb***
>
> **"Yet none takes heed except the one who turns back."**

Let me show you the grammar of this emptying, because it is surgical. Arabic has a construction for **total restriction**: you negate everything — *wa-mā*, "and not... none" — and then you carve out a single exception with *illā*, "except." *None... except.* It is the most exclusive structure the language has. It does not say "most people miss it." It does not say "few people get it." It says: **zero. Nobody. Except one specific type.**

So picture it. The signs were shown to a stadium full of people. The rain fell on every roof. And Allah says: *out of all of them, when it comes to actually taking it in — none. None at all. Except...* — and the spotlight narrows to a single figure in the emptied stadium — **...the one who turns back.**

[PAUSE]

Now look at the two verbs He uses for that one figure, because they are exquisitely chosen.

First: **yatadhakkaru.** The root is *dh-k-r*, remembering. But Allah does not use the simple "remembers." He uses a Form V — *tadhakkara* — and Form V is **reflexive**, it folds the action back onto the doer himself. It means something like: *"to make the remembrance sink into your own self."* Not to recall a fact. To let the truth penetrate inward until it becomes yours. This is the difference between knowing the sign is there and being *changed* by it. Form V is the word for the second one.

And then the figure is named — *man yunīb*, "the one who turns back." The root here is *n-w-b*. And this root is gorgeous. *Nawb* in Arabic carries the idea of **something that returns again and again, in turns** — like *nawba*, a recurring bout, a turn that comes round and round. So **yunīb** — Form IV — is not a person who turned to God once and was done. It is the person who **keeps coming back. Returns and returns. Whose face is permanently inclined homeward**, the way a plant kept in a dark room still bends, every single day, toward the one crack of light.

[PAUSE]

Now put the two together and watch what Allah has just revealed about the human heart.

He has told us that **the signs and the seeing of them are two different things.** The signs are democratic — they fall on everyone like rain. But the *reception* is not. And the deciding factor is not intelligence. It is not education. It is not even how dramatic the sign was. **The deciding factor is the direction the heart was already facing.**

The one who *yunīb* — who was already turning, already inclined, already leaning back toward home — *that* one sees the sunrise and weeps. The one who was facing away sees the exact same sunrise, checks the time, and walks on.

This is why two people can stand at the same graveside, witness the same death, feel the same earth, and one of them is shattered open into faith while the other feels nothing but the inconvenience of the schedule. **The sign was identical. The hearts were facing different directions.**

And here is the mercy hidden in the severity. The ayah is not saying some people are locked out forever. *Yunīb* is a present-tense, ongoing verb — *the one who is turning.* The door is the turning itself. The moment any heart begins to incline back toward Him, that heart steps into the lit circle where the signs finally become legible. You are not waiting to be chosen. **The turning is the choosing.**

What these five words reveal: **God shows the sign to everyone and provides for everyone — but He is shown to, and received by, only the heart that was already on its way home.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So look at what the language of ayah thirteen has built for us. *Yurīkum* — He actively turns your face toward the sign. *Yunazzilu* — He sends down, in endless portions, the food for your body, paired in one breath with the food for your soul. And *wa-mā yatadhakkaru illā man yunīb* — yet all of that lands only in the heart already leaning back toward Him.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being actually lives. Because ayah fourteen is about to tell the one who turned exactly what to do next.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Difference Between Looking and Seeing

Let me start where you already live.

There is a strange experience every one of us has had. You lose your keys. You search the kitchen counter — you look right at it, eyes pointed directly at the surface — and you do not see them. You walk away. Someone else comes in, glances over, and says, "They're right there." And there they are, exactly where your open eyes had already pointed. **You were looking. You were not seeing.**

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it *inattentional blindness*. The eye registers the photons; the mind does not register the object — because the mind was not prepared to receive it. Seeing, it turns out, is not something that happens *to* you when light hits your retina. Seeing is something your inner state has to *agree to.* You see what you are ready to see.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that ordinary, almost silly experience up against our ayah. *Wa-mā yatadhakkaru illā man yunīb.* "None takes heed except the one who turns." Allah is describing inattentional blindness — but raised to the scale of the entire cosmos and the whole meaning of a human life.

The signs are *right there.* On the counter of the sky. The dawn, the rain, the face of your child, the slow healing of a wound, the fact that you woke up at all. The disbeliever in this surah is not blind in the eyes. He has 20/20 vision. He is looking *directly* at the signs — and arguing about them, *yujādil fī āyāt Allāh.* The tragedy of the *jadal*, the disputation, is that it is the perfect picture of the man staring at his own keys and insisting they are not there.

So what is the difference between him and the one who *yunīb*? It is not better eyes. **It is a heart that has already agreed to see.** The turning — the *ināba* — is the inner agreement. It is the heart saying *yes* before the sign even arrives, so that when the sign comes, the door is already open and the truth walks straight in.

Let me take this one layer deeper, because there is a beautiful and uncomfortable implication.

We usually think faith comes *from* the evidence. We imagine the honest path is: stay neutral, fold your arms, wait for the signs to prove themselves, and *then* believe. This ayah quietly tells us that is not how the human heart actually works. **The folded arms are not neutrality. They are a direction.** They are the body of a heart facing away. And a heart facing away will find the rain unconvincing, the dawn ordinary, the grave merely sad.

The one who turns is not being intellectually lazy. He has understood something the disputer has not: that **some truths only become visible to the one who has already begun to walk toward them.** You cannot see the inside of the house from across the street with your arms crossed. You have to start walking up the path. And as you walk — *yurīkum* — He begins turning your face, and you start to see what was invisible from the road.

[PAUSE]

Here is where it touches your tomorrow.

You do not need a bigger sign. I promise you, you do not. You are already drowning in signs — the ayah says they are sent to *you*, *lakum*, right now, in portions, daily. What you need is not more evidence. **What you need is to turn.** To stop, even for thirty seconds tomorrow morning, and let one ordinary thing become an *āyah* again — let the light coming through the window be an arrow your eye travels up, instead of just light. That small turning is *ināba*. And the ayah's promise is that the moment you make it, the signs that were always there become suddenly, almost unbearably, legible.

The practical shift: **Stop asking "why won't God show me a sign?" and start asking "which way is my heart facing?"** Tomorrow, once, deliberately turn your face — and watch how much was already there.

[PAUSE — major]

## Theme Two: The Sincerity That Doesn't Wait for Permission

Now we step into ayah fourteen, and it changes the entire posture of the passage.

Up to now, *Allah* has been the one acting. He shows. He sends down. He is the verb of every sentence. And the human being has been mostly passive — shown to, provided for. But the instant a heart turns — *yunīb* — Allah turns to that heart and gives it its first command. The very first thing He asks of the one who turned:

> فَٱدْعُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ
>
> *fa-dʿū-llāha **mukhliṣīna** lahu-d-dīn*
>
> **"So call upon Allah, making the religion *sincerely* His."**

That little *fa* at the front — "so," "therefore" — is the hinge of the whole passage. *Because* He is the only One showing you signs, *because* He is the only One sending the rain, **therefore** call on Him alone. The logic is airtight. If only one Hand is feeding both halves of you, it would be absurd to lift your face to anyone else.

But let me begin this theme where you live, before the word *ikhlāṣ*.

Think about the most honest moment of your life. Not your most polished moment — your most honest one. For most people, it is a moment of total desperation. The phone call with the bad news. The hospital corridor. The turbulence at thirty thousand feet when the plane drops and your hand grabs the armrest and something in you, with no rehearsal at all, cries out — *God.* Just God. Not for show. Not to look pious. There is no audience in that cry. It is the purest thing you have ever said, and it came out of you stripped of every motive except *help me.*

[PAUSE]

That cry — that motiveless, audienceless, undivided reaching — *that* is **ikhlāṣ.** The word comes from a root meaning **to be pure, unmixed, clarified** — like gold that has had every other metal burned out of it until only the gold is left. *Mukhliṣīna lahu-d-dīn* — "purifying the religion for Him" — means scraping every other motive out of your turning to God until only *He* is left in it. No showing off. No hedging your bets with other doors. No keeping one eye on the crowd. Just Him, undiluted.

And here is the staggering thing the Quran says elsewhere about that turbulence-cry. In Surah Luqman, Allah describes exactly this: *"And when waves cover them like canopies, they call upon Allah, sincere to Him in religion"* — *mukhliṣīna lahu-d-dīn*, the exact same phrase as our ayah (31:32). When the wave is about to swallow the ship, **every human being on earth becomes a perfect muwaḥḥid.** The sincerity arrives by itself. The idols are forgotten. Even the disputer prays with total purity when the water rises over his head.

So if sincerity comes *for free* in the storm — what is ayah fourteen actually asking of us?

[PAUSE]

It is asking us to have, *on the dry calm deck, by choice,* the sincerity that the drowning man has *by force.* To call on Him with that same undivided purity **when there is no wave** — when the plane is steady, when life is comfortable, when nothing is forcing your hand. *That* is the achievement. The drowning man's sincerity is squeezed out of him. The believer's sincerity is *offered.* One is a reflex. The other is worship.

And now the ayah adds the final, blazing clause — and this is where it reaches back and connects to the whole surah:

> وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ
>
> *wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn*
>
> **"...even though the disbelievers dislike it."**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Why would anyone *dislike* you sincerely calling on God? What is it to them? But Allah knows the human heart. He knows that the hardest place to be sincere is not the sinking ship — there, no one is watching. The hardest place to be sincere is **the crowded room where everyone is facing the other way.** The dinner table where faith is faintly embarrassing. The workplace where you'd rather not be seen praying. The court of Pharaoh.

Remember where we are. This whole surah is building toward the believer of Pharaoh's family — the man who carried pure faith *inside the most hostile audience in human history* and finally said it out loud, *wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn*, even though every powerful person in the room despised it. Ayah fourteen is the seed of his courage. It is Allah saying: **the sincerity I want from you is the kind that does not check the room first.** The kind that calls on God with the same purity in the hostile court as in the sinking ship.

Because here is the truth the clause exposes: most of what we mistake for sincerity is *permission-seeking.* We are devoted as long as devotion is socially affordable. We pray loudly where prayer is praised and quietly where it is mocked. And Allah, with three words, cuts the cord between our worship and the crowd's approval. *Mukhliṣīna lahu-d-dīn* — purely *His* — and then, lest you misunderstand what "purely His" costs: *wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn.* Purely His **even if it makes you the only one in the room.**

[PAUSE]

The practical shift: tomorrow, find the one place where your faith feels *socially expensive* — the conversation, the room, the relationship where being devoted to God is faintly embarrassing — and there, do one small sincere act anyway. Not loudly, not to provoke. Just to break, once, the secret contract between your worship and other people's approval. That is *ikhlāṣ wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn.* And it is, perhaps, the single hardest and most liberating thing a human heart can do.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: Watch the Whole Arc Move

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how these two ayahs are built, because the structure *is* the message.

Look at the flow of agency:

**God acts.** *Yurīkum* — He shows. *Yunazzilu* — He sends down. The signs for the soul and the provision for the body, descending together from one sky. The entire opening is God moving toward the human being.

**Then one human responds — inwardly.** Out of the whole crowd that was shown and provided for, the room empties to a single figure: *man yunīb*, the one who turns back, in whom the truth actually sinks in, *yatadhakkaru.* This is the inner pivot. God has descended; now one heart inclines.

**Then that human acts — outwardly.** *Fa-dʿū* — "so call." The turning becomes a calling. The inward incline becomes an outward voice. The heart that faced home now *speaks* toward home.

**And finally, that act is tested against the world.** *Wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn* — even if the crowd hates it. The sincere call is pressed against social pressure and does not break.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the complete arc? **Descent → turning → calling → standing firm.** It begins with God reaching all the way down to you with two kinds of food, and it ends with you standing alone in a hostile room, calling His name with undivided purity. That is the entire spiritual life compressed into two ayahs — and it is also, exactly, the journey of the believer of Pharaoh's family that the rest of this surah is about to tell. These two ayahs are the architecture of his courage, laid down before his story is built on top of them.

## Two Directions, One Truth

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery approached from opposite sides.

From the *words*, we learned that *yurīkum* makes the seeing itself a gift, that the rain and the signs descend together to feed body and soul, and that the totalizing *wa-mā... illā* restricts all of it to the one who turns.

From the *themes*, we learned that seeing is something the heart must agree to before the eye can do it, and that the purest sincerity is the kind offered on the calm deck and in the hostile room, not just squeezed out by the sinking ship.

They meet in one place: **The signs are everywhere and free. What is rare — what everything hinges on — is the direction your heart is facing and the purity of what comes out of it when you call.** God has done the showing and the sending. The turning, and the sincerity of the call, are yours.

## Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time something ordinary became an *āyah* for me — an arrow my eye traveled up toward God — instead of just a thing? What direction was my heart facing that day?
2. I thank God for my food. Do I as readily recognize that the meaning that keeps my soul alive descends from the very same Hand, the same sky?
3. Where in my life am I "looking but not seeing" — staring at a sign and disputing it, like the man insisting his keys are not on the counter?
4. Is my sincerity the kind that the storm forces out of me, or the kind I offer freely on the calm deck? Which one is Allah actually asking for?
5. Where is the room in which my faith feels socially expensive — and what would it mean to be sincere there, *wa-law kariha-l-kāfirūn*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah shows His signs and sends His provision down upon everyone alike, but only the heart already turning back toward Him truly sees them — and that heart's calling-place is sincere, undivided devotion that does not wait for the room's permission.**

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need a louder sign. You are already standing inside more *āyāt* than you could count, sent to you in daily portions from a Hand that is feeding both your body and your soul. What this passage invites is not more evidence — it is a turning. A small, repeatable, present-tense turning of the face toward home. And then, having turned, to let the call that rises out of you be *pure* — His alone — even in the one room where being His is the hardest thing to be.

The signs are His gift. The turning is your answer. Let tomorrow have one real turning in it.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who show us Your signs and send down our provision from the sky — do not let us be among those who look and never see. Turn our faces toward You; make us of those who turn back, again and again, until the turning becomes who we are. And when we call upon You, purify the call until only You are left in it — even when the whole room would rather we stayed silent.* Āmīn.
