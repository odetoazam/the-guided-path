# Not Equal: The Blind, the Seeing, and the Soul That Forgot the Difference

> وَمَا يَسْتَوِي ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ وَٱلْبَصِيرُ وَٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَلَا ٱلْمُسِىٓءُ ۚ قَلِيلًا مَّا تَتَذَكَّرُونَ

> *Wa mā yastawī l-aʿmā wa l-baṣīru wa lladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāti wa lā l-musīʾ. Qalīlan mā tatadhakkarūn.*

> **"And the blind and the seeing are not equal, nor those who believe and do righteous deeds and the evildoer. Little do you take heed."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing in the Room Where the Argument Is Happening

Before we touch a single word of this ayah, I want you to understand the room you've walked into. Because this verse is not a calm meditation delivered into silence. It is a sentence spoken into the middle of an argument — and if you don't feel the argument, you'll miss why the sentence lands the way it does.

Surah Ghafir is also called *Sūrat al-Muʾmin* — the Surah of the Believer — named after a single man. A man inside Pharaoh's own court who hid his faith and then, at the decisive moment, stood up and spoke truth to the most powerful tyrant on earth. That story sits at the center of this surah. And the whole surah breathes with one theme: **the confrontation between arrogance and reality.**

Listen to what comes just a few breaths before our ayah. Allah describes a certain kind of person:

> *"Indeed, those who dispute concerning the signs of Allah without any authority having come to them — there is nothing in their breasts except pride (kibr), which they cannot reach."* (40:56)

Picture that person. Not an honest seeker. Not someone wrestling with doubt. Someone who *argues against the signs of Allah* — and the engine underneath the argument isn't evidence. It's **pride**. A swelling in the chest. A need to be above. To not be told. To not be ranked, measured, judged, or placed below anyone.

And then, immediately after describing that proud disputant, Allah says something that towers over him:

> *"The creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind — but most of the people do not know."* (40:57)

He shrinks the arrogant man against the scale of the cosmos. *You who will not be humbled — look up. The thing you stand on, the sky you stand under, is a greater act of creation than you are.*

[PAUSE]

And **then** — right there, between the vastness of the heavens above and the certainty of the Hour that is announced in the very next verse (40:59) — Allah plants our ayah. A short, level, almost quiet sentence:

*The blind and the seeing are not equal.*

Here is the hook. Here is what I need you to feel the hunger for. The proud disputant's entire posture depends on one hidden assumption — that it doesn't ultimately *matter*. That all of it flattens out in the end. That the one who sees and the one who refuses to see, the one who builds and the one who wrecks, the one who believes and the one who does evil — that when the dust settles, these are roughly the same, and so no one gets to rank him, and no Hour gets to sort him.

This ayah reaches in and pulls out that one assumption like a load-bearing beam. And it does it with an image so simple a child understands it and so deep that we will spend the next hour inside it.

Why does Allah answer arrogance with *blindness and sight*? Why does He weigh the righteous with three words and the evildoer with one? And why — after laying down a truth this obvious — does He end not with a thunderclap but with a sigh: *"Little do you take heed"*?

Let's go and see.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yastawī* — The Verb That Lays Two Things on the Same Level

#### First, the concept — before the word

Let me start somewhere completely ordinary. Imagine a kitchen scale, the old kind, with two pans hanging from a beam. You put an apple in one pan and an apple in the other, and the beam goes flat. *Level.* Balanced. Neither side higher. We have a word for that exact state in English — we say the two are "even," or "on a level," or "they come out equal."

Arabic has a verb that means precisely this physical act of *coming to the same level.* Its root is **س-و-ي (s-w-y)**, and it carries the sense of leveling, evenness, a flat surface with no part raised above another. When a road is *sawiyy*, it's smooth and even. When you make something *sawā'*, you flatten it out.

Hold that picture: **a beam that has gone flat, two things resting at the identical height.**

#### Now, the word

The verb in our ayah is **يَسْتَوِي — *yastawī***. And it is not the simple form of that root. It is **Form VIII** (the *iftaʿala* pattern, here *istawā*), a form the language uses to express a settled, achieved state — the thing has *come to rest* at a level. *Yastawī* is the present-tense verb for two realities sitting at the exact same height on the beam.

And what does Allah do with it? He **negates** it. *Wa mā yastawī* — "And they do **not** come level." The beam does **not** go flat.

#### Sit with this

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say "the believer is *better* than the evildoer," though that is true. He did not say "the seeing person is *superior*." He chose a verb that is, on its surface, almost neutral — the verb of *equivalence*. And then He denied it.

Why is that more powerful than simply asserting superiority?

Because to say "A is better than B" still keeps A and B in the same conversation, on the same scale, comparable. But to say **"these two do not even come level"** is to say something about the entire *attempt to equate them.* It targets not the ranking — it targets the **instinct to flatten.**

[PAUSE]

There is a hunger in the human heart — we will return to it fully in Part 2 — that wants the beam to go flat. That wants to hear: *it all comes out the same in the end.* The arrogant disputant of verse 56 *needs* that beam flat, because a level beam means no one is above him and nothing can judge him.

And Allah, with a single negated verb, refuses. **Mā yastawī.** The beam will not lie flat. It was never going to lie flat. The structure of reality has a tilt in it.

#### One more layer in the verb

Look at the grammar of the sentence and you'll notice something subtle. *Yastawī* is a **singular** verb — and yet it governs a whole crowd: the blind, the seeing, those who believed and worked righteousness, and the evildoer. One verb, holding multiple pairs.

It's as if Allah lays them all out across the same beam at once. Not "the blind isn't equal to the seeing, *and separately* the believer isn't equal to the evildoer." Rather: **the whole field of them does not come level.** One sweeping motion of the hand across all of reality: *none of this flattens.*

> **What this single verb reveals:** Allah did not merely rank the righteous above the wicked. He struck at the deeper lie — the fantasy that they belong on the same level at all. The beam does not go flat.

### Element Two: The Blind and the Seeing — A Physical Image Pointing Somewhere Unseen

#### The concept first

Here is something the Qur'an does constantly, and once you see it you will see it everywhere. Allah speaks about something you can *touch and see* — a star, a wind, a mountain, a seed, an eye — but the thing He is actually pointing at is something you *cannot* see: a state of the soul, a reality of the heart, a truth of the unseen world.

The visible thing is never the destination. It is a stepping stone. He gives you the *seen* so your mind can walk across it to the *unseen.*

#### The word — and what it is really about

So He says: **ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ وَٱلْبَصِيرُ** — *al-aʿmā wa l-baṣīr* — "the blind and the seeing."

Now, on the most literal level, this is obvious and true. A blind man and a sighted man are not in the same condition. No one argues with that. A child knows it.

But ask yourself — is Allah, in the middle of a surah about *faith and arrogance and the signs of Allah*, really pausing to inform us that sighted people can see and blind people cannot?

The classical scholars all understood: the physical pair is the stepping stone. Listen to how directly al-Qurṭubī reads it. *Al-aʿmā wa l-baṣīr*, he says, means **"the believer and the disbeliever, the misguided and the rightly guided."** The blindness here is not the blindness of the eyes. It is the blindness of the heart — the inability to *perceive* the signs that are everywhere.

This is the same truth Allah states flatly elsewhere: *"It is not the eyes that are blind, but blind are the hearts which are within the breasts"* (22:46).

#### Stay with the image — because it does work the abstract word could never do

Here is why Allah reaches for *blindness* and *sight* rather than just saying "the guided and the misguided." Watch what the image does.

A blind man and a seeing man can stand in the **exact same room.** Same furniture. Same sunlight pouring through the same window. Same painting on the same wall. Everything around them is identical. And yet they are living in **two completely different worlds.** One of them is bathed in color and form and depth and light. The other is in darkness, navigating by touch, bumping into the very things the other sees clearly.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The difference between them is **invisible** — and **total.** You could photograph the two men standing side by side and the photo would not show you the gulf between them. From the outside, they're two men in a room. From the inside, one is in light and one is in dark.

*That* is what Allah is telling you about faith.

The believer and the heedless evildoer walk through the **same world.** They see the same sunrise. They hold the same newborn child. They stand under the same night sky thick with stars. Everything around them is identical — the signs of Allah are spread out equally before both of them, like light filling a room.

And one of them *sees.* The sunrise is a sign. The child is an entrusted soul. The stars are an ayah written across the dark. He moves through a world that is *speaking to him.*

And the other walks through that identical room and perceives **nothing.** Not because the signs aren't there — they're everywhere, the room is flooded with them — but because the faculty that perceives them has gone dark.

> **What this image reveals:** The difference between the believer and the heedless is not a difference in the *world they live in.* It is a difference in what they can *perceive* of the same world. They stand in the same light. One is bathed in it; the other is in the dark and doesn't even know the light is on.

And now — can you feel why the verb *yastawī* had to be denied? Lay a blind man and a seeing man on the beam of "are these two the same?" Of course the beam tilts. Not because one is a worse *person*, but because one is *receiving reality* and the other is missing it entirely.

### Element Three: The Asymmetry — Three Words for One Group, One Word for the Other

This is the discovery I most want you to carry. It lives not in any single word but in the *shape* of the sentence — in what Allah gives one side and withholds from the other.

#### The concept: read the architecture, not just the bricks

When you build a balanced comparison, you usually keep both sides symmetrical. "The tall and the short." "The rich and the poor." Equal weight, equal words. The very *form* of the sentence says: *these are two of a kind, opposite ends of one scale.*

So watch what Allah does — and watch what He refuses to do.

#### Look carefully at the two sides

The first pair is perfectly balanced, one word each:

> ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ **/** ٱلْبَصِيرُ
> the blind **/** the seeing

But the second pair is **not balanced at all.** Watch:

> **ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ** — *"those who believed, and did, the righteous deeds"*
> **وَلَا ٱلْمُسِىٓءُ** — *"and not the evildoer."*

Count it. The believing side receives **a whole clause** — a relative pronoun (*those who*), a verb of the heart (*believed*, root **Amn**, Form IV), a verb of the limbs (*did*, root **Eml**), and an object (*the righteous deeds*, root **SlH**). Four moving parts. An entire little world of a life: inner conviction *and* outer action, the heart *and* the hand.

And the other side gets **one word.** *Al-musīʾ.* The evildoer. A single active participle (root **swA**, Form IV) — "the one who does evil." No verbs unrolled for him. No "those who." No description of his inner state. No catalog of his deeds. Just one bare word, and it's done.

[PAUSE]

#### Why would Allah do this? Sit here, because this is the heart of it.

There is a meaning in this asymmetry, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

**Goodness expands. Evil contracts.**

When Allah names the people of faith, the sentence *opens up.* It unfolds. It takes its time — belief, then action, then righteousness, layer upon layer — because a life of faith is *generative.* It branches. It grows. There is always *more* of it to name: more belief, more deeds, more good. The grammar itself blossoms.

And when Allah names the evildoer, the sentence *closes.* One word and the door shuts. Because evil, in the end, is **barren.** It does not generate; it consumes. It does not branch out; it collapses inward. There is less and less of it to describe, because it builds nothing. *Al-musīʾ* — and that's all. There is nowhere else for the word to go.

The very *length* of the two phrases is a portrait of the two destinies. One life keeps unfolding into more. The other shrinks down to a single, final, lonely word.

#### And there is one more thread in this — the extra *lā*

The early grammarians noticed something the casual reader skips. Al-Jalālayn points right at it: *"fīhi ziyādatu lā"* — **"in it is an added 'lā.'"**

The phrase is *wa **lā** l-musīʾ* — "and **not** the evildoer." But grammatically, the negation was *already done* at the start of the sentence. *Mā yastawī* — "they are not equal" — that "not" already covers everyone in the list. So this second *lā* before the evildoer is, strictly, *extra.* The sentence would be grammatically complete without it.

So why is it there?

Because in Arabic, a *repeated* negation is an **intensified** negation. By placing a fresh "not" directly in front of the evildoer — *and **not** the evildoer* — Allah doesn't just include him in the general "these aren't equal." He **singles him out** for his own emphatic denial. As if to say: *and let there be no confusion whatsoever about THIS one. Not him. Especially, emphatically, separately — not him.*

> **What the asymmetry reveals:** In the very architecture of the sentence — three words for the righteous and one for the wicked, plus a second negation pinned to the evildoer alone — Allah shows you that goodness is expansive and generative while evil is contracted and barren, and He slams a separate door of denial on any attempt to slip the evildoer onto the same level.

#### The bridge out of the language

So look at what these three discoveries have built. A verb — *yastawī* — that refuses to let the beam go flat. An image — *the blind and the seeing* — that shows the difference is total and invisible, a matter of what you can perceive of the same shared world. And a shape — three words against one — that reveals goodness as a thing that grows and evil as a thing that shrinks.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange thing happening in your own chest right now as you listen.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Beam Wants to Go Flat — and Why That Is the Deepest Temptation of Our Age

#### Begin where you actually live

Let me not start with the ayah. Let me start with a sentence you have heard a hundred times, and probably said yourself.

*"Who's to say?"*

*"Who's to say what's really right and wrong? Who am I to judge? It's all relative. You have your truth, I have mine. As long as you're a good person, what does the rest matter? In the end, aren't we all the same?"*

This is the air we breathe. It is considered the *humble* position, the *kind* position, the *tolerant* position. To insist that two ways of living are genuinely *not equal* — that one path leads somewhere good and another somewhere ruinous — sounds, to the modern ear, almost like an act of aggression.

And I want to be honest with you about why this idea is so seductive. It is seductive because it offers **peace without cost.** If the beam goes flat — if it all comes out the same — then I never have to do the hard work of choosing the harder path. I never have to look at how I'm living and feel the tilt. I can do as I please and rest in the comforting murmur: *it doesn't ultimately matter; we're all basically the same.*

[PAUSE]

#### Now let the ayah in

And into that comforting murmur, Allah speaks five words: **wa mā yastawī.** *They are not equal.*

But notice *how* He says it. He does not begin with the morally loaded pair. He does not open with "the believer and the evildoer," where you might brace yourself, where your defenses go up. He opens with **the blind and the seeing.**

And here is the genius of it. *Nobody* argues that the blind and the seeing are "really the same." No one looks at a man who cannot see and a man who can and says "who's to say there's a difference? It's all relative." The difference is too *obvious*, too rooted in reality, to deny.

So Allah uses the pair you *cannot* relativize to crack open the pair you *want* to relativize. He gets your "yes, obviously" on the blind and the seeing — and then, in the same breath, on the same beam, governed by the same verb, He lays down the believer and the evildoer. *And these — these too — are not equal.*

He is showing you: your instinct to flatten the moral world is not humility. It is a kind of **blindness** — the very blindness the ayah just named. The man who insists "it's all the same in the end" is not seeing *more* clearly than the believer. He is seeing *less.* He has talked himself into the dark room and called it open-mindedness.

#### The movement deeper

And here is the turn. The flattening instinct *feels* like generosity — *I'm not judging anyone!* — but follow it to its end and see what it actually does. If the believer and the evildoer come out the same, then **every act of goodness is pointless.** Every restraint you ever practiced, every temptation you ever resisted, every kindness that cost you — if the beam is flat, all of it was *wasted.* You denied yourself for *nothing.*

A flat beam does not honor the good. **It robs them.** It takes the person who spent their life building and the person who spent their life wrecking and tells them their lives meant the same. Far from being kind, the flat beam is the cruelest possible verdict on every soul who ever chose the hard right over the easy wrong.

So when Allah says *mā yastawī* — *they are not equal* — hear it correctly. This is not Allah being harsh. **This is Allah refusing to insult the good by equating them with their opposite.** The tilt in the beam is not a threat. For the one who is trying, the tilt is the only thing that makes the trying *matter.*

#### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical turn. The next time you feel that murmur rise — *does it even matter, isn't it all the same, who's to say* — recognize it for what it is. It is not your wisdom speaking. It is your fatigue, looking for permission to stop choosing.

And the practice is small and concrete: **the next time you face a fork where one path is right and harder and the other is easy and wrong, do not ask "does it matter?" Ask instead: "which of these am I — the seeing, or the blind?"** Because the beam is not flat. Your choice has weight. And the weight is a *gift* — it means your life is not a wash. It means the good you do is *kept.*

### Theme Two: "Little Do You Take Heed" — The Disease Is Not Ignorance. It Is Forgetting.

#### Start with a strange human fact

Let me ask you something. Think of the truths you most deeply *know* — that life is short, that this world will end, that the people you love will not be here forever, that you yourself will one day stand before your Lord.

You *know* these things. If I asked you, you would affirm every one without hesitation. They are not new information to you.

So why do you not *live* as though they were true?

Here is one of the most important observations a human being can make about themselves: **The gap between us and a transformed life is almost never a gap of knowledge. It is a gap of remembering.** We do not fail because we don't know. We fail because, in the moment that matters, we *forget what we already know.*

You know the food is bad for you — in the moment of craving, the knowledge goes dim. You know the anger will pass and the harsh word won't — in the heat of it, you forget. You know this world is fleeting — and then you spend a decade chasing it as though it were forever.

Knowledge sits in us like a lamp that keeps going out. We don't need a *new* lamp. We need to keep *relighting* the one we have.

#### Now hear how Allah closes the ayah

After laying down a truth as plain as daylight — *the blind and the seeing are not equal* — Allah does not end with a threat or a thunderclap. He ends with something far more piercing. A diagnosis. Almost a sigh:

> **قَلِيلًا مَّا تَتَذَكَّرُونَ**
> *Qalīlan mā tatadhakkarūn.* — **"Little do you take heed."**

And every word of this closing is chosen with terrible precision.

First, look at the word He uses for what we fail to do. It is **تَتَذَكَّرُونَ — *tatadhakkarūn*** — from the root **ذ-ك-ر (dh-k-r)**, the root of *remembering.* He does not say "little do you *know*" (*taʿlamūn*). He says **"little do you *remember*."** The problem He names is not that the truth never reached you. It is that the truth reached you — *and you keep letting it slip.*

#### Stay with the form of this verb — it is doing quiet work

And the *form* He chooses matters enormously. *Tatadhakkar* is **Form V** in Arabic (the *tafaʿʿala* pattern) — and Form V carries the meaning of an action done **to oneself, with effort, deliberately, repeatedly.** It is not passive remembering, the way a memory drifts up on its own. It is the *active, effortful work* of bringing something back to mind and holding it there. Of *re-collecting* yourself. Of deliberately relighting the lamp.

So the ayah is not accusing you of being uninformed. It is naming exactly the disease we just described: **you have the knowledge, but you do so little of the effortful work of bringing it back to mind.** *Qalīlan* — "little," and Allah front-loads the word for emphasis: *little, so very little,* is the recollecting that you do.

[PAUSE]

#### Go deeper — why this is mercy, not condemnation

Now feel the full weight of where this verb sits. It comes *immediately after* the blind and the seeing. And do you see what it implies? It implies that the difference between the blind and the seeing — between the one who perceives reality and the one who lives in the dark room — **comes down, in large part, to this single faculty: remembering.**

The "seeing" person is not someone with secret information you lack. Very often, the seeing person is simply **the one who remembers** — who keeps relighting the lamp, who brings the truth back to mind before each decision, so that they are *acting in the light of what they know.* And the "blind" person is the one who has the very same knowledge but lets it stay dark — who forgets in exactly the moment the knowledge was needed.

This is staggeringly hopeful. Because it means spiritual sight is not a fixed gift handed to some and denied to others. It is, to a real degree, a *practice.* The seeing keep their lamp lit. And anyone — anyone — can take up the work of relighting.

And notice the mercy in the *tone.* Allah does not say "you do not take heed *at all.*" He says **"little"** — *qalīlan.* There is some remembering in you. The lamp is not gone; it is just guttering, mostly dark, lit too rarely. He is not condemning you to blindness. He is pointing at the small, flickering flame you already have and saying: *this — there is so little of this. And it could be so much more.*

#### One scholarly note, honestly told

There is a beautiful detail the reciters preserved. Most of the early readers read this word as *yatadhakkarūn* — "little do **they** take heed" — keeping it as a report about the disputing people described before. But the reciters of Kufa read it as *tatadhakkarūn* — "little do **you** take heed" — turning suddenly to address *us* directly. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record this difference between the readings.

And subḥān Allāh, hold both in your hand at once, because together they tell the whole truth. In one reading, it is a sad report about *them* — those arrogant ones who would not see. In the other, the finger turns and points at *you,* at me, at the listener — *and how little do **you** remember?* The two readings together refuse to let us stand safely outside the verse, shaking our heads at "those blind people over there." The address swings around. It lands on us.

#### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practice, and it is the most important one I can give you. Your problem — my problem — is almost never that we don't *know.* It is that we don't *remember at the moment of choosing.* So the work is not to acquire more knowledge. The work is to **build remembering into your day.**

Concretely: before a decision that matters — before you speak the angry word, before you reach for the thing you know you shouldn't, before you let a moment of worship slide — *pause and relight the lamp.* Take three seconds to bring back to mind what you already know. *The beam is not flat. This choice has weight. Which am I right now — the seeing or the blind?* That three-second act of *tadhakkur* — of deliberately re-collecting yourself — is the entire difference the ayah is describing. It is sight itself, practiced one moment at a time.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have walked through, and watch how the linguistic discoveries and the human themes turn out to be **one single insight seen from two directions.**

From the side of the *words*, we found: a verb, *yastawī*, that refuses to let the beam of reality go flat. An image, *the blind and the seeing*, showing that two people in the same room, the same light, can live in two utterly different worlds — and the difference is invisible and total. And a *shape* — three unfolding words for the righteous, one bare word for the evildoer — revealing that goodness expands and evil contracts.

From the side of the *heart*, we found: that the deepest temptation of our age is to *want* the beam flat, to murmur "it's all the same" — and that this is not humility but a chosen blindness that robs every good deed of its worth. And we found that what separates the seeing from the blind is not secret knowledge but the small, effortful, repeated act of **remembering** what we already know.

And here is where the two directions meet. **Spiritual sight *is* remembering.** The "seeing" person of the image and the "one who takes heed" of the final phrase are the *same person* — the one who keeps relighting the lamp, who brings the truth back to mind before each fork in the road, and so walks through the shared world *perceiving* it rightly. The blindness Allah warns of and the forgetting Allah names are the **same darkness.** And the cure for both is the same small flame: *tadhakkur.* To remember. Again. And again.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I say "who's to say?" or "it's all the same in the end" — am I being humble, or am I just tired of choosing the harder path? What is that murmur really asking permission for?
2. The blind man and the seeing man stand in the same lit room. As I move through *my* ordinary day — the same sunrises, the same faces, the same signs — how much of it am I actually *perceiving*, and how much am I walking past in the dark?
3. The things I most need to remember, I already know. So where exactly is the lamp going out? In which specific moment of my day does the knowledge I have go dim right when I need it?
4. If goodness *expands* and evil *contracts* — if one life keeps unfolding into more and the other shrinks to a single barren word — which direction is the shape of my life currently moving?
5. The reading that says "little do **you** take heed" turns the finger toward me. Am I willing to stay inside that address — or do I keep myself safe by reading it as being about *other* people, the blind ones "over there"?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The eye that sees and the eye that is blind are not the same — and neither are the soul that believes and works and the soul that does evil; and you live as though they were equal not because you don't *know* the difference, but because you so rarely stop to *remember* it.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to acquire anything new. You already know that the beam is not flat. You already know which path is light and which is dark. The invitation is gentler and harder than learning: it is to **remember**, on purpose, again and again, in the small moments where it counts — and to let that remembering be the act of *seeing* itself. Not one grand awakening, but a lamp relit a hundred times a day. That, and not some secret knowledge, is the entire distance between the blind and the seeing.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who made the blind and the seeing unequal — do not leave us in the dark room calling it light. We do not lack knowledge of You; we lack the remembering of You. So make us of those who take heed — not little, but much. Keep relighting the lamp in our chests, and let us walk through Your world perceiving Your signs, until the day the beam is finally read and we are found, by Your mercy, among the seeing.* Āmīn.
