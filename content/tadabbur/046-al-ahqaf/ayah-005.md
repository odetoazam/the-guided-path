# Calling a Number That Will Never Answer

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first, three ways.

In Arabic:

> **وَمَنْ أَضَلُّ مِمَّن يَدْعُوا۟ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ مَن لَّا يَسْتَجِيبُ لَهُۥٓ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ وَهُمْ عَن دُعَآئِهِمْ غَـٰفِلُونَ**

In transliteration:

> *Wa man aḍallu mimman yadʿū min dūni-llāhi man lā yastajību lahū ilā yawmi-l-qiyāmati wa hum ʿan duʿāʾihim ghāfilūn.*

And in plain English:

> *And who is more astray than the one who calls — upon something other than Allah — that will never answer him, not until the Day of Resurrection? And they, of his calling, are completely unaware.*

### Let's rebuild the world this verse walked into

Picture the place. Mecca. A city built around a sanctuary, and inside and around that sanctuary, stone. Carved figures. Names that had been passed down for generations — al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt, and hundreds more. These were not abstractions to the people of that time. These were the addresses where they sent their deepest needs.

When the rains failed, they came here. When a child was sick, they came here. When the caravan was late and a family's whole fortune was riding on it, a mother stood in front of carved stone and *called*. She poured out the most desperate language a human being is capable of producing — the language of someone with nowhere else to turn — and she aimed all of it at an object that could not hear the first syllable.

And here is what I want you to feel before we go any further: **this was not stupidity.** These were intelligent people. Poets whose language we still study. Traders who navigated deserts by the stars. They were not foolish. They were *misdirected.* And the Quran is about to tell us that misdirection — when it concerns the deepest call of your heart — is the single most catastrophic mistake a human being can make.

Surah Al-Ahqaf opens by establishing the foundation: the heavens and the earth and everything between were created *bil-ḥaqq* — in truth, with purpose, for an appointed term. The universe is not random and it is not silent. It was made by Someone, for something. And then, immediately, the surah turns and asks a question. Not a gentle question. The sharpest question in the human vocabulary.

### The hook

The Quran is about to make a claim about *degrees* of lostness. It is going to identify, out of all the ways a human being can lose their way, the one that sits at the very bottom. The most astray. The furthest gone.

And when you find out what it is — it will not be the sin you expected. It will be something quieter, something almost everyone in this room does in some form, every single day.

Stay with me. Because by the end of this, the word "answer" is going to mean something it has never meant to you before.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The question that is not a question — *وَمَنْ أَضَلُّ*

Let me teach you something about how language works before we touch the ayah.

There is a kind of question that is not actually asking for information. You use it all the time. A friend tells you he lent his life savings to a man he met yesterday, and you say: *"Who does that?"* You are not requesting a list of names. You are saying: **nobody. Nobody sane does that.** The question form is a delivery mechanism for the strongest possible negative statement.

Arabic has a name for this. It is *al-istifhām bi-maʿnā al-nafy* — the interrogative that carries the meaning of total negation. And every classical commentator on this ayah — al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar — stops at these very first two words and says the same thing. Al-Qurṭubī writes it plainly: *wa man aḍallu* means **lā aḥada aḍallu** — *there is no one more astray.*

Now look at the second word: **أَضَلُّ**, *aḍallu*. This comes from the root *ḍād-lām-lām* (ﺽ ل ل) — the root of *ḍalāl*, of being lost, of wandering off the path. But the word is not just "lost." It is built on the form of *ism al-tafḍīl* — the comparative and superlative form. The form of *more* and *most*. *Aḍallu* means "more astray," "more lost."

So put the two pieces together. A rhetorical question that means absolute negation, plus a comparative that means "more lost than." What you get is not "this person is misguided." What you get is:

**No one — in the entire human catalogue of error — is more lost than this.**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with the weight of that. The Quran does not hand out this verdict casually. This exact construction — *wa man aḍallu mimman*, "and who is more astray than the one who…" — is a recurring formula in the Book. It is reserved. It is brought out for specific crimes: for the one who fabricates a lie against Allah, for the one who is reminded of the signs of his Lord and turns away from them. It is a phrase that marks the floor. The bottom of the descent.

And here, in Al-Ahqaf, the Quran spends that heavy phrase — that maximum verdict — not on the murderer, not on the tyrant, not on the thief.

It spends it on the one who *calls on something that cannot answer.*

Notice what that tells you. The Quran is making a claim about priorities that most of us have inverted. We rank sins by their visible damage. The Quran is telling us that the deepest catastrophe is not always the loudest one. It can be the quietest, most private act in the world — a person, alone, directing the call of their heart toward the wrong address.

What this single phrase reveals: **the worst place a human being can end up is not defined by how much harm they did to others, but by where they aimed the deepest cry of their own soul.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The broken circuit — *يَدْعُوا۟* and *دُعَآئِهِمْ*

Now I want you to notice something about the architecture of this ayah, something you cannot see in translation. One single root runs through it twice — and that root is the heartbeat of the entire verse.

The root is *dāl-ʿayn-wāw* (ﺩ ﻉ ﻭ). It appears first as a verb: **يَدْعُوا۟**, *yadʿū* — "he calls." And it appears again near the end as a noun: **دُعَآئِهِمْ**, *duʿāʾihim* — "their calling," "their supplication."

This is the root of *duʿāʾ.* The word every one of us knows. The word for the most intimate act of worship there is — raising your hands and *calling out.* The Prophet ﷺ said *al-duʿāʾ huwa-l-ʿibādah* — supplication *is* worship. So understand what the Quran is doing here. It is not describing some exotic ritual. It is describing **prayer itself.** The act of calling. The reaching-out of a heart that needs.

And here is the concept I want to install in you before we go deeper. A *duʿāʾ* is one half of something. Calling is only ever half of a circuit. The other half is the *response.* A call with no possible answer is not a relationship — it is a noise sent into a vacuum. The entire meaning of calling out depends on there being someone *there* to receive it.

Now hold that, and look at what sits in the exact center of the ayah, between the call and the calling: the thing being called upon. **مَن لَّا يَسْتَجِيبُ** — *man lā yastajīb* — "that which will not answer."

The verb here is **يَسْتَجِيبُ**, *yastajību*, from the root *jīm-wāw-bā* (ﺝ ﻭ ﺏ) — the root of *jawāb*, the answer, the reply. And it sits in **Form X** of the Arabic verb, *istafʿala*. This is the established form in the language of the Quran for *responding to a call.* When you make duʿāʾ, what you are begging for is *istijāba* — that your call be answered. It is the same word the Quran uses when Allah says *ujību daʿwata-l-dāʿ idhā daʿān* — "I answer the call of the caller when he calls upon Me."

So now see the full design. The Quran takes the word for *call* — and places it on both ends. And in the middle, where the *answer* should live, it puts: *the thing that does not answer.* The circuit is drawn for you, and then, right at the connection point, it is severed.

[PAUSE]

This is the verse showing you a person standing at one end of a wire that goes nowhere. They pour everything in. *Yadʿū.* And nothing — for the entire length of their life — ever comes back.

What this doubled root reveals: **the tragedy is not that the call is weak. The call may be passionate, sincere, lifelong. The tragedy is that there is nobody on the other end of the line.**

### Element Three: The time-stamp that hides a horror — *إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ*, and the chilling word *غَـٰفِلُونَ*

Now I want to read an absence with you. This is where tadabbur asks us to be most careful — to notice not only what is said, but the shape of the gap left behind by what is said.

The ayah says the called-upon thing *will not answer him* — and then it adds a time limit: **إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ** — *ilā yawmi-l-qiyāmah* — "until the Day of Resurrection."

Stop. Why a deadline at all? If you simply wanted to say *the idol never answers,* you would say "it never answers." Full stop. But the Quran does not say "never." It says "will not answer you *until* the Day of Resurrection."

Do you hear what that little preposition *ilā* — "until" — quietly implies? A boundary line has been drawn. Silence *up to* a point. And the human ear, hearing "silent until the Day of Resurrection," is forced to ask: *and then what? What happens on that Day, when the silence finally breaks?*

The Quran does not leave you guessing. It answers in the very next verse — ayah 6:

> *And when mankind is gathered, those [false gods] will be their enemies, and will deny their worship of them.*

[PAUSE]

There it is. The thing was silent your entire life — and the *first* time it ever "responds" to you is on the Day of Judgment, when it turns to face you and becomes your *enemy*, when it stands before Allah and says: *I never asked this person to call on me. I don't even know who they are.*

So the time-stamp is not a throwaway detail. It is a hidden door. *Silence now — and worse than silence later.* The lifelong unanswered call doesn't simply stay unanswered. It curdles into testimony against you.

And now the final word lands, and it is, to me, the most quietly devastating word in the entire verse: **غَـٰفِلُونَ**, *ghāfilūn.* From the root *ghayn-fā-lām* (ﻍ ﻑ ﻝ) — *ghafla*, heedlessness, obliviousness, total unawareness.

The verse does not merely say these objects *cannot* answer. It says: **وَهُمْ عَن دُعَآئِهِمْ غَـٰفِلُونَ** — "and they are utterly *unaware* of their calling."

Feel the layers here. Al-Baghawī and al-Muyassar both stop to explain it: these things are *jamād* — inert, lifeless — *lā tasmaʿ wa lā tafham*, they do not hear and they do not understand. And al-Qurṭubī notices something hauntingly precise about the grammar: the verse uses **وَهُمْ**, *hum* — the pronoun for *rational, conscious beings*, the "they" you use for *people.* The Arabic grants these dead objects the grammar of persons — because, al-Qurṭubī says, their worshippers had treated them exactly like kings and masters to be served. The grammar dresses them up as someone. And then the verse strips it all away with one word: *ghāfilūn.* The masters you served like royalty are not even *aware you exist.*

[PAUSE]

What these final words reveal: **it is one thing to call on someone who cannot help you. It is a deeper desolation to pour your life into something that does not even register that you are there.**

### Bringing the language together

So look at what the construction has shown us. A rhetorical question that delivers the harshest verdict in the Book. A call placed on both ends of a wire, with the answer cut out of the middle. A time-stamp that hides a coming betrayal. And a final word — *ghāfilūn* — that reveals the other end was never even *aware.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because this is not, in the end, a verse about stone statues in an Arabian city fourteen hundred years ago.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The disconnected number you keep on calling

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of pain that the modern world has made almost universal. You have texted someone — and watched the message get marked *read* — and then waited, and waited, and nothing came back. You know that specific silence. The little screen that says *seen* and then says nothing else. It is one of the loneliest feelings a person can have, because you have made yourself vulnerable — you reached out — and the reaching landed in a void.

Now make it worse. Imagine you have a phone number saved in your contacts. A number you call when things fall apart. When you're terrified, when you're celebrating, when you can't sleep at three in the morning. You call it constantly. You've been calling it for years. And the truth — the truth you have refused to look at — is that the number was *disconnected* a long time ago. There is no phone ringing on the other end. There is no person. There never was. The line goes nowhere, and it will go nowhere for the rest of your natural life.

[PAUSE]

That is *yadʿū man lā yastajību lahū ilā yawmi-l-qiyāmah.* That is the verse, dropped exactly into your century.

Because here is the thing the ayah understands about you that you might not have admitted to yourself: **everyone is calling something.** Every single human being has a number they dial when life gets real. The question the Quran asks is never "do you worship?" Everyone worships. Everyone has a thing they turn toward in the dark. The only question — the question on which everything hinges — is: *can the thing you're calling actually pick up?*

For some people it's a career. They pour their most desperate energy into it — *just get me this title, this raise, this recognition, and I'll finally be okay.* They are making duʿāʾ. They just aren't making it to Allah.

For some it's another person. A spouse, a parent, a partner, who has been quietly installed in the place where God should be — *if they just love me right, I'll be whole.* And no human being can carry the weight of being someone's god. They will fail you, not out of cruelty, but because the line was never built to bear that current.

For some it's an image. The version of yourself you perform for others. You call out to it constantly — *let them see me as enough* — and it never, ever answers, because an image cannot love you back.

[PAUSE]

And notice — the verse never says the caller is insincere. The Meccans were *sincere.* The man pouring his life into his career is sincere. The sincerity is not in question. **Sincerity aimed at the wrong address does not become an answer. It becomes a tragedy.** You can mean it with your whole heart and still be calling a disconnected number.

So what does this change about tomorrow? It asks you to do one quiet, brave audit. Sit down and ask: *When I am most afraid, what do I actually reach for first?* Not what I say I believe — what I *reach for.* That reflex, that first instinctive grab in the dark — that is your real qiblah. That is the number you've actually saved. And if it is anything that *lā yastajīb* — anything that cannot answer — the most merciful thing you can do for yourself is to stop dialing it, and to dial, instead, the only Line that has never once been busy.

Because the same Quran that says *they will not answer* also says — *wa idhā saʾalaka ʿibādī ʿannī fa-innī qarīb, ujību daʿwata-l-dāʿ idhā daʿān* — "When My servants ask you about Me, I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls on Me." (2:186) The whole tragedy of 46:5 exists only to make you run toward *that.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The terror — and the mercy — of being *known*

Now I want to take you to that last word, *ghāfilūn*, and turn it over, because there is a whole world hiding underneath it.

Start, again, with your life. There is a deep human ache that has nothing to do with religion on the surface — the ache of being *unseen.* Of doing your work and no one noticing. Of carrying a private grief that no one in your life is even aware of. Of being surrounded by people and feeling, in your core, that *nobody actually knows I'm here.* Psychologists have a hundred names for it. The Quran has one image for it: standing in front of something you serve with everything you have — and it is *ghāfil.* Unaware. It does not know your name. It does not know you've been calling. It does not know you exist.

That is the deepest poverty in the verse. Not just that your god can't help — but that your god doesn't even *know about you.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold that against an attribute of Allah that the Quran repeats over and over, almost like it is anticipating this exact wound. Again and again the Quran says: *wa ma-llāhu bi-ghāfilin ʿammā taʿmalūn* — "And Allah is **never** heedless of what you do." The exact word. *Ghāfil.* The thing your false gods are — *ghāfilūn* — is the very thing Allah declares, by name, that He can never be.

Sit with the precision of that. The Quran is not making a generic point. It is drawing a direct line between the defect of the false god and the perfection of the True One. Your idols are *ghāfilūn.* Your Lord is *laysa bi-ghāfil.* The one quality that makes calling on the false god so hopeless — its obliviousness — is the exact quality whose *opposite* defines the One you were made to call.

What does that mean for you, practically? It means there is no such thing, for the believer, as an unwitnessed moment. The tear you cried that no one saw — He saw it. The good you did that no one credited you for — He recorded it. The duʿāʾ you made under your breath, that you were almost embarrassed to make, that you assumed disappeared into the ceiling — it did not disappear. There is no ceiling. *He is never ghāfil.*

[PAUSE]

And yes — this cuts both ways, and the verse means it to. The same attentiveness that catches your tear catches your sin. To be perfectly *known* is terrifying as well as comforting. But here is the mercy hidden inside the terror: **a God who is never heedless is the only kind of God worth calling.** A god who could be distracted, who might miss your call, who could be unaware — that is not a comfort, that is the very horror of 46:5. The attentiveness that makes Him impossible to hide from is the *same* attentiveness that makes Him impossible to call in vain.

So the practical shift is this. Tonight, make one duʿāʾ — small, private, real — and make it while fully believing the thing this verse is teaching by contrast: that the One you are addressing is *not ghāfil.* He is not half-listening. He is not unaware that you've been struggling. He knows you are calling before the words form. Call Him like He's actually there — because the entire point of this ayah is that, unlike everything else you've ever called, **He actually is.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now that we've done the work, step back and look at how this verse is built, because the design is the proof of the message.

The Quran could have said, simply: *do not worship idols, they are useless.* Instead, it constructed a tight, sealed circle:

- It opens with the **maximum verdict** — *no one is more astray.*
- It places **calling** on the front end — *yadʿū.*
- It puts the **broken answer** in the dead center — *man lā yastajīb.*
- It hangs a **hidden horror** on a time-stamp — *until the Day of Resurrection* (and then they become your enemies).
- It places **calling** again on the back end — *duʿāʾihim.*
- And it seals the whole thing with the one word that names the defect — *ghāfilūn.*

Call … no-answer … call. The structure *is* the disconnected phone line. The verse doesn't just describe a severed circuit — it is shaped like one. You enter through "calling" and you exit through "calling," and in the dead center, where connection should be, there is nothing but silence and obliviousness. The form delivers the meaning before you've even parsed the words.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a call with the answer cut out of the middle. Your life shows you the disconnected number you keep dialing. **Same thing.**

The grammar showed us *ghāfilūn* — the unaware god — set against a Lord who is *never* unaware. Your life shows you the ache of being unseen, healed only by the One who never once looks away. **Same thing.**

The whole verse exists to relocate a single thing inside you: the address where you send your deepest call.

### Questions to carry

1. When the floor drops out from under me — the bad diagnosis, the lost job, the 3 a.m. fear — what do I *instinctively* reach for first? Not what I claim to believe. What I actually grab.
2. Is there a person, an image, or an ambition that I have quietly installed in the place where only Allah belongs — and am I angry at them for failing to be God?
3. Have I been treating my own duʿāʾ as if it disappears into a ceiling — as if Allah might be *ghāfil* of it?
4. If Allah is *never* heedless of what I do, what would change in the next twenty-four hours if I actually lived inside that fact?
5. What is one disconnected number in my contacts that I am finally ready to stop calling?

### The one-sentence distillation

**There is no one more lost than the person who pours the deepest call of their heart into something that cannot hear them now and will turn against them later — so aim your call at the only One who is never, ever unaware.**

### The closing invitation

You are not being asked to call less. You are a human being; you will always be calling something. You are being invited to *redirect* the call. To take the same passion, the same desperation, the same lifelong reaching — and aim it, finally, at an address where someone is home.

The disconnected line was never the tragedy the Quran wanted to leave you with. It mentioned it for one reason: so that you would hang up, and dial the Number that has never once failed to answer.

### A brief du'a

*Yā man laysa bi-ghāfil — O You who are never heedless — we confess that we have spent calls on things that could not hear us. Turn our hearts away from every disconnected line, and toward You alone. And when we call You, in the dark, with no words left — answer us, as You promised You would.* Āmīn.
