# Bring Me a Trace: When Devotion Is Asked for Its Evidence

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by reading it to you, and then let's slow everything down.

> قُلْ أَرَءَيْتُم مَّا تَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ أَرُونِى مَاذَا خَلَقُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ أَمْ لَهُمْ شِرْكٌ فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ ۖ ٱئْتُونِى بِكِتَـٰبٍ مِّن قَبْلِ هَـٰذَآ أَوْ أَثَـٰرَةٍ مِّنْ عِلْمٍ إِن كُنتُمْ صَـٰدِقِينَ

*Qul a-ra'aytum mā tad'ūna min dūni-llāhi arūnī mādhā khalaqū mina-l-arḍi am lahum shirkun fi-s-samāwāti'tūnī bi-kitābin min qabli hādhā aw athāratin min 'ilmin in kuntum ṣādiqīn.*

"Say: Have you really considered what you call upon besides Allah? Show me what they have created of the earth — or do they have any share in the heavens? Bring me a scripture from before this one, or some lingering trace of knowledge, if you are truthful."

### Rebuilding the world

I want you to step into Mecca before you read this as an abstract argument. Because this ayah was not revealed into a vacuum of philosophy. It was revealed into a city full of stone.

The Kaʿbah, in those years, was ringed with idols. Three hundred and sixty of them, the reports say. Each one had a name, a lineage, a clientele. Families had inherited their gods the way you inherit a surname. There was Hubal, there was al-Lāt, there was al-ʿUzzā, there was Manāt. People did not worship them out of stupidity. They worshipped them out of *belonging*. These were the gods of their fathers, the gods their grandmothers had wept before, the gods woven into the fabric of who they were as a tribe.

And here is the thing that matters most for understanding this ayah: **almost nobody in Mecca claimed these idols had created the world.** Ask a Meccan polytheist, "Who made the heavens and the earth?" and the Quran itself tells us their answer — *they would say, Allah* (29:61, 31:25). They knew. The Creator was not in dispute.

So what was the idol *for*? It was a means. A bridge. An intermediary. *We only worship them so that they may bring us closer to Allah* — that was the logic the Quran preserves for us in Surah az-Zumar (39:3). The idol was a manager you went through to reach the King. A point of access. A spiritual middleman.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that in your mind, because it changes everything about the question this ayah is about to ask. Allah is not going to argue with people who think a rock made the sky. He is going to do something far more surgical. He is going to walk up to the *middleman* — the intermediary you have given a share of your heart — and ask it for its credentials.

### The hook

There is a courtroom buried in this verse. And the most extraordinary thing about it is *who is put on the stand*. It is not the unbeliever who is cross-examined. It is his god. Allah turns to the most devout idolater in Mecca and says, in effect: *I am not even going to talk to you. Bring me your deity's résumé. Show me one line of it. One.*

And the demand that follows is so complete, so total, that by the time the verse ends there is not a single corner of reality left where the evidence could possibly be hiding. Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Mā tad'ūn* — "what you *call upon*"

Before we touch the word, let me teach you the concept underneath it, because the whole verse pivots on it.

In English we have a flat word: "worship." It sounds formal, ritual, churchy. But the Arabic verb the Quran reaches for here is not the word for ritual worship. The root is **د-ع-و** *(d-ʿ-w)*, and the verb is *tadʿūn*. Its core meaning is **to call, to call upon, to summon, to cry out to.**

Think about what calling actually is. You do not call upon a chair. You do not cry out to a wall. **Calling presupposes that someone is there who can hear you, and who can answer.** When a child is frightened in the dark, they do not call out to the furniture. They call *"Mama."* The word *daʿwah* carries that entire emotional architecture inside it — need, dependence, the assumption of a listening ear, the hope of a response.

So when Allah describes the idols not as "what you worship" but as **what you *call upon*** — *mā tadʿūn* — He has already, in a single word, exposed the relationship. These people in their moment of fear, in their hour of need, in the sleepless night, were *crying out* to these things. They were summoning them. They expected to be heard.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something. Allah does not say *man tadʿūn* — "*whom* you call upon." He says ***mā* tadʿūn** — "***what*** you call upon."

In Arabic, *man* (مَن) is the word you use for rational, conscious beings — for *whos*. *Mā* (مَا) is the word you use for things, objects, the *whats* of the world. And Allah deliberately files these so-called gods under *mā*. Under "things."

Sit with the quiet devastation of that. You are crying out, in the dark, with the full weight of your heart — to a *what*. To an *it*. You are addressing your deepest need to something that grammar itself refuses to grant a personality. The verse hasn't even reached its argument yet, and the noun has already passed sentence.

What this single word reveals: **the tragedy of misplaced calling is not that you worshipped — it is that you cried out, and there was no one of the rank of a "who" on the other end of the line.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Arūnī* — "*Show* me"

Now the verse turns from description to demand, and I need you to hear the precise shape of the demand, because it is built into the very form of the verb.

The word is **أَرُونِى** — *arūnī* — "show me." It comes from the root **ر-أ-ى** *(r-'-y)*, the root of *seeing*. But look closely: this is not the plain verb "to see." This is **Form IV** of that root — *arā* — and Form IV here is **transitive-causative**: it does not mean "to see," it means **"to cause someone to see," "to make me see," "to bring before my eyes."**

Let me make the difference concrete. There is a world of distance between "I understand your point" and "*Show* me." Between "tell me about it" and "put it in my hand." A teacher who says *show your working* is not asking for your conclusion — she is demanding the visible, traceable evidence that leads to it. *Arūnī* is that exact register. It is the language of **"don't tell me — demonstrate it. Make me see it with my own eyes."**

And there is a beautiful, almost cutting symmetry hiding here that the morphology lets us catch. The verse *opened* with **أَرَأَيْتُم** *(a-ra'aytum)* — "have you *seen* / have you considered" — same root, ر-أ-ى. And now it answers itself with **أَرُونِى** — "make me *see*." Allah says: *you* have "seen" fit to take these gods. Now **make Me see** what you saw. Show Me what you found in them that I have missed.

[PAUSE]

And what, exactly, is demanded to be shown? Here the verse becomes architecture. Listen to the four walls it builds, and watch how there is no escaping through any of them.

**First wall — the earth.** *Arūnī mādhā khalaqū mina-l-arḍ* — "Show me **what they have created of the earth.**" Not "did they create the earth" — that would be too easy to deny grandly. The challenge is microscopic and merciless: show me *one created thing*, however small, *from* the earth, that they made. An ant. A seed. A grain of sand. The word خَلَقُوا *(khalaqū)* is a plural verb — *they created* — granting the idols, for the sake of argument, the dignity of acting subjects, and then daring them to point to a single product of their action.

**Second wall — the heavens.** *Am lahum shirkun fi-s-samāwāt* — "**Or do they have any *share* in the heavens?**" The earth was about manufacture; the heavens are about ownership. And the word is **شِرْك** *(shirk)* — a *partnership, a stake, a share.* Notice it is indefinite — *shirkun*, "*any* share at all." Allah lowers the bar to the floor: I am not asking whether your god co-owns the cosmos. I am asking whether it holds even a *sliver*, a fractional stake, a single share certificate in the heavens above you. And this is the very word — *shirk* — that names the entire sin. The crime is named after the share that does not exist.

Hear how the two walls cover the totality of space: **the earth beneath your feet, the heavens over your head.** The horizontal world and the vertical world. Every direction you could point, the question has already been there before you.

What *arūnī* reveals, then: **the demand of true belief is not "feel it" but "show it" — and when the showing is demanded across the whole of creation, the silence that answers is itself the argument.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Athāratin min ʿilm* — "a lingering *trace* of knowledge"

Now the verse does something I find almost tender in its thoroughness. Having sealed off the physical universe, it turns and seals off the world of *texts and traditions* too — and it does so with one of the most exquisite words in the Quran.

The root is **أ-ث-ر** *(a-th-r)*. And before I tell you what *athārah* means, let me give you the root's whole family, because the family is the meaning. From this one root comes *athar* — a **footprint**, the mark a foot leaves in sand after the foot is gone. From it comes *āthār* — **ruins, relics, antiquities**, what remains of a civilization after the civilization has died. And — this will matter to anyone who has studied hadith — from it comes the technical term *athar*: **a transmitted report**, a saying *handed down*, passed from hand to hand across the generations.

Do you feel the common thread running through all of them? **Something that remains after the thing itself is gone. A residue. A trace. The print left behind.**

So when Allah says ***athāratin min ʿilm*** — He is not asking, "do you have knowledge?" He is asking for something far smaller, far more generous, far harder to refuse. He is asking: bring me a **remnant** of knowledge. A *vestige*. A single surviving footprint of authentic transmitted truth — even a fragment, even a scrap inherited from those who came before — that supports what you do.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators circle this word like a jewel, and each catches a different facet of its light. Al-Kalbī said *athārah* means **a remnant of knowledge handed down from the ancients** — something *asnada*, attributed back to them. Mujāhid and ʿIkrimah and Muqātil said it means **a narration carried from the prophets.** Qatādah said it means **a distinct, specialized knowledge.** Do not hear these as three competing answers. Hear them as three camera angles on one reality: *athārah* is the **surviving trace of authentic transmission** — whether it descends through a scripture, through a prophet, or through inherited learning. The footprint of real knowledge in the sand of history.

And now stand back and see what Allah has actually demanded across the whole verse, because the architecture is complete. He asked for evidence from **four** doors, and there is no fifth door in all of human knowing:

1. **The earth** — empirical evidence from the world below.
2. **The heavens** — evidence of a stake in the world above.
3. **A scripture before this one** — *bi-kitābin min qabli hādhā* — revealed, written text.
4. **A trace of knowledge** — *athāratin min ʿilm* — transmitted, inherited learning.

The seen world and the unseen. The made thing and the written thing. Sense-experience and tradition. **Every category by which a human being can ever claim to *know* anything — Allah has named it, opened the door, and said: bring it through.** Empirical, cosmological, scriptural, traditional. The full epistemology of mankind, laid out as four invitations.

And from every door — silence.

What *athārah* reveals: **the case against false devotion is not closed by overwhelming the idolater with proof, but by offering him every avenue of proof and watching him return from each one empty-handed.**

### The bridge

Look at what these three words have built between them. *Tadʿūn* exposed the *relationship* — a heart calling out to a "what." *Arūnī* set the *standard* — show, don't merely feel. *Athārah* completed the *search* — every door of human knowledge thrown open, every one returning empty.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about the things *you and I* call upon, and about what it actually means to build a life on evidence rather than on inheritance.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The thing that cannot create has no claim on your dependence

Let me start where you live, far from any stone idol.

We live in the age of the demand for evidence. *Cite your source. Where's the data? Show me the study.* We have trained ourselves — rightly — to be suspicious of claims that arrive without proof. We will not buy a product without reading the reviews. We will not believe a headline without checking it. We have become a civilization that asks, reflexively, *how do you know?*

And yet. Notice how often we will pour the deepest dependence of our hearts into things we have never once cross-examined.

Think honestly about what you *call upon* — in the *daʿwah* sense, the crying-out sense — when you are afraid at two in the morning. What do you reach for when the diagnosis comes, when the money runs out, when the relationship cracks? For many of us the honest answer is: the bank balance. The career. The reputation. A person. The phone, the scroll, the substance that numbs. We call upon these. We summon them. We expect them to answer the ache.

Now bring this ayah's question — Allah's *exact* question — to each one of them. Not "do I love it?" Not "do I enjoy it?" The question is sharper than that: **"What has it created? Does it own a single share of the heavens?"** *Mādhā khalaqa? Am lahu shirkun fi-s-samāwāt?*

[PAUSE]

The career did not create you. It cannot add one hour to your life or subtract one. The money owns no share of the sky from which your rain and your provision descend. The person you have made into your everything — they cannot create their own next heartbeat, let alone yours. Every single one of these things you call upon is, like the Meccan idol, an *intermediary you have over-trusted* — a *what* you have begun addressing as if it were a *who*.

And here is the precise mercy of the test Allah gives. He does not ask you to stop *using* these things. The earth is to be farmed, the career is to be worked, the doctor is to be consulted. He asks you to stop *calling upon* them — to stop placing in them the cry of ultimate dependence that belongs only to the One who actually creates and actually owns a share, in fact the *whole* share, of the heavens.

The diagnostic is devastatingly simple. **Whatever cannot create, cannot save. And whatever cannot save does not deserve the weight you are putting on it.** The idol's failure to produce evidence is not an abstract theological point. It is the reason your heart keeps breaking on things too small to hold it.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time you feel that 2 a.m. reflex to reach for the thing you call upon, pause for the length of one breath and ask it Allah's question — *what did you ever create?* — and then redirect that same cry, unchanged in its intensity, to the One who created the questioner and the question both. You don't have to manufacture new feeling. You only have to re-aim the cry you already have.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Real faith carries the burden of proof — and that is its dignity, not its weakness

There is a story the modern world tells about religious people, and you have heard it: that faith is what you believe *without* evidence. That the believer is the one who switched off the demand for proof, closed his eyes, and leapt. Belief, in this telling, is the opposite of inquiry.

Now read this ayah again and feel how completely it inverts that story.

**Who is demanding evidence here?** *Allah is.* Revelation itself is the party in this exchange standing up and saying *arūnī* — *show me, bring me, produce your proof, where is your scripture, where is your trace of knowledge.* It is the *idolater* — the traditional one, the one who inherited his gods from his fathers — who has nothing to show. The believer is not the credulous one in this scene. **The believer is the prosecutor.**

Stay with how strange and liberating this is. The Quran does not say "believe and don't ask." It models, in the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ himself — *qul*, "say it" — a faith that walks straight up to the most cherished beliefs of an entire society and asks them to *show their working*. It subjects the inherited, the comfortable, the ancestral, to the cold question: *where is your athārah? Where is even a footprint of real knowledge under this?*

[PAUSE]

And this is precisely the burden Allah is willing to carry for His *own* message. Notice the phrase *bi-kitābin min qabli hādhā* — "a scripture **from before this one.**" The word *hādhā*, "this," is the Quran pointing at *itself.* Allah is saying: I am not asking you for something I have failed to provide. *This* — this recitation in your ears — is My evidence, My *kitāb*, My *athārah min ʿilm*. I have brought My proof. Now you bring yours.

The Quran does not ask to be believed on the same empty terms it has just exposed in the idols. It puts itself forward *as* the evidence and invites the comparison. That is not the posture of a claim afraid of scrutiny. That is the posture of a claim that has *read the room and chosen to be examined.*

So let me say to you what I think this ayah is quietly saying to every one of us who has ever felt embarrassed, in a skeptical age, to believe: **your faith is not the absence of the question. Your faith is the answer that survived the question.** Tawḥīd is not the belief you were too incurious to interrogate. It is the conclusion left standing on the floor of the courtroom after every false claimant has been asked *arūnī* and has walked out with empty hands.

[PAUSE]

But there is a turn here I don't want us to miss, and the verse delivers it in its final breath: *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "**if you are truthful.**" The root is **ص-د-ق** *(ṣ-d-q)*, the root of *truth* and *sincerity*. The whole challenge resolves not into a sneer but into a condition about the *heart*. Because Allah knows the idolater's problem was never really a shortage of evidence. The Creator was never in doubt — they admitted Him with their own mouths. The problem was that they did not *want* the conclusion the evidence pointed to. They preferred the gods that asked less of them.

And that lands on us with equal force. Most of us, when we cling to the thing that cannot create, are not suffering from missing data. We *know* the career cannot save us. We *know* the person is not God. The challenge *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* is not "do you have the proof?" — it is "**are you honest enough to follow the proof you already have?**" The verse begins as an argument about evidence and ends as a question about *sincerity*, because in the end those were always the same question.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** stop waiting for more evidence to begin worshipping rightly. You are not short of proof — the sky over your head and the heartbeat in your chest are *athārah* enough. You are being asked, gently and by name, to be *ṣādiq* — to let your life finally agree with what your mind already concedes. Faith's next step is not more certainty. It is more honesty.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we have walked through it, stand back and see the shape of the whole.

The verse is built as a perfect, sealing structure — a courtroom with no unguarded exit:

- It opens by exposing the **relationship**: you *call upon* — *tadʿūn* — a "what," a *mā*.
- It sets the **standard**: not feeling but sight — *arūnī*, "make me see."
- It then closes the **four doors of all human knowing**, in two pairs:
  - **The physical universe** — the *earth* below (what did they make?) and the *heavens* above (what do they own?).
  - **The world of knowledge** — a *scripture* before this (the written) and a *trace of knowledge* (the transmitted).
- And it seals the whole with the key that turns the lock from argument to heart: *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "**if you are truthful.**"

Every avenue of evidence, named and opened. Every one, returning empty. And the silence that fills the room is the verdict — which is exactly why the very next verse can ask: *and who is more astray than the one who calls upon what will never answer him until the Day of Resurrection?* (46:5). The case had to be airtight *here* for the sentence to be just *there*.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

From the side of *language*: *mā tadʿūn* showed a heart crying to a "thing"; *arūnī* demanded that the thing prove itself; *athārah* opened every door and found every door empty.

From the side of *life*: we, too, cry out to things that cannot create; we, too, are invited to demand that our dependencies show their credentials; and we, too, discover that the problem was never the evidence — it was our willingness to be *ṣādiq* with what the evidence has always plainly said.

**One word for a "thing," one demand to "show," one footprint that was never there.** That is the whole verse, and it is the whole of misplaced devotion, anywhere, in any age.

### Questions to carry

1. What is the *mā* in my life — the *what* — that I have started addressing as if it were a *who*?
2. If I asked the thing I most depend on Allah's own question — *what did you ever create? what share do you own in the sky?* — what would its honest answer be?
3. Is my hesitation in faith really a shortage of evidence — or a shortage of *ṣidq*, of honesty with the evidence I already have?
4. When I am afraid at 2 a.m., who — or *what* — do I instinctively call upon? And what does that instinct reveal about where my heart has quietly placed its trust?
5. Have I treated my belief as the question I never asked — or as the answer that survived being asked?

### One-sentence distillation

**Whatever cannot create has no claim on your cry — and Allah, having opened every door of evidence to the false god and watched it return empty-handed, asks you not for more proof but for the honesty to follow the proof you already hold.**

### The closing invitation

I am not inviting you tonight to *find* new evidence for God. The whole point of this ayah is that the evidence was never the missing piece — the *earth* under you and the *heavens* over you have been testifying your whole life. The invitation is smaller and harder than finding evidence. It is to take one thing you have been *calling upon* — name it, privately, right now — and to quietly ask it Allah's question, and then to let your heart do what an honest heart does when it sees that the thing it leaned on cannot hold weight: turn, with the very same intensity of need, to the One who actually creates. Not with less feeling. With the same feeling, finally aimed home.

### A closing du'ā

*Allah, You who alone created the earth beneath us and own every share of the heavens above us — forgive us for the things we have cried out to that could never answer. You have given us proof enough; give us now the ṣidq, the honest heart, to follow it. Untangle our deepest dependence from every "what," and let it rest, at last, on the only "Who."*

*Āmīn.*
