# The Word That Came Due

## Introduction: A Verdict Spoken Over a Life

Listen to it first in Arabic. Let the sound arrive before the meaning:

> **أُولَٰئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ حَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلْقَوْلُ فِىٓ أُمَمٍ قَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلِهِم مِّنَ ٱلْجِنِّ وَٱلْإِنسِ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ خَٰسِرِينَ**

*Ulā'ika alladhīna ḥaqqa ʿalayhimu al-qawlu fī umamin qad khalat min qablihim mina al-jinni wa al-insi, innahum kānū khāsirīn.*

"Those are the ones against whom the word has come true, among nations of jinn and men that had passed away before them. Indeed, they were losers."

### Rebuild the world

To feel where this ayah falls, you have to stand in the surah a few verses earlier.

Al-Ahqaf has just shown us something tender. In verse 15, a human being turns to Allah at the peak of maturity — *"until, when he reaches his full strength and reaches forty years"* — and prays one of the most beautiful prayers in the Qur'an: *"My Lord, enable me to be grateful for Your favor which You have bestowed upon me and upon my parents, and to do righteousness that pleases You, and make righteous for me my offspring."* A grown person, looking back at a lifetime of unearned gifts, asking only to be allowed to say *thank you* — and to be made worthy of it.

Then verse 16 gives the verdict on that person: **"Those are the ones from whom We accept the best of what they did."**

And then — the turn. Verse 17 shows us the opposite human being. A child who, when his believing parents call him toward faith and warn him gently about the meeting with Allah, answers with a single syllable of disgust: *"Uff to you both!"* — and scoffs, *"Are you promising me that I will be brought forth, when generations before me have already passed and gone?"* He uses the dead as his argument. *Where are they? Nobody came back. Spare me your stories.*

Our ayah, verse 18, is the sentence handed down over **that** second human being.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the climate you must inhabit. The Qur'an does not raise its voice here. It does not thunder. It does something quieter and far heavier. It simply files this person away. It takes the loud, sneering individual who thought he was clever — *generations passed and nobody came back* — and it answers him with his own evidence: *yes. Generations passed. And you are about to become one of them. You are being entered into their register.*

### The hook

There is a word at the center of this ayah — **ḥaqqa**, "came true," "became due." It is a word from the world of debts and verdicts and things that finally fall due. And there is a strange detail beside it: Allah says *the* word — **al-qawl** — as though you already know exactly which word He means.

He never tells you the content of the word. He assumes you remember it.

What word came true against them? And why does the Qur'an answer a man who mocked the dead by quietly placing him *among* them? Stay with me. Because by the end, you will see that this entire verse is the mirror image of the verse two lines above it — and the difference between the two destinies comes down to a single Arabic preposition.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: ḥaqqa ʿalayhim al-qawl — "The Word Came Due Against Them"

### Teaching the concept first

In English we have a phrase from the world of money: a debt *falls due*, or *comes due*. There's a date on a loan. For months it sits in the future, abstract, not yet real to you. Then the day arrives, and suddenly the thing that was only words on a contract becomes a force in your life — the bank can act, the obligation is now *enforceable*. Nothing about the debt changed. What changed is that it crossed from the realm of *stated* into the realm of *binding*.

Arabic has a root that lives exactly in this space: **Ḥ-Q-Q** (حقق). It is one of the great roots of the Qur'an. From it comes *al-ḥaqq* — the truth, the real, that which is firmly established and cannot be dislodged. From it comes *ḥaqq* in the legal sense — a right, a claim, something genuinely *owed*. The root means: that which is solid, fixed, true, and therefore **due**.

So when the verb **ḥaqqa** is used, it doesn't just mean "became true" in the sense of "turned out to be correct." It means something *settled into place as binding reality.* It became *enforceable.*

### Opening the word

> **حَقَّ عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلْقَوْلُ**

Look at the architecture of it. **Ḥaqqa** — "became due," a perfect-tense verb, completed action. **ʿAlayhim** — "against them," "upon them." And **al-qawlu** — "the word," the saying, definite, with the definite article *al-*.

Notice the preposition. **ʿAlā** — *upon, against.* It is the preposition of weight pressing down. When something is *ʿalayka*, it is *on* you, a burden on your shoulders, an obligation against your account. The word did not come *to* them, did not come *for* them. It came **down upon** them.

### Deepening

Let's sit with the strangeness of **al-qawl** — *the* word.

In Arabic, the definite article is not casual. When Allah says *al-qawl* and not simply *qawl* — "a word" — He is pointing to a specific, known, already-existing word. He is treating it as something the listener can identify. As though to say: you know which word I mean.

So which word?

The classical commentators are united on the substance. Al-Jalalayn glosses it plainly: *ḥaqqa ʿalayhim al-qawl* means **"the word of punishment became binding upon them."** Al-Muyassar: *"the punishment of Allah became due upon them, and His penalty and wrath descended upon them."* The "word" is a **sentence** in the judicial sense — a verdict.

But why *the* word, as if pre-existing? Classical commentary draws a thread here that runs all through the Qur'an. There is a decree Allah announced at the very foundation, a sentence He spoke over creation in advance: **"I shall surely fill Hell with jinn and mankind together"** — *la-amla'anna jahannama mina al-jinnati wa al-nāsi ajmaʿīn* (you find it in 32:13, 11:119, 7:18, 38:85). That is a **qawl** — a word — spoken before any of these particular people existed. A standing decree, waiting.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what the ayah is doing. This man in verse 17 thought time and death were his evidence *against* the resurrection. *Generations passed and none returned* — therefore your warning is empty. And Allah's answer is: there was a *word* spoken before you were born, a word about exactly this — about jinn and mankind who choose denial. That word has been standing, due, waiting. And against you — **ʿalayka** — it has now *fallen due.*

The thing he treated as fiction was a contract with a maturity date. And the date arrived.

Watch how precise the tense is. **Ḥaqqa** is perfect — *completed.* It is not "the word will come due." It already has. From the vantage point Allah is speaking from, the matter is closed. The man may still be walking around sneering. But the verdict is already entered. **The word, against him, has come true.**

### Conclusion of the element

*Ḥaqqa ʿalayhim al-qawl* tells us that there is a kind of truth that doesn't ask your opinion. You can deny a debt all you like. Denial is not a payment. The word does not need your belief to come due — it needs only the date to arrive.

## Element Two: qad khalat — "They Had Passed Away" — and the Empty Place

### Teaching the concept first

Picture a house at the end of a long life. The family that filled it for fifty years — the cooking, the arguments, the children on the stairs — is gone now. You walk in and the rooms are *empty.* Not destroyed. Empty. The space that was once so full of them has been *vacated.* That specific feeling — a place that has been emptied of the people who filled it — is the exact center of an Arabic root.

The root is **Kh-L-W** (خلو). At its base, *khalā* means **to become empty, to become vacant.** A *makān khālin* is an empty place. And from this physical emptiness, Arabic builds its word for the passing of time and of peoples: *khalat al-qurūn* — "the generations passed" — literally, *the generations emptied out.* They vacated the space. The room of the world emptied of them.

### Opening the word

> **فِىٓ أُمَمٍ قَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلِهِم**

**Qad khalat** — "had already passed away," "had already emptied out and gone." The particle **qad** before a perfect verb intensifies the *completedness* of it: *已经* — already, definitely, fully done. **Khalat** — the feminine perfect, agreeing with *umam*, nations. They passed. They emptied. They are gone.

### Deepening

Here is the quiet devastation, and it is built right into the grammar.

Go back to verse 17. The mocking man's exact argument was: *"qad khalati al-qurūnu min qablī"* — "generations **have already passed away** before me." He used the verb **khalat.** *The generations emptied out and nobody came back — so your resurrection is a fairy tale.*

And now, in verse 18, Allah takes his own verb and turns it on him: *fī umamin **qad khalat** min qablihim* — "among nations that **had already passed away** before them."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? He said, *"the generations passed — where are they?"* And Allah replies, *"yes — and you are now going to be placed* **among** *those very nations that passed."*

He pointed at the empty houses as proof that nobody is held to account. And the verse answers: those empty houses are not proof of escape. They are the *register you are about to be entered into.* The dead he mocked are not gone into nothing. They have gone *ahead* — into the same accounting that is now coming for him. He thought "passed away" meant "vanished." Allah uses the same word to mean "preceded you into judgment."

Notice the image, and notice it is *physical*, exactly as Allah's method works. *Khalat* — emptied out. A place vacated. The whole human story rendered as one room that keeps emptying — a generation fills it, then *khalat*, it empties; another fills it, *khalat*, it empties. And the man in verse 17 stood in the briefly-full room and laughed at the empty chairs, never feeling the obvious thing: **the room is emptying around him too.** His own chair is being readied for vacancy. *Qad khalat* is not describing strangers. It is describing the seat he is sitting in.

### Conclusion of the element

*Qad khalat* takes the very evidence the denier was proud of — *look how everyone just disappears* — and reveals it as the evidence *against* him. The emptying of the world is not a sign that there's no reckoning. It is the line you are standing in.

## Element Three: mina al-jinni wa al-insi — The Hidden and the Familiar, Both Bound

### Teaching the concept first

Two of the most quietly beautiful words in the Qur'an sit side by side here, and most of us pass over them as just "jinn and humans." But listen to what is inside the two roots, because Allah pairs them on purpose.

**Al-jinn** comes from the root **J-N-N** (جنّ), and that root means one thing at its core: **to be hidden, concealed, veiled from sight.** From it comes *jannah* — a garden, so dense with foliage that the ground is *hidden.* From it comes *janīn* — the fetus, *hidden* in the womb. From it comes *junūn* — madness, the mind *veiled* from reason. And *jinn* — the beings *hidden* from human sight. The whole root is the unseen, the covered, the concealed.

**Al-ins** comes from the root **A-N-S** (أنس), and at its core it means **the familiar, the seen, the companionable, that with which one is at ease.** From it comes *uns* — intimacy, familiarity. *Insān* — the human, the familiar one, the one you can see and know. *Anīs* — a friendly companion.

So the pairing is not just "spirits and people." It is, at the level of the roots themselves: **the hidden and the seen. The concealed and the familiar.** Together they name *the entire population of morally accountable creation* — everything veiled from you and everything visible to you, the whole spectrum, leaving nothing out.

### Opening the words

> **مِّنَ ٱلْجِنِّ وَٱلْإِنسِ**

### Deepening

Why does it matter that *both* are named here?

Because the denier's whole worldview was built on what he could *see.* His argument in verse 17 was the argument of a man who trusts only the visible: *I see people die, I do not see them return, therefore there is nothing beyond what I see.* He is a creature of *al-ins* in the most limited sense — bound entirely to the familiar and the visible, mistaking *"I can't see it"* for *"it isn't there."*

And the answer of verse 18 places him in a register that *spans the seen and the unseen* — *mina al-jinni wa al-insi.* As if to say: the law you are subject to is not a law of the visible world only. It governs realms you cannot even perceive. There are *hidden* nations — *al-jinn*, from the very root of concealment — who passed away and faced this same word coming due. The reckoning is not a quirk of human societies that you might philosophize your way out of. It is woven through the *entire* order of created, responsible beings, visible and invisible alike.

[PAUSE]

Here is something remarkable. The man trusted only what was *seen* and used it to deny what was *unseen.* And the verse confronts him with the fact that even the *unseen* — the jinn, the hidden ones — are under the same decree he is laughing off. His epistemology was *"only the visible is real."* The verse's quiet reply is: *there are whole nations you never saw, who learned otherwise.* Your inability to perceive a thing was never a measure of its reality. The hidden nations could tell you that — if you could hear them.

### Conclusion of the element

*Mina al-jinni wa al-insi* tells us the word that came due is not a parochial human matter. It is a law that runs through the hidden and the seen together — and the man who staked everything on "I only believe what I see" finds himself folded into a company that includes beings he could never have seen at all.

### The bridge

So the language has shown us three things. A **word** that came *due* against them — a standing decree falling enforceable. A people who **emptied out** before them — the denier's own evidence turned into his own line in the queue. And a register spanning **the hidden and the seen** — a law no eye can escape by simply refusing to look.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what it means to *lose*, and about the company a life keeps.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Khusrān — The Vocabulary of a Bad Trade

### Begin where you already live

Every one of us understands a bad investment in our gut. You put money into something. You held it. And when you finally looked at the account, the number was *less* than what you started with. Not zero — *less than zero* relative to where you began. You didn't just fail to gain. You consumed your own capital. There is a particular sickness to that feeling, different from never having tried: *I had something, and I traded it away for nothing, and now even the something is gone.*

That feeling has a name in Arabic, and it is the last word of our ayah.

> **إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ خَٰسِرِينَ** — *"Indeed, they were losers."*

**Khāsirīn**, from the root **Kh-S-R** (خسر), is not a vague word for "unsuccessful." It is, at its root, a **commercial** word. *Khasira fī tijāratihi* — "he lost in his trade." *Khusrān* is the merchant's deficit, the capital eaten away, the ledger that closes in the red. Al-Muyassar names exactly this in his commentary: they are losers *"by selling guidance for misguidance, and bliss for punishment."* It is framed as a *transaction.* A trade. The worst trade ever made.

### Let the ayah illuminate it

Here is what reframes everything: the Qur'an insists that **a human life is capital.** Your time, your breath, your faculties, your fortieth year that verse 15 spoke of — these are not just *yours.* They are an *endowment,* handed to you to *invest.* And every life is, whether you acknowledge it or not, a trade. You are exchanging your irreplaceable days for *something.* The only question is what you are getting in return.

The believer in verse 15 understood this. His whole prayer was the prayer of a man who knows he is holding capital that isn't his: *let me be grateful for Your favor… let me do righteousness… make my offspring righteous.* He is asking to invest the endowment well. And verse 16 confirms his return: *We accept from them the best of what they did.* Profit.

The man in verse 17 was handed the identical capital — life, faculties, even *believing parents* calling him toward the best investment available. And he spent it all on a sneer. He traded the endowment for the brief satisfaction of feeling clever in front of his mother and father. *Uff to you both.* That was the purchase. And verse 18 closes his ledger: **khāsirīn.** Bankrupt. Worse than bankrupt — he consumed the capital itself.

[PAUSE]

### Stay with the tense

Notice one more thing — and it is a thing of terrible weight. *Innahum **kānū** khāsirīn* — "indeed they **were** losers." **Kānū** is past tense. *Were.*

Not "they will lose." Not "they are losing." They *were* losers. The verdict is rendered in the perfect, as a *settled fact already true of their entire lives.* From the vantage point of the word having come due, their whole biography is now legible as one long losing trade. Every day they thought they were getting away with something, the ledger was quietly running red. They *were* losers the entire time — they just hadn't seen the statement yet.

That is the chilling intimacy of *kānū.* It says the loss was not a final-day surprise. It was the standing condition of a life that mistook denial for cleverness. The day of reckoning doesn't *create* the loss. It *discloses* it.

### Connect to the universal struggle, and to tomorrow

We are all, every day, making this trade. We are spending non-refundable hours and buying *something* with them. The terror of *khusrān* is not the terror of dramatic sin. It is the terror of the *quiet bad trade* — a life spent buying things that won't be worth anything at the counter where it matters. Status no one will remember. Grudges that cost us our peace. The cheap satisfaction of the clever dismissal, like the man's *"uff,"* purchased at the price of guidance.

So here is what changes tomorrow. Before you spend a day — *and a day is real capital, it does not come back* — ask the merchant's question that *khusrān* forces on you: **what am I buying with this, and will it still be worth something when the word comes due?** Not "is this fun," not "is this clever," but "is this *profit* on an endowment I will have to account for?" The believer of verse 15 asked to be made *grateful* — because gratitude is the recognition that the capital was never yours to waste. Begin there.

## Theme Two: The Company a Life Keeps — *fī umam* and the Two Demonstratives

### Begin where you already live

Think about how we instinctively understand a person by the company they belong to. We say, *"he's one of those,"* and a whole world is summoned. We understand ourselves this way too — by our people, our lineage, the tradition we stand inside. There is deep human comfort in *belonging to a company* — and deep human dread in being sorted into the wrong one. *Which side of the room am I on? Whose company is my name listed in?*

The Qur'an takes this instinct — the human need to know *which company you belong to* — and makes it the very structure of these verses.

### Let the ayah illuminate it

Look at the preposition the denier gets folded into: *…**fī** umamin qad khalat* — "**among** nations that had passed away."

The classical commentators pause on this little word **fī**, "in/among." Al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi both note it carries the sense of **maʿa** — "*with,* in the company of." This person is not punished alone, as an isolated case. He is *entered into a company.* Filed among a people. The word came due against him *fī umam* — in the midst of, in the ranks of, the nations of jinn and men who chose as he chose. He wanted to be the clever individual, the lone skeptic too smart for his parents' faith. And his eternity is the loss of even that — he becomes one more anonymous name in a vast register of the lost. *fī umam.* Among the nations. One of those.

[PAUSE]

### The movement that transforms everything

Now I want you to hold verse 18 in one hand and reach back two lines to verse 16 with the other. Because they are built from the *same blueprint,* and only when you lay them side by side does the full design appear.

Verse 16, on the grateful believer:
> **أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ** نَتَقَبَّلُ **عَنْهُمْ** أَحْسَنَ مَا عَمِلُوا… **فِىٓ** أَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْجَنَّةِ
> "**Those are the ones** from whom We accept the best of what they did… **among** the companions of the Garden."

Verse 18, on the scornful denier:
> **أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ** حَقَّ **عَلَيْهِمُ** ٱلْقَوْلُ… **فِىٓ** أُمَمٍ قَدْ خَلَتْ
> "**Those are the ones** against whom the word came true… **among** nations that had passed away."

Feel the symmetry. Both open with the identical demonstrative: *ulā'ika alladhīna* — **"those are the ones."** Both end by placing the person *fī* — **among** — a company. The Qur'an is sorting two human beings into two registers, using the exact same grammatical frame, so that the *difference* between them rings out with perfect clarity.

And the difference lives, almost entirely, in **one preposition.**

The believer: *nataqabbalu **ʿanhum*** — We accept *ʿan* them, *off* them, *away from* them. The preposition **ʿan** is the preposition of *lifting away, taking off.* Their deeds are *received and lifted off their account*; their sins are *overlooked* — *natajāwazu ʿan sayyi'ātihim,* passed *over* and *away from* them. Everything moves in the direction of *off, away, lifted.* A burden taken off the shoulders.

The denier: *ḥaqqa **ʿalayhimu** al-qawl* — the word came due *ʿalā* them, *upon* them, *down on* them. The preposition **ʿalā** is the preposition of *weight pressing down.* The word descends and settles *on* the shoulders as an enforceable burden.

[PAUSE]

There it is. The entire distance between Paradise and loss, drawn as two directions of a single line. **ʿan** — *off, lifted away.* **ʿalā** — *upon, pressed down.* For one, things are taken *off.* For the other, the word comes *down.* One is unburdened. One is weighted. And notice even the verbs: for the believer, the action is *taqabbul* — Form V, تَقَبَّلَ, an intensive, gracious *receiving,* Allah Himself stooping to *accept* the best of a flawed person's deeds and look past the rest. For the denier, there is no receiving — only a standing decree *coming due.*

Same opening words. Same final structure of "placed among a company." Opposite prepositions, opposite directions, opposite eternities. The architecture itself is the argument.

### A necessary honesty about the occasion

I should pause here for accuracy, because the classical tradition does. Among the narrations attached to verse 17, some early reports tried to identify the scornful child as a specific named person before his Islam. But al-Baghawi explicitly rejects this for the most famous candidate, noting that he became a believer among the finest of Muslims and therefore *could not* be one upon whom "the word came due." And ʿĀ'isha, may Allah be pleased with her, is reported to have firmly objected to pinning the verse on a particular individual. The sounder reading, which al-Baghawi advances, is that verse 18 describes a **human type, not a fixed person** — a door, not a tombstone. That matters enormously for the next point.

### Connect to the universal struggle, and to tomorrow

Because here is the mercy hidden in the structure. *Ulā'ika* — "those" — is a demonstrative, a *pointing.* And as long as you are still breathing, **you have not yet been pointed at.** The two companies are still being filled. The believer's register — *We accept, off them, away from them, lifted* — is still open for entries. The verse describes a *type* precisely so that you can ask, today, *which type am I building myself into?*

So tomorrow, when your parent, or your conscience, or a verse like this one calls you toward the meeting with Allah — notice the *uff* rising in you. The reflexive scoff. The "spare me." That syllable is a small purchase, and it is buying you a place in a company. And notice, equally, the pull toward gratitude — the verse-15 prayer, *let me be thankful, let me do right.* That is a purchase too, in the other register. You are not yet *ulā'ika.* You are still choosing which *ulā'ika* you will become.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architectural Reveal

Now stand back and see the whole design, which we could only assemble after walking through the words.

The Qur'an placed two verdicts a few lines apart and built them from one mold:

| | **The Grateful One (46:16)** | **The Scornful One (46:18)** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | *ulā'ika alladhīna* — "those are the ones" | *ulā'ika alladhīna* — "those are the ones" |
| The action | *nataqabbalu* — We graciously **accept** (Form V) | *ḥaqqa al-qawl* — the word **came due** |
| The direction | **ʿan-hum** — *off* them, lifted away | **ʿalay-him** — *upon* them, pressed down |
| The placement | *fī* aṣḥābi al-jannah — among the **Garden's people** | *fī* umamin qad khalat — among the **perished nations** |

Same frame. Opposite prepositions. Two human beings, sorted before your eyes — and the hinge of the whole thing is the difference between *off* and *upon,* between a burden *lifted* and a word *descending.*

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

**Ḥaqqa** — the word that *came due* — and **khusrān** — the *loss* — are one truth: a life is a contract with a maturity date, and denial is not a payment. **Qad khalat** — the nations that *emptied out* — and **fī umam** — being *folded into their company* — are one truth: the dead you point at as proof of escape are in fact the line you are standing in. And **mina al-jinni wa al-insi** — the hidden and the seen — is the seal on all of it: this is not a law you can dodge by refusing to look, because it runs through realms your eyes were never built to see.

The man trusted the visible, mocked the unseen, and treated death as the end of accountability. The verse answered every premise: there is a word you couldn't see that was always due; there are nations you couldn't see who already faced it; and the emptying of the world you *thought* was your evidence is the very queue you are joining.

## Questions to Carry

1. If a statement of my account were closed today and read aloud as *kānū* — "they *were*" — what would the standing verdict of my life so far disclose?
2. What am I actually *buying* with my days — and will any of it still be worth something at the counter where the word comes due?
3. When faith or conscience calls me, do I feel the *uff* — the reflexive scoff — rising? What small thing is that syllable purchasing for me?
4. The two companies are still open. By the weight of my ordinary choices, which *ulā'ika* am I quietly enrolling in?
5. The believer's blessings were lifted *off* him (*ʿan*); the denier's word came down *upon* him (*ʿalā*). In the direction of my life right now — is weight being lifted, or accumulating?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The man who mocked the dead as proof that nothing comes due is answered by being quietly entered into their very register — because a life is capital traded against a standing decree, and the only question that was ever real is which company your name is being written into.

## The Closing Invitation

You are not *ulā'ika* yet. That is the whole gift of hearing this while you still breathe. The demonstrative has not been pointed at you; the ledger is still open; the register of those from whom Allah *accepts* — *off them, lifted, overlooked* — is still taking names. Let this verse not frighten you into despair but *wake* you into trade — into spending your irreplaceable days on something that will count as profit when the word, for all of us, comes due. Begin where verse 15 began: not with grand deeds, but with the simple, capital-recognizing prayer to be made *grateful.*

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who *accept* the best of what we do and *overlook* the rest — let the word that comes due against us be a word of mercy and not of loss. Make us of those from whom You lift the burden away, not those upon whom it descends; and before our place in this world empties of us, make us grateful traders of the days You lent us, and write our names among the company of Your Garden. *Āmīn.*
