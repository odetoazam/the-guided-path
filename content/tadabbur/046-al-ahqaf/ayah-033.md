# The God Who Does Not Tire: Resurrection and the Inner "Yes"

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single meaning.

> **أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْاْ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ وَلَمْ يَعْىَ بِخَلْقِهِنَّ بِقَـٰدِرٍ عَلَىٰٓ أَن يُحْـِۧىَ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ ۚ بَلَىٰٓ إِنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ**

*Awa lam yaraw anna-llāha-lladhī khalaqa-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa wa lam yaʿya bi-khalqihinna bi-qādirin ʿalā an yuḥyiya-l-mawtā — balā, innahu ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr.*

"Do they not see that Allah — the One who created the heavens and the earth and was not wearied by their creation — is able to give life to the dead? Yes, indeed! He is over all things All-Powerful."

### Rebuild the world

To feel why this verse exists, you have to stand where its first listeners stood.

We are in the last years of Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ has been preaching for over a decade. By now the leaders of Quraysh have heard the message about God, about worship, about justice. And for the most part, they can live with a lot of it. What they cannot swallow — the thing that makes them recoil, the thing they mock openly in the marketplace — is one single claim:

**That the dead will come back.**

Read the surahs of this period and you will hear it again and again. They pick up a crumbling bone, grind it to powder between their fingers, let the dust fall, and laugh: *this* is going to live again? They say it as the final word, the knockout argument. A man named Ubayy ibn Khalaf, the tradition reports, did exactly that in front of the Prophet ﷺ — crushed an old bone and scattered it as if to say, *try gathering that.*

So this is not an abstract theological dispute. This is the central scandal of the message. Everything hangs on it. If there is no return, there is no reckoning. If there is no reckoning, there is no accountability. If there is no accountability, then do what you want with your wealth, your power, your nights — there is no one to answer to.

And Surah Al-Ahqaf has been building toward this confrontation the whole way through. Its very name, *Al-Ahqaf* — the curving sand dunes — points to the ruins of the people of ʿAad, a mighty civilization buried under the very desert they once ruled. The surah has shown you a whole nation that thought itself permanent, lying now under the sand. It has shown you the arrogance of those who said, *"This is nothing but the tales of the ancients."*

And now, near the close, the surah does something different. It stops arguing about the past. It lifts the listener's chin and points him at the sky.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin — and then I want you to hold the question.

When the Quran sets out to prove the resurrection, it does not start with the resurrection. It does not describe graves opening or trumpets sounding. It reaches for something completely unexpected. It tells you about a God **who does not get tired.**

Why would tiredness even come up? No one in the conversation said God was tired. The deniers never argued, "He's too exhausted to bring us back." So why does Allah, in the middle of His proof, pause to deny something nobody accused Him of?

[PAUSE]

Hold that. Because the answer to that question is hiding the whole secret of why we doubt the things we doubt — and the answer is sitting inside one small, slightly awkward-sounding Arabic word.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Should Not Be There — *wa lam yaʿya* (وَلَمْ يَعْىَ)

#### Teaching the idea first

Let me start with something from ordinary life.

Imagine you've just helped a friend move house. Eight hours of stairs and boxes. At the end, someone asks, "Can you carry one more lamp up to the bedroom?" And you say, "Yeah, I can — I'm not even tired."

Notice what just happened in that sentence. You answered a question about *ability* ("Can you?") by talking about *fatigue* ("I'm not tired"). Those are two different things. Ability is whether you have the power. Fatigue is whether the power has been *used up.*

We slide between them constantly, because in our world they are linked. For us, power is a tank that drains. Every act of strength spends something. You can be perfectly *able* in principle and still unable in practice — because you're spent. Because you've run out.

Hold onto that distinction — ability versus depletion — because Allah is about to take it apart.

#### Opening the word

Look at the heart of the verse:

> **وَلَمْ يَعْىَ بِخَلْقِهِنَّ**
> *"...and He was not wearied by their creation."*

The word is **يَعْىَ** — *yaʿya*, from the root *ʿ-y-y* (عيي). And I want you to feel it in your mouth before we define it. There is a constriction in it — that throat-deep *ʿayn*, the halting little catch of the doubled *yāʾ* that the grammarians tell us is usually swallowed into the tongue (*ʿayya, yaʿyā* — al-Qurṭubī notes the doubling and its assimilation). It is, frankly, a slightly labored word. It does not flow. **It is a word that sounds tired.** And it is the very word Allah uses — and negates.

#### Sitting with it

What does *ʿayiya* mean? The classical lexicographers are precise, and the four commentators we lean on are in complete agreement here. Al-Qurṭubī: *"the meaning of* lam yaʿya *is: He did not become incapable or weak before bringing them into being."* Al-Baghawī: *"He did not fall short of originating them."* Al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar both gloss it the same way — *lam yaʿjiz*, "He was not unable."

But here is what's beautiful, and what a flat translation loses. *ʿAyiya* in Arabic is not just generic "inability." Al-Qurṭubī gives us the texture: *"ʿayiya bi-amrihi"* — a person is *ʿāyin* in a matter when **he cannot find the way to do it.** It is the inability of a man who is overwhelmed, who is at a loss, who has hit the edge of his competence and doesn't know where to turn next. It carries exhaustion *and* bewilderment. It is the feeling of a project too big for you — the feeling of being out of your depth and out of energy at the same time.

That is the word Allah picks up and sets to zero.

[PAUSE]

Now go back to the question from the introduction. Why bring up fatigue at all? Nobody accused God of being tired.

Here is what I want you to see. **Allah is not answering what they said. He is answering what they secretly believe.**

When a human being looks at the heavens and the earth — the scale of it, the sheer staggering output of creation — and then is told "and after all of that, He will gather every scattered atom of every body that ever lived and bring them back," something in us flinches. Not because we've reasoned it out. Because we *project*. We have never produced anything without being depleted by it. We have never built without tiring. And so, beneath the level of argument, we assume the universe must work the way we work: that such an enormous first act of creation must have *cost* something, must have *spent* something, must have left even God — if we're honest about the silent picture in our heads — somehow *drawn down.* And a drawn-down power, surely, would struggle to do it all again.

The verse reaches *underneath* the spoken objection and pulls out the unspoken one. The deniers think their problem is logical — *how can dust become a person?* Allah reveals that their real problem is a smuggled assumption: **they have made God in the image of their own exhaustion.**

And in three words — *wa lam yaʿya* — He severs it. The first creation cost Him nothing. There is no tank. There is no drain. There is no "again" that is harder than the "first," because the "first" took nothing out of Him to begin with.

This is the principle the whole Quran works by: *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bi-l-maḥsūs* — pointing to an unseen reality through something you can feel. The physical image is fatigue, sweat, the slump at the end of a long day. The unseen reality it points to is the **inexhaustibility of divine power** — a truth you cannot picture directly, so Allah hands it to you as the negation of the one thing you know best: your own tiredness.

Compare it with its sister verse, Sūrah Qāf:

> *"And We created the heavens and the earth and what is between them in six days, and no fatigue (lughūb) touched Us."* (50:38)

There it is again. Same surgical move. The cosmos described, and then — almost as an aside — *no tiredness touched Us.* As though the Quran knows that the moment you hear about the size of creation, your mind will silently add the exhaustion, and so it gets there first to remove it.

**What this one word reveals:** Your doubt about being brought back is not really a doubt about God's power. It is a doubt you built by quietly assuming God is as depletable as you are. Remove that one smuggled assumption — *He was not wearied* — and the doubt has nothing left to stand on.

### Element Two: The Letter That Carries a Hidden Question — the *bāʾ* in *bi-qādir* (بِقَادِرٍ)

#### Teaching the idea first

In every language, there is a difference between a *statement* and a *question that already knows its answer.*

If I say, "She is capable of finishing this," that's a flat report. But if I say, "Isn't she capable of finishing this?" — I haven't really asked you anything. I've made a claim and dared you to disagree. The question form is *more* forceful than the statement, not less. It reaches across the table, grabs your collar gently, and says: *you already know this is true; say it with me.*

Arabic has a beautiful, almost invisible way of doing this. And it is happening right here, in a single letter that, strictly speaking, should not be in the sentence.

#### Opening the feature

Look at the predicate of the verse:

> **...بِقَـٰدِرٍ عَلَىٰٓ أَن يُحْـِۧىَ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ**
> *"...[is] able to give life to the dead."*

The word "able" is **قَادِر** — *qādir*. But it does not come bare. It comes with a *bāʾ* attached to its front: **بِقَادِر** — *bi-qādir.*

Now, grammatically, this is the *khabar* — the predicate — of *anna* ("that Allah... is able"). And a predicate like this does not normally take a *bāʾ*. The clean sentence would simply be *anna-llāha... qādirun.* So what is this extra letter doing here?

#### Sitting with it

This is one of those places where the classical grammarians left us a rich conversation, and rather than make you pick one answer, let me show you how every answer is a different camera angle on the *same* truth.

Al-Jalālayn gives the core: *"qādir* is the predicate of *anna*, and the *bāʾ* was added to it **because the speech carries the force of: 'Is not Allah able?'"** — *fī quwwati: a-laysa-llāhu bi-qādir.*

Al-Baghawī widens the lens and reports the *ikhtilāf*, the scholarly difference, openly:
- **Abū ʿUbayda and al-Akhfash** said: the *bāʾ* here is *zāʾida lil-taʾkīd* — an "extra" letter added purely for **emphasis**, the way it appears in other verses for sheer intensity.
- **Al-Kisāʾī and al-Farrāʾ** said something subtler: *"The Arabs insert the bāʾ in interrogation when there is negation,"* as in the everyday phrase *mā aẓunnuka bi-qāʾim* — "I don't think you're standing." On this reading, the *bāʾ* is the fingerprint of a **negative rhetorical question** pressed down into the sentence.

(Al-Baghawī even preserves that Yaʿqūb read it as a verb, *yaqdiru*, "He is able" — but the common reading, the one Abū ʿUbayda preferred, is *bi-qādir.*)

Now watch how these two explanations — emphasis, and hidden negative question — are not rivals. They converge. Whether you say the *bāʾ* is there for raw emphasis, or there because the sentence is secretly shaped like *"Is not Allah able?"*, the *effect on your heart is identical.* The flat statement "Allah is able to revive the dead" has been quietly transformed into something that leans in toward you: ***Is He not able?***

And that changes everything about how the verse *feels*. A statement informs you. This **summons** you. It is not delivering news to a neutral observer; it is confronting someone who is resisting, and pressing him to admit what he already half-knows.

[PAUSE]

#### The grammar confirms itself

Here is the part I find genuinely breathtaking — the place where the structure proves its own meaning.

If the sentence really is carrying a hidden negative question — *"Is He not able?"* — then it has set up a negation. And in Arabic, when someone affirms *against* a negation, you cannot use the ordinary word for "yes." You cannot say *naʿam.* You must use a special particle reserved exactly for this: **بَلَىٰ** — *balā* — the "yes" that overturns a "no."

So look at what comes immediately next in the verse:

> **...بِقَـٰدِرٍ عَلَىٰٓ أَن يُحْـِۧىَ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ ۚ بَلَىٰٓ**
> *"...able to give life to the dead? — **Balā!**"*

There it is. The verse answers itself with *balā* — the precise particle Arabic uses **only** to affirm against a denial. And that is the proof. If the sentence were a plain positive statement, the answer would have been *naʿam.* The presence of *balā* tells you, with the certainty of grammar, that there was a hidden "no" being overturned — which means the *bāʾ* really was carrying that negative question all along. **The end of the verse confirms the beginning. The structure testifies to its own meaning.**

This is what I mean when I say the Quran's construction is the proof. You don't have to take the grammarians' word for the hidden question. The little word *balā* sitting four words later is the receipt.

**What this feature reveals:** The verse is not informing you that God can resurrect. It is reaching across the centuries, taking hold of the part of you that quietly doubts, and pressing you — *Is He not able? Say it.* — and then answering on your behalf, with the one word that means "yes, in spite of every 'no' you were about to offer."

### Element Three: From *qādir* to *qadīr* — The Climb from One Act to All Things

#### Opening the two words

There is one more thing the verse does, and it is so quiet you can read it a hundred times and miss it. The verse opens its claim about power with one word and closes it with a *different* word — and they share a root, but they are not the same.

The first is **بِقَـٰدِر** — *qādir.*
The last is **قَدِيرٌ** — *qadīr.*

Both from the root *q-d-r* (قدر), which carries the meaning of *power, measure, capacity, determination.* But the *forms* are different, and in Arabic, form is meaning.

#### Sitting with it

*Qādir* is an *active participle* — *ism al-fāʿil.* It names someone *doing* a specific thing: "the one who is able [to do this]." Its grammar even points it at a single object — *qādirun ʿalā an yuḥyiya-l-mawtā*, "able **to give life to the dead.**" It is power aimed at one task. Power in a particular instance.

*Qadīr* is a different form entirely — the *intensive* pattern *faʿīl* — and it is one of the Beautiful Names of Allah. It does not name one act. It names a *settled, total, unceasing attribute.* And look at what it is paired with: not "the dead," but **كُلِّ شَىْءٍ** — *kulli shay'in*, "**every** single thing." Power over all things, without exception, without end.

Now feel the *motion* between them.

The verse begins with a modest, almost concessive claim, pitched exactly to the deniers' level: *all right — is He at least able to do this one thing, revive the dead?* It meets the skeptic where he is standing, on the ground of a single disputed act. And then, having won that point, it does not stay there. It *lifts.* It rises from *"able to do this one thing"* (*qādir ʿalā an yuḥyiya-l-mawtā*) to *"over every thing, All-Powerful"* (*ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*).

[PAUSE]

Do you see the staircase? The verse argues you up from the specific to the absolute. It says, in effect: you were stuck on this one case — can the dead live again? But the moment you grant it, you have granted infinitely more than you meant to. Because the same Power that can do *this* is, by its very nature, Power over **everything.** There is no such thing as a God who can do the one hard thing but is otherwise limited. Power of that kind is not partial. Once you have admitted *qādir*, you have already admitted *qadīr.*

Your tiny disputed question — *will I really be brought back?* — turns out to be a doorway into the largest truth there is.

**What this movement reveals:** The verse refuses to leave you arguing about a single case. It takes the one thing you were willing to doubt and uses it to walk you up into the one truth that, once seen, ends all doubting — that He is over *every* thing, *All-Powerful.*

### Bridge

So look at what these three small features have shown us. A word for tiredness, set to zero — *He was not wearied.* A single hidden letter that turns a statement into a summons — *Is He not able?* And a quiet climb from one act to all things — *qādir* rising into *qadīr.*

Three pieces of grammar. But notice: each one is doing the *same* work from a different angle. The first removes the false picture of a depletable God. The second confronts the heart that's resisting. The third lifts that heart from a small doubt to an infinite certainty.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about the way the human heart actually doubts, and the way it actually comes to believe.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why We Really Doubt the Resurrection (and It Isn't Logic)

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a feeling almost everyone over thirty knows. You look at something you accomplished years ago — a thing you built, a season when you ran hard, a version of yourself that did the impossible — and a thought slides in: *I could never do that now.* Not because the task changed. Because *you* feel diminished. You're more tired now. You have less in the tank. The energy that once seemed bottomless, you have learned, has a floor.

This is the deepest law of being a creature: **everything we are runs down.** Our bodies wear. Our attention frays. Our enthusiasm burns off. We have a word for it now — *burnout* — and it has become almost the defining condition of modern life. Even the universe, science tells us, is winding toward heat-death, every system drifting from order to disorder, entropy always collecting its tax. We live inside a reality where *to act is to spend, and to spend is to have less.*

And here is the thing we never notice: **we carry that law in our chests when we think about God.**

[PAUSE]

When a person hears "you will be brought back to life," and something in them quietly refuses, examine that refusal honestly. It almost never comes from a worked-out argument. It comes from a *feeling* — the feeling that it's just too much. Too big. Too costly. *All those people, all that dust, gathered and remade — that's an enormous amount of... effort.* And the moment the word "effort" appears in your mind, you have already lost, because you have imported your own exhaustion into the equation. You have, without meaning to, imagined a God who could get *tired.*

This is exactly what the verse exposes. The deniers thought their objection was about *possibility* — how can scattered bones reassemble? Allah goes deeper than their stated objection and names the real one: *wa lam yaʿya* — He was not wearied. As if to say: *your problem was never the dust. Your problem is that you secretly think the first creation took something out of Me, and the second would take even more. But the first creation cost Me nothing. There is no "more." There is no effort to run out of.*

Let the paradox sit, because it is enormous: for us, the second time is always harder — the encore, the comeback, the rebuild, all of it strains against a diminishing self. The Quran's own logic, stated elsewhere, simply detonates this: *"It is He who originates creation, then repeats it, and that is **easier** for Him"* (30:27). Easier. Not because repetition is genuinely easier for a Power that does not tire — for such a Power "easy" and "hard" don't even apply — but because Allah is speaking to the part of *you* that ranks tasks by effort, and meeting you there, He says: even by your own scale, the thing you find unimaginable is the lighter of the two.

[PAUSE]

So here is what this asks of you tomorrow.

The next time you feel that flinch — about the resurrection, about an answered prayer that seems too large, about Allah fixing a situation in your life that feels far past repair — **catch the assumption underneath it.** You are almost certainly measuring God's power by the only model of power you have ever known: your own, the kind that runs down. Name it when it happens. Say to yourself: *I am imagining a tired God. There is no such thing.* The God who flung the heavens into place and felt nothing drain from Him — your impossible situation does not register on Him as effort at all. *Wa lam yaʿya.* He was not wearied by the universe. He will not be wearied by you.

### Theme Two: The "Yes" That Was Always Inside You

Now I want to take you to the strangest, most tender thing in this verse — that one word, *balā.*

Begin again with something human.

There is a particular kind of "yes" that is not new information — it is a *remembering.* Think of being shown, beyond doubt, something you had actually always known but had talked yourself out of. A friend says, "You know she was right about you, don't you?" — and that "yes" that rises in you is not a fresh conclusion. It's a surrender. It's the part of you that knew all along finally being allowed to speak. The resistance collapses, and what's left underneath is recognition.

That is the texture of *balā.*

Recall what we found in the grammar: *balā* is not the ordinary "yes." It is the "yes" that overturns a "no." It only exists in answer to a negation. Which means that for *balā* to be the right word here, there *must* have been a "no" being overturned — and the verse is overturning the silent, stubborn "no" living in the deniers' chests. *Is He not able? — Balā.* Yes, against everything you were about to say. Yes, in spite of yourself.

[PAUSE]

But now I want to show you something that I think is one of the quiet wonders of the Quran. This is not the only place this exact word, *balā*, appears as humanity's answer. Listen to where else it lives:

> *"And [remember] when your Lord brought forth from the children of Adam, from their loins, their descendants, and made them testify over themselves: **'Am I not your Lord?'** They said, **'Yes (balā)** — we bear witness.'"* (7:172)

This is the primordial covenant — the *mīthāq* — the moment, before this world, when every soul that would ever exist was gathered and asked one question, *alastu bi-rabbikum*, "Am I not your Lord?" And notice the *shape* of that question. It is a negative rhetorical question. *Am I **not**...?* The very same construction as our verse — *is He **not** able?* And the answer the souls gave was not *naʿam.* It was **balā.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The first word the human soul ever spoke — the answer it gave at the very dawn of its existence, affirming its Lord against the implied "no" — was *balā.* And here, in Sūrah Al-Ahqaf, when Allah confronts the human being about the *end* — about being brought back, about resurrection — the word the verse places in our mouth is, again, **balā.**

The same "yes" that began us is the "yes" that affirms our return. The word of the covenant of souls is the word of the resurrection. As though the Quran is telling you: *the "yes" you are being asked for now is not a new one. You said it before. It is written into the floor of your soul. The denier is not refusing to learn something — he is refusing to remember something. And resurrection is simply God keeping the appointment that the soul itself once agreed to.*

This is why the verse can answer *on your behalf.* It is so confident the "yes" is already in you that it doesn't wait for you to say it. It says it for you — *balā* — the way you finish the sentence of someone you have known your whole life.

[PAUSE]

And there is a near-identical scene worth seeing beside it, in Sūrah Yā Sīn — the man crushing the bone, made into a verse:

> *"...Who will give life to bones while they are decayed?"* Say, He will give them life who produced them the first time... *"Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them? **Yes (balā)** — and He is the Knowing Creator."* (36:78–81)

Again — the heavens and the earth as the proof, the hidden negative question, and the *balā.* The same architecture. As if this single answer, *balā*, is the Quran's settled response to the deepest "no" the human heart is capable of: the "no" to ever facing God again.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It reframes what doubt even *is.* When you wrestle with belief in the Hereafter — and many sincere people do — stop treating it as a foreign idea you are trying to import into a resistant mind. Treat it as something *buried* that you are trying to *uncover.* The faith is not outside you, knocking to be let in. It is underneath, waiting to be remembered. Your work is not to manufacture a "yes." It is to clear away the *naʿam*-world's noise — the projections, the smuggled exhaustion, the laughter of the marketplace — until the older, deeper *balā* that your soul already spoke can be heard again. **Belief, here, is less a conclusion than a homecoming.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we've done the work, step back and see how the verse is built — because the shape is the argument.

It opens with a challenge — *Do they not see?* — and immediately plants its foundation in the one thing no one in the conversation could deny: *the One who created the heavens and the earth.* Then, before moving on, it does its quiet surgery on your hidden assumption — *and was not wearied by their creation* — pulling the floor out from under your doubt. Only *then*, standing on cleared ground, does it press the real question, carried in that single hidden letter: *Is He not able to give life to the dead?* And then it answers itself — *balā* — with the word of the ancient covenant. And finally it *climbs*: from this one act to *every* thing, from *qādir* to *qadīr*, leaving you not with a settled dispute but with an infinite horizon.

Creation → no fatigue → the hidden question → the ancient yes → all things. Each step makes the next one inevitable. By the time you reach *qadīr*, you cannot get back off the staircase.

### Bringing it together

Watch how the linguistic discoveries and the human themes turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The grammar said: *wa lam yaʿya* removes the false picture of a depletable God. The heart said: that false picture is exactly the *secret reason we doubt resurrection* — we measure Him by our own exhaustion.

The grammar said: the hidden *bāʾ* makes this a summons — *Is He not able?* — answered by *balā*, the "yes" against a "no." The heart said: that *balā* is the **same word our souls spoke at the covenant** — so the "yes" being asked of us was never new; it's a remembering.

The grammar said: *qādir* climbs into *qadīr.* The heart said: your small private doubt, once surrendered, opens onto the largest certainty there is.

Two doors. One room. The verse takes you in through whichever one you happen to be standing at — the mind's door or the heart's door — and they let you into the same place.

### Questions to carry

1. When you feel resistance to something Allah promises — resurrection, or an answered prayer, or a repair to your life that feels impossible — is your real objection about *His* power, or about a tired God you've quietly built in His place?

2. Where in your life are you treating the "second time" as harder than the first — a comeback, a rebuild, a return to Him after a long absence — as if *you* must supply the energy God supposedly lacks?

3. If the "yes" to your Lord is already written into the floor of your soul, what is the noise you would need to clear away to hear it again?

4. The deniers laughed at a crushed bone. What is the modern equivalent — the thing you "know" is impossible — that you have never actually held up against *ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*?

### One-sentence distillation

The God who built the heavens and felt nothing drain from Him is not too tired to bring you back — and the "yes" to that truth is not a new belief you must construct, but the oldest word your soul ever spoke.

### The closing invitation

Let this verse retire one small, exhausting idea you may have carried your whole life without naming it: the idea that God's power is a finite supply that your problems might somehow strain. Walk out from under that. The next time something feels too far gone for repair — a relationship, a habit, a heart grown cold, your own scattered self — meet it with the verse's own logic: *He was not wearied by the universe.* Whatever you are facing does not register on Him as effort. And the part of you that already knows this — let it answer the way it answered at the very beginning. *Balā.* Yes. In spite of everything. Yes.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You who created the heavens and the earth and were not wearied by their making — we confess the tired god we sometimes imagine in Your place, and we let him go. Uncover in us the *balā* our souls spoke before we were born; let it rise again over every doubt and every "no" we have carried. And You who are *ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*, over every single thing All-Powerful — revive in us what has died, the way You will revive the dead, easily, and without effort, and without end. Āmīn.
