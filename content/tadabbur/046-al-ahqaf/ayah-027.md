# Surrounded by Ruins: The Signs Turned Toward You

### Surah Al-Ahqaf 46:27

> وَلَقَدْ أَهْلَكْنَا مَا حَوْلَكُم مِّنَ ٱلْقُرَىٰ وَصَرَّفْنَا ٱلْـَٔايَـٰتِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَرْجِعُونَ
>
> *Wa-laqad ahlaknā mā ḥawlakum mina-l-qurā wa-ṣarrafnā-l-āyāti laʿallahum yarjiʿūn.*
>
> **"And certainly We destroyed the towns that surround you, and We turned the signs about — presenting them from every angle — so that they might return."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Land That Remembers

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand somewhere.

Stand on a trade road in seventh-century Arabia. You are a merchant from Mecca, and twice a year your caravan makes the long journey north toward Syria, and another route south toward Yemen. You have made this journey since you were a boy walking beside your father's camel. And on that road — you pass things.

You pass them so often you have stopped seeing them.

You pass the broken country of al-Ḥijr, the carved-stone dwellings of Thamūd, hewn into the cliffs by a people who once thought themselves untouchable, now silent, wind-scoured, empty. You pass the dead region near the Dead Sea where the people of Lūṭ once lived, the ground itself overturned. And to the south, in the curving dunes — *al-aḥqāf*, the very word that names this surah — lie buried the people of ʿĀd, who built monuments on every high place and asked, *"Who is mightier than us in strength?"* The sand answered them. The sand is still answering.

[PAUSE]

These are not legends to the man on that road. These are landmarks. They are on his *map*. He navigates by them. The geography of God's judgment is the geography of his commute.

And here is the strange thing — the thing this ayah is about. **He has stopped seeing it.** The ruins have become scenery. The graveyard of nations has become the wallpaper of his daily life. He passes the bones of certainty and feels nothing, because familiarity has a way of turning a warning into a landmark, and a landmark into nothing at all.

Now hold that picture, because this surah has been building toward this moment. Just before our ayah, Allah described ʿĀd — a people He had *established in ways He has not established you* — given hearing, given sight, given hearts and minds far sharper than the Meccans who now mock the Prophet ﷺ. And it *availed them nothing*. The wind came. They were gone before morning.

And then — verse 27. Allah does something that should make the hair on your arms stand up. He doesn't point to history. He points to *the ground beneath your feet*. He says: look around you. Not in books. **Around you.** The destroyed towns are not a story. They are your *neighbors*. They are the silent houses at the edge of your map.

Here is what we are going to sit with tonight. In just ten words, Allah does two things that, when you finally see how they fit together, will change the way you read every warning you have ever ignored. First, He *encircles* the listener with ruins. And then He uses a single, breathtaking verb to describe how He has been pleading with humanity — a verb that comes from the world of jewelers and money-changers and the turning of the wind. And buried in the grammar is a shift — from *you* to *they* — that quietly puts you in the seat of the condemned, watching the replay of your own trial.

Let's go slowly. There is more here than ten words should be able to hold.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Mā Ḥawlakum* — "What Surrounds You"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In English, when we want to locate something, we tend to name it. "I destroyed the cities of Thamūd and Lūṭ and ʿĀd." We list. We specify. Naming feels precise, and we equate precision with power.

Arabic can do something English struggles to do gracefully: it can locate by *relationship* rather than by *name*. The word here is **حَوْلَكُم** — *ḥawlakum*. The root is **Ḥ-W-L**, and it carries the sense of *turning around, encircling, that which is in a ring about a thing*. From this same root comes *ḥawl*, a year — because a year is time that comes *full circle* back to where it began. *Ḥawlakum* does not mean "near you." It means **all around you. On every side. Encircling you.**

Now notice what Allah did *not* do. He did not say, "We destroyed Thamūd and ʿĀd and the people of Lūṭ." The classical commentators — al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Jalālayn — all tell us *which* towns are meant: al-Ḥijr of Thamūd, the overturned land of Lūṭ, the dunes of ʿĀd. The early listeners knew. *Wa-kānat akhbāruhum mutawātiratan ʿindahum*, says al-Qurṭubī — "their stories were passed down among them in unbroken report." So the identities were never in doubt.

Then why does the ayah leave them unnamed and say only **"what surrounds you of the towns"**?

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because the absence of the names is not vagueness. It is *technique*.

When you name a thing, you put it in a box. "Thamūd" becomes a historical entity — *over there*, in the past, belonging to someone else. A name creates distance the moment you speak it. But "what surrounds you" — that cannot be put in a box, because the box would have to be drawn around *you*. The grammar refuses to let the listener step outside the circle. You are not reading about ruins. You are *standing in the middle of them*.

Notice something else. The word مَا — *mā*, "what" — is even more impersonal than naming a people. It does not say "those *whom* We destroyed." It says "*what* We destroyed." The towns are reduced to *objects*. To rubble. To *whatness*. The people who once filled them — who had names, lineages, poetry, pride — are now just *mā*, "stuff that used to be there." That is what destruction does. It turns a *who* into a *what*.

And here is the turn that should land in your chest. The living Meccan, hearing this, is being asked a silent question. *They were a "who" once, too. They had a map, and on the edges of their map were the ruins of someone else. And now they are the ruins on the edge of yours. So tell me — on whose map are you a landmark not yet drawn?*

[PAUSE]

That is the first word. *Ḥawlakum.* You are encircled. Hold it. We will come back to that circle, because Allah is about to draw a second one.

---

### Element Two: *Ṣarrafnā* — The Verb of the Jeweler and the Wind

Now we come to the heart of the ayah. One word. And to feel it, I need to first put three ordinary images in your hands.

**First image.** A jeweler holds a cut diamond up to the lamp. He does not show it to you flat, from one face. He *turns* it — slowly, between thumb and finger — so that one facet after another catches the light. Same stone. Same fire inside it. But he keeps *turning* it so that every angle has its moment to flash.

**Second image.** A money-changer in the old market — in Arabic, a *ṣarrāf*. His whole trade is *ṣarf*: taking a coin of one kind and *turning* it into another. Gold into silver. Foreign into local. The same value, made available in every currency the traveler might need.

**Third image.** The wind. The Quran elsewhere speaks of *taṣrīf al-riyāḥ* — the *turning about* of the winds (2:164), the way Allah sends them now from the north, now from the south, now warm, now cold, now bearing rain, now bearing dust. The same air, *turned* into a thousand different errands.

Hold those three. The jeweler turning the gem. The money-changer turning the coin. The hand of God turning the wind. Because they all come from one root: **Ṣ-R-F**, *to turn, to divert, to change the direction and the face of a thing*.

Now look at the ayah:

> **وَصَرَّفْنَا ٱلْـَٔايَـٰتِ** — *wa-ṣarrafnā-l-āyāt* — "and We turned the signs about."

Here is the grammar that matters, and I want to be precise, because this is the load-bearing beam of the whole reflection. The verb is not the simple Form I, *ṣarafnā*. It is **Form II — *ṣarrafnā*** — with the doubled middle letter, the *shaddah*. In Arabic, this doubling — *taḍʿīf* — characteristically adds *intensity, repetition, and distribution*. Form I would be *to turn a thing once*. Form II — *ṣarrafa* — is *to turn it again and again, this way and that, over and over, into every available form.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

Allah did not say *"We sent the signs."* He did not say *"We gave the signs."* He said: **We *turned the signs about* — we presented them from every facet, in every currency, on every wind.** Like the jeweler who will not let the diamond rest on one face. Like the money-changer who will convert the truth into whatever coin your hand can hold. The signs of God came to humanity not once, not flatly, not in a single key — but *turned, and turned, and turned again*, so that no human being could ever say, *"It was shown to me in a way I could not grasp."*

Al-Qurṭubī opens the meaning along exactly these lines. *Ṣarrafnā al-āyāt*, he says, means *bayyannāhā* — "We made them clear" — *the proofs, the evidences, the varieties of clear signs and admonitions.* And he preserves a second facet of the same gem: that it includes the *turning about of the signs of the Qurʾān* — *in promise and in threat, in story and in inimitable speech.* Notice he does not make us choose. The signs of the wrecked towns *and* the signs of the recited verses are both being *turned* toward the listener. The ruined geography is one facet. The recited Qurʾān is another facet. **Same light. Different angle.**

This is the mercy that hides inside repetition. We tend to hear repetition as impatience — a parent raising their voice, saying the same thing louder. But *taṣrīf* is the opposite of louder. It is *more patient*. It is the teacher who, when the child does not understand the first explanation, does not repeat it word-for-word more forcefully — but **reframes it.** Draws a different picture. Finds another door into the same room. *You didn't see it as a diamond's face? Here, let me turn it. You couldn't hold it as gold? Here, let me change it into silver.*

[PAUSE]

So when you stand on that road and pass the ruins and feel nothing — understand what Allah is telling you. *I did not show you the warning once and move on. I have been turning it in front of you your whole life. The ruins on your map are one turn of the stone. The verses in your ears are another. The wind that took ʿĀd is another. I have been, this entire time, a jeweler at the lamp, turning and turning, waiting for one facet to finally catch your eye.*

That is *ṣarrafnā*. Sit with how gentle it is. The verb of the destroyer of towns is, at the same moment, the verb of the most patient teacher who ever lived.

### Element Three: The Quiet Shift from *You* to *They*

Now I want to show you something that lives entirely in the pronouns — and it is so easy to walk past that most translations smooth it over.

Read the ayah's pronouns in order:

- "the towns that surround **you**" — *ḥawla**kum*** — second person. *You*, the living Meccans, directly addressed.
- "so that **they** might return" — *laʿalla**hum*** — third person. *They.*

[PAUSE]

Did you catch the seam? Halfway through ten words, the *you* becomes a *they*. Allah is speaking *to* you — and then suddenly speaking *about* someone in the third person, as if you have left the room.

In Arabic rhetoric, this kind of deliberate shift of person is a recognized and powerful device. And here the scholars give us more than one reading of *who* "they" are — and, in the spirit of this reflection, each reading is a camera angle on the same truth, not a competitor to be eliminated.

**The first reading** — carried by al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, and al-Baghawī — is that "they" are *the people of the destroyed towns themselves*. The sense becomes: *We turned the signs about for those vanished peoples — so that they might return from their disbelief — but they did not, so destruction came.* On this reading, the verse is a closed history. The signs were turned. The chance was given. The return was refused. The ruins are the verdict.

**The second reading**, which al-Qurṭubī preserves alongside it, takes *ṣarrafnā al-āyāt* as the *turning about of the Qurʾān's signs* toward *these* present listeners — the Meccans — *that they* (the Meccans, now spoken of in the third person) *might return.*

Now watch what happens when you hold both readings at once — because the ayah is built to let you.

[PAUSE]

Under the first reading, "they" are the *dead*. Under the second, "they" are *you*. And the grammar — by sliding from *you* to *they* in a single breath — **lets the two groups bleed into each other.** The living listener, addressed as *you* in the first half, finds himself referred to as *they* in the second half — the very same grammatical position occupied by the vanished peoples. The pronoun shift does what no argument could: it *quietly seats you in the chair of the condemned*, and lets you watch the signs being turned toward "them" — and then you realize, with a chill, that *them* is a mirror.

This is the genius of leaving it ambiguous. A named, settled "they" would let you stay a spectator. The drifting "they" pulls you out of the audience and onto the stage. **The warning given to the dead is being re-run, and you are no longer sure whether you are watching it or starring in it.**

And both readings converge on one word, which is where we are headed next: *yarjiʿūn* — *that they might **return***. To which we now turn.

---

### Bridge

So here is what the language has shown us. *Ḥawlakum* drew a ring of ruins around the listener and refused to let him name his way to safety. *Ṣarrafnā* revealed that the warnings encircling him were not flat or single, but *turned* before his eyes a thousand patient times, like a gem at a lamp. And the slip from *you* to *they* quietly moved him from the seat of the watcher into the seat of the watched.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about *reality itself*. About why we can be surrounded by the clearest signs in the world and still feel nothing. And about why God speaks, even to the doomed, in the language of *hope*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How We Stop Seeing What Surrounds Us

Let me start nowhere near the Qurʾān. Let me start in your own house.

There is a smell in your home right now that you cannot detect. Every home has one — a particular scent, neither good nor bad, just *yours*. Visitors notice it the moment they walk in. You haven't smelled it in years. Not because it's gone. Because your brain, after enough exposure, simply *stopped reporting it*. Psychologists call this *habituation*, or sensory adaptation. The nervous system, flooded with a constant signal, makes an executive decision: *this is not news anymore.* And it cuts the feed.

This is one of the most useful things your brain does. If you felt the shirt on your skin every second, you'd go mad. Habituation lets you ignore the constant so you can notice the *new*.

But here is the terrible cost. **The mechanism cannot tell the difference between a constant that is harmless and a constant that is killing you.** The man who lives next to the train tracks stops hearing the train. The man who lives next to a slow gas leak stops smelling it too. The very faculty that protects you from the shirt on your back will, with equal efficiency, blind you to the thing that surrounds you and means to take your life.

[PAUSE]

Now go back to the road. The Meccan merchant passing the ruins of Thamūd.

The first time he saw those carved cliffs as a boy, I promise you he *felt* something. A chill. A question. *Who lived here? What happened to them?* But the tenth time? The hundredth? The signal became a constant, and the brain did what brains do. It cut the feed. The graveyard of a nation became a *milestone* — "we're halfway to Syria." The verdict of God became a *landmark*. He habituated to the warning the way you've habituated to the smell of your own home.

And this is *exactly* the disease the ayah is diagnosing. Allah says the towns are *ḥawlakum* — surrounding you, on every side, all the time, constantly — and that is *precisely* why they've gone invisible. **What surrounds us always, we cease to see.** The omnipresence of the sign is the very reason for our blindness to it.

Now let the ayah turn the diamond, because Allah anticipated the disease and engineered the cure into the second word. *Ṣarrafnā.* He *varies* the sign. He turns it. Why? Because habituation feeds on *sameness*. Your brain stops reporting the *constant* signal — so the only way to break through is to make the signal *new*. To turn the gem so a different facet flashes. The teacher who reframes is not being repetitive; she is *defeating habituation*. Allah does not send the warning in the same key over and over precisely *because* He knows the same key would go numb. He sends it as ruins, then as recited verse, then as wind, then as the death of someone you love, then as a fear that wakes you at 3 a.m. *Taṣrīf* is the divine answer to the human tendency to go numb.

[PAUSE]

So bring it home, to *your* road, today.

What surrounds *you* that you have stopped seeing? You drive past the cemetery on your commute and feel nothing — but every stone there belonged to someone who also thought death was a thing that happened to other people. You scroll past the news of a death and your thumb does not even slow down. You have prayed *Maghrib* ten thousand times and the sun has set ten thousand times in colors that would have stopped you dead if you'd seen them once — and you have habituated to the daily collapse of the sky. The signs did not get quieter. *You* got quieter.

Here is the practice, and it is small enough to do tomorrow. **Once — just once tomorrow — refuse to habituate.** Pick one thing you pass every day without seeing: the graveyard, the sunset, the face of a parent who is older than they were last year, the verse you've recited a thousand times. And look at it as if it were the *first* time, or the *last*. Let it be *news* again. That deliberate de-habituation — choosing to see what surrounds you — is the human half of *taṣrīf*. Allah keeps turning the gem. The least we can do is open our eyes when a new facet flashes.

### Theme Two: Why God Pleads in the Language of "Perhaps"

Now I want to take you to the most tender word in the ayah, and it is one we almost skipped: **لَعَلَّ** — *laʿalla*. "So that they might…" "In the hope that…" "Perhaps."

Let me set the human scene first.

Think of a parent standing at a door at midnight, waiting for a teenager who has been making ruinous choices. The parent has said everything. Explained it ten ways. Pleaded, reasoned, drawn the picture of where this road ends. And the parent — who can see the cliff the child cannot — says, softly, to no one: *"Maybe this time. Maybe it gets through. Maybe they'll turn around."*

That word — *maybe* — is one of the most loving words a human being can say. Because *maybe* means *I have not given up on you*. *Maybe* means *I am still leaving the door open*. *Maybe* means *your choice still matters to me, even after everything.*

Now hold that, and look at Who is speaking in our ayah.

Allah — who knows the future as He knows the past, for whom there is no "perhaps," no suspense, no waiting at any door — Allah describes His own turning of the signs with the word *laʿalla*. **"…that they might return."** And here the commentators are blunt about the outcome. Al-Baghawī finishes the sentence for us: *laʿallahum yarjiʿūn — ʿan kufrihim, fa-lam yarjiʿū, fa-ahlaknāhum* — "that they might return from their disbelief; but they did not return, so We destroyed them." Al-Qurṭubī says it even more starkly: *laʿallahum yarjiʿūn — fa-lam yarjiʿū.* "That they might return — and they did not."

[PAUSE]

Sit with the weight of that.

Allah, who *knew with perfect certainty* that they would not return — *still framed His signs in the language of "perhaps they will."* He did not say, "We turned the signs about, knowing they would refuse." He said, "We turned the signs about — *that they might return*." He clothed His foreknowledge in the garment of hope.

Why?

Because *laʿalla* is not a statement about what Allah *knows*. **It is a statement about how Allah *deals with us*.** The "perhaps" does not describe a gap in God's knowledge. It describes the *dignity God extends to human choice*. He treats you — He treats every doomed nation, every stubborn heart — as a *genuine agent whose return is genuinely worth pleading for*. The door is *really* open. The invitation is *really* sincere. The fact that He knows what you will choose does not make the offer a formality. He still turns the gem. He still waits at the door. He still says *maybe* — to people He knows will not come — because *that is what mercy does*. Mercy keeps the door open past the point of reason.

[PAUSE]

There is something here that should break your heart and rebuild it in the same motion.

We often imagine that God's foreknowledge makes us small — that if He already knows, then our choices are theater. This ayah says the opposite. This ayah says that God, *knowing*, **still pleads.** Still varies the signs. Still turns the diamond to the lamp for the ten-thousandth time, *for your eyes*, hoping — in the language He chose for Himself — that *this* facet, *this* time, might be the one that turns you around. Your return is not a foregone conclusion to be processed. It is a thing *longed for*. Even when refused, it was *wanted*.

And now the word we have been walking toward this whole time: **يَرْجِعُونَ** — *yarjiʿūn*. *That they might **return***. The root is **R-J-ʿ**, *to come back, to go back to the origin*. It is the very root in the words you say when death comes: *innā lillāhi wa-innā ilayhi **rājiʿūn*** — "to Allah we belong, and to Him we are *returning*." 

Do you see the choice the root lays bare? There are only two *returns*. You can *return* now, by your own feet, walking back to your Lord while the door is open and the signs are still turning. Or you will *return* later, carried, *rājiʿūn*, when the wind has come and you have become — like the towns — a *what* on the edge of someone else's map. **The return is not optional. Only its manner is.** The towns that surround the Meccan did not return the first way. So they returned the second. And the ayah is, gently, ferociously, begging you to choose the first.

[PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow?

When the next sign comes — and it will, because He has not stopped turning the gem — when the next funeral, the next near-miss, the next verse that suddenly lands, the next 3 a.m. fear arrives, do not file it as scenery. Hear it as the *maybe*. Hear it as God, who knows everything, still leaving the door open *for you specifically*. And do the one thing the towns did not. **Return** — a small return, today. A prayer you'd been delaying. A wrong you'd been nursing, set down. One step back toward the center of the circle. That step *is* the answer to a question God has been asking you your whole life, in a hundred turned facets: *Will you come back while coming back is still a thing you can do with your own two feet?*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: Two Circles and a Center

Now that we have done the work, let me show you the shape of the whole thing — because it is built with a precision that only becomes visible once you've held all the pieces.

This ayah draws **two circles around the listener, and points to one center.**

The **first circle** is *ḥawlakum* — the ring of *ruins*. The dead towns, encircling the living listener on every side. This is the geography of *judgment already executed*. The verdict made of stone and sand.

The **second circle** is *ṣarrafnā* — the *turning* of the signs. The gem rotated before the eye, the warning presented from every facet, *all around* the listener in time the way the ruins surround him in space. This is the geography of *mercy still in motion*. The plea made of recited verses and turning winds.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? You are standing inside two concentric rings. The outer ring is what happens to those who do not return: *ruins*. The inner ring is the means by which you are begged to return: *signs, turned and turned*. The judgment encircles you. But *inside* the judgment, closer to you, is the mercy — the signs are nearer than the ruins. And at the dead center of both circles is the single point the whole ayah is reaching for: ***yarjiʿūn*** — *return*. Come back to the center. Come back to your Lord.

You are surrounded by ruins. You are surrounded, more closely, by signs. And you are *called*, from the very middle, *home*.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes were never two things. They were one truth approached from two directions.

*Ḥawlakum* — the unnamed, encircling ruins — is the grammatical form of **habituation**: what surrounds us always, we stop seeing. *Ṣarrafnā* — the intensive, repeated *turning* of the signs — is the grammatical form of **God's cure for habituation**: He varies the warning precisely because sameness goes numb. And *laʿalla… yarjiʿūn* — "perhaps they will return" — is the grammatical form of **mercy that pleads even where it foreknows refusal**: the open door, the dignified choice, the *maybe* spoken to those He knows will not come, simply because that is who He is.

The words *are* the lesson. The grammar *is* the mercy.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the "smell of my own home" in my spiritual life — the warning so constant that I have stopped detecting it entirely?
2. The ruins were once a thriving people on whose map *others* were the cautionary tale. On whose map am I a landmark not yet drawn?
3. When was the last time a sign was *turned* toward me — a death, a fear, a verse that landed — and I filed it as scenery instead of hearing the *maybe*?
4. Allah pleads in the language of "perhaps" even with those He knows will refuse. Do I extend that same dignity — that same open door — to the people I have already written off?
5. The return is not optional, only its manner. Am I returning now, on my own feet — or postponing until I am carried?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You are encircled by the ruins of those who did not return and, more closely still, by signs that God keeps turning toward you like a jeweler at the lamp — and the only question the ayah asks is whether you will come back now, while coming back is still something you can choose.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow, on your own road, you will pass something you have stopped seeing. Let it become *news* again — once. And when the next sign turns toward you, do not let your thumb keep scrolling. Hear it for what it is: the patient, foreknowing, door-holding *maybe* of the One who turns the gem ten thousand times for your eyes alone. And then take one step back toward the center. That step is the whole of what He has been asking.

### Du'āʾ

O Allah, You who turn the signs about for us when we have gone numb to them — break our habituation, and let us see what surrounds us before it is too late to see. You who plead with the language of "perhaps" even where You already know — do not let us be among those who were turned toward and would not turn. Let us return now, on our own feet, while the door is open and the wind has not yet come. Āmīn.
