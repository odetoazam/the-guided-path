# The Tongue You Can Carry: One Light, Two Doors

## Introduction

Let me place the words in front of you first. Sit with the sound of them before we touch the meaning.

> **وَمِن قَبْلِهِۦ كِتَـٰبُ مُوسَىٰٓ إِمَامًا وَرَحْمَةً ۚ وَهَـٰذَا كِتَـٰبٌ مُّصَدِّقٌ لِّسَانًا عَرَبِيًّا لِّيُنذِرَ ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟ وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُحْسِنِينَ**

*Wa min qablihi kitābu Mūsā imāman wa raḥmah. Wa hādhā kitābun muṣaddiqun lisānan ʿarabiyyan li-yundhira alladhīna ẓalamū wa bushrā lil-muḥsinīn.*

"And before it was the Scripture of Musa — a guide that leads, and a mercy. And this is a confirming Book, in an Arabic tongue, to warn those who have wronged, and as glad tidings for those who do good."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel what this ayah is doing, you have to stand where its first listeners stood.

Imagine Makkah. A man stands among his people and says: *a Book has come down to me from the Lord of the worlds.* And the objection comes back, sharp and confident: *if this were real, where is it in the record? Where is the warrant for it? Why has nothing before you spoken of this?*

This is the argument the surah has been having. The opening verses of Al-Ahqaf throw down a challenge to the deniers — *bring me a scripture from before this, or any trace of knowledge, if you are truthful.* The deniers were treating this revelation as if it had appeared out of nowhere. An interruption. A break in the world. Something with no roots and therefore no authority.

And into that accusation — *you have no precedent, you stand alone, you are a rupture* — this ayah answers.

[PAUSE]

It does not answer by saying *I don't need a precedent.* It answers by saying something far more disarming. It says: **there was a Book before this one.** The Scripture of Musa. And it goes further — it tells you what that earlier Book *was* for the people who received it: a leader they walked behind, and a mercy that held them. And then it says of this new revelation: *this one confirms that one.*

Think about how unexpected that is. The natural move, when someone challenges your authority, is to assert your independence — *I owe nothing to what came before.* This ayah does the opposite. It reaches back. It honors the older Book. It places this revelation not as a replacement but as the next word in a single, unbroken sentence that Allah has been speaking across the centuries.

### The Hook

And here is what I want you to hunt for as we go.

Two scriptures are named in this ayah. But they are not described with the same words. The Torah is given two gifts — *a guide* and *a mercy.* The Quran is given something different — it is *confirming,* and it comes *in an Arabic tongue.* Why the difference? Why does each Book get its own pair of descriptions?

And then, at the very end, the ayah splits humanity into two — those who wronged, and those who do good. But watch carefully, because Allah does not describe these two groups in the same grammar. One of them is named by something they *did.* The other is named by something they *are.* That single asymmetry — buried in the difference between a verb and a noun — may be one of the most merciful things in this entire verse.

Stay with me. Let's open it slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Imāman* — the guide that walks in front

Before we touch the word, let me give you a picture you already know.

Imagine a mountain trail at dusk, a group of travelers, and one person who walks at the front carrying the only lantern. That person is not merely *with* the group. They are *ahead* of it. Everyone else orients to the back of that figure. Where the light goes, the feet follow. The leader does not push from behind — they draw forward from in front. You don't obey them so much as *follow* them.

Hold that picture. Now look at what Allah calls the Torah.

> **إِمَامًا** — *imāman* — "a guide that leads."

The word comes from the root *a-m-m* (أمم), and this is one of the most quietly profound roots in the whole language. It is the root of **imām** — the one you stand behind in prayer, whose movements yours follow. It is the root of **umm** — a mother, the source you came from and orient back to. It is the root of **ummah** — a community gathered around a shared direction. And it is the root of **amām** — the space *in front of you.*

Do you feel what binds all of those together? **The thing that goes before. The thing you face. The thing that sets your direction.**

So when Allah calls the Torah an *imām,* He is not merely calling it a guidebook you consult. The classical commentators — al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi — note that *imāman* here is grammatically a **state**, a *ḥāl*, describing the condition in which the Book existed for its people: *yuqtadā bihi* — "something to be followed, walked behind." It was placed *in front of* a nation, and they were meant to walk in its light.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something. Let's sit with this.

The imām in prayer is in front of you — but the imām is *also a servant,* also bowing, also prostrating, also facing the same Qiblah you face. The leader is not the destination. The leader is the one who shows you where the destination is by moving toward it first.

That is what a divine Book is. It walks ahead of you, but it is not itself the One you are walking toward. It carries the lantern. It is not the dawn.

And here is what is remarkable about the grammar. The scholars noted a second reading — that *imāman* and *raḥmatan* might stand on an implied verb: *anzalnāhu imāman wa raḥmah*, "We sent it down as a guide and a mercy." Both readings are alive in the tradition, and they don't compete — they layer. One tells you what the Book *was* for its people. The other tells you what Allah *intended* it to be when He sent it. The leadership was not an accident of how the people used it. It was woven into the sending.

So the Torah was an *imām* — and right beside it, joined by a single *wa*:

> **وَرَحْمَةً** — *wa raḥmah* — "and a mercy."

Stay with this pairing, because it is doing something precise. A guide *leads.* A mercy *holds.* One is out in front, setting your direction. The other is underneath you, catching you. A revelation that only led would be a demand — *here is the path, keep up.* A revelation that only comforted would be a sedative — *rest, nothing is asked of you.* The Torah was both. It walked ahead and it held beneath. It pulled you forward and it caught you when you fell.

**This is what a Book from Allah is: a lantern in front and a floor beneath, in the same breath.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Lisānan ʿArabiyyan* — the tongue, not the language

Now we come to the Quran's own description, and I need to teach you a small thing about Arabic before we open the word, because the entire weight of it hangs on a choice most translations erase.

In Arabic there are two ways to speak of a "language."

There is **lugha** (لغة) — language as a system. Vocabulary, grammar, the abstract structure. When you say "I am studying the Arabic *language,*" you would use *lugha.* It is the thing on the page, the thing in the dictionary, the thing that exists whether or not any mouth is speaking it.

And then there is **lisān** (لسان) — which is, first and most literally, **the tongue.** The piece of flesh in your mouth. The wet, warm, moving muscle that makes sound. Only by extension does it come to mean "language" — and it never fully leaves the body behind. When you use *lisān,* the language is still attached to a living mouth.

Now look at what Allah chose:

> **لِّسَانًا عَرَبِيًّا** — *lisānan ʿarabiyyan* — "in an Arabic **tongue.**"

He did not say *bi-lughatin ʿarabiyyah,* "in the Arabic language," the abstract system. He said *lisān.* **A tongue.** The Book came down not as a structure on a page but as something spoken, something carried in a mouth, something that lives only when a human being moves their own tongue to say it.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with why this matters.

The Torah, in this ayah, is described by what it *does for its people* — it leads them, it has mercy on them. But the Quran is described by *how it arrives* — confirming, and in a tongue. And of all the ways Allah could have named the Quran's Arabic-ness, He named it through the organ of speech.

Notice something. A tongue is portable. You cannot leave your tongue at the temple. You cannot lock it in a sanctuary and visit it on holy days. It travels everywhere you travel. It is with you in the marketplace, in your bed, in your grief, in the dark when you cannot sleep. **A Book given as a *lisān* is a Book that goes where you go.**

And a tongue is *yours.* The same muscle you use to ask for water, to call your child, to lie, to confess, to laugh — *that* is the instrument Allah chose to carry His final words. He did not hand humanity a sealed scroll to be admired from a distance. He placed His speech onto the most intimate, most ordinary, most constantly-used part of the body. The Quran was made to be *said.*

Here's what's remarkable. Al-Baghawi notes that *lisānan ʿarabiyyan* stands in the accusative as a *ḥāl* — a description of the *state* in which this Book confirms. So the confirming and the tongue are bound together: this Book confirms what came before **precisely by being spoken, in this tongue, among these people.** The confirmation is not an abstract claim filed in heaven. It is something that happens on human lips, in a real language, in a real place.

And think about what it means that the tongue is *ʿarabī* — clear, expressive, eloquent. The root *ʿ-r-b* (عرب) carries the sense of clarity, of speech that makes things plain. This is not a tongue that obscures. It is a tongue built to *reveal.* When the same surah-family elsewhere insists the Quran came in a *clear* Arabic tongue, it is answering an ancient suspicion — that the divine must be murky, that holiness hides itself in fog. No. **This light came in a tongue made for clarity, placed in a mouth made for speaking, so that it could go everywhere a human goes and be understood when it arrived.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The verb and the noun — how Allah names the wrongdoer and the one who does good

This is the one I most want you to see. And to see it, you need one small grammatical fact, taught plainly.

In Arabic — as in every language — there is a difference between a **verb** and a **noun.** A verb is an *event.* It happens in time, it starts and stops, it is something done. A noun — and especially a participle, a "doer-word" like *teacher* or *builder* — is a *state of being.* It describes not an event but a standing identity. "He taught me" is a verb: an event that occurred and ended. "He is a teacher" is a noun: a description of *who he is.*

Hold that distinction like a blade, because Allah is about to use it to cut something open.

Look at the end of the ayah. It splits all of humanity into two groups, and gives each a purpose for the Book:

> **لِّيُنذِرَ ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟** — "to warn those who **have wronged**"
> **وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُحْسِنِينَ** — "and as glad tidings for **the doers of good**"

Now watch the grammar, because Allah did not build these two halves symmetrically. There are *two* asymmetries hidden here, and each one is a mercy.

**First asymmetry — how the two groups are named.**

The first group is named with a **verb**: *ẓalamū* (ظلموا) — "they wronged." Root *ẓ-l-m,* a Form I past-tense verb. It is an *event.* Something they *did.* It happened in time, which means — and stay with me here — **it is the kind of thing that has an end.** A verb describes an action, and an action can be stopped. The person is not called *a wrongdoer,* a fixed identity. They are called *those who committed the act of wronging.* The door is described as something they walked through, not as a room they are sealed inside.

The second group is named with a **noun** — a participle: *al-muḥsinīn* (المحسنين) — "the doers of good." Root *ḥ-s-n,* and it is a Form IV active participle, *muḥsin.* This is not an event. It is an **identity.** Not "those who did a good deed once," but "those who *are* good-doers" — the ones for whom *iḥsān*, excellence, beautiful doing, has become *who they are.*

Do you see what Allah has done?

[PAUSE]

**He named the wrongdoing as an action — temporary, finishable, escapable. And He named the goodness as an identity — settled, established, a thing you become.**

The grammar itself is hope. To the one who has wronged, Allah does not say *you are a wrongdoer* — a sentence that sounds like a verdict, a name carved into stone. He says *you are one who did an act of wronging.* And an act can be left behind. The verb is an open door. You are described by what you *did,* precisely so that you can stop doing it and no longer be described that way.

But to the one who does good, Allah grants the noun — the stable thing, the identity that doesn't evaporate when the moment passes. *Iḥsān* is not flagged as a one-time event that might never recur. It is held as a standing reality, a name you carry.

The commentators tell us *alladhīna ẓalamū* here are those who wronged themselves through denial and disobedience — al-Muyassar adds *ẓalamū anfusahum,* "they wronged *their own souls.*" Even the wrong, in the tafsir, is turned inward: the wrongdoer's first and deepest victim is himself. And *al-muḥsinīn* are those who made their faith and their obedience beautiful — who did not merely comply, but did the good *well.*

**Second asymmetry — what each group is given.**

Now look again, even more closely. To the wrongdoers, the Book comes as a **verb**: *li-yundhira* (لينذر), "to warn" — Form IV, an active process, something the Book *does to* them. Warning is an intervention. It reaches out, grabs the sleeve, says *stop.* It is dynamic, urgent, in motion — because it is trying to interrupt an action that is still happening.

But to the doers of good, the Book comes as a **noun**: *bushrā* (بشرى), "glad tidings" — not a verb, not something being done to them, but a *thing,* a gift, a state that simply *is* theirs. Warning has to chase. Glad tidings can rest. You don't have to *do* anything to the muḥsinīn — you simply *give* them the good news, because they are already facing the right way.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the full shape of it. **For the wrongdoer: an action they committed, met by an action of warning — because something is still in motion and must be turned. For the good-doer: an identity they have become, met by a gift that simply belongs to them — because the turning is already done.**

The root of *bushrā* — *b-sh-r* — is the same root as *bashar,* human skin, the face. *Bushrā* is news so good it shows *on your face* before you can say a word — the brightening of the skin, the involuntary lifting. Allah did not promise the muḥsinīn a future reward filed away somewhere distant. He promised them *the thing that already lights up the face.*

**This single ayah holds both the warning that chases the one still running, and the light that already rests on the face of the one who turned. And it built both promises into the difference between a verb and a noun.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us.

*Imāman* — a Book that walks in front of you carrying the light, and beneath you as a mercy that catches you.

*Lisānan ʿarabiyyan* — a Book placed not on a distant altar but on the human tongue, portable, intimate, made to be spoken everywhere you go.

And the verb against the noun — wrongdoing named as an act you can leave, goodness named as an identity you can become; warning that chases, glad tidings that rests.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about you.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The fear that the new erases the old

Let me start somewhere very human.

There is a particular grief that comes when something new arrives and seems to cancel what came before. A company is acquired and the old team's work is quietly deleted. A second marriage makes the children of the first feel erased. A new translation of a beloved book makes the old one feel obsolete, and something in you mourns it. We carry a deep, often unspoken fear: **that to make room for what is next, the world must throw away what was.** That arrival means erasure. That the new word silences the old one.

And we apply this fear to faith too. Many people — believers and skeptics alike — imagine revelation as a series of replacements. One scripture cancels the last. One community declares the previous one void. As if God kept changing His mind, or kept failing and starting over.

Now bring that fear to this ayah. Watch what it does to it.

> "And before it was the Scripture of Musa — a guide, a mercy. And this is a Book **confirming**..."

The word is *muṣaddiq* (مصدّق). Root *ṣ-d-q* — the root of *ṣidq,* truth; of *ṣadīq,* a true friend; of *ṣadaqah,* the charity that proves your faith is sincere. And here it appears as a Form II active participle. Form II in Arabic intensifies and makes-declarative: *ṣaddaqa* doesn't just mean "to be true" — it means *to actively declare something true, to verify it, to stand up and confirm it.*

So the Quran is not introduced as the thing that *replaces* the Torah. It is introduced as the thing that *stands up and testifies that the Torah was true.*

[PAUSE]

Feel how completely this dissolves the fear of erasure.

The new revelation's *first described act* toward the old one is not to cancel it but to *confirm* it. It walks into the courtroom of history and says, on behalf of every previous Book: *this was real. This came from the same Source. I am not here to bury it — I am here to vouch for it.*

This is a God who does not erase to advance. The Torah was an *imām* and a *raḥmah* for its people — Allah says so, in His own final Book, with honor, without subtraction. The arrival of the Quran does not demote the Torah's truth. **Revelation is not a series of replacements. It is one long sentence, and each Book is the next word, confirming that the sentence has been coherent all along.**

Let me build this further, because there is something even more tender underneath.

Think about *why* a person fears the new erasing the old. It is usually because they have known a world where power *does* work that way — where the strong overwrite the weak, where to win is to make the loser disappear. We project that onto the divine. We assume God competes the way we compete.

But this ayah shows you a Source that operates on a completely different principle. It *gathers.* It confirms. It says of what came before: *true, true, and again true.* The same Hand wrote the lantern carried by Musa and the tongue given to the final Prophet ﷺ — and the second never needed to dim the first to shine.

Now turn it inward, because here is where it touches your life directly.

If this is how Allah relates to *time* — confirming, not erasing — then consider how He relates to *your* time. The version of you that prayed sincerely ten years ago, before you drifted — is that self erased? The good you did in a season you now look back on with embarrassment — was it deleted when you changed? **A Lord who confirms the truth of what came before does not throw away your earlier sincerity just because a later self failed.** He is *muṣaddiq.* He testifies that the real thing was real. Your past devotion is not voided by your present distance. It is written, confirmed, held.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** When you return to Him after a long absence — and you will, at some point, return — you are not starting from zero, as if the believing child you once were has been erased. You are rejoining a sentence you were always part of. The same God who confirmed the Torah after centuries confirms the truth of *your* earliest yes. Return is not reconstruction from rubble. It is recognition: *yes — this was always true.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: You are what you did, or you are what you became

Let me take you into the most ordinary human experience there is: the way we name ourselves after our failures.

A person tells a lie, and somewhere in the night a voice settles over them: *I am a liar.* Not *I lied* — but *I am a liar,* as if it were a species, a permanent classification, a thing carved into the bone. Someone relapses, and thinks: *I am an addict, this is what I am.* Someone fails their family in a hard season and concludes: *I am a bad parent, a bad child, a bad person* — full stop, identity, sentence passed.

Notice what the mind does there. It takes a *verb* — an action, an event in time — and it silently upgrades it into a *noun.* "I did a wrong thing" becomes "I am a wrong thing." And the moment that conversion happens, despair has a place to live. Because you can stop *doing.* But how do you stop *being?*

This is, I think, one of the quietest and most destructive moves the human soul makes against itself. We name our sins as identities and our goodness as accidents. *I lied* becomes *I am a liar.* But *I helped someone* stays merely *I did a nice thing once* — never *I am good.* We let the darkness define our being and the light remain a fluke.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah does with the exact same grammar — and watch Him reverse our every instinct.

> **لِّيُنذِرَ ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟ وَبُشْرَىٰ لِلْمُحْسِنِينَ**

The wronging — Allah keeps it a *verb.* *Ẓalamū.* "They committed the act of wronging." He refuses to convert it into an identity. He does not call them *al-ẓālimīn* as a final species-name carved in stone here; He names them by the *action,* and an action is bounded — it has an end, it can be abandoned, you can walk back out the door you walked in through. **Allah names your sin the way that leaves the exit open.**

And the goodness — He makes it a *noun.* *Al-muḥsinīn.* Not "those who did a good deed once," but "the ones who *are* doers of good." He grants to *iḥsān* the very permanence we wrongly grant to our sins. He lets the *light* become the identity.

Do you see how completely this overturns the soul's cruelty against itself?

We say: *my sin is who I am, my goodness was a fluke.*
Allah says: *your sin is something you did, your goodness is who you can become.*

[PAUSE]

Let me build through this, because the reversal goes deeper the longer you stay with it.

Why would Allah grammar it this way? Because *iḥsān* — beautiful doing — is meant to be *cultivated into a self.* You don't become a muḥsin in one act. You become it the way a person becomes a craftsman: through repetition, through returning to the good again and again until it stops being something you do and becomes something you are. The noun is an invitation: *keep doing the good until the good is your name.*

But wronging is meant to be *interrupted,* not inhabited. So Allah keeps it a verb — and sends warning, *li-yundhira,* an active reaching-out — precisely to catch the action *while it is still an action,* before the soul does its terrible alchemy and freezes the verb into a noun. The warning is mercy moving fast, trying to reach you before you start believing that what you *did* is what you *are.*

And here is the paradox to sit with — don't resolve it too quickly. The wrongdoer is offered a *warning,* which sounds harsh, and the good-doer is offered *glad tidings,* which sounds soft. We instinctively want the gift, not the warning. But look closer. **The warning is the more urgent love.** Glad tidings can wait — the muḥsin is already facing the right way; the good news will keep. But the warning *cannot* wait, because the wrongdoer is mid-action, still moving, and every moment risks the verb hardening into a noun. Allah sends the urgent thing to the one in danger and the restful thing to the one who is safe. That is not inequality. **That is triage. That is a physician running to the bleeding patient and smiling gently at the healing one.**

Connect this to the universal struggle. Every human being lives somewhere on the line between these two words — *ẓalamū* and *muḥsinīn* — and most of us cross it many times in a single day. The question this ayah presses on you is not *which group am I in, forever?* The grammar has already refused that question. The question is: **which word am I letting define me?** Am I letting a verb — a thing I did — masquerade as a noun, a thing I am? Am I sealing myself into a sin Allah Himself left as an open action?

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the practice. Tonight, when the voice comes — and it comes to everyone — and says *I am a failure, I am a sinner, this is what I am* — answer it with Allah's own grammar. Say: *No. I did a wrong thing. That is a verb. And a verb can end.* And then, with the same breath, take one of your small goods — a prayer you made, a kindness, a restraint — and let *that* be the noun. *I am someone who, by His grace, does good.* Do not let the darkness be your identity and the light be your accident. **Reverse it, the way Allah reversed it. Let the verb be the sin you are leaving, and the noun be the self you are becoming.**

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture beneath the verse

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how the whole ayah is built — because it has a shape, and the shape is the argument.

Look at the two halves.

**First half — the Torah:** *kitābu Mūsā* — given two gifts, *imāman wa raḥmah,* a guide and a mercy. A Book described by what it *was for its people.*

**Second half — the Quran:** *hādhā kitābun muṣaddiqun lisānan ʿarabiyyan* — given two descriptions, *confirming* and *in a tongue.* A Book described by *how it arrived* and *what it does to the past.*

And then the verse opens into two human doors, each with its own pairing: the wrongdoers, met by a *verb* of warning; the good-doers, met by a *noun* of glad tidings.

Do you see the design? **Two Books, named in parallel. Two responses, named in opposition.** The first half shows revelation reaching back through time to confirm and honor. The second half shows revelation reaching forward into the heart to warn and to gladden. The verse is a hinge — one side faces history, the other faces you. And the pivot in the middle is that single word, *muṣaddiq* — *confirming* — the word that joins the backward-facing honor to the forward-facing summons.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The *lisān* — the tongue — told us this Book was made to be *carried,* portable, intimate, said. And the theme of confirmation told us this Book was made to *gather,* not erase — to carry the truth of the past forward intact. Both are the same divine character: **a God who does not leave things behind. Not the earlier scriptures. Not the earlier you. Not the verb you are trying to escape.**

And the grammar of *ẓalamū* against *al-muḥsinīn* — the verb against the noun — is the same mercy as *muṣaddiq.* A Lord who confirms the truth of what came before is a Lord who refuses to let your wrong-action become your permanent name, and refuses to let your goodness evaporate as a fluke. **He confirms what is true and leaves open what can change.** That is one attribute, seen once in how He treats time and once in how He treats your soul.

### Questions to carry

- Where in my life have I taken a *verb* — something I did — and frozen it into a *noun,* a name I now carry as identity? What would it feel like to say *I did that* instead of *I am that*?
- Have I let my goodness remain an accident in my own eyes — a fluke I don't take seriously — while letting my failures define who I think I am? What would it mean to reverse that, the way Allah reversed it?
- When I imagine returning to Allah after distance, do I imagine starting from rubble — or do I trust that He is *muṣaddiq,* the One who confirms that my earliest sincerity was real and is not erased?
- The Quran came as a *tongue* — made to be spoken, carried everywhere I go. When did I last actually *say* it aloud, let it move my own tongue, rather than only read it with my eyes?
- The warning runs to the one in danger; the glad tidings rest on the one who turned. Which am I being sent right now — and am I willing to receive the urgent love of a warning as mercy, not as rejection?

### One-sentence distillation

Allah sends one unbroken stream of light — the Torah as a leader and a mercy, the Quran confirming it in a tongue you can carry everywhere — and into that single light He names two doors with deliberate grammar: wrongdoing kept as a verb you can leave, and goodness offered as a noun you can become.

### The closing invitation

So here is what might shift after this.

You came, perhaps, carrying a name you gave yourself — *I am a failure, I am far, I am the one who ruined it.* This ayah does not argue with your pain. It re-grammars it. It says: that is a verb. It happened. It can end. And the good in you — the small, real, unglamorous good — *that* is the noun. That is the self Allah is inviting you to keep doing until it becomes who you are.

And it tells you that the Light walking in front of you has never erased a single true thing — not the scriptures before it, not the believer you used to be. He is *muṣaddiq.* He confirms. Walk back into the sentence you were always part of.

### A closing du'a

*Yā Muṣaddiq* — O You who confirm what is true — confirm in us the truth of every sincere step we ever took toward You, and do not let our failures be erased into despair nor our goodness be erased into nothing. You named wrongdoing as an action; give us the courage to leave it. You named the good-doers by who they are; make *iḥsān* our name before You call us by it. And place Your Book on our tongues, not only in our eyes — so that it may go everywhere we go, a guide in front of us and a mercy beneath us, until we meet You. *Āmīn.*
