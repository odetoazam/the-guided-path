# When the Worshipped Turn Enemy

### وَإِذَا حُشِرَ ٱلنَّاسُ كَانُوا۟ لَهُمْ أَعْدَآءً وَكَانُوا۟ بِعِبَادَتِهِمْ كَٰفِرِينَ

*wa-idhā ḥushira an-nāsu kānū lahum aʿdāʾan wa-kānū bi-ʿibādatihim kāfirīn*

**"And when the people are gathered, those [they called upon] will be enemies to them, and they will deny their worship."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Thing You Built Your Life Around

Let me ask you to picture something before we touch a single word of this ayah.

Picture a person who has organized their entire inner life around one thing. Maybe it's a career they have given everything to — the late nights, the missed dinners, the slow erosion of every other relationship, all of it poured into this one altar. Maybe it's a person — someone they orbit, someone whose approval has become the sun their whole sky turns toward. Maybe it's an image of themselves, a reputation, a following, a number that goes up. Maybe it's an ideology, a tribe, a cause that has quietly taken the place in their heart that nothing should occupy except God.

Whatever it is, here is the defining feature: **they pour, and it receives.** Day after day, they direct the deepest currents of their attention, their fear, their hope, their devotion — toward this thing. And the thing just sits there. It takes the devotion. It never asks for it, never refuses it, never answers it. It is simply *there*, absorbing a lifetime of orientation.

Now I want you to hold a question in your mind, because the Quran is about to answer it, and the answer is more frightening and more merciful than anything we would invent on our own.

**The question is this: what happens to that relationship on the day it can no longer be avoided? On the day of total clarity — when the lights come up and every illusion is stripped away — what does the thing you worshipped do?**

We assume one of two answers. Either it keeps being indifferent — it just sits there, useless but harmless. Or, if we're optimistic, maybe it finally turns toward us, finally reciprocates, finally pays back the devotion.

[PAUSE]

The Quran gives a third answer that almost no one expects. And to feel its full weight, we have to stand where the first listeners stood.

### Rebuilding the World

This surah, Al-Ahqaf, opens with a courtroom. Not a sermon — a courtroom. In the ayahs just before this one, Allah is conducting a cross-examination of the people of Makkah who called upon idols, intermediaries, beings they imagined could bring them closer to God. And the challenge Allah issues is devastating in its precision: *"Show Me. Show Me what they created of the earth. Bring Me a book from before this one, or some trace of real knowledge — anything — if you are telling the truth"* (46:4).

It is the demand of a prosecutor who already knows the defense has nothing.

Then, in the ayah immediately before ours, comes a line that should stop us cold. Allah describes these objects of devotion as **ghāfilūn** — *"and they are unaware of their call"* (46:5). The people are crying out, pleading, directing the most intimate movements of the heart toward these beings — and the beings *do not even know it is happening.* The devotion lands on something that has no idea it is being devoted to.

That is the silence of this world. A one-way street. You pour, and the thing receives without receiving, because it isn't even aware enough to receive.

And then comes our ayah. And the silence breaks.

### The Hook

Here is what I need you to feel before we begin. In this world, the false god is merely *unaware* of you. That is the worst it does — it ignores you by being incapable of doing anything else.

But the Quran says a day is coming when that same silent, unaware, indifferent thing will **open its mouth.** And when it finally speaks — after a lifetime of silence — it will not say *"thank you."* It will not say *"I'm sorry I couldn't help."*

It will turn to you, in front of all the gathered creation, and it will become your **enemy.** And then it will do something stranger still — it will look God in the face and say: *"I never knew this person. I deny their worship. It has nothing to do with me."*

The thing you gave your life to will deny it ever happened.

Let's understand how Allah builds this, word by word — because the construction is the proof.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: حُشِرَ — The Verb That Strips Away Your Agency

Before we look at the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs carry voice — specifically, the difference between *active* and *passive*.

In every language, there's a difference between "I gathered the crowd" and "the crowd was gathered." In the first, you are the one doing it; you have agency, control, choice. In the second, something happens *to* the crowd — they are acted upon, moved by a force outside themselves. The crowd has no say. Arabic marks this distinction with great precision through a change in the internal vowels of the verb. The active *ḥashara* (he gathered) becomes the passive *ḥushira* (it/he was gathered) — same letters, different inner sound, completely different power dynamic.

Now look at how this ayah opens:

**وَإِذَا حُشِرَ ٱلنَّاسُ** — *"And when the people are gathered..."*

The verb is **ḥushira** — passive. Not "when the people *gather*," as if they stroll in voluntarily. **When the people *are gathered*** — when they are collected, assembled, brought together by a force entirely outside their control.

Let's sit with this, because there's a whole theology hidden in that single vowel pattern.

Think about how we move through this world. We choose where to go. We decide who to spend our time with, what to pursue, what to worship. The fundamental experience of being alive is the experience of *agency* — the sense that I am the author of my movements. Even the person worshipping a false god is *choosing* it. They walk to the altar on their own two feet.

But the word **ḥushira** announces that this entire mode of existence is about to end. On that Day, no one walks to the gathering. Everyone *is walked.*

[PAUSE]

And there's more in the root itself. The Arabic root of this verb — *ḥā-shīn-rā* — does not simply mean "to assemble" in a gentle sense. The lexicographers tell us it carries the texture of **driving** — herding, the way a herder drives livestock together into a confined space, with no consultation of the animals' preferences. It is gathering-by-compulsion. Gathering as something done *to* a herd.

Notice what Allah is doing. The very people who, in this world, imagined themselves sophisticated — selecting their gods, curating their devotions, masters of their own spiritual lives — are described, on that Day, with a word that places them in the position of a driven flock. Everything they thought made them autonomous agents is gone. The choosing is over. Now there is only the being-gathered.

Here is what this single passive verb reveals: **the entire confidence of the worshipper rests on the temporary illusion that they are in control.** And the moment that illusion lifts — the moment they are *gathered* rather than gathering — the relationship with everything they worshipped is about to be renegotiated on terms they cannot set.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: كَانُوا — The Past Tense That Hasn't Happened Yet

Now I want to show you something that, once you see it, you cannot un-see. And it lives in a word so small we would normally read right past it.

First, the concept. In Arabic, as in English, there is a past tense and there is a future. *Kāna* means "he was." It is the perfect tense — completed, done, finished, in the past. If I want to tell you about something that *will* happen, I would normally reach for a future construction, not a past one. You don't describe tomorrow's events in the past tense. That would be strange.

Unless you want to say something that no future tense can say.

Look carefully at the structure of our ayah:

**وَإِذَا حُشِرَ ٱلنَّاسُ كَانُوا۟ لَهُمْ أَعْدَآءً**
*"And when the people are gathered, they **were** enemies to them..."*

The verb **kānū** is past tense. *They were.* But the event it describes — the gathering of all humanity for Judgment — has **not happened.** It is future. It is, from where we sit, the most distant future imaginable.

So why does Allah describe a future event in the past tense?

Let's slow down, because this is one of the most beautiful and most under-noticed features of how the Quran speaks about the Last Day. Throughout the Quran, when Allah describes the events of Judgment, He repeatedly slips into the perfect tense — *the trumpet was blown, the earth was flattened, the records were laid open* — for things that, by our reckoning, are still to come.

This is not a grammatical accident. It is theology written into syntax.

[PAUSE]

Here is what it means. We live our lives treating the future as the realm of *uncertainty.* The past is fixed — it happened, it cannot be changed, it is solid. The future is liquid, open, unknowable. We hedge against it. We say "maybe," "perhaps," "if things go well." And because the Day of Judgment lives in that liquid future, we treat *it* as uncertain too. We file it under "someday, possibly, eventually" — and a someday-possibly never reorganizes a life.

But Allah reaches into His description of that Day and stamps it with the **past tense** — the tense of things that are already over and done. **He is telling us that from the vantage point of absolute knowledge, this is not a maybe. It is as fixed as yesterday.** The enmity of these false gods toward their worshippers is so certain that the only honest way to describe it is as something that has *already occurred.*

Notice something remarkable. We think we are the ones standing in solid reality, looking out at a misty, uncertain future. The grammar of this ayah quietly reverses the picture. **The Day of Judgment is the solid thing.** It is *we* who are standing in the mist — in the brief, dissolving illusion of "not yet." The thing we call "the uncertain future" is, in the sight of God, more finished and more real than the chair you are sitting in.

What this invites us to consider is sobering: every time we postpone the reckoning in our hearts — *later, someday, I have time* — we are arguing with a verb that is already in the past tense. We are insisting "maybe" at something Allah has already called "done."

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: كَافِرِينَ — When the Worshipped Become the Disbelievers

Now we arrive at the word that turns this entire ayah inside out. And to feel it, you need one piece of background.

The word **kāfir** — and its plural **kāfirīn** — is the most loaded word in this entire vocabulary. We usually translate it "disbeliever." But the root meaning, attested across the classical lexicons, is older and more physical than "disbelief." The root *kāf-fā-rā* originally means **to cover, to conceal, to bury.** The farmer who covers the seed with soil is, in old Arabic, performing an act of *kufr* — he buries it. So *kufr*, at its core, is not merely "not believing." It is **active covering-over.** It is taking something real and burying it, denying it, disowning it, refusing to acknowledge what is plainly there.

Now — who, in the world of this surah, are the *kāfirīn*? Up to this point, obviously: the people. The worshippers. The ones who took God's truth and buried it under their idols. That is who the word has belonged to.

Watch what Allah does in the final three words:

**وَكَانُوا۟ بِعِبَادَتِهِمْ كَٰفِرِينَ**
*"...and they were, of their worship, **deniers.**"*

The *they* here — grammatically — is the false gods. The objects of worship. And the word Allah assigns to *them* is **kāfirīn.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the reversal? In this world, the *worshippers* were the kāfirīn — they buried the truth of God beneath their devotions. But on that Day, the title is stripped from the worshippers and handed to **the worshipped.** The idols, the intermediaries, the beings that were prayed to — *they* become the deniers. *They* are the ones who now "cover over" and "bury" the relationship. They look at the very worship that was poured onto them for a lifetime, and they bury it. *"I never received this. This worship — I deny it. It has nothing to do with me."*

And notice the exact phrase: **bi-ʿibādatihim kāfirīn** — *"deniers of their worship."* The thing being denied is **the worship itself** — *ʿibāda*, from the root meaning servitude, the act of devoting oneself. The most intimate thing a human being ever does — to bow the heart toward something — is the precise thing the false god now disowns.

Let's go one layer deeper, because the classical commentators saw something here we should not miss. Al-Qurtubī notes that these "worshipped" beings are not all of one kind. Some were angels — and the angels become enemies of those who worshipped them, because the angels never asked to be worshipped and are appalled by it. Some were jinn and devils — and they disown their followers and curse them. And some were lifeless idols — which, the commentators say, Allah may give the power of speech on that Day precisely so they can testify against the ones who carved them. Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī both anchor this in the parallel where the worshipped declare: **"We dissociate ourselves from them before You — it was not us they were worshipping"** (28:63).

[PAUSE]

So sit with the full architecture of betrayal now, because the surah has built it in three rising stages, and you have walked up every step:

In **this world**, the false god is **ghāfil** — *unaware* of you (46:5). It does not even register your devotion. That is stage one: indifference.

On **that Day**, the same false god becomes **ʿaduww** — your *enemy.* The silence breaks, and what comes out is hostility. That is stage two: enmity.

And then, in the same breath, it becomes **kāfir bi-ʿibādatihim** — a *denier* of the very worship it received. It buries the relationship, disowns it before God, swears it never happened. That is stage three: total disownment.

**Unaware. Then enemy. Then denier.** From not-knowing-you, to opposing-you, to swearing-you-never-existed. That is the complete career of every devotion offered to anything other than Allah.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Betrayal Built Into Every False Attachment

I want to start somewhere very human, very modern, very far from idols of stone.

There is a particular kind of grief that people describe after they have poured years into something that does not, in the end, hold them. The person who gave two decades to a company and is let go in a ten-minute meeting, walked out by security past the desk they built their identity at. The person who organized their entire sense of self around someone's love, and discovers — at the moment they most needed that love — that it was never as solid as their devotion to it. The person who chased a certain status, a certain number of followers, a certain seat at a certain table, and finally got it, and felt the cold realization that the thing cannot love them back. It cannot even *see* them. It was **ghāfil** the entire time.

Here is the pattern that runs underneath all of these, and the Quran names it with surgical accuracy. **The defining feature of a false attachment is asymmetry.** You pour in one direction. The thing receives in no direction, because it cannot. The relationship was never mutual. You imagined a relationship; there was only your half of it, projected onto something incapable of holding up its end.

And this is the mercy hidden inside a terrifying ayah. Because Allah is not waiting until the Day of Judgment to *inform* us of this. He is telling us *now,* while there is still time to redirect, what every false attachment actually is and what it will eventually do.

[PAUSE]

Let's let the ayah illuminate the human experience, movement by movement.

The first thing the ayah shows us is that **indifference is not neutral — it is enmity that hasn't matured yet.** We comfort ourselves about our misplaced attachments by saying "well, it's not *hurting* anyone." The career isn't evil. The image isn't malicious. The person we idolize isn't cruel. They're just... there. Indifferent. And we read indifference as safety. But the ayah traces a line directly from the *ghafla* of 46:5 to the *ʿadāwa* of 46:6 — from "unaware of you" to "enemy of you" — and it tells us these are not two different things. They are the same thing at two different temperatures. **The thing that is merely unaware of you in comfort will be actively against you in crisis.** Indifference is just enmity that the Day of Reckoning has not yet heated to the point of speech.

The second movement is harder. The ayah tells us not only that the false god turns enemy — but that it turns **denier.** It does not merely abandon you; it *erases the record.* It looks at God and says the worship never happened. And here we have to ask the uncomfortable question: why would that be the specific form of betrayal?

Because — and stay with me here — **the false god is trying to save itself.** On that Day, even the idol wants distance from the sin. Even the thing that was worshipped does not want to be caught holding the worship that belonged to God alone. So it does the most cowardly thing imaginable: it disowns the one person who was loyal to it. The worshipper was *faithful* — gave a lifetime of devotion — and is repaid by having that very faithfulness denied to their face. **The one you were loyal to will, to save itself, pretend it never knew you.**

[PAUSE]

The third movement is where this stops being about other people and becomes about us. Because it is very easy to hear "false gods" and picture stone idols in ancient Makkah and feel safely modern and monotheistic. But the root of the word *ʿibāda* — worship — is *servitude,* orientation, the bowing of the heart. And the heart bows to whatever it organizes itself around. **The question is never whether you worship. The question is only what.**

So the diagnostic the ayah hands you is brutal and clarifying: *look at what you are pouring devotion into — and ask whether it can hold up its end on the worst day of your life.* The career that cannot enter the grave with you. The reputation that the people maintaining it will abandon the instant it costs them something. The person whose approval you have made into your sun, who will stand at their own reckoning thinking only of themselves. Every one of these is, in the language of this ayah, **ghāfil** now and on a trajectory toward **ʿaduww** and **kāfir** later. Not because they hate you. Because they were *never able to hold what you placed on them.* You handed the weight of a soul to something built to carry nothing.

[PAUSE]

And now the universal struggle underneath all of it: we keep doing this because the false god offers something Allah does not — it offers itself *immediately,* on *our* terms, without demanding that we change. The idol never asks you to become better. The number never asks you to repent. The image never asks you to humble yourself. That is the entire appeal: a god that makes no claim on your character. But the price of a god that asks nothing of you is a god that *gives* nothing to you when it counts — and worse, one that will *testify against you* for having bowed to it.

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?** Here is the practical application, and it is concrete. Take the single thing you are most afraid to lose — the attachment whose removal would feel like the end of you. Hold it up against one question: *will this be my enemy or my advocate on the Day I am gathered?* If your devotion to it is pulling you away from God, the ayah has already told you its verdict — it will deny you. The redirection does not require you to abandon the career or the relationship or the work. It requires you to **demote it** — to move it from the place of worship to the place of a tool, something you use in service of the One who will not deny you. The wealth that you *worship* will be your enemy; the wealth you *spend* for Allah will be your witness. Same wealth. Different altar.

### Theme Two: The Tense of Certainty and the Mercy of the Warning

Let me bring you to a second, quieter human experience — one we almost never name, but live inside constantly.

We are, all of us, extraordinarily skilled at treating certain things as uncertain. Not because the evidence is unclear, but because the certainty is *inconvenient.* Every human being knows, with total intellectual confidence, that they will die. The data is unanimous; the success rate of mortality is one hundred percent. And yet almost no one *lives* as though death is certain. We file it under "someday." We treat the single most guaranteed event of our existence as a vague rumor.

Why? Because there is a gap — a vast, treacherous gap — between knowing something is *certain* and *feeling* it as *near.* The mind can hold "this will definitely happen" and "but not in any way that affects my Tuesday" at the same time, with no apparent contradiction. We are masters of the certain-but-distant. It is one of the nafs's most effective sedatives.

And into exactly this gap, Allah places a verb in the past tense.

[PAUSE]

Remember what we uncovered: **kānū** — *"they were enemies"* — describes a future event in the grammar of a completed past. We treated this as a linguistic curiosity in Part One. Now let it become a key to your own psychology.

The reason Allah describes the Day of Gathering in the perfect tense is that He is *attacking the gap directly.* He is refusing to let us file this under "someday-maybe." By stamping the future with the tense of the already-done, He is saying: *the thing you are tempted to treat as a distant possibility is, in reality, more finished than your own past.* Your past can be misremembered, forgiven, transformed. This Day cannot be edited. It simply *is* — already, fixedly, in the present tense of God's knowledge.

Let the paradox sit without rushing to resolve it: **the most certain event in your existence is the one you feel as least real.** The chair beneath you feels solid and the Judgment feels like mist — and the grammar of this ayah tells you that you have it exactly backwards. The chair is dissolving as you sit in it. The Judgment is the bedrock.

[PAUSE]

But here is where the ayah turns from terror to mercy, and we must not miss it, because it would be a sin against the text to leave you only in fear.

Think about *when* you are being told this. You are being told that the false gods will become enemies and deniers — and you are being told it **now.** Before the gathering. While the false gods are still merely *ghāfil* and not yet *ʿaduww.* While there is still a gap between "they are unaware of your worship" and "they deny your worship to your face."

That gap — the gap we just called the nafs's sedative — is, seen from the other side, **the space of mercy.** It is the time Allah has given you to switch altars while switching is still free. The whole reason He describes the future in the past tense is not to crush you with certainty but to *lend you* His certainty now, so that you can act on it while action still changes the outcome. He is letting you see the verdict before the trial, so that you can change your plea.

[PAUSE]

This is the staggering generosity buried inside the warning. A judge who shows you the verdict in advance is not threatening you — *he is giving you the only thing that could possibly save you: foreknowledge, while there is still time to act on it.* The ayah is a spoiler for your own Judgment Day, handed to you by the One who will judge, precisely so you are not surprised, precisely so you are not on the wrong side of that enmity when the verb finally arrives in its real tense.

What this asks of us is a single, profound shift: to start treating the certain as certain. Not intellectually — we already do that. *Existentially.* To let the past-tense Day press its solidity into our Tuesday.

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?** Here is the practical application, and it is simple enough to do today. Take one decision you are currently making as though Judgment is uncertain — one place where you are saying "I'll fix that relationship with Allah later," "I'll deal with that sin eventually," "I'll get serious about my prayer someday." Now apply the grammar of this ayah to it: speak it back to yourself in the past tense. *"The Day came. I was gathered. What did I bring?"* Not as morbid fantasy — as the act of borrowing Allah's certainty into the present, where it can still reorganize a single choice. One choice, made as though the certain thing is certain. That is the entire invitation of *kānū.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing the Two Roads Together

We walked into this ayah from two directions, and I want to show you now that they were always the same road.

The **linguistic journey** took us through three words. **Ḥushira** — the passive verb that strips away your agency and reveals that the day is coming when you will be gathered, not gather. **Kānū** — the past tense laid over a future event, telling us this is so certain it is already done. And **kāfirīn** — the title of "denier" ripped from the worshippers and pinned onto the worshipped, the great reversal where the thing you served disowns your service to its face.

The **thematic journey** took us through two truths. That **every false attachment is a betrayal waiting to mature** — indifferent now, your enemy later, your denier in the end. And that **Allah has lovingly leaked you the verdict in advance,** writing the certain future in the past tense so that you might feel its certainty while you can still change your standing in it.

Now watch them fuse. The three words and the two truths are one insight viewed from two sides:

**Everything you give your heart to is being weighed right now for what it will do on the Day you are gathered — and Allah has told you the result in advance, in the tense of the already-done, precisely so you can change your altar before the verb comes true.**

The grammar *is* the mercy. The passive verb that takes away your control is the same God lovingly removing your illusions *now,* on the page, where it costs you nothing, rather than only *then,* in the gathering, where it would cost you everything. The architecture is the proof: a God who only wanted to condemn would not have shown you the courtroom from the outside, with the door still open behind you.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. **What is the one thing in my life that, if I am honest, I treat as a god — the thing I organize my fear and hope and devotion around?** And on the Day I am gathered: is it built to defend me, or to deny me?

2. **Where am I living as though the Judgment is uncertain** — what specific "later" am I telling myself, and what would change if I spoke it in the past tense: *"the Day came, and I had not"?*

3. The false gods become *kāfirīn* — they bury the worship to save themselves. **Is there anyone, or anything, I am being loyal to that would disown me the moment my loyalty cost it something?**

4. Allah showed me the verdict *before* the trial. **What is that foreknowledge worth to me — and what is one choice I will make differently because of it, today, while the gap of mercy is still open?**

5. The only One who will *not* become my enemy on that Day, who will *not* deny my worship, is the One I was made to worship. **What would it look like to move even one devotion off a false altar and onto His?**

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Everything you worship other than Allah is unaware of you now, will be your enemy then, and will finally deny it ever knew you — and He has told you this in advance, in the tense of the already-done, so that you might change altars while the door is still open.**

### The Closing Invitation

This is not, in the end, an ayah about idols of stone. It is an ayah about the heart's habit of bowing — and about the staggering kindness of a God who, rather than letting us discover too late what our devotions will do, hands us the verdict now, while it can still be appealed.

So the shift this ayah invites is not fear. It is *audit.* A quiet, honest inventory of where your worship actually goes — not where you say it goes, but where your attention, your dread, your hope actually flow when no one is watching. And then a single, deliberate act of redirection: taking even one thing off the altar it does not deserve, and turning it back toward the One who will meet you in the gathering not as an enemy, but as the only One who was ever able to hold what you placed on Him.

You will be gathered. The verb is already past. The only question the ayah leaves open is which face will turn toward you when the silence finally breaks.

### A Du'a From These Words

*O Allah, You who will gather us when we can no longer gather ourselves — do not let us stand among those whom their own devotions denied. Pry loose from our hearts every false god that is unaware of us now and will be our enemy then, and turn our worship — all of it — toward You alone, the only One who will not disown us on the Day we are gathered. Let our deeds be witnesses for us and not enemies against us, and let us feel the certainty of that Day now, while there is still time to change our standing in it.*

*Āmīn.*
