# The Named Term and the Turned-Away Face

### Surah Al-Ahqaf, Ayah 3

> مَا خَلَقْنَا ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَآ إِلَّا بِٱلْحَقِّ وَأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى ۚ وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ عَمَّآ أُنذِرُوا۟ مُعْرِضُونَ

*Mā khalaqnā as-samāwāti wal-arḍa wa mā baynahumā illā bil-ḥaqqi wa ajalin musammā. Wal-laḏīna kafarū ʿammā unḏirū muʿriḍūn.*

> "We did not create the heavens and the earth and all that lies between them except in truth and for an appointed term. Yet those who disbelieve turn away from what they are warned of."

---

## INTRODUCTION: Two Sentences That Refuse to Stay Apart

I want you to notice something before we even begin. This is a single ayah, and it is made of two sentences that, on the surface, have nothing to do with each other.

The first sentence is the largest claim a being can make. It is about *everything* — the heavens, the earth, and the entire middle distance of existence between them. It tells you why all of it is here.

The second sentence is about a small, specific failure. It is about a certain kind of person and a certain motion their body makes. It is almost intimate. It is the description of a shoulder turning, a face angling away.

And Allah has welded them into one verse.

[PAUSE]

Why would the cosmos and a turned shoulder belong in the same breath? Why does the architecture of the universe lead, in the space of a single conjunction, to a description of someone simply *not looking*?

Let me rebuild the world this landed in.

### The world of the early Meccan listener

The surahs that open with **Ḥā-Mīm** — and Al-Ahqaf is the last of them — were spoken into a community that was not, for the most part, arguing. We tend to imagine the opposition to the Prophet ﷺ as a great debate, philosophers trading objections across a table. It was rarely that. The dominant posture of Mecca's elite was not the raised fist of the counter-argument. It was the turned back. The changed subject. The look that slides past you to something more interesting.

The Quran has a precise word for this, and we will live inside that word today. But feel the situation first. A man stands among his own people. He tells them the sky above them and the ground beneath them were not an accident, that there is a reckoning coming, that they have been *warned*. And the response is not "prove it."

The response is a yawn. A shoulder. A walking-away.

[PAUSE]

That is the emotional climate of this ayah. And into that climate, Allah does something stunning. He does not begin by scolding the ones who turn away. He begins by describing the universe they are standing in. He sets the stage so enormously, so deliberately, that by the time we arrive at the turned-away face, that small gesture has become almost unbearable to watch.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hunger for as we begin.

Allah gives the heavens and the earth *two gifts* in this ayah. He builds them on something, and He grants them something. Two endowments, named precisely.

And then He turns to the human being who disbelieves — and notice what that human *gives back*.

The whole ayah is an exchange. A cosmos that received, and a creature that returns the gesture. Once you see what was given and what was returned, you will not be able to unsee it.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *illā bil-ḥaqq* — The Universe Was Built *On* Something

#### First, the everyday idea

Imagine someone hands you a house. You walk through it — solid walls, a roof that holds, doors that close true. Now imagine you ask, "What is it built *on*?" If the answer is "nothing — it just sits on the dirt, no foundation," your relationship to that house changes instantly. You stop trusting the floor. Every comfort in the house is now provisional.

A building is not only made of materials. It is built *upon* something. The foundation is invisible once the house is up, but it is the most important thing about it. It is what decides whether the house is *true* — whether it will still be standing, plumb and level, in fifty years.

Hold that idea: *what a thing is built upon*.

#### Now, open the words

Allah says:

> مَا خَلَقْنَا ... إِلَّا بِٱلْحَقِّ

*Mā khalaqnā ... illā bil-ḥaqq* — "We did not create ... except **bil-ḥaqq**."

Notice the shape of the sentence first. It is a negation followed by an exception: *mā ... illā*. "Not ... except." In Arabic this construction is one of the strongest ways to make a point. You do not say "We created it with truth." You say "We did **not** create it with anything *except* truth." You close every other door first, and leave open only one.

So before we even reach the word *ḥaqq*, the grammar has already done something: it has eliminated every alternative. Whatever this universe was built on, the sentence has ruled out *everything else*.

[PAUSE]

Now the word itself. **بِٱلْحَقِّ** — *bil-ḥaqq*. The root is *ḥā-qāf-qāf*, and across the Arabic language it carries a cluster of meanings that all lean the same direction: truth, reality, rightness, that which is firmly established, that which is *due* — an obligation, a right owed. *Ḥaqq* is not soft truth, the truth of an opinion. It is the truth of a load-bearing beam. It is what is *real* and what is *owed*.

And notice the little letter at the front: the **ba**. *Bi*-l-ḥaqq. That ba is doing quiet, heavy work. In Arabic the ba can mean "with," "by means of," "by way of," "accompanied by." So *bil-ḥaqq* is not a decoration on the creating. It is the *medium* of the creating. The heavens and the earth were not created and *then* truth was added. They were created **by way of** truth, **accompanied by** truth, with truth as the very thing they ride on.

The universe is built *on* something. That something is *ḥaqq*.

#### Let's sit with what that rules out

Here is where the *mā ... illā* construction pays off. By saying creation happened by nothing *except* truth, Allah is silently naming the thing creation is *not*.

And the Quran elsewhere says it out loud. In Sūrah al-Anbiyāʾ: *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them as players"* — **lāʿibīn** (21:16). In Sūrah Ṣād: *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in vain"* — **bāṭilan** (38:27). In Sūrah al-Muʾminūn: *"Did you then think that We created you uselessly?"* — **ʿabathan** (23:115).

Play. Vanity. Uselessness. *Bāṭil* — the exact opposite of *ḥaqq*. A *bāṭil* is something with no foundation, a claim that collapses, a house on no ground.

So when Allah says the universe was built on nothing *except ḥaqq*, He is answering, across the whole Quran, one specific human suspicion: **the suspicion that none of this means anything.** The suspicion that it is all play, all froth, a cosmic accident that will dissolve back into nothing and signify nothing.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the heart of it. *Ḥaqq* is not only "truth" as in "factually accurate." It is "truth" as in *rightful purpose*, *that which is due*. Al-Qurṭubī, reflecting on this very ayah, brings forward a reading: that "bil-ḥaqq" points toward *recompense* — that the heavens and earth were established as the stage on which the wrongdoer and the doer-of-good are each given their due, citing the parallel in Sūrah an-Najm: *"...that He may recompense those who did evil for what they did, and recompense those who did good with the best"* (53:31). And al-Muyassar adds the other face of the same coin: created bil-ḥaqq means created so that servants would *know the greatness of their Maker and worship Him alone*, and would *establish truth and justice among themselves*.

Do you feel the two directions opening from one word? *Ḥaqq* points **up** — toward the Maker who is owed recognition. And *ḥaqq* points **across** — toward the justice owed between people. The same foundation holds up both.

This is what the universe is built on. Not on play. On a *debt of truth* — owed upward to the One who made it, and owed outward among the ones who live in it.

**What this single word reveals:** You are not standing on dirt with no foundation. You are standing on *ḥaqq* — and a foundation of truth is, by its nature, a foundation that holds you accountable to be true.

### Element Two: *wa ajalin musammā* — A Term That Has a Name You Were Not Told

#### First, the everyday idea

There is a strange psychological fact about deadlines. A task with no deadline does not feel free — it feels *unreal*. "Someday I'll write that letter" is a sentence that never becomes an action. But "this is due Friday at noon" — suddenly the thing is solid. It has edges. It is *real* because it ends.

A boundary is what makes a thing serious. The frame is what makes the painting a painting and not just smeared wall. The final whistle is what makes the game matter. Remove the ending, and you don't get more of the thing — you get *less*. You get something that never had to mean anything because it never had to stop.

Hold that: *an ending is what makes a thing real*.

#### Now, open the words

Allah's second gift to creation, in the same breath as the first:

> وَأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى

*wa ajalin musammā* — "and an appointed term."

Two words. Look at each.

**أَجَلٍ** — *ajal*. The root *hamza-jīm-lām* gives us a *term*, a *fixed span of time*, a *due-date*. An *ajal* is not just "time." It is *bounded* time — a measured allotment with an end-point built in. When the Quran speaks of a person's *ajal*, it means the appointed end of their life. Here it is the *ajal* of the cosmos itself.

Now notice something in the Arabic that is easy to walk past. The word *ajalin* carries **tanwīn** — that little *-in* ending, the mark of an *indefinite* noun. In Arabic, indefiniteness often signals vagueness: "a term," *some* term, an unspecified span. On its own, *ajal* would leave the term floating, unknown, blurry.

And then comes the second word and does something almost paradoxical to the first.

**مُّسَمًّى** — *musammā*. This is a passive participle from the root *sīn-mīm-wāw*, the root of *naming* (*sammā*, "he named"). In its derived form it means **"named," "designated," "specified."** A *musammā* term is a term that has been *given a name* — a term that has been *precisely fixed*.

Sit with the collision of these two words. *Ajalin* — *indefinite*, a term left open. *Musammā* — *named*, a term precisely fixed.

[PAUSE]

How can a single term be both *unspecified* and *named*?

Here is the answer, and it is one of the quietest, most precise things in the verse. The term is **indefinite to you. It is named to Him.**

The *tanwīn* of vagueness faces *us*: we do not know the day. The participle *musammā* faces *Allah*: with Him it has a name, a fixed coordinate, an exact appointment already written. The grammar holds both at once — and in holding both, it draws the precise border between what is hidden from the creation and what is known to the Creator.

#### Read the absence

Now ask the question this methodology always asks of a passive participle: *musammā* — "named" — **named by whom?**

A passive participle deliberately drops the agent. The verb hides the namer. Arabic could have said "a term We named." It did not. It said "a *named* term" — and let the Namer stand just offstage, unmentioned, *assumed*.

Why withhold the Namer? Because the withholding *is* the point. The same God who built the cosmos on *ḥaqq* has set its expiry, named it precisely, and **kept the name to Himself.** The classical commentators — Ibn ʿAbbās as reported by al-Qurṭubī, and al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn alike — read this *ajal musammā* as *the Day of Resurrection*, the appointed end "to which the heavens and the earth run their course," the term that points to their *fanāʾ*, their passing-away. The universe has a death certificate already signed, already dated. You simply have not been shown the date.

[PAUSE]

And feel what that does. An ending you *cannot see* but *cannot doubt* is the most serious kind of ending there is. If you knew the date, you would procrastinate up to it. Because you *don't* know it — because it is *musammā* to Him and *vague* to you — every single moment becomes the one that might sit just before the line.

#### One more thing — the buried resonance

Look at the roots living inside this ayah. *as-Samāwāt* — the heavens — and *musammā* — the named term — share the consonants of the root *sīn-mīm-wāw*. The lexicographers debate whether *samāʾ* (the high heaven) and *ism/sammā* (name) ultimately spring from one source, so I will not build doctrine on it. But let your ear simply *hear* it: the ayah opens with **as-samāwāt** and turns, four words later, to **musammā**. The *heavens* and the *named*. The highest thing in creation, and the fixing of its end, ringing on the same consonants.

I only point it out so you hear that this ayah is *composed*, not assembled — that even its sounds are doing quiet work.

**What these two words reveal:** Your universe is not only built on truth — it is *bounded* by an appointment whose date is named, precise, certain, and deliberately hidden from you, so that the certainty of the ending and the secrecy of its hour press on every hour you are given.

### Element Three: *muʿriḍūn* — Not a Verb. A Standing Posture.

#### First, the everyday idea

Think about the difference between these two sentences: "He got angry," and "He is an angry person."

The first is an event — it happened once, it passed. The second is a *character* — it is who he *is*, a stable trait, a way of being that does not switch off. In English we feel the difference between a verb (a thing you *did*) and a description (a thing you *are*).

Arabic marks this difference with great force, and the ayah uses it like a closing chord.

#### Now, open the word

After the cosmos — built on truth, bounded by a named term — Allah turns:

> وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ عَمَّآ أُنذِرُوا۟ مُعْرِضُونَ

*Wal-laḏīna kafarū ʿammā unḏirū muʿriḍūn* — "Yet those who disbelieve, from what they are warned of, are *turning away*."

Three things to open here, fast and then deep.

First, **أُنذِرُوا** — *unḏirū*, "they were warned." Root *nūn-ḏāl-rā*, the root of *warning* — and specifically warning about *danger*, the warning a sentry shouts. It is a Form IV verb here in the *passive*: they did not seek the warning; the warning *came to them*. It was delivered. Al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn gloss it plainly: warned in the Qurʾān of the *resurrection and the reckoning*, frightened of the punishment. The warning reached their ears. That is established. The failure is not that they were not told.

Second — and this is the structural hinge of the whole ayah — notice that Allah does **not** give them a verb. He could have said *yuʿriḍūn*, "they turn away" — a verb, an action, a thing they *do*. Instead He ends on **مُعْرِضُونَ** — *muʿriḍūn*, an **active participle**. A *noun-like* word. A *description of state*.

#### Why that one grammatical choice changes everything

In Arabic, a sentence built on a participle like this — *"and those who disbelieve ... are ones-who-turn-away"* — is a **nominal sentence**. And nominal sentences carry a flavor that verbal sentences do not. A verb (*yuʿriḍūn*) would suggest an *ongoing action*, something occurring. A participle in a nominal frame (*muʿriḍūn*) suggests **a fixed, settled, characteristic state** — *thubūt*, stability, permanence. It is the difference between "he is turning away right now" and "he is, by his very standing, a turned-away person."

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the contrast the ayah has just built?

The heavens and the earth were given an *ajal musammā* — a **named, bounded, finite** term. They are *defined by their ending*. They are going somewhere; they will arrive; they will stop.

And the disbeliever is described by a **participle of permanence** — a turning-away with no built-in end, a posture that has become a *standing condition*. The cosmos has a finish line. The averted face just... keeps facing away.

The one thing in the verse that *should* be small and temporary — a man's momentary glance aside — is rendered in the grammar of *permanence*. And the vast cosmos, which feels permanent, is the thing given a *named, expiring term*.

The ayah has inverted what we assume. **The universe is the temporary thing. The turned-away posture is the thing that hardens into forever.**

#### The word itself — what "turning away" actually is

Third, the meaning of **iʿrāḍ**. Root *ʿayn-rā-ḍād*, related to *ʿarḍ* — the *side*, the *breadth* of a thing. To do *iʿrāḍ* is to turn your *side* to something. To give it your shoulder. To angle your *breadth* away so that you no longer face it.

Stay with how *physical* this is. The Quran did not say these people *argued against* the warning, or *disproved* it, or even *hated* it. It said they turned their *side* to it. *Iʿrāḍ* is not the posture of the debater. It is the posture of someone walking past a person calling their name and pretending not to hear. Al-Muyassar captures the texture exactly: *they do not take heed and they do not reflect* — *lā yattaʿiẓūna wa lā yatafakkarūn*. There is no engagement to refute. There is only a face that has decided not to turn.

[PAUSE]

And here is what should unsettle us. *Iʿrāḍ* is *easier* than disbelief-by-argument. The one who argues has at least *faced* the thing. The *muʿriḍ* never even turns around. It is the lowest-effort refusal there is — and the ayah marks it, with the participle, as the one that *sets like concrete* into a permanent state.

**What this word reveals:** The thing that damns is not always the raised fist of denial. More often it is the lowered effort of the turned shoulder — and a posture maintained long enough stops being a thing you *do* and becomes a thing you *are*.

#### The bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three precise things: a universe built **on truth**, *bil-ḥaqq*; bounded by a **named, hidden term**, *ajalin musammā*; and met by a creature whose **turning-away has hardened into a standing state**, *muʿriḍūn*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the precise shape of our own days.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Cure for the Suspicion That Nothing Means Anything

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular feeling that visits the modern person, usually late, usually alone. It is not doubt about a specific doctrine. It is something vaguer and heavier: the suspicion that the whole thing — your job, your striving, the species, the stars — is just *happening*, for no reason, on the way to nothing. Thinkers have a word for it: nihilism. But you don't need the word. You've felt the thing. The 3 a.m. sense that you could vanish and the universe would not so much as ripple, because there was never any *point* to ripple about.

That feeling is not new. It is exactly the suspicion this ayah was built to answer. *Lāʿibīn. Bāṭilan. ʿAbathan.* Players, vanity, uselessness. The Quran names the disease by name and then denies it: **"We did not create ... except *bil-ḥaqq*."**

Now watch how the ayah heals it, because it does not heal it the way we expect.

[PAUSE]

We expect comfort to come in the form of *removing limits*. "Don't worry — you're infinite, you're limitless, the universe is boundless." But the ayah gives you the opposite. It gives you **two boundaries** and calls them gifts. The universe is *bil-ḥaqq* — bounded by *truth*, which is to say it is *not* permitted to mean whatever you want; it means something *specific* and *owed*. And it is *ajalin musammā* — bounded by an *ending*, a named term, a finish line you cannot see.

Here is the movement I want you to feel: **the boundary is the meaning.** A game without a final whistle is not freedom; it is a game that cannot matter. A life without an *ajal* is not a gift; it is a sentence with no full stop, a thing that never has to mean anything because it never has to end. The very thing the modern soul fears — *limit, ending, accountability* — is the thing that the ayah hands over as the cure for meaninglessness.

[PAUSE]

Think of it this way. Why does a final visit with someone you love carry more weight than the thousand ordinary visits? Because it is *bounded*. The ending pours significance backward into every minute. Now hear the ayah: the *whole universe* is that final visit. It is *ajalin musammā* — it has a last day, named and dated and hidden. Which means every ordinary minute inside it is soaked in the significance of a thing that *will end and is owed truth*.

The cure for "nothing means anything" is not "you are infinite." It is: **you are finite, on purpose, and the purpose is real.** *Bil-ḥaqq wa ajalin musammā.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** When the flatness comes — when a task feels pointless or a day feels like nothing — do not reach for distraction to fill the void. Reach for the *boundary*. Remind yourself that this hour sits inside a creation built on *ḥaqq* and pointed at a *named term*. Then do the next thing as though it is owed — because, the ayah says, it is. Meaning is not found by escaping the limit. It is found by *honoring* it.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Sin of the Slowly Turning Shoulder

Now the harder one. The one that is about us more than we'd like.

Begin again where you live. You are scrolling. A piece of content appears — a reminder of death, a verse, a call to something you know is true and know you've been avoiding. And your thumb moves. Not a decision. Barely a thought. The thing is gone and the next thing is already loading. You did not *argue* with the reminder. You did not *reject* it. You simply... did not turn toward it. You gave it your *side*.

That is *iʿrāḍ*. The Quran's word for the elite of Mecca is the Quran's word for that thumb-flick. **Muʿriḍūn.** Ones who turn the shoulder.

I want us to be honest about how comfortable this sin is. We tend to picture the people the Quran warns as snarling deniers, fists in the air, shouting at the Prophet ﷺ. But the ayah does not describe a shout. It describes a *posture*. These are not people who failed to be convinced. *ʿAmmā unḏirū* — the warning *reached* them. They heard it perfectly. They simply *angled away*. And that is far closer to home, because *iʿrāḍ* requires no conviction, no counter-argument, no effort at all. It is the sin of the path of least resistance. You don't have to *do* anything to turn away. You only have to keep *not turning toward*.

[PAUSE]

And here is the movement that should make us sit up. Recall the grammar. Allah did not give these people a *verb*. He gave them a *participle of permanence*. *Muʿriḍūn* — not "they turned away once," but "they *are* turned-away ones," a settled, characteristic, standing state.

That is the spiritual physics the ayah is teaching. **A posture repeated becomes a posture fixed.** The first time you angle away from a truth, it is an act. The hundredth time, it is no longer something you do — it is something you *are*. The thumb-flick, practiced daily, does not stay a thumb-flick. It hardens into a *self*. A person who has turned away ten thousand times is no longer a person who turns away. He is a *muʿriḍ* — turned-away in his very standing, the way *as-samāwāt* are high in their very standing.

[PAUSE]

Let the paradox sit, because the ayah refuses to resolve it gently: the *easiest* refusal is the most *permanent*. The loud denier at least *faced* the thing — he turned around to shout. The *muʿriḍ* never turned around at all, and precisely because he never engaged, there was never a moment of confrontation in which he might have been changed. The shoulder that never turns toward the warning is the shoulder that can harden forever, because nothing ever happened to it. Avoidance feels like *not deciding*. The ayah says avoidance *is* the decision — the most durable one, because it is never revisited.

And notice the cruelty of the contrast the verse has set up. The heavens and the earth — vast, silent, obedient — *accept* their *ajal musammā*. They hold their place on the foundation of *ḥaqq* and they will meet their named term without protest. The one creature given the capacity to *understand* the warning is the one creature that turns its side to it. Everything in the cosmos faces its Maker's truth. The human alone gives it his shoulder.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Hunt the small turn. Not the dramatic crisis of faith — the *iʿrāḍ* you do without noticing. The reminder you scroll past. The thought of death you change the subject on. The verse you "don't have time for" right now. Catch the shoulder *as it begins to angle*, and turn the face back. Once. Because the ayah's terror is not that you will one day loudly reject the truth. It is that you will quietly, comfortably, a thousand unremarkable times, simply decline to look — until declining-to-look is no longer something you do but someone you've become.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, now that we can see it

Look at the whole ayah from above, now that we have lived inside it.

It is built as an **exchange**, and the conjunction *wa* — "yet," "but" — is the hinge it pivots on.

On one side: the **Creator gives**. He gives the heavens and the earth and the between two endowments — a *foundation* (*bil-ḥaqq*) and a *boundary* (*ajalin musammā*). Truth to stand on, and a named term to move toward. The cosmos *receives*, and holds its place, and faces forward.

On the other side, across the pivot: the **creature returns the gesture** — and returns it inverted. Given truth and a warning of the term, the human who disbelieves offers back not gratitude, not even argument, but a *posture*: *muʿriḍūn*. The turned side. The averted face.

The Maker faces His creation with truth. The creature faces His truth with its shoulder.

[PAUSE]

And see how the two halves are weighted to teach us by their imbalance. The cosmos — which we assume is permanent — is given a *finite, named term*. The turning-away — which we assume is a small, passing gesture — is given the *grammar of permanence*. The ayah has quietly swapped what lasts. The stars will end on their appointed day. The turned shoulder, left alone, hardens into forever.

### Two directions, one insight

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same discovery approached from two sides.

From the side of the *words*, we found: a universe built on *ḥaqq* and bounded by a *named term*, met by a participle of permanent turning-away.

From the side of the *soul*, we found: that meaning *is* the boundary, that finitude is the gift — and that the deadliest refusal is not the loud one but the quiet, comfortable, repeated angling-away that sets like concrete.

They are one thing. **A universe this purposeful, this bounded, this *true*, makes only one response unthinkable — and it is the easiest one to fall into.** To turn your side to it.

### Questions to carry

1. When meaning drains out of a day, do I reach for *distraction* to fill the emptiness — or for the *boundary*, the reminder that this hour is built on truth and pointed at a named end?

2. The term is *musammā* — named, fixed, hidden from me. If I truly held that the date is real and the date is secret, what would I stop postponing today?

3. Where is my *iʿrāḍ*? Not my doubts — my *avoidances*. What truth do I reliably scroll past, change the subject on, "not have time for"?

4. A posture repeated becomes a self. Which small turning-away of mine is in the process of hardening into *who I am*?

5. The heavens face their Maker's truth without protest. What would it cost me, once, to turn my whole face toward what I already know is true?

### One-sentence distillation

The same God who built nothing without truth and set a named, hidden ending for all of it is warning you — and in a universe saturated with meaning, the one tragedy is the face that has grown comfortable turning away.

### The closing invitation

You do not need to win an argument today. The people in this ayah lost without ever having one. You need only to *turn your face*. To catch the shoulder as it begins to angle away from something true, and bring it back — once, deliberately, while turning-back is still something you can do and has not yet become something you've forgotten how to do.

The universe is not asking you to be infinite. It is inviting you to be *true* — *bil-ḥaqq* — for the length of a term whose name only Allah knows.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who created nothing in vain and set for everything a named and certain term, do not let our faces be among the faces that turn away. You have warned us in mercy; make us of those who *turn toward*. And when our own *ajal musammā* arrives — the one whose name You have kept — let it find us facing You, not giving You our side. *Āmīn.*
