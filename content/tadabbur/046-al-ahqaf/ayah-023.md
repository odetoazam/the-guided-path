# Knowledge Belongs to Him, the Message Belongs to Me

### Surah Al-Ahqaf, Ayah 23

> قَالَ إِنَّمَا ٱلْعِلْمُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ وَأُبَلِّغُكُم مَّآ أُرْسِلْتُ بِهِۦ وَلَـٰكِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰكُمْ قَوْمًا تَجْهَلُونَ

*Qāla innamā l-ʿilmu ʿinda Llāh, wa-uballighukum mā ursiltu bih, wa-lākinnī arākum qawman tajhalūn.*

**"He said: Knowledge is only with Allah, and I convey to you that with which I was sent. But I see you are a people behaving in ignorance."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Dare in the Dunes

Let us rebuild the world this verse stands in. Because if we walk in cold, we will hear only a polite religious sentence. And this is not a polite religious sentence. This is a man standing in front of a crowd that has just dared him to call down his own God's punishment.

Picture the people of 'Aad. The Quran does not describe them as primitive. It describes them as the opposite. They were giants of civilization — builders of *Iram dhāt al-ʿimād*, "Iram of the pillars," a city the Quran says had no equal made in all the lands (89:7-8). They carved homes into mountains. They raised columns into the sky. They lived in the *aḥqāf* — the long, curved dunes of southern Arabia — and they bent even the desert to their ambition. These were not ignorant people. By every measure the human eye can take, they were the most advanced, most accomplished, most *intelligent* people of their age.

And to these people Allah sent one of their own — Hud, peace be upon him. He came with one message: *the One who gave you all of this is One. Worship Him alone.*

And how did the most sophisticated people of their time respond? Look at the verse just before ours. They said:

> "Have you come to turn us away from our gods? Then **bring us what you threaten us with**, if you are of the truthful." (46:22)

Sit with the arrogance of that. They are not asking a question. They are issuing a challenge. *You say there's a punishment coming? Fine. Schedule it. Give us the date. Make it appear. Then maybe we'll believe you.* They want to put the unseen on a delivery timeline. They want to audit God before they bow to Him. They are treating the punishment of Allah like a contractor's quote — *show us the invoice and the due date, and we'll decide whether to pay.*

[PAUSE]

Now — here is the hook. Hud is a prophet. He is in direct contact with the One who *does* know when the punishment is coming. The crowd has just handed him the perfect opportunity to silence them with information. Every instinct of a man defending his honor would be to fire back: *You want a date? Here. The wind comes in seven days. Watch.*

Hud says none of that.

His very first word in response is a word that hands the entire question away. He answers a demand for knowledge by confessing what he does **not** know. And in that confession — in a prophet calmly saying *that is not mine to know* — is one of the most quietly revolutionary postures a human being can take.

Why would Allah preserve, for all time, a prophet's admission of the limits of his own knowing? What is being protected here? Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Innamā* — The Little Word That Builds a Wall

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you a tool in Arabic, because the whole opening turns on it.

In English, when we want to limit something, we use extra words. We say, "Knowledge is found *only* with God," or "*Nobody but* God has this knowledge." We bolt the restriction on from the outside.

Arabic has a single particle that does this from the inside. It is *innamā* (إِنَّمَا). Grammarians call it a particle of *ḥaṣr* — restriction, confinement, walling-off. When you place *innamā* in front of a statement, you are not just saying a thing is true. You are saying it is true *and the opposite is shut out.* You are building a wall around the claim and bolting the door.

Think of the everyday difference. If I say, "I have the keys," that's information. If I say, *"Only* I have the keys" — *innamā* — I've now told you something more: not just that I have them, but that you do not, and he does not, and she does not. The restriction defines everyone who is excluded.

Now hear Hud's first move:

> **إِنَّمَا ٱلْعِلْمُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ**
> *Innamā l-ʿilmu ʿinda Llāh.*
> "Knowledge is **only** with Allah."

Notice what he does. The crowd demanded knowledge *from him* — *when is the punishment, prophet?* And Hud's answer does not begin with "I don't know." It begins with a wall. *Innamā.* Knowledge — *al-ʿilm*, with the definite article, **the** knowledge, the knowledge of this matter you're demanding — is walled off, confined, sealed, **with Allah alone.**

Let's sit with what the wall excludes. By saying *only with Allah*, Hud has in one breath excluded **himself**. He, the prophet, the man with a direct line to the heavens — he places himself on the *outside* of that wall, standing with the crowd in the territory of not-knowing. The classical commentators are unanimous and plain about this. Al-Jalālayn glosses it: *"He alone is the One who knows when the punishment will come to you."* Al-Qurṭubī spells out the exclusion word for word: *"Knowledge of the timing of the punishment is with Allah — ʿindahu, with Him — **lā ʿindī**, not with me."*

[PAUSE]

Stay with that phrase the mufassir adds: *lā ʿindī.* **Not with me.** This is a prophet, in the middle of a confrontation, voluntarily emptying his own hands. The crowd tried to make his credibility depend on his having the information. And Hud refuses to let his credibility live there. He will not pretend to a knowledge he was not given, even when pretending would win the argument.

Here is what is remarkable. We tend to think the strength of a prophet is in what he *knows*. But the verse locates his integrity in what he *refuses to claim*. The wall of *innamā* is not a wall of weakness. It is the most honest sentence a created being can say about the unseen: *that door is not mine. It was never mine. It belongs to Him.*

And notice the second thing the wall does — and this is subtle. *ʿinda Llāh*, "with Allah," uses *ʿinda*, a word for nearness, for what is held close, in someone's keeping. The knowledge of the appointed hour is not floating in the universe for the clever to discover. It is *held*, intimately, with Him. You cannot engineer your way to it. You cannot build a pillar tall enough to reach it. The people of Iram, who could build anything, are being told: *this one thing you demanded cannot be built. It is kept. And it is not kept with you.*

What does this single particle reveal? **That the dignity of the messenger is not in knowing everything, but in knowing exactly where the boundary of his knowing falls — and refusing to cross it even to win.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Uballighukum* — The One Verb That Defines a Prophet's Whole Job

Now watch what Hud says immediately after building the wall. Having confessed what he does *not* have, he tells them precisely what he *does* have — and it is one word.

> **وَأُبَلِّغُكُم مَّآ أُرْسِلْتُ بِهِۦ**
> *wa-uballighukum mā ursiltu bih.*
> "And I convey to you that with which I was sent."

The verb is *uballighu* — from the root *b-l-gh* (بلغ). Let me teach you this root, because it is one of the most important words in the entire vocabulary of prophethood.

The root *balagha* in its simple form means: to reach, to arrive, to attain the end of a journey. When a traveler *balagha* the city, he reached it. When a boy *balagha*, he reached maturity. The root is about **arrival** — something getting all the way to its destination.

But Hud does not use the simple form. He uses **Form II** — *uballighu*, with the doubled, stressed middle letter (the *shadda* on the *lām*). And here is the law of Arabic morphology you need: Form II takes the meaning of the simple verb and makes it *transitive and intensive* — it means *to make something reach its destination, thoroughly, completely, all the way.* If *balagha* is "to arrive," then *ballagha* is **"to make arrive" — to deliver, to convey, to carry a thing across the full distance until it lands.**

So when Hud says *uballighukum*, he is not saying "I tell you things." He is saying: **"I make the message reach you. I carry it the entire distance from its Sender until it arrives, intact, at your ears."** This is *tablīgh* — the technical name for the prophetic office itself. A prophet is a *muballigh*: a deliverer. A carrier whose entire job is faithful arrival.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the shape of what Hud has built across two phrases. Look at the division of labor he has just drawn:

- **The knowledge** — *al-ʿilm* — is sealed with Allah. Not his.
- **The conveying** — *al-tablīgh* — that is his. His whole portion.

He has cut the world cleanly in two. *I do not own the content. I own the carrying.* The mailman is not the author of the letter. He does not know what every letter says, he did not write them, he cannot change them — and his integrity has nothing to do with authorship. His integrity is entirely in this: **did the letter arrive, unopened, unaltered, complete?**

This is why the verb matters so much. Hud is telling the crowd: *you are demanding I be the author. You want me to produce the punishment, set its date, manufacture the proof. That is not my office. I am the carrier. And a carrier who starts inventing the contents of the letters has stopped being a carrier and become a forger.*

There is a profound humility built into this verb that the Quran is teaching all who would carry truth. Notice — Hud does not say *uballighukum mā aqūlu*, "I convey to you what I say." He says *uballighukum **mā ursiltu bih*** — "what **I was sent with.**" The content is not his. He is downstream of it. Even the message he carries is described as something handed to him to carry, not something originating in him.

And here is the bridge to our third element, hiding in that very phrase — *ursiltu*, "I was sent."

What this single verb reveals: **A faithful carrier of the truth is not measured by how much he knows or how cleverly he can answer, but by whether what arrives in your ears is exactly, unaltered, what was given to him to carry.**

### Element Three: *Ursiltu* and *Tajhalūn* — The Passive That Erases Him, and the Verb That Frames the Whole Ayah

Two small features close out the linguistic journey, and together they reveal the architecture of the entire verse.

**First, the passive: *ursiltu*.** From the root *r-s-l* (رسل) — the same root as *rasūl*, messenger. Hud says *mā ursiltu bih* — "that with which **I was sent**." This is **Form IV in the passive voice.** Notice what the passive does: it removes the agent from the sentence. Hud does not say "that which I carry of my own choosing." He does not even name who sent him. He says *I was sent* — and the sender is left grammatically invisible, because the sender is too obvious and too exalted to need naming.

Do you see the consistency? Across three phrases Hud has systematically removed himself as a source of anything:

- The knowledge? *Not mine — His.*
- The message? *Not authored by me — I was sent with it.*
- Even the sending? *Not my own initiative — I was sent.*

A passive verb is a small thing. But here it is a man stepping out of the center of his own sentence, so that the only One left standing in the middle is Allah. The grammar enacts the theology. The messenger makes himself transparent so that the Sender can be seen through him.

[PAUSE]

**Now the second feature — and this is where the whole verse suddenly snaps into shape.** Look at the final word:

> **وَلَـٰكِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰكُمْ قَوْمًا تَجْهَلُونَ**
> *wa-lākinnī arākum qawman tajhalūn.*
> "But I see you to be a people **behaving in ignorance.**"

The final verb is *tajhalūn*, from the root *j-h-l* (جهل). Now — *jahl* is usually translated "ignorance," and that is half-right and half-dangerously-wrong. In Arabic, *jahl* is not primarily the absence of information. Its true and ancient opposite is not only *ʿilm* (knowledge) but also *ḥilm* — forbearance, self-possession, gravity. *Jahl* is the condition of the rash, the reckless, the one who behaves like a fool *regardless of what he knows.* The pre-Islamic age of the Arabs is called *al-Jāhiliyya* — not because they lacked poetry or intelligence (they had the finest in the world) but because they *behaved* with reckless, violent, ungoverned arrogance.

And notice the **tense.** Hud does not say *jahiltum* — "you were ignorant," a finished fact, a gap in your education. He uses the imperfect, the ongoing present: *tajhalūn* — "you **are behaving** ignorantly, you keep on acting in jahl." Al-Muyassar and al-Qurṭubī both pin down exactly what the jahl is: *jahl fī isti'jālikum al-ʿadhāb* — "ignorance **in your rushing to hasten the punishment**." It is not that they lacked data. They were *daring God to destroy them and calling it cleverness.* That is *jahl*: intelligence in the service of recklessness.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Now stand back and look at the whole verse at once, because here is the architecture, and it is breathtaking.

The verse **opens** with *al-ʿILM* — knowledge.
The verse **closes** with *tajhalŪN* — ignorance.

> **ٱلْعِلْمُ** ... عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ... وَأُبَلِّغُكُم ... وَلَـٰكِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰكُمْ قَوْمًا **تَجْهَلُونَ**

The two bookends of the verse are the two poles of the human soul: *ʿilm* on one end, *jahl* on the other. **Knowledge — and ignorance.** And where does each one live?

- *al-ʿilm* — lives *ʿinda Llāh*, **with Allah.**
- *tajhalūn* — lives with *you*, the crowd.

Knowledge belongs *there*. Ignorance sits *here*. And what stands in the **middle** of the verse, in the exact space between the divine knowledge and the human ignorance?

***Uballighukum*** — "I convey to you." **The messenger.** The carrier.

[PAUSE]

Look at what Allah has built. The entire verse is a map of revelation itself:

**KNOWLEDGE (with Allah) → CONVEYANCE (the messenger) → IGNORANCE (the people)**

The prophet is not standing *above* the people, lording knowledge over them. He is standing *between* — a bridge thrown across the chasm from the shore of perfect divine knowledge to the shore of human ignorance, carrying across it the one thing that can close the gap. The structure of the sentence **is** the structure of his mission. He confessed he doesn't own the knowledge (left bank). He named the ignorance honestly (right bank). And he placed himself, the carrier, in the only place a messenger ever stands — *in between.*

That is the proof. The verse doesn't just describe prophethood. Its very word-order *is* prophethood.

### Bridge

The grammar has shown us a man who walls off knowledge as God's alone, who defines himself purely as a carrier, who erases himself from his own sentence, and who names his people's condition not as a lack of facts but as a recklessness of the heart — all inside a verse whose architecture is a diagram of revelation.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how *we* are to live, in a world that demands we know everything and is dying of a particular kind of ignorance.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Unbearable Weight of Having to Know — and the Relief Hud Models

Let me begin where you actually live.

You wake up and before your feet hit the floor, the question machine is already running. *Will the diagnosis be okay? Will the job survive the layoffs? Will my child turn out alright? Will the marriage hold? Am I going to be okay?* We live drowning in a compulsion to know the outcome before it arrives. We refresh the screen. We replay the conversation. We run the scenario for the hundredth time, as if the hundred-and-first run will finally yield the future.

There is a name for the modern condition, and psychologists have studied it carefully: **intolerance of uncertainty.** It is now understood to sit at the root of much of our anxiety. The anxious mind is not primarily afraid of bad outcomes — it is afraid of *not knowing* the outcome. It would almost rather have certain bad news than uncertain good news, because the *not-knowing itself* is the torment. We treat the unknown as a problem to be solved, a debt we owe ourselves, a lock we are obligated to pick.

[PAUSE]

Now look again at Hud, standing in front of that crowd. They have weaponized exactly this. *When, Hud? Give us the date. Resolve the uncertainty or admit you're a fraud.* They are demanding he do the very thing the anxious heart demands of itself every morning: *produce the future. Know the unknowable. Close the open loop.*

And watch the peace in his answer. *Innamā l-ʿilmu ʿinda Llāh.* **The knowledge is only with Allah.**

Hear what that is, spiritually. It is not a shrug. It is not "I don't know" said in defeat. It is a man **handing the unbearable weight to the only One whose shoulders were built to carry it.** The knowledge of the appointed hour is not a debt Hud owes the crowd, and it is not a lock he is obligated to pick. It is *ʿinda Llāh* — held close, in God's keeping. And the instant Hud says that, you can feel a weight leave him. He is not responsible for knowing. He was never responsible for knowing. He is responsible only for carrying his message faithfully — and the outcome, the timing, the result, is *kept, by Another, who keeps it well.*

This is one of the most underrated mercies in all of Islam. We think *tawakkul*, reliance on Allah, is mainly about provision — *He'll send the rizq.* But there is a deeper layer: **tawakkul is also the permission to not know.** It is Allah saying, *the future is My department, not yours. I have not made your peace depend on your foreknowledge. I have made it depend on your faithfulness.* The verse *Lā taʿlamu nafsun mādhā taksibu ghadan* — "no soul knows what it will earn tomorrow" (31:34) — is not a threat. It is a *release.* You were never supposed to know tomorrow. The not-knowing is not a bug in your life. It is the designed shape of your servanthood.

The people of 'Aad could build anything. But they could not build a window into tomorrow — and rather than accept that with *ḥilm*, they raged against it with *jahl*. Their whole tragedy is the anxious modern heart writ large: *if I cannot control the outcome, I will at least demand to know it — and if I cannot know it, I will destroy the one who tells me it isn't mine to know.*

[PAUSE]

**So what changes tomorrow?** Here is the practical turn. The next time you feel the compulsive grip of *I have to know how this ends* — catch it, and say Hud's sentence over your own life: *Innamā l-ʿilmu ʿinda Llāh.* The knowledge of this is only with Allah. Not with me. And then ask the only question that is actually yours: *not "what will happen?" but "what is mine to faithfully carry today?"* Hud's serenity came from collapsing his entire responsibility down to one thing — *deliver the message* — and trusting the rest to its Keeper. Your serenity is in the same place. You are not the knower of outcomes. You are the carrier of your duty. Carry it well. Leave the date to Him.

### Theme Two: The Cleverest People Can Be the Most Ignorant — Because Jahl Is a Posture, Not a Gap

Let me start, again, with something you know.

We have all met them. The brilliant person who cannot see the obvious. The genius who is a fool about his own life. The most educated man in the room who behaves with the recklessness of a child. We've watched a society reach the summit of technical knowledge — able to map a genome, split an atom, model the weather a week out — and at the very same moment lose the ability to answer the simplest questions a human heart must answer: *what is this all for? how should I live? to Whom do I answer?* We have built the city of Iram, and forgotten the One who taught us to build.

This is the paradox the verse forces us to confront, and it is devastating once you see it. Allah is describing the *most advanced civilization of its age* — and the single word He chooses to define their condition is *tajhalūn.* **You are behaving in ignorance.** The people of the pillars. The carvers of mountains. *Ignorant.*

How? They had every fact. What did they lack?

Remember what *jahl* actually means. It is not the absence of information. Its opposite is *ḥilm* — gravity, forbearance, the self-possession to receive truth without rage. **Jahl is a posture of the soul, not a gap in the data.** It is the *recklessness* that demands proof while refusing to learn, that dares the unseen while mocking the messenger, that mistakes its own cleverness for wisdom and its own arrogance for strength. The 'Aad were not ignorant of facts. They were ignorant in the only way that destroys a person: they were *too proud to be taught.* When the truth came in the mouth of one of their own, their response was not *teach us more* but *prove it on our terms or die a liar.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what Hud actually says — and what he *doesn't.* He does not say *"you are a people who do not know"* — *lā taʿlamūn.* He says *"you are a people who **behave** in jahl"* — *tajhalūn,* in that ongoing, present, *active* tense. He is not insulting their intelligence. He is diagnosing their **conduct.** Their problem is not an empty head. It is a hardened posture — *I keep on acting recklessly toward what I have been shown.* The mufassirūn nail it: their jahl was *fī isti'jālihim al-ʿadhāb,* in their *rushing* to dare the punishment. The jahl was an *action,* a recklessness, a way of carrying themselves toward God — not a vacancy in their understanding.

This is the most sobering mirror in the verse, and we must let it turn toward us. Because we live in the most information-saturated age in human history. We can know anything in three seconds. And we should ask, honestly: *has all this knowing made us less jāhil — or more?* You can have a thousand facts about the deen and still carry yourself toward your Lord with the reckless posture of the 'Aad — demanding signs, setting conditions, postponing surrender until the proof arrives gift-wrapped on our terms. **Information is not the cure for jahl. Humility is.** The 'Aad prove that you can be a genius and a fool in the same breath, because the two qualities are measured on entirely different axes — one in the head, the other in the heart.

[PAUSE]

And here is the quiet hope folded inside the diagnosis. If jahl were a gap in the data, only the educated could escape it — and most of humanity would be lost. But because jahl is a *posture*, the unlettered shepherd who receives the truth with *ḥilm* — with humble, teachable gravity — has already escaped it, while the architect of Iram, armored in his certainty, is drowning in it. The door out of jahl is not a degree. It is a bowed head. It is reachable by everyone, this very moment.

**So what changes tomorrow?** Audit yourself not for what you *know* but for how you *carry* what you've been shown. When the truth reaches you — in an ayah, in a reminder, in the correction of someone who loves you — watch your posture. Is it *ḥilm* — *teach me, I receive this* — or is it the flicker of 'Aad — *prove it, justify it, who are you to tell me*? The cure for being a clever fool is not more cleverness. It is the willingness, today, to be taught.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

We can now see the whole verse standing as one structure. Let me show you the bones.

The verse is a perfect arc from one bank of the soul to the other:

> **ʿILM** (knowledge) → *ʿinda Llāh* (with Allah) → **UBALLIGHUKUM** (I convey) → *arākum* (I see you) → **TAJHALŪN** (you act in ignorance)

**Knowledge** sits at the start, and it lives *with Allah.* **Ignorance** sits at the end, and it lives *with the people.* And dead-center, in the exact middle, between the divine knowing and the human not-knowing, stands the one word *uballighukum* — **the messenger, conveying.** The verse is a diagram of revelation: truth descends from the bank of perfect knowledge, crosses the bridge of the faithful carrier, and reaches the bank of those who — if they will only trade their *jahl* for *ḥilm* — can be saved by it.

That is not decoration. That is the proof of what the verse *is.* Hud told them his entire role in one sentence, and the sentence *enacts* the role in its very shape.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a man who **walls off knowledge as God's** (*innamā*), **defines himself as a mere carrier** (*uballighu*), and **erases himself from his sentence** (*ursiltu*). The themes showed us why this is the only sane way to live: because the unbearable weight of *having to know the outcome* was never ours to carry — it is *ʿinda Llāh* — and because the deadliest ignorance is not a gap in the head but a *posture* of the heart that armors the clever against being taught.

Put them together and you get the whole teaching: **Knowledge belongs to Allah; the carrying belongs to you; and the only thing standing between divine knowledge and your salvation is whether you receive the carried message with the humble gravity of ḥilm — or dare it with the reckless cleverness of jahl.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What outcome am I currently demanding to *know* — and what would it feel like to say over it, honestly, *innamā l-ʿilmu ʿinda Llāh,* and hand the weight back to its Keeper?
2. Where in my life am I trying to be the *author* of the letter, when my actual job was only ever to be its faithful *carrier*?
3. When truth reaches me through a reminder or a correction, what is my reflexive posture — the bowed head of *ḥilm,* or the flicker of *prove-it* that destroyed the people of the pillars?
4. Have I confused my access to *information* with freedom from *jahl* — and could I be, like 'Aad, brilliant and reckless in the very same breath?
5. Could I, like Hud, say "I do not know" in a moment when knowing would win me the argument — for the sake of not claiming what was never mine?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**True knowledge is sealed with Allah alone, the messenger's whole dignity is in faithfully carrying what he was given and nothing more, and the real ruin of a people is never a lack of facts but the reckless ignorance of a heart too proud to be taught.**

### The Closing Invitation

Let something shift. Not a resolution to know more — you already know enough to drown in. An invitation to *carry* more faithfully and *demand* less arrogantly. To take the outcomes you have been clutching and lay them down where they have always belonged — *ʿinda Llāh.* To take the posture of the 'Aad, wherever it lives in you, and trade it — quietly, today — for a bowed and teachable head. You are not the Knower. You were never asked to be. You are the carrier. Carry well, and leave the rest in the hands of the One who keeps it.

### Du'ā

O Allah, the knowledge is Yours alone, *ʿindaka*, held close in Your keeping — so take from us the weight of needing to know what You did not give us, and teach us the peace of trusting its Keeper. Make us faithful carriers of the little You have placed in our hands, and save us from the *jahl* of the heart that demands proof while refusing to bow. Soften us with *ḥilm* before the truth, that we may not be among the people of the pillars who knew everything — except how to be taught. *Āmīn.*
