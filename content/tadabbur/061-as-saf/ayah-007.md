# While the Door Was Open

## The Crime of Manufacturing Darkness Against the Light

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first, three ways.

**The Arabic:**

> وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ ٱلْكَذِبَ وَهُوَ يُدْعَىٰٓ إِلَى ٱلْإِسْلَـٰمِ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

**The sound of it, roughly:**

> *Wa man aẓlamu mimmani-ftarā ʿala-llāhi-l-kadhiba wa huwa yudʿā ila-l-islām. Wa-llāhu lā yahdi-l-qawma-ẓ-ẓālimīn.*

**And the meaning, in plain English:**

> "And who is more wrong than the one who invents a lie against Allah — while he is being invited to surrender? And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people."

Now I want you to hear where this verse is standing.

Surah As-Saff is a short, intense surah. It opens the way several surahs open — with everything in the heavens and the earth declaring Allah's perfection. And then, almost immediately, it turns and asks the believers a question that stings to this day: *"Why do you say what you do not do?"* It is a surah about the gap. The gap between the word and the deed. The gap between the tongue and the hand.

Then it walks us through history. It reminds us of Musa, whose own people wounded him even after they knew he was sent to them. It reminds us of 'Isa, who stood before his people and told them plainly: I am a messenger of Allah to you, confirming what came before me, and announcing one who comes after me. And what did they do with that clarity? Some of them called it sorcery.

[PAUSE]

So picture the scene the original listener was inside of. Picture a people standing in the full daylight of a prophet's voice. The invitation is being spoken to them directly. The hand is being extended. The door is open and the light is pouring through it.

And in that exact moment — not before, not after, but *while the door stands open* — they sit down and begin to manufacture.

They begin to build something. To assemble a story. To cut and stitch a falsehood about the God who is, at that very second, calling them home.

That is the scene this ayah is photographing.

Here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows, because the whole verse turns on it: **We all assume the worst sin is the loud, dramatic one — the murder, the betrayal, the open war against the truth. But what if the worst injustice in the sight of Allah is something quieter? What if it is the specific crime of building a lie about God at the precise moment He is reaching for you?**

Stay with me. Because the Arabic of this verse does not merely *describe* that crime. It re-enacts it, word by word, so that you feel its texture in your own chest.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Aẓlamu* — and the question that has no answer

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In English, when we want to say something is the most extreme example of a thing, we usually just say it: *"This is the worst.* *No one is more cruel than him."* We make a flat statement and move on.

Arabic has a quieter, more devastating tool. Instead of *telling* you something is the most extreme, it can *ask* you a question that it knows you cannot answer. It is called the rhetorical interrogative — *istifhām* used not to seek information, but to corner you.

Think of how a parent does this. A child has done something thoughtless, and the parent doesn't say *"that was wrong."* The parent says, *"Who does that? Who treats their own brother like that?"* And the child stands there silent — because the question is built so that the only honest answer is *no one.* The silence is the verdict. The question has tried the case and closed it in four words.

Now look at how Allah opens this ayah.

> وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ
> *Wa man aẓlamu* — "And who is more wrong...?"

He does not say *"the one who invents a lie is a great wrongdoer."* He asks you: *who is more wrong than this person?* And He leaves the silence hanging.

The grammarians have a name for this exact construction when it appears in the Quran — and it appears again and again with this same opening, *wa man aẓlamu*, "and who is more wrong." It is what they call *istifhām inkārī* — a question whose hidden meaning is flat negation. As al-Jalalayn says simply on this verse: *wa man aẓlamu* means *lā aḥada aẓlam* — "there is no one more wrong." Al-Qurtubi confirms it in the same breath: *"there is no one more unjust than the one who invents a lie against Allah."*

[PAUSE]

So the verse has placed this person at the absolute floor. The very bottom of the scale of injustice. Not "very bad." Not "among the worst." **There is no one beneath him.**

Now sit with the word itself: **أَظْلَمُ**, *aẓlamu*.

It comes from the root **ẓ-l-m** (ظلم), and you must know what this root actually means, because we have flattened it in translation. We render it "wrongdoing" or "injustice," and that is correct — but it is the surface of the word.

At its root, *ẓulm* in the classical lexicons means **to put a thing in the place that is not its place.** To displace. To take something and set it where it does not belong. The Arabs would say a man committed *ẓulm* against the earth when he dug a well in the wrong spot. Injustice, in the Arabic imagination, is not primarily about cruelty — it is about **dislocation.** Putting the right thing in the wrong place.

And there is a second meaning braided into the very same root, and the early Arabic preserves both: *ẓulma* (ظُلمة) — **darkness.** The dark of a moonless night. The two meanings live in one root because, to the Arab ear, they were one idea: when you put things out of their place, you cannot see; and where you cannot see, you put things out of their place. Injustice and darkness are the same act named twice.

So when Allah asks *"who is more in darkness than this one?"* — *who has displaced reality more violently than this one?* — hold that, because it is going to detonate at the end of the verse. The person who fabricates a god, or fabricates lies *about* God, has taken the single most foundational truth in existence and **moved it out of its place.** He has taken the Light and tried to file it in the cabinet of darkness.

There is no greater dislocation possible. That is why the question has no answer.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Iftarā* — the lie you have to *build*

Now watch what kind of lying the verse names. Because the Quran has many words for falsehood, and the one chosen here is doing precise work.

> ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ ٱلْكَذِبَ
> *iftarā ʿala-llāhi-l-kadhib* — "he fabricated, against Allah, the lie."

Two words for the falsehood sit side by side here, and that should make us pause: *iftarā* (the verb) and *al-kadhib* (the noun, "the lie"). If *iftarā* simply meant "to lie," then *iftarā al-kadhib* would be a redundancy — "he lied the lie." Arabic does not waste words like that. So the two cannot be saying the same thing. The verb must be carrying something the noun is not.

Let me teach you the verb.

**ٱفْتَرَىٰ** is from the root **f-r-y** (فري). And here is what that root means in its plain, physical, everyday sense — the sense a desert craftsman would have known: *farā* means **to cut, to slice, to work a piece of leather or cloth, to fashion something by cutting.** When a man took a fresh hide and cut it and shaped it into a waterskin, the verb for what his hands were doing was from this very root. It is the language of **manufacture.** Of fabrication in the literal sense — making a fabric, working raw material into a constructed thing.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The Quran did not say this man *spoke* a lie. It said he **manufactured** one. He took raw material and he *cut it and shaped it and stitched it into a finished object* and he set the maker's mark of Allah upon it. *Against Allah.* He fabricated, and then he claimed the fabrication came from God.

This is what the verb form is telling us too. The corpus marks *iftarā* as **Form VIII** — the *ifta'ala* pattern. One of the things this form characteristically does in Arabic is turn an action *back upon the doer*, give it a sense of effortful, deliberate self-involvement. This is not a lie that slipped out. This is a lie the man *labored over.* He invested himself in its construction. He rolled up his sleeves and built it.

And now the two words make perfect sense. *Iftarā* is the **act of construction** — the cutting, the stitching, the manufacturing. *Al-kadhib* is the **finished product** — the lie itself, the assembled falsehood. "He manufactured — against Allah — the lie." The verse names both the factory and the thing it produced.

Look at what al-Jalalayn says this manufactured lie actually consists of: *"attributing a partner and a child to Him, and describing His signs as sorcery."* These are not impulsive slips of the tongue. Attributing a son to God is a *theology* — it has to be built, defended, elaborated. Calling revelation "sorcery" is a *whole explanatory framework* erected to file the light under darkness. This is constructed falsehood. Architecture, not accident.

And here the classical commentators differ slightly in where they lay the weight — a difference worth noticing, because it tells you how wide the verse's net is cast. Al-Jalalayn gives the full constellation: partner, child, *and* the branding of God's signs as sorcery. Al-Tabari, reading the same words, drives straight at one piece of it and makes it concrete: the fabricated lie, he says, is precisely the deniers' charge against the Prophet ﷺ — *"he is a sorcerer, and what he brought is sorcery."* That is not a random example. Look back two verses: the surah has just shown us 'Isa standing before his people with perfect clarity, and some of them answering, *"this is plain sorcery."* Al-Tabari is telling us the fabrication in our verse is the *same manufactured charge, rebuilt* — the identical factory now running against Muḥammad ﷺ. The lie that files the Light under darkness has a recurring blueprint, and the Quran is showing us the assembly line across the generations.

[PAUSE]

And here is where the first two elements lock together, and I want you to feel the click.

*Ẓulm* — we said — is putting a thing where it does not belong. And *iftirā'* — we now see — is **the labor of building a false place to put the truth in.** The fabricator does not merely misspeak. He constructs an entire wrong location, an elaborate false cabinet, and then he picks up Allah — the most real thing there is — and he files Him there. The manufacturing *is* the displacement. The two roots are describing one continuous act: building the darkness and then dragging the Light into it.

That is why no one is *aẓlamu* than him. He has not stumbled into the dark. **He has been manufacturing it.**

### Element Three: *Wa huwa yudʿā* — the word that turns a sin into a tragedy

Now we arrive at the word that breaks the heart. And I need to teach you one more piece of grammar, because everything depends on it.

In Arabic there is a construction called the **ḥāl** — the circumstantial clause. Its job is to freeze the action in a single frame and tell you *what else was happening at the exact same moment.* It is the difference between "he stole the money" and "he stole the money *while his mother was praying for him in the next room.*" The ḥāl does not add information. It adds **simultaneity.** It forces two pictures into one frame so you cannot look at the act without seeing its backdrop.

You spot it most often by a little word: **wa** followed immediately by a pronoun. *Wa huwa* — "while he..." *Wa hum* — "while they..." The moment you hear *wa huwa*, brace yourself, because Arabic is about to overlay a second image on top of the first.

Now read it:

> ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ ٱلْكَذِبَ **وَهُوَ يُدْعَىٰٓ إِلَى ٱلْإِسْلَـٰمِ**
> "He manufactured a lie against Allah — **while he is being invited to surrender.**"

[PAUSE]

Sit with that *while*.

The fabrication does not happen in a vacuum. It does not happen in a world where God is silent and the man is groping in the dark, doing his sincere best. It happens — *wa huwa* — at the precise moment the invitation is being extended to him. The two frames are forced into one. In one hand he holds the falsehood he is stitching together. And reaching toward his other hand, at that very second, is the open door of Islam.

This is what al-Qurtubi calls it directly — *hādhā taʿajjub*. This is an expression of **astonishment.** Astonishment at the one who rejects after the proofs have appeared plainly before him. The ḥāl is what makes it astonishing. Without it, you have a sinner. With it, you have a man slamming a door that is being held open for him — and reaching for a lie while mercy reaches for him.

Now look closer at the verb *yudʿā* — "he is being invited." Two features of it are doing quiet, enormous work.

**First: it is passive.** *Yudʿā* — "he is being called." The verse does not say *who* is calling. The Caller is unnamed. And in Arabic, when the actor is dropped from a passive verb like this, it is often because the actor is so obvious, so overwhelming, that naming Him would almost diminish Him. *Who* is doing the inviting? The whole surah has already told you — the One whom everything in the heavens and earth glorifies. The invitation does not come from a peer. It comes from the Source. And He has chosen to leave Himself unnamed in the grammar precisely so that the man's crime stands out in sharper relief: he is fabricating against *the very One* who, in the same breath, is calling him.

**Second: it is the imperfect tense — *yudʿā*, not *duʿiya*.** Arabic distinguishes the completed action (the past, *duʿiya*, "he was called") from the ongoing, unfinished one (the imperfect, *yudʿā*, "he is being called, he keeps being called"). And notice the deliberate contrast the verse builds: his fabrication, *iftarā*, is in the **perfect** tense — done, finished, a completed act of construction. But the invitation, *yudʿā*, is in the **imperfect** — ongoing, repeated, *still being extended.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the asymmetry? Do you see what Allah has done with two tenses?

The man's lie is finished. He built it and set it down and walked away from it: *done.* But the invitation is **not** finished. It is still open. It is still being spoken. *Yudʿā* — the calling has not stopped even after the lie was completed. The door he is fabricating reasons to slam is, grammatically, **still open as the verse speaks.**

This is the mercy hidden inside the rebuke. The very tense of the verb refuses to close the door on him.

And now — the destination. *Yudʿā ila-l-**islām***. He is being invited to **Islam.** And you must hear that final word with its root meaning, or you will miss the whole point. **Islam** is from the root **s-l-m** (سلم) — the root of *salām*, peace; of *salāma*, wholeness, soundness, safety; of being *intact* and *unbroken*. Al-Muyassar explains the invitation as a call *"to enter into Islam and to devote worship purely to Allah alone."* To surrender — yes — but surrender into **peace.** Into wholeness. Into the one place where a soul is finally *intact.*

So lay the full picture out, now that every word is open:

A man sits in the dark cabinet he has built with his own hands — *ẓulm*, displacement, darkness. He is laboring over a manufactured lie — *iftirā'*, construction, fabrication. And the whole time — *wa huwa* — a voice he refuses to name is calling him, and has not stopped calling him — *yudʿā*, ongoing, present — into the light, into safety, into the one wholeness his soul was made for — *al-islām*, peace.

[PAUSE]

That is the most unjust thing a human being can do. Not to sin in the dark where no light reaches. But to **manufacture darkness while standing in an open doorway of light.**

### Bridge

We have watched three words do their work. *Aẓlamu* placed this man at the absolute floor of injustice and told us that injustice *is* darkness, the displacement of what is real. *Iftarā* showed us that his crime was not a slip but a *construction* — a fabricated, labored-over lie wearing God's name. And *wa huwa yudʿā* froze the whole thing in a single unbearable frame: the lie being built while the invitation is being extended, the door held open by the very tense of the verb.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the texture of our own lives. Because you and I are not, most of us, sitting in seventh-century Arabia inventing a theology of partners for God. So we are tempted to think this verse is about *them.* It is not. The grammar we just uncovered is a mirror. Let me hold it up.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The sin that needs a lit room

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a particular kind of guilt that we all know — the guilt of doing something wrong *when we knew better.* Not the wrong we did in ignorance. We forgive ourselves that one quickly; we say, *I didn't know.* The wrong that haunts us is the one we did **with the lights on.** The one where someone was kind to us right before we betrayed them. The one where the warning was clear, the off-ramp was right there, the better path was visible — and we took the other road anyway.

Think about why that specific memory burns hotter than the others. It is the same wrong action. The harm done might even be smaller. So why does it sit heavier on the chest?

Because **proximity to the open door is itself part of the crime.**

We feel this instinctively in human relationships. If a stranger is cold to you, it stings a little. If the friend who just told you *"I'm always here for you"* is cold to you an hour later, it wounds — because the kindness was *right there.* The closeness of the offered good makes the chosen wrong heavier. Not because the act changed. Because the act now stands against a backdrop of light.

[PAUSE]

This is exactly the architecture of our ayah. The verse does not call this man the most unjust because of the *content* of his lie alone. It builds his condemnation out of the *ḥāl* — the simultaneity. *While he is being invited.* The backdrop of light is what makes the darkness he manufactures so total.

And here is where it stops being about them and becomes about us. Every one of us is, right now, *being invited.* The invitation in our lives is not loud. It rarely is. It is the quiet pull toward the prayer you keep postponing. It is the verse that landed too precisely yesterday to be coincidence. It is the moment of clarity at 3 a.m. when you knew exactly what you were doing wrong and exactly what you should do instead. It is *yudʿā* — present tense, ongoing, still being extended.

And the question the verse puts to us is not *"have you sinned?"* Everyone has sinned. The question is sharper and more uncomfortable: **What are you building in the moments when you can feel the door being held open?**

Because there is a version of all of us that, when the clarity comes — when we *feel* the invitation — does not surrender. Instead we get to work. We start manufacturing. We *iftarā* — we construct. We build the elaborate justification for why now isn't the time. We stitch together the theology of "later." We cut and shape the comfortable lie — *"a God this merciful wouldn't really mind,"* *"I'll be a different person once life calms down,"* *"that pull I felt wasn't really from Him."* We are not stumbling in the dark. We are doing skilled construction work in a lit room.

[PAUSE]

That is the warning, and it is meant for the believer reading this verse, not only the disbeliever it first described. The most dangerous moment in your spiritual life is not the moment of darkness. It is the moment of **light that you decide to argue with.** The clarity you choose to fabricate your way around.

So what does this change about tomorrow? It is very concrete. **The next time you feel the invitation — that quiet, specific pull toward something you know is right — notice the instant your mind begins to build the case against it.** That construction site, in that exact moment, is the thing this verse is warning you about. The fabrication is not in the sin you commit an hour later. It is in the lie you start manufacturing *the second the door opens.* Catch yourself at the construction site. That is where the whole battle is.

### Theme Two: Why the lights stay off — and who turned them off

Now let me take you to the end of the verse, because it raises a question that troubles many sincere hearts, and the answer is one of the most merciful things in the Quran once you see it.

> وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ
> "And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people."

Read coldly, this can frighten a person. *Allah does not guide them?* Does that mean He withholds guidance arbitrarily? That some people are simply locked out, the door barred from the divine side, no matter what they do?

Let me address that head-on, the way the believer already in pain over their sins needs it addressed.

Here is the human experience that unlocks it. Have you ever spent a long time in a dark room — and then walked into bright sunlight? What happens? You cannot see. The light is *fully present* — pouring in, abundant, free — and yet you are blind in it. You throw your hand up. You squint. You see *less* in the full light than you saw in the dim room. Why? Not because the light failed you. Because **you spent so long in the dark that your eyes can no longer receive it.**

[PAUSE]

Now go back to the root we opened at the very beginning. *Ẓulm* — injustice — is the twin of *ẓulma* — darkness. The verse does not say *"Allah does not guide people"* arbitrarily. It says *"Allah does not guide **the wrongdoing** people"* — *al-qawma-ẓ-ẓālimīn* — the people characterized by *ẓulm*, the people who have been **living in the darkness they built.**

Do you see what the verse is actually telling you? The guidance — the light — is not being withheld out of caprice. Look at the whole ayah as one motion: the man is being invited, *yudʿā*, the light is pouring toward him, the door is open, the calling has not stopped. The light is *fully present.* And yet he does not see it. Why? Al-Muyassar gives the answer with beautiful precision: Allah does not grant *tawfīq* — does not lead them to *"what contains their success"* — to **those who have wronged themselves through disbelief and association.** The condition that blinds them is one they manufactured. *Ẓālimīn* — they are the ones who built the dark room. And now they stand in the doorway of the sun and cannot receive it.

This is not a locked door. **It is a blinded eye.** And the eye was blinded by all the time spent in the cabinet of fabrication.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how merciful this actually is, because it inverts the fear. The verse is *not* saying *"some souls are barred from God's light."* It is saying: **the light never stops shining. Allah never stops inviting** — that is the whole point of *yudʿā* being present-tense and ongoing. What changes is not the light. What changes is the *capacity to receive it* — and that capacity is exactly what *ẓulm* destroys. Every act of manufacturing darkness dilates the pupil a little more in the wrong direction, accustoms the eye a little more to the dark, until standing in full sunlight one sees nothing.

So the sentence *"Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people"* is not a wall built to keep people out. It is a **diagnosis** of what the wrongdoer has done to his own eyes. The tragedy is not that the light is absent. The tragedy is that it is fully, mercifully present — *and he cannot see it, because of the darkness he himself built.* (Al-Jalalayn glosses *al-ẓālimīn* here with a single word — *al-kāfirīn*, the disbelievers — so that "the wrongdoing people" is not a vague category but precisely those who chose the fabrication over the invitation. *Ẓulm* names the act; *kufr* names the choice underneath it.)

And watch what comes next in the surah, because Ibn Kathir reads these verses as one breath. Immediately after this ayah comes: *"They intend to extinguish the light of Allah with their mouths — but Allah will perfect His light, however much the disbelievers hate it"* (61:8). Hold the two together. The very mouth that *manufactured* the lie in verse 7 is the mouth that, in verse 8, is now puffing at the sun trying to blow it out. The fabrication and the extinguishing are the same organ doing the same futile work. And the answer the surah gives is not that the light might survive — it is that the light *will be perfected,* completed, brought to its fullness, **regardless.** The man blinded himself; he never so much as dimmed the sun. That is the mercy folded into the warning: the darkness he builds is real, but it is only ever local — a cabinet he shuts around his own eyes, never a shadow he can throw across the light itself.

And this circles us all the way back to the surah's opening wound: *"Why do you say what you do not do?"* That, too, is a small fabrication. A small lie manufactured between the tongue and the hand. A little darkness built in the gap. And the surah's warning is that these small constructions are not harmless. Every fabricated word, every lit-room lie we stitch together, is a few more minutes spent in the dark — a little more adjustment of the eye away from the sun.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? This: **stop praying only for guidance, and start guarding your eyes' capacity to receive it.** The light is not the scarce thing — the verse promises it is always being extended, *yudʿā*. The scarce thing is a heart still able to see when the light arrives. And what protects that capacity is refusing the small fabrications — the little justifications, the comfortable lies, the said-but-not-done. Every honest moment, every time you *refuse* to start building the case against the truth you feel, you keep your eyes adjusted to the light. You stay a person who, when the door opens, can actually walk through it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Watch how the whole verse is built, now that we have walked through it. It is a single, tightening motion, and every part is the same insight seen from a different side.

It opens with a question that has no answer — *who is more wrong?* — and the word *aẓlamu* tells us that injustice *is* darkness, the displacement of what is real out of its rightful place. Then it shows us the displacement in action: *iftarā* — not a spoken slip but a **manufactured** lie, cut and stitched and stamped with God's name, a whole false room built to file the Light in. Then it freezes the construction in a single frame with *wa huwa yudʿā* — the lie being built *while the invitation is being extended*, the door held open by the very tense of the verb, the Caller too obvious to name. And then it closes by telling us why the man, standing in that open doorway, sees nothing: *Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people* — not because the light fails, but because the eye that lived in the dark can no longer receive it.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same truth from two directions. The grammar said: *this man manufactures darkness in an open doorway of light.* The human heart said: *the heaviest sins are the ones we commit with the lights on, the invitations we argue with, the clarity we fabricate our way around.* They are one verse. They are one mirror.

### Questions to carry

1. When the quiet invitation comes — the pull toward the prayer, the verse that lands too precisely, the 3 a.m. clarity — what do I usually do in the very next instant? Do I surrender, or do I start building?

2. What is the comfortable lie I have manufactured and stamped with God's name? The "a merciful God wouldn't mind," the "later, when life calms down" — what have I cut and stitched and set down as if it came from Him?

3. The verse says the invitation is ongoing, present-tense, still being extended. Do I actually believe the door is still open for me — or have I quietly decided it has closed?

4. Where, in my life, am I standing in full light and seeing nothing? And how long did I spend in the dark to get there?

5. The surah opened by asking why we say what we do not do. What is the gap between my tongue and my hand right now — and is it widening or closing?

### One-sentence distillation

The gravest injustice is not sinning in the dark, but manufacturing darkness while standing in an open doorway of light — and the only thing that ever bars us from guidance is the blindness we build with our own hands.

### The closing invitation

So here is what I would invite you to let shift. Not guilt — the verse is not trying to crush you; its central verb, *yudʿā*, is present-tense precisely because the door has not closed. The invitation is to **vigilance at the construction site.** To learn to recognize the exact moment your mind begins to build the case against a truth you can feel. That moment — not the sin an hour later — is where this verse lives. Catch yourself there. Set down the tools. And turn, instead, toward the door that is, as the grammar promises, *still open.*

### Du'a

O Allah, You whom everything in the heavens and the earth glorifies — keep our eyes adjusted to Your light, so that when You call, and You have never stopped calling, we can still see the door. Do not let us become skilled builders of comfortable darkness. And when we feel the pull of Your invitation, give us the mercy to surrender into it before we manufacture a single reason not to. *Āmīn.*
