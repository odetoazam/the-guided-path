# When You Ask "How" and Allah Answers "Who"

## INTRODUCTION

Let me put the words in front of you first. Sit with them before we go anywhere.

> قَالَ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِى غُلَـٰمٌ وَقَدْ بَلَغَنِىَ ٱلْكِبَرُ وَٱمْرَأَتِى عَاقِرٌ ۖ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ ٱللَّهُ يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَآءُ

*Qāla rabbi annā yakūnu lī ghulāmun wa qad balaghaniya-l-kibaru wa-mraʾatī ʿāqir. Qāla kadhālika-llāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ.*

"He said, 'My Lord, how can there be a boy for me, when old age has already reached me and my wife is barren?' He said, 'Thus — Allah does whatever He wills.'"

Now let me rebuild the moment, because if you don't feel where this man is standing, the ayah will pass through you as a piece of information instead of landing as what it is.

This is Zakariyya. An old man. A prophet. A guardian of the temple. And for his entire adult life he has carried a private ache that he has never let become loud — the ache of a man with no child, no heir, no one to carry the trust after him. He has watched his own body do what bodies do: slow, stiffen, grow heavy. He has watched his wife pass through the years that close the door on motherhood. Somewhere along the way, quietly, without ever announcing it, he had filed the verdict. *Not for us. That season is over.*

And then something cracks the world open. Just before this ayah, he had seen Maryam — under his own care — receiving fruit out of season, sustenance that arrived by no road he could trace. He asks her, "Maryam, where is this from?" And she says, "It is from Allah. Indeed Allah provides to whom He wills without measure." [PAUSE]

Something in that answer lit a fire in an old man's chest. *Without measure.* He goes to his Lord — in secret, the Qur'an says, a hidden call — and he asks for the thing he had stopped asking for. And the answer comes back, swift and total: a son. A name already chosen — Yahya. And not merely a son: the angels announced him, as Ibn Kathir relays the verse before this one, as one who would confirm a word from Allah, a *sayyid* — noble, a leader among his people — and *ḥaṣūr*, a man who masters his own desires, a prophet from among the righteous (3:39). In the parallel telling in Surah Maryam, Allah even calls the name itself unprecedented — *lam najʿal lahu min qablu samiyyā*, "never before had anyone been given that name" (19:7).

So here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows. The man got exactly what he prayed for. The "yes" arrived, immediate and overflowing.

**So why is his very first word a question of disbelief?**

"How can there be a boy for me?"

He prayed for it. He received it. And his instant, instinctive reflex is to look at the gift and say — *but that's not possible.* Stay with that. There is something in this ayah about the gap between what we beg Allah for and what we actually believe He can do. And Allah's answer to that gap is one of the shortest, most decisive sentences in the entire Qur'an. Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Annā* — The Question That Isn't Quite Doubt

Before we touch the word, let me teach you something about questions, because in your own language you already know this and have never noticed it.

There are two completely different things hiding inside the English word "how." Imagine a friend tells you she just paid off her entire mortgage. You can say "*How?*" — meaning, *by what method, walk me through the mechanism, I want the steps.* That's a question hunting for a process. But now imagine she tells you she survived a car that rolled four times and walked away without a scratch. You say "*How…*" — and that's not a request for the physics of crumple zones. That's wonder. That's a sound the soul makes when it stands in front of something too big to file. Same word. Completely different organ producing it.

Arabic does not blur these two. Arabic has a word for the cold, procedural "how" — *kayfa* (كيف). And it has a different word, the word Zakariyya actually uses here:

> أَنَّىٰ — *annā*

Now look at it. Don't explain it yet — just look. *Annā.* The classical scholars, including al-Qurtubi right here on this ayah, tell us *annā bi-maʿnā kayfa* — that *annā* carries the meaning of "how." True. But it carries more, and the "more" is the whole point. *Annā* in the Arabic tongue also means *min ayna* — "from where? from which direction? whence?" It is a "how" with a horizon built into it. It doesn't just ask about method; it asks about *source* — from what conceivable direction could such a thing even come toward me?

Let's sit with this. When Zakariyya says *annā yakūnu lī ghulām*, he is not standing there demanding Allah explain the biology. He is a man turning his head in every direction — at his own aged body, at his wife, at the empty years — and finding no road on the map by which this child could arrive. *From where? From which direction could a son possibly come to a man like me?*

Notice something. That is not the question of a skeptic. That is the question of a man overwhelmed. And the tafsir confirms exactly this reading: al-Muyassar describes him as speaking *faraḥan mutaʿajjiban* — "in joy and astonishment." Al-Jalalayn says something even more striking — that Allah *inspired* the question in him so that the answer could be given, *because his soul had begun to ache with longing for the thing he'd been promised.* [PAUSE]

Do you feel the shift? We tend to read "how can this be?" as wavering faith. But the very letters he chose tell us this is not doubt clawing at the promise. This is wonder so large it spills out as a question. His tongue is asking "from where," while his heart already knows the answer is "from Him."

Here is what this single word reveals, and then we'll move: **there is a kind of "how" that is not the enemy of faith but its overflow — the gasp of a heart that believes more than its circumstances can hold.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Balaghaniya-l-kibar* — When Old Age Comes Hunting for You

Now I want to show you something in the grammar that you would walk straight past in translation, and that the classical scholars stopped and circled in red.

First, the concept. In every language, the same fact can be said two ways, and the *direction* of the sentence carries a hidden meaning. "I caught the bus" versus "the bus caught me." "I reached the deadline" versus "the deadline reached me." When *you* are the subject — *I reached* — you are the actor, in control, doing the reaching. But flip it — *it reached me* — and suddenly you are the object, the target, the one being arrived at. The thing is now the hunter. You are the prey.

Hold that, and look at what Zakariyya says:

> وَقَدْ بَلَغَنِىَ ٱلْكِبَرُ — *wa qad balaghaniya-l-kibar*

The natural way for a human to describe aging is: *balaghtu-l-kibara* — "I have reached old age." I, the subject, arrived at that stage. That's how we all speak. But that is **not** what the verse says. The verse says *balaghaniya-l-kibar* — literally, "**old age has reached me.**" *Al-kibar*, old age, is the grammatical subject — the one doing the verb. And Zakariyya — the *nī*, the "me" attached to the verb — is the object. The thing being reached.

Al-Baghawi pauses right here and names it precisely. He says: *hādhā min al-maqlūb* — "this is an inversion." The expected order has been deliberately turned around. He explains: it should be "I reached old age," but it is phrased as old age reaching *him* — the way you'd say *balaghanī-l-jahd*, "the hardship reached me," when you mean *I am sunk in hardship, it has come and overtaken me.*

Now feel what that inversion does. [PAUSE]

When you say "I reached old age," you are still the one walking. You have agency. You arrived somewhere. But when you say "**old age reached me**," old age becomes a thing that came *for* you, that tracked you down, that closed the distance while you stood still, and finally *arrived at your door* and let itself in. Notice the verb is *balagha* — the same root used for an arrow reaching its target, a message reaching its destination, a traveler reaching the city. Old age, in Zakariyya's mouth, is something that has completed its journey **toward him.** It found him. It is here. The pursuit is over and it won.

This is a man describing himself as a target that has already been struck. Stay with how heavy that is. He is not saying "I have grown old" — a neutral fact. He is saying *the thing that ends fruitfulness has hunted me down and reached me.* He is at the absolute floor of human possibility. Al-Jalalayn even hands us the figures the commentators carried — Zakariyya at a hundred and twenty years, his wife at ninety-eight: this is not a door quietly drifting shut but one bolted for decades. And then, as if the verse wants to seal the door completely —

> وَٱمْرَأَتِى عَاقِرٌ — "and my wife is barren."

One more word here, and listen to it with your ear, not just your mind: *ʿāqir.* The root — ʿayn, qāf, rāʾ — is a hard, blocked, closing-off cluster of sounds. The same root family gives Arabic the sense of *ʿaqr* — to wound, to hamstring, to cut something down at the root so it cannot rise. *ʿĀqir* doesn't merely mean "she had no children." It names a closed root, a tree that does not bear, a thing cut off at the source. The word itself sounds like a door swinging shut.

And here al-Tabari catches something a translation buries completely. *ʿĀqir* is not a feminine word. He notes that the Arabs said *imraʾatun ʿāqir* — a barren woman — but they also said *rajulun ʿāqir*, a barren *man*, and he cites the old line of the poet ʿĀmir ibn al-Ṭufayl who uses the word of himself. Sit with what that does to the verse. Zakariyya names the barrenness aloud only of his wife — *ʿāqir* — yet by his own preceding words he has already confessed his own: *old age has reached me.* The very word he lays on her is the word the language would have let him level at himself. Two closed roots on one tree, and he has counted them both.

So look at what Zakariyya has built in half a verse. Two locks on the same door. The man: *old age has reached me* — fruitfulness over. The woman: *barren* — the root itself closed. He has, with total honesty, sealed every human exit. [PAUSE]

And here's what this reveals — hold it, because the second half of the ayah is going to detonate against exactly this: **Zakariyya has just given the most complete, most articulate, most grammatically precise description of impossibility that a human being can construct. He has done the math perfectly. Every variable accounted for. The answer is zero.** And he is about to be shown that he was solving the wrong equation entirely.

### Element Three: *Kadhālika* — The Answer That Refuses the Question

Here is the most important thing in this verse, and everything has been preparing you to see it.

A man has just asked "how?" He has laid out, in careful sequence, *why* it cannot be: aged body, barren wife, closed door. He has handed his Lord a fully reasoned proof of impossibility. Every honest instinct in us expects the response to engage that proof. To explain. To say: *I will restore your youth, I will open her womb, here is the mechanism by which the zero becomes one.*

Now read the answer Allah gives:

> قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ ٱللَّهُ يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَآءُ — "He said: **Thus. Allah does whatever He wills.**"

Stop. Look at what just happened. [PAUSE]

He asked *how.* And the answer **does not contain a how.** There is no mechanism here. No biology. No step-by-step. No "I will do this and then that." The answer begins with a single word — *kadhālika*, "thus, like that, just so" — and that word doesn't describe a process. It points. It is the verbal equivalent of a hand gesturing and saying: *it will be — like that.* It refuses, completely and deliberately, to enter the conversation about method that Zakariyya started.

Why? Because the question "how" assumes the wrong thing. "How" is a question you ask about *causes* — about which lever pulls which gear. It is the right question if the world runs on machinery and even Allah must work the machinery. But that is precisely the assumption being dismantled. *Kadhālika* sweeps the entire machinery off the table and replaces it with two words: ***Allāhu yafʿal.*** **Allah does.**

Al-Muyassar glosses the closing phrase in exactly this register: Allah does whatever He wills *min al-afʿāl al-ʿajība al-mukhālifa lil-ʿāda* — "of the wondrous acts that run against the customary order." Hear the precision of that. Not acts that work the ordinary causes faster, or harder, or better — acts that step *outside* the *ʿāda* altogether, the habitual chain of cause and effect we mistake for the floor of reality. Zakariyya was reasoning from the *ʿāda*: old men and barren women do not, in the ordinary run of things, have sons. He was right about the *ʿāda*. He was simply wrong to think the *ʿāda* was the limit of what could happen.

And notice — *this is where the Name lands.* Through the whole encounter the speech has been carried by the angels, by the voice of the messenger. Now, at the decisive moment, the verse turns and plants the very Name of God: ***Allāh.*** Not "He." Not "your Lord." The full, total Name — placed exactly where a human had piled up his impossibilities. The classical commentators note the slight question of *who* speaks this line — al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi report a difference: was Zakariyya's "*rabbi*" addressed directly to his Lord, or, as al-Kalbi held, to the angel Jibril ("my master")? Either way, the answer that returns does not stay at the level of the messenger. It rises straight to the Source: *Allah does whatever He wills.*

Let's go deeper, because the structure of that closing phrase is itself the teaching. *Yafʿalu mā yashāʾ* — "He does whatever He wills." Two verbs, both in the present-continuous tense, both Form I — the plainest, most foundational form of the verb, no causative scaffolding, no intensifier needed. *Yafʿal* — He does. *Yashāʾ* — He wills. And the object between them is *mā* — "whatever," utterly unrestricted, unbounded, no limit named.

Here is the quiet thunder of it. For Allah, there is **no gap between willing and doing.** For you and me, the distance between "I want this" and "this exists" is paved with mechanism — with steps, resources, conditions, biology, time, luck. That whole distance is what Zakariyya was describing when he listed his age and his wife's barrenness: he was mapping the *gap*, and finding it uncrossable. But the answer tells him there is no gap. *He wills — He does.* The willing **is** the doing. There is no machinery in between for old age and barrenness to obstruct. Al-Jalalayn fixes this precisely in his gloss of the closing phrase: *lā yuʿjizuhu ʿanhu shayʾ* — "nothing can render Him incapable of it." Hear the exactness of that. Not that the obstacles are large but surmountable — that for Him there is no category of obstacle at all. The *mā*, the "whatever," has no exceptions quietly written into its margins.

So here is what this final phrase reveals, and it is the whole ayah in one line: **Zakariyya asked a question about mechanism, and Allah answered with a question about identity. He asked "how can this happen?" — and was told, in effect, "you are asking the wrong question. Don't ask *how.* Ask *Who.*"** [PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, standing together. *Annā* — a wonder so large it came out as a question. *Balaghaniya-l-kibar* — a man who saw himself as a target already struck, every human door honestly sealed. *Kadhālika* — an answer that refused his entire frame, swept away the machinery, and named the Doer.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way you and I actually live inside our own impossibilities.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Audit We Run the Moment After We Pray

Let me start where you live.

You have done this. I have done this. You want something badly — a healing, a marriage, a way out of a debt, a child, a door to open in a closed career. And you pray for it. You actually ask Allah for it, sincerely, maybe with tears. And then — watch yourself, because this is fast and almost invisible — the moment after you pray, a second voice starts up. And that second voice does not pray. That second voice *audits.*

It says: *but realistically.* It says: *let's look at the numbers.* It says: *I'm too old for that now. The market's closed. The diagnosis was clear. The years for that are behind me. Be reasonable.* And within about ten seconds of having asked the Lord of the heavens and the earth for something, you have quietly talked yourself back down to what your own resources can produce. You asked the Unlimited, and then you went and checked your own bank balance to see if the request was affordable.

That is *exactly* what Zakariyya does in this ayah — and the Qur'an preserves it, I believe, because it is so deeply, achingly human. He prayed. The "yes" came. And his first reflex was to run the audit: *aged body — checked. Barren wife — checked. Conclusion: this does not compute.* He wasn't being faithless. He was being a careful, honest accountant of his own poverty. He counted what he had, and what he had was zero.

Here is the thing we have to see. **His arithmetic was completely correct. And completely irrelevant.** Every number he listed was true. He really was old. She really was barren. He didn't get a single fact wrong. His error wasn't in the data — it was in thinking the data was the deciding factor at all. He was solving the equation of *his resources*, when the equation that actually governs reality is *Allah's will.* And in that equation, his age and her barrenness are not even variables. They contribute nothing and they obstruct nothing. *Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ.*

Let the paradox sit, because we shouldn't rush past it: the very completeness of his impossibility is what makes the gift unmistakable. If a young, healthy couple has a child, the cause hides Allah — "well, that's just biology." But when the gift arrives precisely where every human cause has been *honestly and totally exhausted* — old man, barren woman, sealed door — then the gift carries a signature no one can forge. It announces its Source. The impossibility isn't an obstacle to the miracle. **The impossibility is the miracle's signature.** [PAUSE]

And notice how al-Jalalayn reads the *kadhālika* itself: it means Allah's creating *ghulāman minkumā* — "a boy *from the two of you.*" Stay with that little word *minkumā*. The answer is not "I will give you a different, fertile wife"; it is not "I will set this aged couple aside and work around them." The son comes *from the two of them* — from exactly this hundred-and-twenty-year-old man and this barren wife, in precisely the bodies that filed the verdict of impossibility. Allah does not route around the sealed door. He brings the gift *through* it. The closed root is the very place the new branch grows.

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the specific, practical thing. Catch the auditor. The next time you ask Allah for something and feel that second voice start adding up your liabilities — *too old, too late, too broke, too far gone* — recognize what it is. It is Zakariyya's reflex. And it is not wrong about the facts; it is wrong about who decides. The discipline is not to lie to yourself about your circumstances. Zakariyya didn't deny he was old. The discipline is to finish the sentence the way the ayah finishes it: *yes, all of that is true — and Allah does whatever He wills.* State your poverty honestly, then refuse to let it be the verdict. Let Him be the verdict.

### Theme Two: When You Ask "How," He Answers "Who"

Now the deeper movement. And I want to begin again in your life, not in the verse.

Think about what actually robs you of peace when you're waiting on something impossible. It is almost never the *outcome* itself. It's the *how.* Your mind doesn't sit still; it runs scenarios. *Okay, but how would this even work out? Where would the money come from? Which door could possibly open? What's the path?* And when you can't trace a path — when every route on your mental map dead-ends — your heart sinks. We have quietly tied our peace to our ability to *see the mechanism.* If I can imagine how, I can hope. If I can't imagine how, I despair.

That is the trap Zakariyya is standing in when he asks *annā* — "from which direction?" He is scanning the map for a road, and there is no road. And as long as your peace depends on finding the road, you will lose your peace every time the road is hidden — which, for the things that matter most, is most of the time.

Now watch how Allah breaks the trap. He does not give Zakariyya the road. Read it again: He never answers the "how." He doesn't say "I'll make you young again" or "I'll heal her womb" — even though that's presumably what happened. He withholds the mechanism entirely. And that withholding is *mercy*, not evasion. Because if Allah had given him a "how," Zakariyya's faith would have rested on the *how* — on the mechanism being plausible. Instead, Allah moves his faith off the mechanism altogether and rests it on the Name. *Kadhālika — Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ.* You don't need the road. You need to know the One who doesn't travel roads.

Let this land slowly. **When you ask "how will this ever work out?", a "how" is not actually what your soul needs — because any "how" Allah could give you would itself depend on a hundred further "hows" you can't see.** A mechanism only relocates the uncertainty; it never removes it. The only thing that actually removes it is the One for whom willing and doing are a single act, with no mechanism in between for circumstance to break. The question was never "how." The question was always "Who." And the moment you know the Who, the how becomes His concern, not yours.

This is the same root, by the way, that runs through the whole Qur'an's language about creation — *idhā qaḍā amran fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu kun fa-yakūn* — "when He decrees a matter, He only says to it 'Be,' and it is." No process. No gap. No machinery. Just will, and existence answering. Zakariyya's son is a small enactment of that cosmic principle, planted into one old man's despair so that you and I could see it at human scale.

And notice the tenderness in *how* it's enacted. Allah doesn't rebuke Zakariyya for asking. The angel doesn't say "how dare you doubt." Al-Jalalayn even suggests Allah *inspired* the question so the magnificent answer could be given. Your "how" is not a sin. It's an invitation. Allah lets you voice your impossibility in full — He lets you spell out every locked door — *and then* He answers, not by arguing with your facts, but by reminding you whose hand is on the lock. [PAUSE]

So, practically, for tomorrow. When you find your mind grinding on the *mechanism* — replaying how it could possibly work out, exhausting yourself searching for a route that isn't there — try this. Stop searching for the road. Turn the sentence around. Don't ask "how will this happen?" Ask "Who is the One I'm asking?" Move your attention from the gap between you and the outcome, to the One for whom there is no gap. The road was never your job. *Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ.* Let Him keep the map.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this single verse mirror each other, now that we've done the work.

The first half is **Zakariyya's whole world** — and it is built entirely out of *causes.* "A boy for me" — *but* old age has reached me, *and* my wife is barren. It is a sentence made of mechanisms, every clause a gear, and all the gears are jammed. It is the human posture: reading reality off our own resources, doing honest arithmetic, and arriving at zero.

The second half is **Allah's answer** — and it contains no causes at all. *Kadhālika — Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ.* Not one mechanism. Just the Name, and the will, and the doing, with nothing in between.

And do you see — the linguistic discovery and the thematic discovery are the *same* discovery, approached from two directions. The grammar of *balaghaniya-l-kibar* — old age as the hunter, the man as the struck target — is the grammar of a human being who experiences himself as the *object* of forces beyond his control. And the grammar of *yafʿalu mā yashāʾ* — Allah as pure subject, pure actor, with an unrestricted "whatever" for an object — is the grammar of the only One who is never anyone's object. The whole verse is the meeting of those two grammars: the passive man, reached and overtaken by his circumstances, standing in front of the absolute Subject who is reached and overtaken by nothing. And the gift — Yahya — is born precisely in the space where the first grammar surrenders to the second.

The word that bridges them is *kadhālika.* "Thus." It is Allah's refusal to let the conversation stay in the realm of mechanism. It is the hinge on which the whole verse turns from human impossibility to divine ease.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. What is the thing you stopped praying for — not because you decided it was forbidden, but because you quietly decided it was *impossible?* What "zero" did your own arithmetic hand you, that you accepted as the verdict?

2. When you pray, can you hear the second voice — the auditor — start up the moment you finish? What does it count? And have you ever once told it: *yes, all true, and Allah does whatever He wills?*

3. Where in your life are you exhausting yourself searching for the *how* — the road, the mechanism, the path — when the thing actually asked of you is to know the *Who?*

4. Is your peace currently resting on a mechanism you can see — or on a Name?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you stand at the edge of the impossible and your honest arithmetic returns zero, the answer is not a better calculation but a different question: stop asking *how this could be* and remember *Who is being asked* — for between His willing and His doing there is no gap for your impossibility to fit inside.

### The Closing Invitation

I am not going to ask you to pretend your circumstances aren't real. Zakariyya didn't. He named his age. He named her barrenness. He told the truth about every locked door. The invitation is not to lie about your poverty — it's to stop treating it as the final word. To let your honest "I am old, she is barren" be followed, the way the verse follows it, by *Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashāʾ.* To carry one impossibility back to Him this week — the one you'd quietly buried — and lay it down again, not because the facts have changed, but because you've remembered Whose hand is on the lock.

### A Du'a

O Allah, for whom there is no distance between willing and doing — we have so often counted our own poverty and called it Your limit. Forgive us for asking *how* when we should have remembered *Who You are.* Open for us the doors we have written off as sealed, the way You opened them for Zakariyya at the very edge of his old age — *kadhālika*, just like that — and let our hearts rest not on the roads we can see, but on the One who does whatever He wills. Āmīn.
