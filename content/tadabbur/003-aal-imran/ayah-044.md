# You Were Not There: The Absent Witness and the News That Weighs

## Introduction — Rebuilding the Room

Let me put you somewhere first. Before the ayah, the room.

* Recitation:*

> **ذَٰلِكَ مِنْ أَنۢبَآءِ ٱلْغَيْبِ نُوحِيهِ إِلَيْكَ ۚ وَمَا كُنتَ لَدَيْهِمْ إِذْ يُلْقُونَ أَقْلَٰمَهُمْ أَيُّهُمْ يَكْفُلُ مَرْيَمَ وَمَا كُنتَ لَدَيْهِمْ إِذْ يَخْتَصِمُونَ**

> *Dhālika min anbāʾi-l-ghaybi nūḥīhi ilayk. Wa mā kunta ladayhim idh yulqūna aqlāmahum, ayyuhum yakfulu Maryam, wa mā kunta ladayhim idh yakhtaṣimūn.*

> *"That is from the news of the unseen which We reveal to you. And you were not with them when they cast their pens to decide which of them should take charge of Maryam, nor were you with them when they were disputing."*

For a stretch of verses now, something strange has been happening in Surah Aal-Imran. A man from Makkah — unlettered, a merchant, someone who never sat in a single rabbinic study circle, who never read the scrolls kept in the synagogues of Madinah and the wider Hijaz — has been narrating, in detail, the private interior history of the household of Imran. The vow whispered by a woman over her unborn child. The naming of Maryam. The chamber where she was found with provision out of season. The old prophet Zakariyya, childless, undone, asking for a sign — and being struck silent for three days.

These are not Makkan stories. No Arab tribe carried these in its poetry. These belonged to the People of the Book — guarded, scriptural, the specialist knowledge of priests and scholars. And al-Ṭabarī sharpens the point further: this was not even common knowledge *among* the People of the Book. It was, he says, from the *hidden* history of that people — news that neither you, Muhammad, nor your own folk had any access to, and which only **a few** of the learned rabbis and monks of the two Scriptures knew at all. Not the marketplace, not the Arabs, not even the ordinary believer of the older faiths — a handful of specialists, and no one else.

So picture the listener. Picture a Jewish scholar in Madinah, or a Christian delegation from Najran — the kind who would later come and sit with the Prophet ﷺ precisely about this surah. They are listening to an Arab recite *their* sacred history back to them. And it is accurate. Where does an unlettered man in the desert get the inner life of the Temple in Jerusalem from a thousand years before?

[PAUSE]

Now — here is the moment. Right at the end of the narrative, the camera does something cinematic. It stops telling the story. It turns. It looks straight at Muhammad ﷺ and says, in effect: *Where do you think this came from? You weren't there. You weren't in the room. You didn't see the priests cast their lots over who would raise this girl. You didn't watch them argue.*

**The argument is not "look how much Muhammad knows." The argument is the exact opposite. It is: "look how much Muhammad could not possibly have known."** His absence is the evidence. His ignorance, on its own, is the proof.

Here is the hook I want you to carry into this hour. We are going to discover that in this ayah, *not being there* is treated as a stronger witness than being there. That the unseen, when Allah unveils it, becomes more vivid than the things in front of your eyes. And that the people in this forgotten little scene were fighting over something the rest of us spend our lives fighting to avoid.

Let's earn it.

# Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: *Anbāʾ* — The News That Carries Weight

Let me teach you something about your own language first, before we touch the Arabic.

In English we have "news," and we have "information," and we have "a report." But notice — there are two very different kinds of report. There's the report that tells you the bus is four minutes late. And there's the report that begins, "Please sit down. There's something I have to tell you." Same grammatical category — *a report* — but they are not the same animal. One is data. The other is a turning point. One you forget by lunch. The other reorganizes your life.

English has to lean on tone and context to tell these apart. Arabic builds the difference into the *word itself*.

Now look at what Allah chose:

> **مِنْ أَنۢبَآءِ ٱلْغَيْبِ** — *min anbāʾi-l-ghayb* — "from the **anbāʾ** of the unseen."

*Anbāʾ* (root **n-b-ʾ**) is the plural of *nabaʾ*. And the lexicographers are careful here. A *nabaʾ* is not just any piece of news. The classical lexicons — and you will feel this once it's named — define *nabaʾ* as news of **great consequence**, news that carries weight, news whose arrival *matters*, and which is conveyed with a degree of certainty. It is the difference between *khabar* (a report, any report) and *nabaʾ* (the report you cannot un-hear).

Sit with this. From the very same root comes the word **نَبِيّ** — *nabiyy* — **a Prophet**. A prophet is the one who carries the *nabaʾ*: the weight-bearing news from heaven. Allah did not choose a flat word for "information" here. He reached for the word whose own family contains *prophethood*.

[PAUSE]

So when the ayah says this is **min anbāʾi-l-ghayb**, it is not saying "here is some trivia about the unseen you happened not to know." It is saying: *this is consequential news, news that lands with the full weight of certainty, news of the kind only a prophet carries.* And notice the small word **مِنْ** — *min* — "**from** the news of the unseen." Not "this is the unseen," but "this is a *portion drawn from* it." A handful brought up from an ocean. Which quietly tells you how vast the reservoir is, and how measured the giving.

And the *that* — **ذَٰلِكَ**, *dhālika* — reaches further back than you might assume. Al-Ṭabarī notes that the demonstrative does not point only at the little scene of the pens; it gathers up the *whole* preceding sweep — the vow of Imran's wife, the naming of Maryam, the chamber and its provision, Zakariyya and his son Yaḥyā, all the way back to *"Indeed Allah chose Adam and Nūḥ and the family of Ibrāhīm and the family of ʿImrān"* (3:33). One small word reaches its arm across an entire passage and lifts all of it at once, then sets it down with a single verdict: *all of this — every line of it — is anbāʾ al-ghayb.*

What does this single word reveal? **That revelation is not Allah satisfying your curiosity. It is Allah entrusting you with weight.** The news that matters. Carry it accordingly.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Nūḥīhi* — Revelation in the Present, Forever Arriving

Here is the second word, and it hides a tense that changes everything.

> **نُوحِيهِ إِلَيْكَ** — *nūḥīhi ilayk* — "We reveal it to you."

Two things to teach before we open it.

First, the form. *Nūḥī* is from the root **w-ḥ-y** — the root of *waḥy*, revelation itself. And it is in **Form IV** (the *afʿala* pattern). Let me explain what Form IV does without jargon. In Arabic, the base verb often describes a simple action. The fourth form takes that action and *directs* it — it makes the verb reach out and deliver something to a recipient. So the base sense of *w-ḥ-y* — a subtle, swift, hidden communication — becomes, in Form IV, *to convey that hidden communication to someone specifically.* Revelation is not Allah thinking out loud. It is Allah *aiming* speech at a named heart. And the aim is right there in the word: *ilayk* — "**to you**," singular, pointed.

Second — and this is the one I want you to feel — the **tense**. Arabic, like English, can say "We revealed it" (past, finished) or "We reveal it" (present, ongoing). Allah chose the **imperfect**: *nūḥīhi*, the ongoing form. **"We are revealing it to you."** Right now. In the act.

Why does that matter? Because the *content* He's describing — the priests, the pens, the dispute — is ancient. A thousand years gone. By every natural law that story belongs to the deep past. And yet the *verb of its delivery* is in the living present. The event is old; the revealing is happening *now*, in the moment of recitation.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because here is something remarkable. Look at the verbs describing the ancient scene: **يُلْقُونَ** *yulqūn* — "they **are casting** their pens" — present tense. **يَخْتَصِمُونَ** *yakhtaṣimūn* — "they **are disputing**" — present tense. The grammar should have used the past. The event is over. But Allah films it in the present continuous, as though it were unfolding in front of you, live.

Do you see the inversion? **The thing that is physically present — the Prophet's own seventh-century Makkah — is barely in frame. The thing that is utterly absent — a temple courtyard a millennium ago — is rendered in vivid, breathing present tense.** Revelation collapses the distance. Under *waḥy*, the unseen is not "back then." It is *here, now, in motion.* Allah is not reporting a memory to His Prophet. He is opening a window and letting him watch.

This is the physical-to-spiritual stepping stone. The grammar of a verb tense is the *visible* thing. What it points to is the *invisible* thing: that for the One who reveals, there is no "long ago." All of time lies open before Him in a single, perpetual present, and when He chooses to show you a corner of it, the corner becomes *now*.

What does *nūḥīhi* reveal? **That what reaches the Prophet ﷺ is not the cold record of an event but the live broadcast of it — and that the past, for Allah, was never past.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: *Wa Mā Kunta Ladayhim* — The Sentence Built Out of Absence

Now the strangest construction in the ayah. And to feel it, we have to notice what *isn't* there.

Read the architecture:

> **وَمَا كُنتَ لَدَيْهِمْ** ... *wa mā kunta ladayhim* — "And you were **not** with them..."
>
> ... *idh yulqūna aqlāmahum* — "...when they were casting their pens..."
>
> **وَمَا كُنتَ لَدَيْهِمْ** ... *wa mā kunta ladayhim* — "And you were **not** with them..."
>
> ... *idh yakhtaṣimūn* — "...when they were disputing."

Twice. The same phrase of *absence*, repeated. **مَا** — *mā* — the flat negation: "not." **كُنتَ** — *kunta* — "you were." **لَدَيْهِمْ** — *ladayhim* — "right there with them, in their very presence."

Here is the thing about evidence. Imagine a man describes a crime scene in perfect detail — the position of every object, the words exchanged. Your first instinct is: *he must have been there.* Presence is the ordinary explanation for knowledge. We know things because we witnessed them. That is the entire basis of testimony, of journalism, of the courtroom: *were you there?*

So watch what Allah does. He grants the detail — the pens, the lots, the argument — and then He *removes the ordinary explanation.* **"You were not there."** Not once but twice, hammered in. He deliberately demolishes the only natural account of how Muhammad ﷺ could know this. He leaves a hole exactly where the explanation should be.

And that hole has only one thing that fits it. If not presence — then revelation. If not eyes — then *waḥy*. **By negating the witness, Allah isolates the source.** The absence is not a weakness in the argument; the absence *is* the argument. It is reasoning by elimination, and the eliminated term is the Prophet's own physical presence.

[PAUSE]

This is the principle the methodology calls reading *absence* as carefully as presence — and here Allah Himself has built an entire proof out of an absence. The most important fact in the sentence is a *non-event*: a man who was *not in a room.*

And notice the repetition is not lazy. The first absence is from the moment of **procedure** — *idh yulqūna aqlāmahum*, when they cast the lots. The second is from the moment of **conflict** — *idh yakhtaṣimūn*, when they quarreled over it. Allah covers both phases. *You weren't there when they cast the pens. You weren't there when they fought.* Every angle of the scene — the formal drawing of lots and the heated dispute behind it — is sealed off from natural access. The proof is airtight on both ends.

There is a subtlety here the classical commentators are careful about. The verse lists the *casting of pens* first and the *disputing* second, but the actual chain of events ran the other way. Al-Muyassar and al-Jalālayn both read the lot-casting as the *resolution* of the dispute, not its prelude: the priests first **disagreed** over who was most deserving of Maryam's care, and when the quarrel could not be settled by rank or argument, they turned to the *qurʿah* — the drawing of lots — to decide it. Al-Jalālayn adds the physical detail that they cast their pens *into the water* (*fī l-māʾ yaqtariʿūn*) so that the outcome would *show itself plainly to them* — the lot was not their judgment but a sign they agreed to read. So the two clauses are not simply consecutive snapshots; they name the two faces of one episode, the contention and the device that ended it. And whichever order you take them in, the point of the ayah is untouched: the Prophet ﷺ was present for *neither* — not the argument, not its settling.

[PAUSE]

Let me draw the three threads together before we leave the words. *Anbāʾ* told us this is **weighty news**. *Nūḥīhi* told us it is **live, present, ongoing revelation**. *Wa mā kunta ladayhim* told us the Prophet ﷺ **had no natural way to know it.** Put them in sequence and you have a complete syllogism pressed into twenty words: *Here is consequential, certain knowledge of the unseen (anbāʾ al-ghayb); it is arriving to you right now (nūḥīhi); and you were not, and could not have been, its witness (mā kunta ladayhim). Therefore — there is a Sender.*

The whole ayah is a quiet, devastating piece of logic. And it never raises its voice. Al-Jalālayn spells out the unspoken conclusion the verse leaves hanging in the air: *fa-taʿrifu dhālika fa-tukhbiru bihi, wa-innamā ʿaraftahu min jihati l-waḥy* — "so you come to know this and tell of it, and you knew it *only* by way of revelation." The classical reading lands exactly where the grammar pointed: subtract every natural channel, and *waḥy* is the lone term left standing.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how we know, and about what these forgotten people were fighting over.

# Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Reliability of What You Did Not See

Let me start where you actually live.

Almost everything you are certain of, you never witnessed. You have never seen an electron. You have never been to the bottom of the ocean or the surface of another planet. You are confident your great-grandmother existed, but you never met her. You believe a continent called Antarctica is there under the ice, though you'll likely never stand on it.

How do you *know* these things? Not by witnessing — by **testimony**. By trusting a *source*. And here is the quiet truth of human knowledge: the vast majority of what any of us holds to be true rests not on our own eyes but on our judgment about *who to believe.* You are not, mostly, a knower of facts. You are a *truster of sources.* The whole question of your intellectual life is: **whose report do I accept, and why?**

So the real question this ayah puts to the listener is not "is this story true?" It is the deeper one underneath all knowing: **when someone tells you of what you cannot verify, what makes their word trustworthy?**

[PAUSE]

Watch how Allah answers it — because He answers it the way you would actually trust a source in real life.

In a courtroom, the testimony that convicts is the testimony that *could not have been obtained any other way.* When a witness knows a detail only the perpetrator could know — a detail no newspaper printed, no bystander saw — that detail is worth more than a thousand vague impressions. Investigators call it *guilty knowledge*: information whose mere possession proves a specific access.

That is precisely the structure here. The Prophet ﷺ possesses a detail — the casting of pens over Maryam — that he had *no channel* to acquire. He didn't read it; he was unlettered. He didn't witness it; he wasn't born for a thousand years. He didn't inherit it; it wasn't Arab lore. The *possession of the knowledge*, combined with the *impossibility of the access*, is the entire proof. Not "trust him because he's impressive." Rather: **"trust the source, because the knowledge in his hands could only have come through the door that revelation opens."**

[PAUSE]

Now let this turn inward, because it is not only an argument for the seventh century. It is a mirror for you.

Most of your spiritual doubts are doubts of *witness*. "I've never seen an angel." "I didn't witness the splitting of the sea." "I wasn't there at Badr." And the nafs whispers: *if you didn't see it, how can you be sure?* This ayah quietly dismantles that whisper. Because it shows you that **the most reliable knowledge a human being can have is precisely the knowledge that does not depend on his having been present.** Your not-having-been-there is not a disqualification. It is the very condition under which you are asked to trust the *Source* rather than the *self.*

And there is a humility built into this for the Prophet ﷺ himself. The greatest of all human beings is being told, in the gentlest way: *the most magnificent knowledge you carry, you did not earn by being clever or present. It was given.* If the Messenger's own knowledge of the unseen is pure gift, pure *nūḥīhi ilayk*, then what of ours? Everything we know of the unseen — Heaven, Hell, the angels, the Day — every bit of it is *anbāʾ al-ghayb* handed down. We are all, in the end, recipients. None of us were there.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** When the thought comes — *you can't be sure, you weren't there* — answer it the way the ayah does. *Correct. I was not there. And being there was never the basis of my certainty. My certainty rests on knowing the One who told me.* The faith you carry is not weaker for being received rather than witnessed. Received knowledge, from a trustworthy Source, is the firmest ground a human being ever stands on. You believe a hundred things on far thinner authority every single day.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: They Fought Over the Right to *Serve*

Now look closely at *what* these people were actually disputing — because it overturns something in us.

> *...ayyuhum **yakfulu** Maryam...* — "...which of them should **take charge of** Maryam..."

The verb is **يَكْفُلُ** — *yakfulu*, from the root **k-f-l**. And *kafāla* is one of the most beautiful words in the Quran's vocabulary of care. It does not mean ownership. It does not mean control. A *kafīl* is a **guarantor** — the one who takes full responsibility for another, who pledges to provide, to protect, to nurture at his own cost. Al-Jalālayn glosses *yakful* with a single tender word — *yurabbī*, "to raise, to bring up" — so the contest was, quite literally, over who would get to *parent* this child. It is the same root behind the *kafāla* of an orphan, the relationship the Prophet ﷺ called being "like these two" — holding up two fingers together — in Paradise. To be someone's *kāfil* is to take their entire burden onto your own back and call it an honor.

Now — the lexical sources and the tafsir agree on the scene. The priests of the temple, the religious elite, the men of standing — al-Jalālayn and the classical commentators describe it — were casting their pens into the water for a *qurʿah*, a drawing of lots, *li-yaẓhara lahum ayyuhum yakful* — to make plain to them which one would take charge of her. And what were they competing for, with such intensity that it became a *dispute* — *yakhtaṣimūn*?

**The right to raise an orphan girl.** The privilege of *spending themselves* on a child who could offer them nothing in return.

[PAUSE]

Stop and feel how foreign that is.

We do not cast lots over who gets to serve. We cast lots — metaphorically, constantly — over who gets to *receive.* We compete for the bigger inheritance, the better seat, the credit, the corner office, the lighter share of the chores. Our disputes are almost always about how to get *more* and carry *less.* The whole machinery of the ego runs on *acquisition.* Show me what most people argue over and I will show you a fight about who takes home the larger piece.

These people inverted it completely. Their dispute — and the Quran preserves it for all time with the heavy verb *yakhtaṣimūn*, Form VIII, the form that carries the sense of contention they took *upon themselves*, internalized, wrestled over — their entire heated quarrel was about **who gets to give the most.** Who gets the *responsibility.* Who gets to wake at night, to provide, to protect, to pour themselves out for a child of the temple.

[PAUSE]

And here is the layer beneath the layer. Why such fierce competition to raise *this* girl in particular?

Because they understood, even dimly, that to serve someone beloved to Allah is itself the highest honor a person can be given. Maryam was *muṣṭafāh* and *muṭahhara* — chosen and purified, lifted "above the women of the worlds," as the angels had proclaimed to her in the very verses standing immediately before this one: *"O Maryam, indeed Allah has chosen you and purified you and chosen you above the women of the nations"* (3:42). Ibn Kathīr sets our ayah down right on the heel of that proclamation — the divine signature pressed onto the page the moment after Maryam's election is announced. To be near such a soul, to be the hands that fed and sheltered her, was to be near the favor of Allah. They were not fighting over a chore. They were fighting over **proximity to the sacred.** They grasped something we keep forgetting: that *the one who serves the beloved is honored by the beloved's Beloved.*

This reframes the whole emotional weight of *kafāla.* Service is not the lowly thing the ego thinks it is — the thing you delegate, avoid, resent. In the economy of the unseen, **the right to serve is the prize itself.** The position everyone in that courtyard wanted was the position of the one who carries the weight. They were richer than us, those quarreling priests, because they knew where the honor actually lay.

[PAUSE]

And do not miss who *won.* Al-Muyassar tells us plainly how it ended: the lot fell to **Zakariyya** — *fa-aṣābat Zakariyyā* — and he gained her *kafāla*. The prize for the fiercest competition to *give* went to a prophet of Allah, the very man the surah has already shown us bent in the prayer-niche, undone, begging his Lord for a child of his own (3:37–38). The one who fought hardest for the right to pour himself out for another's child was the one whose own longing for a child Allah was about to answer. There is a quiet justice threaded through this: the burden he won — the *kafāla* of Maryam — became the doorway to the blessing he sought. The hands that reached for the heavier share were the hands Allah chose to fill. Notice, too, that this verse caps the very passage in which we have *watched* Zakariyya care for her; the lot we were never told the outcome of, the tafsir quietly closes — and the winner is the man we already know.

**What does this change about tomorrow?**

Watch your own disputes this week — the small ones, in the home, at work, in the masjid. Notice the direction they run. Almost all of them, if you're honest, run *toward* acquiring and *away* from carrying. Now try, just once, to dispute in the other direction. To compete for the burden. To want the harder share of the service — the late-night feeding, the difficult relative, the task no one volunteers for, the orphan no one is rushing to *kafāla*. Cast your pen into *that* water.

Because the people Allah chose to immortalize in His final Book were not immortalized for what they took. They were immortalized for what they *fought to give.* And the One who watched that courtyard — *wa mā kunta ladayhim*, you were not there, but **He was** — is the same One watching yours.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture Beneath the Twenty Words

Step back now and see the shape, because there is one.

The ayah has a perfect two-part body, framed by a declaration:

> **The Frame:** *That is from the news of the unseen which We reveal to you.*
>
> **Panel One:** *And you were not with them when they cast their pens — which of them should take charge of Maryam —*
>
> **Panel Two:** *nor were you with them when they were disputing.*

Two panels of *absence*, hung on either side of one act of *revelation*. The structure itself is the argument. The center is the source — *We reveal*. The two wings are the proof — *you were not there, you were not there.* The form *is* the meaning: **revelation stands in the middle, exactly where the witness is missing.** Where the Prophet's presence is absent, Allah's revealing presence fills the gap. The empty space in the human knower is the precise shape of the divine Sender.

## The Two Discoveries Were One Discovery

Our linguistic journey and our themes have been climbing the same mountain from two faces.

The words taught us: this is *weighty* news (*anbāʾ*), *live* and ongoing (*nūḥīhi*), and reaching a man who *could not have witnessed it* (*mā kunta ladayhim*). The themes taught us: that received knowledge from a trustworthy Source is the firmest certainty a human ever has — and that the souls Allah honors are the ones who fight to *serve* rather than to acquire.

And see how they fuse. The very fact that the Prophet ﷺ *was not there* — the absence that proves revelation — is the same fact that proves the courtyard scene is *real.* This story of people competing to give was not invented by an Arab to flatter anyone. It came from beyond the veil, from the One who *was* there when no human eye recorded it. **The proof of the Source and the lesson of service are locked together: only the One who witnessed that beautiful, forgotten dispute could have told us about it — and He told us, across a thousand years and an unlettered tongue, so that we would learn to dispute the way they did.**

## Questions to Carry

1. How much of what I am certain of did I actually witness — and how much do I hold on trust? And have I been honest about which Source I have chosen to trust with the unseen?
2. When the whisper comes — *you weren't there, how can you be sure* — do I treat my absence as a weakness in my faith, when this ayah treats it as the very ground of certainty?
3. In my disputes this week, which direction do they run — toward taking more, or toward carrying more?
4. Is there a *kafāla* in front of me right now — a person, a burden, a service no one is rushing toward — that I have been treating as a chore rather than a prize?
5. If Allah was the unseen Witness in that temple courtyard, He is the unseen Witness in my home and my heart. What would I do differently in the next hour if I truly held that?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The Prophet's not being there is the proof that Allah told him — and what Allah chose to tell us, of all the hidden history of the world, is the story of people who quarreled for the honor of giving.

## The Closing Invitation

You will never witness most of what matters. You did not see the angels, you were not at the temple, you will not stand at the Resurrection until it stands before you. This ayah does not ask you to pretend otherwise. It invites you to something steadier than witnessing: to *know your Source* — and then to carry the news the way the *nabaʾ* deserves, as weight, not trivia. And it invites you, when you next find yourself in a dispute, to ask which way you are pulling. Toward the larger piece — or toward the heavier load that the people Allah loved could not stop reaching for.

## A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You were present where no one was watching, and You unveiled it so we would know You are the One who tells the truth about the unseen. Make our certainty rest on trusting You, not on having seen. And let us be among those who quarrel only over the right to serve what You love — who cast our pens, gladly, toward the heavier share.* Āmīn.
