# What It Costs to Reach Righteousness

## The Ayah

> لَن تَنَالُوا۟ ٱلْبِرَّ حَتَّىٰ تُنفِقُوا۟ مِمَّا تُحِبُّونَ ۚ وَمَا تُنفِقُوا۟ مِن شَىْءٍ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِهِۦ عَلِيمٌ

*Lan tanālu l-birra ḥattā tunfiqū mimmā tuḥibbūn. Wa mā tunfiqū min shay'in fa-inna Llāha bihī ʿalīm.*

**"You will never attain righteousness until you spend from that which you love. And whatever you spend — of anything — then indeed Allah is All-Knowing of it."**

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word, let me put you somewhere.

You are standing in Madinah, in the early years of the community. The believers around you are not strangers to sacrifice. Many of them left everything in Makkah — their homes, their businesses, the graves of their parents — and walked into a city where they owned nothing and knew almost no one. They have given. They have bled. They have buried friends at Badr and Uhud.

These are not stingy people. These are not people who need to be told to be generous.

And it is *to these people* — the ones who have already given so much — that this ayah comes. [PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment, because it changes everything. This is not a verse scolding misers. This is a verse spoken to people who thought they had already arrived, and Allah gently tells them: *not yet.* There is a doorway you have not walked through. There is a height you have not reached. And there is one specific thing standing between you and it.

The classical narrations preserve a beautiful moment around this ayah. Among them, al-Qurtubi reports — and the well-known hadith confirms — that there was a man named Abu Talha, one of the wealthiest of the Anṣār in Madinah. His most beloved possession was a garden called Bayruḥā, directly facing the mosque. The Prophet ﷺ used to enter it and drink from its sweet water. It was the apple of Abu Talha's eye.

When this ayah was revealed — *you will never attain righteousness until you spend from what you love* — Abu Talha did not wait. He stood and said: "O Messenger of Allah, the most beloved of my wealth to me is Bayruḥā, and it is a charity for the sake of Allah." He gave away the one thing he loved most, the moment he understood that his love was the very thing standing between him and birr.

Here is the hook. Here is the question I want burning in you for the next hour:

**Why can righteousness only be bought with the thing you don't want to let go of?** Why does Allah place the price of arrival on the exact object your heart is gripping? And what is it about *love* — specifically love — that turns an ordinary act of giving into the gateway of righteousness?

Let's find out together. Slowly. The way it deserves.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Word لَن — "Never"

### Teaching the concept first

Let me teach you something about how Arabic says "no" to the future.

In English, we have one main way to deny the future: "you will not." And it's flexible — "you will not finish today" might just mean *today*; tomorrow is open. Our negation is soft and time-bound.

Arabic is more precise. It has a particle whose entire job is to slam a door on a future possibility — to say not merely "this won't happen," but "this will *never* happen under these conditions." That particle is **لَن** (*lan*).

Think of the difference between a parent saying "you're not going out" and a parent saying "you will never go out *until your homework is done.*" The first is a mood. The second is a law. *Lan* is the law.

### Opening the word

Now look at how Allah opens the ayah:

> **لَن** تَنَالُوا۟ ٱلْبِرَّ

Not *lā tanālūn* (a general "you don't attain"). Not a description of a tendency. **Lan.** *You will never attain it.*

### Sitting with it

Notice what this does. Allah did not say, "It is difficult to reach righteousness without spending from what you love." He did not say, "It is better if you spend from what you love." He drew an absolute line. The road to birr has a gate, and this gate does not open by any other key.

Stay with me here, because this is where it gets striking. We are comfortable with the idea that righteousness is a *spectrum* — that we inch toward it, that good deeds accumulate like coins, that we are all somewhere on the ladder. And there is truth in that. But *lan* introduces something else: a **threshold**. A point below which you are simply on the *outside* of birr, no matter how many other good things you pile up.

You can pray. You can fast. You can speak truth, keep your word, harm no one. And the ayah, with that single word *lan*, says: there is a chamber of righteousness whose door stays shut until one specific thing happens. [PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: **Righteousness is not only an accumulation. It has a threshold — and the threshold is love surrendered.**

## Element Two: تَنَالُوا — The Reach

### Teaching the concept first

Every word in the Quran is chosen, and the difference between two near-synonyms is often the difference between an ordinary sentence and a window.

Allah could have used a flat verb for "attain" — something like *tablughū* (to arrive at) or *taḥṣulū ʿalā* (to obtain). Instead He chose a verb from the root **ن-ي-ل** (*n-y-l*): **تَنَالُوا** (*tanālū*).

Here is what the lexicons preserve about this root. *Nāla / yanālu* carries the sense of **reaching for something and grasping it** — specifically the image of *extending yourself toward something to take hold of it*. It is the word used for stretching your hand up to **pluck fruit from a branch**, for reaching out to *obtain* something that requires you to extend beyond where you are standing.

### Opening the word

So when Allah says **لَن تَنَالُوا۟ ٱلْبِرَّ**, the image embedded in the verb is not "you will never *be at* righteousness." It is closer to: **"you will never *reach up and take hold of* righteousness."** [PAUSE]

### Sitting with it — the physical image as stepping stone

Now here is where we follow the Quran's own method. Allah gives us a physical, bodily image — *reaching, stretching, plucking* — and the image is not the point. It is the stepping stone toward something unseen.

Picture the fruit on the high branch. Why can't you take it where you stand? Because it is *above* you. To get it, you must *rise* — onto your toes, off your heels, stretching your whole frame upward, leaving the comfortable flat-footed posture you were in.

This is the picture Allah paints of birr. Righteousness is not lying on the ground at your feet to be scooped up. It hangs *above* the level where you currently stand. And to take hold of it you must **rise above your present self** — you must leave the flat-footed comfort of giving only what costs nothing.

Notice something remarkable. The verb already tells you, before the rest of the sentence arrives, that birr requires *effort beyond your current reach*. And then the ayah tells you exactly what the "stretching" is: **spending from what you love.** The letting-go of the beloved thing *is* the rising onto your toes. It is the act of extending past where you comfortably stand. [PAUSE]

What this word reveals: **Righteousness is positioned above you on purpose. It can only be plucked by someone willing to rise — and the rising is the surrender of what the heart clings to.**

## Element Three: مِمَّا تُحِبُّونَ — "From What You Love" (and the silence inside it)

### Teaching the concept first — the little word مِن

I want to teach you the smallest, most easily-skipped word in this ayah, because it carries an enormous weight.

Look: Allah did not say *ḥattā tunfiqū mā tuḥibbūn* — "until you spend **what** you love." He said *ḥattā tunfiqū* **مِمَّا** *tuḥibbūn* — "until you spend **from** what you love," where *mimmā* is *min* + *mā*.

That tiny **مِن** (*min*) is what grammarians call *min al-tabʿīḍiyya* — the **partitive** *min*. It means "**a part of**," "**from among**." It is the difference between "give me your bread" and "give me **some of** your bread."

### Opening the construction

So the condition is not: *give away everything you love.* The condition is: **give from — a portion of — that which you love.** [PAUSE]

Do you feel the mercy hidden in that one letter? Allah does not demand that you strip yourself bare of every beloved thing. He asks that the category of things you draw your giving *from* must include the things you love. Reach into the basket where your heart's treasures are kept — and give *from there*. Not necessarily all of it. But genuinely *from* it.

### Sitting with the silence — what Allah did *not* say

Now, the absence. This is the deepest part of the ayah, and it lives in what is **missing**.

Read it again: *until you spend from what you love.* And ask the obvious question: **love *what*?** Money? Land? Your home? Your time? Your child? Your reputation? Your comfort?

**Allah does not say.** [PAUSE]

The object of "love" is left completely unspecified. There is a deliberate silence where you expect a noun. The classical commentators, reading the verse against its context and the practice of the Companions, understood the primary application as **wealth** — al-Jalalayn glosses *mimmā tuḥibbūn* as "from your wealth," and al-Muyassar reads it as charity from what you love. That is the established, primary meaning, and Abu Talha's garden is its living proof.

But notice *why* the wording stays open. Had Allah named a specific object — "spend from your *gold*" — then the man who loves not gold but *land* would be exempt. The woman who loves not money but *status* would walk free. By leaving the beloved object unnamed, the ayah reaches into **every single heart** and finds the one thing *that* heart is gripping. [PAUSE]

This is a test that *calibrates itself to you.* It does not ask everyone for the same thing. It asks each person for *their* thing. The verse is a key cut to the shape of your particular attachment. Whatever it is that you would least like to mention right now — that flicker of resistance you just felt — *that* is the verse speaking to you personally.

What this construction reveals: **Allah asks for a portion, not for everything — but He asks for it from precisely the place that hurts, and He leaves the place unnamed so that no heart can claim the verse isn't about it.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Four: مِن شَىْءٍ and عَلِيمٌ — Nothing Is Too Small to Be Seen

### Opening the second half

There is a second movement in this ayah, and it does something tender after the demand.

> وَمَا تُنفِقُوا۟ **مِن شَىْءٍ** فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِهِۦ **عَلِيمٌ**

"And whatever you spend — **of anything** — then indeed Allah is **All-Knowing** of it."

After raising the bar to *what you love*, you might fear the opposite danger: that your small gift, the modest thing you *can* part with, is beneath notice. So Allah, in the same breath, says *min shayʾin* — "of **any thing**," *any thing whatsoever*, however small.

That word **شَىْء** (*shayʾ*) is the most generic, smallest-unit word in the language — "a thing," "anything at all." Paired with *min*, it sweeps down to the tiniest gift: a date, a coin, a kind word, a cup of water. And over that smallest possible thing Allah places **عَلِيمٌ** — *ʿAlīm*, the One whose knowledge is total and exact.

### Sitting with it

Feel the two halves working together. The first half lifts the ceiling: *reach high, give from what you love.* The second half lowers a floor of mercy beneath you: *and nothing you give, no matter how small, falls outside My knowing.* [PAUSE]

There is a precision in *ʿAlīm* that ordinary "knowing" misses. It is not that Allah is merely *aware* that you gave. It is that He knows **the thing itself** — its exact worth to you, what it cost your heart, what you whispered to yourself as you let it go, the love you felt for it that made the giving heavy. The Form of the giving and the *weight* behind it — both are fully known.

This is why the ayah does not end with "Allah will reward it" (though al-Jalalayn notes the reward is implied). It ends with *Allah knows it.* Because to the heart that gave away something it loved in secret, fearing it was unseen and insignificant — the deepest comfort is not "you'll be paid." It is **"I saw. I know exactly what that cost you."** [PAUSE]

What this reveals: **The same God who set the bar at what you love guarantees that even your smallest offering is fully, intimately known.**

## Bridge

So the words have shown us a structure. *Lan* — an absolute threshold. *Tanālū* — a righteousness positioned above us, reached only by rising. *Mimmā tuḥibbūn* — a portion drawn from the beloved, the object left unnamed so it finds every heart. And *min shayʾ... ʿAlīm* — a floor of mercy where nothing given is too small to be perfectly known.

Now let's ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the strange economy of the human heart.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Economy of Attachment — Why Love Is the Currency

### Beginning where you already live

Let me start with something you already do, without thinking about it.

When you declutter your closet, what goes in the donation bag? Be honest. It's the shirt you stopped wearing. The coat that doesn't fit. The gadget you upgraded from. We give away what we have already, quietly, stopped wanting. The donation pile is, very often, the *exhaustion* pile — the things our hearts have already released, which we then route through charity on their way out the door.

There is nothing wrong with this. It is good. The poor are warmed by your old coat. But notice the *transaction inside you*: when you give the thing you no longer want, **your heart does not move.** Nothing leaves you. You feel, if anything, lighter and more organized. The act costs your hands but not your heart.

And here, gently, the ayah arrives and turns the whole thing over. [PAUSE]

### Letting the ayah illuminate it

*Lan tanālu l-birra ḥattā tunfiqū mimmā tuḥibbūn.* You will never reach righteousness through the things your heart has already let go of.

Why not? Because **birr is not measured by what leaves your hands. It is measured by what leaves your heart.** [PAUSE]

Think about what charity is actually *for*, spiritually. It is not, at the deepest level, a wealth-transfer program — Allah does not need your money to feed the poor; He could feed them without you. Charity is a **surgery on the heart.** Its real purpose is to loosen the grip of the *dunya* on you, finger by finger. And here is the logic that the ayah exposes with brutal clarity:

**You cannot loosen a grip that isn't gripping anything.** [PAUSE]

The old coat in the donation bag — your heart wasn't holding it. So giving it loosens nothing; there was no grip to loosen. But the thing you *love* — that you are holding tightly. And when you pry your own fingers off of it, *for Allah*, something real happens inside you. A grip is broken. The dunya loses a handle it had on you. You rise — *tanālū*, you reach up off your heels — a little freer than you were. **That** is the movement toward birr.

### Building through it

Go deeper. There is a hidden mercy in why Allah ties righteousness to *love* rather than to *amount*.

If birr were tied to *amount*, it would belong to the rich. The wealthy could buy their way up the ladder and the poor would be locked out. But Allah did not say "spend a great deal." He said **"spend from what you love"** — and the poor love things too. The widow loves her single coin. The child loves her one toy. The laborer loves his single day of rest. By making the currency *love* rather than *quantity*, Allah opened the gate of righteousness **equally to every economic class.** The pauper who gives the one date he was saving has paid in the exact same currency as Abu Talha giving his garden: *the beloved thing.* [PAUSE]

This is staggering when you sit with it. Birr is not for sale. It cannot be purchased with surplus. It can only be reached by the universal human act of *letting go of something you wanted to keep* — an act available to the richest and the poorest in identical measure.

### The paradox — let it sit

And here is a paradox I will not rush to resolve. The ayah asks you to give away what you love. But the deepest secret of it is this: **what you refuse to give away, you do not fully own — it owns you.** [PAUSE]

The thing you cannot bear to part with has a hand around your throat. Abu Talha's garden was beautiful, but the moment he could not imagine giving it, *it* possessed *him.* The instant he gave it for Allah, he possessed it — and himself — in a way he never had while clutching it. We think holding tight is owning. The ayah whispers that holding tight is a kind of slavery, and the open hand is the only free one.

### Connecting to the universal struggle, and tomorrow

Every human being who has ever lived knows this struggle: the white-knuckle grip on something we are terrified to lose. It might be money. It might be a relationship we won't release to Allah's decree. It might be a grudge — yes, even a grudge is a thing we *love* to hold. The ayah's logic extends to all of it: you will not rise to the next height of your soul while your hand is full of something you refuse to open.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Find the one thing you'd least want to give. Not your leftovers — your *favorite*. And give *some of it*, deliberately, this week, for Allah. Not because the recipient needs it more than your old coat would serve them, but because *your heart needs the surgery.* Watch what happens inside you when you pry your own fingers loose. That loosening is the sound of birr coming within reach.

## Theme Two: The Eye That Sees the Small Thing

### Beginning where you already live

Here is a feeling almost everyone carries: **the suspicion that our small good deeds don't matter.**

We live in a world of metrics and scale. The charity gala raises millions; your five dollars feels like a rounding error. The influencer's viral fundraiser feeds thousands; your quiet help to one neighbor disappears into the noise. We have been trained by our age to believe that *impact* requires *scale*, and so the small, private, unwitnessed good deed feels almost pointless. *Who will ever know? What difference does it make?*

This feeling is corrosive. It is, in fact, one of the great quiet thieves of good deeds — because the person who believes their small act is invisible often simply stops doing it.

### Letting the ayah illuminate it

And into exactly this wound, the second half of the ayah speaks:

> وَمَا تُنفِقُوا۟ مِن شَىْءٍ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِهِۦ عَلِيمٌ

"And whatever you spend — **of anything** — then indeed Allah is **All-Knowing** of it." [PAUSE]

*Min shayʾ.* Of any *thing.* The smallest possible unit of giving. And over it: *ʿAlīm.* The One who knows it completely.

Notice the gift Allah is giving here. He is dismantling the entire premise that *scale equals significance*. In the economy of the dunya, the small deed vanishes into the crowd. In the economy of Allah, **there is no crowd.** Each thing is seen singly, in full, by itself. Your five dollars is not lost in the noise of the gala — because to *ʿAlīm* there is no noise. There is only *this thing, fully known.* [PAUSE]

### Building through it

Go deeper into what *ʿAlīm* means here. It would have been enough, you might think, for Allah to say *Allah sees it* (*baṣīr*) or *Allah hears your intention* (*samīʿ*). Why *ʿAlīm* — All-*Knowing*?

Because seeing registers the *act*, but knowing registers the *meaning*. *ʿAlīm* knows not only that you gave, but what it was worth to you. He knows the difference between the rich man's casual hundred and the poor man's agonized one. He knows the love you felt for the thing — the very *mimmā tuḥibbūn* of the first half — because His knowledge reaches *into* the heart where the love lived. [PAUSE]

Do you see how the two halves are secretly one? The first half asks you to give from what you *love.* The second half assures you that Allah *knows* — and the only way to truly reward a gift given from love is to *know the love that made it costly.* A bookkeeper who only counts the amount would reward the rich man more. *ʿAlīm* rewards the *weight*, because He alone can weigh it.

### The paradox — let it sit

And here is the quiet paradox. The same attribute that comforts you — *Allah knows my smallest good* — also exposes you: *Allah knows my smallest withholding.* [PAUSE]

*ʿAlīm* does not selectively see the good. He knows the gift you gave *and* the gift you found a reason not to give. He knows the love behind the open hand *and* the love behind the closed one. This is not meant to terrify you. It is meant to make your secret generosity feel *worth it* — and your secret stinginess feel *seen.* The believer lives in the warmth and the weight of being fully known.

### Connecting to the universal struggle, and tomorrow

Every one of us has done a small good thing and felt it disappear unacknowledged. The neighbor you helped who never thanked you. The money you gave anonymously. The kindness no one saw. The ayah comes to you and says: **none of it disappeared.** It was received, in full, by the only audience that ultimately matters — and not merely received but *known*, with all its weight and cost.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Do one good thing this week that *no one will ever know about.* No mention, no hint, no photo. Give it, and then let the silence around it be filled by *one* certainty — *fa-inna Llāha bihī ʿalīm.* Allah knows it. Let that be enough. Practice letting that be enough, until the hunger for human acknowledgment quietly dies, and you taste the freedom of doing good for an Audience of One.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found, from two directions.

**From the words:** *Lan* — an absolute threshold on the road to righteousness. *Tanālū* — a birr positioned above us, plucked only by those who rise. *Mimmā tuḥibbūn* — a portion drawn from the beloved thing, the object left unnamed so the verse finds *your* heart specifically. *Min shayʾ... ʿAlīm* — and a floor of mercy beneath it all, where the smallest gift is fully, intimately known.

**From the themes:** Righteousness is a surgery of the heart that can only be performed on a heart that is actually gripping something — so Allah set the price at *love*, the one currency the rich and poor possess equally. And the God who set that price guarantees that nothing you give, however small, is lost in the noise — because to *ʿAlīm* there is no noise.

They are the same insight from two sides. **The first half asks for the gift that costs your heart. The second half is the receipt — signed by the only One who can read what it cost.** The demand and the comfort are a single sentence, and they were never meant to be separated.

## Questions to Carry

1. What is the one thing I would *least* like to give away for Allah's sake right now? (Notice the resistance — that is the verse addressing me by name.)
2. When I give, do I reach into the basket of things my heart has already released — or into the basket of things I love?
3. Is there something I am gripping so tightly that, without realizing it, *it* now owns *me*?
4. Do I do good only where it will be seen and counted — or have I learned to let *fa-inna Llāha bihī ʿalīm* be enough?
5. What grip in my heart needs loosening before I can rise to the next height of my soul?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**You will never reach the righteousness you long for by handing God your leftovers — only what costs your heart can carry you upward to Him, and not the smallest piece of it ever escapes His knowing.**

## The Closing Invitation

This week, find your Bayruḥā. The thing you love. And give *some* of it — deliberately, quietly, for Allah alone. Not because anyone needs it more than your surplus would serve them, but because your heart needs to feel its own grip loosen. Watch what happens when you pry your fingers open for His sake. That loosening — that small rising onto your toes — is the sound of birr coming, at last, within your reach. Let it be an invitation, not a burden. The door has a key, and the key is in your hand. It is simply the thing your hand is afraid to open.

## A Brief Du'a

O *ʿAlīm*, who knows the weight of every gift and the love behind every open and closed hand — loosen our grip on what we love, that we may rise to the righteousness You placed above us. Let us give from the basket of our treasures and not our leftovers, and when no one else sees, let it be enough — more than enough — that **You** know. Āmīn.
