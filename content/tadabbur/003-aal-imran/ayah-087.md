# The Distance They Chose

## Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 87

> [ayah:3:87] أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ جَزَآؤُهُمْ أَنَّ عَلَيْهِمْ لَعْنَةَ ٱللَّهِ وَٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ وَٱلنَّاسِ أَجْمَعِينَ

*Ulā'ika jazā'uhum anna ʿalayhim laʿnata-llāhi wal-malā'ikati wan-nāsi ajmaʿīn.*

**"Those — their recompense is that upon them rests the curse of Allah, and of the angels, and of all people together."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Before we can hear this ayah, we have to feel where it falls.

Picture Madinah in the early years. A community is forming — fragile, hunted, holding onto something it can barely believe it found. And around it move people who *had* believed. Not strangers to faith. Not people who never heard. People who saw the light, walked toward it, stood inside it for a while — and then turned and walked back out into the dark.

The ayah just before this one — ayah 86 — asks a question that almost sounds like grief: *"How shall Allah guide a people who disbelieved after their belief, and after they had witnessed that the Messenger is true, and after the clear signs had come to them?"* Read that slowly. It is not the question you ask about someone who never knew. It is the question you ask about someone who **knew, and left anyway**.

And then comes our ayah. The verdict. The recompense.

Now — here is the thing that should stop us. On its surface, this is one of the heaviest verses in the Qur'an. *The curse of Allah, and of the angels, and of all people together.* You could read it as a door slamming shut. A sentence with no appeal.

But sit with me. Because if you read just two ayahs further — to ayah 89 — Allah says: *"Except those who repent after that and set themselves right."* The door that sounded like it slammed is, in fact, **still open**. The heaviest verdict in this passage is immediately followed by the widest mercy.

So here is our question — the one we need answered before we leave today:

**What kind of curse is it that still has a door left open inside it?**

Let's find out. We'll do it slowly. We'll let the words teach us before we ask them for conclusions.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Word That Points From Far Away — *Ulā'ika* (أُولَـٰئِكَ)

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah.

When you point at something in Arabic, the *word* you choose tells the listener how far away it is. If a thing is near you — in your hand, at your side — you say *hādhā*, "this." If a thing is far — across the field, on the far hill — you say *dhālika*, "that." And for a group of people far away, you say *ulā'ika* — "those, over there."

We do this in English too, but we feel it less. Think of a parent, heartbroken, gesturing toward a child who has left home and gone a path they begged him not to take. They don't say *"my son."* They say, quietly, *"those people."* The grammar of distance is the grammar of estrangement. When we push someone out of our heart, our **language steps back from them** before our feet ever do.

Now look at how this ayah opens.

> **أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ** — *Ulā'ika* — "Those..."

Allah does not say *hā'ulā'i*, "these ones here." He uses the far form. He points at them **from a distance**.

And here is what should make us sit up. Who are "those"? They are the people of ayah 86 — the ones who *disbelieved after their belief*. The ones who walked away.

Notice something. **They are the ones who created the distance.** They were near — they had believed, they had stood inside the light. And then they stepped back. They put the distance there. And the very first word of their recompense simply *names* the distance they made. The grammar does not invent their punishment. It **describes the position they put themselves in.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it only gets deeper. Hold the word *ulā'ika* — "those far ones" — in one hand. We are going to pick up another word later that means *being pushed far away*. And when the two meet, you will see that this ayah is not arbitrary at all. It is shaped like a mirror.

For now, let the first word land: **the recompense of turning away begins by being addressed as one who has turned away.** Before any punishment is named, the distance is already in the pronoun.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Word "Recompense" — and Why It Is Terrifying That It's Used Here — *Jazā'* (جَزَاء)

Here is a concept worth learning carefully, because most of us carry the wrong picture of it.

The word in this ayah is **جَزَآؤُهُمْ** — *jazā'uhum*, "their recompense." The root is *j-z-y*. And in classical Arabic, *jazā'* does not simply mean "punishment." It means **the exact return that corresponds to an act.** A requital that *fits*. It is the word you would use for wages — what is *owed* to you in precise measure for what you did. The same root gives us *jizyah*. It carries the sense of something that *suffices* as a return, that settles the account exactly.

Let me give you the everyday picture. *Jazā'* is the echo in a canyon. You stand at the edge and you shout — and what comes back to you is **your own voice, in your own shape**, returned. Not a punishment imposed from outside. A correspondence. You sent something out; the same thing came back.

Now feel the weight of the word being used *here*.

> أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ **جَزَآؤُهُمْ** — "Those — their *recompense*..."

Allah calls the curse their *jazā'*. Their **exact return**. The thing that corresponds, in precise measure, to what they did.

Let's ask the question the word is begging us to ask: *recompense for what?* And what *is* the return?

Go back to what they did. They had drawn near in faith — and then they pushed it away. They distanced themselves from the source of mercy. They sent that out.

And the echo that comes back? **Distance from mercy.** That is exactly what the rest of the ayah describes. They pushed mercy away; the return that fits is to find themselves far from it.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how disciplined this is? The word *jazā'* tells us this is not a wildness of wrath. It is **correspondence**. They did not receive something foreign to their deed. They received their deed, returned. The canyon gave back the voice that was shouted into it.

And here is the quiet mercy hidden inside even this terrifying word. Because *jazā'* is a *correspondence to an act* — it is bound to the act. **Change the act, and you change what corresponds to it.** A wage stops being owed when the work stops. This is precisely why, two ayahs later, repentance can dissolve the whole thing. The *jazā'* was never a brand burned into the soul. It was an echo. And an echo only lasts as long as you keep shouting.

Hold that. We're coming back to it.

---

## Element Three: The Word "Curse," and the Three Witnesses — *Laʿnah* (لَعْنَة) and *Ajmaʿīn* (أَجْمَعِينَ)

We come now to the word everyone fears, and I want us to actually understand it instead of flinching at it.

> أَنَّ عَلَيْهِمْ **لَعْنَةَ** ٱللَّهِ — "...that upon them is the **curse** of Allah..."

The word is *laʿnah*, from the root *l-ʿ-n*. In English "curse" makes us imagine something almost magical — a spell, a hex, a force flung at someone. **That is not what the Arabic means.**

*Laʿn*, in its root sense, means **to drive away, to expel, to distance.** Al-Muyassar, explaining this very ayah, makes it explicit: *fahum maṭrūdūn min raḥmatillāh* — "they are **driven away from the mercy of Allah**." That is what the curse *is*. Not a bolt of fire. **A distancing.** To be put far from mercy. To be sent out of the circle.

Now — [PAUSE] — pick up the word we set down at the very beginning.

*Ulā'ika.* "Those far ones."

The ayah **opened** by pointing at them from a distance. And the ayah **closes** by naming their recompense as being *driven to a distance* from mercy. The first word and the central word are the **same gesture**. The form of the verse is the meaning of the verse. They stepped back from mercy; they are addressed as those who stand back; and their recompense is to remain at the distance they chose.

This is not coincidence. This is the ayah showing you, in its very architecture, that the punishment of turning away **is the turning away itself, made permanent** — unless something interrupts it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at *who* is named as distancing them. Notice Allah builds a complete cosmos of witnesses, in three tiers:

> لَعْنَةَ **ٱللَّهِ** وَ**ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ** وَ**ٱلنَّاسِ** — "the curse of **Allah**, and the **angels**, and the **people**..."

Three realms. The Divine. The unseen world of the angels. And the seen world of human beings. Above, between, and around. There is no tier of reality left out. Every level of existence is named as turning away from the one who turned away.

And then — the seal:

> **أَجْمَعِينَ** — *ajmaʿīn* — "**all together.**"

The root here is *j-m-ʿ*, the root of *gathering*. Grammatically, *ajmaʿīn* is what Arabic calls *tawkīd* — a corroborative, a word whose only job is to **close every gap** and say: *all of them, with no exception, gathered as one.*

Sit with the loneliness of this. Think of the deepest social exile a person can imagine — to be blocked, unfriended, shut out by everyone they know. Now lift that out of the human scale entirely. This is being out of step with **the whole of creation, gathered together.** The whole gathering faces one way; the soul that turned away faces the other. Not one being in all the realms stands with it.

[PAUSE]

That is the full weight of the verdict. We will not soften it. It is real, and it is total.

---

### The Bridge

So what have the words shown us?

*Ulā'ika* — they are addressed from the distance they created.
*Jazā'* — the curse is not foreign wrath; it is their own deed returned in exact measure, like an echo.
*Laʿnah* — the curse itself *is* distance from mercy — the same distance the opening word named.
*Ajmaʿīn* — and that distance is total: the whole of creation, gathered, faces away.

The verse is a closed mirror. Distance at the start, distance at the heart, totality at the end.

And yet — we said there is a door left open inside it. Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words, but about **reality itself** — about us, and about the One who left that door open.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Particular Grief of Leaving After You Arrived

Let me start where you live, not where the ayah lives.

There is a specific kind of pain that only exists for people who *had* something. The pain of the empty side of the bed is sharper than the pain of having never married. The ache of the friendship that broke is deeper than the ache of the friend you never made. **You cannot lose what you never held.** And there is a grief reserved entirely for the one who held it and let go.

This is the exact territory of our passage. Remember ayah 86: *"a people who disbelieved **after their belief**."* The Qur'an is not, here, talking about someone who never came near. It is talking about the one who tasted the water and then chose the desert. The one who *knew* — who "witnessed that the Messenger is true," who saw "the clear signs come to them" — and then turned.

In our generation we have a word for the shape of this journey. We call it *deconstruction* — the slow walking-back of a faith once held. And let me be careful and fair: not everyone wrestling with doubt is in this ayah. Doubt that is *seeking* is not the thing being described. The Qur'an is describing a *settled turning* — *kafarū*, a deliberate covering-over of a light you already saw clearly. Wrestling toward the light is not what is condemned here. Walking away from it with your eyes open is.

So why does the Qur'an treat this turning so much more gravely than ordinary distance from God?

Here is the first movement: **because the one who left already had the evidence.** For the one who never knew, mercy is patient — *"and We never punish until We send a messenger"* (17:15). The signs are still on their way. But the one who turned away after belief has nothing left to wait for. The signs already arrived. The light already came. There is no further proof on its way to change their mind — they already met the proof, and stepped back from it. Ayah 86's grief — *"how shall Allah guide..."* — is not the grief of God being unwilling. It is the picture of a soul that has **used up its own openings**.

Now the second movement, and it turns everything. Look again at *jazā'* — the echo, the exact return. If the recompense corresponds to the act, then notice what their act *was*: it was an act of **distancing themselves**. So when the ayah says they are "distanced from mercy," it is not God reaching out to push them. **It is God describing where they have already walked to.** The *laʿnah* is not a hand that shoves. It is the name for the place a person ends up when they keep walking away from the only source of mercy there is.

[PAUSE]

And now the third movement — the door. Ayah 89: *"Except those who repent after that, and set themselves right — for Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."*

Feel what this means against everything we built. The "distance" was never a *wall*. It was a *direction*. The whole tragedy of these verses is the tragedy of a person facing the wrong way. And the moment described in ayah 89 — *tāba*, "to repent," which in its root means simply **to turn back** — is the moment the person stops walking away and turns around. The instant they turn, the distance begins to close. Because mercy never moved. They did.

Al-Qurtubi notes precisely this: the curse stands *"in dāmū ʿalā kufrihim"* — **"if they persist in their disbelief."** The verdict is bound to the persistence. It is a *condition*, not a brand. Remove the persistence, and you remove the curse.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It means the most dangerous word in your spiritual life is not *"I fell."* It is *"too late."* The ayah's architecture — the mirror of distance — exists precisely to be broken by one act: turning around. So the practical application is brutally simple. If there is something you walked away from — a prayer you abandoned, a closeness you once had and quietly let die — the question is never *how far have I gone.* Distance is not the obstacle. **Direction is.** Turn, and the math of the echo reverses. Stop shouting "away," and the canyon goes quiet.

## Theme Two: The Whole of Creation Has a Direction — and So Do You

Let me build you a picture before we name the theme.

Imagine standing in a vast crowd — every person you can see, stretching to the horizon — and a great announcement comes, and the entire crowd turns to face one direction. And you, for whatever reason, turn the other way. You are not punished by anyone. No one strikes you. But you experience something almost physical: the sense of being *out of step with everything.* Of standing crosswise to reality itself.

Now recall the three witnesses of our ayah: **Allah, the angels, and all people, gathered together.** What is this image actually telling us? It is telling us that **existence has a grain.** Creation is not neutral. The angels glorify; the heavens and the earth declare; even, the Qur'an tells us elsewhere, the thunder and the birds praise (24:41, 17:44). All of it is oriented. All of it faces one way. The whole of being is, by its nature, *turned toward its Maker.*

So when the ayah says the curse of "all of them together" rests on the one who turned away — here is the deeper reading. It is not, in the first place, that the angels and the people are *angry* at him in some petty sense. It is that **he is now standing crosswise to the grain of everything that exists.** Every other thing is oriented toward mercy. He alone has turned his back to it. The "curse of all" is the felt reality of being **out of step with the entire orchestra of creation** — the one instrument playing against the score.

[PAUSE]

Let this sit, because it dignifies even the warning. The ayah is not describing a cruel and crowded mob. It is describing a *cosmos with a direction*, and a soul that has chosen to face away from it. The loneliness of the *laʿnah* is the loneliness of being the only thing in creation that is not, at this moment, turned toward home.

And now feel the flip side, the consolation hidden in the same image. If creation has a grain — if everything is already turned toward Allah — then **the moment you turn back, you are not turning alone.** You are falling back into step with everything. The angels who "seek forgiveness for those on earth" (42:5) are now facing the same way as you. The whole orchestra you were playing against — you are suddenly inside it. *Tawbah* is not a lonely act of crawling back to an angry God. It is the relief of a single dancer finally catching the rhythm the whole floor was already keeping.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It reframes obedience itself. Every act of turning toward Allah — the prayer you make on time, the wrong you refuse, the eye you lower — is you falling *into step* with the deepest movement of reality. You are not forcing yourself against the grain of the universe to please a distant God. You are aligning with the way *everything already is.* And every act of turning away is the harder thing, the unnatural thing — swimming against the current of all creation. So when faith feels like effort and sin feels like ease, remember the orchestra: the music was always playing toward Him. You were only ever asked to face the same way as the stars.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now that we've done the work, look at the shape of the whole verse.

> **أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ** جَزَآؤُهُمْ أَنَّ عَلَيْهِمْ **لَعْنَةَ** ٱللَّهِ وَٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ وَٱلنَّاسِ **أَجْمَعِينَ**

It is a mirror built on one idea: **distance.**

- It **opens** with *ulā'ika* — pointing at them from far away. *Distance named.*
- Its **heart** is *laʿnah* — being driven far from mercy. *Distance as recompense.*
- And in between sits *jazā'* — the quiet logic that the distance they receive is the exact echo of the distance they made.
- It **closes** with *ajmaʿīn* — and the distance is total, the whole of creation turned the other way.

The verse does not impose a punishment from outside. **It describes, with perfect symmetry, the position of a soul that turned away** — and holds that position up like a mirror so the person can see exactly where they are standing.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The *words* showed us a verse shaped like distance — open, center, and close all naming the same gesture. The *themes* showed us why: because turning away from mercy is, quite literally, *creating distance from the only place mercy lives.* The grammar of the ayah and the spiritual reality it describes are one thing. To turn from God is to walk away; to be "cursed" is to be far; and the recompense simply lets you stand where your own feet carried you.

And both directions arrive at the same hidden door: **distance is not a wall, it is a direction — and direction can be reversed.** This is why the most fearsome verdict in the passage is followed two lines later by *"except those who turn back."* The mirror was built to be broken by a single movement: turning around.

## Questions to Carry

1. Is there something I once held in faith — a closeness, a practice, a certainty — that I have quietly walked away from, while telling myself the distance is now too far to cross?
2. When I feel far from Allah, do I assume *He* moved? Or can I sit honestly with the possibility that I am simply facing the wrong way?
3. The word *jazā'* says my returns correspond to my acts. What is the *echo* I am currently shouting into the canyon of my life?
4. When obedience feels like swimming against the current, have I forgotten that I am actually the one out of step — that the whole of creation is already turned toward Him?
5. If turning back closes the distance *immediately*, what exactly am I waiting for?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The recompense of turning away from Allah is simply being left at the distance you walked to — a distance that the smallest act of turning back begins, at once, to close.**

## The Closing Invitation

Do not leave today afraid of a curse. Leave today understanding what the curse *is* — and therefore how it ends. It is distance. And you were never told the distance is uncrossable; you were told, two ayahs later, that turning around is enough. So let this be the shift: stop measuring how far you've gone, and check which way you're facing. Mercy did not move. The whole of creation is already turned toward Him. You are invited — gently, this very moment — to face the same way.

## A Du'a

O Allah, You named their recompense as distance, and then in Your mercy left the door of return wide open — so do not let us mistake distance for a wall. Whenever we have turned away, turn our hearts back before our walking carries us too far to see the door. And gather us, O Allah, with the *ajmaʿīn* — with all of creation that is turned toward You — and never leave us the one soul facing the other way. *Āmīn.*
