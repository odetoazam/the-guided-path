# The Argument You Were Never Equipped to Win

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with the sound of them before we say anything about the meaning.

> هَـٰٓأَنتُمْ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ حَـٰجَجْتُمْ فِيمَا لَكُم بِهِۦ عِلْمٌ فَلِمَ تُحَآجُّونَ فِيمَا لَيْسَ لَكُم بِهِۦ عِلْمٌ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ

*Hā-antum hā'ulā'i ḥājajtum fīmā lakum bihi ʿilm, falima tuḥājjūna fīmā laysa lakum bihi ʿilm, wallāhu yaʿlamu wa antum lā taʿlamūn.*

"Here you are — the very ones who argued about that of which you have some knowledge. So why do you argue about that of which you have no knowledge at all? And Allah knows, while you do not know."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel the weight of this, you have to stand where its first listeners stood.

Picture Madinah in its early years. A new community is forming, and around it stand older communities — learned, scriptural, proud of their lineage of prophets. And a conversation is happening, sometimes warmly, often not, about who owns Ibrahim.

Because everyone wanted Ibrahim. Ibrahim is the patriarch, the father, the friend of God. To claim his religion is to claim the deepest root of legitimacy. So one group says, *Ibrahim was one of us — a Jew.* Another says, *No, he was of us — a Christian.* And both are building a case, marshalling arguments, leaning forward into the dispute.

And the Qur'an, just one verse before this, quietly drops a fact that should have ended the whole thing: the Torah and the Gospel **were not sent down until after him** (3:65). Ibrahim lived centuries before Moses, centuries more before Jesus. The categories you are fighting over did not yet exist when he walked the earth. You are arguing about which room of a house a man lived in — when the man died before the house was built. Ibn Kathir lets the rebuke land with its full edge: *have you then no sense?* — *a-fa-lā taʿqilūn* — the dispute is not merely mistaken, it is a failure of plain reasoning.

And notice where the passage is heading. One breath later (3:67) the Qur'an settles the whole question outright: *Ibrahim was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a pure ḥanīf, a Muslim, and he was not of the idolaters.* The classical commentators read this entire exchange as, in the end, a *tabriʾa* — an exoneration of Ibrahim (so al-Jalalayn), clearing him of every party's claim. The verse we are sitting in is the hinge of that clearing.

That is the moment this ayah lands in. Not a lecture about history. A mirror held up to the *posture* of people who argue.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I want you to forget it until we earn it.

This verse does not say *you are wrong*. It does not refute the claim. It does something far more surgical. It draws a line — a clean, bright line — straight through the middle of your arguing. On one side: the things you actually know something about. On the other side: the things you are just *guessing* about with the same confidence.

And it asks one question, one of the most uncomfortable questions in the entire Qur'an:

**Why does your certainty not shrink when your knowledge runs out?**

Stay with that discomfort. We're going to live inside it for a while.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that means "to aim at each other"

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the verse, because without it the heart of this ayah stays closed.

Arabic builds meaning into the *shape* of a verb, not just its letters. There is a particular pattern — scholars call it Form III, the pattern of *mufāʿala* — and its core job is to express an action done **back and forth between two parties**. It takes a simple action and makes it a contest. The plain verb *kataba* means "he wrote." Push it into this pattern — *kātaba* — and it means "he corresponded," he wrote-and-was-written-to, letters flying both directions. *Qatala*, "he killed." *Qātala* — "he fought," blow for blow. The pattern itself means: *two sides, facing each other, each acting on the other.*

Now look at the verb in our ayah:

> حَـٰجَجْتُمْ ... تُحَآجُّونَ

*ḥājajtum ... tuḥājjūn.*

This is that pattern. Form III. Not "you spoke." Not "you believed." **You argued — at each other.** Mutual contention. Two sides leaning in, each pushing against the other.

Now let me open the root, because the root is breathtaking.

The root is *ḥ-j-j* — ح ج ج. And here is what the lexicographers tell us lives at the bottom of it: the core idea is *qaṣd* — **to aim, to head toward, to intend a destination.** From this one root, Arabic grows three words that at first seem unrelated:

- *ḥujja* — a proof, an argument, the thing you aim at your opponent to overcome them.
- *maḥajja* — a clear road, the path you head down toward where you're going.
- And *Ḥajj* — the pilgrimage. The journey of heading toward the House.

[PAUSE]

Let that sit. The same root that gives us *pilgrimage* gives us *argument*. Because both are acts of *aiming yourself at a destination*.

So what is a *muḥājja*, the thing they were doing? It is two people who have each made the *other person their destination*. Each one is aiming his proofs at the other like a traveller aiming at a city. The goal is no longer truth. The goal is *the other person's defeat*. You have turned a human being into the target you are heading toward.

Notice what that quietly exposes. When the verse calls their activity *muḥājja*, it is not neutrally saying "you discussed." It is saying: **you turned this into a contest where the prize is winning, and the opponent is the destination.** The truth about Ibrahim was never where you were heading. *Each other* was where you were heading.

And here is the thing we all need to hear: this is the most natural drift in the world. A conversation that begins as "what is true?" curdles, almost without our noticing, into "how do I win?" The root tells you the danger is built into the act of arguing itself. The moment your opponent becomes your destination, truth has already left the room.

One word — *tuḥājjūn* — and we have a complete diagnosis of what goes wrong in human dispute.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The tense that changes underneath you

Now I want to show you something that is invisible in English translation and devastating in Arabic.

Look again at the two verbs. They are the *same word* — and yet they are not.

> حَـٰجَجْتُمْ — *ḥājajtum* — "you argued"
>
> تُحَآجُّونَ — *tuḥājjūn* — "you argue / you keep arguing"

Arabic, like every language, marks time on its verbs, but it does it through completion. The first form, *ḥājajtum*, is what grammarians call the perfect — a *completed* action. Done. Sealed. In the past. The second, *tuḥājjūn*, is the imperfect — an action that is *ongoing, unfinished, still happening, still renewing itself right now*.

So read the verse again with new eyes:

*You argued* — past, completed, a thing that already happened — *about what you had some knowledge of.* Fine. That's behind you.

*So why do you keep on arguing* — present, live, unfolding as we speak — *about what you have no knowledge of?*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the trap closing?

The first argument is granted to them gently, as a finished fact: *you did this, and you had some grounds.* But the second verb is left wide open in the present tense — because it is **still happening.** They have not stopped. The verse catches them in the act. Not "why did you argue" but "why **are you, right now, still** arguing" — about the very thing you are least equipped to argue about.

This is the verse standing in front of you while your mouth is still moving. It is not describing a past mistake. It is interrupting a present one.

And there is a deeper cut here. The thing you had knowledge of — that argument is *over*, completed, in the perfect tense. But the thing you have no knowledge of — that one *won't stop.* Notice the pattern of the human heart that the grammar just exposed: **the less we actually know, the longer we argue.** The settled knowledge concludes. The empty guessing runs forever. Where there is real ʿilm, the matter rests. Where there is none, the noise never ends.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The knowledge that comes in two sizes — and the knowledge that has no object at all

This is the spine of the verse, so let me build it carefully.

Look at the two parallel phrases. They are almost identical — and the almost is everything.

> فِيمَا لَكُم بِهِۦ عِلْمٌ — *fīmā lakum bihi ʿilm* — "about that of which you have knowledge"
>
> فِيمَا لَيْسَ لَكُم بِهِۦ عِلْمٌ — *fīmā laysa lakum bihi ʿilm* — "about that of which you have NO knowledge"

One word inserted — *laysa*, "there is not" — flips the entire ground beneath their feet. The architecture is a perfect mirror with a single negation snapped into the second half. Same people, same activity, same confidence — only the *knowledge* has vanished. And their behavior didn't change at all. That is the indictment: the arguing stayed exactly the same on both sides of the line, but the knowledge fell away on one side. **Their certainty was never coming from knowledge in the first place.** If it were, it would have collapsed the moment the knowledge did.

Now, two precise things about the word *ʿilm* here that the grammar hands us.

First — it is *ʿilmun*, indefinite. Not *al-ʿilm*, "the knowledge," the complete and total knowledge. Just *ʿilmun* — "**some** knowledge, a portion of knowledge." And the classical commentators are careful to say what that grounded knowledge actually was. Al-Jalalayn glosses the first clause as the matter of Moses and Jesus — *and the claim they staked to be following the religion of those two prophets.* Al-Muyassar makes it more concrete still: the thing they had real knowledge of was *the affair of their own religion*, what they held true in their own books — and the dispute they waged about it was one they brought to the Prophet ﷺ himself. They had grounds, in other words, to argue about their *own* scripture. What they had no grounds for was Ibrahim, who stood centuries before all of it. And yet even on the side where they had a basis, the Qur'an does not flatter them with mastery. It grants them *a measure*. Some. Enough to have a basis. The verse is scrupulously fair: it concedes them exactly as much as they actually had, and not one grain more. That fairness is itself a teaching — the One rebuking them is precise even in His rebuke.

Second, and this is the one I want you to carry home. Look at the final clause:

> وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ
>
> *wallāhu yaʿlamu wa antum lā taʿlamūn* — "And Allah knows, while you do not know."

Read it as a grammarian reads it. *Allāhu yaʿlamu* — "Allah knows." Knows **what?**

The verse does not say.

There is no object. The sentence simply stops: *Allah knows.* Full stop. In Arabic, a transitive verb like *yaʿlam* is reaching out for an object — knows *what?* — and the verse deliberately gives it none. And what happens when you remove the object from "He knows"? The knowing becomes **unbounded.** He doesn't know *this matter* or *that fact* — He simply **knows**, with no edge, no limit, no horizon. Drop the object and the knowing expands to fill everything.

The classical reading arrives at the same place from another angle. Al-Muyassar fills in the object the grammar withholds and writes: *He knows all matters even in their hiddenness* — *yaʿlamu l-umūra ʿalā khafāʾihā* — the things buried, concealed, sealed away from every human eye. So the knowing is not only unbounded in *extent*; it reaches precisely into the places where your knowledge cannot follow — the hidden, the long-buried, the truth of a man who walked the earth before any of your categories were born.

Now look at its mirror: *wa antum lā taʿlamūn* — "and you do not know." Also no object. Also unbounded — but in the opposite direction. Your *not-knowing* spreads out just as wide.

[PAUSE]

Sit in the space between those two open phrases. On one side, a knowing with no boundary. On the other, a not-knowing with no boundary. Allah did not write "Allah knows the truth about Ibrahim and you don't." That would have been about Ibrahim. By stripping away the object, the verse lifts off the page and becomes about **knowledge itself** — about the infinite distance between the One who knows and the ones who do not.

This is the absence that speaks louder than any presence. The missing object is the whole point. The verse refuses to name *what* Allah knows, because the answer is: **everything, and you cannot even conceive the categories.**

So here is what three words of grammar — a verb form, a tense, and a missing object — have shown us. They argued like combatants aiming at each other. They kept arguing even as their knowledge ran out. And standing over the whole scene is a single quiet sentence with no object at all: *He knows; you do not.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the thing your own heart does every single day.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The strange law that confidence rises as knowledge falls

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You have done this. I have done this. Think of the last time you argued hard about something — really dug in, raised your voice a little, felt your chest tighten. Now ask yourself, honestly: *how much did you actually know about it?*

There is a cruel and well-documented pattern in human beings. The less we genuinely understand a subject, the *more* certain we tend to feel about it. The true expert is full of qualifications — "well, it depends," "in most cases," "I'd want to check." The person who read one headline is the one ready to fight you in the comments. Real knowledge makes you careful. A little knowledge makes you loud.

We have a thousand modern names for this — the loudest opinions on the topics we've studied least, the supreme confidence of the person who has never done the thing they're criticizing. But the Qur'an named the mechanism fourteen centuries ago, and named it more precisely than any of our slogans: *falima tuḥājjūna fīmā laysa lakum bihi ʿilm* — **why do you argue about that of which you have no knowledge?**

Notice it does not say "why are you *wrong*." Being wrong is forgivable; everyone is wrong sometimes. It says: why do you carry *this much heat* into a place where you have *this little light*? The sin being named is not error. It is the **mismatch** — the gap between how hard you're pushing and how little you actually have to push with.

And remember what we found in the grammar. The argument they *had* knowledge of was in the perfect tense — finished, concluded, at rest. The argument they had *no* knowledge of was in the imperfect — endless, unfinished, still going. This is the law the verse exposes: **knowledge brings things to rest; ignorance keeps them spinning.** When you truly know something, you can let it go — you don't need to win, because the matter is settled in reality whether anyone agrees with you or not. It is precisely the things we *don't* know that we cannot stop fighting about, because there is no solid floor of knowledge to stand on, so we substitute *volume* for *ground*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the human door back in. The next time you feel that familiar heat rising in an argument — that need to win — try asking yourself the question this verse asks. Not "am I right?" That question keeps you in the fight. Ask instead: *"How much do I actually know here — and is my certainty borrowing against knowledge I don't have?"*

Because much of the unbearable noise of our age — online, at the table, in our own heads — is people arguing in the present tense, endlessly, about things they have no *ʿilm* of, with a confidence they never earned. And the verse offers an escape from that noise. It is not "learn more so you can argue better." It is something more freeing: **you are allowed to not-know. You are allowed to put the argument down.** The verb can stay in the past tense. You can let it be finished.

What this changes tomorrow: pick one topic you habitually argue about with heat and little study — and the next time it comes up, say the four hardest words in any language. *"I don't actually know."* And feel what happens in your chest when you do. That is the verse working in you.

### Theme Two: The peace on the other side of "I do not know"

Now let me take you to the deepest place in this verse, because the last six words are not a humiliation. They are a doorway.

*Wallāhu yaʿlamu wa antum lā taʿlamūn.* "And Allah knows, while you do not."

We hear that and instinctively flinch, because we've been trained to experience "you don't know" as a *defeat*. The whole architecture of the ego is built on the project of *seeming to know* — having the answer, holding the position, never being caught empty. To not-know feels like exposure. Like nakedness.

But turn the sentence over and look at its other face.

If *Allah* knows — fully, with no object and no limit, the way the grammar showed us — then the truth about every matter you are agonizing over is **already held.** It is not lost. It is not undecided. It is not waiting for your argument to rescue it. The reality of Ibrahim, the reality of every disputed thing, the reality of *your own situation that you lie awake turning over* — all of it is fully known, perfectly, by the One who made it. Nothing is leaking out of the universe because you couldn't prove your point.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight lift? "You do not know" is only crushing if *you* are the last line of defense for the truth — if it all depends on you getting it right and winning the argument. But the verse pairs your not-knowing with **His knowing**, and that pairing is mercy. It says: *the truth has a Keeper, and it is not you.* You can set down the impossible burden of having to know everything, because you were never the one holding it up.

This is the difference between two kinds of not-knowing. There is the anxious not-knowing of the person who *needs* to know and can't — that one is torment. And there is the restful not-knowing of the person who has handed the knowing to the One who actually has it — *wallāhu yaʿlam* — and that one is peace. The same three words, "I don't know," are a wound in the first mouth and a *release* in the second. What changes is whether you finish the sentence the way the verse does: "...and **Allah** knows."

Watch how the Prophet ﷺ himself modeled this. When he was asked about things beyond him, the answer that came was *qul lā aʿlamu* — "say, I do not know" — and *qul innamā al-ʿilmu ʿinda-Llāh*, "the knowledge is only with Allah" (67:26). The greatest of creation was given, as a *teaching*, the dignity of saying "I don't know" — because true greatness was never about pretending to be God. It was about knowing, with total clarity, exactly where you end and where He begins.

And notice the mercy hidden in the order of the final clause. The verse does not end on *you do not know* — it does not leave you in the dark. It says **Allah knows** *first*, and *then* "you do not know." Your ignorance is placed inside the frame of His knowledge, never standing alone. You are not abandoned in the dark. You are a small not-knowing held inside an infinite Knowing. That is the safest place a human being can stand.

[PAUSE]

What this changes tomorrow: the next time you are spinning at 2am over a thing you cannot figure out — a relationship, a fear, an outcome you can't control, a question with no answer in reach — try ending the spinning the way the verse ends it. Not "I'll figure it out." You might not. But: *"Allah knows. And I do not. And that is enough."* Hand the knowing back to its Owner. And sleep.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now that we've done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with terrifying symmetry.

It opens by pointing a finger: *hā-antum hā'ulā'i* — "here you are, you people" — the *hā* of alerting, of grabbing attention, like a hand on your shoulder turning you around to face yourself.

Then it draws the line:

- *You argued* (past, finished) — about what you had **some** knowledge of.
- *You keep arguing* (present, endless) — about what you have **no** knowledge of.

A perfect mirror, with one word — *laysa*, "not" — dropped into the second half to reverse it. Same people. Same heat. Knowledge present, then knowledge gone — and the heat *never changed*.

And then, over the whole scene, the verse rises to its summit and resolves every line beneath it:

> *wallāhu yaʿlamu* — and Allah knows [— no object, no limit —]
> *wa antum lā taʿlamūn* — and you do not know.

The two halves of the human argument — "what you know" and "what you don't" — both collapse upward into this. Because next to *His* knowing, even the side where you had *some* knowledge is, in the end, *not knowing*. The whole disputing mob, both their confident half and their guessing half, stands under one sentence: *you do not know.* And one Voice stands above it: *He knows.*

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight from two directions.

The **grammar** showed us a verb of mutual combat (*tuḥājjūn*) that never stops in the present tense, attached to a knowledge that has run out — and a final "He knows" with no object, expanding to fill all of reality.

The **themes** showed us the human heart doing exactly that: arguing loudest where it knows least, unable to stop — and the peace that arrives the moment it hands the knowing back to the One whose knowing has no edge.

The verse diagnoses the disease and hands you the cure in nineteen words. The disease: confidence unhooked from knowledge, contention that has made winning its destination. The cure: *wallāhu yaʿlamu wa antum lā taʿlamūn* — and the rest that lives inside those words once you stop hearing them as a defeat and start hearing them as a release.

### Questions to carry

1. What is one argument I am holding right now in the *present tense* — still going, still unsettled — that I have almost no real *ʿilm* about? What would it cost me to let the verb become past tense?
2. When I argue, am I aiming at the *truth* — or have I made the *other person* my destination, the thing I'm trying to overcome?
3. Where in my life is "I don't know" a wound I'm hiding — and where could it become a release I'm refusing?
4. Do I let my certainty *shrink* when my knowledge runs out? Or does it stay loud regardless?
5. Can I say *"Allah knows, and I do not"* about the thing that keeps me awake — and actually mean it enough to sleep?

### One-sentence distillation

When your knowledge runs out, your arguing should too — because the truth you're fighting over is already fully held by the One who knows, and your only task is to know where you end and He begins.

### The closing invitation

You don't have to win every argument. You were never supposed to. There is a line running through your life between what you actually know and what you only feel strongly about — and this verse is an invitation to find that line and *honor* it. To let go where the knowledge ends. To carry your certainty only as far as your *ʿilm* will fund it, and not one step further.

And on the other side of that honesty is not humiliation. It is rest. The rest of a small knower who has finally handed the impossible weight of knowing-everything back to the One who has carried it all along.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You know and we do not. Teach us to hold our certainty only as far as You have given us light, and to lay down every argument that has outrun our knowledge. And where we cannot know — let us find rest, not fear, in the truth that *You* know. آمين.
