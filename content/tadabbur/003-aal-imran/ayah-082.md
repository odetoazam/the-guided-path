# Turning Away After the Covenant: The Geometry of *Fisq*

> فَمَن تَوَلَّىٰ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْفَـٰسِقُونَ
>
> *Fa-man tawallā baʿda dhālika fa-ulāʾika humu-l-fāsiqūn*
>
> "Then whoever turns away after that — it is they who are the defiantly disobedient."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world before the verse

To stand inside this ayah, you cannot start at the ayah. You have to start one verse earlier, in a scene that has no equal in the entire Qur'an.

In verse 81, Allah pulls back the curtain on a moment before all of history — or above all of history — and shows us something almost too large to hold. He says He took a covenant. Not from a person. Not from a nation. **From the prophets.** From every single one of them. Allah took a binding pledge from Nūḥ, from Ibrāhīm, from Mūsā, from ʿĪsā — from the whole golden chain of the messengers — and the content of the pledge was this: *whatever Scripture and wisdom I give you, then a messenger comes after, confirming what is with you — you will most certainly believe in him, and you will most certainly support him.*

Sit with the weight of that for a moment. These are the greatest human beings who ever lived. The ones chosen to carry revelation. And even *they* were bound by a covenant. Even *they* were told: your station does not exempt you from recognizing the truth when it comes; it obligates you to it more than anyone. Allah ends verse 81 by asking them directly — *"Do you affirm, and do you take up My burden on that?"* — and they answer: *"We affirm."* And Allah says: *"Then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses."*

[PAUSE]

So now there is a covenant on the table. Affirmed. Witnessed. Sealed at the highest possible level of creation.

And then comes verse 82. Seven words. A door swings shut.

### The hook

Here is what should stop us. The previous verse is enormous — prophets, scriptures, wisdom, witnessing, the divine "I am with you." And then the consequence of breaking all of that is delivered in a sentence so short you could miss it: *"Then whoever turns away after that — it is they who are the fāsiqūn."*

That's it. No thunder. No catalogue of punishments. Just a quiet, devastating naming.

But hidden inside those seven words are three things I want us to uncover slowly. First: the verb for "turns away" comes from a root that, in almost every other form, means *closeness, loyalty, protection.* The Arabic for "to turn your back" is built from the word for "to be someone's closest ally." Why? Second: there is a tiny extra pronoun in this verse that grammatically does nothing — and yet changes everything. And third: the word *fāsiqūn* itself. We translate it "defiantly disobedient." But the Arabs first used this word for something you have held in your hand a thousand times — **a date splitting out of its skin.**

Let's earn each of these.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tawallā* — and the root that holds both loyalty and betrayal

Before we touch the word, let me give you a concept from how Arabic is built, because without it the verse stays flat.

In Arabic, almost every word grows from a three-letter root, like a seed. From that one seed, the language grows a whole tree of related words by changing the pattern — the vowels, the doublings, the prefixes. Each "pattern" is called a *form*. And here is the beautiful part: all the words on one tree share a hidden DNA. The root carries a core meaning, and every branch is a different expression of that same core.

Hold that idea. Now look at our verb.

> تَوَلَّىٰ — *tawallā* — "he turned away"

The root underneath it is **و-ل-ي (w-l-y).** Now — where else have you met this root?

You have met it everywhere. *Walī* — a close friend, a protector, a guardian. *Mawlā* — a patron, a master, an intimate ally. *Wilāya* — guardianship, the bond of allegiance. *Awliyāʾ Allāh* — the friends of Allah, those closest to Him. When Allah says in Sūrat al-Baqarah, *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū* — "Allah is the *walī* of those who believe" — that is this exact root. The root **w-l-y at its core means nearness. Drawing close. Standing on someone's side.**

[PAUSE]

So how — *how* — does the very same root give us *tawallā*, "to turn away"?

Let's sit with this, because it is not an accident of language. It is a window.

The root w-l-y is fundamentally about *which way you are facing.* It is about orientation, about turning toward. A *walī* is the one you turn toward, the one whose side you take. And the moment you take this same root and put it into this particular form — Form V, *tafaʿʿala*, *tawallā* — you get the act of turning *itself.* In classical usage *tawallā* literally means *to turn one's face, to turn one's body, to present someone with your back instead of your front.*

So the tragedy is encoded in the grammar. The faculty that is *meant* for drawing near is the very faculty being used to turn away. It is the same muscle. The one who could have been a *walī* — who could have turned *toward* the truth and stood at its side — uses the exact same God-given capacity for allegiance, and turns it the other way.

There is one more thing the form tells us. Form V — *tafaʿʿala* — in Arabic typically carries a **reflexive** weight: the action curves back onto the doer himself. Not "something turned him away." *He turned himself away.* This is not someone who was pushed. Not someone who never heard. This is a deliberate pivot of the self, performed by the self, against the self. (This is the documented function of the form here — reflexive, not caused-by-another.)

And notice what the classical mufassirūn do with this word. Al-Jalālayn glosses *tawallā* simply as *aʿraḍa* — "he turned aside, he averted himself." Al-Muyassar expands it: *he turned away from the call of Islam after this clarification and this covenant.* The turning is not random. **It is turning away from something specific that was already presented and already affirmed.**

Al-Ṭabarī goes further still, and his reading adds something the shorter glosses leave folded up. He unpacks *tawallā* as a turning away from *two* things at once: from *belief* in the messengers, and from their *nuṣrah* — their active support and defense. Remember that verse 81 bound the prophets to both: *"you will most certainly believe in him, and you will most certainly support him."* So the turning away al-Ṭabarī describes is not merely a private withholding of assent; it is also the withdrawal of the hand that was pledged to help. And he names the act for what it is — *adbara* ("he turned his back") and *nakatha ʿahdahu wa mīthāqahu*: "he broke his covenant and his pledge." The turning away is not mere disinterest. It is the violation of a sworn bond — to turn the back, in al-Ṭabarī's reading, is to break a promise you had already given.

What this single word reveals: the failure described here is not ignorance. It is not the failure of someone who never faced the truth. It is the failure of someone who faced it, who had the full capacity to stand at its side — and who used that very capacity to pivot away.

### Element Two: The little pronoun that does nothing — and changes everything

Now I want to show you something that, in English, is literally untranslatable, and most translations quietly swallow it.

Look at the back half of the verse:

> فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْفَـٰسِقُونَ — *fa-ulāʾika **humu**-l-fāsiqūn*

The straightforward way to say "those are the fāsiqūn" in Arabic would be just: *ulāʾika-l-fāsiqūn.* "Those — the fāsiqūn." Subject, predicate, done. It would be complete. It would be grammatically perfect.

But Allah inserts an extra word in the middle: **هُم — *hum* — "they."** *Those — they — the fāsiqūn.* If you translate it word for word it sounds redundant, almost broken English: "Then those, they, the disobedient."

Here is the concept you need. In Arabic, a pronoun dropped into the middle of a sentence like this, between the subject and what's being said about it, has a name: *ḍamīr al-faṣl* — **the pronoun of separation.** And it is one of the most powerful tools of emphasis in the language. It does not add information. It adds *force.* It does two things at once.

First, it **restricts.** It builds a wall around the predicate and says: *this, and only this.* "Those — they and no one else — are the fāsiqūn." It locks the description onto them.

Second, when it is paired with the definite article on the predicate — and here we have *al-fāsiqūn,* "**the** fāsiqūn," with the *al-* — it signals the *complete, quintessential* version of the description. Not "those are some people who do some wrong." But "**those are *the* transgressors** — the very definition of the word, the full and finished article."

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference. Imagine a teacher returning an exam. One says: "You made some mistakes." Another looks you in the eye and says, slowly: "You — you are *the* one who failed this." Same fact. Completely different gravity. The second sentence isn't carrying more *information.* It is carrying more *weight.* It is a verdict, not a comment.

That is what this *hum* is doing. After the colossal build-up of the prophetic covenant, Allah does not say, casually, "such people are wrongdoers." He renders a sealed verdict: *those — they, precisely they — are the complete fāsiqūn.* The smallest word in the verse is the one carrying the judgment.

And do you see the symmetry of mercy and severity here? The grammar of restriction — "they and only they" — also quietly tells us something gentle: *this verdict falls only on the one who actually turned away after the covenant.* It is not a blanket thrown over everyone. It is a wall built precisely around the one act of betrayal. The same emphasis that condemns also limits.

What this little pronoun reveals: Allah is not describing these people. He is *naming* them. The verse is a sentence in the legal sense — a judgment handed down — and the *ḍamīr al-faṣl* is the gavel.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Al-Fāsiqūn* — the date that breaks out of its skin

Now the word the gavel comes down on. We translate it "the defiantly disobedient." It is accurate. But it is the dried-out, abstract version of an image that is alive.

> ٱلْفَـٰسِقُونَ — *al-fāsiqūn* — from the root **ف-س-ق (f-s-q).**

Here is the concept of how Arabic words carry meaning. The most abstract, moral, spiritual words in the language almost always began as *physical, sensory, touchable* words. The Arabs named the unseen by pointing at the seen. So to feel what *fisq* really means, we have to find the physical thing the desert Arab was looking at when he first used the word.

And the classical lexicons preserve it exactly. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, in his *Mufradāt*, and the great dictionary *Lisān al-ʿArab*, both record the origin: the Arabs said *fasaqati-r-ruṭabah* — **the ripe date came out of its skin.** When a date swells and breaks out of the protective husk that contained it, when it bursts its own covering — that is *fasaqa.* The core meaning of *fisq* is **to exit. To break out of an enclosure. To come out of the boundary that was holding you.**

[PAUSE]

Now everything changes.

Look at what the mufassirūn say with this image in hand. Al-Ṭabarī — the earliest and most exhaustive of the commentators — closes his comment on this very verse by defining the fāsiqūn in a single word: *al-khārijūn*, "those who have gone out." Al-Qurṭubī says the same: *al-fāsiq* is *al-khārij* — "the one who has gone out." He repeats it for emphasis: the fāsiqūn are *al-khārijūn ʿani-l-īmān* — those who have exited from faith. Al-Baghawī: *al-ʿāṣūn al-khārijūn ʿani-l-īmān* — the disobedient ones who have gone out from belief. Al-Muyassar makes the boundary explicit: *al-khārijūn ʿan dīni-llāh wa ṭāʿati rabbihim* — those who have exited the religion of Allah and the obedience of their Lord. Four lexicographers and exegetes, across centuries, reach for the same image: not "wrongdoers," but *those who went out.*

Do you see it? The fāsiq is not someone *outside* who never came in. The fāsiq is someone who **was inside the skin** — inside the boundary of the covenant, inside the protective enclosure of faith — and who *broke out of it.* Just like the date. The date was held, protected, contained by its skin. And then it burst out.

That is why *fisq* is so much heavier than simple "disobedience." A stranger who walks past your house has not betrayed you. The one who was *inside* — who was warmed, fed, protected, included — and who then breaks out and turns his back: *that* is *fisq.*

And listen to the sound of the word itself. *Fa-sa-qa.* It is a hard word. The *qāf* is a stone dropped at the back of the throat. There is nothing soft in it. The mouth has to break to say it. The word *sounds* like a rupture — like something tearing out of its place. The phonetics enact the meaning.

What this word reveals: the verse is not describing people who failed to find the boundary of faith. It is describing people who were *held within it* — by covenant, by clarity, by affirmation — and who burst out of it from the inside. The protection was around them. They broke through it. Outward.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. *Tawallā* — they used the very faculty of nearness to turn away. *Hum al-fāsiqūn* — and the verdict came down, sealed and restricted, precisely on them. *Al-fāsiqūn* — and what they are is not "outsiders," but those who broke out from *inside* the enclosure of the covenant.

Three words, three pictures: a body turning its back, a gavel falling, a date bursting its skin.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about the human heart itself. Because this verse is not really about the prophets, or about ancient nations. It is a diagnosis of something that lives in every one of us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why "after that" is the heaviest phrase in the verse

Let me start where you already live.

There is a particular kind of pain that human beings reserve for one specific situation. It is not the pain of being wronged by a stranger. A stranger cuts you off in traffic, is rude in a queue, takes something that wasn't theirs — it stings, but it passes. The pain I mean is different. It is the pain of being betrayed *by someone who promised.*

Think about the difference between a stranger who never knew you, and a friend you trusted who turned on you after years. Think about the difference between a debt owed by an acquaintance, and a vow broken by a spouse. Think about the difference between a stranger's silence and the silence of someone who once said *I will always be there.* In every legal system humans have ever built, we treat the breaking of a *known* obligation as graver than an act done in ignorance. We have a whole separate category for it: *breach of trust.* Betrayal. We feel, in our bones, that knowing more makes turning away worse.

Now look at the three smallest, easiest-to-skip words in the verse:

> بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ — *baʿda dhālika* — "after that."

Everything turns on these two words. Allah does not say "whoever turns away *are* the fāsiqūn." He says "whoever turns away **after that.**" After *what?* The demonstrative *dhālika* — "that" — points backward, like a finger, to everything in verse 81. After the covenant. After the scriptures and the wisdom. After the confirming messenger came. After the prophets themselves affirmed: *"We affirm."* After Allah said: *"I am with you among the witnesses."* Al-Ṭabarī fixes the referent precisely: *baʿda dhālika*, he says, means *baʿda al-ʿahd wa-l-mīthāq alladhī akhadhahu Allāhu ʿalayhi* — "after the covenant and the pledge that Allah took upon him." The "that" is not vague. It is the sworn bond itself.

[PAUSE]

The fisq is not in the turning alone. The fisq is in the turning *after.* The verse is built so that the weight does not fall on the act of leaving — it falls on the *fullness of what was known before the leaving.*

This is the verse holding a mirror to that thing your own conscience already knows. You already feel that the friend's betrayal is worse than the stranger's. You already feel that breaking a vow is graver than never making one. The verse takes that instinct — which Allah placed in you — and shows you it is a fragment of a divine principle. **The more clearly the truth has been shown to you, the heavier your turning away becomes.**

And here is where it stops being about ancient nations and becomes about us. Because every one of us is living *baʿda dhālika.* After that. We did not stand at the covenant of the prophets — but we have had our own "thats." The Ramadan where the Qur'an felt alive. The moment of duʿā that was answered so precisely you couldn't deny it. The lecture, the verse, the loss, the night of clarity when you *knew* what was real and what was noise. Every one of those is a *dhālika.* And every day after it, we live *baʿda dhālika.*

Which means the question the verse quietly puts to us is not "have you sinned?" The question is: **"What did you already know when you did?"**

The danger of the spiritual life is not ignorance. It is the slow forgetting of what we once clearly saw. It is letting the *dhālika* fade until we can act as though it never happened — as though we never affirmed, never witnessed, never knew. The verse refuses to let us forget. It nails the "after" to the front of the verdict.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means the goal is not only to learn more — it is to *stay faithful to what you have already been shown.* Before you reach for new knowledge, ask: am I living up to the *dhālika* I already have? The most dangerous gap in a believer's life is not the gap between ignorance and knowledge. It is the gap between **what you know and what you do.** Closing that gap, even by an inch — acting on one thing you already clearly know is true — is worth more than a library of new information left unlived.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The heart has a direction — and *walāya* is which way it faces

Let me come at the second theme through something almost physical.

A face has a direction. At every moment, your face is pointed *somewhere.* You cannot face two directions at once. To turn toward one thing is, in the same motion, to turn away from another. When you turn to greet someone entering the room, your back, in that instant, turns to whoever is behind you. There is no neutral. The face is always committed.

Now remember what we uncovered about the root w-l-y. It is the root of *facing.* *Walī* — the one you face, the one you turn toward, whose side you take. *Tawallā* — to turn the face away. **The same root, because they are the same act seen from two sides.** Every turning-toward is a turning-away. Every turning-away is a turning-toward something else.

This is the deep teaching hidden in the grammar: **the heart is never neutral.** It is always facing. It is always in a state of *walāya* toward *something.* The question is never "is my heart committed?" The heart is always committed. The only question is: *to what is it turned?*

[PAUSE]

So when the verse says *tawallā* — "he turned away" — it is not describing someone who became neutral, who stepped back into some safe, uncommitted middle ground. There is no such ground. The one who turns away from the covenant has not become *nothing.* In the very act of turning his back on the truth, **he has turned his face toward something else.** The desires of the self. The approval of the crowd. The comfort of not having to change. He has simply chosen a different *walī.*

This is why the Qur'an, just a few sūrahs earlier, gives us the perfect counter-image. *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū yukhrijuhum mina-ẓ-ẓulumāti ila-n-nūr* — "Allah is the *walī* of those who believe; He brings them out of the darknesses into the light." Look at the same root again — *walī* — and look at the same verb of "bringing out" (*yukhrij*, from the root of *khārij*, "the one who exits," the very word the mufassirūn used for the fāsiq).

Do you see the terrible symmetry?

- The **believer** lets Allah be his *walī* — and Allah *brings him out* (*yukhrij*) of darkness into light.
- The **fāsiq** *turns himself away* (*tawallā*) — and in doing so *breaks himself out* (*khārij*) of the light, back into the dark.

Same root for the protector. Same image of "coming out." But one is *brought out* of darkness by the One he turned toward — and the other *breaks out* of the light by the turning he chose. The direction of the face determines the direction of the exit.

[PAUSE]

Now make it personal, because this is not abstract theology. This is the mechanics of your daily heart.

Every single day, in dozens of small moments, your heart turns. The alarm goes for Fajr and there is a turning — toward the prayer, or toward the pillow, and whichever you choose, your face has committed. A harsh word forms on your tongue and there is a turning — toward restraint, or toward release. A scroll, a glance, a choice of what to feed the soul — each one is a tiny *walāya.* We are not facing the big covenant of the prophets. We are facing a thousand miniature covenants a day. And in each, the same physics applies: to turn toward one is to turn your back on the other.

The reason this matters so much is that **direction compounds.** A face turned one degree away does not feel like betrayal in the moment. It feels like nothing. But a heart that turns away one degree at a time, day after day, eventually finds itself facing entirely the wrong direction — and the worst part is, it happened so gradually it never felt like a single act of *tawallā.* The date does not burst its skin all at once on the surface. The pressure builds quietly from within, until one day the skin simply gives.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means you stop asking "have I left Islam?" — a question so big it lets all the small turnings hide beneath it — and start asking the real question: **"Which way is my face turned, right now, in this small thing?"** Catch the heart in the act of turning. Because the only reliable protection against the great turning-away is faithfulness in the small turning-toward. Turn your face back, deliberately, in one small moment today — one prayer prayed on time, one duʿā made with presence, one anger swallowed for His sake. That is the muscle of *walāya.* And it is the same muscle that, neglected, becomes *tawallā.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: covenant, consequence, and the cosmos

Now step back and see how the pieces fit — because the placement of this verse is itself an argument.

Verse 81 is the **covenant** — vast, ancient, witnessed, affirmed by the prophets themselves.
Verse 82 is the **consequence** — seven words naming what it is to turn away from such a thing: *fisq.*
And verse 83, the very next verse, opens with astonishment: *"Is it other than the religion of Allah they seek, while to Him has submitted whoever is in the heavens and the earth?"*

Watch the logic of the three together. First Allah establishes that the truth was *given and affirmed* (81). Then He names turning from it as *breaking out of the enclosure* (82). And then He reveals what the enclosure actually *was* (83): it was the *dīn* to which the entire cosmos already submits — every star, every cell, every angel, willingly or not. So the fāsiq who "breaks out" of the boundary of faith is not breaking out into freedom. **He is breaking out of the one harmony that the whole universe is inside of.** He is the single date that bursts its skin while the entire orchard hangs faithfully on the branch.

That is the architecture. The verse we studied is the hinge: between the covenant that was given and the cosmos that never broke it stands the lonely figure of the one who turned his face away.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight from two directions.

The *words* told us: *tawallā* (the faculty of nearness used to turn away — and, in al-Ṭabarī's reading, the breaking of a sworn pledge), *hum al-fāsiqūn* (the sealed verdict), *fisq* (bursting out of the skin that held you).

The *themes* told us: betrayal is graver than ignorance because of what was *known* ("after that"), and the heart is never neutral — it is always facing *somewhere*, so to turn away is always to turn toward something lesser.

Both directions arrive at the same place: **The tragedy this verse mourns is not the tragedy of the person who never knew. It is the tragedy of the one who was held — inside the covenant, inside the light, inside the harmony of all things — and who used his own God-given capacity for loyalty to turn his face the other way and break himself out.**

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. What is my *dhālika* — the thing I have already been clearly shown, that I cannot honestly claim ignorance of? And am I living *baʿda dhālika* faithfully, or as though it never happened?
2. Where, today, is the gap widest between **what I know** and **what I do**? Not what I don't know — what I *do.*
3. If the heart is always facing somewhere — which way is mine turned right now, in this season of my life? And if I'm honest, what is the *other* thing I've turned toward whenever I've turned away from Him?
4. The date bursts its skin from pressure that builds quietly within. What pressure is building quietly in me — what small, daily turnings-away that have not yet felt like betrayal?
5. Allah is the *walī* who brings His people *out* of darkness. Have I actually let Him be my *walī* — or am I asking Him to protect a face I keep turning the other way?

### One-sentence distillation

To turn away after the truth has been given and affirmed is not weakness but *fisq* — using your own capacity for loyalty to break yourself out of the very covenant that was holding, protecting, and including you.

### The closing invitation

This verse can be read as a threat. But read it once more through everything we've uncovered, and it becomes something else: an invitation extended *from inside the enclosure.* You are still inside. The skin has not burst. The covenant still holds you. The light is still around you. The verse is not slamming a door on you — it is showing you, with terrible clarity, what is on the *other* side of the door, so that you will not casually wander through it.

So the shift it asks for is small and immediate: not a grand transformation, but a single, deliberate turning of the face. One degree back toward Him, today, in one concrete thing. That is the whole of it. *Tawallā* is built one degree at a time — and so, by the mercy of Allah, is *walāya.*

### A brief duʿā

O Allah, *Walī* of those who believe — keep our faces turned toward You. We have seen what we have seen, and we have known what we have known; do not let us live as though we never saw it. Hold us inside the covenant. And when we feel the quiet pressure to break out and turn away, turn our hearts back to You before the skin ever gives. *Āmīn.*
