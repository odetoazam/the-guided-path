# The Man No One Could Claim

## Introduction: A Quarrel Over a Grave

I want you to picture an argument. Not a calm theological discussion — an argument. Voices rising. Two communities, each certain, each insisting on something about a man who had been dead for thousands of years.

The man is Ibrahim — Abraham. And the argument is about who he *belonged* to.

The Jewish community of Madinah said: he was one of us. He is the father of our line, the root of our covenant, the first of our patriarchs. He was a Jew.

The Christian community said: no, he was one of *us*. He prefigured everything we believe. He was, in spirit, a Christian.

And the pagans of Makkah, in their own way, had also folded Ibrahim into their story — the builder of their Kaʿbah, the ancestor they honored even as they hung idols on the walls of the house he raised for the One God.

Everyone wanted him. Everyone reached for him. Each group held up his name like a flag and said: *this is ours.*

And then the Quran does something I want you to feel the full strangeness of before we go any further.

[PAUSE]

It refuses to hand him over.

Listen to the verse:

> **مَا كَانَ إِبْرَٰهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَٰكِن كَانَ حَنِيفًا مُّسْلِمًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ**

*Mā kāna Ibrāhīmu yahūdiyyan wa lā naṣrāniyyan wa lākin kāna ḥanīfan musliman wa mā kāna mina-l-mushrikīn.*

> "Ibrahim was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a ḥanīf — one inclined wholly to the truth — a muslim, one who surrendered to God; and he was never of those who associate partners with Him."

Now here is what I want you to notice immediately, before we open a single word.

The verse just before this one (3:65) had already made a simple, almost devastating point: *the Torah and the Gospel were not sent down until after him.* Think about what that means. To call Ibrahim a Jew or a Christian is not just wrong — it is **chronologically impossible.** It is like calling someone a citizen of a country that would not be founded for another thousand years. You are draping a man in a flag that did not yet exist.

So this verse is the answer to a quarrel. But here is the hook — here is the thing I want you to carry with you through everything that follows:

When Allah refuses to give Ibrahim to any group, He does not leave him with *no* identity. He gives him two words instead. Two words that are not the names of any tribe, any nation, any era. Two words so old they precede every label human beings have ever fought over.

And the question that should keep us here for the next hour is this: **what kind of identity is left for a person once you strip away every label they could be claimed by?** What is underneath all of it?

Stay with me. Because the answer Allah gives is not a smaller identity. It is a vastly larger one.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Architecture of "Was Never" — مَا كَانَ

Let me begin with something that looks like nothing. A grammar point so quiet you would read straight past it.

Before I show you where it lives in the verse, let me teach you the tool itself, in plain terms.

In Arabic, there is a verb — *kāna* — that simply means "was." It is the verb of being, of existence in the past. Now, in English, when we want to deny something about the past, we say "he was not." One word of negation, one verb. Done.

But Arabic has a particular construction: **mā kāna.** The negative particle *mā* placed directly in front of the verb *kāna*. And this construction carries a flavor that "was not" in English does not quite capture. *Mā kāna* does not simply deny a single moment. It tends to deny the thing **across the whole stretch of time** — it says not just "he was not this" but "it was never the case that he was this," "this was never in him at all," "at no point did this describe him."

Think of the difference between someone saying "I wasn't angry" — a statement about one moment — and someone saying "anger was never part of who I am." The second reaches across an entire life. *Mā kāna* leans toward the second.

Now look at the verse, and count.

> **مَا كَانَ** إِبْرَٰهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَٰكِن **كَانَ** حَنِيفًا مُّسْلِمًا **وَمَا كَانَ** مِنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ

Three times the verb *kāna* appears. And notice the shape it makes.

It **opens** with *mā kāna* — "he was never."
In the **middle**, stripped of negation, just *kāna* — "he simply *was*."
And it **closes**, again, with *wa mā kāna* — "and he was never."

[PAUSE]

Do you see the architecture? Two walls of negation on the outside — *mā kāna* at the start, *wa mā kāna* at the end — and between them, held safe in the center, a single unguarded affirmation: *kāna ḥanīfan musliman.* He simply was a ḥanīf, a muslim.

The negations are the outer shell. The affirmation is the kernel inside.

Notice something. The verse does not say "Ibrahim was a ḥanīf, and by the way he wasn't a Jew or Christian." It does not lead with what he *was*. It leads with what he was **never** — and it ends with what he was **never** — and only in the protected middle does it tell you what he was.

Why build it this way?

Here is what this invites us to consider. When a thing is being fought over, when every party is trying to drag it into their camp, the first work is not to declare what it is. The first work is to **pull it free** of all the false claims. You cannot place the diamond on the table until you have pried open every hand that was gripping it. The two outer negations are those hands being pried open — *not yours, Jews; not yours, Christians; not yours, idolaters* — and only when all the hands are empty does the verse set down, in the clear space of the middle, what Ibrahim actually was.

And there is something even quieter here. Look at *where* the third negation sits. The first two — "not a Jew, not a Christian" — are paired together at the front. But "not of the polytheists" is held back. It is separated from the others, pushed all the way to the end, *after* the affirmation. As if to say: the People of the Book at least worshipped the One God in some form, so their claim is addressed and dismissed together at the front. But shirk — associating partners with God — is the one thing that had to be sealed off completely, placed last, after we have already established that he was a muslim, so that no one could imagine even a sliver of it in him.

This is the grammar of a verdict. Every claim heard. Every claim denied. The truth set down, untouchable, in the middle.

That is what *mā kāna* — repeated, bracketing, "was never" — reveals.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Word That Leans — حَنِيفًا

Now we come to the heart of it. The first of the two words Allah gives Ibrahim in place of every label: **ḥanīf.**

Let me, again, teach the concept before we use it.

In Arabic, words grow from roots — usually three consonants that carry a core idea, and every word built on that root inherits a flavor of it. The root here is *ḥā–nūn–fā* (ḥ-n-f). And I want you to sit with what this root actually means at its base, because it is going to surprise you.

The root *ḥanafa* means: **to incline, to lean, to turn away from one direction toward another.**

Now stop. Think about what we would *expect* the word for a person of pure, upright faith to be built from. We would expect a root meaning "to stand straight," "to be firm," "to be fixed," "to be upright and unmoving." That is how we usually imagine the person of true religion — like a pillar, planted, immovable.

But that is not the root Allah chose.

He chose the root that means **to lean.** To incline. To turn.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how strange and how beautiful that is.

The classical commentators caught this precisely. Al-Baghawi defines the ḥanīf as *al-māʾil ʿan al-adyān kullihā ilā al-dīn al-mustaqīm* — "the one who **inclines away** from all the [corrupted] religions **toward** the upright path." Al-Qurtubi, drawing on the explanation already given earlier in Sūrat al-Baqarah, ties it to the same root sense of turning. The ḥanīf is not someone standing still. The ḥanīf is someone **caught in the act of turning** — leaning away from every distortion, every idol, every false claim, and leaning *toward* the One.

Do you feel the difference this makes?

If faith were "standing straight," then faith would be a position — something you achieve and then occupy, a spot you stand on. But Allah did not name it that way. He named it as **a direction of lean.** An orientation. A continuous inclination.

The ḥanīf is defined not by where he is standing but by **which way he is leaning.**

And this is load-bearing — let me show you why it matters, not as poetry but as a description of how a real human soul actually works.

Picture a plant on a windowsill. The plant is not standing at attention like a soldier. The plant is *bent.* It is leaning, visibly, toward the light coming through the glass. And here is the thing: that lean is not weakness. That lean is the plant's entire health. The lean *is* the life. A plant that has stopped leaning toward the light is a plant that is dying. Its whole orientation — its bend, its inclination, the way every leaf turns — is the living proof of what it is reaching for.

That is the ḥanīf.

Not a statue of faith. A *living lean* toward the One. A soul whose entire posture is turned away from the many and toward the One, the way the plant turns away from the shadow and toward the sun.

And notice what this dissolves. The People of the Book were arguing about Ibrahim as though faith were a *label you are stamped with* — Jew, Christian, a category you are filed under. But the word ḥanīf says: that is not what faith is. Faith is not a stamp. **Faith is a direction.** You cannot inherit a lean. You cannot be born already leaning. A direction is something the living soul must do, must keep doing, in every moment. The plant does not lean toward the sun once and then stop. It leans all day, following, turning, for as long as it is alive.

So when Allah calls Ibrahim a ḥanīf, He is not filing him under a category. He is describing the **shape of his living soul** — bent away from every false thing, turned wholly toward the True.

That is the first word. Let it settle before we take the second.

## Element Three: The Word of Surrender — مُّسْلِمًا

The second word comes right behind it: **musliman.** A muslim.

Now — be careful here, because this is a word we think we already know, and the things we think we already know are the things we stop actually hearing.

Let me teach the form first.

This word is built on the root *s-l-m* (sīn–lām–mīm), and it comes through what Arabic grammarians call **Form IV** — a specific pattern, *afʿala*, that reshapes the base meaning of a root. The base sense of *s-l-m* circles around peace, safety, wholeness, soundness — being intact, being free of defect. The Form IV verb built from it, *aslama*, carries the sense of **giving oneself over, handing oneself across, surrendering.** And *muslim* is the active participle of that verb — "one who is in the act of surrendering, one who hands himself over."

I want to be precise about what this form is doing, because it is easy to get wrong. Form IV here is not "causing" anything in the way we sometimes flatten it. The sense is *transitive and self-directed*: to deliver oneself up, to consign one's whole self into another's hands. Al-Qurtubi gives the plain Arabic meaning of the muslim as *al-mutadhallil li-amri-llāh, al-munṭāʿ lah* — "the one who makes himself low before the command of God, fully yielding to Him." Al-Muyassar renders Ibrahim's state as *mustaslim li-rabbih* — utterly surrendered to his Lord.

So "muslim," here, is not the name of the religion we hold a card for. Remember — Judaism and Christianity, as al-Muyassar notes, "did not exist except after him," and the same is true of Islam as an *organized, named community.* So when this verse calls Ibrahim a *muslim,* it cannot mean "a member of the religion called Islam" in the institutional sense — that community was thousands of years in his future. It means something prior to all of that. It means **the act itself.** The surrender. The handing-over of the whole self to God.

[PAUSE]

And now watch how the two words fit together, because this is the moment the verse fully opens.

Ḥanīf — the lean. Muslim — the surrender.

These are not two separate descriptions sitting side by side. They are **two halves of a single motion.**

Think of it this way. There is a difference between *leaning toward* something and *falling into* it. You can lean toward a person across a table — incline toward them — and still hold yourself back, still keep your weight in your own chair. The lean is the orientation. But surrender is when you let your weight go. When you stop holding yourself up. When you give your weight over entirely and let yourself be caught.

*Ḥanīf* is the lean toward God — the orientation, the turning, the inclining away from everything false.

*Muslim* is the letting-go — the actual release of one's own weight into God's keeping.

A person can incline toward the truth and still, secretly, keep their weight to themselves — admire God, lean toward Him, and yet never quite hand themselves over. And a person can *say* they have surrendered while leaning, in their heart, toward a hundred other things.

But Ibrahim was both. He **leaned** all the way, and then he **let go** all the way. The orientation and the release, complete, in one man. Bent entirely toward the One — and then poured entirely into the One's hands.

That is what these two words, set side by side with no conjunction softening the join — *ḥanīfan musliman* — actually paint. Not two traits. One act, seen from two sides: the turning, and the giving.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So step back and see what the language has shown us.

The construction *mā kāna … wa mā kāna* pried open every grasping hand and set the truth down in a protected center. The word *ḥanīf* told us that faith is not a position but a living lean — a direction the soul must keep choosing. And the word *muslim* told us that the lean is completed only in the letting-go, the full surrender of one's weight into God.

Three words. And not one of them is the name of a tribe, a nation, or an era.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about us. About the thing every human heart is doing when it reaches, the way those communities reached, to claim a name for itself.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Hunger to Belong to a Name

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a set of words you use to say who you are. Some of them you were born into — a family name, a nationality, an ethnicity, a hometown. Some of them you chose — a profession, a politics, a team you support, a community you joined. And here is the thing about all of these words: they feel like *you.* When someone insults the group, you flinch as though they insulted *you.* When the team wins, *you* won. The label has fused with the self.

This is not a flaw in you. It is one of the deepest currents in human nature. We are built to belong. We need a *we.* The loneliest sentence in any language is "I don't belong anywhere." And so we reach, constantly, for names that will gather us into a *we* — and once we have them, we defend them, because to lose the name feels like losing the self.

Now look again at the people in this verse's story. The Jews and the Christians of Madinah were not being foolish. They were doing the most human thing imaginable. They were reaching for a great ancestor — Ibrahim, the father, the root, the source of dignity and legitimacy — and trying to gather him into their *we.* "He is ours" is just another way of saying "*we* are his — we come from greatness, we are rooted in something noble." The quarrel over Ibrahim was, underneath, a quarrel over each community's own identity, fought through the body of a dead patriarch.

And Allah's answer cuts straight through the center of that hunger.

[PAUSE]

He does not say "Ibrahim belongs to the *Muslims* instead of you" — which is what we might expect, and which would simply be one more group claiming the prize. He says something far stranger and far deeper. He says: *Ibrahim was never a Jew or a Christian — and he was a ḥanīf, a muslim.* He removes Ibrahim from the entire **economy of group-ownership.** He lifts him out of the quarrel altogether.

Because the names they were fighting over — Jew, Christian — were, the verse reminds us, *later* than Ibrahim. They were labels invented by history, downstream of the man. And what Allah restores to Ibrahim is the thing that was true of him *before* any label existed: he was a soul leaning toward God and surrendered to God. That is not a label. **That is what the labels were supposed to point at and forgot to.**

Here is the movement I want you to feel. The communities were holding up the *packaging* and fighting over whose name was printed on it. And Allah quietly opened the box and showed them what was actually inside — and it had no brand name at all.

Now bring this home, because it is not about them. It is about you.

Every one of us carries a stack of labels we are tempted to mistake for our soul. "I am a [this kind of Muslim]." "I am from [this place], not that one." "I follow [this scholar], this *madhhab*, this movement, this group." And none of these is necessarily wrong — Ibrahim, after all, was not label-*less*; he was a ḥanīf, a muslim. The danger is not having a name. The danger is when **the name replaces the lean.** When you are so busy defending your membership in the group that you forget the group was only ever supposed to point you toward God — and you find, if you are honest, that you can fight ferociously for the label while your actual soul has stopped leaning toward Him at all.

Ask yourself, gently: if every label were stripped from you — your school of thought, your community, your reputation as a religious person — *would there still be a lean?* Would there still be a soul turning toward God underneath it all? Or is the label all that is left, with nothing inside the box?

[PAUSE]

The practical shift is small and quietly devastating. The next time you feel that flush of defensiveness on behalf of your group, your label, your camp — pause and ask: *am I defending God right now, or am I defending my belonging?* They feel identical from the inside. They are not the same thing. Ibrahim teaches us that the only belonging that finally matters is the belonging of the surrendered soul to its Lord — and that one needs no group to defend it.

## Theme Two: Faith as a Direction You Keep Choosing, Not a Place You Arrive

Now I want to return to that astonishing root — *ḥanafa,* to lean, to incline — and let it do its deepest work on how we actually live our faith day to day.

Here is a quiet trap almost every religious person falls into. We treat faith as a **destination we have already reached.** "I'm a practicing Muslim." Past tense, settled, done. I prayed, I fasted, I believe — I have *arrived* at the place called faith, and now I live there. It is an address. It is where I am from.

And the moment faith becomes an address, something dies. Because an address is static. You do not have to *do* anything to keep living at an address. You are just there. And so we coast. We carry the name "believer" while the actual living motion of belief — the turning, the reaching, the leaning — has quietly stopped. Like the plant that has been moved away from the window and no longer bends toward anything, because there is no longer any light it is reaching for. It still looks like a plant. For a while.

But the word *ḥanīf* refuses this entirely. It tells us that faith was **never an address.** It was always a *lean.* A direction. Something the living soul does in every moment, the way the plant turns toward the sun all day, every day, for as long as it is alive.

[PAUSE]

Let me build this carefully, because it changes everything about how we measure ourselves.

If faith is an address, then the question is "have I arrived?" — and the honest answer fills you either with false security ("yes, I'm there, I'm fine") or with despair ("no, and I never will be"). Both of those are diseases. The complacency of the one who thinks he has arrived, and the hopelessness of the one who thinks he never can.

But if faith is a *lean* — a direction — then the question changes completely. The question is no longer "have I arrived?" The question is: **"which way am I leaning right now?"** And that is a question you can answer honestly in any moment, no matter how far you feel from the destination. You do not have to be standing in the light. You only have to be *turning toward it.*

This is the mercy hidden inside the grammar. The ḥanīf is not the one who has reached perfection. The ḥanīf is the one whose face is *turned the right way.* And turning your face — that, you can always do. Right now. From wherever you are. However far you have wandered. The lean is available to the sinner and the saint alike, because the lean is not about where you are standing. It is about which direction you have set your soul.

And here is where the second word comes back to complete it. *Ḥanīf* — you lean. *Muslim* — you let your weight go into God's hands. Put them together and you have the whole spiritual life in two motions: **turn toward Him, then trust Him with your weight.** Not "achieve Him." Not "arrive at Him." Turn, and let go. Turn, and let go. Over and over, every day, the way the plant turns and the way the falling man lets himself be caught.

There is a reason this matters so much for a heart that feels it has failed. Allah did not preserve Ibrahim's greatness in some title that only the spiritual elite could ever hold. He preserved it in a *lean* and a *letting-go* — two things available to anyone, in any condition, in this very breath. The most broken person reading this is one turn of the heart away from being, in this moment, a ḥanīf. That is how close it always is.

[PAUSE]

So the practical shift here is a change in the question you ask yourself at the end of each day. Not "am I a good Muslim?" — that is the address question, and it will only flatter you or crush you. Ask instead: **"today, which way was I leaning?"** Was my face, on balance, turned toward Him or away? Did I, even once, let my weight go into His hands instead of carrying it all myself? Those are the ḥanīf's questions. And they are answerable, every single night, by anyone — which is exactly why Allah, in His mercy, chose a word that means *to lean* instead of a word that means *to have arrived.*

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we have walked through the words and the themes, look back at the whole verse and see how perfectly it is built — because the structure itself is the argument.

> **مَا كَانَ** إِبْرَٰهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا — *he was never a Jew or a Christian*
> وَلَٰكِن **كَانَ** حَنِيفًا مُّسْلِمًا — *but he simply was: leaning, surrendered*
> **وَمَا كَانَ** مِنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ — *and he was never of those who associate partners*

A negation. An affirmation. A negation.

The two outer walls — *mā kāna … wa mā kāna* — are everything Ibrahim was **not:** the labels, the eras, the false claims, the divided heart of shirk. And held safe in the protected center, between those walls, is everything he **was:** *ḥanīfan musliman* — the lean and the letting-go.

The structure *is* the meaning. Strip away every label a soul could be claimed by — peel off the outer walls — and what remains, standing clear in the center, is not nothing. It is the most essential thing there is: a soul turned toward God and surrendered to Him.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes turn out to be the same insight approached from two directions.

The grammar said: pull every grasping hand off the diamond before you set it down. The human truth said: peel away every borrowed label before you can find the actual soul underneath.

The word *ḥanīf* said: faith is a lean, not a position. The human truth said: stop asking whether you have arrived, and start asking which way you are turned.

The word *muslim* said: the lean completes itself in the letting-go. The human truth said: turn toward Him, then trust Him with your weight.

It is all one thing. Everyone in that ancient quarrel was holding up a *name* and shouting "this is who we are." And Allah, with fourteen words, quietly set down the only identity that was ever real: not a name you are stamped with, but a direction you keep choosing, and a surrender you keep making.

## Questions to Carry

1. If every label were stripped from me tomorrow — my reputation as a religious person, my community, my school of thought — would there still be a *lean*? Would there still be a soul turning toward God underneath it all?

2. When I feel that hot rush of defensiveness on behalf of "my group," am I defending God in that moment — or am I defending my own belonging? Can I tell the difference honestly?

3. Do I treat faith as an *address* I have already arrived at, or as a *direction* I have to keep choosing in every breath?

4. There is leaning toward God, and there is letting my weight go into His hands. Which one do I actually struggle with more — the turning, or the trusting?

5. What is one label I carry that has quietly replaced the lean it was supposed to point me toward?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Ibrahim belonged to no party and no era — he was simply a soul leaning wholly away from every falsehood and surrendering its full weight to God, and that nameless turning, not any inherited label, is the only thing in him worth inheriting.

## The Closing Invitation

I am not going to ask you to abandon your labels. Ibrahim was not label-less — he was a ḥanīf, a muslim. The invitation is smaller and harder than that. It is to look, honestly, at whether there is still something *inside* your labels. To open the box and check whether the lean is still there, or whether you have been defending empty packaging for a long time.

And if you find the lean has weakened — if the plant has drifted away from the window — the mercy of this verse is that you are never more than one turn away. You do not have to arrive anywhere. You only have to turn your face. That, you can do right now, before you finish reading this sentence.

## A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who named Your beloved Ibrahim not by any tribe or age but by his turning toward You — turn our faces the way You turned his. Make us of those who lean toward You away from every falsehood, and who let the full weight of their souls fall into Your hands. And do not let us mistake the name "believer" for the living lean of belief; keep our hearts inclined toward You until the moment we meet You. Āmīn.
