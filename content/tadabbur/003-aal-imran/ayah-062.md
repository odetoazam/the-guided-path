# The True Account: When the Story Follows the Footprints

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first, and then let me give you the room it was spoken in.

> [ayah:3:62] إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْقَصَصُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۚ وَمَا مِنْ إِلَـٰهٍ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*Inna hādhā lahuwa l-qaṣaṣu l-ḥaqq. Wa mā min ilāhin illā Allāh. Wa inna Allāha lahuwa l-ʿazīzu l-ḥakīm.*

"Indeed, this is the true account. There is no god except Allah. And indeed, Allah — He alone is the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."

Seventeen words. Read quickly, it sounds like a summary — the kind of line you'd put at the bottom of a long page to wrap it up. *In conclusion.* And that is exactly how most of us hear it and move on.

But to hear what is actually happening here, you have to stand where the first listeners stood.

### Rebuild the world

A delegation has arrived in Madinah. They have come from Najran, in the south — the Christians of Najran, a learned, organized, dignified community. They have come to debate the Prophet ﷺ about Isa, son of Maryam. Who was he? A prophet? A son of God? God himself walking the earth?

And for an entire stretch of this surah, the Quran has been laying out the account of Isa with extraordinary care. His mother Maryam, chosen and purified. The annunciation. The miraculous birth. And then — the line that must have stopped the room — *the likeness of Isa, with Allah, is as the likeness of Adam.* (3:59) He created him from dust, then said *Be*, and he was.

In other words: you are amazed that Isa had no father? Adam had no father *and* no mother. If a fatherless birth makes someone divine, then Adam has the greater claim. The logic is airtight, and it is gentle, and it is devastating.

Then comes the *mubāhala* in 3:61 — the call to mutual invocation. *If anyone still disputes after the knowledge that has come to you, then say: come, let us gather our sons and your sons, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves, and let us earnestly invoke and place the curse of Allah upon the liars.* It is the most serious thing you can say in a disagreement: *let us both stand before God and ask Him to destroy whichever of us is lying.* The tradition tells us the delegation of Najran looked at the Prophet ﷺ, looked at his family standing ready, and declined to take the oath. They chose a treaty instead. Something in them knew.

[PAUSE]

So now picture the moment *immediately after* all of that. The arguments are made. The proofs are laid out. The challenge is on the table. And into that charged silence, Allah does not add another proof. He does something else entirely. He pronounces a verdict over the whole account:

**This. This is the true one.**

That is our verse. And the question I want you to carry into the next hour is this: in an age — *theirs and ours* — where everyone has a version of the story, where the same man Isa is claimed by a dozen theologies, where "truth" feels like whatever your community happens to repeat — what does it actually *mean* for Allah to step in and say, *this is the true account*? Not "a good account." Not "my account." **The true one.**

There is a word buried in that phrase that, once you see what it really means, will change how you read every story in the Quran.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: القَصَص — the story that follows footprints

Here is something we do every day without noticing.

When a friend tells you what happened at work, they are *selecting*. They leave out the boring parts, sharpen the conflict, give themselves the best lines. When the news reports an event, it *frames*. When a memoir is written, it *shapes*. Every story is a choice about what to include, what to emphasize, and what to quietly drop. We don't usually call this lying. We call it *telling a story*. The very act of narration bends the raw material into a shape.

Hold onto that. Because the Arabic word the Quran chooses here for "account" carries inside it the opposite instinct.

The word is **القَصَص** — *al-qaṣaṣ.*

Now, you could translate it as "story" or "narrative" and not be wrong. But to translate it that way and stop is to walk past the treasure. Because *al-qaṣaṣ* comes from the root **qāf-ṣād-ṣād (q-ṣ-ṣ)**, and the root meaning is not "to make up a tale."

Let me teach you the root before I use it.

The classical lexicons, and al-Qurtubī states this directly in his commentary on this very verse, tell us that *qaṣṣa* originally means **to follow a track** — to follow someone's footprints. The Arabs would say *fulānun yaquṣṣu athara fulān* — "so-and-so is tracking the trace of so-and-so," following his footprints across the ground. al-Qurtubī writes that a *qaṣaṣ* is called a *qaṣaṣ* "because the meanings follow one upon another in it" — *li-anna l-maʿānī tatatābaʿu fīhā* — one thing following the next, the way a tracker follows print after print across the sand.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this image, because it is doing enormous work.

A tracker in the desert is the opposite of a storyteller. The storyteller invents. The tracker is *forbidden* to invent. He kneels down, he reads the mark in the sand — the depth of the print, the spread of the toes, the direction of travel — and he tells you only what the ground actually says. If he embellishes, the trail dies. The whole discipline of tracking is the discipline of *not adding anything that isn't there.* You follow the trace. You report the trace. The trace is the master; you are only its servant.

So when Allah calls this account *al-qaṣaṣ*, He is not reaching for the word that means "a tale we tell." He is reaching for the word whose deepest fiber means **following the actual footprints of what happened.** This is not a story bent into a pleasing shape. This is the trail read exactly as it lies in the sand.

Do you feel the difference? Najran has its narrative about Isa. Other communities have theirs. The marketplace of the seventh century — like the marketplace of today — was full of *stories* about this man, each one shaped by the community that told it, each one adding its footprint to his. And the Quran does not say, *here is a better story to add to the pile.* It says: **this is the tracking.** This is what the ground actually shows. Everyone else has been *inventing* the trail. This one *reads* it.

### And then the seal: الحَقّ

And now look at the word that comes immediately after, because Allah does not leave *al-qaṣaṣ* alone. He binds it to a second word:

**القَصَصُ الحَقّ** — *al-qaṣaṣu l-ḥaqq.* The true account.

*Al-ḥaqq*, from the root **ḥ-q-q**. This is one of the heaviest words in the entire Quran. It does not merely mean "true" in the sense of "factually accurate," the way we'd say a sum is correct. *Al-ḥaqq* is the reality that is *fixed, established, owed, due* — the thing that is *firmly real* as opposed to the thing that merely seems. It is the opposite of *bāṭil*, the vain, the void, the thing that collapses when you lean on it. *Al-ḥaqq* is what is *there* when all the projections and wishes and inventions burn away.

So stack the two words. *Al-qaṣaṣ* — the trail read exactly as it lies. *Al-ḥaqq* — the fixed, established, undeniable reality. Together: **this is the trail of what is real, read exactly as it lies.** al-Jalālayn captures the certainty of it in two words — *al-ḥaqq alladhī lā shakka fīh* — "the truth in which there is no doubt." al-Muyassar echoes it: *al-naba'u l-ḥaqqu lladhī lā shakka fīh* — the true report in which there is no doubt at all.

And notice how far back the word *this* reaches. al-Jalālayn pauses on the demonstrative *hādhā* and glosses it in a single word — *al-madhkūr*, "that which has been mentioned." Not this sentence, not this last claim, but the entire foregoing account: Maryam, the annunciation, the birth, the likeness to Adam, the *mubāhala* — all of it gathered under one pointing finger and pronounced real. And al-Ṭabarī tells us precisely *what* this true account contains. The verdict, he explains, is being pronounced over everything Allah had just disclosed to the Prophet ﷺ about Isa — *that he is My servant and My messenger, My word which I cast unto Maryam, and a spirit from Me* (*ʿabdī wa rasūlī wa kalimatī alqaytuhā ilā Maryam wa rūḥun minnī*). That is the trail, read exactly as it lies: not a man inflated into a god, not a prophet diminished into a fiction, but a servant, a messenger, a word, a spirit. The footprints in the sand show a creature — an honored, miraculous, beloved creature — and nothing more, and nothing less. *This* is the true account: Isa as he actually was, neither stripped of his wonder nor crowned with a divinity he never claimed.

Here is what is remarkable. Notice *what* this verdict is being pronounced over. It is being pronounced over the account of **Isa** — the most contested figure in the religious history of that world, the man around whom more competing stories had gathered than almost any other. And it is precisely *there*, over the most fought-over story, that Allah plants the word *qaṣaṣ* — *I am not adding to the legends. I am reading the footprints.* — and then seals it with *al-ḥaqq* — *and what the footprints show is what is real.*

[PAUSE]

One word, then, has reframed the whole passage. The long account of Isa was never a *theological position competing with other positions.* It was the trail itself. Everything else was someone's footprint pressed on top of it.

### Element Two: لَهُوَ — the certainty Allah inserts into the sentence

Now I want to slow down on something most translations cannot carry across, because it is not a *word* you can point to — it is a piece of grammar that injects pure certainty into the heart of the sentence.

Look at the Arabic again: *Inna hādhā **lahuwa** l-qaṣaṣu l-ḥaqq.*

Let me teach you the structure first, in plain terms.

In Arabic, the most ordinary way to say "this is the truth" is simply *hādhā l-ḥaqq.* Two words. Complete. Correct. Nothing missing. If a student wrote that on an exam, it would be marked right.

But that is not what Allah says. Allah builds the sentence with *three* added intensifiers stacked on top of that plain statement:

First, **إِنَّ** — *inna* — the particle that opens a sentence with emphasis, roughly "indeed," "truly." Already we are no longer in plain speech.

Second, **لَ** — the *lām* of emphasis, prefixed to what follows — another layer of "surely."

And third — this is the subtle one — **هُوَ** — *huwa*, the word "he/it," dropped into the middle of the sentence where, grammatically, *you do not need it at all.*

That third element has a name in Arabic grammar: *ḍamīr al-faṣl*, the **pronoun of separation**, or sometimes *ḍamīr al-shaʾn.* Teach yourself to feel what it does with an everyday example. Imagine two ways of answering "who broke the window?"

You could say: "The boy broke it." Flat. Functional.

Or you could say: "The boy — *he* is the one who broke it." That extra "*he*" does something. It isolates the subject, spotlights it, and shuts the door on every other candidate. *Not anyone else. Him. He is the one.* In English we lean on our voice to do that. In Arabic, they have an actual word for it, and Allah places it here.

So now hear the verse with its real weight: not "this is the true account," but something closer to —

**"Indeed, this — surely it, it alone and nothing else — is the true account."**

[PAUSE]

Three intensifiers stacked on a sentence that was already complete without any of them. Why? What is all this certainty *for*?

Here is what I want you to notice. The verse is responding to a room full of doubt. The Najran delegation had every reason for uncertainty — generations of competing doctrine, learned men on every side, the genuine difficulty of the question. And uncertainty is contagious; it spreads through a debate like cold through a body. Into that, the answer is not a louder argument. It is a *different register entirely* — the register of one who is not arguing because He is not uncertain. The triple emphasis is the sound of bedrock. You do not pile up *inna* and *lām* and the pronoun of separation when you are trying to *convince*. You speak this way when you are *witnessing* — when you have seen the thing with your own eyes and are simply telling the people in the room what is so.

And watch — Allah does it *twice*. The exact same construction returns at the end of the verse: *wa inna Allāha **lahuwa** l-ʿazīzu l-ḥakīm.* "And indeed Allah — surely He, He alone — is the All-Mighty, the All-Wise." The same three intensifiers. The same pronoun of separation.

The verse has two pillars of absolute certainty, built identically, standing at its two ends. Hold that picture. We will come back to what they are holding up between them.

### Element Three: وَمَا مِنْ إِلَـٰهٍ — closing the last loophole

Now to the center. Between those two pillars of certainty sits the sentence the whole passage was driving toward:

**وَمَا مِنْ إِلَـٰهٍ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ** — *wa mā min ilāhin illā Allāh.* "And there is no god except Allah."

Again, let me show you what Allah did *not* say, so you can see what He did.

The plain, complete way to say "there is no god but Allah" in Arabic is *mā ilāhun illā Allāh.* That is grammatically whole. That is, in fact, very close to the second half of the *shahāda* itself. Nothing is wrong with it.

But the verse adds one tiny word: **مِنْ** — *min.* *Mā **min** ilāhin illā Allāh.*

The grammarians have a name for this *min.* They call it *min al-zāʾida* — the "extra" or "augmentative" *min.* And the moment you hear "extra," your instinct is to think *decorative, skippable.* Resist that completely. al-Qurtubī tells us exactly why it is there: *"min" zāʾidatun lit-tawkīd* — "the *min* is added **for emphasis.**" al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn note the same — that grammatically it could be removed and the meaning would stand, but it was placed there to do something the bare sentence cannot do.

Here is what it does. Teach yourself the difference with English again.

"There is no apple in the basket." Fine — there's no apple.

"There is not a *single* apple in the basket." Now I have closed every loophole. Not one. Not even a sliver of one. Nothing of that kind, in any quantity, at all.

That second sentence is what *min* does in Arabic. *Mā ilāhun illā Allāh* says "there is no god but Allah." *Mā **min** ilāhin illā Allāh* says **"there is not so much as a single instance of a god — not one, not any, of any kind — except Allah."** The *min* reaches into the category of "god" and sweeps it utterly empty, leaving exactly one Being standing.

[PAUSE]

Do you see why this particular phrasing, here, after this particular passage? The entire debate with Najran was about *whether the category of divinity has more than one occupant.* Is Isa in it? Is there a son in it? A spirit, a partner, a second? And the verse does not merely say *no.* It uses the one grammatical particle whose whole job is to **empty a category to zero before naming its single exception.** *Not one. Not any. Of any kind. Except Allah.*

And al-Ṭabarī puts his finger on *why* the category empties to exactly one. There is, he says, no object of worship that *deserves* the worship of creation *bi-mulkihi iyyāhum* — "by His ownership of them" — except the One whom you worship, and He is Allah. Read that slowly. Worship is the right of the *Owner.* And only One owns. Every other candidate for divinity is itself owned — including Isa, who did not author his own existence but was *given* his life, *given* his word, *given* his miracles, the servant whose very breath was a loan. The owned do not receive worship; the Owner does. That is the floor beneath the floor: the category of "god" empties to one not by decree alone but because there is only one Being who is not Himself somebody's creation.

And now — read the absence. Look back at the architecture of the whole verse. The two outer statements get the full triple emphasis: *inna... lahuwa.* But this central sentence, the *lā ilāha illā Allāh*, is *not* dressed in *inna* and the pronoun of separation. It is stated more bareley — carried by the single quiet *min.*

Why the difference? Sit with it. The two things that get the heaviest emphasis are the claims a doubting heart might *resist*: "this is the true account" (you might dispute it) and "Allah is the Mighty and Wise" (you might wonder, after all this talk of Isa, whether He is). Those need the pillars of *inna.* But the middle — *there is no god but Allah* — is not presented as a claim to be argued into you. It is presented as the **floor.** The bedrock that does not need shoring up because everything else is already standing on it. You do not prop up the ground. The *min* is enough — a single quiet word that simply confirms what the ground already is.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

So let us gather what the words alone have shown us, before we ask what it means for our lives.

*Al-qaṣaṣ* — this is not a story bent into shape. It is the trail of the real, read exactly as it lies in the sand. *Al-ḥaqq* seals it: what the trail shows is not appearance but the fixed, undeniable reality. The doubled *lahuwa* builds two pillars of pure certainty at the two ends of the verse. And between them, the quiet *min* empties the entire category of divinity down to one.

The grammar has drawn us a picture of *what is real and how we know it.* Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we are meant to live inside a world full of competing stories.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Living in the age of "your truth" and "my truth"

I want to start where you already live.

You have heard, probably this week, some version of the phrase: *"that's your truth, and this is mine."* It is one of the defining ideas of our moment. The thought behind it is generous, even kind: who am I to tell you that your experience is wrong? And there is something real in it — humility is good, and people *do* experience the same event differently.

But notice what happens when you push it all the way. If everything is "a truth," then nothing is *the* truth. If every account is just a community telling its own story in its own interest, then the only question left is *whose story is louder, whose is more powerful, whose has the better marketing.* Truth becomes a popularity contest. And deep down — be honest — that exhausts you. Because some part of you does not actually believe that the question "did this happen or not?" is just a matter of taste. Some part of you still wants to know **what is actually so.**

[PAUSE]

This is exactly the world the Najran delegation lived in. Multiple communities, each with a sincere, ancient, internally-coherent account of Isa. Each could say to the other: *that's your story; this is ours.* And they could have stayed there forever, two narratives passing in the night, never touching ground.

Now hear the verse drop into that world. *Inna hādhā lahuwa l-qaṣaṣu l-ḥaqq.* And remember what *qaṣaṣ* actually means. Allah does not enter the marketplace of stories as one more vendor shouting *mine is best.* He enters as **the One who was there** — the One who does not need to construct an account because He witnessed the footprints being made. The tracker who invents is a fraud. But the One who *laid the trail in the first place* does not even need to track it. He simply reports what He Himself did.

This is the quiet, staggering claim under the word *qaṣaṣ*: every other account of Isa is a human being standing over footprints they did not make, trying to read them. This account is the **Maker of the footprints telling you where the feet went.**

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes where you go for the floor. When the world tells you that everything is narrative, that there is no solid ground, that it is stories all the way down — the believer has a place to stand that is not "my story is louder than yours." It is: *there is One who was there, who does not bend the account for any community's comfort, and He has told us how the trail actually lies.* You are not choosing the prettiest story. You are reading the surveyor's record.

And here is the practical edge of it. The next time you feel that vertigo — the modern dizziness of *how can anyone know anything* — do not respond by gripping your opinion harder. Respond by going back to the *qaṣaṣ.* Open the account Allah Himself gave. Not because it is yours, not because it is your community's, but because of the claim the word itself makes: *this one was not invented. This one was witnessed.* That is a different kind of certainty than the certainty of the loud. It is the certainty of the *ground.*

### Theme Two: Why "no god but Allah" needed Might and Wisdom standing guard over it

Now to the second thing, and this one I find almost unbearably beautiful once you see it.

Let me start, again, in human experience.

Think about why human beings have, throughout history, imagined that God needs a son, or a partner, or a council of lesser gods. Strip it down and there are really two anxieties underneath.

The first anxiety is about **power.** A king needs an heir because the king will *die*, and his line needs to continue, and one man cannot hold the whole kingdom alone — he needs sons, ministers, an army. We project this upward. Surely the Divine, too, needs help, needs continuation, needs a second to share the load. A son is what you give to a power that is *not enough by itself.*

The second anxiety is about **competence.** We look at the world — its suffering, its apparent chaos, the death of the innocent, the prosperity of the cruel — and a quiet doubt creeps in: *maybe whoever is running this isn't fully in control. Maybe mistakes are being made. Maybe it needs correcting.* A God who needs a rescuer, a redeemer to fix what went wrong, is a God whose original plan must have had a flaw in it.

[PAUSE]

Now watch how the verse ends. After *there is no god but Allah*, it closes with two names — and not just any two. *Wa inna Allāha lahuwa l-**ʿazīzu** l-**ḥakīm**.* The All-Mighty. The All-Wise.

*Al-ʿAzīz*, from the root **ʿ-z-z** — the One of overwhelming might, the One who cannot be overcome. al-Qurtubī glosses it precisely: *al-ladhī lā yughlab* — "the One who is never defeated." al-Jalālayn says *al-ʿazīz fī mulkih* — mighty in His dominion, in His very kingship.

*Al-Ḥakīm*, from the root **ḥ-k-m** — the All-Wise, the One whose every act is placed with perfect wisdom. al-Jalālayn: *al-ḥakīm fī ṣunʿih* — wise in His making, in His crafting of everything He does. al-Muyassar: *al-ḥakīm fī tadbīrih wa fiʿlih* — wise in His planning and His doing.

Now put it together with the two human anxieties, and feel the verse answer them one by one, in order.

The anxiety about power — *surely He needs a son to help carry it all*? **Al-ʿAzīz.** He is the One who *cannot be overpowered.* A son, a partner, a helper — these are the equipment of the *weak.* You take a partner because you are not enough alone. You raise an heir because you will not last. But the One who is *al-ʿAzīz* will never be defeated and will never end. **He does not need a second, because He was never in danger of being insufficient.** The whole premise of "God needs a son" collapses the instant you understand that He is *al-ʿAzīz.*

[PAUSE]

And the anxiety about competence — *surely something went wrong that needs fixing*? **Al-Ḥakīm.** Everything He did — including everything in the account of Isa you just heard: the fatherless birth, the miracles, the trial of it all — was placed with perfect wisdom. There is no flaw to redeem, no error in the original draft, no plan that fell apart and needed a rescue mission. *Al-Ḥakīm* means it was *meant*, all of it, exactly as it unfolded.

So look at what the architecture is actually doing. We said earlier that this verse has two pillars of pure certainty — the doubled *lahuwa* — standing at its two ends, with *lā ilāha illā Allāh* in the middle. Now you can see what those pillars are made of, and what they are holding up.

The first pillar: *this is the true account.* The witness's certainty.

The middle, the floor: *there is no god but Allah.*

The second pillar: *Allah is al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm.*

And here is the wonder. The closing two names are not decoration. They are the **two legs the central truth stands on.** *Why* is there no god but Allah? Because He is *al-ʿAzīz* — too mighty to need a partner — and *al-Ḥakīm* — too wise to have a flaw that requires one. The might removes the *need* for another god; the wisdom removes the *room* for another god's correction. Take away either name and a door cracks open for a second deity. Keep both, and the door is sealed. **Tawhīd is not just asserted in this verse; it is structurally defended by the two names that close it.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

When you are afraid — and you will be — notice which anxiety has you. Is it the power-anxiety: *who is going to help me, who is strong enough to carry this, I am alone with a load too heavy*? Then say *al-ʿAzīz*, and remember that the One you belong to is never overpowered, never insufficient, never in need of reinforcement — and neither, therefore, is the help He can send you. Or is it the competence-anxiety: *this is chaos, this makes no sense, this was a mistake, something has gone wrong with my life that cannot be fixed*? Then say *al-Ḥakīm*, and remember that the same wisdom that placed a fatherless child in the arms of Maryam and made him speak from the cradle is the wisdom placing the events of your life — that nothing in it is a flaw in the draft. It is *al-qaṣaṣ al-ḥaqq* for you, too: the true account, being written by *al-Ḥakīm.*

Two names. The exact medicine for the two oldest fears of the human heart.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse at once, because the shape of it is the proof of it.

> **إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْقَصَصُ ٱلْحَقُّ** — *the witness's certainty: this trail is real*
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**وَمَا مِنْ إِلَـٰهٍ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ** — *the floor: the category emptied to One*
>
> **وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ** — *the defense: too mighty to need, too wise to fail*

Two pillars of identical construction — *inna... lahuwa* — standing guard at the two ends. Between them, carried by a single quiet *min*, the bedrock truth that everything rests on. The first pillar establishes *that the account is real.* The second pillar establishes *the two attributes that make its central conclusion unbreakable.* And the conclusion sits protected in the middle, needing no emphasis, because it is the ground the whole structure stands on.

You could not say *lā ilāha illā Allāh* more completely than this verse says it. It states it, it empties the category around it with *min*, and then it props it up on the two divine names that remove the only two reasons anyone ever invented a second god.

### Bringing it together

And now see that the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

*Al-qaṣaṣ* told us: this is not a story; it is the trail read by the One who made it. And *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* told us *why* that trail can be trusted absolutely — because the One reporting it is too mighty to be threatened into bending it, and too wise to have anything in it worth hiding. The Might guarantees He has no *motive* to deceive; the Wisdom guarantees He has no *error* to cover. The witness is *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — and that is exactly why His *qaṣaṣ* is *al-ḥaqq.*

The word at the start and the names at the end are reaching for each other across the verse, and the *lā ilāha illā Allāh* is what they are protecting between them.

### Questions to carry

- When I feel the modern vertigo — *everyone has their own truth* — where do I actually go for solid ground? To the loudest voice in my feed, or to the trail read by the One who was there?
- Which of the two old fears tends to grip me — that no one strong enough will help me (*al-ʿAzīz*), or that something has gone irreparably wrong with my life (*al-Ḥakīm*)? Which name have I been forgetting to say?
- Do I treat *lā ilāha illā Allāh* as a *slogan I repeat*, or as the *floor I stand on* — the thing everything else in my life is structurally resting upon?
- When I tell the story of my own life, am I *inventing a narrative* that flatters me, or am I willing to *read the footprints* of what actually happened — including the parts I'd rather edit out?
- Is there a place in my faith where I have been quietly choosing the prettier story over the true account?

### One-sentence distillation

This is not one story among many — it is the trail of the real, read by the One mighty enough to have no reason to bend it and wise enough to have nothing in it to hide; therefore there is, and can be, no god but Him alone.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to live in the dizziness of *everything is just a story.* There is an account that was witnessed, not invented — and its single, central, defended conclusion is the lightest and heaviest sentence a human being can say: *there is no god but Allah.* The invitation of this verse is simply to let that sentence move from being a thing you *recite* to being the ground you *stand on* — propped up, every time you waver, by the two names that close the verse: He is mighty enough that you are never truly alone, and wise enough that nothing in your life is a mistake.

### A brief du'ā

O Allah, *al-ʿAzīz* — You who are never overcome — let my heart rest in the truth that You are enough, and that the help You send is enough. O Allah, *al-Ḥakīm* — You who place all things with perfect wisdom — let me trust that the account You are writing of my life is *al-qaṣaṣ al-ḥaqq*, the true one, even in the parts I cannot yet read. And let *lā ilāha illā Allāh* be not a phrase on my tongue but the ground beneath my feet, until the day I meet You. Āmīn.
