# The Covenant of the Prophets: When the Most-Given Owe the Most

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to it first in the language it was revealed in. Don't worry about catching every word yet. Just feel the shape of it — the way it rises into a command, drops into a question, lifts again into an answer.

> **وَإِذْ أَخَذَ ٱللَّهُ مِيثَٰقَ ٱلنَّبِيِّۦنَ لَمَآ ءَاتَيْتُكُم مِّن كِتَٰبٍ وَحِكْمَةٍ ثُمَّ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مُّصَدِّقٌ لِّمَا مَعَكُمْ لَتُؤْمِنُنَّ بِهِۦ وَلَتَنصُرُنَّهُۥ ۚ قَالَ ءَأَقْرَرْتُمْ وَأَخَذْتُمْ عَلَىٰ ذَٰلِكُمْ إِصْرِى ۖ قَالُوٓاْ أَقْرَرْنَا ۚ قَالَ فَٱشْهَدُوا۟ وَأَنَا۠ مَعَكُم مِّنَ ٱلشَّٰهِدِينَ**

*Wa idh akhadha-llāhu mīthāqa-n-nabiyyīn, lamā ātaytukum min kitābin wa ḥikmatin thumma jāʾakum rasūlun muṣaddiqun limā maʿakum, latuʾminunna bihi wa latanṣurunnah. Qāla a-aqrartum wa akhadhtum ʿalā dhālikum iṣrī? Qālū aqrarnā. Qāla fashhadū wa ana maʿakum mina-sh-shāhidīn.*

"And remember when Allah took the covenant of the prophets: *Whatever I have given you of Scripture and wisdom — then a messenger comes to you confirming what is with you — you shall most certainly believe in him and you shall most certainly support him.* He said: *Do you affirm this, and take up My binding pledge on that?* They said: *We affirm.* He said: *Then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses.*"

### Rebuild the world

To understand why this verse lands the way it does, you have to understand the argument it interrupts.

For most of this section of Aal-Imran, a conversation has been unfolding with the People of the Book — the inheritors of Moses and Jesus, the keepers of the Torah and the Gospel. And underneath their resistance to the new message there is a quiet, powerful assumption. It is the assumption of *seniority*. We came first. Our prophets came before yours. Our scripture is older. Who is this unlettered man from Makkah to ask *us* to follow *him*? The whole posture rests on a hierarchy of time: the older covenant outranks the newer one.

Now feel what this verse does to that.

Allah does not argue with the seniority claim. He goes *over its head*. He reaches back — past Muhammad ﷺ, past Jesus, past Moses, past every named prophet — to a moment when **the prophets themselves** stood before their Lord. And in that moment, a pledge was taken. Not from the followers. From the prophets. The very figures whose authority the People of the Book are invoking were themselves placed under oath — an oath that pointed *forward*, to a messenger yet to come.

[PAUSE]

Imagine the most senior figure you can think of in any tradition — the founder, the one everyone defers to. Now imagine discovering that this founder, at the very start, signed a document promising to defer to someone who would arrive later. Everything inverts. The seniority you were leaning on turns out to lean, itself, on something further still.

### The Hook

There is a word buried in the middle of this verse that should stop us cold. When the prophets are told what they owe this future messenger, the verb doesn't merely say "believe." It is built so that refusal becomes almost grammatically impossible — the Arabic clamps down on it like a lock turning.

And then, at the very end, Allah does something that should make us tremble. After the prophets affirm, He says: *bear witness.* And then He adds four words that change the entire weight of the scene — *and I am with you among the witnesses.*

Why would the One who is taking the oath also stand as a witness to it? What is Allah telling us about the nature of a promise — and about what it means to have been *given* something?

Let's go slowly. There is more here than a single sitting can exhaust.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مِيثَاق — the knot that does not slip

Before we touch the word, let's build the idea underneath it.

Think about the difference between two kinds of agreement. There is the casual "yeah, sure, I'll be there" — the kind that evaporates the moment something better comes up. And there is the other kind: the signature on a mortgage, the vow at a wedding, the oath a surgeon takes. What separates them? Not sincerity in the moment. Both might be sincere. What separates them is **bindingness** — the sense that this thing is now *tied*, that you cannot simply walk it back, that something has been knotted that will hold under strain.

Arabic has a word for exactly that quality of tied-ness. Hold it in your mind: the root **و-ث-ق** (*w-th-q*).

From this single root the language grows a family of words. *Wathiqa* — to trust, to rely on something because it holds. *Wuthqā* — that which is firm and dependable; you know it from *al-ʿurwa al-wuthqā*, "the most trustworthy handhold," the grip that will not break. *Wathāq* — the cord or bond used to tie a captive. And our word: **مِيثَاق** — *mīthāq* — a pledge so firm it is essentially a *binding*.

[PAUSE]

Now notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say Allah *made an agreement* with the prophets. He did not use a soft word. He said:

> **أَخَذَ ٱللَّهُ مِيثَٰقَ ٱلنَّبِيِّۦنَ**
> *Allah took the mīthāq of the prophets.*

Two things are doing work here. First, the word *mīthāq* itself — from the root of binding, of the cord that holds the captive, of the handhold that does not break. This is not a handshake. This is a knot tied by the hand of God.

And second — *akhadha*, "took." Not "gave," not "offered," not "proposed." **Took.** You take what is now in your grip. The language places the covenant firmly in Allah's possession the moment it is made. He took it. It is held.

al-Ṭabarī, glossing the word, says something that closes the loop on the root itself: the *mīthāq* of the prophets is *what they bound upon themselves* — *mā wathaqū bihi ʿalā anfusihim* — of obedience to Allah in all He commanded and forbade. Hear the verb hiding inside the noun. *Mīthāq* is not just a thing they were given; it is the thing they *made firm* (*wathaqū*) upon their own souls. The binding was tied from both ends — by the hand of God, and by the assent of the bound.

Let's sit with who this binding is taken *from*. مِيثَاق ٱلنَّبِيِّينَ — the covenant of *the prophets*. The classical commentators — al-Qurtubi gathers the views of Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Qatāda, Ṭāwūs, al-Suddī, and al-Ḥasan — read this as the covenant taken from the prophets themselves: that each would affirm the truth of the others, that each would instruct his people toward the one to come. These are not weak souls who need a heavy cord to keep them in line. These are the *strongest* believers who ever walked the earth. The ones who spoke to God directly.

And *they* are the ones bound.

Here is what that reveals. **The closer you are to God, the more firmly you are tied to Him.** We tend to imagine that nearness to Allah means freedom from obligation — that the saints float above the rules the rest of us are chained to. The grammar says the opposite. The prophets, the nearest of all, are the ones from whom the *firmest* knot is taken. Intimacy is not the loosening of the bond. Intimacy *is* the bond, pulled tight.

And there is a further dimension that turns the whole scene toward the listener. Ibn Kathīr, reporting from ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib and Ibn ʿAbbās, notes that Allah took from every prophet the pledge that, were Muhammad ﷺ sent in his lifetime, he would believe in him and support him — and that each prophet was charged to bind his own *nation* to the same. The cord does not stop at the prophets. Through them it runs down to the communities they led — which is precisely why a covenant taken from prophets long dead falls, with full force, on the People of the Book listening in Madinah. al-Muyassar reads it the same way: the *mīthāq* was taken upon every prophet to believe in Muhammad ﷺ, *and* upon the nations of those prophets through them. The seniority the listeners lean on does not exempt them from the covenant — it *enrolls* them in it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: لَتُؤْمِنُنَّ وَلَتَنصُرُنَّهُ — the grammar that leaves no exit

Let me teach you a feature of Arabic that English simply cannot reproduce, and once you see it you will never read this verse the same way.

In English, if I want to make a promise stronger, I have to add words. "I will go" becomes "I will *definitely* go," becomes "I *swear* I will go, I promise, you have my word." I pile adverbs on top of the verb because the verb itself can't carry more weight.

Arabic does it differently. Arabic can reach *inside* the verb and load it. There is a device called the **nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīla** — the "heavy nūn of emphasis." You take a verb, you prefix it with an emphatic *lām*, and you clamp a doubled, weighted *nūn* onto its end. The result is a verb that, by its very shape, has become an oath. It no longer merely describes an action. It *vows* it.

Now look at what the prophets are told they owe the coming messenger:

> **لَتُؤْمِنُنَّ بِهِۦ وَلَتَنصُرُنَّهُۥ**
> *latuʾminunna bihi wa latanṣurunnah*

Both verbs are clamped. *La-tuʾminu-nna* — "you shall most assuredly believe in him." *La-tanṣuru-nna-hu* — "you shall most assuredly support him." Two locks turned, one after the other. This is not "you should believe." It is not even "you must believe." It is the strongest possible form the language has: a vow built into the bone of the verb.

Notice, too, that these two verbs are the answer to the binding. The grammar of the whole sentence is the grammar of an *oath and its response* — and the commentators point this out: the *lām* at the front of the clause is the *lām* of the oath, and *latuʾminunna* is its sworn conclusion. The structure is: *By the covenant I have taken — you shall believe, you shall support.*

And look at the **two** verbs Allah chose. Not one. He could have stopped at *belief*. Believing would seem to be enough. But belief that stays inside the chest is not what is demanded. The second clamp falls on **نصر** — *naṣr*, to help, to support, to come to the aid of. Believing and *standing up for*. The inner conviction and the outer allegiance. Allah locks them both, separately, because He knows the human capacity to believe something privately while abandoning it publicly. The covenant forecloses that escape. You shall believe — *and* you shall be seen to stand with him.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Why would prophets — *prophets* — need their belief locked into a vow? Are these not the very people whose faith is beyond question?

Here is what the construction quietly tells us. The hardest thing being asked of them is not belief in God. They already have that, completely. The hardest thing is to **believe in someone who comes after you, and to lower yourself to support him.** To accept that what you carry — your scripture, your wisdom — is not the final word, but a confirmation of something still to arrive. *That* is the test that needs the heavy nūn. Not faith in the unseen God. Faith that you are not the last chapter.

### Element Three: مُصَدِّق — the one who comes not to cancel but to confirm

There is a single word describing the coming messenger that resolves what the heavy nūn just opened up. Watch it carefully:

> **ثُمَّ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مُّصَدِّقٌ لِّمَا مَعَكُمْ**
> *then comes to you a messenger, **confirming** what is with you.*

The word is **مُصَدِّق** — *muṣaddiq*, from the root **ص-د-ق** (*ṣ-d-q*), the root of *ṣidq*: truth, truthfulness, sincerity. The form here is Form II — *ṣaddaqa* — which here carries a declarative-transitive force: not "to be true" but **"to declare true," "to affirm as truth," "to verify."**

This matters enormously, so let me make it concrete. Imagine two letters arrive about your inheritance. The first says: *everything the previous lawyer told you is void; start over.* The second says: *everything in your existing documents is genuine — and here is the final clause that completes them.* The first cancels. The second **confirms**. The first asks you to throw away what you held. The second tells you that what you held was true all along, and honors it by completing it.

The coming messenger is the *second* letter. He is *muṣaddiq* — he comes to verify what the prophets already carry, not to erase it.

[PAUSE]

Now feel how this dissolves the prophets' burden. The heavy nūn demanded something that sounded almost humiliating — believe in one who comes after you, support him, accept you are not the final word. But *muṣaddiq* reveals that to follow this messenger is not to betray your own scripture. **It is to see your own scripture vindicated.** The thing you spent your life carrying is not discarded; it is *confirmed*. Supporting him is not abandoning your message. It is your message arriving at its destination.

This is why, in al-Muyassar's reading and al-Qurtubi's, the covenant of the prophets is essentially a covenant of mutual *confirmation*: each prophet affirming the truth of the others, the chain holding together across the centuries, every link verifying the next. The *naṣr*, the support, *is* the tasdīq, the confirmation. To help the later one is to be true to the earlier.

And there is one more thing the grammar slips in — almost too quietly to notice. The classical readers differed over the small word **لَمَا** at the head of the covenant. Most read it *la-mā*, with an emphatic *lām* — "indeed, whatever I have given you." But Ḥamza, as al-Baghawi records, read it *li-mā*, with the *lām* of purpose — "**for the sake of** whatever I have given you." Hold both, the way the tradition holds both, as two camera angles on one truth. *Indeed, whatever I gave you* — emphasizing the gift. And *because of what I gave you* — making the gift itself the very *reason* for the obligation. The more I gave you, the more this is required of you. We will come back to that. It is the heart of the matter.

### The bridge

So here is what the words have shown us. A *mīthāq* — a knot tied by God's own hand, taken from the strongest souls alive. Two verbs clamped shut with the heaviest emphasis the language possesses — believe, and stand up. And a single word, *muṣaddiq*, that turns what looked like surrender into vindication.

But these are observations about language. Now let's ask what they reveal not just about words — but about the shape of our own lives. Because this covenant was taken from prophets. And yet the verse was revealed for *us* to overhear. Why?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: To whom much is given — the weight of having been given

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that belongs only to capable people. Think of the one in every family that everyone calls when something goes wrong — because they always know what to do. Think of the doctor who cannot un-know what a symptom means, even off-duty, even at a dinner party. Think of the friend everyone confides in, who carries everyone's secrets and has no one to carry their own. There is a strange, lonely law that governs all of them: **the more you are able to give, the more is silently demanded of you.** Capacity becomes obligation. The gift becomes the leash.

We feel this. We sometimes resent it. *Why is it always me?* And underneath the resentment is a buried intuition — that being given much is not purely a privilege. It is also a *weight*.

Now hear the verse name that law out loud, and put it at the very center of the covenant.

> **لَمَآ ءَاتَيْتُكُم مِّن كِتَٰبٍ وَحِكْمَةٍ ... لَتُؤْمِنُنَّ بِهِۦ وَلَتَنصُرُنَّهُۥ**
> *Whatever I have given you of Scripture and wisdom ... you shall believe in him and support him.*

Watch the sentence's logic. It does not say "because you are prophets, you must obey." It says: *because of what I gave you* — Scripture, wisdom — *therefore* this is owed. The obligation flows directly out of the gift. Remember Ḥamza's reading: *li-mā* — *for the sake of what I gave you.* The very thing that made them honored — the revelation in their hands — is the very thing that binds them tightest. **The gift is the source of the demand.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the spiritual physics of this. We instinctively think of Allah's gifts as pure ease — health, knowledge, wealth, faith, the Qur'an in our homes. We thank Him for them and imagine the account is settled. But this verse reveals that **every gift opens a corresponding account of responsibility.** The Scripture given to the prophets did not merely elevate them. It *obligated* them — so completely that even *they* could not plead exemption. The greatest gift produced the heaviest covenant.

And here is the thing that should reach us where we sit. If the prophets — given the most, the purest, the highest — were bound *because* of what they were given, then what about us? We hold the final Scripture. We hold a wisdom they longed to see. The same logic that bound them runs straight through to our chests. *Whatever I have given you* is not a verse about the past. It is a sentence with our name in the blank.

Let this reframe the question we ask about our own lives. We tend to ask: *what have I been given?* — and we count, and we feel either grateful or cheated. The verse asks a harder question underneath it: *given what I have, what is now owed?* The Qur'an on your shelf is not only a mercy. It is a *mīthāq*. To have it is to be tied to it.

What does this change tomorrow? It changes what you feel when you reach for the Mushaf, or hear an ayah you've heard a hundred times. The reflex of "I am blessed to have this" stays — but beneath it, quietly, a second reflex should grow: *and therefore something is asked of me that is not asked of one who never received it.* Knowledge you did not act on is not neutral. It is an open covenant. The cure for the weight is not to put the gift down. It is to do with it what the covenant requires — to believe, and to *stand up*.

### Theme Two: "Bear witness — and I am with you among the witnesses"

Let me start again from human experience.

Why do we make people say things *out loud*? Why the spoken vow at a wedding, when both already know? Why the raised hand in court, the signature with a witness watching, the public announcement of a commitment we could have kept private? There is something we understand intuitively: a commitment spoken before witnesses is a different *kind* of commitment than one held silently in the heart. Witnessing changes the weight. Once others have heard you say it, you are no longer only accountable to yourself. The promise has left your private chamber and entered the world, where it can be remembered, and where you can be held to it.

Now watch the end of the verse move through exactly this, step by deliberate step.

> **قَالَ ءَأَقْرَرْتُمْ ... قَالُوٓاْ أَقْرَرْنَا ... قَالَ فَٱشْهَدُوا۟ وَأَنَا۠ مَعَكُم مِّنَ ٱلشَّٰهِدِينَ**
> *He said: Do you affirm? ... They said: We affirm. ... He said: Then bear witness — and I am with you among the witnesses.*

First, the word **أَقْرَرْتُمْ** — *a-aqrartum*, "do you affirm?" — from the root **ق-ر-ر** (*q-r-r*). This is a root worth pausing on. Its core sense is *settling*, *coming to rest*, *being fixed in place*. From it comes *qarār* — a settled, stable abode. From it comes *qurrat al-ʿayn* — the "coolness of the eye," the deep rest of a heart that has found peace. And from it, in Form IV, comes *aqarra* — **to affirm, to acknowledge, to confess** — literally, *to make a thing settle and stand firm*. When you *aqarra* something, you cause the truth to *come to rest* in the open. To affirm is to take what was unsettled and fix it in place.

So when Allah asks *a-aqrartum* — He is not asking "do you understand?" He is asking: *will you let this settle in you and stand?*

And He pairs the question with a word we should not pass over: *a-aqrartum wa akhadhtum ʿalā dhālikum* — **إِصْرِى** — *iṣrī*. The root **أ-ص-ر** (*ʾ-ṣ-r*) names something that *holds a thing down and confines it in place* — a weight, a heavy binding. It is the very word the believers will plead against at the close of al-Baqarah: *rabbanā wa lā taḥmil ʿalaynā iṣran kamā ḥamaltahu ʿalā-lladhīna min qablinā* — "our Lord, do not lay upon us a burden — *iṣr* — like the one You laid on those before us" (2:286). The classical commentators — Ibn ʿAbbās among them, as Ibn Kathīr and al-Ṭabarī record — gloss *iṣrī* here as *My covenant*, My solemn and weighty pledge. So Allah is not asking merely "do you agree?" He is asking: *have you taken the full weight of this upon yourselves?* Affirmation by itself is a word; *iṣr* is a load lifted onto the shoulders. He asks them to do both — to let the truth settle (*aqarra*) and to shoulder its weight (*iṣr*) — and only then do they answer.

And the prophets answer with the same root as the question's first verb, the same settling: **أَقْرَرْنَا** — *aqrarnā* — "we have let it settle; we affirm." The thing is now fixed. It has come to rest.

[PAUSE]

And then comes the command that should make us pause longest: **فَٱشْهَدُوا۟** — *fashhadū* — "then bear witness." Witness to *what*? al-Muyassar unfolds the command in two directions at once: *let some of you bear witness over others* — the prophets attesting to one another's pledge — *and bear witness over your nations*, binding the very communities they lead into the same testimony. To each other, then; and through each other, to everyone they carry. The prophets are made witnesses over the covenant they just entered. The private settling becomes a public testimony. The thing in the heart is now spoken into the open, before others, where it cannot be quietly walked back.

But the verse does not end with the prophets witnessing. It ends with four words that should stop your breath:

> **وَأَنَا۠ مَعَكُم مِّنَ ٱلشَّٰهِدِينَ**
> *and I am with you among the witnesses.*

[PAUSE]

Think about what has just happened. Allah *took* the covenant. Allah *is* the one to whom it is owed. By every human logic, He is the other party — the one across the table. And then He says: *and I am with you among the witnesses.* He steps around to the prophets' own side and stands *with* them, watching.

Why? Why would the One who imposes the oath also station Himself as its witness?

Here is what it opens. A witness is the one who *remembers* — the one before whom you cannot later pretend it never happened. By naming Himself a witness, Allah is telling the prophets, and telling us: **this is never forgotten.** The covenant does not fade with the centuries. There is no statute of limitations, no quiet expiry. The One who sees all has logged it, and stands ready to recall it on the Day every account is opened. And al-Muyassar catches the reach of His witnessing: *I am with you among the witnesses* — *ʿalaykum wa ʿalayhim*, **over you and over them**. Not the prophets alone, but the nations enrolled through them; the same eye that recorded the pledge records who kept it, all the way down the chain.

And the very next breath of revelation gives that memory its edge. As Ibn Kathīr brings the two verses together, Allah continues: *fa-man tawallā baʿda dhālika fa-ulāʾika humu-l-fāsiqūn* — "then whoever turns away after this, *those* are the ones who have broken faith" (3:82). The witnessing is not ceremonial. To turn from a covenant the All-Seeing has witnessed is not a private lapse you can quietly absorb — it is *fisq*, a stepping outside the bond itself. The witness who stands *with* you is also the witness who will name the turning-away for exactly what it is.

And yet — feel the strange tenderness in *maʿakum*, "*with* you." He does not say "and I am *over* you, watching." He says "*with* you, among the witnesses." It is the language of *accompaniment*, not surveillance. The same God who binds you does not abandon you to the binding and walk away. He stays. He stands in the room. The covenant is heavy, yes — but you do not carry the memory of it alone. The One who holds you to it is also the One who is *with* you in it.

[PAUSE]

What does this change tomorrow? It changes the seriousness of what you say to Allah. We make commitments to Him constantly — half-whispered after a sin, *never again*; the resolution at the start of Ramadan; the quiet vow on the prayer mat. And we treat them as though, having been spoken privately, they can be privately retired. This verse tells us: **there are no private vows with Allah.** Every one is made before the ultimate Witness, who does not forget, and who is *with* you in it. That should make us slower to promise — and, once we have promised, far slower to break it. To live as though Allah is *among the witnesses* of your life is to live a life that you would not be ashamed to have recalled, in full, on the Day He recalls it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we have walked through the words, step back and see the shape of the whole verse. It is built as a perfect descending-then-ascending dialogue:

1. **Allah states the covenant** — *whatever I have given you ... you shall believe and support* (the gift, then the obligation).
2. **Allah asks** — *do you affirm, and take up My binding pledge?*
3. **They answer** — *we affirm.*
4. **Allah commands and joins** — *then bear witness, and I am with you among the witnesses.*

Statement → question → affirmation → witnessing. It is the exact structure of how a binding agreement is ratified anywhere on earth: the terms are laid out, consent is sought, consent is given, and the agreement is witnessed and sealed. Allah cast an eternal pre-historic covenant in the most recognizable legal shape a human heart can understand — so that we would feel, in our bones, that this is *real*, and *binding*, and *ours*.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The **language** showed us a knot (*mīthāq*) tied by God's own hand, two verbs locked shut with the heaviest emphasis the tongue can carry, and a word (*muṣaddiq*) that turns surrender into vindication.

The **themes** showed us why: *because of what you were given*, you are bound — the gift is the leash; and *because Allah Himself witnesses*, the binding is never forgotten and never faced alone.

Put them together and the whole verse says one thing: **To be given by Allah is to be bound to Allah — and He who binds you stays with you in the binding.** The People of the Book leaned on the seniority of their scripture. The verse answers: that very scripture was a *mīthāq*, and the covenant it created pointed forward, not backward. What you were given was never a trophy to rest on. It was a cord, tied to your wrist, with the other end in the hand of God.

### Questions to carry

1. When I count Allah's gifts to me, do I ever ask what each one *obligates* — or only what each one *provides*?
2. The prophets' hardest test was to accept that they were not the final word. Where in my own life is my faith really a faith that *I* am not the center?
3. What have I been *given* that I am quietly refusing to *stand up for*?
4. I have made private vows to Allah after my failures. If Allah is "among the witnesses" of every one — how many are still open against me?
5. Does it comfort me or unsettle me that the God who binds me says *I am with you*? And what does my reaction reveal about how I see Him?

### One-sentence distillation

What Allah gives you is never only a gift — it is a covenant, taken by His own hand, witnessed by Him forever, and binding precisely *because* of how much He gave.

### The closing invitation

Let something shift. The next time you open the Qur'an, or remember a knowledge you have, or feel the quiet privilege of having been guided — let the gratitude stay, but let a second feeling rise beneath it: *I have been given. Therefore I am bound. And the One who bound me is with me.* Not a weight to crush you. A cord to hold you — its other end in the most trustworthy hand there is, *al-ʿurwa al-wuthqā*, the grip that never breaks.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You took the covenant of Your prophets and they affirmed, and You stood with them among the witnesses. We confess that You have given us Scripture and wisdom we did nothing to earn. So let us be among those who *affirm* and then *stand up* — and on the Day You recall every covenant, find us faithful to the cord You tied to our wrist. *Āmīn.*
