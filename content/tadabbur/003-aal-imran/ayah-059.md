# Near Allah: The Likeness of Isa, the Dust of Adam, and the Word That Is Still Speaking

> إِنَّ مَثَلَ عِيسَىٰ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ ءَادَمَ ۖ خَلَقَهُۥ مِن تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ قَالَ لَهُۥ كُن فَيَكُونُ
>
> *Inna mathala ʿĪsā ʿinda-llāhi ka-mathali Ādam — khalaqahu min turābin thumma qāla lahu kun fa-yakūn.*
>
> **"Indeed, the likeness of Isa in the sight of Allah is like the likeness of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, 'Be,' and he is."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Delegation Walks Into Madinah

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

A delegation has come to Madinah. They are Christians from Najran — and they are not casual visitors. They are scholars, bishops, men of standing, who have ridden a long way to sit across from the Prophet ﷺ and settle the question that defines their entire faith: *Who is Isa, the son of Maryam?*

And they have what feels, to them, like an unanswerable argument. It is the strongest card in their hand, and they lay it on the table early. In one of the major narrations preserved for this passage — reported by al-Baghawī — they press the Prophet ﷺ: *"You insult our master. You call him merely a servant of Allah."* And he ﷺ answers: *"Yes — he is the servant of Allah, and His Messenger, and His word which He cast to Maryam the virgin."* And they grow angry, and they play their card:

**"Have you ever seen a human being born without a father?"**

[PAUSE]

Sit inside that question for a moment. Because it is not a foolish question. It is, in fact, a *very good* question. They are reasoning the way a thoughtful person reasons. They are saying: *the ordinary explanation has failed. A child arrives with no father. Cause and effect has broken down. And when the ordinary breaks down, something extraordinary must be standing behind it.* And then they take one more step — the step that becomes a whole theology: *the extraordinary thing standing behind it must be divinity itself. This child must share in the nature of God.*

Here is what I want you to notice before we even open the verse. Allah does **not** answer this argument the way you might expect. He does not say, *"That's not such a big miracle."* He does not minimize. He does not deny that something staggering happened in the womb of Maryam. The Quran honors Isa — it calls him a word from Allah, a spirit from Him, a sign to the worlds.

So how do you answer an argument you refuse to weaken?

[PAUSE]

You answer it by **relocating** it. You take the miracle that they are standing on, and you show them a *bigger* one — a greater wonder that they themselves have *never* called divine. And you ask: if the smaller wonder proves divinity, why didn't the greater one?

That is the trap that closes in this verse. And it closes in fifteen words. By the end of our time together, I want you to feel three things that this short ayah is doing all at once: it is **winning a debate** that shaped centuries; it is teaching you **how God reckons reality** versus how human eyes reckon it; and — quietly, almost in passing — it is telling *you*, personally, what it means that you exist at all.

Let's begin where the verse begins.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: عِندَ ٱللَّهِ — "In the Sight of Allah"

### Teaching the idea first

Before we go to the word, let me give you something from ordinary life.

Imagine a coin lying on a table. From directly above, it is a perfect circle. Tilt your head, look from the edge, and it becomes a thin line — barely there. Same coin. Same object. *Two completely different truths, depending on where you are standing.*

Now here is the thing we forget: when those two views disagree, we instinctively trust *our* view. We assume the way it looks *from where I happen to be standing* is the way it actually is. We rarely stop and ask: *is there a vantage point that sees it as it truly is — and is my vantage point that one?*

Hold onto the idea of **a vantage point that defines the truth of a thing.** Because the Quran is about to name one.

### Opening the word

Look at how the verse frames its entire claim:

> إِنَّ مَثَلَ عِيسَىٰ **عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** كَمَثَلِ ءَادَمَ

*"Indeed the likeness of Isa — **ʿinda-llāh, in the sight of Allah** — is like the likeness of Adam."*

The word is **عِندَ** — *ʿinda*. We translate it softly as "near" or "with," but in Arabic it carries the sense of *in the presence of, in the estimation of, in the reckoning of.* When you say something is *ʿinda* someone, you are speaking of how it stands *in their court, by their measure.*

Notice that Allah did not have to put this phrase here at all. The sentence works without it: *the likeness of Isa is like the likeness of Adam.* Clean. Complete. So why insert **ʿinda-llāh** right in the middle, splitting the subject from its predicate, forcing us to pause on it?

### Sitting with it

Let's sit with this.

The men of Najran built their entire case on a vantage point — *the human one*. From where they stood, looking at the world through the logic of cause and effect, a fatherless birth pointed straight up to the heavens. **From their angle, the coin was a circle.** And they were not being stupid. They were being *human*. This is exactly how human reckoning works: the more something violates our expectations, the more we reach for the supernatural to explain it.

And into that, Allah drops two words: **ʿinda-llāh.**

It is as though He says: *You have told Me how Isa looks from where you are standing. Let Me tell you how he stands where it actually counts — in My presence, in My reckoning.* And the moment you shift to *that* vantage point, the whole picture re-forms. The man whose birth seemed to break the laws of the universe is suddenly standing right next to Adam — who broke them *more*.

Notice something. The argument of Najran was, at its heart, an argument *from human limitation*. *We have never seen this. We cannot explain this. Therefore — God.* They reasoned from the edge of their own knowledge to a conclusion about the divine. And **ʿinda-llāh** dismantles that in a breath, because it says: *the question was never how strange this looks to you. The question is what it is before Me.* Your astonishment is data about *you* — about the size of your knowledge — not data about the nature of Isa.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. This single phrase reframes not just this debate, but a permanent feature of how we go wrong. **We deify what astonishes us.** Whatever overwhelms our ability to explain it, we are tempted to treat as ultimate. And the cure — the whole cure — is in two words. Before you let your astonishment harden into a conclusion, *change vantage points.* Ask not *"how does this look from where I stand?"* but *"what is this ʿinda-llāh — in the sight of the One who made both the thing and the standing-place?"*

What this one word reveals: **the truth of a thing is not its appearance to us, but its standing before Allah — and the gap between those two is exactly where humanity loses its way.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: كَمَثَلِ ءَادَمَ — The Stranger Compared to the Stranger

### Teaching the idea first

Let me give you a way debaters and judges think.

Suppose someone argues: *"This thing is special — therefore it must be in a special category."* The sharpest way to answer is not to deny that it is special. It is to find something *even more* special that everyone already agrees belongs in the *ordinary* category. Because then you've shown: *being this special clearly does not move a thing into your special category — look, here is something more remarkable than your example, and you yourself have always filed it under "ordinary creature."*

The classical scholars had a name for exactly this move. They called it **تشبيه الغريب بالأغرب** — *tashbīh al-gharīb bil-aghrab* — **comparing the strange thing to the stranger thing.** Al-Jalālayn names it directly here: this is the comparison of the strange to the *more* strange, *"so that it cuts off the opponent more decisively and lands more firmly in the soul."*

Hold that phrase: *more decisively, and more firmly in the soul.* It tells you this is not only a logical move — it is a move designed to *land in the heart*, not just win on paper.

### Opening the comparison

> كَمَثَلِ **ءَادَمَ**

*"...like the likeness of Adam."*

Najran's wonder was a man born from a mother but no father. Allah answers with **Adam** — a man born from **no father *and* no mother.** Isa had one parent. Adam had *zero.* If the absence of a father makes Isa divine, then the absence of *both* parents should make Adam *more* divine still.

[PAUSE]

And here is the move that snaps the trap shut: **no one ever called Adam a god.** Not the Christians, not the Jews, not the pagans. Every community that ever heard of Adam filed him under one heading: *a created human being, a servant of God.* Al-Muyassar puts it plainly: Adam was created with neither father nor mother, *"and all agreed that he is a servant among the servants of Allah."*

So the verse has the opponent checkmated by their own logic:

> *Either the absence of a father proves nothing about divinity — in which case Isa, born without a father, is no god...*
>
> *...or it proves everything — in which case Adam, born without father OR mother, is the greater god, and your entire tradition has been wrong about him from the beginning.*

There is no third door. The argument that was supposed to *establish* the divinity of Isa, when followed honestly, *destroys* it.

### Going deeper — the precision of the likeness

But stay with me, because the scholars noticed something subtle here, and it is too beautiful to skip.

A careful reader objects: *but the comparison isn't even exact! Adam was made from dust — Isa was not made from dust. So how is Isa "like" Adam?*

Al-Qurtubī takes this objection seriously and answers it with a principle that will outlive this debate: **a thing can be compared to another, even when there are real differences between them, as long as they meet in a single shared trait.** The likeness here, he says, is not *"both were made from dust."* The likeness is precisely this one thing: **both were brought into being outside the normal chain of human cause — by the direct creative act of Allah.** That is the shared trait. That is the hinge the whole comparison turns on. Everything else — the dust, the womb, the details — falls away as irrelevant to the point being made.

And al-Qurtubī draws out something even larger: this verse, he says, is a **proof of the validity of *qiyās*** — of reasoning by analogy itself. Allah is modeling how to think rightly: *find the shared cause, reason from the known to the unknown, and don't be distracted by surface differences that don't bear on the point.* The verse doesn't just win an argument — it teaches a *method* of arriving at truth.

### Reading the absence

Now read what the verse *withholds*, because the silence speaks.

For Adam, the verse specifies his material: **خَلَقَهُۥ مِن تُرَابٍ** — *"He created him from dust."* But for Isa — the one this entire passage is *about* — the verse says nothing about his substance. No mention of what he was formed from. Why the asymmetry?

Because the point was never the *substance*. If Allah had said *"He created Isa from such-and-such,"* He would have pulled your eye toward the *material* — and the material is exactly the distraction al-Qurtubī warned us against. By specifying dust *only* for Adam, and leaving Isa's substance unmentioned, the verse keeps your eye locked on the one thing that matters: not *what they were made of*, but **that they were made — by a word.**

What this reveals: **the case for Isa's divinity and the case against it rest on the very same fact — and which way it falls depends entirely on whether you reason from appearances or from the Creator's act.**

## Element Three: كُن فَيَكُونُ — "Be," and He *Is*

### Teaching the idea first

Now we arrive at the heart of the verse — and at one of the most quietly astonishing grammatical events in the entire Quran. To feel it, you need one small piece of grammar, and I promise to make it simple.

In any language, when you tell a story about the past, your verbs stay in the past. *"He walked in. He sat down. He spoke."* If suddenly one verb jumped into the present tense — *"He walked in, he sat down, and he* **speaks**" — your ear would snag on it. Something would feel deliberate. A storyteller who does that is doing it *on purpose*, to make you feel the moment is happening *right now, in front of you.*

Hold that: **a sudden shift from past to present tense, in the middle of a past story, pulls the past into the living present.**

### Opening the words

Look at the sequence, and watch the tenses move:

> خَلَقَهُۥ مِن تُرَابٍ — *He **created** him from dust* — past.
>
> ثُمَّ قَالَ لَهُۥ — *then He **said** to him* — past.
>
> كُن — ***"Be"*** — a command.
>
> فَيَكُونُ — *and he* ***is.***

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the snag?

Every verb in this story is in the past. *Created. Said.* We are deep in the narrative of an event that happened at the dawn of the human race. And the grammar leads us right to the edge of expecting one more past-tense verb: *"Be" — and he* ***was.***

But that is not what Allah says.

He says **فَيَكُونُ — fa-yakūn — "and he *is.*"** Present tense. Imperfect. *Ongoing.*

The grammar *breaks* — on purpose. Al-Jalālayn notes the meaning here as *fa-kāna* — "and he was" — confirming that the *event* did complete; Adam came to be. But Allah did not *say* "was." He reached past the natural past-tense verb and chose the living present. **Why?**

### Sitting with the break

Let's sit with this, because the classical grammarians give us two readings, and they are not rivals — they are two windows onto the same light.

**The first reading:** this is *ḥikāyat al-ḥāl* — the narration of a state *as if it is happening before your eyes.* Allah is not reporting a dusty fact from prehistory. He is **re-staging the act of creation in the present tense** so that you do not read it as a historian — you *witness* it. The command *"Be"* is not an echo from long ago. It is happening now, in front of you, as you read. The creation of Adam stops being a story you were *told* and becomes an event you are *watching.*

**The second reading** goes even deeper. The present tense in Arabic carries the sense of the *continuous, the habitual, the always-true.* So *fa-yakūn* — "and it is" — points beyond Adam, beyond Isa, to a **standing principle of all reality**: *whenever* Allah wills a thing, He says to it "Be," **and it is.** Not "was" — *is.* Always. The verse momentarily lifts its eyes off the single case of Adam and shows you the *machinery of existence itself*: a Word, and a being-into-existence that has never once paused. (You will hear this exact construction echo across the Quran — *"His command, when He intends a thing, is only that He says to it 'Be,' and it* ***is***" — Yā-Sīn 36:82.)

[PAUSE]

Now bring both readings together and feel what they do to the debate.

The men of Najran were stunned that a man could come into being without a father. And the verse answers: *you are stunned by the wrong thing.* The wonder is not the missing father. **The wonder is the word "Be."** A father is just one ordinary channel that the creative word usually flows through. Remove the father, remove the mother, remove every channel — and *nothing changes for Allah*, because the creating was never in the channel. It was always in the **Word.** Isa without a father, Adam without parents, you with two parents — every single one of us is the same event: *kun fa-yakūn.* A command, and a coming-to-be.

### The sound of it

And listen — *listen* — to the sound. **كُن** — *kun.* One closed, clipped syllable. Two letters. The shortest possible thing a mouth can do and still command. There is no effort in it. No straining. No "let there be light and a great labor of making." Just — *kun.* And then **فَيَكُونُ** — *fa-yakūn* — the sound opens and flows out, the being pouring forth from the command as effortlessly as breath. The phonetics *enact the theology*: the smallest exertion conceivable, and an entire existence answers.

What this reveals: **the miracle Najran worshipped was never the broken law of nature — it was the Word behind every law, the "Be" that has never stopped being spoken.**

## Bridge: From the Words to the World

Look at what these fifteen words have built.

**ʿinda-llāh** taught us that the truth of a thing is its standing before Allah, not its appearance to us. **Ka-mathali Ādam** took the opponent's strongest evidence and turned it into the very thing that refutes them — by comparing the strange to the stranger. And **kun fa-yakūn** relocated the entire miracle: not in the absence of a father, but in the presence of a Word that is *still being spoken.*

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about how to win an argument from fourteen centuries ago, but about **reality itself**, and about the two of us, sitting here, *existing.*

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: We Worship What Astonishes Us

Let me start somewhere very modern, very close to home.

Think about how we react to genius. To a person who can do something no one else can do — sing in a way that stops a room, solve what no one else can solve, build what no one imagined. Watch what happens in the language around them. We say someone is *a god* at what they do. We call talent *divine*. We speak of *worshipping* an artist, *idolizing* an athlete. Listen to the words — they are not random. When something exceeds our ability to explain it, **the human heart reaches, almost automatically, for the vocabulary of worship.**

This is not a modern flaw. It is *the* ancient flaw, and the men of Najran are simply its most sincere, most sophisticated example. They saw something genuinely beyond explanation — a child with no father — and their hearts did exactly what hearts do: *this exceeds me, therefore this is divine.*

And here is the uncomfortable thing. They were not lazy thinkers. They were *careful* ones. The error was not a failure of intelligence. It was a failure of **vantage point.** They reasoned from inside their own astonishment — and astonishment, remember, is only a measurement of the *size of your own knowledge.* The smaller your understanding, the more things will astonish you, and the more gods you will be tempted to make.

Now let the verse illuminate this. **ʿinda-llāh.** *In the sight of Allah.* The cure for misplaced worship is not to become *less* amazed by the world — the Quran wants you *more* amazed, not less. The cure is to **redirect the amazement.** When you stand before something staggering — the birth of Isa, the genius of a mind, the vastness of a galaxy, the impossible engineering of your own beating heart — do not let the astonishment stop *on the thing.* Let it pass *through* the thing and land on the One whose word said *"Be"* to it.

Because here is the deep structure the verse exposes: **everything that astonishes you is a created thing.** The genius, the miracle, the cosmos — all of it is on the *kun fa-yakūn* side of the ledger. All of it is a *response* to the Word, never the Speaker of it. The moment you remember that, idolatry becomes *impossible* — not forbidden by a rule, but impossible by clear sight. You cannot worship Isa once you have seen that he stands exactly where Adam stands: a wonder *spoken into being*, never the One who spoke.

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time something overwhelms you — a talent, a beauty, a force, a fear — catch the reflex. Feel your heart begin to reach for the vocabulary of ultimacy. And gently move it one step further: *this is a "Be," not the One who says "Be."* Let your astonishment become a pointer instead of a destination. That single redirection is, very nearly, the whole of *tawḥīd* lived out in real time.

## Theme Two: You Are a Word Still Being Spoken

Now let me bring this home in the most personal way I can.

There is a feeling most of us carry, usually without naming it: the feeling that we are *self-standing.* That we exist the way a rock exists — just *there*, inertly, requiring nothing to keep us going. We woke up this morning and assumed, without a thought, that we would simply *continue.* Existence feels like a possession we own outright, paid off, ours.

Now look again at that tense. **فَيَكُونُ.** *And he* ***is.*** Not "was." *Is.* Present. Ongoing. The grammarians told us this points to a standing, continuous reality — *whenever* Allah wills, He says "Be," and it *is*.

Sit with what that does to *you.*

It means your existence is not a past event. It is not that, long ago, Allah said "Be" to you and then stepped back and left you running on your own, like a wind-up toy released across the floor. The present tense says otherwise. **You are being held in being right now.** This breath. This heartbeat. This moment of awareness reading these words — every instant of it is a fresh *"and he is,"* a continuous reply to a Word that has not stopped. You are not a thing that *was* created. You are a thing that *is being* sustained, syllable by syllable, in the ongoing present of *fa-yakūn.*

[PAUSE]

Feel the two directions this can be felt in.

There is a *trembling* in it. You are not self-standing. Remove the Word for a single instant and there is no slow collapse, no fade — there is simply *nothing*, the way the light vanishes the instant the sun does. Your continued existence is not owed to you. It is *gifted* to you, continuously, by a generosity so constant that you have mistaken it for the natural order of things. The most reliable fact of your life — *that you exist* — turns out to be the most dependent.

But there is also an *unbearable intimacy* in it. The same Word that said "Be" to Adam at the dawn of the species is saying "Be" to *you* — now — in this room. You are not a leftover of an ancient act of creation. You are a *current event* in the attention of God. The Creator is not a clockmaker who wound the universe and walked away. He is the One whose *"Be"* is, at this very second, the only reason there is a "you" here to wonder about Him.

And now watch the two themes fuse into one. The men of Najran looked at Isa and saw a being so wondrous he *must* be God. But the verse has shown us that *every* being is wondrous in exactly this way — *you* are a word spoken by God, held in the present tense, dependent breath by breath. If wonder-at-existence made a thing divine, then *you* would be divine, and Adam would be divine, and so would the dust. The very thing that tempts us to deify the creation is the thing that, seen rightly, marks it *unmistakably as creation.* Your contingency is not a small fact about you. It is the loudest evidence in your life of the One who is *not* contingent.

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** there is an old practice the righteous kept — to pause at the edge of sleep and at the moment of waking, and to feel existence as a *return*. *"Praise be to the One who gave us life after He caused us to die."* You do not have to wait for resurrection to taste this. Every morning *is* a small *kun fa-yakūn* — a Word renewed, a being granted again that was never guaranteed. Tomorrow, when you first become aware that you are awake, before the day's noise begins, let one thought arrive ahead of all the others: *I did not keep myself in being through the night. He said "Be," and here I am.* Let your first breath be received, not assumed.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Watch how the two halves of this verse turn out to be one truth seen from two sides.

The **linguistic journey** moved from *ʿinda-llāh* (the vantage point that defines reality) to *ka-mathali Ādam* (the strange answered by the stranger) to *kun fa-yakūn* (the Word still being spoken). The **thematic journey** moved from *the heart's reflex to deify what astonishes it* to *your own existence as a word held in the present tense.*

They meet in a single point. **Najran's error and our daily forgetfulness are the same error.** Najran looked at a wondrous creature and mistook it for the Creator. We look at our own wondrous existence and forget the Creator entirely — we assume we simply *are.* One error worships the creation; the other ignores the One sustaining it. And both are cured by the same two words and the same shift of tense: see every wonder *ʿinda-llāh*, and hear behind every existing thing the *"Be"* that is *still — right now — saying "is."*

The verse defended the *servanthood* of Isa. But in doing so, it handed *you* your own. To be a creature is no insult — it is to be a word *on the lips of God.* The most honored thing Najran could imagine for Isa was that he be divine. The verse offers something the heart should find even more staggering: to be *spoken* by the Divine, and sustained by Him, in this very breath.

## Questions to Carry

1. What is the thing in your life right now that most astonishes you — and has your astonishment stopped *on it*, or passed *through* it to the One who said "Be" to it?
2. When you reason about God, how often do you reason from the *edge of your own knowledge* — *"I can't explain this, therefore…"* — the way Najran did? What would it mean to reason instead from *His* vantage point?
3. If your existence is a continuous *"and he is"* rather than a finished *"he was"* — what changes about how you receive *this* breath?
4. Where in your life are you living as though you are *self-standing* — owing your existence to no one?
5. The verse honors a creature by calling him exactly that — a creature. Why does the heart resist the honor of *servanthood*, and reach instead for the counterfeit of *self-sufficiency*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Measured not by how astonishing it looks to human eyes but by where it stands before Allah, the wonder of a being is never a claim to share His nature — it is the loudest evidence of His Word, "Be," which has never once stopped being spoken.**

## The Closing Invitation

You came to this verse perhaps expecting a piece of old theology — a settled argument about someone else's beliefs. Let it be more than that. Let *ʿinda-llāh* become a habit of the eyes: a reflex to ask, of everything that overwhelms you, *not* "what is this to me?" but "what is this before God?" And let *fa-yakūn* become a habit of the breath: a quiet awareness, returning through the day, that you are not coasting on an existence you secured for yourself, but being held — *now* — in the present tense of His Word. Not a duty laid on you. An invitation to *see* what is already true, and to live awake inside it.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, who said to dust "Be," and Adam was — and who says "Be" to me in every breath I have never earned — let me not stop my wonder on the things You have made, but carry it home to You. Open my eyes to see all things as they stand ʿinda-llāh, in Your sight; and let me receive my very existence the way it is given — moment by moment, as a word still on Your lips. Make me content, and grateful, and honored, to be Your servant. Āmīn.*
