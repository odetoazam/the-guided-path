# Before the Labels

## Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 65

> [ayah:3:65] يَـٰٓأَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ لِمَ تُحَآجُّونَ فِىٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ وَمَآ أُنزِلَتِ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةُ وَٱلْإِنجِيلُ إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِهِۦٓ ۚ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

*Yā ahla l-kitābi lima tuḥājjūna fī Ibrāhīma wa-mā unzilati t-Tawrātu wa-l-Injīlu illā min baʿdih, a-falā taʿqilūn.*

**"O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Ibrahim, when the Torah and the Gospel were not sent down until after him? Will you not, then, use your reason?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Quarrel Over a Grave

Let me put you in the room.

Madinah, the early years. A delegation has come — learned men, scholars of scripture, people who carry centuries of revelation in their memory and on their tongues. And there is an argument unfolding. Not a small one. It is an argument over ownership of a man who has been dead for thousands of years.

The Jews say: *Ibrahim was one of us. He was a Jew. We are his true heirs.*

The Christians say: *No. Ibrahim was one of us. He belonged to our path. We are his true children.*

And the Muslims, this new community gathered around the Prophet ﷺ, are being told the same thing the Arabs were told before Islam: *whoever you are, you are latecomers. The real Ibrahim belongs to the established religions. Pick a side. Take a number.*

This is the world this ayah falls into. Understand the emotional temperature of it. Ibrahim — Abraham — is not a minor figure here. He is **the** figure. He is the father of prophets, the one who smashed the idols, the one who built the House, the friend of God, *khalīlullāh*. To own Ibrahim is to own the franchise of monotheism itself. To be able to say "he was ours" is to claim the deed to the whole inheritance of guidance.

So everyone is pulling on him. Everyone wants the title.

And into this loud, ancient, self-assured argument, Allah does not send down a long polemic. He does not match their volume. He sends down a question. A short one. So short that one of its words has had a letter quietly removed from it — we will come back to that, because that missing letter is doing something extraordinary.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Allah is about to win this entire argument — an argument involving the most contested figure in the religious history of the Near East — not with a sermon, not with a miracle, not with an assertion of authority. He is going to win it with a **calendar**. With a single fact about *when*. And when you see how He does it, you will realize that the same trap these scholars fell into is a trap your own heart falls into, quietly, almost every day.

Stay with me. Let's open it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تُحَآجُّونَ — The Word for a Tug-of-War

Before we touch the word in the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work, because Arabic does something English cannot.

In English, the verb "to argue" is just "to argue." If I want to say two people are arguing *with each other*, *against each other*, locked in mutual struggle, I have to add words: "they argued with one another." The reciprocity, the back-and-forth, the *againstness* — I have to bolt it on.

Arabic builds that meaning **into the shape of the verb itself.**

Arabic verbs come in patterns, called forms. There is a basic form — the plain action. And then there is a form, called Form III, that is shaped specifically for actions you do *toward another party*, in a kind of reciprocal contest. It's the form of engagement-against. Think of a verb like *qātala* — not just "to kill," but "to do battle with," two armies locked together. Or *sābaqa* — "to race against." The very mold of the word implies an opponent, a second hand on the rope.

Now look at what Allah chose:

**تُحَآجُّونَ** — *tuḥājjūna.*

This is Form III. And its root is **ḥ-j-j** — the root that gives us *ḥujjah*, a proof, an argument, a piece of evidence. So the bare meaning is something like "to exchange proofs." But because Allah cast it in Form III, it is not calm exchange. It is **mutual combat with arguments.** It is two parties, each gripping their evidence, *pulling against each other.*

Let me give you the analogy, and I want you to hold it for the rest of this reflection, because it is load-bearing.

Picture a rope. Ibrahim is in the middle of it — not even a participant, just an object lying across the centerline. On one end, the Jews are pulling: *he's ours.* On the other end, the Christians are pulling: *no, he's ours.* Heels dug in. Faces red. Each side absolutely certain that if they pull hard enough, history will move and Ibrahim will slide to their side of the line.

That is what تُحَآجُّونَ paints. Form III is the tug-of-war. The word itself has *two ends and a strain in the middle.*

[PAUSE]

Now notice something, and let it sit with you.

A tug-of-war can only happen over something that is **between** the two sides. You cannot have a tug-of-war over something that is behind both of you. If a rock is sitting a hundred feet *behind* both teams, no amount of pulling does anything. The whole contest assumes the prize is in the contested middle.

And here is the quiet devastation of the ayah, which we will unfold fully in a moment: **Ibrahim was never in the middle.** He was behind both of them. Centuries behind. The tug-of-war these scholars are exhausting themselves with is a tug-of-war over a man who is standing in neither of their territories — because he stood *before* either territory was drawn on the map.

So the very first word of the question, تُحَآجُّونَ, is already an act of mercy. Allah is not just refuting them. He is showing them that the *strain in their arms is for nothing.* They are pulling on a rope tied to no one.

There is one more layer in this root, and I offer it as a contemplative resonance rather than a grammatical claim. The same three letters — ḥ-j-j — give us the word *Ḥajj*, the pilgrimage. And what is Ḥajj? It is the journey to the House that **Ibrahim built.** Sit with the irony for a moment. They are using the root of *ḥujjah*, arguing-over Ibrahim, when the same root in its other great meaning, *Ḥajj*, would have led them *to* Ibrahim — to his House, to his footprints, to his actual legacy. The energy spent arguing over him could have been a journey toward him. One letter-pattern, two destinies: contention, or pilgrimage.

What this single word reveals: **the dispute was not difficult to win because it was hard. It was impossible to win because it was empty.** The rope had no one at the center.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: لِمَ — The Question With a Letter Removed

Now to that missing letter I promised you.

The second word of the actual challenge is لِمَ — *lima* — "why?" or "for what reason?"

Here is the grammar, and it is documented, not speculative. This little word is built from two pieces: the preposition *li-* ("for") and the question-word *mā* ("what"). Put them together and you get *li-mā* — "for what?", "why?" That is its origin. *Li-mā.*

But look at how it appears in the Quran. The *mā* has lost its alif. It is not *li-mā*. It is *lima* — clipped, shortened, the long vowel cut off.

Al-Qurtubī pauses on exactly this in his commentary. He notes that the original is *li-mā*, and that the alif was dropped — *ḥudhifat al-alif* — to distinguish the **question** from the **statement.** When *mā* is asking, it gets shortened. When *mā* is merely describing, it keeps its full length. The Arabic tongue compresses the interrogative. The question is built lean.

Now — the grammar is settled. Let me offer you what this invites us to feel.

There is something fitting, almost physical, about a *question* being the most compressed word in the verse. Think about how a real, urgent question leaves your mouth. When you genuinely need to know *why* someone is doing something self-destructive — when you watch someone you love insist on something that is hurting them — the question does not come out long and ornate. It comes out short. *Why?* Just that. The urgency strips it down.

**لِمَ** is a question with no fat on it. No padding, no rhetorical buildup. Allah does not say "I would like to inquire as to the reasoning behind your engagement in this dispute." He says, in effect: *Why.* One breath. The word itself has been put on a diet to match the urgency of the asking.

And notice who is being asked. *Yā ahla l-kitāb* — "O People of the **Book**." Allah addresses them by the very thing they are proud of: that they are people of scripture, people of *reading*, people who possess the written record. And then He asks them a question whose answer is *written in their own books* — the dates, the sequence, the chronology. He is essentially saying: *You, of all people — you who hold the calendar in your hands — why are you arguing against your own pages?*

[PAUSE]

The compression of لِمَ does something else. A long question can be argued with. It gives you handholds, clauses to dispute, premises to attack. But *why?* — bare, single-syllabled — leaves you nowhere to hide. It does not present a thesis you can rebut. It simply turns the spotlight back on you and asks you to account for yourself.

What this word reveals: **the most piercing challenge is often the shortest one.** Allah did not need a paragraph. He needed one stripped-down word, aimed at people who should have known better, asking them to explain a behavior that cannot be explained.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: وَمَآ أُنزِلَتِ... إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِهِ — The Calendar as a Weapon

Now we arrive at the heart of the argument, and I want to teach you the structure before we feel its force, because the structure *is* the force.

There is a construction in Arabic — and in many languages — called restriction, or exclusivity. It is built from two pieces working together: a negation, and then an exception. *Nothing... except.* *Not... but.* In Arabic the pair is *mā... illā* — "not... except." When you wrap a statement in *mā... illā*, you are not just saying something is true. You are slamming every door except one. You are saying: *there is no other possibility. Full stop. Only this.*

Now read what Allah says:

**وَمَآ أُنزِلَتِ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةُ وَٱلْإِنجِيلُ إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِهِ**

*"And the Torah and the Gospel were **not** sent down **except** after him."*

There it is. *Mā... illā.* The locked construction. He is not saying "the Torah and Gospel came rather late." He is saying: **there is no version of history in which they came before him. The door to that possibility does not exist.**

And look at the verb: **أُنزِلَتِ** — *unzilat* — "were sent down." It is passive. Notice what the passive does here. It does not say "*you* received the Torah" or "Moses brought the Gospel." It removes the human carriers and points only at the *event* and its *timing.* The spotlight is not on who delivered the scripture. It is purely on **when it arrived.** And the answer is: *min baʿdihi* — "from after him."

Let me make the chronology concrete, the way the classical commentators did. Al-Baghawī, in his tafsīr, spells out the gap with actual numbers: between Ibrahim and Mūsā — to whom the Torah was given — there were roughly a thousand years. Between Mūsā and ʿĪsā — to whom the Gospel was given — roughly two thousand more. These are the traditional reckonings, and whatever the exact figures, the *direction* is undeniable and uncontested: Ibrahim comes first. Long, long first.

So here is the argument, laid bare. The Jews say "Ibrahim was a Jew." But "Jew," as a defined religious community with the Torah at its center, *begins with the Torah* — which came a millennium after Ibrahim died. The Christians say "Ibrahim was a Christian." But "Christian," as a defined community with the Gospel at its center, *begins with the Gospel* — which came two millennia after he died.

And the classical commentators press the chronology one notch tighter than you might expect. Both al-Muyassar and al-Jalalayn observe that the very *labels* — "Judaism" and "Christianity" as named, bounded religions — did not even arise with the scriptures. They use a precise verb: these things *ḥadathat*, they were newly *originated*, they came into being. Al-Jalalayn says it plainly: *only after the two scriptures were sent down did "Judaism" and "Christianity" originate.* So there is not one gap here but two, stacked. The scriptures came after Ibrahim — and the labels came after the scriptures. The thing they are fighting to pin on him is not just later than him; it is later than the very books they would use to define it. They are reaching back across two thresholds of time to brand a man who lived before either was crossed.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the logical impossibility now? It is the cleanest argument in the world.

**You cannot belong to a house that was built after you were buried.**

Ibrahim cannot be a follower of the Torah any more than your great-great-grandfather can be a fan of a song that was written last year. It is not that it's unlikely. It is that it is *temporally impossible.* The category did not exist yet. To call Ibrahim a Jew or a Christian is not a bold claim — it is an **anachronism**, a thing dragged backward out of its time and stuck onto a man who lived before it was conceivable.

This is the calendar as a weapon. Allah does not argue about Ibrahim's *beliefs* — He doesn't need to. He simply points at the **dates** and lets the contradiction collapse on its own. The proof is not theological. It is chronological. And chronology cannot be argued with, because it does not have opinions.

And now — *now* — go back and feel the first word again. **تُحَآجُّونَ.** The tug-of-war. Both teams straining over a rope, certain the prize is in the contested middle. And the calendar quietly informs them: *the prize was never in the middle. He was behind you both the whole time. You have been pulling against each other over empty rope.*

[PAUSE — major]

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have done together.

تُحَآجُّونَ showed us the *shape of the error* — a tug-of-war, mutual pulling over something that isn't between you.

لِمَ showed us the *tone of the correction* — short, urgent, aimed at people who hold the answer in their own hands.

وَمَآ أُنزِلَتِ... إِلَّا showed us the *mechanism of the truth* — a locked door of chronology that no argument can pick.

But none of this is really about two ancient delegations and their genealogical quarrel. If it were, this would be a history lesson, and history lessons do not get sent down from above the seven heavens to be recited until the end of time. So let us now ask what this reveals not just about a fight over Ibrahim — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Things We Drag Backward Onto the Great

Let me start where you live, far from any seventh-century delegation.

You have a hero. Everyone does. Maybe it's a historical figure — a leader, a thinker, a saint. Maybe it's a grandparent who died before you were old enough to really know them. And here is something the human heart does almost automatically: it **recruits** them. It enlists them onto its own team.

Watch how it happens in our own time. A figure from centuries ago dies, and then every modern movement claims him. "He would have voted for us." "She would have supported our cause." "If he were alive today, he'd be on my side of this exact argument." We take a person who lived before our categories existed and we dress them in our colors. We make the dead endorse the living. We pull the great ones backward through time and stand them behind our particular banner.

Why do we do this? Because **borrowed authority feels like our own.** If Ibrahim is ours, then we are right by inheritance. We don't have to win the argument on its merits — we win it by lineage. "We are the heirs. We hold the deed." It is the laziest and most seductive form of being right: not because your life reflects the truth, but because you've claimed the property rights to someone who did.

This is exactly what the People of the Book were doing. Al-Tabari describes the quarrel with a telling word: each faction claimed that Ibrahim *followed the creed of their own sect* — that he held to the *niḥlah*, the particular party-religion, that they themselves held. And al-Tabari is careful to register two distinct moves in the divine response, not one. First, Allah *found fault with them* — *ʿābahum* — for the very act of making such a claim; the claiming itself is treated as blameworthy, not merely as an honest mistake about a date. And then, second, He *pointed to its self-contradiction* — *dalla ʿalā munāqaḍatihi*. That layering matters. It would have been enough to show the claim was false. But Allah first marks it as *culpable* — because to drag a prophet into your faction is not a neutral error of chronology; it is an act of appropriation, and appropriation is a thing the heart chooses. That is the heart of it. The claim does not need to be argued down from the outside. It refutes itself from the inside, the moment you lay it beside the dates. You are claiming a man for a sect that did not exist while he lived. The contradiction is not between you and Allah; it is between you and *you*.

And this is what Allah's calendar exposes. He is saying: *you have inverted the entire relationship.* You think Ibrahim derives his honor from belonging to your religion. But the truth is the reverse — **your religion, in whatever truth it once held, derives from the path Ibrahim was already walking.** He is not the branch hanging off your tree. He is closer to the root. You don't get to claim him. If anything, you are accountable to him.

[PAUSE]

Now turn the mirror, gently, toward yourself — because this is not only about claiming dead heroes.

We do the same thing with God.

Listen closely, because this is subtle. We take the partisan thing — our group, our madhhab, our community, our particular flavor of practice, the specific shape our religiosity happens to have taken — and we drag it backward and stamp it onto the Eternal. We say, in effect, "God belongs to my camp." We make the Lord of all the worlds into a member of our faction. We assume that our particular way of being religious is the original, the universal, the timeless — when often it is a latecomer, a local development, a Torah-and-Gospel that arrived long after the simple, unlabeled submission it claims to represent.

And the antidote is the same antidote in the ayah: **chronology.** *Remember what came first.* Before the labels, before the factions, before "our group" and "their group," there was a man standing alone in the dark, looking at a star, then the moon, then the sun, and saying *I do not love things that set — I have turned my face to the One who made them.* That came **first.** That pure, label-less turning toward God — *that* is the root. Everything else is *min baʿdihi* — after him, after it, downstream.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. The next time you feel the warm certainty of *being on the right team* — the next time your rightness comes from your *belonging* rather than your *submission* — stop and ask the chronological question. *Did I earn this, or did I inherit a label? Am I walking Ibrahim's path, or just claiming his name?* Because Allah is about to say, two ayahs from now, that Ibrahim "was not of the idolaters" — and the whole point is that the path is something you *walk*, not a card you *hold.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ — When the Mind Is Not the Problem

The ayah ends on a word I have been saving.

**أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ** — *a-falā taʿqilūn* — "Will you not, then, use your reason?" The root is *ʿ-q-l*, the root of *ʿaql* — the intellect, the reasoning mind. Originally, the word *ʿaql* carried the image of *binding* — the same root sense as the rope you use to tie a camel so it doesn't wander off. The intellect is the faculty that *ties you down* to reality, that keeps you from drifting into nonsense.

Notice how the verse ends. It does not end with "will you not *believe*?" It does not end with "will you not *submit*?" It ends with **"will you not use your reason?"** Allah closes a religious argument by appealing not to faith, not to authority, not to fear — but to the *mind.* He treats the chronological proof as something the intellect, if it were simply *used*, would have to accept.

This is worth dwelling on, because there is a tired assumption — inside and outside the Muslim community — that faith and reason are enemies, that religion asks you to switch off your mind at the door. And here is the Quran, in the middle of its most charged interfaith debate, doing the *opposite.* It lays out a clean factual argument and then asks: *won't you think?* It assumes that the truth and the functioning intellect are on the **same side.**

And notice precisely *what* the intellect is being asked to perceive. Al-Jalalayn supplies the unspoken object of the verb: *a-falā taʿqilūn* — will you not reason, and so grasp the *buṭlān*, the sheer *invalidity*, of your own claim? The mind is not being asked to generate a new truth out of nothing. It is being asked to register a collapse that has already happened — to see that the position has fallen apart under the weight of a date. Reasoning here is not invention; it is honest acknowledgment of what the facts have already settled.

[PAUSE]

But now here is the deeper, more unsettling thing. Sit with it.

These were the **People of the Book.** Scholars. Brilliant men. Their *minds worked perfectly.* They could parse grammar, debate theology, recite genealogies going back centuries. The chronology of the Torah and the Gospel was *literally written in the scriptures they had memorized.* They had every fact they needed.

So why did they get it wrong?

Because the obstacle was never the intellect. **The obstacle was the heart.**

This is the most important thing in the ayah, and it is the most relevant thing to your life. *A-falā taʿqilūn* is not asking them to *acquire* information they lack. It is asking them to *use* the intellect they are *suppressing.* They have the calendar. They've read it a thousand times. But identity — pride, belonging, the warm certainty of "we are the chosen heirs" — has reached in and **switched the reasoning off.** They are not unable to see. They are *unwilling* to see, and the unwillingness has disguised itself as a position.

We have a name for this now. We call it **motivated reasoning.** When a fact threatens your identity, your mind does not coldly evaluate it. It *defends* you. It generates objections, finds reasons to doubt, suddenly discovers that the question is "more complicated than it seems." The intellect — that camel-rope meant to tie you to reality — gets quietly repurposed into a lawyer hired to defend whatever you already wanted to believe. The smartest people are often the *best* at this, because the more powerful your mind, the better the defense attorney it makes.

That is why Allah says *a-falā taʿqilūn* — *will you not USE it?* The implication is that the faculty is sitting right there, fully functional, and they have simply taken it off the case the moment it threatened their belonging. The tragedy is not stupidity. The tragedy is a working mind held hostage by an unwilling heart.

[PAUSE]

So bring it home. The question Allah asks the People of the Book is the question He is asking *you*, every time a truth arrives that costs you something.

When the Qurʾan presents you with something clear — a command, a warning, an account you'd rather not face — notice the move your mind makes. Does it *receive* the truth, or does it immediately start *building a defense*? Does it ask "is this true?" or does it ask "how can I get around this?" The presence of a quick, clever objection is not always a sign of intelligence. Sometimes it is the sound of the heart hiring a lawyer.

The practical application is almost frighteningly simple: **the next time you feel your mind rushing to dismiss something the Qurʾan says, pause and ask — am I reasoning, or am I defending?** Am I using my ʿaql to find the truth, or to protect my comfort? Because the People of the Book had every fact. What they lacked was the willingness to let the fact win. And Allah, with infinite gentleness and infinite seriousness, simply asked them: *won't you think?*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the whole ayah is really one motion, seen from two directions.

From the **linguistic** side: a tug-of-war (تُحَآجُّونَ) over a man who was never between the two teams, challenged by a stripped-down *why* (لِمَ), refuted by a locked door of chronology (وَمَآ أُنزِلَتِ... إِلَّا), and closed with an appeal to the mind (أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ).

From the **human** side: we drag the great ones backward onto our team to borrow their authority — and we do the same with God — until a single fact about *what came first* collapses the whole project; and the only reason we don't see it is that our heart has quietly switched our reasoning off.

They are the same insight. **The error is putting your label before the truth, and then using your mind to defend the label instead of to find the truth.** The cure is chronology of the heart: *remember what came first.* Pure submission came before every faction. Reality came before your defense of it. God came before your camp.

### The Architecture

And see how perfectly this ayah is engineered as a doorway. It does not stand alone. It ends with *a-falā taʿqilūn* — "won't you think?" — and the verse that immediately follows, 3:66, presses the very nerve it just touched. As Ibn Kathir lays out the sequence, Allah turns and says, in effect: *here you are, you have argued about things of which you do have some knowledge — so why now argue about something of which you have no knowledge at all? Allah knows, and you do not.* Read it as the second turn of the screw. In 3:65 He shows that the dates make their claim impossible; in 3:66 He shows that they are reaching past the edge of their own knowledge entirely — disputing about a man and an age they were never present for, against the One who was. The challenge to *reason* in 65 becomes a challenge to admit the *limits* of reason in 66: there is a difference between what you can know and what only Allah knows, and wisdom is knowing which is which.

And then, in 3:67, comes the answer to the thinking it all demanded:

*"Ibrahim was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a ḥanīf, a muslim — and he was not of the idolaters."*

3:65 clears the ground by demolishing the false claims. 3:66 forbids the overreach that built them. 3:67 raises on the cleared ground the true identity: not a member of any later faction, but a man of *pure, upright, label-less submission.* The chronology in our ayah was never just a debating trick. It was clearing away the dust of every *min baʿdihi* — everything that came "after" — so that the original thing, the thing that came **first**, could finally be seen.

### Questions to Carry

1. Who are the great ones — heroes, ancestors, scholars — that I have quietly recruited onto *my* team to borrow their authority? Am I walking their path, or just holding their name?
2. Where does my rightness come from: from my *submission*, or from my *belonging* to a group?
3. When the Qurʾan tells me something that costs me, does my mind *receive* it or *defend against* it? How would I even tell the difference?
4. What in my religious life is *min baʿdihi* — a latecomer I have mistaken for the original — and what is truly first?
5. Is my intellect a rope tying me to reality, or a lawyer I have hired to defend my comfort?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You cannot belong to a house built after you were buried — and the only reason the brilliant could not see this was that their hearts had taken their minds off the case.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah does not ask you to be less intelligent. It asks you to let your intelligence *finish its job* — to follow the fact all the way to where it leads, even when it leads away from your team and toward the bare, first, unlabeled truth. The invitation is to stop pulling on the rope, look behind you, and notice that the One you are arguing *about* has been standing *before* you the whole time. Not to win the argument. To walk the path.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who sent down the Torah and the Gospel and the Qurʾan, each in its time — let me not drag my labels backward and call them eternal. Free my mind to follow Your truth even when it costs my pride, and let my certainty come not from the team I belong to, but from the submission I actually live. Make me, like Your friend Ibrahim, a ḥanīf turned wholly toward You — before, and beneath, every name. Āmīn.
