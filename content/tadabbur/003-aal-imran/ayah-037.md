# The Provision That Arrives Before You Ask
### A Reflection on Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 37

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Prayer Answered in a Way No One Expected

Let me read it to you first. Sit with the sound before the meaning.

> **فَتَقَبَّلَهَا رَبُّهَا بِقَبُولٍ حَسَنٍ وَأَنبَتَهَا نَبَاتًا حَسَنًا وَكَفَّلَهَا زَكَرِيَّا**
>
> *Fataqabbalahā rabbuhā biqabūlin ḥasanin wa-anbatahā nabātan ḥasanan wa-kaffalahā Zakariyyā.*
>
> **"So her Lord accepted her with a beautiful acceptance, and made her grow in a beautiful growing, and placed her in the care of Zakariya."**

And then:

> **كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا ٱلْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِندَهَا رِزْقًا**
>
> *Kullamā dakhala ʿalayhā Zakariyyā l-miḥrāba wajada ʿindahā rizqā.*
>
> **"Whenever Zakariya entered upon her in the prayer-chamber, he found provision with her."**

Now let me rebuild the world this verse fell into.

A woman — the wife of ʿImran — is pregnant. Before the child is even born, she makes a vow. She lifts her hands and dedicates what is in her womb to the service of the sanctuary, *muḥarraran* — set free for God alone, belonging to no worldly use. In her culture, this was a vow for a son. The temple servants were men. The sanctuary's work was men's work. She is, in effect, promising God a boy.

And then she gives birth to a girl.

Listen to the verse just before ours: *"And when she delivered her, she said, 'My Lord, I have delivered a female' — and Allah knew best what she had delivered — 'and the male is not like the female.'"* You can hear the disappointment folded into surrender. Her plan has collapsed. The vessel she prepared for God doesn't fit the shape she imagined. She names the baby Maryam and asks God to protect her and her offspring from the rejected one.

This is the climate our verse enters. A vow made in hope. An outcome that looks, by every human measure, like the prayer was *not* answered the way it was meant to be. A mother holding a daughter she didn't plan for, whispering, *please, protect her anyway.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the hook. Watch what God does with a prayer that seemed to miss.

The mother offered a servant for the sanctuary. God did not simply *accept* the offering and file it away. Read what comes next, and notice that every single verb in the first half of this verse has **God as its subject and Maryam as its object.** She accepted. She grew her. She sheltered her. She fed her — through a stunned old prophet who keeps walking into her chamber and finding food that no one delivered.

The mother gave God a girl she thought was the wrong gift.

God turned that girl into the woman whose name is the only woman's name in the entire Quran, the mother of a prophet, the sign for all the worlds.

What happened in the space between the disappointed vow and the chosen woman? That is what this verse is about. Let's go slowly enough to actually see it.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Verb That Reaches Out — *Taqabbala*

### First, a concept from your own life

Imagine you give someone a gift. There are two ways they can take it.

The first: they take it, say "thank you," and set it on a table. *Received.* Transaction complete. This is the simple verb — in Arabic, *qabila*, "to accept."

The second: their face changes. They turn the gift over in their hands. They ask you about it. They use it, display it, come back to it. They don't just *accept* the gift — they *receive it toward themselves*, they lean into it, they make a relationship out of it. The acceptance is active, eager, engaged.

Arabic has a way of marking that second kind of acceptance. It belongs to a verb pattern — grammarians call it **Form V**, *tafaʿʿala* — and one of its core functions is to show the subject taking an action *in toward himself*, with effort and engagement, almost personally absorbing it.

Now look at the word God chose.

### Opening the word

The verse does not say *qabilahā rabbuhā* — "her Lord accepted her." It says:

> **فَتَقَبَّلَهَا رَبُّهَا** — *fataqabbalahā rabbuhā*

**Taqabbala.** Form V. The eager, engaged, reaching-toward acceptance.

Let's sit with this. The mother offered Maryam as a *muḥarrar*, a dedicated servant. By the social logic of the time, that offering was, in a sense, defective — a girl where a boy was expected. The kind of gift a person might politely set on the table.

God does not set her on the table.

The form of the verb tells you He *reached for her.* He took her in toward Himself. This is not passive permission; it is active embrace.

### Deeper — the doubling that follows

Now notice what God does immediately after the verb. He does something Arabic reserves for emphasis. He follows the verb of accepting with the *noun* of accepting:

> **بِقَبُولٍ حَسَنٍ** — *biqabūlin ḥasanin* — "with a beautiful acceptance"

In Arabic this construction — placing a verbal noun after its own verb — is called the **maf'ūl muṭlaq**, the absolute object, or the cognate accusative. It is the language doubling back on itself to remove all doubt about intensity. We do something like it in English when we say, "She slept a deep sleep," or "He laughed a real laugh." The repeated word isn't redundant — it's the language pressing down on the meaning.

So God did not merely accept-her. He **accepted-her with an acceptance** — and then qualified even that with *ḥasan*, beautiful, excellent, good.

Here is what's remarkable, and the early grammarians noticed it. The verb is Form V — *taqabbala*, the reaching, engaged form. But the noun He pairs with it, *qabūl*, is the simpler Form I masdar, from *qabila*. Al-Tabari pauses on exactly this. The verbal noun, he observes, has been *brought out on a form other than that of its own verb* — had it simply matched the verb, the verse would have read *fataqabbalahā... taqabbulan ḥasanan.* Instead it reaches back to the plainer root-noun, *qabūl* — and, al-Tabari notes, this is something the Arabs do often, slipping the lighter masdar in where the heavier one would stand. The effect is precise: the *act* is the engaged, reaching Form V, while the *thing accomplished* is named with the oldest, simplest word for acceptance there is. A rare construction for a rare acceptance.

[PAUSE]

Put the layers together. A verb that means He leaned in toward her. A noun that doubles down on the act. An adjective that calls it beautiful. And a sound — *taqabbala biqabūlin* — that literally repeats the root *q-b-l* three times in a row, so your ear hears acceptance, acceptance, acceptance, like a door opening and opening and opening.

What does this one word reveal? **That God's acceptance is never lukewarm.** When He receives a soul that turned to Him, He does not merely permit it entry. He reaches. The mother feared her gift was diminished. The grammar tells us it was embraced more completely than a gift given perfectly.

## Element Two: The Verb of the Gardener — *Anbatahā Nabātan*

### A concept first: how we talk about growing children

Think about how you describe a child growing up. We say "she was raised." "He was brought up." Language about children tends to be language about *people* — nurturing, teaching, parenting.

But there is another vocabulary entirely. It belongs to the soil. We speak of *cultivating*, *planting*, *nurturing a seedling*, *putting down roots*, *flourishing.* This is the language of the farmer, the gardener — of things that grow from the ground up, slowly, by a life that is in them but not from them. A gardener does not *manufacture* a plant. He plants a seed and the *growth* comes from somewhere beyond his hand.

Hold that distinction. God is about to describe the raising of a human girl — and He reaches not for the vocabulary of parenting, but for the vocabulary of the garden.

### Opening the word

> **وَأَنبَتَهَا نَبَاتًا حَسَنًا** — *wa-anbatahā nabātan ḥasanan*
>
> "and made her grow in a beautiful growing."

The root here is *n-b-t* — and across the Quran, this root is the root of **vegetation.** It is what God says about rain hitting dead earth and the land bursting into green. It is the word for crops, for herbs, for the seed that splits and pushes upward. *Anbata* — Form IV, the causative — means literally *to cause to sprout, to make grow as a plant grows.*

God did not say *rabbāhā*, "He raised her" as a parent raises a child. He said *anbatahā* — **"He caused her to grow as the earth causes a plant to grow."**

### Why this image, and not another?

This is the heart of how the Quran teaches. It reaches for something you can *see* — a plant breaking the soil, reaching toward light, unfurling leaf by leaf — to point you toward something you *cannot* see: the secret, interior cultivation of a human soul.

Stay with me, because the image is doing precise work.

Consider what a plant *is.* A plant cannot grow itself. It cannot decide to be taller. The life within it is a gift; the conditions around it — water, light, soil — are arranged by another. And yet the growth is *real growth, from the inside out.* It is not assembled like a machine. It is *unfolded* like a living thing.

So when God says He *anbatahā* — made her sprout — He is telling you that Maryam's excellence was **cultivated, not constructed.** It came up from within her by His arrangement of everything around her. And notice *what* the commentators say was grown. Al-Jalalayn glosses the "beautiful growing" first as **character** — *anshaʾahā bi-khuluqin ḥasan*, "He brought her up with a beautiful disposition" — before adding the famous image that she grew in a single day as much as an ordinary child grows in a year. Read that order carefully: the first fruit of God's cultivation was not height or speed but *khuluq*, the inward shape of the soul. Whether you take the day-for-a-year detail literally or as a picture of accelerated, blessed flourishing, the point of the *word* stands on its own: her growth was God's gardening, and what He grew first was her character.

And then — the cognate accusative again. **Nabātan ḥasanan.** "A beautiful growing." Just as He doubled the acceptance, He doubles the growth. He didn't merely grow her; He grew her *a growing*, and qualified it as *ḥasan* — the very same word He used for the acceptance.

Do you hear the rhyme God is building?

- *Biqabūlin **ḥasan**in* — a beautiful acceptance
- *Nabātan **ḥasan**an* — a beautiful growing

[PAUSE]

The same beauty that received her is the beauty that grew her. The reception and the cultivation are one continuous act of divine attention. **He did not accept her and then leave her to grow on her own.** The acceptance *became* the cultivation. To be received by God beautifully is to be grown by God beautifully.

What does this word reveal? **That when God takes charge of a soul, He does not install it — He cultivates it.** Growth under God's care is organic, patient, alive, and it comes up from the inside. You are not a project He builds. You are a seed He plants.

## Element Three: The Verb of Finding — *Wajada*, and the Provision With No Name

### A concept: the difference between bringing and finding

There are two very different sentences:

"He brought food to the room."
"He found food in the room."

The first one has an obvious chain of cause. Someone carried it. You know where it came from. The second sentence has a *gap* in it — a mystery. To *find* something is to encounter it already there, arriving by a path you did not witness. Finding implies an unseen giver.

Now watch which verb God uses.

### Opening the word

> **كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا ٱلْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِندَهَا رِزْقًا**
>
> "Whenever Zakariya entered upon her in the prayer-chamber, **he found** provision with her."

The verb is **wajada** — *he found.* Not *she received.* Not *someone brought.* Not *he gave her.* He **found** it already there. Al-Muyassar fills in the texture of that finding: what Zakariya kept encountering was *rizqan haniʾan muʿaddan* — provision *wholesome and already laid out*, set ready by no hand he could name. The food was not in the middle of arriving; it was finished, prepared, waiting. Found, never fetched.

And before the verb, one small word carries enormous weight: **kullamā** — *"every single time."* Not once. Not occasionally. *Whenever.* Every time the old prophet pushed open the door of that chamber, the provision was already waiting. The miracle was not a single event he could explain away. It was a standing condition of her life.

### The word He left undefined

Now read the word for the provision: **rizqan.** "Provision." And notice — it is **indefinite.** Not *the* provision, not *her* provision, not *the fruit* or *the bread.* Just *rizqan* — "some provision," unnamed, unspecified.

This is an absence we must read as carefully as any presence. God *could* have told us exactly what it was. The classical commentators fill the gap with their narrations — al-Qurtubi and others transmit that it was the fruit of summer in winter and the fruit of winter in summer, food out of its season, food that could not have come by any ordinary supply line. That tradition is beautiful and it makes the point vivid: this was provision that broke the rules of cause and effect.

But God Himself left the noun *open.* And the openness is the message.

[PAUSE]

Why leave it unnamed? Because the moment you name *what* it was, your mind starts working on the *how.* "Ah, fruit — so who grew the fruit, who carried it, which servant..." The named object invites the human search for a mechanism. The *unnamed* object refuses you that exit. It leaves you standing in the doorway with old Zakariya, staring at provision that has no supply chain you can trace.

And so Zakariya asks the only question a rational man can ask:

> **يَٰمَرْيَمُ أَنَّىٰ لَكِ هَٰذَا** — *"O Maryam, from where does this come to you?"*

The word *annā* is not the simple "where" of geography. It is the *annā* of astonishment — "*however* did this come to you? By what possible route?" It is the question of a man whose entire framework of cause and effect has just hit a wall.

### Her answer — and what she does *not* say

Here is the verse's quiet summit. Maryam answers:

> **هُوَ مِنْ عِندِ ٱللَّهِ** — *"It is from the presence of Allah."*

Stay with what she does **not** say. She does not say *how.* She does not narrate a mechanism. She does not say "an angel brought it" or "it appeared at dawn." She is asked a *how* question — *annā* — and she answers with a *who* and a *where-from*: **min ʿindillāh**, from the *presence* of God.

That phrase — *min ʿind* — means "from the direct presence of," from God's own side, without intermediary. Where Zakariya's mind is searching the horizontal plane — *which earthly path?* — Maryam simply points up. She redirects the entire question from *mechanism* to *Source.*

And then the verse closes with the principle that makes sense of everything:

> **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَرْزُقُ مَن يَشَآءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ**
>
> "Indeed, Allah provides for whom He wills **without reckoning.**"

### *Bighayri ḥisāb* — without reckoning

Hold this last phrase in your hand. *Ḥisāb* is from the root of *counting, accounting, calculation* — the ledger, the math, the careful tally of what goes out against what comes in. *Bighayri ḥisāb* — **"without an account."**

It carries at least three layers at once, and the commentators hold them together rather than choosing:

It means **without limit** — provision so vast it isn't being counted.

It means **without the recipient being held to account** — given freely, not as wages earned, not as a debt that must be repaid.

And it means **without your calculation** — outside the math *you* would do. You add up your causes: my job, my effort, my supply lines. God's giving is *bighayri ḥisāb* — it does not run through your arithmetic.

[PAUSE]

What do these words reveal together? **That God's provision arrives by finding, not by fetching.** It can be *waiting* for you before you ever traced a path to it. And the right response to it is not to solve the mechanism, but to name the Source.

### Bridge

So the construction has shown us three things. A God who *reaches* to accept (*taqabbala*). A God who *cultivates* what He accepts (*anbata*). A God who *provisions* what He cultivates from a source beyond all accounting (*rizqan... bighayri ḥisāb*).

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about your own life.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Provision That Is Already in the Room

Let me start where you actually live.

You lie awake doing the math. You know this math. It is the ledger of *how will this be enough.* The income against the bills. The hours against the deadline. The energy you have against the people who need you. You run the numbers, and the numbers don't close, and the anxiety in your chest is precisely the gap between what's coming in and what's going out. That gap has a name. The Quran just named it. It is *ḥisāb* — reckoning, accounting, the tally.

Almost all of our fear about the future is a fear about the ledger. *It won't add up.*

Now walk into Maryam's chamber.

Here is a young woman, alone, withdrawn in a prayer-niche, with no job, no field, no trade, no visible means. By every column of the human ledger, her provision *should not add up.* And yet — *kullamā*, every single time — the provision is already there. Not because she solved the math. The math was never the source. **The provision was never coming through the ledger at all.**

Let the ayah illuminate your sleepless night. Your anxiety assumes that what reaches you must come *through* your accounting — through the channels you can see and control. So when the channels look too thin, you panic. But the verse is telling you that the One who provides does so *bighayri ḥisāb* — He is not bound by your ledger. He can put provision *in the room* by a door you didn't know existed.

This does not mean stop working. Maryam was in the *miḥrāb* — the place of worship and striving. Zakariya farmed and prayed and served. The point is not that effort is useless. The point is **where you locate the source.** Effort is the door you walk through. It is not the thing that fills the room.

[PAUSE]

Now go one layer deeper, because there is a paradox here we should not rush past.

Notice *who* asks the question. Zakariya. He is a *prophet of God.* A man whose entire life is built on knowing that God provides. And *he* is the one standing in the doorway, astonished, asking *"annā laki hādhā — however did this come to you?"* While *Maryam*, the young woman with no title, is the one giving the calm answer: *min ʿindillāh.*

Why does God let the prophet be the one who's stunned?

Because knowing the doctrine and *living inside* the wonder are not the same thing. Zakariya *knew* God was the Provider. But standing in front of provision that had no supply chain — even he was struck silent by it. And what does that encounter do to him? Read the very next verse: *"There, Zakariya called upon his Lord"* — *hunālika* — *right there, in that moment, he prayed* for a child of his own, though he was old and his wife barren. Maryam's unreckoned provision *re-ignited his own impossible hope.*

This is the quiet teaching: **when you witness God provide for someone else outside the ledger, it should not make you envious — it should make you pray bigger.** Zakariya saw out-of-season fruit and immediately asked God for an out-of-season child. He let someone else's miracle expand his own sense of what God could do.

So tomorrow, when you see a person provided for in a way that makes no earthly sense — the family that always seems carried, the friend whose path keeps opening — let it do to you what it did to Zakariya. Not *"why them?"* But *"if God provides like that... then let me ask Him for the thing I had given up on."*

**The practical change:** the next time you catch yourself running the ledger at 2 a.m., stop and finish the sentence Maryam started. Not *"how will this add up?"* but *"it is from the presence of Allah — and He provides bighayri ḥisāb."* Then ask Him for the thing you stopped asking for.

## Theme Two: To Be Planted Is to Be Loved Slowly

Here is something most of us quietly believe and never say out loud: that God's love, if we have it, should feel like *rescue.* Sudden. Dramatic. A door flung open, a problem solved, a life changed in a night. We are waiting for the lightning.

But look again at what God *did* with the soul He loved in this verse.

He *anbatahā* — He **planted** her.

And planting is the opposite of lightning. Planting is the slowest love there is.

Think about what it means to be a seed in the ground. For a long time, nothing visible happens. There is no green, no sign, no reassurance. From the outside it looks like burial. The seed in the dark soil has no way of knowing it is being grown — it only knows it is covered, hidden, alone in the dark. And yet *that* is exactly the condition of growth. The darkness is not the absence of care. The darkness is the *site* of cultivation.

Sit with that, because so many of us are living in a season that *feels* like burial. The years where nothing visible is happening. Where the prayers seem unanswered, the life seems stalled, and from the outside — and even from the inside — it looks like you've been put away in the dark and forgotten.

The verse says: *that may be exactly when you are being grown.*

God did not describe Maryam's formation as an event. He described it as *nabāt* — a process, organic, unhurried, mostly invisible. The chosen woman of all the worlds was not assembled overnight. She was *cultivated.* Day after day in the *miḥrāb*, in a routine that looked, to any observer, like nothing was happening. And in that "nothing," God was growing the soul that would carry a prophet.

[PAUSE]

Now let me hold up the paradox at the center of the plant image, because it will set you free.

A plant grows *from the inside* — and yet it cannot grow *by itself.* Both are true at once. The life is genuinely *in* the seed; the growth is genuinely *its own.* And yet every condition that allows that growth — the water, the warmth, the light, the breaking-open of the shell — comes from *outside* the seed, arranged by a hand the seed never sees.

This is the exact relationship between you and your Lord.

Your growth is *real.* It is *yours.* Your worship, your patience, your turning back to Him again and again — these are genuine acts that genuinely rise from within you. You are not a puppet. *And yet* none of it would unfold without Him arranging the soil of your life — the very capacity to turn to Him, the *tawfīq*, the conditions, the rain. The same verse that says *He grew her* sits beside her own active devotion in the *miḥrāb.* Both. At once. **You strive like a seed strives upward, and He grows you like only the Gardener can.**

So the question is not "do I grow myself or does God grow me?" The plant doesn't ask that. The plant just keeps its roots in the soil and its face to the light, and the growth happens by a power beyond it.

**The practical change:** the next time you are in a season that feels like burial — a stalled year, a long winter of unanswered prayer — stop reading it as abandonment. Ask instead: *what is being grown in this dark?* And then do the only thing a planted thing can do: stay rooted where God put you (your salah, your dhikr, your small daily *miḥrāb*) and keep your face toward the light. You will not see the growth while it happens. Seeds never do. But the verse is your evidence that the dark is where the chosen are cultivated.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: One Act in Four Verbs

Now that we've done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole.

The first half of this verse is **four divine verbs, in a chain, each one the consequence of the last** — and Maryam is the object of every single one:

1. **فَتَقَبَّلَهَا** — He *accepted* her (the reaching, Form V embrace)
2. **وَأَنبَتَهَا** — He *grew* her (the gardener's cultivation)
3. **وَكَفَّلَهَا** — He *sheltered* her (placed her in Zakariya's care — and the form of the verb, *kaffala*, tells you *God* arranged the guardian; in the well-known reading with the doubled letter, it is God who *caused* Zakariya to take charge). Al-Jalalayn preserves *how* it happened, and it is worth knowing: when Maryam's mother brought the dedicated child to the temple-keepers, the *aḥbār* of Bayt al-Maqdis, they did not shrug her off — they *competed* over her, for she was the daughter of their imam. Zakariya pressed his own claim, since his wife was her maternal aunt; the others insisted on settling it by lot. So they went out — twenty-nine of them — to the river Jordan and cast their pens into the current, and the lot fell to Zakariya. Even the lottery, the verse insists, was God placing her exactly where He willed. What looked like chance was *kaffala* — His arrangement wearing the clothing of a coincidence.
4. **وَجَدَ ... رِزْقًا** — He *provided* for her (the provision found, not fetched)

Acceptance → cultivation → shelter → provision. This is not a list. It is a *sequence of care* — the complete arc of how God takes responsibility for a soul that was entrusted to Him. He receives it, He grows it, He shelters it, He feeds it. Nothing is left out. The mother handed over her child at the start of the verse; by the end, God has personally done everything a guardian could do, and more than any guardian could.

And notice the hinge in the middle. The provision (*rizq*) and the growth (*nabāt*) rhyme by design. **What grows a plant?** Provision — water, nourishment, sustenance from outside itself. So *anbatahā* (He grew her) and *yarzuqu* (He provides) are the same act seen twice: the One who *grows* you is the One who *feeds* you, and He feeds *bighayri ḥisāb.*

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed you a God who **reaches** to accept, **cultivates** rather than constructs, and **provisions** by a path you cannot trace. The themes showed you that this is the answer to your two deepest fears: the fear that *it won't add up* (and He provides without reckoning) and the fear that *I've been forgotten in the dark* (and the dark is where He plants the ones He's growing).

A mother offered God a gift she thought was diminished. The verse is the record of what God does with everything we surrender to Him while fearing it isn't enough. **He doesn't just accept it. He grows it into something neither we nor anyone else could have imagined.**

## Questions to Carry

1. What have I surrendered to God while secretly believing it was the "wrong gift" — and what might He be growing it into?
2. When I run my 2 a.m. ledger, where am I locating the source of my provision — in the channels I can see, or in the One who can put provision *in the room*?
3. Am I in a season that feels like burial? What if it is cultivation?
4. When I see God provide for someone else outside all reckoning, does it make me envious — or does it, like Zakariya, make me pray bigger?
5. Maryam answered a *how* question with a *Who* answer. In my own anxieties, am I demanding to know the mechanism when I have been given the Source?

## The One-Sentence Distillation

When God receives a soul, He does not merely accept it — He reaches for it, plants it, shelters it, and feeds it from a presence beyond all reckoning; so the provision you are losing sleep over may already be waiting in the room.

## The Closing Invitation

Let something shift. Not a new burden — a lighter one. You have been carrying the weight of the ledger, the math that won't close, the fear that the dark season means you've been left behind. This verse invites you to set that weight down, not by pretending the numbers don't matter, but by remembering they were never the source. Stay in your *miḥrāb*. Keep your roots in the soil and your face to the light. And when the provision appears by a door you didn't expect, do what Maryam did: don't explain it — *name its Source*, and then ask Him for the thing you'd given up on.

## A Du'a from These Words

*O Allah, You who accepted Maryam with a beautiful acceptance — receive what we surrender to You, even the gifts we fear are not enough. You who grew her in a beautiful growing — cultivate us in the seasons that feel like burial, and let our roots hold while You do the unseen work. And You who provide for whom You will without reckoning — put our provision in the room before we ask, and let our hearts answer every fear the way she answered: it is from the presence of Allah.* Āmīn.
