## Introduction

*فَإِن تَوَلَّوْاْ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلِيمُۢ بِٱلْمُفْسِدِينَ*

*Fa-in tawallaw fa-inna Allāha ʿAlīmun bil-mufsidīn*

*"And if they turn away — then indeed, Allah is All-Knowing of the corrupters."*

— Surah Aal-Imran, 3:63

---

### Rebuilding the World

The year is approximately 10 AH. A delegation from Najran has arrived in Madinah — a significant Christian city in southern Arabia, home to a community with deep roots in scripture and centuries of theological formation. These are not uninformed people. They are bishops, scholars, educated men who have given their lives to sacred text.

The exchange that precedes this verse is among the most theologically charged dialogue in the entire Quran. The Prophet ﷺ and the delegation have been in extended conversation about Jesus — his nature, his birth, what his existence means. And then Allah intervenes directly, delivering a sequence of ayahs that builds toward something unprecedented in Islamic history.

In verse 61, Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ to issue a *mubahala* — a mutual invocation of divine curse upon the liars. Bring your children. Bring your wives. Bring your closest people. Stand together and pray: may God's curse fall on the side that is lying. It is the ultimate test of conviction. Not an argument. An existential wager.

The delegation deliberates. And declines.

This verse is what Allah says when they decline.

---

### The Hook

Six Arabic words.

A conditional. A divine name. A divine attribute. A label.

No fire. No flood. No announced reckoning. Just: *if they turn away — then Allah is all-knowing of the corrupters.*

Something enormous is happening in this small, quiet sentence. It takes the entire drama of the mubahala — the theological argument, the invitation, the ultimate test — and closes it not with a declaration of war but with a declaration of *knowing*.

And embedded in that closing is a word that renames everything.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: تَوَلَّوْاْ — When "Turning Away" Has a Root

Before we enter this word, let me teach you something about how Arabic morphology works — because it is doing essential load-bearing work here.

Arabic is built on three-letter roots. When the same root is shaped into different verb patterns, it carries recognizable family resemblance while doing entirely different work. Form V of a verb — the *tafa'ala* pattern — is typically *reflexive* or *middle*. It takes the action implied by the prior form and turns it back upon the subject. What Form II does to someone else, Form V does to yourself.

Now enter the root: **و-ل-ي** (*waw-lam-ya*).

In its fundamental sense, this root carries the meaning of *closeness*. Of proximity. Of being near someone. It is the root of *walī* (protector, close friend, guardian), of *mawlā* (master, patron), of *wilāya* (guardianship, authority that arises from nearness). When you describe someone as your *walī*, you are describing someone who is *close* to you — close enough to protect, to care for, to be loyal to.

This is where turning away begins.

**تَوَلَّوْاْ** — root: wly. Form V. Third person masculine plural. Perfect tense.

Take a root that means closeness. Dress it in the reflexive Form V. And watch what happens.

[PAUSE]

To *tawallā* — to turn away — is, at the level of the root, **to perform a deliberate act of undoing closeness**. It is the reflexive movement away from proximity. Not passive drift. Not slow fading. An active, bodily rotation.

Hold the context in view.

The Najran delegation had *come*. They had drawn near. They had sat with the Prophet ﷺ, engaged his words, heard the arguments, received the mubahala invitation. Proximity had happened — not just physically but epistemically. They had been *close* to the evidence. They had stood at the edge of it.

And then they turned away.

The word Allah chooses for that turning — **تَوَلَّوْاْ** — carries inside it the memory of the closeness being left behind. It is not a word for those who never came near. It is a word for those who *were* near, and chose to move.

Notice something: the word sounds like what it is. *Ta-WAL-law* — the doubled *lam*, the weight of it, the sense of something revolving. There is almost a physical sensation to the word — a body rotating away, a back presented where a face was.

[PAUSE]

Stay with the significance of Form V here.

If someone had simply never encountered the truth, this word would not be used for them. But for the one who *engaged*, who *deliberated*, who came close enough to see — when that person moves away, the word used is **تَوَلَّوْاْ**. Because the closeness was real. And the turning is a turning *from* that real closeness.

Before this verse says anything else, it tells us: *we are talking about a deliberate, reflexive movement away from proximity to truth.* Not ignorance. Not incapacity. A choice made from within closeness.

And the classical commentators are careful to name precisely *what* it is they turned away from — because the verb alone does not say. Al-Tabari ties this turning directly to the declaration that immediately precedes it in verse 62: this is *the true account* of Jesus — that he is Allah's servant and messenger, His word cast to Mary and a spirit from Him — and that there is no god but Allah. To *tawallā* here is to rotate away from *that*. Al-Muyassar sharpens it further: they turned away from *believing you and following you*, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ — the refusal is at once doctrinal and personal. And al-Jalalayn renders it most simply of all: they *turned away from faith* — *aʿraḍū ʿan al-īmān*. Three angles, one movement: away from the truth declared, away from the one who declared it, away from belief itself.

### Element Two: المُفْسِدِينَ — The Label They Didn't Choose

Now we arrive at what the classical commentators themselves identified as the most remarkable linguistic move in this verse.

Al-Jalalayn — one of the foundational works of Quranic tafsir — notes specifically that this verse employs *wadh al-dhahir mawda' al-mudmar*: substituting the overt noun where the pronoun was expected.

Let me explain what this means, because it is doing real structural work.

In Arabic, when you say "if they turn away, then Allah knows ___," the grammatically expected completion is: "knows *them*." The pronoun. Simple. Direct. That is what the grammar predicts.

But Allah does not say *knows them*.

He says: **Allah is all-knowing of المُفْسِدِينَ — the corrupters.**

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter?

Because the people who turned away did not see themselves as corrupters. They saw themselves as faithful Christians protecting their theological inheritance. They saw themselves as responsible clergy, shepherding their community's beliefs across generations. They saw themselves as exercising scholarly judgment about a theological claim. From the inside, their refusal of the mubahala was not corruption — it was responsibility.

*They* did not use the word *corrupters* for themselves.

But Allah does. And He uses it precisely where the grammar expected Him to say *them*.

Now look at what this word is made of. The root: **ف-س-د** (*fa-sin-dal*). In its base Form I, *fasada* means: to spoil, to decay, to go bad — like food that has rotted, like water that has become stagnant, like a wound that has become infected. There is a biological quality to it. Something that was sound has broken down.

Form IV of this root — **أَفْسَدَ** — takes that spoiling and makes it *active and transitive*. Not just: to be corrupt. But: to bring corruption about. To cause fasad. To spread the spoiling into the world around you.

المُفْسِدِينَ — the active participle of Form IV, masculine plural — means *those who cause corruption*. Not just corrupted people. People who *generate* corruption outward.

[PAUSE]

Al-Baghawi, in his tafsir, specifies what this fasad looks like here: those who worship other than Allah, and who *call others* to worship other than Allah. The corruption is not contained. It radiates. The *mufsid* is not someone whose personal relationship with God has decayed in private — it is someone whose turning away creates a field of decay around them.

Now hold these two words together:

They **تَوَلَّوْاْ** — turned away.
Allah calls them **المُفْسِدِينَ** — those who spread corruption.

The act they performed is framed as private: they turned away. The identity Allah assigns them is public and spreading: they are corrupters.

Something about turning away from truth — especially after proximity to it, especially from a position of influence — does not stay contained. The Najran bishops were not ordinary individuals. They led communities. Their theological verdict shaped what their people believed. When they turned away, a community turned away with them.

**Turning away from truth in a position of influence is not a private retreat. It is the seeding of fasad.**

[PAUSE — long]

Now the bridge.

The linguistic construction has shown us two things from two directions: the turning away was reflexive and deliberate (Form V of wly), and the consequence of that turning was not merely personal but generative of corruption in others (Form IV active participle of fsd). These are not two separate observations. They are the inside and outside of the same act.

What this invites us to consider — beyond the grammar — is how every act of turning away from truth has an audience it doesn't see itself as having.

### Element Three: عَلِيمُۢ — The Knowledge That Needs No Supplement

When you or I encounter wrongdoing, our instinct is to announce consequences. *I know what you did* — and then the next thing. The punishment. The response. The action.

This verse does something different.

After the conditional — *if they turn away* — after the label — *the corrupters* — there is no punishment stated.

Just: **فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلِيمُۢ** — *then indeed, Allah is All-Knowing.*

Full stop.

[PAUSE]

Look at the word **عَلِيمُۢ**. Root: **ع-ل-م** (*ayn-lam-mim*) — the root of knowledge. But the pattern here — **فَعِيل** — is an intensive form. It is not "one who knows" in the ordinary sense. It is a *saturative* quality. Comprehensively Knowing. Knowing in a way that leaves no corner unlit.

This is not Allah saying "I will check." It is Allah saying: *I already know — and My knowing is complete.*

But then — why stop there? Why not say "He will punish them" or "He will call them to account"?

[PAUSE]

Notice the absence.

When a father catches a child in a serious wrong and says — slowly, quietly — "I know what you did." And then says nothing more. The silence that follows is not empty. It is a space filled with the full weight of everything that knowledge implies. The knowing contains the reckoning without announcing it. The not-saying is itself a form of weight.

Allah's closing of this verse with knowledge rather than punishment is not a lesser statement. It is a deeper one. Punishment is an event. **Being known is a state.**

And here the classical commentators heard something the surface of the verse does not spell out. Both al-Muyassar and al-Jalalayn gloss *Allah is all-knowing of the corrupters* with a single added clause — *fa-yujāzīhim*, "so He will requite them." Al-Muyassar makes it explicit: they are the corrupters, *and Allah knows them, and will recompense them for it*. So the reckoning is not absent from the verse — it is *folded inside the knowing*. To the classical ear, "He knows them" already carries "He will repay them," the way a verdict already carries its sentence. The verse does not need to add the punishment as a separate clause, because for the One whose knowing is **عَلِيمُۢ** — complete, intensive, leaving no corner unlit — to be fully known by Him *is* to be already accounted for. The silence after the knowing is not the absence of consequence. It is consequence held in reserve, certain, inside the knowing itself.

This is not Allah saying "I will check." It is Allah saying: *I already know — and My knowing is complete.*

But then — why stop there? Why not say "He will punish them" or "He will call them to account"?

The Najran delegation was not being told that consequences were coming. They were being told that they are *already seen* — already categorized — already named by a name they did not choose for themselves. And the one who sees them, the one who knows them, is **عَلِيمُۢ** — the one whose knowing is constitutive of who He is, not merely something He does.

**The verse closes with knowledge because knowledge is the deeper reality. Everything else flows from it.**

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Gap Between Our Names and God's Names

There is a quiet, persistent gap in human self-knowledge.

We are, almost all of us, the heroes of our own story. The person who does something harmful almost always has a narrative — a reason, a justification, a larger context that makes the action understandable from the inside. We rarely see ourselves doing the thing we condemn in others.

The Najran bishops had a narrative. They were protecting orthodoxy. They were shepherding a flock. They had theological reasons — centuries of them — for their position on Jesus. From the inside, their refusal of the mubahala was not corruption. It was faithfulness to their tradition. It was responsible leadership.

And then Allah calls them **المُفْسِدِينَ**.

[PAUSE]

What is being revealed here is not simply that they were wrong on a theological question. It is something deeper: **the names we give our actions and the names Allah gives our actions are often not the same thing.**

We call it conviction. He calls it turning away from closeness to truth.
We call it caution. He calls it refusing the test.
We call it faithfulness. He calls it spreading fasad.

The Quran returns to this gap repeatedly. The people who drowned with Pharaoh thought they were defending civilization. The people who drove prophets from their cities thought they were protecting their communities. Every act of rejection in Quranic history came wrapped in a narrative that made internal sense.

The question the verse presses on every listener — not just the Najran delegation — is: **where in my life is there a gap between what I call my action and what Allah calls it?**

[PAUSE]

There is a concept in modern psychology called *motivated reasoning* — the tendency of the mind to work backward from a desired conclusion, constructing reasons that justify where we already wanted to go. We do not assess evidence and then make a decision. We often make the decision and then assess the evidence in its light. The conclusion comes first. The reasons come after.

The Najran delegation had access to everything they needed. The Prophet ﷺ in front of them. The Quran being recited. The arguments laid out. The mubahala invitation open. And they declined — not from incapacity but from cost. If they accepted, everything changed. Their authority. Their community's identity. Their life's work. Their families' histories.

When the cost of truth is high enough, the mind finds reasons to reject it. And those reasons feel, from the inside, like integrity.

But from outside — from the view that sees clearly — they are named المُفْسِدِينَ.

[PAUSE]

One question to carry forward from this:

Where in my own life is the cost of accepting truth high enough that I might be building a story? Not about someone else — about myself. A relationship I haven't repaired because the narrative I've constructed around it has become comfortable. A neglected obligation I've been explaining to myself with language that sounds reasonable. A conviction I've been avoiding testing because testing it might disturb what I've built around it.

The gap between our names and God's names is not only a historical observation about scholars from Najran. It is a standing condition — and a standing invitation to honest examination.

### Theme Two: The Weight of Being Known

Think about what it feels like to be *seen*.

Not observed. *Seen.* Someone knowing not just what you did, but what it *was*. The thing behind the thing. The reason behind the reason. The interior shape of the decision that preceded the act.

Most of the time, we move through the world carrying the quiet comfort that our interior life is largely opaque to others. People see our actions; they don't see the calculation behind them. They see our words; they don't see the deliberation that produced them. There is always a layer of privacy between what we are inside and what the world encounters.

[PAUSE]

This verse removes that privacy — not by announcing punishment but by announcing *knowing*.

**فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلِيمُۢ** — then indeed, Allah is *al-Aleem* — the one whose knowledge is complete, intensive, comprehensive. Leaving no interior unlit.

The word **عَلِيمُۢ** is not a verb describing what Allah is currently doing. It is a *quality*. A name. An ontological descriptor. Allah's knowing is not a response to the event of turning away. It is a constitutive feature of His being that was always there, that encompasses the act of turning away and its perpetrators — their reasoning, their self-justification, the narrative they are carrying home.

What the verse does by closing with **عَلِيمُۢ بِٱلْمُفْسِدِينَ** is this: it places the turners-away inside the permanent field of divine awareness. They are known. Their act is known. Their motive is known. The gap between the name they give themselves and the name Allah gives them — that gap is known too.

[PAUSE]

There is a strange doubling here that repays sitting with.

The verse calls them corrupters. But the one who knows them is **عَلِيمُۢ** — comprehensively knowing. The one who spreads fasad and the one who comprehensively sees are placed in direct, stated relation. The verse does not say "Allah will eventually deal with the mufsideen." It says: **Allah, in His essential quality of knowing, knows them** — present tense, constitutive, permanent. And yet, as the classical readers heard, that very knowing leans forward into requital: *He knows them, and He will repay them.* The state of being known and the certainty of being recompensed are, in this verse, one and the same.

This is simultaneously the most merciful and most sobering statement imaginable.

Merciful — because nothing is hidden from the One who judges. The full picture is seen. Including the pressures. The inherited belief. The cost of acceptance. The fear behind the refusal. Allah does not judge an incomplete picture.

Sobering — because the self-justification they are carrying — the internal narrative that makes their decision feel like faithfulness — He knows that too. He knows it as constructed. He sees where the conclusion came before the reasons. The private story we tell ourselves is not private from Him.

[PAUSE]

The practical invitation here is not guilt. It is precision in self-examination.

If Allah already knows the mufsideen — sees them as they are, not as they see themselves — then the invitation in this verse is to try to see ourselves more as He sees us. To hold our self-narratives a little more lightly. To bring what we privately justify before honest interior examination and ask: *does this look different from outside my story?*

The practice the Islamic tradition calls *muhasaba* — self-accounting, interior reckoning — is precisely this: not waiting for the accounting of the Day, but beginning it now. Closing the gap, one degree at a time, between the name we give ourselves and the name Allah would give us.

**Being known by Allah is not something that begins at death. It is the standing condition of every living soul. The question is only whether we live as though we know it.**

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture of the Verse

Step back and look at what this verse does in three movements.

**A conditional** — *if they turn away.* An open door that they could have walked through. The possibility of another outcome is preserved in the grammar. This is not destiny pronounced over them. It is a choice acknowledged.

**A label** — *the corrupters*. Not "the ones who turned away" — but the ones whose turning away generates fasad in the world around them. The private act is renamed with its public consequence. The grammar expected *them*. Allah said المُفْسِدِينَ.

**A knowing** — *Allah is all-knowing of them*. No punishment announced. Just: *He knows.* And the knowing, in its completeness and permanence, is the ground on which every reckoning rests — the reckoning the classical commentators heard folded inside the very word.

These three movements are not three separate claims. They are one sentence describing one reality from three angles: the act, the identity that act reveals, and the audience before whom it is performed.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and thematic work converge on a single recognition.

The Form V of **تَوَلَّوْاْ** told us that turning away is an *undoing of closeness* — an active, reflexive movement away from proximity to truth. It is a word for people who came close enough to see, and then chose to rotate away.

The substitution of **المُفْسِدِينَ** for the expected pronoun told us that this turning away is not the end of the story — it begins a story of spreading corruption, because those who turn away from truth in positions of influence do not only decline truth for themselves. The Form IV transitive active participle names them as generators of fasad outward.

And **عَلِيمُۢ** — the intensive, ontological knowing — told us that the true audience for this act is not the community they lead, not history, not even the Prophet ﷺ. The audience is Allah. And His knowing is not partial. It is comprehensive. It encompasses not just what they did but what they called what they did — and, in the reading of the mufassirūn, it carries its own quiet promise of requital.

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you. They are not rhetorical. Sit with them honestly.

1. Where in my life am I using the language of faithfulness or responsibility for something that, seen from outside my narrative, might carry a different name?

2. The Najran delegation had proximity to truth — they came, they engaged, they heard. Where have *I* been brought close to something I know is true and then turned away — not from ignorance, but from the cost of acceptance?

3. What would change in how I live if I genuinely inhabited the knowledge that **عَلِيمُۢ** — the comprehensive, intensive knowing — sees not just my acts but the interior story I've constructed around them?

4. Who in my life carries influence the way the Najran bishops carried influence — whose turning away does not stay private but travels with them into a community?

5. Is there any part of my interior accounting that I have been avoiding bringing to honest light — because the light might require a change I am not yet ready to make?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When a person turns their back on truth after having stood close to it, they do not simply decline — they become, in the divine naming of things, a spreader of corruption; and the one who remains present is not silence but the comprehensive knowing of Allah, which is itself the ground of every reckoning still to come.

---

### The Closing Invitation

After this reflection, one shift may be possible.

Not dramatic. Not performative. Something interior.

The next time you are constructing a narrative about why you did something — or why you did not do something you know you should have — pause for one breath before the narrative is complete. Ask: *does this look different from outside my story?*

Not to paralyze yourself with self-doubt. But to close, one degree at a time, the gap between the name you give your actions and the name that is already written beside them in the sight of the One whose knowing leaves nothing unlit.

The mubahala was declined. The dialogue ended. But the verse did not close with an ending. It closed with a *knowing* — permanent, comprehensive, waiting.

Which means the invitation is still open.
