# The Signature of the Righteous

### Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 17

> **ٱلصَّـٰبِرِينَ وَٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ وَٱلْقَـٰنِتِينَ وَٱلْمُنفِقِينَ وَٱلْمُسْتَغْفِرِينَ بِٱلْأَسْحَارِ**
>
> *aṣ-ṣābirīna waṣ-ṣādiqīna wal-qānitīna wal-munfiqīna wal-mustaghfirīna bil-asḥār*
>
> "[They are] those who are patient, those who are truthful, the devoutly obedient, those who spend, and those who seek forgiveness before the dawn."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

A few verses earlier, Allah has been speaking about the things people love and chase — and He names them with a precision that stings. Women, sons, "heaped-up heaps" of gold and silver, branded horses, cattle, fields. *That* is the inventory of human desire, Allah says, *that* is "the enjoyment of the worldly life." And then He turns, almost gently, and says: but with Allah is the best place of return.

And then He does something tender. He asks: *Shall I tell you of something better than all of that?*

And He describes Gardens beneath which rivers flow, for those who are conscious of Him. Spouses made pure. And — this is verse 16 — He lets us hear them speak. He puts their own words in our ears:

*"Our Lord, indeed we have believed, so forgive us our sins and protect us from the punishment of the Fire."*

[PAUSE]

So now picture the listener in Madinah hearing this. They have just been shown a category of human being who has won — who has been promised the Gardens, the purified companionship, the nearness of Allah. The natural question rises in every chest: *Who are these people? What do they actually look like? If I passed one of them in the market, how would I know?*

And verse 17 is the answer. It is the portrait. Five brushstrokes, and the face appears.

Here is the strand running through this whole opening of Aal-Imran: the surah is about to plunge into argument — with the People of the Book, about Jesus, about the nature of revelation — and then into the open wound of Uhud, where the believers bled and some of them broke. Before all of that, Allah pauses to show us the inner anatomy of the soul that survives all of it. Five traits. This is the spine that holds a person upright when the arguments rage and the arrows fall.

**Here is the hook.** Allah lists five qualities of the people He has just promised Paradise. Four of them, He leaves wide open — He does not tell you *what* they are patient about, *what* they are truthful in, *what* they spend. He leaves the doors swinging. But the fifth and final quality — the one He chooses to *end* the portrait on — He pins to a single, exact, almost secret hour of the night.

Why does the list of the people of Paradise end not with a triumph — not with their patience, not with their victory, not with their generosity — but with them awake in the dark, **asking to be forgiven**?

Stay with me. The answer changes what you think holiness is.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Grammar of *Being*, Not *Doing*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the verse, because without it the verse stays flat.

In English, we have a clumsy way of turning an action into a kind of person. We say someone is "a giver." We say someone is "a forgiver," "a fighter," "a dreamer." Notice what that little *-er* does. It stops describing what you *did* once and starts describing what you *are*. "He gave me money yesterday" is an event. "He is a giver" is a character. One is a snapshot; the other is a portrait.

Arabic has a precise, built-in machinery for exactly this. It is called the **ism al-fāʿil** — the active participle. You take a root, you pour it into a particular mold, and out comes not the *act* but the *actor*. Not "he was patient on Tuesday" but **the patient one** — patience as a settled, ongoing identity.

Now look at the verse. Look at the shape of every single word:

**aṣ-ṣābir**īn — the patient ones
**aṣ-ṣādiq**īn — the truthful ones
**al-qānit**īn — the devoutly obedient ones
**al-munfiq**īn — the spending ones
**al-mustaghfir**īn — the forgiveness-seeking ones

[PAUSE]

Every one of them is an *ism al-fāʿil*. Every single one. Allah did not say "those who were patient," using a past-tense verb, an event that happened and ended. He did not even say "those who are patient," using a present-tense verb, an action in progress that might stop tomorrow. He used the form that means **this is who they are.** Patience is not something they did. It is something they became. It has hardened into identity the way water becomes ice — same substance, but now it holds a shape.

Let's sit with what that means. There is a vast difference between a person who *tells the truth* and a person who *is* truthful. The first is describing an action they sometimes perform. The second is describing a material they are made of. You can catch the first one lying on a bad day. The second one would have to stop being themselves in order to lie.

And here is what is remarkable: Allah is not describing the believer's best moments. He is describing their baseline. Their resting state. When the camera is off, when the test is over, when nobody is grading them — this is still what they are. The participle freezes the trait into permanence.

So before we even ask *what* they are patient about, the grammar has already told us something staggering about the standard. Allah is not promising Paradise to people who occasionally behaved well. He is describing people for whom righteousness has become the grain of the wood.

One clear thing this single grammatical mold reveals: **the people of Paradise are not defined by their actions, but by what their actions have made of them.**

[PAUSE]

There's a quieter point hidden here too, and the classical grammarians caught it. Al-Baghawī notes that you can read these five words in two ways — as an accusative of praise (*"the patient ones!"* — a standalone burst of admiration) or as a genitive adjective describing the God-conscious back in verse 15. Both readings are sound. And notice that the ambiguity itself is a gift: these traits are at once *a description* of who the believers are **and** *a celebration* of it. The grammar refuses to separate the fact from the praise — because to truly see these qualities is already to honor them.

---

### Element Two: The Four Open Doors and the One Closed Hour

Now we come to the strangest thing in the verse, and it is a thing of *absence*. To see it, you have to notice what Allah did *not* say.

Let me teach the principle first. In Arabic, as in any language, a verb or a description usually wants an object. If I say "she gives," your mind immediately reaches for the next word — gives *what?* If I say "he is patient," you wait for — patient *with what?* The sentence feels unfinished until the object lands. Grammarians call this the expectation of a complement. The mind leans forward, waiting.

Now watch Allah *refuse* to satisfy that lean. Four times in a row.

**Patient** — about what? He doesn't say.
**Truthful** — in what? He doesn't say.
**Obedient** — to whom, in what? He doesn't say.
**Spending** — what, on whom, from where? He doesn't say.

Four open doors. Four sentences that, by the rules of the language, *could* have been completed and were deliberately left swinging.

What does silence like that do? Listen to what the mufassirūn did with just the first word, *ṣābirīn*. Because Allah left it open, al-Muyassar pours *three* meanings into it: patient upon obedience, patient away from disobedience, *and* patient under the painful decrees that strike them. Al-Qurṭubī adds patience against the pull of desires. Al-Baghawī adds patience in hardship, in adversity, and "in the hour of battle." Al-Ṭabarī, reading the word's unspecified object against the whole sweep of the Quran, names the same three arenas in language that will ring familiar if you know Surah al-Baqarah: patience in *al-ba'sā'* — hardship, in *al-ḍarrā'* — distress, and at *ḥīna al-ba's* — the hour of battle itself. The phrasing he uses is almost verbatim from 2:177. The patience of the righteous praised in al-Baqarah and the patience of the *muttaqīn* praised here are one thing. And then consider the context: Aal-Imran is heading toward Uhud. These five words describing the patient ones appear *before* the battle chapter arrives — so that when the reader reaches the arrows and the blood and the broken line, they already have the name for what the survivors were doing. They were being *ṣābirīn*.

None of them are inventing — they are reading the **open door.** Because Allah named no object, the word now holds *every* object. The patience here is not patience-about-one-thing. It is patience as a total stance toward existence.

That is the genius of the unspecified. A defined word is a single coin. An undefined word is a blank cheque that the believer's whole life is invited to fill in. By withholding the object, Allah made these four traits *infinite in scope.*

[PAUSE]

Watch how the open door works with *ṣādiqīn* specifically, because the classical scholars did not let that word rest at its surface. Al-Ṭabarī reads the unspecified object of their truthfulness as everything a believer has professed: they are truthful to Allah by *verifying* their acknowledgment of Him — not in words, but through the texture of their lives, enacting what He commanded and refraining from what He prohibited. Al-Muyassar confirms it from a parallel angle: their truthfulness runs through both their speech *and* their deeds. Al-Jalālayn pins the object directly: truthful *in their faith*, meaning the content of their belief is not contradicted by how they live. What all three are converging on is a classical definition of *ṣidq* that has no clean equivalent in the English word "honesty." Honesty is about what you say. *Ṣidq* is about whether you *are* what you say. The gap between your declaration of faith and your actual life — in the classical view — is itself a kind of untruth, even if every syllable you spoke was technically accurate. The *ṣādiqīn* are those who have closed that gap.

With *munfiqīn*, al-Jalālayn draws the open door slightly tighter: they are the *mutaṣaddiqīn*, the almsgivers — charitable spending specifically, not just any disbursement. And al-Muyassar opens it in a different direction: they spend *secretly and publicly*, meaning the unspecified door covers not just what they give but the full range of how they give — the anonymous gift and the visible one. Together, the two readings hold the whole spectrum of generosity: from the coin slipped in the dark to the public act of open-handed giving.

[PAUSE]

And now — the fifth.

**al-mustaghfirīn bil-asḥār.** Those who seek forgiveness... *in the pre-dawn hours.*

After four wide-open doors, Allah suddenly does the opposite. He takes the final trait and He nails it to an exact coordinate in time. Not "those who seek forgiveness" — left open like the others — but those who seek forgiveness **at the saḥar**, the last sliver of darkness before the sky goes pale.

Notice the asymmetry. Feel how deliberate it is. He could have left the fifth open like the four before it. He could have specified a time for *patience* — but patience has no clock, it is needed at every hour. He could have specified a place for *spending* — but giving is good wherever it lands. Out of all five traits, the *only* one Allah binds to a specific, narrow, named time is **the asking for forgiveness.** And He binds it to the most private, most hidden, most reluctant hour of the entire day.

Why?

Hold that question. We have to open the word *saḥar* before we can answer it.

---

### Element Three: The Architecture of the Climb — and the Hour Before the Light

Let me show you something about the *order* of these five words, because they are not a random pile. They are a staircase.

In Arabic, verb roots can be poured into different "forms," and each form carries a flavor. Look at the morphological forms climbing through this verse:

- **ṣābir, ṣādiq, qānit** — these three are the simplest mold, Form I. Patient. Truthful. Devout. Quiet, foundational states of being.
- **munfiq** — this is Form IV. The root *n-f-q* in this mold means to *expend*, to actively put your wealth out of your hand and into the world. It is no longer a still state; it is an outward motion. The self begins to *give itself away.*
- **mustaghfir** — and this is Form X, the *istafʿala* pattern. And Form X has a signature meaning that you can hear in the very sound of it: *isti-* — to **seek**, to **ask for**, to **request** something you do not have and cannot manufacture yourself. *Istighfār* is not "forgiving." It is *seeking* forgiveness — stretching out an empty hand toward the only One who owns it.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the shape of the climb? The portrait begins inside the self — patience, truth, devotion, the inner soil. Then it moves outward — spending, the self giving to others. And then, at the very summit, it turns and reaches *upward* — the empty hand, the asking. The grammar itself ascends: from *being*, to *giving*, to **seeking.** And it is the *seeking* form, the form of the outstretched hand, that Allah chose to crown the list.

The most spiritually accomplished person in the verse is not standing tall in their achievement. They are kneeling in the dark with an open palm.

And now, finally, the hour.

**al-asḥār** is the plural of *saḥar* — and the plural matters; not one dawn but every dawn, again and again. The *saḥar* is the last portion of the night, the deep cold hour just before the first light. The four mufassirūn light it up from four angles, and they don't contradict — they're four cameras on one truth. Al-Jalālayn says it was singled out because it is **the time of heedlessness and the sweetness of sleep** — the hour when every instinct in your body begs you to stay under the blanket. Al-Muyassar says it is **the time most likely for acceptance, the hour of answered prayer.** Al-Qurṭubī records a beautiful difference: Anas ibn Mālik said *al-mustaghfirīn* are simply those who *ask* for forgiveness, while Qatāda said they are those who *pray* at that hour — and al-Qurṭubī resolves it with a smile: there is no contradiction, *they pray and they seek forgiveness, both at once.* Al-Baghawī, through Mujāhid, Qatāda and al-Kalbī, confirms it: the ones praying in the pre-dawn dark.

And al-Jalālayn adds one more detail, perhaps the most arresting of all: the *mustaghfirīn* seek forgiveness *by saying "Allāhumma-ghfir lanā"* — O Allah, forgive us. If that prayer sounds familiar, it should. Verse 16 has already given it to us. The God-conscious speak there: *Rabbanā fa-ghfir lanā dhunūbanā wa qinā ʿadhāb al-nār.* What verse 17 then reveals is *when* they speak it. The prayer you heard them offer in verse 16 — they offer it again at the saḥar, night after night. The two verses form a closed loop: verse 16 gives us the text of the petition; verse 17 gives us the hour of its utterance. And the hour is the most honest hour, the hour before the self has assembled its daily defenses, the hour before the world's noise makes it easier to avoid the truth about what you carry.

[PAUSE]

Here is the physical image Allah hands us, and we must not stop at the image — we must follow where it points. The *saḥar* is the threshold. It is the moment when the night is at its darkest and the day has not yet broken. The believer is awake *in that exact seam* — in the last of the dark — washing their soul with *istighfār* so that when the light breaks, they meet it clean.

Don't analyze the darkness. Ask what the darkness is *pointing to.* The pre-dawn black that is about to become light — that is the sin about to become forgiven. The threshold of the day is the stepping stone, and the threshold of the soul is the destination. They rise in the one to enact the other. They stand in the physical dark-before-light to rehearse the spiritual dark-before-light: the self stained at night, made luminous by morning, by the simple, devastating act of asking.

So now we can return to our question with the bridge built. *Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Pinnacle Is an Apology

Let me start where you actually live.

Think about how we imagine arrival. The whole machinery of modern life is built around the idea that growth means needing less help. The toddler learns to walk so they no longer need to be carried. The student graduates so they no longer need to be taught. The professional rises until the training wheels come off entirely. We have built our entire concept of maturity around the slow shedding of need — around becoming someone who does not lean. To need is to be not-yet. To have arrived is to no longer need.

[PAUSE]

And then this verse appears, and it does not merely challenge that picture. It tears the map in half.

Look at the five people Allah has assembled here. *Ṣābirīn* — they have trained themselves to remain standing when everything tries to knock them down. *Ṣādiqīn* — they have closed the gap between what they believe and how they live, until their whole life is one continuous truth. *Qānitīn* — they have bent their will to the will of Allah so thoroughly that obedience has become their natural motion. *Munfiqīn* — they have loosened their grip on their own wealth until giving flows out of them the way water flows downhill.

These are not beginners. These are people who have done serious work on themselves. If you met one of them, you might feel you were in the presence of someone who had *arrived.*

And then verse 17 shows you what they do when the household goes quiet and the city goes dark.

They are awake. They are at the pre-dawn threshold. And they are asking to be forgiven.

[PAUSE]

Not because they have failed. Not because something went wrong yesterday. Not because they are the lesser members of the group, the ones still working up to the standard of the patient and the truthful. The *mustaghfirīn bil-asḥār* is not a consolation category. It is the crown of the list. Allah placed it last — and in Arabic, the last position in a sequence is the position of emphasis, the landing spot where the meaning comes to rest. The portrait of the people of Paradise does not close with a fanfare. It closes with an open hand in the dark.

Ibn Kathir, describing these same *muttaqīn*, calls them those who "pray and beg Allah's pardon in the last hours of the night." *Pray* **and** *beg*. The two together: the form and the content. The standing body and the stretched-out soul. It is not enough to be present at the saḥar if you are only performing presence. They are there with the whole instrument of the self — the will that got them out of bed, and the honesty that brings them to their knees.

So we return to the question: *why?*

Why does the person who has achieved all four of the preceding qualities — who has built patience, truth, obedience, and generosity into the structure of their being — why do they end their portrait not in triumph but in asking?

Because they have seen something that the self-satisfied have not. They have been near enough to Allah to understand something about the nature of nearness itself.

[PAUSE]

Here is the discovery: *proximity to Allah does not reduce your sense of need. It increases it.*

The person who prays five times and is only half-attentive does not feel the distance. They are too far away to feel what distance means. But the person who has truly tasted the saḥar — who has stood in the pre-dawn cold and felt the nearness, who has heard the silence of a world asleep and found that in that silence a Voice is close — that person knows. They know that between the Creator and the creature, there is a gap that no amount of obedience can fully close. Not because the effort is wrong. But because the gap is *structural.* It is the nature of what we are.

The patient one knows that their patience has edges they haven't reached yet. The truthful one knows the corners where they have not yet been honest. The devout one knows the prayers they offered distracted, the prostrations that were form without presence. The generous one knows the moments they held on too tightly. And none of them can repair this on their own. The repair has to come from outside the self — from the only One whose forgiveness actually covers.

That is what *isti-* means. Form X. The reaching toward what you cannot manufacture yourself.

The pinnacle is an apology. And it is an apology from people who are completely right to make it.

---

### Theme Two: What You Build and What You Receive

Stay with me for a moment in the structure of the five, because there is something in the arrangement that I want you to see.

The first four traits — patience, truthfulness, obedience, generosity — are things that human beings can *build.* Not easily. Not without struggle. But they are buildable. You develop patience by enduring things you would rather avoid, again and again, until something in you learns to hold still. You build truthfulness by choosing the accurate word over the comfortable one so many times that accuracy becomes your reflex. You cultivate obedience by submitting your will to Allah's in the small things until submission no longer requires effort — until it is simply what you do. You practice generosity by releasing what you hold until releasing becomes your posture toward the world.

These are the fruits of a life's work. They are real and they are hard and they are honored by Allah in this verse. He names them. He praises them. He makes them the description of those He has promised the Gardens.

[PAUSE]

But the fifth is different in kind.

*Istighfār* — forgiveness-seeking — is not a character trait you can build by practice. You cannot rehearse your way into being forgiven. You can seek it; you cannot manufacture it. It lives entirely on the side of Allah, not the side of the believer. The believer's only act is the asking. The outcome — the actual covering of the sin, the actual washing-clean of the record — is entirely His to give or withhold.

This is why Form X is so exactly right. The *istafʿala* pattern, the *mustaghfir*, does not claim the action. It *seeks* the action, as from a source outside itself. Contrast it with what a word in Form II would carry: *ghaffara*, to actively make someone forgive — the agent imposing the outcome. Form X says nothing like that. It says: I am reaching toward what You hold. I am asking You to do what only You can do. The form itself is a confession of asymmetry.

[PAUSE]

And so the portrait becomes complete in a way it could not have been without the fifth. The first four describe what the believer has built. The fifth describes what they know they cannot build. Together, the five say something whole: I have done what I can. I have been patient in what I faced, truthful in how I lived, devoted in my standing, open-handed with what I had. And I know — I know — that none of that is sufficient on its own for what I need. I need You to do what I cannot do for myself.

That is the complete portrait. Not character alone. Character *and* acknowledged need.

The theologians of the Islamic tradition have long recognized that the greatest danger for the person on the spiritual path is not sin — sin can be repented. The greatest danger is the hardening that comes from believing you have earned your way. The *mustaghfirīn bil-asḥār*, by definition, have escaped that danger. They are still asking. They will never stop asking. Not because they have failed, but because they have understood.

---

### Theme Three: The Hour Itself

Let me close with the *saḥar* — the hour itself — because I don't think we can leave it as a detail. I think it is the point.

The *asḥār* is the plural of *saḥar*, and the plural matters. Not one dawn, not a single night of contrition after a particularly heavy week, but the *asḥār* — the pre-dawns, again and again, morning after morning, across the whole arc of a life. Allah does not describe someone who once rose in the dark and asked for forgiveness. He describes a *habit* of the pre-dawn. A recurring architecture. Built into the week, into the season, into the self.

And the classical commentators are right to point out what the pre-dawn costs. Al-Jalālayn names it precisely: it is the time of heedlessness and the sweetness of sleep. The body is pulled down. The warmth is real. The darkness outside the window is complete. Everything that is physical in you says: *stay.* And the *mustaghfirīn bil-asḥār* say: *no.*

But I want you to notice something beyond the cost. I want you to notice the *position.*

The saḥar is the last dark before the light. It is the hour *inside* the transition. The night has not yet ended; the day has not yet begun. The sleeper lies on one side of a threshold, and the dawn-seeker stands on the other. And what do they do in that threshold? They ask to be forgiven.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means for how they meet the day.

The person who falls asleep with yesterday's weight, who carries the accumulated smallness and failures of the day before into the morning, who never finds the moment to set it down — that person wakes already carrying something. They begin the day in debt to themselves, under a cloud they have never faced directly.

But the *mustaghfirīn* have stood at the threshold and spoken. They have brought what they carry — the weight, the smallness, the crooked moments — and set it before Allah in the only hour when the world is quiet enough to be honest. And they have asked. Whatever the outcome in the unseen, something is different in them when the light breaks. They have been honest. They have not pretended the debt isn't there. They have named it, before the only One who can actually do something about it, in the only hour private enough to say what you would not say in daylight.

And now the day rises on a person who has already faced themselves.

[PAUSE]

The Quran returns to this portrait. Surah al-Dhāriyāt 51:18 puts it even more simply: *wa bil-asḥāri hum yastaghfirūn* — and in the pre-dawns, they were seeking forgiveness. Same people, same hour, same act. And Surah al-Zumar 39:9 draws near the same figure from yet another angle: the one who is devoutly obedient through the hours of the night, prostrating and standing, *fearing the Hereafter and hoping for the mercy of his Lord*. The *qānitīn* of the night are also those who hold fear and hope simultaneously — and both of those are reasons to ask for forgiveness. Fear of the account. Hope that the asking will be answered. The Quran's portrait of the righteous returns to this scene so consistently that it becomes impossible to read it as incidental. The pre-dawn *istighfār* is not one arrow in their quiver. It is the orientation that makes all the other arrows fly straight.

[PAUSE]

You cannot be patient all day if you have not first acknowledged in the dark that your patience has edges. You cannot be truthful in the market if you have not first told the truth to Allah about where you were not truthful yesterday. You cannot be devout in the five prayers if you have never stripped away the performance and stood in the cold with nothing to offer but an open hand. The saḥar is not a sixth trait layered on top of the four. It is the *root* that feeds the four.

And here is the thread that runs all the way back to the beginning of the passage.

These are the same people whose prayer was given to us in verse 16: *Rabbanā fa-ghfir lanā dhunūbanā wa qinā ʿadhāb al-nār.* Our Lord, forgive us our sins and save us from the punishment of the Fire. That is the prayer Allah quoted from their own lips. And now verse 17 tells us something we might have missed: they are not saying that prayer in a single moment of contrition after a fall. They are saying it — as al-Jalālayn records, with the specific utterance *Allāhumma-ghfir lanā* — at the pre-dawn, repeatedly, as a *practice.* Verse 16 gives us the text of the prayer. Verse 17 gives us the hour of its utterance. Read together, what they say is: the prayer of the God-conscious is not a crisis prayer. It is a morning prayer. Not prompted by disaster. Prompted by dawn.

## CLOSING

So now I can answer the question I asked you at the beginning.

Why does the portrait of the people of Paradise end not with a triumph but with them in the dark, asking to be forgiven?

Because the person who is still asking — after all their patience and truthfulness and devotion and generosity — is the person who has finally understood what holiness actually is.

Holiness is not arrival. It is not the moment when you no longer need anything. The tradition does not point us toward self-sufficient saints who stand above the fray, untouched, needing nothing from Allah but to be received. The tradition points us toward people who are still in the conversation. Who are still reaching. Who have done the work — and the first four traits are serious work — and who have done the work precisely because the work has taught them how much they owe.

[PAUSE]

The patient one has discovered what they cannot endure on their own.
The truthful one has discovered how many corners of their life are still unlit.
The obedient one has discovered the distance between submission and wholeness.
The generous one has discovered how tightly the hand wants to close.

And they bring all of this — all of this discovery — to the threshold of the dawn, and they ask.

That is not weakness. That is the most honest thing a human being can do.

And Allah — in a verse that is itself a portrait of what He loves — ends the list right there. He does not add a sixth quality above it. He does not reserve the Gardens for those who have progressed beyond the asking. He paints the *mustaghfirīn bil-asḥār* — the ones who seek forgiveness in the pre-dawn hours — at the summit of the portrait. At the end of the list. At the landing spot where Arabic places its emphasis.

[PAUSE]

The last word of the portrait is not their achievement. It is their prayer.

And that, perhaps, is the most quietly radical thing this verse teaches. In a world that locates arrival in self-sufficiency, in a spiritual culture that can too easily mistake eloquence for depth and productivity for piety — this verse says: the one who has gone furthest is the one still awake in the dark, still asking, still convinced that they need what only He can give.

The signature of the righteous is not what they have built.

It is what they still ask for.
