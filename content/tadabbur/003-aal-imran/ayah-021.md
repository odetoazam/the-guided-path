# The Mockery in the Word "Congratulations"

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah three times. Once in Arabic. Once so you can hear its shape. And once in plain English.

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يَكْفُرُونَ بِـَٔايَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ وَيَقْتُلُونَ ٱلنَّبِيِّـۧنَ بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ وَيَقْتُلُونَ ٱلَّذِينَ يَأْمُرُونَ بِٱلْقِسْطِ مِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ فَبَشِّرْهُم بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

*Inna alladhīna yakfurūna bi-āyāti-llāhi wa-yaqtulūna an-nabiyyīna bi-ghayri ḥaqqin wa-yaqtulūna alladhīna ya'murūna bil-qisṭi mina-n-nāsi fa-bashshir-hum bi-'adhābin alīm.*

> "Indeed, those who disbelieve in the signs of Allah, and kill the prophets without right, and kill those who command justice from among the people — so **give them glad tidings** of a painful punishment."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this ayah, you have to stand where its first listeners stood.

This is Madinah. Early years. The surah we are in — Aal-Imran — is not, in this stretch, a private conversation between Allah and the believers. It is a public, sustained dialogue with the People of the Book, the inheritors of Mūsā and 'Īsā, peace be upon them. The verses just before this one have built a careful, almost tender argument: *the religion with Allah is submission* (3:19). Those who were given the Book did not divide over it out of ignorance — they divided *after* knowledge came to them, out of envy between themselves. And then the door is held open with a gentleness that should stop your heart: "Say to them — *have you submitted your face?*" (3:20). Not "you are condemned." *Have you come home yet?*

So the climate of this passage is invitation. Argument made with an open hand.

And then comes verse 21 — and the temperature drops.

Because Allah now turns from those who merely *turn away* to a different category of human being altogether. Not the one who hears the truth and shrugs. The one who hears the truth and reaches for a weapon. Allah is naming the most extreme pattern in the entire history of revelation: the people who did not just reject the prophets, but *killed* them — and then, when ordinary believers stood up to carry the same message, killed them too.

This is the dark backdrop against which the gentleness of the previous verses suddenly glows. The invitation was real. And here is what it was being offered *against.*

### The Hook

Now I want you to notice one word, and then I want you to let it bother you for the next hour.

After this catalogue of the worst crimes a human community can commit — rejecting Allah's signs, murdering His prophets, murdering the people who simply *asked for fairness* — what is the verb Allah chooses to deliver their sentence?

**فَبَشِّرْهُم** — *fa-bashshir-hum.*

"Give them **good news.** Congratulate them. Bring them the glad tidings."

[PAUSE]

That is not the word you expect. You expect *warn them.* You expect *threaten them.* Instead, the word for a face lighting up at wonderful news — the word a man hears when he's told *it's a son,* the word a traveler hears when told *you're almost home* — that is the word Allah selects to introduce a *painful punishment.*

Why would Allah congratulate them?

Hold that question. By the time we answer it, it will have rearranged how you understand the justice of God.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Three Verbs That Never Stop — يَكْفُرُونَ ... وَيَقْتُلُونَ ... وَيَقْتُلُونَ

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we touch the ayah, because once you have it, the verse opens differently.

Arabic, like English, can speak of an action as *finished* or as *ongoing.* In English, "he lied" closes the book — it happened, it's done. But "he lies" is something else entirely. "He lies" is not a report about one event. It is a description of a *character.* It tells you what to expect tomorrow. When you say of someone, "he lies," you are not narrating his past. You are warning about his nature.

Arabic has this same distinction. The past-tense form (*al-māḍī*) closes an action. The present-tense form — *al-muḍāri'* — leaves it open, recurring, ongoing, characteristic.

Now look at the three crimes in our ayah. Every single one of them is in the *present* tense:

- **يَكْفُرُونَ** — *yakfurūna* — "they disbelieve" (not *kafarū*, "they disbelieved")
- **وَيَقْتُلُونَ** — *wa-yaqtulūna* — "and they kill" (not *qatalū*, "and they killed")
- **وَيَقْتُلُونَ** — *wa-yaqtulūna* — "and they kill"

Stay with this. Because there is something strange here that you might walk right past.

The killing of the prophets is a *historical* fact. By the time of this revelation, those murders were centuries in the past. Zakariyyā, Yaḥyā, the long line of prophets sent to Banī Isrā'īl, peace be upon them — these were closed chapters. The natural way to report a closed chapter is the past tense: *they killed.*

But Allah does not say *they killed.* He says **they kill.**

[PAUSE]

Why?

Because Allah is not reporting an *event.* He is exposing a *disposition.* The present tense lifts the crime out of history and reveals it as a *standing trait* — something that is true of these people not because of what their ancestors' hands did, but because of what their own hearts *are.* The hand that killed the prophet is dead and buried. But the *posture* that killed the prophet — the refusal of the sign, the hatred of the one who corrects you — that is alive in the room. It is present tense. It is *now.*

This is why, in tafsir, the immediate referents of this verse and its surrounding context are people who, with their own hands, never touched a prophet. Al-Baghawī notes the referent includes those of the People of the Book in the Prophet's ﷺ own time. They had not personally killed Zakariyyā. And yet they are folded into the verb. Why? Because **the present tense doesn't indict the hand. It indicts the heart that would have done the same.**

There is a hadith principle that illuminates this exactly — that the one who is *pleased* with a deed is, in the scales, a partner to it though oceans and centuries separate him from the doer. The present tense *yaqtulūn* is the Qur'anic grammar of that principle. You who would have raised the weapon — you who, hearing the prophet corrected you, feel the old murderous reflex stir even if your society's laws keep your hand still — *you* are in this verse. It is not history. It is a mirror.

And now notice the *order* of the three verbs, because it is an ascent into darkness, step by step:

**First: they disbelieve in the signs of Allah** — *yakfurūna bi-āyāti-llāh.* This is the root. The refusal of the *āyah,* the sign. Everything begins here, in the privacy of the heart that will not accept evidence it does not like.

**Second: they kill the prophets** — they move from refusing the *sign* to destroying the *bearer of the sign.* The disbelief could not stay contained in the chest. It needed to silence the mouth that spoke the truth.

Al-Tabari preserves a significant debate over the verb form precisely here. Some Kufan readers — tracing their reading to a variant attributed to Ibn Masʿūd's mushaf — read this as *yuqātilūna*, Form III: "they engage in combat with the prophets." The vast majority of readers across every major center — Madinah, Makkah, Basra, Kufa — read *yaqtulūna*, Form I: "they kill outright." Al-Tabari affirms the majority, and the difference is not trivial. Form III *qātala* implies a bilateral contest, two parties meeting force with force, as if the prophets were somehow combatants in a war. Form I *yaqtula* strips that framing entirely: this was not combat between equals. It was killing. The majority reading refuses to grant the killers even the cover of calling it a fight.

**Third: they kill those who command justice — from among the people** — *mina-n-nās.* And here is the final, terrible step, which we will return to. After the prophets are gone, ordinary people stand up and simply ask for *fairness* — and they are killed too.

Three verbs. A staircase descending. Refuse the truth → kill the one who brings it → kill anyone who even *echoes* it. This is not three separate sins. It is one sin maturing. And the present tense tells you it has not finished maturing yet. It is *yaqtulūn.* Ongoing. Present. A pattern still looking for new victims.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Absence That Accuses — بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ

Now I want to show you a phrase that should not be there. And the fact that it *is* there is the whole point.

After "they kill the prophets," Allah adds: **بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ** — *bi-ghayri ḥaqq* — "without right," "unjustly," "with no rightful cause."

Sit with the strangeness of this.

Is there a *right* way to kill a prophet? Could there be a *justified* murder of a messenger of God? Of course not. It is the single most unambiguous wrong a human being can commit. So why would Allah qualify it — why specify "without right" — when killing a prophet is *by definition* without right? It is like saying "a square with four sides," or "wet water." The qualifier seems to add nothing.

This is what I want you to learn to do: when Arabic gives you a word that logic says is unnecessary, **the word is not adding information. It is exposing a posture.**

Here is what *bi-ghayri ḥaqq* is doing. It is not informing *us* that the killing was unjust — we knew that. It is stripping the killers of the lie they told *themselves.*

Because no one — *no one* — kills a prophet while admitting "I am murdering a man of God for no reason." That is not how the human heart works. The heart that does monstrous things first builds itself a *ḥaqq,* a "right," a justification. *He is a blasphemer. He is a threat to our nation. He is dividing our families. He breaks our traditions. The law demands it. God Himself would want this.* Every prophet-killer in history walked to the act draped in a sense of righteousness. They killed *with* a reason — in their own minds.

And Allah, with two words, tears the drapery off. *Bi-ghayri ḥaqq.* "There was no right. Whatever you told yourself, whatever law you cited, whatever flag you wrapped it in — it was *naked.* It was wrong all the way down."

[PAUSE]

This is the mercy hidden inside the severity of this phrase. Allah refuses to let the most self-righteous violence pass under the cover of righteousness. He names it. And He is teaching every reader, in every century, a piece of spiritual self-defense: **the feeling of being justified is not the same as being justified.** The murderers *felt* they had ḥaqq. The feeling was a lie. And the most dangerous evil in the world is never the evil that knows it is evil — it is the evil that is certain it is good.

The Kufan variant reading mentioned a moment ago — *yuqātilūna*, 'they engage in combat with' — performs the same maneuver linguistically that *bi-ghayri ḥaqq* performs semantically. Both are attempting, from different angles, to dress the killings as something mutual, something defensible. Al-Tabari's affirmation of *yaqtulūna* and the Qur'an's insertion of *bi-ghayri ḥaqq* are two voices saying the same thing: **there is no frame in which this was legitimate.** No verb form and no claimed justification changes what it was.

Now read the absence — because there is one. Allah attaches *bi-ghayri ḥaqq,* "without right," to the killing of the *prophets.* But when He comes to the third crime — killing those who command justice — He does **not** repeat it. He does not say "and they kill, without right, those who command justice." The qualifier appears once and is then withheld.

What does that withholding say?

Here is what it invites us to consider. The third group is *defined* by the very thing the killers are violating. They are *alladhīna ya'murūna bil-qisṭ* — "those who command **justice**." Their entire identity in the verse is *qisṭ,* fairness, the right. So to say "they killed the people of justice *without right*" would almost be redundant in a different way — the wrongness is no longer something Allah needs to add from the outside, because it is sitting inside the description of the victims. You killed *justice itself,* embodied in human form. The "without right" has migrated from a tagged-on phrase into the very name of the people you destroyed. The absence is not a gap. It is the point arriving from a different door.

### Element Three: The Word That Should Mean Joy — فَبَشِّرْهُم

Now we arrive at the word from the hook. The word that made you uncomfortable in the introduction. Let me earn it for you fully.

The root is **ب-ش-ر** — *b-sh-r.* And before it ever meant "good news," it meant something physical. *Al-bashara,* from this same root, is the **skin** — specifically the outer skin of the face, the surface where your inner state becomes visible to the world. *Basharan sawiyyā,* a well-formed human, is named from this root — the creature of *skin.*

So why does the word for "good news" — *bushrā,* *bishāra* — grow from the word for *skin*?

Because **bushrā is the kind of news so good that it changes your skin.** It is the news that floods your face. The color rises, the eyes widen, the surface of you — your *bashara* — transforms before you can say a word. *Bishāra* is not just information that pleases you. It is information that *shows.* It writes itself on your body. Think of the face of someone told the test was clear, the child is safe, the loved one survived. The news goes straight past the ears and into the skin.

That is the word. *Fa-bashshir-hum.* "So make their faces light up. Bring them the news that changes their skin."

And its object? **بِعَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ** — *bi-'adhābin alīm* — "a painful punishment."

[PAUSE]

Now. What is happening here?

The grammarians and mufassirūn have a precise name for this. It is **تَهَكُّم** — *tahakkum* — rhetorical mockery, savage irony. Al-Jalālayn says it directly, in plain words: *wa dhikr al-bishāra tahakkum bihim* — "the mention of 'glad tidings' here is a **mockery** of them." Allah does not stumble into an odd word choice. He selects the word for joy *precisely because* it is the opposite of what is coming, and the gap between the word and the reality is itself the punishment beginning.

Let me show you why this is so much heavier than a simple threat.

If Allah had said *fa-andhir-hum,* "so warn them," that would be a straightforward sentence of judgment. Stern, but expected. It would meet them as enemies meet enemies.

But *fa-bashshir-hum* does something crueler and more precise than a warning. A warning maintains its distance. It says: *enemy, receive this judgment.* It keeps sender and receiver on opposite sides of a line. But *bashshir* closes that distance. It is the word of proximity and warmth — the voice of the one who runs to the door because the news is too good to deliver slowly. To use it here is to force the criminals into an unbearable intimacy with their verdict.

And there is a deeper precision to it. Who were the prophets? They were, in the Qur'anic vocabulary, *mubashshirūn* — bringers of glad tidings. The title appears as one of the defining designations of every prophet sent: *mubashshiran wa-nadhīrā,* "a bringer of glad tidings and a warner." These people killed the *mubashshirūn.* They silenced the voices carrying the most extraordinary *bushrā* in existence: *that there is a God, that He has not abandoned you, that there is a way home.* That was the news they refused. That was the news they killed.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says *fa-bashshir-hum,* the full weight lands. You destroyed the *bushrā.* So *bushrā* comes for you — in the form of your own verdict, shaped exactly by what you chose to destroy.

Al-Jalālayn adds a grammatical observation worth pausing over: the *fa-* that opens *fa-bashshir-hum* appears in the predicate of *inna* because the subject — *alladhīna*, "those who" — structurally resembles a conditional clause in Arabic grammar. The rule is: when the subject of *inna* has that conditional character, the predicate takes *fa-* as a marker of consequence. The grammar is encoding something theological: *given who they are, this verdict follows with the force of logical necessity.* This is not a punishment imposed from outside, arriving as an arbitrary sentence. It is a consequence that their own choices built, step by step, through each present-tense verb. **The** ***fa-*** **is not "then." It is "therefore."**

[PAUSE]

This is what *tahakkum* finally is: not cruelty dressed as irony, but justice with the precision of a mirror. They covered the *āyāt* — so their works are covered, lost in this world and the next (the following verse, 3:22, completes the thought: "those are the ones whose deeds have become worthless in this world and the Hereafter, and for them there will be no helpers"). They killed the bearers of glad tidings — so glad tidings are what announce their doom. The form of the doom is the exact shape of the choice. **Allah is not responding to them from outside their logic. He is completing their logic, all the way to its end.**

### Element Four: The Ones Who Died on the Same Day — مِنَ النَّاسِ

We return now to the third crime, as promised.

**وَيَقْتُلُونَ الَّذِينَ يَأْمُرُونَ بِالْقِسْطِ مِنَ النَّاسِ** — "and they kill those who command justice *from among the people.*"

*Mina-n-nās.* From among the people. Two words at the end of the verse, and they change the entire scale of the indictment.

The ayah has descended in stages. It began with *āyāt Allāh* — the signs of God: revelation, miracle, the speech of the Unseen entering time. It moved to *an-nabiyyīn* — the prophets, the specific human vessels of that revelation, each one chosen and sent. And now it lands at *al-nās* — people. Just *people.* No title. No office. No special appointment from above. The generic Arabic word for humanity, the undifferentiated crowd.

The descent is intentional and devastating. After the prophets were silenced, the message did not disappear. It lived in anyone whose conscience had absorbed it — in ordinary men and women who heard the injustice, felt its wrongness in their bones, and could not stay silent. Al-Muyassar identifies this third group as those who follow *ṭarīq al-anbiyā'* — the path of the prophets. They are not a separate category from the prophets; they are the prophets' heirs. The prophet was killed. The obligation he carried — *command justice, forbid wrong* — passed to anyone with a living conscience. And so the killers had to keep killing.

Al-Jalālayn transmits a report with a specificity that should arrest you. Forty-three prophets were killed among them. When word spread that same day, one hundred and seventy of their own people — ordinary members of the community, not prophets, not scholars carrying divine appointment — stood up and condemned the killings. *This is wrong. Stop.* And they were killed. *Min yawmihim.* On the same day.

[PAUSE]

One hundred and seventy people. Not armed. Not powerful. Simply unable to watch injustice in silence. Killed before the sun set on the day the prophets fell.

This is the full weight of *mina-n-nās.* These were not extraordinary human beings. They had no miracles. They were given no revelation. They simply had consciences that could not be quiet, and they spoke. And speaking was enough to make them targets. Because the heart that kills the prophet kills the justice-seeker for the same reason: both threaten the same thing — the impunity of power that wants to act without anyone saying *wrong.* The prophet threatened that impunity with the word of God. The ordinary person threatens it with the word of conscience. Both must be silenced.

*Mina-n-nās* is not a downgrade in the verse's catalogue. It is the revelation of how total the rejection is. This is not a rejection of the extraordinary alone — of prophets, of miracles, of divine revelation. It is a rejection of *decency itself,* in its plainest human form. When you have killed the prophet and then also killed the one hundred and seventy who simply asked for fairness, you have refused everything. There is nothing left to refuse.

And the present tense again: *yaqtulūna.* They kill. Present. Ongoing. The pattern is still moving.

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE VERDICT

### One Sin, Three Scales

By now the structure of this ayah should be visible as a single building, not three separate rooms.

**The interior: yakfurūna.** They cover the sign. The root *k-f-r* means to conceal, to pull something over what is present. Disbelief in this verse is not the humble uncertainty of the sincere seeker — *what if I'm wrong?* It is the active work of covering: taking the evidence and ensuring it cannot reach the heart. It is a private act, invisible from the outside, committed in the space between a sign and a soul.

**The interior becomes exterior: yaqtulūna an-nabiyyīn.** The covering could not stay inside. The prophet's voice kept arriving, kept opening what had been sealed, kept giving others the chance to see what the refuser was refusing to see. So the voice had to be stopped. The private sin became public violence.

**The violence cannot stop: yaqtulūna alladhīna ya'murūna bil-qisṭ.** After the prophets, anyone who echoed the message. After the revelation, anyone with a conscience. The covering-over required more and more covering-over, because the truth kept finding new mouths. This is not three crimes. It is one crime attempting to become total.

Every commentator who examines this verse grapples with the same question: why did Allah frame this as *ongoing* — present tense, all three verbs — when the prophets were killed centuries before revelation? Ibn Kathir frames the passage as a chastisement of the People of the Book in the Prophet's ﷺ own time: not because they personally raised the weapons, but because the disposition — the will to silence the correcting voice — was alive in them. They would not have committed the killing with their hands in Madinah, because the law would not allow it. But they were pleased with it in the past, and the pleasure is the partnership. The present tense indicts the heart, not only the hand. It does not ask: did your hand do this? It asks: would it have?

### The Bushrā They Were Offered and the Bushrā They Receive

There is one final dimension of *fa-bashshir-hum bi-'adhābin alīm* that should sit with you.

Throughout the Qur'an, *bashshir* is Allah's word for mercy. *Wa-bashshir al-mu'minīn* — give glad tidings to the believers. *Bashshir-hu bi-ghulāmin ḥalīm* — give him glad tidings of a gentle son. The word is reserved, again and again, for the announcement of God's generosity: of paradise, of divine favor, of the news that this life had meaning and what waits is better.

Every prophet carried this *bushrā* to the people who received them. Not merely warnings about punishment — the message was always, at its root, *there is a God who made you and loves what you can become, and He has sent someone to tell you the way.* That is the original glad tidings. That is the *bushrā* these people killed.

When Allah says *fa-bashshir-hum bi-'adhābin alīm,* He is performing a precise reversal. You refused the real *bushrā* — the news of mercy, the invitation of 3:20 that came in the verse just before this one: *have you submitted your face? Have you come home?* You refused it at the level of the sign. You silenced it at the level of the prophet. You killed it at the level of the one hundred and seventy ordinary people who stood up on the same day just to echo it. So here is *bushrā* for you — in the only shape your choices have left room for. **The grammar of joy is still the grammar of joy. What has changed is everything you put inside it.**

### The Invitation That Stands Behind the Verdict

And this — the connection to 3:20, the verse just before — is where this ayah reveals its full purpose in the passage.

Verse 3:20 is an open door: *qul li-alladhīna ūtū al-kitāb wa-l-ummiyyīna a-aslam-tum* — "Say to those given the Book and to the unlettered: have you submitted?" The language is almost impossibly gentle. After everything — after centuries of prophets killed, after the Book given and then divided out of envy, after the full history Allah has just narrated — the question is not *you are condemned* but *have you come home yet?*

Verse 3:21 arrives immediately after, and it does not withdraw the gentleness. It *explains* it. It names what the invitation is being held against. The door of 3:20 is not naive. It does not pretend there is no darkness at the end of the other path. It is being extended with full knowledge of what the darkest form of rejection looks like — forty-three prophets killed, one hundred and seventy ordinary people killed on the same day for the crime of asking for fairness — and it is still saying: *have you come home yet?*

The gentleness does not diminish when you know the severity of the alternative. It intensifies. It becomes almost unbearable in its sincerity. Here is the door. Here is what stands behind the door that closes. The glad tidings of mercy, or the glad tidings of punishment. Both are *bushrā.* The only variable is the one who receives them — and what they have done with every sign placed before them.

## CLOSING: WHAT THIS AYAH ASKS OF YOU

You are not a prophet-killer. None of us likely are. This verse might feel sealed in its historical distance — a permanent record of an ancient pattern.

But the present tense will not close.

*Yakfurūna.* They cover the sign. What is the *āyah* in front of you right now — not the Qur'anic verse specifically, but the sign in your own life: the evidence that something needs to change, the voice that has been telling you a true thing you have been refusing to hear, the correction you have been covering over with a *ḥaqq,* a justification that sounds right but isn't? The ayah does not require you to have killed anyone. It asks what you have done with the sign. It asks whether the first verb is active.

*Bi-ghayri ḥaqq.* The killers had their reasons. Every one of them. The most dangerous moment is not when you know you are doing wrong — it is when your wrongdoing feels, to you, entirely justified. *Bi-ghayri ḥaqq* names the distance between the feeling of ḥaqq and the reality of it. The murderers felt righteous. The feeling was a lie. The most dangerous evil in the world is not the evil that knows it is evil. It is the evil that is certain it is good.

*Wa-yaqtulūna alladhīna ya'murūna bil-qisṭi mina-n-nās.* Those one hundred and seventy ordinary people. They did not wait to see what the more powerful figures would do. They could not. The prophets had been killed, and they stood up on the same day and said *this is wrong.* They had no special appointment. No revealed text backing their particular stance. They had only the recognition of injustice, and the inability to be quiet about it. And they are in the Qur'an. Their names are not recorded. Their faces are not described. But their act — standing up when it would have been safer to stay seated — is in the eternal record.

There is a question this verse places at the feet of anyone who reads it: when the ordinary moment arrives — when someone in your presence is being treated unjustly, and speaking up is genuinely costly — which verb are you?

Are you *mina-n-nās*, the ordinary human being who commanded justice when it would have been much easier not to? Or has the old first verb done its quiet work, sealing the evidence before the conscience could act?

Both verbs are present tense. Both are ongoing. The choice is available now.

The original *bushrā* is still being offered. It was the message of 3:20, the verse just before this one. The door was open then and the door is open now. *Have you submitted your face? Have you come home yet?*

That is the glad tidings that was being offered before the verdict was ever necessary. It is still, even now, being offered first.
