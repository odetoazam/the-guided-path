# A Word From Him: The Child Who Was Named Before He Was Born

## Introduction

Let me begin by placing the words in front of you — three times, so they can settle.

**The Arabic:**

> إِذْ قَالَتِ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ يَـٰمَرْيَمُ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكِ بِكَلِمَةٍ مِّنْهُ ٱسْمُهُ ٱلْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ٱبْنُ مَرْيَمَ وَجِيهًا فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا وَٱلْءَاخِرَةِ وَمِنَ ٱلْمُقَرَّبِينَ

**The transliteration:**

> *Idh qālati l-malāʾikatu yā Maryamu inna llāha yubashshiruki bi-kalimatin minhu, ismuhu l-Masīḥu ʿĪsā bnu Maryam, wajīhan fi d-dunyā wa-l-ākhirati wa mina l-muqarrabīn.*

**The English:**

> When the angels said, "O Maryam, indeed Allah gives you glad tidings of a word from Him. His name is the Messiah, ʿIsa, the son of Maryam — honored in this world and the Hereafter, and among those brought near."

### Let us rebuild the world.

Before we can hear what is being said, we have to stand where Maryam is standing.

She is a young woman, alone. She has spent her life in a sanctuary — the *miḥrāb* — given over to the worship of Allah before she was even born, vowed by her mother while she was still in the womb. She is, in every sense, a woman of the unseen. Provision arrives to her out of nowhere; Zakariyya finds fruit out of season at her side and asks where it came from, and she answers, simply, that it is from Allah.

So she is already living at the membrane between the seen and the unseen. And now that membrane tears open completely. The angels — and the classical commentators, including al-Jalālayn, understand this to be Jibrīl — address her directly. **"Yā Maryam."** Not a vision she has to interpret. A *name*, called out. Hers.

Now hold one more thing in your mind, because it changes everything about how this ayah lands.

This conversation is being *retold*. The opening word is **إِذْ — *idh* — "When..."** — a word that points backward, that says *recall the moment when*. Allah is narrating to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ a private scene he never witnessed, in a sanctuary he never entered, centuries before he was born. Al-Muyassar makes this explicit: *you were not there, O Prophet, when the angels said this.* We are being handed a recording of the unseen. We are eavesdropping, by divine permission, on the most consequential annunciation in human history.

### The Hook

And here is what should stop you.

The child has not yet been conceived. Maryam herself, in the very next ayah, will protest that no man has touched her. There is no pregnancy. There is no body. There is, biologically, *no one there yet.*

And yet he already has a **name.** He already has a **category** — he is called something stranger than "a boy." He already has a **rank** in two worlds that do not yet contain him.

Heaven has filled out the entire identity of a person who does not, by any earthly measure, exist.

[PAUSE]

What does it mean to be *named before you are made?* And what is Allah showing us — about ʿIsa, about Maryam, and quietly, about *you* — by announcing a person before announcing his existence?

Let us go slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: "A word from Him" — بِكَلِمَةٍ مِّنْهُ

#### First, the concept.

In ordinary speech, we make a quiet distinction all the time between a *thing* and a *word*. A thing exists in itself. A word *points* — it carries meaning beyond itself, it refers, it announces. When I say a word, the word comes *from* me, it bears my intention, but it is not a piece of my body. It is my will, made audible.

Hold that distinction. Because Allah is about to apply it to a human being.

#### Now, the word itself.

The angels do not say to Maryam, "Allah gives you good news of a *son*." They do not say "a boy," or "a child." They say:

> بِكَلِمَةٍ مِّنْهُ — *bi-kalimatin minhu* — "of a **word** from Him."

A person is being announced under the name of a *word*. The root here is **ك-ل-م (k-l-m)** — the root of *kalām*, speech; of *kalimah*, a word; of *taklīm*, the act of being spoken to. ʿIsa is introduced to his own mother not first as flesh, but as **speech.**

Let us sit with this.

Why would Allah name a human being "a word"?

The classical mufassirūn answer with one voice on the surface meaning. Al-Qurṭubī and others note that here *kalimah* carries the sense of *walad* — a child — which is itself why, as we will see, the grammar shifts. But none of them stop at "it just means child," because the word is doing something the word "child" could never do.

**Here is what is remarkable.** Think of what a word *is* in the Quran's own theology of creation. How does Allah bring things into being? The next ayahs in this very surah tell us — and ayah 59 makes it unmistakable: *the likeness of ʿIsa with Allah is as the likeness of Adam; He created him from dust, then said to him "Be" — kun — and he was.* ʿIsa comes into existence not through the chain of ordinary causes — not through a father — but through the **direct word "Be."**

So when ʿIsa is called *a word from Him*, it is not poetry. It is precise. He is the living realization of *kun*. He is what happens when Allah's creative speech bypasses the entire machinery of cause and effect. A man whose very *category* is a demonstration that **Allah's word makes reality.**

[PAUSE]

But now look at the second half of the phrase, because this is where centuries of theological confusion either begins or ends — depending on whether you read one tiny preposition.

> مِّنْهُ — *minhu* — "**from** Him."

Not *part of* Him. *From* Him. *Min* — the preposition of origin, of source, of *coming-out-of-and-pointing-back-to.*

When you speak a word, the word is *from* you. It originates in you, it carries your meaning — and precisely because it is *from* you, it is **not** you. My word is not a limb torn off me. It is a sign that points back to me as its source while remaining entirely other than me, entirely created, entirely dependent.

This is the load-bearing center of the ayah. A word is the most intimate thing — it comes from your inmost will — and simultaneously the most clearly *separate* thing — it is not your substance. Allah chose, of all the things ʿIsa could have been named, the one name that holds *both* truths at once: utterly **from** Allah, and therefore utterly **not** Allah. The intimacy and the separation, in a single breath.

The same surah will later say it flatly in ayah 4:171: ʿIsa is *only* a messenger of Allah and *His word which He cast unto Maryam.* The word "word" does not raise him to divinity. It is the very vocabulary that *protects* against it. He is *kalimatun minhu* — a sign, from the Speaker, pointing back at the Speaker, and never to be mistaken for the Speaker.

**What this single word reveals:** ʿIsa's deepest identity is not "extraordinary man" and not "divine being." It is *sign.* He is a word God spoke — meaningful, originating in God, pointing to God, and entirely other than God.

### Element Two: "His name" — when the grammar leans toward the unborn

#### First, the concept.

In Arabic, every noun has a gender, and pronouns must agree with it. If I am referring back to a feminine noun, I use a feminine pronoun. If masculine, masculine. It is one of the most basic rules of the language, the kind a child learns first. Pronoun agreement is the grammatical equivalent of subject-verb agreement in English — break it and a native ear flinches.

Keep that rule in your hand.

#### Now, watch what Allah does.

The word just used — كَلِمَة, *kalimah*, "word" — is **grammatically feminine.** Every rule of Arabic says the next pronoun should agree with it. If we were referring back to *kalimah*, the phrase should be **اسمُها — *ismuhā* — "her name"** or "its name," feminine.

But that is not what the ayah says. The ayah says:

> ٱسْمُهُ — *ismuhu* — "**his** name." Masculine.

[PAUSE]

The grammar *breaks* — or rather, it leans. It refuses to agree with the word *kalimah* sitting right next to it, and instead reaches *forward* to a person who has not yet been mentioned, not yet conceived, not yet born. Al-Baghawī notices this precisely and explains it: *the pronoun reverts to ʿIsa* — *radda l-kināyata ilā ʿĪsā.* The masculine pronoun overrides the feminine noun because the heart of the sentence is not the abstract category "word" — it is *him*, the boy.

Notice what this does. For one heartbeat, ʿIsa is an abstraction — *a word.* And then the grammar itself cannot hold him as an abstraction. The pronoun lunges toward the real, particular, masculine child. The language *insists* on his personhood before his body exists.

Stay with this, because it tells you something about how Allah sees a human being. To us, a person becomes "real" at conception, or at birth, or — in our anxious modern way — once they have *accomplished* something, *become* somebody. To Allah, the personhood is fixed and named while the womb is still empty. The masculine *hu* in *ismuhu* is heaven overriding its own grammar to say: *he is already someone.*

#### And then the name is given in full.

> ٱسْمُهُ ٱلْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ٱبْنُ مَرْيَمَ — *His name is the Messiah, ʿIsa, the son of Maryam.*

Three parts. A title — *al-Masīḥ.* A given name — *ʿĪsā.* And a lineage — *ibn Maryam.*

On the title, the classical commentators offer not a single answer but a rich composite, and it is worth holding all the angles at once. Al-Baghawī gathers the views: that *Masīḥ* is *faʿīl* in the sense of *mafʿūl* — one who was *wiped clean*, purified of sin; or one *anointed* with blessing; or, in one narration, wiped by Jibrīl's wing so that Shayṭān could find no way to him. Al-Qurṭubī adds, on the authority of Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, that it carries the sense of *al-ṣiddīq*, the truthful one. Do not read these as competing options to choose between. Read them as camera angles on one figure: purified, blessed, protected, truthful. The title is a portrait.

But look now at the lineage, because here is an **absence that speaks louder than any presence.**

> ٱبْنُ مَرْيَمَ — *ibn Maryam* — "the son of **Maryam**."

The root **ب-ن-ي (b-n-y)** — *ibn*, son. And the son is attached to his **mother.**

Stop and feel how strange this is. In the entire culture of that time and place — and most cultures since — a person is named through the *father.* You are *ibn* so-and-so, son of your father. Lineage runs through the male line. To name a child by his mother is, in that world, almost a grammatical impossibility. It is the linguistic signature of a missing father.

Al-Jalālayn catches it exactly: Allah addressed him *as her son* — *bi-nisbatihi ilayhā* — precisely *to alert* us that **she will bear him with no father** — *tanbīhan ʿalā annahā taliduhu bilā ab.* The very form of the name is the miracle. The absence of the father's name *is* the announcement of the virgin birth. You do not need ayah 47, where Maryam asks "how can this be?" — the impossibility is already encoded in the syntax of the name. *Ibn Maryam.* A name with a hole in it exactly where every other name has a father — and that hole is the sign of Allah.

**What this element reveals:** before ʿIsa exists, the grammar of his name already carries his entire theology — a pronoun that insists he is a real person, a title that paints him purified and truthful, and a lineage with a father-shaped absence that announces, all by itself, that he is a creation of Allah's word and not the ordinary fruit of men.

### Element Three: "Honored... and brought near" — the gift you cannot earn

#### First, the concept.

Think about how honor works in our world. Almost all of it is earned, or at least we tell ourselves it is. You achieve, and then you are recognized. You climb, and then you arrive. Status is the *reward at the end* of a road you walked. We are deeply, instinctively committed to the idea that standing must be *deserved* — earned by the one who holds it.

Hold that assumption up to the light, because this ayah is about to take it apart with two words.

#### The first word: وَجِيهًا — *wajīhan* — "honored."

The root is one of the most beautiful in the Quran: **و-ج-ه (w-j-h)** — the root of *wajh*, the **face.**

A *wajīh* is, literally, a person who *has a face* — and in Arabic that is an idiom of enormous weight. To "have a face" before people is to have standing, recognition, the kind of honor that opens doors. The *wajīh* is the one whose face is known, whose word carries, whose presence in a gathering changes what is possible. Al-Jalālayn glosses it simply: *dhā jāh* — a man of high standing; al-Ṭabarī fills in the texture, describing the *wajīh* as the man whom *kings and people honor and magnify.*

And here al-Ṭabarī notices something a careful ear can hardly believe. That very word for prestige — *jāh* — is itself the word *wajh*, "face," with its letters transposed: the *wāw* that opens *wajh* slid inward, and *wajh* became *jāh.* Prestige, in Arabic, is quite literally *face* rearranged — the language buried the one word inside the other. To have standing is, etymologically, to have a face. But notice where al-Ṭabarī finally anchors that standing. It is not merely status among people; it is *manzilatun ʿāliyatun ʿinda Llāh* — a high rank *with Allah*, honor and nobility in His sight. And the ayah extends it across both realms — *fi d-dunyā wa-l-ākhirah* — honored in this world and the next: his standing in this world through prophethood, and in the Hereafter through intercession and elevated ranks.

Now feel the resonance the root carries. To be *wajīh* — to have a *face* — and recall what we said about ʿIsa being *minhu*, a word *from* Him, a sign that *points back.* A face is precisely the part of a person that *faces toward* — that turns to and is recognized by another. His honor is not a possession he holds against the world; al-Ṭabarī has told us where it points — *ʿinda Llāh* — it is a face turned toward Allah and recognized by Him.

#### The second word — and this is the quiet thunder of the whole ayah: ٱلْمُقَرَّبِينَ — *al-muqarrabīn* — "those brought near."

The root is **ق-ر-ب (q-r-b)** — nearness. But everything hangs on the *form*.

This is not *al-qāribīn* — "those who draw near," those who do the approaching. It is **al-muqarrabīn** — built on Form II, the doubled, intensified pattern, and crucially in its **passive** sense: those who *have been brought near.* The drawing-near is *done to them.* They are not the subject of the verb; they are its object. Someone reached down and pulled them close.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what the grammar is doing?

We assume nearness to God is a *climb.* You strive, you discipline the self, you ascend rung by rung, and at the summit, if you are worthy, you arrive near Him. The whole architecture of our spiritual imagination is a *ladder.*

And the word *al-muqarrabīn* quietly dismantles the ladder. It does not describe people who *arrived.* It describes people who were **carried.** The nearness is not the trophy at the end of their effort. It is a gift placed on them — and placed on this one *before he had drawn a single breath, taken a single step, performed a single act of worship.* ʿIsa is named among the *muqarrabīn* while still unconceived. There is no possible reading in which he *earned* his place. He was *brought.* And al-Muyassar quietly fixes the horizon of that nearness: it is nearness *ʿinda Llāh yawma l-qiyāmah* — near to Allah *on the Day of Resurrection.* The gift announced over an empty womb is one whose fullest unveiling lies at the very end of all things — promised at the beginning, consummated at the last.

And the two honors the ayah names turn out to be quietly one. When al-Jalālayn unpacks where ʿIsa's standing actually lands in each realm, he reads his honor *in this world* as his *prophethood* and his honor *in the Hereafter* as his *shafāʿah* — his intercession — *and the high ranks.* Hold that beside the word that immediately follows: *muqarrabīn.* Intercession is the privilege of none but the brought-near; only the one admitted close to the King is given leave to speak on another's behalf. So his being *wajīh* in the ākhira and his being *muqarrab* are not two separate gifts but one gift seen twice — the nearness is the standing, and the standing, on that Day, becomes advocacy. His closeness to Allah does not stay a private warmth he keeps; it overflows as a voice raised for others.

And notice the breathtaking asymmetry of this entire announcement. Compare Maryam to Zakariyya a few ayahs earlier. Zakariyya *asked* — he prayed, he pleaded for a child, and was answered. Maryam **asks for nothing.** The angels do not request her consent; they do not say "would you accept?" They say *inna llāha yubashshiruki* — *indeed Allah gives you glad tidings* — a settled fact, already decided in heaven. Even the verb of announcement, **يُبَشِّرُكِ — *yubashshiruki*** — from the root **ب-ش-ر (b-sh-r)**, in the intensive Form II, the doubled *shīn* pressing the joy deeper — describes good news *delivered to* her, not bargained for. She is on the receiving end of grace from the first syllable to the last.

**What this element reveals:** the highest honor in two worlds, and nearness to Allah Himself, are here presented not as wages but as *gifts* — bestowed, brought, announced — on one who had done nothing yet, to teach us forever where standing before God actually comes from.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So watch what these words, laid side by side, have shown us. ʿIsa is *a word from Him* — a sign that originates in God and points back to God, never to be confused with God. The grammar of his very *name* leans toward his personhood and carries, in the shape of an absent father, the announcement of his miraculous making. And his honor and his nearness are *given* — bestowed before he existed, brought rather than earned.

Three words. *Kalimah. Ibn. Muqarrabīn.* A theology of who ʿIsa is, and quietly, a theology of who *you* are.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: You Were Named Before You Were Made

Let me start where you actually live.

Almost all of us are quietly exhausted by the project of *becoming somebody.* We were handed, somewhere around adolescence, a blank space where a self was supposed to go, and we have been frantically filling it in ever since — with grades, with titles, with followers, with the approval of people whose names we will forget. We believe, at the deepest level, that we are *not yet* someone, and that we must *make* ourselves into one before we run out of time. Identity, to the modern person, is a *construction project.* And like all construction projects, it is never finished, always over budget, and one bad season from collapse.

Now bring that exhausted self to this ayah.

ʿIsa is named, titled, ranked in two worlds, and counted among the nearest to Allah — *before he is conceived.* His identity is not built; it is *bestowed.* It does not arrive at the end of a life of achievement. It is spoken over an empty womb. Heaven fills in the blank space before there is even a person to worry about filling it.

Let the ayah illuminate your situation. The Quran is showing you a person whose entire worth was settled by Allah's word *before* he had done one single thing — and then, only afterward, did the life unfold. The naming came first. The becoming came second. The identity was the *cause* of the life, not its *reward.*

[PAUSE]

Now — I have to be honest with you, in the way the methodology of careful tadabbur demands. ʿIsa is a prophet, a *kalimah*, utterly unique; you are not a second ʿIsa, and I am not claiming you are. But the *principle* the ayah teaches about how Allah relates to a human being is one you are invited to receive. The Quran tells you elsewhere that Allah knew you, fashioned you, took the covenant from your soul before this world. You, too, were *known and named* in the unseen before your body was assembled. Your existence is also, in its own way, a *word He spoke* — *kun* — and there you were.

So here is the quiet revolution. If your worth were a construction project, then every failure is structural damage and every rival is a threat. But if your worth was *spoken over you* before you could earn or forfeit it — then your striving changes character entirely. You no longer strive to *become* someone in God's eyes. You strive to *live up to* the someone He already named. The pressure inverts. You are not auditioning for a self. You are answering to one already given.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time you feel the gnawing need to prove you are someone — through a post, a purchase, a performance — pause and ask: *am I building a self, or living up to the one Allah already named?* Do one act tomorrow not to *become* worthy, but as a response to a worth already granted. Pray it. Give it. Forgive it. Not to fill the blank — the blank was filled before you arrived.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Nearness Is Something Done To You

Again, let me begin in the human room before I open the divine one.

There is a particular kind of spiritual tiredness that the most sincere people carry. It is the tiredness of the *climber.* You have decided to get close to Allah, and so you have made it a project — more prayer, more dhikr, more discipline — and you measure your nearness by your output, like a man checking his altitude on a mountain. And it works, for a while. And then you have a dry season, or you fall, or the feeling simply goes flat, and the climber's logic turns cruel: *if nearness is something I climb to, then this distance is my failure. I did not work hard enough. I am falling.* The ladder that promised you God becomes the stick that beats you.

I want you to bring that tiredness to the word *al-muqarrabīn.*

We saw the grammar. They are not the *climbers.* They are the *brought-near.* The nearness is *done to them.* And the supreme example of it — ʿIsa — was brought near before he had climbed a single rung, before he could even stand.

Let this sink down through the layers.

If nearness were *purely* a climb, then it would be, in the end, *your achievement* — and a God you can reach by your own effort is a God your effort can also lose, a summit you can fall from by your own weakness. But the Quran refuses to make the most precious thing in existence depend on the steadiness of the weakest thing in existence — your willpower. **It makes nearness a gift He gives, not a height you seize.**

Now — hold the paradox, and do not rush to resolve it, because both halves are true. The Quran is *full* of striving. *Prostrate and draw near* — *wa-sjud wa-qtarib.* You are commanded to approach. The *muqarrabūn* are also, in their lives, the most devoted of worshippers. So which is it — do you climb, or are you carried?

Here is the resolution the ayah quietly offers, and it is the resolution of every deep thing in the relationship with Allah: **your striving is not the rope you climb. Your striving is how you say *yes* to being carried.** ʿIsa was *brought* near — and his entire life was, then, an unbroken devotion. The devotion did not *purchase* the nearness. The devotion was the shape that *receiving* the nearness took. You do not pray to *manufacture* closeness to Allah, as if your rakʿahs were rungs. You pray to *open your hands* to a closeness only He can give — and to keep them open.

[PAUSE]

Feel how this frees you. The climber's dry season is a catastrophe — proof of falling. But for the one who knows nearness is *brought*, the dry season is something else entirely: it is a season to keep the hands open and *wait on the One who carries*, knowing that the carrying was never your doing and the dryness is not your verdict. You are not the engine of your own nearness. You never were. *Al-muqarrabīn* — they were *brought.* And the same Hand that brought them is the only Hand that brings you.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** stop auditing your nearness by your output. Tomorrow, when you stand to pray, do not come as a climber checking his altitude. Come as one with open hands. Say, in your own words, before you begin: *Yā Allah, I cannot reach You. Bring me near.* Make the *duʿāʾ* of the one who knows he must be carried. That posture — not the quantity — is the whole secret of the *muqarrabīn.*

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we found from two directions, and see that they are one thing.

From the **words**, we learned: ʿIsa is *a word from Him* — a sign that originates in God and points back to Him. His *name* leans toward his personhood and carries the miracle of his making in the shape of an absent father. And his honor and nearness are *bestowed* — *wajīhan*, given a face; *muqarrabīn*, brought near — before he existed.

From the **themes**, we learned: that a human being is *named before he is made* — worth spoken over us before we could earn it; and that *nearness is something done to us* — a gift we open our hands to, not a height we seize.

They are the same insight from two angles. The grammar of *minhu* — *from Him* — and the passive of *muqarrabīn* — *brought near* — are both saying the one thing this ayah exists to say: **everything that matters comes down, as a gift, from Allah.** ʿIsa came down as a word. His honor came down as a bestowal. His nearness came down as a carrying. And so does yours. The whole ayah moves in one direction — *from Him, to her, to us.* Downward. Grace.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I still running the *construction project* — trying to *become* someone — when Allah may have already named me in the unseen?
2. When my worship goes dry, do I read it as *my failure to climb* — or as a season to keep my hands open to the One who carries?
3. ʿIsa's name carried a father-shaped *absence* that pointed to Allah. What absences in my own life — things I lack, things withheld — might be *signs pointing to Him* rather than verdicts against me?
4. Do I treat nearness to Allah as something I *earn* and can therefore *lose by failure* — or as something He *gives* and I can only *ask for and receive*?
5. Maryam was given a staggering gift she never asked for. What have I been *given*, unrequested, that I have been too busy to recognize as *glad tidings from Him*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Before ʿIsa drew a breath, Allah named him *a word from Himself*, gave him a face in two worlds, and counted him among those *brought near* — teaching us that the deepest identity and the highest nearness are not built or climbed, but spoken, given, and carried down from Him.

### The Closing Invitation

Something can shift after this, if you let it. You can set down — even for one day — the exhausting belief that you must *make yourself* into someone before you are worthy of God's nearness. You can begin instead from the other end: that you were known, named, and called toward Him before you could lift a finger, and that your whole striving is simply the way you say *yes* to a grace already moving toward you. Not a ladder to climb. A gift to receive, with open hands.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, You who spoke ʿIsa into being with a word and named him before he was made — name me, too, among those You bring near. I cannot climb to You; reach down and carry me. And let everything I do tomorrow be not an attempt to earn what You give, but a way of opening my hands to it. Āmīn.*
