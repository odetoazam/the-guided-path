# And To Him Return All Matters

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with how short it is.

> **وَلِلَّهِ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ**

*Wa lillāhi mā fis-samāwāti wa mā fil-arḍ. Wa ilallāhi turjaʿul-umūr.*

**"And to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth. And to Allah all matters are returned."**

Eleven words. Two sentences. You could read it in five seconds and move on, because it sounds like the kind of thing the Quran says often — *everything belongs to Allah, everything goes back to Allah.* You've heard it. Your eye slides past it the way your eye slides past a sign you've seen a thousand times on the same road.

I want to stop you on this road.

### Rebuilding the world

To feel why this ayah lands where it lands, you have to know what came *just before* it.

The whole back half of Surah Aal-Imran is a courtroom. Allah has been weighing communities. There are the People of the Book — some of whom believe, many of whom turn away. There are the believers who hold firm under pressure — who, as al-Ṭabarī notes, stood fast on affirmation and *kept the covenants they had pledged.* There are those who buy disbelief with their faith. And the surah has been doing something the human heart desperately needs: it has been *sorting*. Who is rewarded. Who is at a loss. Whose striving counts. Whose betrayal is recorded.

And right before our ayah, in 3:108, Allah says something that should make the hair on your arms stand up:

> **"And Allah does not intend injustice to the worlds."**

Think about who first heard that. A small, pressured community in Madinah. People who had been driven from homes. People watching former allies turn hostile. People who had buried loved ones and wondered — privately, in the dark — *is any of this being counted? Does it land anywhere? Or does it just evaporate?*

Allah says: I do not intend injustice to anyone. Not a particle of it.

And then — *immediately* — comes our ayah. As if the listener, having heard "no injustice," instinctively asks the next question: **"But how can You guarantee that? How does anyone guarantee perfect justice?"**

And the answer is not an argument. It is a fact about who owns reality.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice, and then we'll earn it slowly.

This ayah makes *two* claims, and they look almost identical, almost repetitive. *To Allah belongs everything.* And *to Allah everything returns.* Ownership, then return.

But why both? If He already **owns** everything, why does it need to **return** to Him? You don't say of your own house, "this house is mine, and one day this house will come back to me." That's strange. It's already yours.

[PAUSE]

That small strangeness — *He owns it, and yet it returns to Him* — is a door. And behind that door is one of the most stabilizing truths a frightened heart can hold. Let's walk toward it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that comes first — وَلِلَّهِ

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because once you have it, you'll never read these verses the same way.

In English, word order is mostly fixed. "The matters return to Allah." Subject, verb, where-to. If I want to emphasize "to Allah," I have to *do* something extra — I have to add stress with my voice, or italicize, or write "it is to **Allah** that they return."

Arabic has a quieter, more surgical tool. In Arabic, when you take a phrase that would normally come *later* in the sentence and you move it to the *front*, that fronting carries meaning all by itself. The grammarians have a principle for it: **taqdīm mā ḥaqquhu at-taʾkhīr yufīdu al-ḥaṣr** — *bringing forward what would normally come later produces restriction.* Exclusivity. *Only.*

Here's an everyday version. If I say, "I drink coffee in the morning," that's neutral. But if I rearrange it to lead with the coffee — "Coffee, in the morning, I drink" — you instinctively feel I'm setting coffee *against* other options. *Coffee, specifically — not tea.* The fronting itself whispers *only.*

Now look at how the ayah opens.

> **وَلِلَّهِ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ**

It does not say *mā fis-samāwāti li-llāh* — "whatever is in the heavens belongs to Allah," with Allah arriving at the end as one fact among many. It leads with Him. **Wa lillāhi** — *And to Allah [belongs]...* The owner is named *before* the thing owned is even mentioned.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with that little prefix, the **lām** — that *li* attached to the front of *Allah*. The grammarians call it the **lām of possession**, *lām al-milk* — the same particle you'd use to say "this book is *li*-Zayd," Zayd's. It is the language of *belonging.*

And it has been placed first.

So before you are even told *what* belongs to Allah, you are told *to whom it all belongs.* The ownership is established before the inventory is read. It is as if a will is opened and the first words are not the list of the estate but the single name of the heir — and only *then* does the endless list of possessions unfurl: *whatever is in the heavens... whatever is in the earth.*

What does the fronting *do* to your heart? It closes a door. It does not say "Allah owns a great deal." It says, by its very architecture, **Allah owns it, and the question of whether anyone else has a share is over before it began.** Notice — there is no "and to others, a little." The structure leaves no room on the page for a second owner.

This is what the mufassirūn are reaching for when they gloss these words. Al-Jalālayn unpacks *lillāh* with three words: **mulkan wa khalqan wa ʿabīdan** — *as possession, as creation, and as servants.* He owns it the way an owner owns property, the way a maker owns what he made, and the way a master is owed the very existence of the slave. Al-Muyassar adds the dimension of management — **khalqan wa tadbīran** — *creating it and running it.* Not an absentee landlord. The One whose ownership includes the day-to-day governing of every atom.

One word and a prefix. *Wa lillāhi.* And already the universe has exactly one owner, and the matter is not up for negotiation.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The word that refuses to name anything — مَا

Now look at *what* He owns. And notice that the ayah does something almost careless-sounding.

> **مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ**

*mā* fis-samāwāt — *whatever* is in the heavens. *wa mā* fil-arḍ — *and whatever* is in the earth.

Here is the feature, and you have to read it as an *absence* to feel it. Allah does not list. He could have. He could have said: the sun and the moon, the angels and the stars, the mountains and the seas, the gold in the ground and the birds in the air. The Quran elsewhere *loves* to itemize creation — it will walk you through bees and camels and clouds and the alternation of night and day.

Here, He names *nothing.* He uses **mā** — the most open, unspecified relative word in the language. *Whatever.* *Whatsoever.*

Let me teach you why that emptiness is full.

The moment you start a list, you create an edge. A list, by its nature, *ends.* If I tell you "I own the table, the chairs, and the lamp," your mind immediately, automatically, asks: *and what about the rug? the books? the window?* A list invites the question *what about the things you didn't mention.* Listing is the language of the finite — it is how beings who own *some* things speak.

But **mā** has no edge. *Whatever is in the heavens.* You cannot stand at the boundary of *whatever* and point to something outside it. There is no outside. The word is built to contain everything you know, everything you don't know, everything that exists now and everything that will, the things you could name and the things no human tongue has a name for.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference between *He owns the heavens and the earth* and *He owns whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth*? The first owns the *containers.* The second reaches *inside* the containers and claims every single thing the containers hold — down to the thought passing through your mind as you read this, which is, after all, *something in the earth.*

This is **totality achieved through refusing to specify.** The vagueness is not laziness. It is the only way human language can gesture at *all* without falsely implying *all-that-I-managed-to-list.* The unspecified word is larger than any list could ever be.

And notice the pairing the ayah does specify: **as-samāwāt wa-l-arḍ** — the heavens and the earth. This is the Quran's standard shorthand for *the entire created order, the seen and the unseen, the above and the below.* By naming the two poles and filling each with *whatever*, the ayah draws a circle around all of existence and writes a single name inside it.

So now hold the first two pieces together. The fronted **lillāh** said: *one owner, no partners.* The unspecified **mā** said: *and what He owns has no edge, no exception, nothing left out.* One owner. Of everything. Without remainder.

That is the first sentence. Now watch what the second sentence does with it.

### Element Three: The verb with no visible hand — تُرْجَعُ

> **وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ**

*Wa ilallāhi turjaʿul-umūr.* "And to Allah are returned all matters."

Two features are working here at once, and they are both about what's *hidden.*

**First — the fronting again.** Just like the opening, the destination is pulled to the front. Not *turjaʿul-umūru ilallāh* (the matters return to Allah) but **ilallāhi turjaʿu** — *to Allah* they return, with the destination announced before the verb. Same surgical move. Same meaning: *to Him* — and the structure quietly forecloses *anywhere else.* There is no other terminus. Every road has exactly one end.

**Second, and this is the one I want you to feel** — the verb is **passive.** *Turjaʿ.* Not *tarjiʿ* (they return, actively, on their own) but *turjaʿ* — they *are returned.* They are brought back. Something does the returning *to* them.

Let me teach you what the Arabic passive does, because this is doing enormous theological work in a single vowel pattern.

When Arabic uses the passive — *al-fiʿl al-majhūl*, literally *the verb whose doer is unknown / unnamed* — it deliberately removes the agent from the sentence. *The matters are returned* — by whom? The grammar does not say. The hand that does the returning is left out of the frame.

Now — *why* would the Quran hide the agent here, in a sentence whose entire subject is Allah? Surely if there were ever a place to say "Allah returns all matters to Himself," this is it.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because the hiding is the point.

When the agent is removed, the action becomes a *property of reality itself* rather than an event someone has to perform. Think of the difference between "the security guard locks this building at night" and "this building gets locked at night." The first makes you think of a person who could be absent, could forget, could be bribed. The second makes lock-up feel like a *law* of the building — inevitable, automatic, just how this place works.

*Turjaʿul-umūr* — *the matters are returned* — makes the return of all things to Allah feel exactly like that: not an action He has to remember to perform, not a verdict that depends on a court being in session, but a **structural inevitability of the universe.** The way water finds the lowest point. The way a thrown stone comes down. Things go back to Allah not because a process is initiated but because *that is the shape of existence.* It is the current beneath everything, always running in one direction.

And there's a tenderness in the hidden hand, too. The passive removes the *threat* of an executioner while keeping the *certainty* of the return. It does not say "Allah will drag the matters back." It says, quietly, *they are returned* — gently, surely, like a tide that does not need to announce itself to be undefeatable.

And then the verb is **imperfect** — *turjaʿu*, the form for the ongoing and the recurring, not the one-and-done past. This is not only the Day of Judgment, though it is certainly that. It is *now.* Matters are *being* returned, continuously. Every affair that seems to spin off on its own — every injustice that looks unaddressed, every consequence that seems to escape — is, in the grammar's own tense, *in the process of being handed back* to the One who owns it.

Finally, the subject: **al-umūr** — *the matters, the affairs.* Definite, with the *al-* of generality, and plural. Not *some* matters. *The* matters — all of them, the whole category. Al-Jalālayn glosses the verb simply: **taṣīr** — *they come to be, they end up.* Everything ends up there.

(A note for the careful reader, because honesty about the text is part of loving it. The recitation most of the Muslim world carries — that of Ḥafṣ — reads this verb as the passive, *turjaʿ*, the reading we have been tracing. Some of the classical readers recite it actively, *tarjiʿ* — *all matters return* — and al-Jalālayn's own wording, *taṣīr*, "they come to be," leans that way. The destination is identical either way: *to Allah*, and nowhere else. But where it is read in the passive, it adds the extra grace we have been unfolding — a hand you do not see, doing the returning.)

So the second sentence completes the first with terrifying symmetry. The first said: *He owns everything, with no partner and no exception.* The second says: *and everything is, right now and at the end, being returned to that owner — by a hand you cannot see, as surely as gravity.*

### The bridge

Look at what the construction has shown us. Three small features:

- A **fronted lām** that closes the universe down to one owner.
- An **unspecified mā** that opens that ownership to include literally everything.
- A **passive, imperfect verb** that makes the return of all things feel less like a sentence handed down and more like the deepest law of how reality is built.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how to *live* inside a reality shaped like this.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The unbearable lightness of owning nothing

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular exhaustion that comes from *holding on.* You know it. It's the exhaustion of the grip. We spend our lives clutching — clutching reputation, clutching the people we love, clutching our plans, clutching the version of the future we've decided we need. And clutching is tiring in a way that nothing else is, because it never rests. The thing you grip can always slip. So part of you is always on guard, always afraid, always doing the low-grade work of *keeping.*

Psychologists who study anxiety will tell you that a huge portion of human distress is the felt gap between *what I am responsible for* and *what I can actually control.* When that gap is wide — when you feel responsible for outcomes you cannot command — you live in chronic alarm. The mind treats every uncontrollable thing it has claimed as its own as a threat to be managed. And you cannot manage the weather, the markets, other people's hearts, or the day of your death. So you burn.

Now bring our ayah into that fire.

> **And to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth.**

The *mā* — *whatever* — we worked so hard to feel as totality? Run yourself through it. Your body: in the earth. Your children: in the earth. Your reputation, your savings, your unfinished work, the outcome you're losing sleep over — *whatever is in the heavens and the earth.* Every single thing your grip is closed around right now appears, by name, inside that boundless *whatever.*

And the fronted *lillāh* says: **it was never yours to hold.** Not because it's being taken from you cruelly — but because the deed was always in another name. You have been standing guard, exhausted, over a house you do not own, terrified of losing what was never on your ledger to begin with.

[PAUSE]

Feel what that does. It does not make you smaller. It makes you *lighter.*

Because here is the strange mercy hidden in *to Allah belongs everything*: if it all belongs to Him, then the crushing job of *keeping it from slipping* was never your job. The owner keeps His own property. The Mālik, the One whose ownership al-Muyassar described as *khalqan wa tadbīran* — creation *and management* — is the one running it. Not you. You were never the load-bearing wall of your own life. You only thought you were, and the thinking was breaking your back.

This is the difference between a *renter* and an *owner* when the roof leaks at 3 a.m. The owner lies awake doing the math, sick with it. The renter makes one call. Tawḥīd — the truth that He alone owns it all — is the discovery that you have been an owner in your anxiety and a renter in reality, and that you are allowed, finally, to make the call and go back to sleep.

What does this change tomorrow? Take the one thing you are clutching hardest right now — picture it specifically. Then say, *lillāh.* It belongs to Him. Not *I give up on it.* Not *I stop trying.* You will still act, still strive, still tie your camel. But the *outcome* — the part that was never in your hand anyway — you set it back on the owner's shelf. You do your work and you unclench. That is the whole spiritual physics of *tawakkul*: effort in the hand, outcome on His shelf.

### Theme Two: The return that makes injustice bearable

Now the second sentence. And to feel it, I have to take you somewhere harder.

There is a wound specific to watching injustice *get away with it.* It is one thing to suffer. It is another, sharper thing to suffer and watch the cause of your suffering walk off untouched — prosperous, comfortable, never named, never called to account. Every human being who has lived long enough has stood at that wall: the betrayal nobody saw, the cruelty that paid off, the lie that won. And the question that rises is not "why did this happen." It is darker. It is: **"does it land anywhere? or does it just... dissolve?"**

That is precisely the wound the original listeners of Aal-Imran carried. They had been wronged. And the surah, one verse earlier, had pressed a promise into that wound: *Allah does not intend injustice to the worlds.* But a promise of justice is only as strong as the One who guarantees it. A judge who does not control the courtroom cannot guarantee a verdict. A king who does not own the realm cannot promise that the fugitive will be brought back.

Al-Ṭabarī, reading our ayah as the ground beneath that promise, asks the question underneath the question: *why* would the Owner of everything never wrong anyone? His answer is piercing, and it reaches past power to motive. Injustice, he observes, is something only the *needy* commit — the wrongdoer wrongs another only to add to his standing a standing he lacks, to seize some advantage he does not already hold. Oppression is always a grab at something missing. But the One to whom *whatever is in the heavens and the earth* already belongs is missing nothing. There is no advantage outside His ownership for Him to reach toward, no power He could gain by wronging you that He does not already possess without limit. *He has no need of injustice.* And so the ownership in our verse is not only the *power* to guarantee justice; it is the very thing that drains away every conceivable *motive* for it. The Owner of all cannot be bribed, cannot be threatened, cannot be tempted — because there is nothing left for Him to want.

So watch how the ayah *funds* the promise of justice with the fact of ownership and return.

> **And to Allah all matters are returned.**

Remember what we found in *turjaʿ* — the passive, the hidden hand, the inevitability built into the grammar. The matters are not *hopefully* returned, *eventually* returned if someone remembers to file the paperwork. They **are returned** — as a law of reality, like the stone that must come down. There is no affair so small it slips the net, no perpetrator so powerful he can post his deed somewhere outside *whatever is in the heavens and the earth* — because there *is* no outside. We proved that with *mā.* The boundlessness of His ownership is exactly what makes escape impossible.

[PAUSE]

Do you see how the two sentences are one argument? *He owns everything* — so there is nowhere for any matter to flee. *And everything is being returned to Him* — so nothing that fled in appearance has actually escaped in reality. The injustice you watched walk off the field is, in the tense of the verb, *right now being walked back* toward its owner. The grammar will not let it leave.

Al-Muyassar puts the destination of this return in words that should steady you: *wa maṣīru jamīʿil-khalāʾiqi ilayhi waḥdah, fa-yujāzī kullan ʿalā qadri istiḥqāqih* — *the destination of all creatures is to Him alone, and He will recompense each according to exactly what they merit.* Not approximately. Not on average. *ʿalā qadri istiḥqāqih* — to the precise measure of what is deserved.

And here is where I have to be honest with you, because this truth cuts in two directions, and a reflection that only comforts you is lying to you.

The return that guarantees *your* wrong will be addressed also guarantees that *yours* will be. *Al-umūr* — *all* matters — does not have an exemption clause with your name in it. The same boundlessness that means no one escapes accountability *to* you means you do not escape accountability *for* you. The hand that returns the betrayer's deed returns yours.

[PAUSE]

So sit in the strange double-grace of this. The doctrine of return is, at once, the deepest comfort and the most serious summons. It is comfort because nothing done *to* you is lost — it is filed, held, and will be settled *ʿalā qadri istiḥqāq.* And it is summons because nothing done *by* you is lost either — which means the way you live, the way you treat the person who cannot repay you, the private deed no one witnessed, is *also* in the current, *also* being returned.

What does this change tomorrow? Two things, and they are the same thing seen from both ends. First: when you are wronged and there is no earthly redress, you are released from the poison of having to be the universe's accountant. You do not have to make it land. *It is being returned.* Hand it to the Owner. Second: live as someone whose every matter is in that same current. Before you do the thing you could get away with — the unkindness no one will see, the corner you could cut — remember that *getting away with it* is a category that does not exist in a universe with no outside. There is no "away." There is only *to Allah.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now that we've done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole ayah, because it is built with a symmetry you can only appreciate having walked through it.

**Sentence one** opens with *lillāh* — *to Allah* fronted — and speaks of **ownership.**
**Sentence two** opens with *ilallāh* — *to Allah* fronted — and speaks of **return.**

Two sentences. Both leading with His name pulled to the front. Both closing the universe around Him. The first establishes *static* sovereignty — He **owns** all that exists. The second establishes *dynamic* sovereignty — all that exists is *flowing back* to Him. Being and becoming. The noun-sentence of possession, the verb-sentence of motion. The structure itself says: *He holds it all still, and He draws it all home.* Same Owner. From two directions.

And now the strangeness from the very beginning resolves. Remember? *He owns it — so why must it return to Him?* Here is the answer the architecture gives: the **ownership** (sentence one) is the *fact*; the **return** (sentence two) is that fact *becoming visible.* Right now, ownership is hidden — the betrayer walks free, the clutched thing feels like yours, the universe looks ownerless and adrift. The *return* is the moment the hidden ownership is *unveiled* — when every matter visibly arrives at the feet of the One to whom it always, secretly, belonged. It does not return because it left. It returns because the truth of who owned it all along is finally made plain.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **fronted lām** that closes the universe to one owner *is* the lightness of owning nothing — you can finally unclench, because the deed was never in your name.

The **unspecified mā** that leaves no edge *is* the impossibility of injustice escaping — there is no outside to flee to.

The **passive, hidden-handed verb** that makes return feel like gravity *is* the steadiness you can stand on when you've been wronged and when you fear your own reckoning — because what is built into reality does not depend on anyone remembering to enforce it.

One ownership. No edge. Inevitable return. The whole frightened, grasping, justice-starved human condition, answered in eleven words.

### Questions to carry

1. What is the one thing your grip is closed around most tightly right now — and what would it feel like, just for today, to set it back on the Owner's shelf and still do your work?
2. Where in your life are you exhausting yourself standing guard over a house you do not own?
3. Is there a wound of injustice you've been trying to make "land" yourself — and what would change if you genuinely believed it *is being returned*, with or without your help?
4. The same current that carries others' deeds back carries yours. Knowing there is no "away" in this universe — is there a private matter of yours you'd live differently?
5. He owns *whatever is in the heavens and the earth.* When did you last let that *whatever* include **you** — not your things, but you yourself, held by the Owner?

### One-sentence distillation

Because Allah alone owns everything without exception and everything is, by the very law of reality, being returned to Him, you can finally put down what was never yours to hold — and trust that nothing, neither the wrong done to you nor the deed done by you, is ever lost.

### The closing invitation

You came to this ayah able to read it in five seconds. *Everything belongs to Allah, everything returns to Allah.* Familiar. Smooth. A sign on a road you've driven a thousand times.

Drive it again tomorrow, slower. When the anxiety of *keeping* rises in your chest, hear the fronted *lillāh* — *it was never your weight.* When you watch something unjust walk away untouched, hear the hidden hand of *turjaʿ* — *it is already being walked back.* And when you fear your own account, hear *al-umūr* — *all* matters — and let it make you gentle, careful, and honest in the dark, because there is no outside to this house.

This is not a sign on the road. It is the road, and where it ends, and Who is waiting there.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, to You belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth — so loosen my grip on what was never mine, and let me carry only what You ask me to carry. You to whom all matters return: settle what was done to me as You see fit, and make me gentle and watchful with what I do, knowing there is no escaping the road back to You. Make my return to You the lightest and most longed-for thing in my life. Āmīn.*
