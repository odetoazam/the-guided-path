# The Whisper and the Proclamation
### Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 39

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first in Arabic, then slowly in English, and I want you to notice that this entire ayah is a single uninterrupted motion — one breath from the calling to the naming.

> فَنَادَتْهُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ وَهُوَ قَآئِمٌ يُصَلِّى فِى ٱلْمِحْرَابِ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكَ بِيَحْيَىٰ مُصَدِّقًۢا بِكَلِمَةٍ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَسَيِّدًا وَحَصُورًا وَنَبِيًّا مِّنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ

*Fa-nādat-hu al-malā'ikatu wa-huwa qā'imun yuṣallī fī al-miḥrāb, anna Allāha yubashshiruka bi-Yaḥyā, muṣaddiqan bi-kalimatin mina Allāh, wa-sayyidan wa-ḥaṣūran wa-nabiyyan mina al-ṣāliḥīn.*

"So the angels called to him while he stood praying in the prayer-niche: that Allah gives you glad tidings of Yahya — confirming a Word from Allah, and a noble one, and restrained, and a prophet from among the righteous."

### Let me rebuild the moment

To feel what is happening here, you have to stand where Zakariyya is standing.

He is an old man. The Quran does not let us forget this — a few ayahs earlier, in Surah Maryam, he says, *"My bones have grown feeble and my head is alight with grey."* His wife is barren. Not late, not struggling — barren. In the arithmetic of the human body, this account is closed. There will be no child. He has made his peace with it the way old men make peace with things, which is to say he has folded the longing up and put it somewhere he does not look.

And then he walks into the sanctuary where Maryam is being raised under his care, and he finds her with fruit that should not be there — winter fruit in summer, summer fruit in winter, sustenance with no earthly supply chain. He asks her where it comes from. She says, *"It is from Allah. Allah provides for whom He wills without measure."*

[PAUSE]

And something cracks open in the old man.

Because what he has just seen is a living proof that the rules he had surrendered to are not the final word. Provision out of season. Sustenance where the supply has run dry. And his own situation — a child where the body has run dry — is *exactly that shape.* The same miracle, transposed from a basket of fruit to a barren womb.

So Zakariyya turns. And he prays. And here is the detail the Quran preserves about *how* he prayed — it is in Surah Maryam, ayah 3: he called upon his Lord **نِدَآءً خَفِيًّا** — *a hidden call. A secret, whispered cry.* He did not announce it. He did not even say it aloud. He tucked the most impossible request of his life into the quietest voice he had, almost ashamed of the size of what he was asking.

That is the man standing in the niche.

### The Hook

Now hold two words side by side, and let them disturb you a little.

In Surah Maryam, the way he prayed: **نِدَآء** — a *call.* But a hidden one. A whisper.

And here, the way heaven responded: **فَنَادَتْهُ** — *they called to him.* The same root. The same word for "call."

He whispered a call. Heaven returned a call.

But watch what changed between them. **And watch where the answer found him.** Because the answer did not wait for him to finish. It did not arrive the next morning, or after the prayer, or as a feeling on the road home.

It interrupted him mid-sentence — *while he was still standing.* Still praying. Still asking.

Stay with that. We are going to live inside it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: فَنَادَتْهُ — He whispered; they proclaimed

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because the whole insight lives in a distinction the language makes and English does not.

Arabic has more than one way to say "to speak to someone." There is *qāla* — to say. There is *hamasa* — to whisper. There is *nājā* — to speak in confidence, intimately, close to the ear. And then there is the word in our ayah: **نَادَى** — *nādā.* From the root **ن-د-و / ن-د-ي**.

*Nidā'* is not just speech. *Nidā'* is **a call across a distance.** It is the word you use when you raise your voice to reach someone who is not beside you. When the muezzin calls the adhan, that is *nidā'.* When you cup your hands and shout someone's name across a field, that is *nidā'.* It carries the assumption of a gap — a space between the caller and the called that the voice has to cross.

Now. Open the ayah:

> فَنَادَتْهُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ

*"So the angels called to him."* **Nādat-hu** — they called *across* to him.

Let's sit with this.

Remember how Zakariyya prayed. **نِدَآءً خَفِيًّا** — a *hidden* call. He used the very same root, *nidā'.* He, too, was "calling." But he muffled it. He pressed it down into a whisper so faint that the text has to specifically tell us it was *khafiyy* — concealed, hushed, the kind of voice you use when you are not sure you have the right to be asking at all.

Notice something remarkable. He called *quietly toward heaven.* Heaven called *back* — and the word for heaven's reply is the loud word, the across-the-distance word, the proclamation word. **Nādā.**

Here is the asymmetry, and it is the whole point:

**He whispered. They proclaimed.**

He was ashamed of the size of his request, so he shrank his voice to match his sense of his own deserving. And the response came back at full volume — not matching the smallness of his asking, but matching the *greatness of the One being asked.*

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet law being shown to us here. **The volume of heaven's answer is not calibrated to the volume of your asking.** You can whisper a prayer you are half-ashamed of, a prayer so unlikely you barely move your lips — and the reply can come back as a *proclamation,* announced by angels, recorded in a Book that will be recited until the end of the world.

The grammar carries one more thing. Look at the subject of the verb: **ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ** — *the angels,* plural, with the definite article. He called as one frail voice in an empty room. He was answered by a *plurality* — a chorus, a delegation. One whisper. A multitude in reply.

Here the commentators do part ways, and the disagreement is worth knowing. Most take *al-malā'ika* at face value — the angelic host. Al-Jalalayn, though, glosses the plural as denoting **Jibrīl** specifically, the foremost of them sent to carry the news. The Arabic permits both: the language will sometimes name a single eminent figure with a plural of honor, or name the herald by naming his whole order. But notice that even the narrower reading does not shrink the moment — whether a host called or its chief herald called on the host's behalf, the address still crossed the distance to one man.

And the classical reciters even preserve a tenderness in the verb's *shape.* Most read **fa-nādat-hu** — with the feminine ending, because *malā'ika* is grammatically treated as a collective. But Hamza and al-Kisā'ī read **fa-nādā-hu** — the masculine form, a variant al-Tabari records and al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi note. The two readings are not a contradiction; they are two camera angles on one event — the call as a unified collective act, and the call as direct, personal, aimed at one man. There is even a variant in the particle that follows: *anna* with a fatḥa ("*that* Allah gives you glad tidings") or, as al-Jalalayn notes, *inna* with a kasra, which the grammarians say presumes an unspoken *"saying"* — turning what follows into **direct speech,** the angels' own voice breaking in: *Allah gives you glad tidings…* The reading does not change the news; it changes how close you stand to hearing it spoken aloud. Either way, the point stands: the address was specific. It had his name on it.

What this single verb reveals: **the size of your voice does not set the size of the response. Whisper to the One who proclaims.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: يُبَشِّرُكَ — The news that shows on your skin

Now the content of the call. The angels say: **يُبَشِّرُكَ** — *Allah yubashshiruka.* "Allah gives you glad tidings."

Let me teach you the root before we open the word, because English flattens it into something it is not.

The root is **ب-ش-ر**, *b-sh-r.* And here is the thing your translation will never tell you: this same root gives the Arabic word **بَشَرَة** — *basharah,* meaning **the skin.** The outer surface of the human being. The face. The complexion.

The lexicographers — al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* among them — make the connection explicit. *Bushrā,* glad tidings, is so named because **good news is the kind of news that shows on your skin.** It is the news that changes your complexion. When you hear it, the blood rises to your face. Your expression breaks open. The body announces, before the mouth can, that something good has arrived.

*Bushrā* is not information. **It is news that the body cannot hide.**

Now watch the morphology, because it is doing work. The angels do not use the bare Form I verb. They use **Form II — بَشَّرَ, bashshara —** with the doubled middle letter, the *shadda.* In Arabic, that doubling is not decorative. Form II *intensifies.* It takes the meaning and presses on it.

So this is not merely "He informs you of good news." It is closer to: **He floods you with it. He overwhelms your face with it. He makes the joy break across your skin.**

And remember who is receiving this. An old man. Feeble bones. A head "alight with grey." A face, by his own description, written over with age and resignation.

[PAUSE]

And the word chosen for what is about to happen to that face is the word for **news that changes your complexion.** The grey-haired, weathered, resigned face of a man who had folded his longing away — and Allah sends a word designed precisely to make *that* face come alive again.

Notice something. He whispered because he was ashamed of the size of the ask. The response is built from a root that means *you will not be able to keep this off your face.* The shame that made him quiet is answered by a joy that refuses to stay quiet in the body. The whisper goes in; the radiance comes out.

Here is what is remarkable. The same root, *b-sh-r,* is the root from which the Quran also names the human being — **بَشَر,** *bashar* — the human, named *for his skin,* for the very surface that betrays his joy and his grief, that flushes and pales, that cannot keep a secret. To be human, in the deep logic of the language, is to be the creature whose inner state surfaces on the body. And *bushrā* is the news aimed exactly there — at the most human, most uncoverable part of us.

What this word reveals: **Allah's good news is not addressed to your intellect alone. It is addressed to the part of you that cannot pretend. It comes to change your face.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: وَحَصُورًا — When restraint is named as glory

Now the strangest word in the ayah. The angels describe the promised son with four qualities. Listen to them: he will be *muṣaddiq* — one who confirms a Word from Allah; *sayyid* — noble, a master; **ḥaṣūr** — restrained; and *nabiyy* — a prophet, from among the righteous.

And before we land on the strange one, let the first quality open for a moment, because the classical commentators fill it with a name. The *Word from Allah* that Yahya will *confirm* — al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar agree — is **ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.** And ʿĪsā is *called* a *kalima,* a Word, for the reason al-Jalalayn gives plainly: he was **created by the word كُن — *Be.*** A divine command, with no human father, made into a living child. So the very first thing heaven announces about this long-awaited son is that his founding work will be to *testify* to that Word — the forerunner named before the One he runs ahead of, the cousin who will point and say *true.* And the last quality seals the list: *nabiyy mina al-ṣāliḥīn,* a prophet from among the righteous — those who, as al-Muyassar puts it, **reached the very summit of righteousness.** That is the frame. Now look at what heaven planted in the middle of it.

Three of those four are exactly what you would expect in glad tidings about a great son. *He confirms the truth. He is noble. He is a prophet.* These are the things a father longs to hear.

And then, planted right in the middle, is **حَصُورًا — ḥaṣūr.** And it means *restrained. Held back. Withholding.*

Stop and feel how odd this is. You have waited your entire life for a son. The angels arrive to announce him. And among the very first things they tell you about this long-awaited child is — that he *holds himself back?*

Let me teach you the root, because the word's strangeness is its whole gift.

The root is **ح-ص-ر**, *ḥ-ṣ-r.* It means to **confine, to restrain, to enclose, to hold within bounds.** From it comes *ḥiṣār* — a **siege.** *Maḥṣūr* — one who is hemmed in, surrounded, contained. The root is about *limitation* — a wall around a thing, a boundary that holds it in.

And listen to the *sound* of the word. **Ḥaṣūr.** Say it. The **ḥ** is a constriction deep in the throat — you have to narrow the passage to make it. The **ṣ** is a tight, emphatic hiss, the tongue pressed and braced. This is not a soft, open, flowing word. **The word itself is constricted. The mouth that says it has to hold itself back to make the sound.** The phonetics *enact* the meaning. *Ḥaṣūr* sounds like restraint because saying it *is* an act of restraint in the throat.

And the *form* — **فَعُول,** *faʿūl* — is an intensive pattern. It does not mean "one who occasionally restrains." It means **one whose very nature is restraint.** One in whom holding-back is not an effort but a constitution.

Now what is being restrained? Here the classical scholars give us a composite, and I want to give you all of it as layers, not as a menu.

Al-Jalalayn says *ḥaṣūr* here means **mamnūʿ min al-nisā'** — restrained from women, in the sense of being free from the pull of mere appetite. Al-Muyassar broadens it: **lā ya'tī al-dhunūba wa-l-shahawāt al-ḍārra** — one who does not approach sins and harmful desires. Al-Qurtubi gathers the range of the early commentators: some held it meant restraint specifically in the domain of desire; others, restraint from sin altogether. And it is reported in the classical commentary — narrated, not as a ruling but as a description of his purity — that he never committed a sin and never even *inclined* toward one.

Put the layers together and the portrait is not a man who is *incapable.* It is a man who is *sovereign* over himself. *Ḥaṣūr* is not the eunuch and not the weakling. *Ḥaṣūr* is the one who has a wall around his appetites — and **he built the wall, and he holds the gate.**

[PAUSE]

Now look at what sits right next to it. **سَيِّدًا وَحَصُورًا** — *sayyid* and *ḥaṣūr.* Noble *and* restrained. And these two are deliberately paired.

Let me open *sayyid* too, because the pairing is the lesson. *Sayyid* — from the root **س-و-د** — is the **master, the chief, the one to whom people turn.** Al-Jalalayn glosses it tightly as *matbūʿ* — **the one who is followed.** And here is a startling thing about that root: it is the same root family as *sawād,* meaning *the great mass, the multitude, the body of people.* A *sayyid* is, in the deep sense of the word, **the one the multitude gathers around.** The leader. The center of gravity in a crowd.

So the angels name him *sayyid* — the one over others — and in the very same breath *ḥaṣūr* — the one over himself.

[PAUSE]

And there it is. **You are not fit to be master of others until you are master of yourself.** The Quran will not let the two words separate. It puts *sayyid* and *ḥaṣūr* shoulder to shoulder, nobility and restraint, authority over the crowd and authority over the appetite — and it tells us, in the architecture of a single phrase, that the second is the foundation of the first.

What this word reveals: **the Quran lists a man's power to say "no" to himself among his glories. Restraint is not the absence of greatness. In the language of heaven, it is one of its names.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us. **Nādā** — that the answer comes back louder than the asking. **Yubashshiru** — that the good news is aimed at the part of you that cannot pretend, that it comes to change your very face. **Ḥaṣūr** — that among the highest things heaven says about a man is that he holds himself back.

Now let us ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the way prayer works. And about what greatness actually is.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The answer arrives inside the asking

Let me start where you live.

You have prayed for something. Really prayed — not the polite, rounded-off prayers, but the kind you almost don't dare say out loud because saying it out loud would mean admitting how much you want it, and how unlikely it is. Maybe it is a person. Maybe it is healing. Maybe it is a door that has been closed so long you have stopped pushing on it.

And there is a particular loneliness in that kind of prayer. It is the gap. You speak — into what feels like a ceiling, a quiet room, an indifferent sky. And then you *wait.* And the waiting is where faith goes to get tested, because in the silence between the asking and any answer, the mind starts to whisper: *nobody heard that. You are talking to yourself.*

Now look — *really* look — at the structure of this ayah.

> فَنَادَتْهُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ **وَهُوَ قَآئِمٌ يُصَلِّى فِى ٱلْمِحْرَابِ**

"So the angels called to him **while he was standing, praying, in the prayer-niche.**"

That phrase — *while he was standing, praying* — is a circumstantial clause, a **ḥāl,** in the grammar. It pins the timing. And the timing is the miracle.

The answer did **not** come after the prayer. He did not finish, say his closing, walk home, and receive the news a week later. The angels called to him **wa-huwa qā'imun yuṣallī** — *while he was still on his feet. Still in the niche. Still in the act of asking.*

Stay with me, because this dismantles the whole loneliness of the gap.

He thought he was speaking into silence. He thought there was a distance between his whispered *nidā'* and any hearing of it. And the truth — the truth the ayah freezes in place — is that **the answer was already on its way while his lips were still moving.** Heaven did not receive the request and then begin to deliberate. The response overlapped the appeal. The "yes" was running toward him before the "please" had finished leaving his mouth.

[PAUSE]

Notice *where* it found him, too. **فِى ٱلْمِحْرَابِ** — *in the miḥrāb.* The commentators read it plainly: al-Jalalayn glosses it simply as **the sanctuary, the place of worship** — the room where he stood before his Lord. The niche. And here is something the language quietly carries beneath that plain sense: the word *miḥrāb* is connected by the lexicographers to the root **ح-ر-ب,** the root of **ḥarb — war, struggle.** The *miḥrāb* is, in one reading, *the place of the struggle* — the front line, the foremost station, the spot where the real battle of the self is fought. It is no accident that the place a person turns to fight their hardest inner war is also the place where the answer breaks in.

The lesson lands as a question: *where was Zakariyya when the impossible became possible?* He was not out searching for a solution. He was not negotiating with physicians or resigning himself to fate. **He was in the place of prayer, doing the one thing that looked, from the outside, like it would change nothing.** Standing. Asking. Refusing to let the closed door have the last word.

And *that* is where heaven met him. Not after he gave up praying. Not as a reward for some achievement out in the world. **Inside the act itself.** The prayer was not the request you submit and then leave; the prayer was the *room* the answer walked into.

Let me build this one more turn. Why does the body matter — *qā'im,* *standing?* Because the ayah refuses to make this abstract. It does not say "while his heart was turned to God." It says **standing.** On his feet. The whole frail, grey, aged body of him, *upright* before his Lord. The posture of a man who, despite his bones, despite his wife's barrenness, despite every reasonable calculation, **still stood up to ask.**

And here is the universal thing. The standing was not for God's benefit. God did not need Zakariyya vertical to hear him. The standing was for *Zakariyya.* It was the body's way of insisting, against all the evidence, *I have not closed this account. I am still here. I am still asking.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you think the silence *is.* When you pray and hear nothing back, you have been taught to read the silence as absence — as the gap before maybe-an-answer or maybe-not. This ayah re-reads the silence for you. The silence is not the gap before the answer. **The silence may be the answer already in motion, running toward you, not yet arrived at your senses but already dispatched from its source.**

The practical shift is almost embarrassingly simple: **stay standing.** Do not treat prayer as a message you send and then walk away from to wait by the door. Treat it as the place where you remain — because, as this ayah testifies, the answer has a way of arriving *while you are still there asking.* The one who keeps standing in the niche is the one who is present when heaven calls.

### Theme Two: The greatness that is measured by restraint

Now let me start, again, where you actually live.

We are surrounded, every hour, by a single message dressed in a thousand costumes. The message is: **you are what you can acquire, consume, and indulge.** Greatness is the bigger house, the louder life, the appetite that is never told no. Self-denial is for people who *can't* — the ones who restrain themselves because they have no other option. And freedom — *real* freedom — is the freedom to want, and to take, and to have, without a wall in the way.

We have built an entire civilization on the idea that **the unrestrained self is the liberated self.**

And then this ayah lays down four words about a man heaven is honoring — *muṣaddiq, sayyid, ḥaṣūr, nabiyy* — and **right in the center of his glory it places his restraint.** *Ḥaṣūr.* The one who holds himself back. Not as a footnote. Not as a limitation he had to overcome. As one of the **named honors** the angels announce about him before he is even born.

Let that collide with the message you swim in every day.

[PAUSE]

Here is the thing we have inverted. We think the person of appetite is the powerful one and the person of restraint is the deprived one. The Quran sees it exactly backwards — and once you see it the Quran's way, you cannot un-see it.

Who is actually free? The man who *cannot* walk past the thing he craves — is he free? He calls himself free because no one is stopping him. But something *is* stopping him: the craving itself. He is on a leash; he just happens to be the one holding both ends and calling it liberty. He cannot say no. And a person who cannot say no to himself is not a master. **He is a servant who has mistaken his cell for a throne.**

And *ḥaṣūr* — the one with the wall around his appetites, the one who holds the gate — *he* is the free one. Not because he has no desires. Yahya was not a stone; he was a living prophet. But because his desires do not command him. He commands them. The wall is not a prison built around him by someone else. **It is a fortress he built himself, and he keeps the only key.**

Now bring back the pairing, because this is where it becomes practical. **سَيِّدًا وَحَصُورًا.** *Sayyid* and *ḥaṣūr,* in the same breath. The one who leads the multitude, and the one who restrains himself. And we said the order is not accidental — the restraint is the foundation of the nobility.

Think about why. *Why* can you not be trusted with authority over others until you have authority over yourself? Because authority without self-restraint is the most dangerous thing in the world. Give power to a man who cannot say no to his own appetites, and you have not made a leader — **you have armed an appetite.** Every tyrant in history was a man with great power over others and no wall around himself. The two failures are the same failure. *Sayyid* without *ḥaṣūr* is a catastrophe waiting for an opportunity.

This is why heaven announces them together. The Quran is showing us the *order of operations* of a real human being: **first you become master of yourself; only then are you fit to be anything to anyone else.** The wall comes before the throne. Always.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It reframes every small "no" you say to yourself. You have been taught to experience restraint as *loss* — the dessert you didn't eat, the comment you didn't make, the scroll you didn't open, the desire you didn't chase. Subtraction. Deprivation. The lesser life.

This ayah tells you to read those moments as their opposite. **Every time you hold yourself back from something your appetite demands, you are not shrinking. You are doing the single thing the angels named as a glory of a prophet.** You are building the wall. You are taking the key. You are becoming — in the smallest, most repeatable way — a *sayyid* over the one person you were actually put in charge of: yourself.

The practical application is to pick *one* gate this week and be its keeper. One appetite — the phone, the food, the word you want to say but shouldn't, the look you should lower. And when you hold it back, do not grieve the thing you denied yourself. **Name it correctly.** Say: *this is ḥaṣūr. This is the wall going up, brick by brick.* Because heaven counts that. Heaven counted it as a glory worth announcing across the sky to an old man in a niche.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of this ayah turn out to be one truth seen from two directions.

The first half is about **prayer** — the whispered *nidā'* answered by the proclaimed *nidā',* the response that arrives *while he is still standing,* the good news aimed at his very face. It is the story of a man receiving something he could never have produced himself.

The second half is about **restraint** — the son who will be *ḥaṣūr,* master of himself before he is master of others, his self-denial counted among his glories. And note where that son's life will begin: as a *muṣaddiq,* the one who confirms the Word — the first voice to testify to ʿĪsā. Before he holds himself back from anything, his founding act is to bear witness to the truth of another.

And here is how they are the same insight. **The first half is Zakariyya holding nothing back from God. The second half is Yahya holding himself back from everything else.**

The father pours out his whole heart — whispered, ashamed, but *poured out,* standing, refusing to quit asking. He restrains *nothing* before his Lord. And the son's defining glory is that he restrains *everything* before his appetites. Total openness upward; total mastery inward. The two are not opposites — **they are the same posture aimed in two directions.** The self that withholds nothing from God is exactly the self that becomes able to withhold itself from sin. You empty yourself before the One, and that is precisely how you gain the strength to hold the gate against the many.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. When I pray for something and hear nothing back, what do I *believe* the silence is? Have I been reading a dispatched answer as an absent one?

2. Is there a prayer I have stopped saying — not because it was answered, but because I decided I was too old, too far gone, too unlikely? Zakariyya's account was *closed* when he reopened it. What have I closed?

3. When I deny myself something, do I experience it as loss — or as the building of a wall whose only keeper is me?

4. Where in my life do I have *sayyid* without *ḥaṣūr* — authority over others, or wanting it, while having no real authority over myself?

5. What would change if I believed that the answer arrives *inside* the asking — that the place to be is not past the prayer, but in it, still standing?

### The one sentence

**You whisper, ashamed of the size of your asking, into what feels like silence — and the truth is that heaven answers louder than you asked, while you are still standing, with news aimed at your very face; and the greatness it announces is measured not by what a man can seize, but by what he can hold himself back from.**

### The closing invitation

Let something small shift. Not a vow — an invitation.

This week, when you pray, *stay standing* a moment longer than you mean to — as if the answer might arrive before you finish. And once this week, when an appetite pulls and you hold it back, do not call it deprivation. Call it by its real name. Call it *ḥaṣūr* — and know that the same heaven that announced it as a glory to a grey-haired man in a niche is watching you build your wall, brick by quiet brick.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You who answered the whisper with a proclamation — we whisper to You too, ashamed of the size of what we ask. Answer us while we are still standing. Make the good news break across our faces. And teach us the strength of *ḥaṣūr* — to be masters of ourselves before You make us anything to anyone else. *Āmīn.*
