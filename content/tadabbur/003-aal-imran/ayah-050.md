# Confirming Before Changing

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

Let me first give you the words, three ways.

In Arabic:

> [ayah:3:50] وَمُصَدِّقًا لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَىَّ مِنَ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةِ وَلِأُحِلَّ لَكُم بَعْضَ ٱلَّذِى حُرِّمَ عَلَيْكُمْ ۚ وَجِئْتُكُم بِـَٔايَةٍ مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ فَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُونِ

In transliteration:

> *Wa muṣaddiqan limā bayna yadayya mina-t-Tawrāti, wa li-uḥilla lakum baʿḍa-lladhī ḥurrima ʿalaykum. Wa jiʾtukum bi-āyatin min Rabbikum, fattaqu-llāha wa aṭīʿūn.*

And in plain English:

> "And confirming what was before me of the Torah, and so that I may make lawful for you some of what was forbidden to you. And I have come to you with a sign from your Lord — so be conscious of Allah, and obey me."

Now — before we touch a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

Imagine a people who have carried a Law for over a thousand years. The Law of Moses. It is not a book on a shelf to them; it is the air they breathe. It tells them what to eat and what to leave, when to rest, how to atone, how to grieve, how to marry. It is their identity, their boundary, their inheritance. And it is heavy. Over centuries, layers had settled onto it — some of it revelation, some of it the weight of their own history, their own choices, their own hardenings.

And then a man stands before them. A man with no army, no throne, born under a cloud of scandal his mother bore in silence. He heals the blind. He gives life to clay. And he begins to speak about their Law.

[PAUSE]

Now — put yourself inside their fear for a moment. When a new teacher arrives speaking about your most sacred inheritance, there are two things the human heart immediately braces for. *Is he here to erase us? Is he here to tell us that everything our fathers held was wrong, that we begin again at zero, that the thousand years are ashes?*

That is the dread of every people who love what they have received. And it is exactly that dread Jesus reaches for first.

Listen to his opening word. Not *"I have come to abolish."* Not *"I have come to replace."* The very first thing out of his mouth is a word that means: **I am here to certify that what you hold is true.**

Here is the hook I want to leave hanging over this whole reflection. This is a verse about *change* — Jesus is, in fact, about to change something, to lift a prohibition that had bound them. And yet the architecture of the verse spends its opening breath not on the change, but on the *continuity*. Why would a reformer lead with confirmation? Why would the one bringing relief begin by binding himself to the very Law he is about to amend?

Stay with that question. Because the answer reorganizes how you understand mercy itself.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Muṣaddiqan* — The Active Participle of Confirmation

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we open this word, because it changes everything you'll hear in it.

Arabic has a way of describing a person not by an action they *did once*, but by a state they are *continuously embodying*. We call it the active participle. In English we have a faint version of it. There is a difference between saying *"she wrote"* and saying *"she is a writer."* The first is an event. The second is an identity. One happened; the other is what she *is*.

When Jesus describes himself here, he does not say *"I confirmed your Torah"* — a finished act. He uses the participle: **مُصَدِّقًا**, *muṣaddiqan* — "confirming," ongoing, a posture he is standing in as he speaks. It is not something he did on the way over. It is what he *is* as he stands before them.

Now look closer at the verb underneath it. The root is *ṣ-d-q* — صدق — the root of truth, of *ṣidq*, sincerity, the word a true friend is called: *ṣadīq*. But the word here is not the simple Form I "to be true." It is **Form II** — *ṣaddaqa* — and Form II in Arabic does something specific. It takes a meaning and makes it *active, deliberate, declarative*. Form I would be *to be true*. Form II — *ṣaddaqa* — means **to actively declare something true, to certify it, to vouch for it.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the difference. Jesus is not saying, "What you have happens to be correct, and I happen to agree." He is saying something far more deliberate. **I am standing here as your Torah's witness. I vouch for it. I put my own prophethood behind its truth.**

Notice what that does. A weak teacher distinguishes himself from his predecessors to look important. He needs the past to be wrong so that he can be necessary. But here is a prophet whose first move is to *tie himself to* what came before — to make his own credibility rest on the truth of Moses. He does not climb by stepping on the Torah. He climbs by lifting it up.

And here is what's remarkable about the phrase that follows: **لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَىَّ** — *limā bayna yadayya* — literally, *"for what is between my two hands."* That is the Arabic idiom for what comes *before* you, what stands in front of you. But pause on the physical image, because the Quran almost never reaches for the body without reason.

The past — the Torah, a thousand years old — is described not as something *behind* him, receding, fading. It is **between his hands.** In front of him. Present. Close enough to hold. Time, which we always imagine as the past slipping away behind us, is rendered here as something standing *right before his face*, near enough to touch.

And there is a quiet grammatical confirmation of all this. Al-Ṭabarī, working through the syntax, notes that *muṣaddiqan* sits in the accusative as a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial state — bound directly to the words *"I have come to you."* His proof is precisely this first-person phrase, *bayna yadayya*, "between *my* two hands." The confirming is not some detached title hung on Jesus from the outside; it is the very condition in which he *arrives.* He does not come and then, separately, confirm. He comes *confirming.* The witnessing is the walk itself.

What does that do to the relationship? It means the inheritance is not a corpse he has come to bury. It is a living thing he is holding up to the light and saying: *this is true. I have come to confirm it, not to walk away from it.*

What this single word reveals: **before the Quran lets Jesus change anything, it makes him a guardian of what came before.** The reformer is first a witness.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Ḥurrima* and *Uḥilla* — The Tightening and the Opening

Now we arrive at the change itself. And I want you to hear it the way Arabic builds it, because the two halves of this idea are shaped to *sound* like what they mean.

First the prohibition: **حُرِّمَ** — *ḥurrima* — "was forbidden." Say it slowly. Feel where it sits in the mouth. The heavy *ḥā* from deep in the throat, the doubled, rolling *rā* — *ḥur-ri-ma*. It is a constricted, tightened word. It closes. And the meaning matches: this is **Form II** again — *ḥarrama* — the intensive form, *to make strictly, heavily forbidden*. Not a mild "this is not preferred." A wall. A binding. The shape of the word is the shape of a closed door.

Now the relief: **أُحِلَّ** — *uḥilla* — "I make lawful." Hear how it opens. The soft alif, the breath, the flowing *lām* — *u-ḥil-la*. The root *ḥ-l-l* — حلل — is the root of *untying*. A *ḥalāl* thing is, at its root, a thing *unknotted, released, set loose*. The same root gives us the word for unpacking your bags and *settling*, the knot of the journey undone. And this is **Form IV** — *aḥalla* — the form that takes a state and *brings it into being*: to render a thing lawful, to undo the knot.

[PAUSE]

So listen to what Jesus is actually placing side by side. **حُرِّمَ** — the tightening, the binding, the closed throat. **لِأُحِلَّ** — the untying, the opening, the released breath. The verse is doing with sound what it is doing with law: it lets you *hear* a knot being loosened.

And it was not loosened in the abstract. Al-Jalālayn names the concrete shape of the relief: of what had been forbidden them, Jesus made lawful certain *fish and fowl* — and he is specific even here, naming *mā lā ṣīṣata lahu*, the kinds without a spur — particular things that the Law had closed off. So this is not a grand theological gesture floating above daily life; it reaches all the way down to what a family could now place on the table at evening. And one detail matters enormously for what comes next: al-Muyassar is careful to say the loosening came *bi-waḥyin min Allāh* — *by revelation from Allah.* Jesus is not editing the Law by his own judgment, lifting what he personally found heavy. He unties only what *God* directs him to untie. Al-Muyassar then gives the loosening its proper name — *takhfīfan min Allāh wa raḥmatan*: **a lightening from Allah, and a mercy.** Not a concession wrung out of the Law, but mercy descending *into* it.

But now — read the absence. Because there are two silences in this half of the verse, and both of them speak.

**The first silence: who forbade it?** Look at *ḥurrima* — "was forbidden." It is **passive.** Agentless. The Quran does not say "what *I* forbade," or even, here, "what *Allah* forbade upon you." It simply says: *what was forbidden upon you.* The hand that closed the door is left unnamed.

Why leave it blank? The classical commentators sat with exactly this. Al-Qurtubī reports a reading carried in the tradition: that certain things were forbidden to the Children of Israel **as a consequence of their own transgressions** — that the prohibition was not in the original Torah as pure burden, but had settled onto them through what their own hands had earned. (Other scholars read it differently, and we should be honest that there is *ikhtilāf* here — a genuine difference among the mufassirūn about which prohibitions are meant.) But hold that first reading for a moment, because the agentless passive *invites* it. If part of what bound them was the weight of their own history, then the silence is a mercy: the verse does not stand there and say *"this is the burden you brought on yourselves."* It simply says *it was forbidden* — and then moves immediately to lifting it.

**The second silence — and this is the one I don't want you to miss — is the word بَعْضَ, *baʿḍ*: "some."**

*"To make lawful for you **some** of what was forbidden."*

Not *all*. Some. A measured portion.

[PAUSE]

Now — there is a real scholarly conversation about this word, and I want to give it to you honestly as a single picture rather than a menu. Al-Baghawī reports that Abū ʿUbayda read *baʿḍ* here as actually meaning *kull* — "all" — citing the Arabs' habit of saying "some" while meaning the whole, and he quotes a line of the poet Labīd to prove the usage. So one camera angle says: the relief was total. But the dominant, plain reading — the one al-Jalālayn and al-Qurtubī carry — takes *baʿḍ* at its word: **some.** A part. Jesus lifts a portion of the burden, and *leaves the rest standing.*

And I want to dwell on that plain reading, because look at what it guards.

If Jesus had said *"I make lawful for you everything that was forbidden,"* he would have contradicted his own opening word. He had just declared himself *muṣaddiqan* — the one *confirming* the Torah. You cannot confirm a Law in one breath and dissolve it entirely in the next. The little word *baʿḍ* — "some" — is what holds the two halves of the verse together. **The confirmation is preserved precisely because the relief is measured.** He honors the structure by lifting only part of the load, not by tearing the whole thing down.

What these two words reveal together: **true mercy is not the abolition of all limits. It is the precise, knowing loosening of the one knot that has become a wound — while the rest of the Law stands honored and intact.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The *Fā* of Consequence — *Fattaqu-llāha wa aṭīʿūn*

Now watch how the whole verse lands. Everything we have walked through — the confirming, the lifting of some of the burden, the sign from the Lord — all of it pours into one small connective particle and two commands.

The particle is the **فَ** — the *fā* — at the front of *fattaqū*. In Arabic this *fā* is not just "and." It is the *fā* of **consequence**: *"so," "and therefore."* It tells you that everything before it was the *reason*, and what comes after it is the *response that reason demands.*

So the structure of the entire verse, laid bare, is an argument:

*Because* I confirm your Torah — *because* I have lifted some of your burden — *because* I have come with a sign from your Lord — **therefore**: be conscious of Allah, and obey me.

And al-Jalālayn catches something easy to miss in that third clause. *"And I have come to you with a sign from your Lord"* — Jesus had *already* said almost exactly this one verse earlier (3:49). Why say it again? Al-Jalālayn answers: he repeats it deliberately — *karrarahu taʾkīdan* — for emphasis, *and* as the very foundation on which to build (*li-yabniya ʿalayhi*) the command that follows. The repetition is not redundancy; it is the laying of a cornerstone. He restates the authority precisely so that the *fā* of consequence has something solid to rest its weight upon. *Because* the sign is from your Lord — and I am reminding you of that on purpose — *therefore* protect yourselves, and follow.

And al-Muyassar adds a quiet glint to what that sign actually *is.* He glosses *bi-āyatin min Rabbikum* as *bi-ḥujjatin min Rabbikum ʿalā ṣidqi mā aqūlu lakum* — a *proof* from your Lord, *of the truthfulness of what I am telling you.* Now hear the root come full circle. The verse opened with **مُصَدِّقًا**, *muṣaddiqan*, from *ṣ-d-q* — the one who *vouches for the truth* of the Torah. And its closing evidence is a sign that, in turn, vouches for *his own ṣidq.* The witness to the past arrives carrying his own credential of truth. The bookends of the verse — *I confirm the truth* at the start, *here is the proof of my truthfulness* at the end — are cut from a single root. Confirmation runs the whole length of it.

Look at the two commands he chooses. First: **ٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ** — *ittaqu-llāh.* The root is *w-q-y* — وقي — *to guard, to shield, to protect oneself.* And it is **Form VIII**, the reflexive form: the action turns back on the doer. *Taqwā* is not "fear" in the sense of cowering. At its root it is **the act of placing a shield between yourself and harm** — wrapping yourself in a protecting awareness of God. So his first command is not "tremble." It is: *protect yourselves — by living in awareness of the One who sent me.*

And only *then*, the second: **وَأَطِيعُونِ** — *wa aṭīʿūn* — "and obey me." Notice the order, and notice it is deliberate. He does not begin with *"obey me."* He begins with *"be conscious of Allah."* He anchors the obedience owed to *him* in the consciousness owed to *God* — so that following the messenger is never detached from, and never competes with, the worship of the One who sent him. The taqwā is the soil; the obedience grows from it.

And the commentators are careful to say *what* this obedience is obedience *to.* It is not a blank claim on their persons. Al-Muyassar reads it as *"obey me in what I convey to you from Allah"* — obedience bounded by the message, not the man. And al-Jalālayn specifies its content even further: obey me *fīmā āmurukum bihi min tawḥīd Allāh wa ṭāʿatihi* — in what I command you of *the Oneness of Allah* and His obedience. So the messenger's whole claim resolves, in the end, back into God: follow me — *into the worship of the One who sent me.* The arrow of obedience never stops at Jesus. It passes through him and lands on tawḥīd.

There is even a small, tender thing in the last word's shape. *Aṭīʿūni* — "obey me" — has had its final *yā* clipped away in the Uthmani script: **وَأَطِيعُونِ**, ending on the *kasra*, the soft *-ni*, the trace of the dropped "me." The word almost leans forward, unfinished, reaching toward the listener. It does not slam shut. It calls.

[PAUSE]

What this final movement reveals: **the verse is not a list of facts about Jesus. It is a case being built — and every claim is evidence marshaled toward one verdict: live in awareness of God, and follow the one He sent.**

### Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. A participle that makes Jesus a *witness* to the past before he is a reformer of it. A pair of words — one constricted, one opened — that let you *hear* a burden being untied, guarded by a single word, *"some,"* that keeps the loosening from becoming a demolition. And a *fā* of consequence that gathers all of it into a single plea: *protect yourselves, and follow.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the burdens you carry, and about what real reform actually looks like.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Burden You Did Not Choose, and the Mercy That Knows Which Knot to Untie

Let me start where you already live.

Every one of us is carrying something we did not pack. You inherited a body with its weaknesses. You inherited a family with its patterns — the silence at the dinner table, the temper that runs in the blood, the particular way your people worry about money or shame or each other. You inherited a religion, sometimes practiced beautifully, sometimes practiced as a list of "don'ts" handed down with more fear than understanding. And much of what binds you tightest was not chosen by you at all. It settled onto you, layer by layer, before you were old enough to consent.

And here is the thing about an inherited burden. After a while, you can no longer tell which part of it is *real* — a true limit, a genuine boundary that protects you — and which part is just *weight*: a knot tied long ago, by someone's fear or someone's sin, that everyone simply kept re-tying because no one knew how to undo it.

That is the human condition that this verse walks straight into. The Children of Israel were carrying a Law, and woven through it — according to that reading al-Qurtubī preserves — were prohibitions that had settled onto them *through their own history*, through what their own choices had earned. They could no longer separate the protecting limit from the inherited wound. It had all become, simply, *the way things are.*

And into that comes a mercy that does something very particular. Watch it carefully, because this is the whole theme.

It does not say: *throw it all off.* The voice that says "every limit is oppression, every inheritance is a cage, burn it down and be free" — that is not the voice of Jesus in this verse. He *confirms* the Law. He stands as its witness.

And it does not say: *carry all of it forever, every knot, no matter how it cuts.* That is not his voice either. He reaches in — *li-uḥilla*, "so that I may untie" — and he loosens **a measured portion**. *Baʿḍ.* Some.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the precision of that? The mercy here is not a flood that washes away the riverbanks. It is a surgeon's hand that knows *exactly which knot has become a wound* and loosens that one — while leaving intact every limit that was actually holding you together.

This is the mercy most of us never learned to expect. We think our only two options are *bear everything* or *break free of everything*. We think faith is either total submission to every weight we inherited, or total rejection of the whole inheritance. And the verse stands between those two and says: **no. The work is discernment.** Some of what binds you is true and protective — keep it, honor it, it is confirmed. And some of it is a knot that sin or history tied, and the Most Merciful sent His messengers, in part, *to untie that.*

Now bring it home to your own life, because this is not only history. When you sit with your own inheritance — your family's patterns, your community's unspoken rules, the version of religion you were handed — you are allowed to ask the Jesus-question of it: *which part of this is the confirmed Torah, the real limit that guards me — and which part is baʿḍu-lladhī ḥurrima, a knot tied by someone's fear that I have been carrying as if it were from God?*

That is not rebellion. Notice — Jesus does the loosening *as a prophet, on God's authority,* "with a sign from your Lord." So the discernment is not yours to do by appetite, tearing off whatever chafes. It is done in *taqwā* — in awareness of God, by His guidance, through what He revealed. **The relief and the reverence arrive together, or they are not from Him.**

What this changes about tomorrow: the next time a weight you have always carried starts to feel like it might be cutting into you, do not reach immediately for either *"I must just endure it"* or *"I'll throw the whole thing off."* Sit with it in taqwā. Take it to the revealed sources, to people of knowledge. Ask which knot is real and which is inherited. The God who sent a prophet specifically *to untie some of the burden* is not a God who wants you crushed under weights He never tied.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Reformer Who Bows Before He Builds

Now let me take you to the second thing this verse quietly teaches — and it speaks to something deep in the modern temperament.

We live in an age in love with rupture. The startup that promises to *disrupt* an entire industry. The thinker who builds a reputation by declaring everything before him obsolete. The reformer whose first move is always to tell you that the past was darkness and that enlightenment begins with *him*. We have been trained to believe that the way to be important is to be *new* — to distinguish yourself by what you reject.

And there is a thrill in it, isn't there? It feels powerful to tear down. It feels like clarity. The young heart especially — and many of us never stop being that young heart — wants to *clear the table* and start over, certain that the old ways were the problem and that we, finally, see clearly.

Now look at Jesus.

He is, by any honest measure, one of the great transformative figures in the history of the world. He performs miracles that no one before him performed. He brings a relief, a lifting of burden, that the Law of Moses did not contain. If anyone had grounds to walk in and say *"forget what you were taught, everything begins now with me,"* it was him.

And what does the Quran put in his mouth as the *first* word of his mission?

**مُصَدِّقًا.** *Confirming.* I am the witness to what came before me. I vouch for your Torah. I tie my own truth to the truth of Moses.

[PAUSE]

Stop and feel how counterintuitive that is. The most powerful reformer leads not with *"you were wrong"* but with *"what you hold is true, and I am here to honor it."* He bows to the inheritance *before* he touches it. He establishes himself as its servant before he acts as its physician.

And here is why that order is not just humility — it is *the very thing that makes real change possible.* Think about it structurally. Only the one who has *confirmed* the Torah has the standing to *adjust* it. If Jesus had walked in tearing it down, every loosening he offered would have been just another opinion, another man's preference against a thousand years. But because he first stood as the Law's witness — because he placed his prophethood *behind* it — the relief he then brings carries weight. The confirmation is what *earns* the change. The bow is what makes the building trustworthy.

The disrupter who rejects everything has no authority to fix anything, because he has shown he does not understand what he is touching. He sees only weight where the wise see structure. But the one who first says *"this is true, this is precious, I am its servant"* — *he* is the only one you can trust to then say *"and this one knot, here, has become a wound; let me untie it."* He has proven he can tell the difference between the load-bearing wall and the cobweb.

[PAUSE]

Now turn this on your own life — because most of us are reformers of *something.* You want to change your family's patterns. You want to fix the masjid, the organization, the marriage, the way your community does things. You have seen what is broken and you are *right* about a lot of it.

The verse asks you: what is your *first* word? Do you arrive as the disrupter — *"everything you've done is wrong, I see clearly, step aside"*? Or do you arrive the way Jesus arrived — *"what is good here, I confirm. What our elders built that was true, I honor. I am its servant. And* then*, on that ground, let me show you the one knot that has become a wound"*?

One of those gets you exiled, dismissed as arrogant, your real insights buried under everyone's defensiveness. The other — the harder, slower, more reverent path — is the only one that actually heals anything.

What this changes about tomorrow: before you reform the thing you are sure needs reforming, find your *muṣaddiqan* first. Name out loud what is *true* and *good* in what you are about to change. Bow to the inheritance before you build on it. Not as a tactic — as the truth. Because you cannot rightly untie a knot in a rope whose strength you never respected.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have walked through all of it, step back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with extraordinary balance:

> **Confirming** what was before me of the Torah *(the bow to the past)*
> **and to make lawful some** of what was forbidden *(the measured change)*
> **and I have come with a sign from your Lord** *(the authority)*
> **so be conscious of Allah and obey me** *(the verdict)*

See how it moves. It begins by anchoring backward — *confirming.* Then it makes its forward motion — *but only some,* a change measured precisely so it never breaks the anchor. Then it grounds both the confirming and the changing in something higher than Jesus himself — *a sign from your Lord.* And only then, with all the evidence laid, does it ask for the response: *taqwā,* then obedience. **Continuity, then mercy, then authority, then the call.** Nothing is out of place.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight seen from two directions.

The grammar showed us a participle of confirmation guarding a measured loosening — *muṣaddiqan* holding hands with *baʿḍ.* And the themes showed us why: that **real mercy and real reform are the same act** — the precise loosening of the one knot that wounds, performed only by the one who first honored the whole rope. The God who sends a prophet to untie *some* of your burden is the same God who has that prophet *confirm* the rest. He does not crush you, and He does not abandon the structure that holds you up. He discerns. And He asks you to learn that discernment too — in awareness of Him.

### Questions to Carry

- What am I carrying right now that I have assumed is "from God" — but might be *baʿḍu-lladhī ḥurrima*, a knot tied by someone's fear or history that I never had to keep re-tying?
- When I want to change something — in my family, my community, myself — what is my *first* word: rejection, or confirmation? Do I bow before I build?
- Where in my life am I demanding *total* freedom from limits, when what I actually need is the discernment to loosen one wound and keep the rest?
- Have I anchored my reverence for any teacher, scholar, or guide in *taqwā* of Allah first — the way Jesus ordered it — so that following them never competes with worshipping Him?
- Can I tell the difference, in my own inherited religion, between the load-bearing wall and the cobweb?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Jesus comes not to overturn but to confirm — and within that faithful confirmation, to untie a measured portion of the burden — so that honoring what came before and being relieved of what crushed you turn out to be the same mercy from the same Lord.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what might shift. Stop believing that your only choices are to bear every weight or break free of everything. There is a third way, and it is the way of the prophets: to stand as a witness to what is true, to revere the structure that holds you, *and* — on that very ground, in awareness of God — to let the Most Merciful show you which single knot has become a wound, and loosen it. Honor the inheritance. Then let it be healed. Both. Together. From Him.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who sent Your messengers not to crush us but to confirm what is true and untie what wounds us — give us the discernment of those who can tell the load-bearing limit from the inherited knot. Make us witnesses to the good before we are reformers of the broken, and let every relief we seek be sought in awareness of You and obedience to what You revealed. Loosen from us the burdens we were never meant to carry, and keep firm in us every limit that guards us. Āmīn.*
