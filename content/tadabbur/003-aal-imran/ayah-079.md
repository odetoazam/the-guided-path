# Be of the Lord, Not Slaves of Me
### Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 79

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with the sound of them before we open a single one.

> **مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ أَن يُؤْتِيَهُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَٱلْحُكْمَ وَٱلنُّبُوَّةَ ثُمَّ يَقُولَ لِلنَّاسِ كُونُوا۟ عِبَادًا لِّى مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ وَلَٰكِن كُونُوا۟ رَبَّٰنِيِّۧنَ بِمَا كُنتُمْ تُعَلِّمُونَ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَبِمَا كُنتُمْ تَدْرُسُونَ**

*Mā kāna li-basharin an yu'tiyahu-llāhu-l-kitāba wal-ḥukma wan-nubuwwata thumma yaqūla lin-nāsi kūnū ʿibādan lī min dūni-llāhi wa-lākin kūnū rabbāniyyīna bimā kuntum tuʿallimūna-l-kitāba wa-bimā kuntum tadrusūn.*

"It is not for any human being that Allah should give him the Scripture, and the authority of judgment, and prophethood — and then he would turn to people and say, 'Be slaves to me, rather than to Allah.' Rather he would say, 'Be people of the Lord, because of what you have been teaching of the Scripture, and because of what you have been studying.'"

### Rebuild the world

To feel why this verse was sent, you have to stand in a particular room.

A delegation of Christians from Najran — a city in the south of the Arabian Peninsula, learned, dignified, devout — has come to Madinah to debate the Prophet ﷺ about the nature of Jesus, son of Mary, peace be upon him. This whole stretch of Surah Aal-Imran is that conversation. And in the heat of it, a claim surfaces — preserved in the commentary of al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi — that Jesus himself had told his followers to take him as a lord.

And there is a second tradition, reported by al-Jalālayn, woven into the same moment: that some among the believers, overwhelmed with love for the Messenger ﷺ, asked whether they might prostrate to him the way other nations bowed to their prophets and great ones.

Notice — and I want you to hold this — the classical sources do **not** agree on a single occasion here. Some say the verse answers the Christians about Jesus. Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿAṭāʾ, as al-Baghawī reports, say it answers a moment concerning Muhammad ﷺ himself. We will not flatten that disagreement, because the disagreement is itself a gift: it tells us this verse is not about one prophet. It is about **the office of prophethood as such**. About what it is impossible for any true messenger — Jesus, Muhammad, any of them — to ever do.

So here is the room. Sincere people, on the edge of love tipping over into worship. A teacher so luminous that his students want to make him the destination instead of the door.

### The Hook

Now watch the verse do something strange.

When it describes the false call — the thing a true prophet would *never* say — it gives us the words in full: *"Be slaves to me."*

But when it gives us what a true prophet **does** say, the words shift. He does not say the obvious thing. He does not say, "Be slaves to Allah instead of me." He reaches for a completely different word — a rare, dense, almost untranslatable word — **rabbāniyyīn**.

Why the shift? Why, at the exact moment you'd expect a clean mirror — *not slaves of me, but slaves of God* — does the verse refuse the mirror and hand you something else entirely?

[PAUSE]

That refusal is the whole verse. Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Mā kāna* — the door that was never there

Before we touch the Arabic, let me teach you a small structure in our own speech.

There is a difference between saying "he didn't do it" and saying "it is not in him to do it." The first reports an event. The second denies a *possibility* — it says the thing is foreign to his very nature. If I tell you, "A mother — it is not in her to abandon her newborn in the cold," I haven't told you about one mother on one night. I've told you something about what motherhood *is*. I've closed a door and shown you it was never really a door.

Now the verse. It opens: **مَا كَانَ لِبَشَرٍ** — *mā kāna li-basharin*.

The word كَانَ (*kāna*) is the verb "to be," in the perfect tense — "it was." With the negation مَا (*mā*) in front of it, and the little preposition لِ (*li*, "for"), you get a fixed Arabic construction. And the classical scholars are explicit about what it means. Al-Qurṭubī stops directly on it: **مَا كَانَ مَعْنَاهُ مَا يَنْبَغِي** — *"'It was not' here means: it is not befitting, it cannot rightly be."* Al-Ṭabarī lands on the very same gloss — *mā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min al-bashar*, "it is not fitting for any human being at all."

[PAUSE]

Let that land. The verse does not say, "No prophet has happened to do this." It says something far heavier: **it is not in the nature of the thing for it to happen at all.** A human being whom Allah has loaded with Scripture, and judgment, and prophethood, turning around to claim worship — that is not a path a prophet declined to walk. It is a path that does not exist for him.

And al-Qurṭubī adds the proof, because Allah uses this exact construction elsewhere for things that are categorically impossible: *"It is not for Allah to take a son"* — مَا كَانَ لِلَّهِ أَن يَتَّخِذَ مِن وَلَدٍ. Same *mā kāna*. Same closed door. You don't say that about a choice God happened not to make. You say it about something that contradicts what He *is*.

So the very first two words have already told you: the more genuine the prophet, the more impossible the self-worship. The gifts and the humility are not in tension. They rise together.

And pause on the middle of those three gifts, because the classical readings spread out across it in a way worth seeing. The verse says Allah gives him **ٱلْكِتَٰب** (the Scripture), then **ٱلْحُكْم** (*al-ḥukm*), then **ٱلنُّبُوَّة** (prophethood). That middle word, often flattened to "judgment," is read three ways by the commentators — not in contradiction but in widening circles. Al-Ṭabarī glosses it as *faṣl al-ḥikma*: the decisive discernment of wisdom, the power to cut cleanly to what is right. Al-Jalālayn reads it as *al-fahm li-l-sharīʿa*: a deep understanding of the sacred law. Al-Muyassar takes it furthest — to being *made an arbiter among God's creation*, one whose word settles disputes between people. Hold all three together and *al-ḥukm* is the gift of God-given clarity: to see truly, to understand the law, to judge between souls. And mark this: it is precisely the gift that most flatters a human ego — the power to be *right*, to *decide*, to be the one others come to — that the verse insists a true carrier still never converts into a throne.

Notice the word the verse chooses for "human being": **بَشَر** — *bashar*. Not *rajul* (a man), not a name. *Bashar* — the raw category, skin and humanity, the most general word for our kind. Al-Qurṭubī notes it covers the one and the many alike, and al-Ṭabarī sharpens the grammar of it: *bashar* is a collective name for the children of Adam with no singular drawn from its own letters — like *qawm* (a people) or *khalq* (creation) — and yet it can also name a single individual. The verse is deliberately speaking at the widest possible altitude. Whatever divine gift a *human* carries, the human stays a human. The Scripture passes through him; it does not promote him out of his own skin.

One clear statement, then we move: **The opening words don't describe a prophet's good behavior. They describe the impossibility of his bad behavior — and locate that impossibility in the very nature of being a true messenger.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Thumma* — the hinge where the soul could turn

Here is a word so small you would read past it. **ثُمَّ** — *thumma*. "Then."

Teach yourself its weight first, in plain terms. In Arabic, there are different words for "and then." There is فَ (*fa*), which means immediately, smoothly, no gap. And there is ثُمَّ (*thumma*), which carries *distance* — a "then" with space inside it. A sequence with a pause, a separation, sometimes the sense of "and after all that…"

Now feel where the verse places it.

*"…that Allah should give him the Scripture, and the authority of judgment, and prophethood — **thumma** — he would say to people, 'Be slaves to me.'"*

The *thumma* sits in the gap between **receiving everything from God** and **claiming everything for yourself**. And the choice of *thumma* over *fa* is doing quiet, devastating work. It says: this corruption, if it ever came, would not come in the instant of receiving the gift. It would come *after*. After the Scripture had settled. After the people had gathered. After the authority had grown comfortable. *Thumma* is the long, slow distance in which a heart could, in principle, curdle — turning the gift it was handed into a throne it sits on.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it is precisely how the danger works in real life. No one is handed a pulpit and worships themselves on day one. The drift happens in the *thumma* — in the long after, where the gratitude slowly gets re-labeled as entitlement, where "God gave me this" quietly becomes "I have this." The verse names the exact stretch of road where a soul gets lost, and then tells you: **for a true prophet, even across that whole distance, the turn never comes.** He walks the entire length of *thumma* and arrives still pointing away from himself.

What this single particle reveals: the test of sincerity is not the moment of receiving. It is everything that comes *after* receiving — and the genuine carrier of God's gift passes that long test without ever converting trust into a claim.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Rabbāniyyīn* — the word that breaks the mirror

Now we return to the hook. The asymmetry. And this is the heart of the verse.

Look at the two calls side by side — the false one the verse forbids, and the true one it commands:

- The false call, in full: **كُونُوا۟ عِبَادًا لِّى مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ** — *"Be slaves to me, rather than to Allah."*
- The true call, in full: **وَلَٰكِن كُونُوا۟ رَبَّٰنِيِّۧنَ** — *"Rather, be **rabbāniyyīn**."*

Your ear expects symmetry. *Not slaves of me — but slaves of God.* That would be the clean correction. The verse sets up the mirror… and then shatters it. It does not say *kūnū ʿibādan lillāh* (be slaves to God). It says *kūnū rabbāniyyīn*. A different word. A different *world*.

Let's open it.

**ʿibād** (*ʿibādan*) comes from the root ع-ب-د (*ʿabada*) — to serve, to be a slave, to worship. It is a magnificent and true word; the highest title the Qur'an gives the Prophet ﷺ is *ʿabduhu*, "His slave." So why is it *not* the word here? Because in this sentence, the false teacher has just used it for himself — *ʿibādan lī*, slaves *to me*. The word *ʿabd* in this construction points to an **owner you belong to**. And the verse, with surgical care, will not let the true call be a mere swap of owners — me out, God in — as if the human heart were a deed of property simply changing hands.

Instead it reaches for **رَبَّانِيِّين** — *rabbāniyyīn*.

This word is built on the root ر-ب-ب (*rabb*) — Lord, Master, and crucially, **Nurturer**. The same root gives us *tarbiya*: to raise, to nurture, to bring something from a seed to its completion stage by stage, with patience. A *rabb* is not just an owner. A *rabb* is the one who *grows* you.

And then look at the form of the word. To the root *rabb*, the Arabic has added an alif and a nūn — the ـان ending — and a relational yāʾ. Al-Jalālayn pauses precisely here and tells us *why*: **بِزِيَادَةِ أَلِفٍ وَنُونٍ تَفْخِيمًا** — *"with the addition of alif and nūn, for magnification — tafkhīm."* That added shape is not decoration. In Arabic, this kind of extension intensifies and exalts the meaning. So *rabbānī* is not merely "one who belongs to the Lord." It is something like **one who is utterly of the Lord — saturated with Him, nurtured by Him, devoted to Him, shaped by Him through and through.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel the genius of the broken mirror.

The false teacher says: *be slaves to me* — be **possessions**, objects pointed at his ego. If the verse had answered *be slaves to God*, the human being would still have been described as a possession; only the address would have changed. But that is not the dignity Allah wants to hand His servants here. So instead of saying "transfer your slavehood to God," He says: **become rabbāniyyīn — become people the Lord Himself raises.** Not merely owned by Him. *Cultivated* by Him. In a relationship of nurture, not just title.

The false call reduces you. The true call *grows* you. That is why the mirror had to break. A perfect grammatical mirror would have flattered the symmetry of the sentence and betrayed the meaning of the rescue. The drift toward a human master shrinks a person into an object. The return to God is not a smaller cage with a better owner — it is the *Rabb* who takes a seed and brings it to a tree.

And the gathered classical readings give us the full portrait of who a *rabbānī* is — not as competing options, but as facets of one figure. Al-Jalālayn: **عُلَمَاءُ عَامِلُونَ** — *scholars who act on what they know*. Al-Muyassar: *the wise, the deeply understanding, the learned* — ḥukamāʾ, fuqahāʾ, ʿulamāʾ. Put the angles together and one image stands up: **a person whose knowledge has become character, whose learning has been nurtured all the way into who they are.** Not a head full of Scripture. A self remade by it.

What this one word reveals: **the alternative to worshipping a human is not merely worshipping God *instead* — it is being grown by God into someone whole.** The verse refuses to let your rescue from one master be anything less than your cultivation by the true one.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So the words have shown us three things. *Mā kāna* — that self-worship is not declined by a true prophet but impossible for him. *Thumma* — that the danger lives in the long *after*, and he survives the whole of it. And *rabbāniyyīn* — that the rescue God offers isn't a change of owners but a transformation of the self.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the people who teach us, and about what knowledge is actually *for*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The teacher who is a door, not a destination

Let me start where you already live.

You have, at some point, fallen under the spell of a teacher. Maybe a professor, a coach, a shaykh, a mentor, an author whose voice got into your head. There is a particular feeling when someone opens a door in your mind that you couldn't open yourself. Gratitude, yes — but something more dangerous mixed in. A pull toward the *person*. You start quoting them constantly. You want their approval more than you want the truth they pointed at. Slowly, almost invisibly, the teacher stops being the window you look *through* and becomes the wall you look *at*.

This is the oldest trap in spiritual life, and our age has industrialized it. We call it the cult of personality. The guru with the merchandise. The influencer whose followers defend him past all reason. The movement that cannot survive the death of its founder, because the founder was never a door — he had made himself the destination.

Now bring the verse.

Allah is describing the one human being most entitled, by every earthly logic, to be made a destination. A *prophet*. Someone carrying Scripture from the sky, the authority to judge, the seal of God's selection. If anyone could justify saying "look at me," it is this person. And the verse says: **it is not even in the nature of the thing.** The fuller the hands God fills, the more emphatically the true carrier points past himself.

Here is the movement I want you to feel: **the gifts that would tempt a fraud to claim worship are the very gifts a true servant uses to redirect it.** The Scripture, the judgment, the prophethood — the false self would hoard them as evidence of its own greatness. The *rabbānī* spends them as arrows, all pointing the same direction: away from me, toward the One who gave them.

This is exactly how Ibn Kathīr frames the whole passage: no prophet ever called people to worship him, or to worship anything other than Allah. And the very next verse shuts the last loophole — it adds that a prophet would never even *command you to take the angels and the prophets as lords*. Watch how complete that is. Not "don't worship the fraud" only; not even "don't worship *this* prophet." The door is barred at every hinge — not the messenger, not the angels, not the righteous dead. Every figure bright enough to tempt your devotion is named and refused. The principle is not about one suspicious teacher; it is a wall built around the human heart on every side that worship could leak out.

[PAUSE]

And now turn the verse around so it faces *you*, because most of us will never lead nations — but every one of us teaches *something* to *someone*. You are a parent. An older sibling. A manager. A friend people come to for advice. The one in the family who knows the deen a little better. The moment you have something others lack, the *thumma* opens beneath your feet — the long after, where being needed becomes being adored, where helping people quietly becomes wanting people to need you.

The verse hands you the diagnostic question. Whatever you've been given — knowledge, money, beauty, influence, a platform of any size — ask: **am I making people more attached to me, or more attached to God through me?** Does time near me leave others more dependent, or more free? A door or a destination?

The practical application is almost brutally concrete: the next time someone praises you for something true, practice the prophetic reflex *in that exact second* — turn the praise upward before it can pool in your chest. Not as performance. As survival. Because the verse just told you the drift happens in the *after*, and the only people who survive the after are the ones who built the reflex of redirection while the gift was still small.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Knowledge that grows you — or knowledge that just sits

Now the second depth, and it lives in the tail of the verse, the part we haven't fully opened.

*"…be rabbāniyyīn, **because of what you have been teaching of the Scripture, and because of what you have been studying.**"*

Start, again, where you live. You are drowning in information. You have read more, watched more, saved more, bookmarked more than any generation in human history. You can summon a lecture on any topic in seconds. And yet — be honest — there is a strange gap. The knowing and the becoming have come apart. You *know* you should be more patient, more grateful, more disciplined, kinder to your family. You have *heard* it a hundred times. And the knowing sits there, on a shelf in the mind, while the life goes on unchanged. Information has become a thing we *collect* rather than a thing that *changes* us.

The Qur'an has a word for the gap, and a cure for it, right here.

Look at the two verbs in the tail. **تُعَلِّمُونَ** (*tuʿallimūn*) — you teach — and **تَدْرُسُونَ** (*tadrusūn*) — you study. And look at the frame around them: **بِمَا كُنتُمْ** — *bimā kuntum* — "because of what you *have been* [doing]." That little construction, *kuntum* plus the ongoing verb, is the Arabic of **sustained, habitual action** — not a thing you did once, but a thing you have been doing, continuously, as a way of life.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the structure is telling you. The verse does not say, "Be rabbāniyyīn — now go learn." It says you *become* rabbānī **bimā kuntum** — *through* the long habit of teaching and studying. The transformation is not a separate step you take after the knowledge. It is what the knowledge *does to you* when you stay with it, return to it, hand it on, and sit with it again — for years.

And al-Muyassar opens the two verbs out into their living parts, so you can see the workshop the *rabbānī* is grown in. To *study* (*tadrusūn*), in his reading, is three things at once — to commit to memory (*ḥifẓ*), to grasp with knowledge (*ʿilm*), and to penetrate to understanding (*fiqh*) — not a single glance but a descent through layers. And to *teach* (*tuʿallimūn*) is to pass on *what God revealed* — not your own cleverness, but His words moving through you to others. Memorize, understand, hand on; return, and do it again. That is the slow apparatus by which a Book becomes a person.

And notice the pairing the verse refuses to separate: *teaching* and *studying*, side by side, both in the continuous form. One of the documented readings even shades the first verb toward *taʿlamūn* — "you *know*" — so the gathered sense holds knowing, teaching, and studying in one breath. The point of the cluster is that real knowledge is not a tank you fill once and draw from forever. It is a current. You study so you can teach; you teach and discover you must study again; and somewhere in that loop, repeated across years, the Scripture stops being something you *have* and becomes something you *are*.

That is the difference between the two words the verse set against each other. *ʿAbd* of a false master is a static thing — a possession sitting in a ledger. *Rabbānī* is a grown thing — the end of a long *tarbiya*, a slow cultivation by the Lord, carried out precisely *through* the sustained labor of learning and giving. The knowledge was the soil. The *rabbānī* is the tree.

[PAUSE]

So here is the practical turn, and it is gentle but demanding. Stop measuring your knowledge by how much you have *gathered*. Start measuring it by how much it has *changed* you. The test of a thing you learned is not "can I recall it" but "has it moved into my hands, my tongue, my patience, my prayer." And the mechanism the verse gives you is not mysterious: **stay in the loop.** Take one thing you truly know to be true — one ayah, one teaching — and instead of adding a tenth new thing on top of it, *return* to it. Study it again. Teach it to one person. Live it for a week. That returning, sustained over time, is the *bimā kuntum* — the long habit out of which a *rabbānī* is grown.

Knowledge that only sits makes you a library. Knowledge you keep teaching and re-studying makes you *of the Lord*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and see how the verse is built, because the shape is the argument.

It opens by **closing a door** — *mā kāna li-basharin* — it is not in the nature of a true prophet to claim worship. Then it walks you across the dangerous distance — *thumma* — the long after where a lesser soul would turn. It names the false call in full — *be slaves to me* — and then, at the pivot, it **refuses the mirror**: not "be slaves to God," but *be rabbāniyyīn*. And finally it tells you the *means* by which a rabbānī is made — *the sustained teaching and studying of the Scripture.*

See the line it draws? The verse begins at the top — the prophet, the highest carrier of knowledge — and ends with *you*, the ordinary student and teacher of Scripture. And it connects you with a single thread: **the great prophet and the humble teacher are doing the same thing — using knowledge to point away from the self and toward the Lord.** The prophet does it from the mountaintop; you do it at your kitchen table. Same direction. Same refusal to become the destination.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were one insight seen from two sides.

The grammar said: the true carrier never makes himself the destination (*mā kāna*), and never drifts into it across the long after (*thumma*), and offers people *cultivation*, not a change of cage (*rabbāniyyīn*).

The themes said: a true teacher is a door, not a wall — and the knowledge that does that work is the knowledge you keep teaching and re-studying until it grows you into a different person.

They meet in one place: **the more God gives you, the more you are meant to disappear behind the gift — and the proof that you have, is that the people around you end up closer to *Him*, not closer to *you*.**

### Questions to carry

1. Think of what God has given you that others lack. Across the long *thumma* — the after — has it been making people more attached to me, or to God through me?
2. When praise comes, where does it pool? Do I redirect it upward in the moment, or does it settle quietly into my chest as something I begin to feel I'm owed?
3. Of everything I "know," how much has crossed from my mind into my hands, my tongue, my patience? What do I *have* that I have not yet *become*?
4. Is there one true thing I keep skating past in my hunger for the next new thing — one ayah, one teaching, I have never stayed with long enough to be grown by?
5. Do I want to be a *library* — full and admired — or a *rabbānī* — changed?

### One-sentence distillation

The truest carriers of God's gifts are the ones who use them to vanish behind God — and the knowledge that lets you do that is not the knowledge you stack up, but the knowledge you keep teaching and re-studying until it grows you into someone wholly of the Lord.

### The closing invitation

Something can shift after this — gently, without drama. Not a vow to become a scholar. Just a turning of the compass. The next time you are given anything — a true word to say, a moment of influence, a flash of praise — let the prophetic reflex fire: *point it past yourself.* And take the one thing you most deeply know and refuse to abandon it for novelty; sit with it until it works its way into your character. That is the long road of every *rabbānī*, and it is open to you today.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You who are *al-Rabb* — the One who nurtures what He owns — do not let what You have given us become a throne we sit on; let it become an arrow that points to You. Make us *rabbāniyyīn*: people You yourself have grown, whose knowledge has become character, who teach so that others draw nearer to You and never to us. And keep our hearts turned, across the whole long *after*, toward You alone. Āmīn.
