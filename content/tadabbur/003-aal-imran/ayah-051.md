# My Lord and Your Lord: The Servant Who Bowed First

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me take you back to the room where they were spoken.

> **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ رَبِّى وَرَبُّكُمْ فَٱعْبُدُوهُ ۗ هَـٰذَا صِرَٰطٌ مُّسْتَقِيمٌ**
>
> *Inna-Llāha rabbī wa-rabbukum fa-ʿbudūhu — hādhā ṣirāṭun mustaqīm.*
>
> **"Indeed Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him. This is a straight path."**

Nine words. That's the whole ayah. And I want you to sit with how short that is, because of where it sits.

### Rebuild the world

Go back with me. There is a man standing in front of his people. And this man — by every account they have just witnessed — can do what no one else alive can do.

Look at the ayahs just before this one. He shapes a bird out of clay and breathes into it, and it becomes a living bird. He passes his hand over eyes that have never seen, and they see. He touches skin eaten away by leprosy, and it heals. He speaks to the dead, and the dead get up and walk.

Now — put yourself in that crowd. You have watched a man raise a corpse. What is the natural next thought of the human heart? **"What is he?"** The mind reaches, almost helplessly, for a category big enough to hold what it just saw. And the category it reaches for, again and again across human history, is: *he must be more than a man. He must be divine.*

This is the most dangerous moment in ʿĪsā's entire mission. Not the moment of rejection — the moment of *adoration*. Because the very power that proves his truthfulness is the power that could swallow his message whole. The miracles point up — toward God. But human hearts, dazzled, tend to stop at the hand that performed them.

[PAUSE]

And it is *precisely here*, at the peak of his capacity, with every eye on him and the temptation of an entire people ready to crown him — that he opens his mouth. And what comes out is not "Look at me." What comes out is a sentence that puts him on his knees right next to the people watching him.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then we'll spend the next hour unpacking why it is the way it is:

In the moment he could have claimed the most, **he claimed servanthood — and he claimed it for himself *first*, before he asked it of anyone else.**

There is an order to these words. A sequence. And that sequence is doing something so quiet and so devastating that we read right past it. He does not say "Allah is *your* Lord." He says **"my Lord"** — *and then* — "and your Lord."

Why would he name his own servitude before he commands theirs? Why bow first?

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: رَبِّى وَرَبُّكُمْ — "My Lord *and* Your Lord"

#### Teach the concept first

Before we touch the Arabic, let me teach you one small thing about how language carries authority — because once you see it, you'll never unsee it in this ayah.

When a leader wants to command, the most natural grammar in any language is the grammar of *separation*. "You must do this." "I need you to." The word "you" pointed outward, the speaker standing apart, above, issuing. The instruction flows downhill from the one who is exempt to the ones who are bound.

Now picture a different posture. Picture a leader who, before telling you to do anything, first names himself as bound by the very same thing. "*We* must do this." Not "you" — but a "we" in which he is the first member. The instruction no longer flows downhill. It flows from inside the same circle he's asking you to stand in.

Hold that distinction. Because this ayah is built on it.

#### Open the words

Look at the two words at the heart of the sentence:

**رَبِّى** — *rabbī* — "my Lord."
**وَرَبُّكُمْ** — *wa-rabbukum* — "and your Lord."

Same root both times: **ر-ب-ب** (*rabb*) — Lord, Sustainer, the One who owns, nurtures, and brings to completion. A *rabb* is not just a master; in the Arabic it is the one who raises a thing from its beginning to its fullness — the way you'd say a gardener is the *rabb* of his garden, the one under whose care it grows.

The word appears twice. Once with the ending **ـِى** — *the ya of the speaker*, "my." Once with the ending **ـكُمْ** — *kum*, "your," plural.

#### Deepen

Now. Notice the order. [PAUSE]

He says **رَبِّى** — *my* Lord — **first.**

Stay with this. He is speaking *to* them. The whole point of the sentence is to redirect *them*. The efficient thing, the expected thing, the thing a man asserting authority over a crowd would say, is: *"Allah is your Lord — worship Him."* Point outward. Issue the command.

But that is not what he does. Before the word "your" ever arrives, he plants the word "**my**." He binds *himself* before he binds them. He steps into the circle of servitude and *only then* turns and says: and you are here too.

Al-Muyassar reads the verse exactly this way. Listen to the gloss: *"fa-anā wa-antum sawāʾ fī al-ʿubūdiyyati wa-l-khuḍūʿi lah"* — **"so I and you are equal in servitude and in submission to Him."** *Sawāʾ.* Equal. Level. The same ground.

Think about what that word does to the moment. The man who just raised the dead is telling the people who just watched him raise the dead: *you and I are standing on the same floor.* Whatever altitude the miracle seemed to give him — he hands it straight back. The hand that healed and the eyes that were healed belong to the same category: servants of one Lord.

And watch how he *arrives* at that word "my." Two verses earlier, presenting his miracles, he had told them he came "with a sign **from your Lord**" (3:49) — *your* Lord, pointed outward, the natural speech of a messenger sent *to* a people. But here, at the climax, the pronoun widens to enfold him: not "your Lord" alone but **"my Lord and your Lord."** Al-Muyassar catches the exact weight of it — the One he summons them to is *rabbī wa-rabbukum*, his Lord and theirs, *waḥdahu*, **He alone**. ʿĪsā is the caller, the one *inviting* (*"alladhī adʿūkum ilayh"* — "the One I am calling you to"); but the first thing he establishes about the One he calls to is that the caller stands beneath that same Lordship alongside everyone he calls.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's remarkable. He could have built a hierarchy and still been truthful. He could have said: "Allah is my Lord in a special way, and your Lord too." There is no such qualifier. The *rabb* is identical. The relationship word does not change between him and them. It is *rabbī wa-rabbukum* — the very same *rabb*, simply pointed first at himself and then at them, like a man placing his own hand on the rope before he tells you to grab on.

This is the answer to our hook. **Why bow first?** Because a call to servitude is only trustworthy from someone already on their knees. If he had stood above them and commanded worship, the worship could so easily have curdled — into worship *of him*. By naming his own servitude first, he closes that door with his own body. *I am not the destination. Watch me face the same direction I'm asking you to face.*

#### Conclude the element

So this small grammatical fact — the word "my" arriving before the word "your" — is not decoration. It is the entire theology of ʿĪsā in the Qur'an, compressed into a pronoun order. **The one with the most reason to be exalted insists, in the structure of his own sentence, on being levelled.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: فَٱعْبُدُوهُ — The "So" That Carries Everything

#### Teach the concept first

In Arabic there is a tiny letter — **فَ** (*fa*) — that we usually translate "so" or "then." It looks like nothing. But it is one of the most load-bearing letters in the language, because **فَ** is the letter of *consequence*. It says: what comes next is not a new, separate idea. What comes next *follows*. It is the result, the necessary outflow, of what was just said.

Think of how we use "so" in English when it really means something: "The door is open — *so* come in." The "so" tells you the second half isn't arbitrary. It's *produced* by the first half. Remove the open door and the invitation makes no sense.

Keep that in your mind: **فَ** means *therefore. As a result. Because of what I just told you.*

#### Open the word

**فَٱعْبُدُوهُ** — *fa-ʿbudūhu* — "**so** worship Him."

Break it open:
- **فَ** — "so / therefore," the consequence
- **ٱعْبُدُو** — *uʿbudū*, a command: "worship," from the root **ع-ب-د** (*ʿabada*) — the same root as *ʿabd*, a servant, a slave. To worship in Arabic is to *enslave yourself willingly* — to make yourself the property of the One you turn to.
- **هُ** — "Him."

#### Deepen

Notice what the **فَ** does to the whole sentence. It welds the command onto the statement before it.

"Indeed Allah is my Lord and your Lord — **therefore** — worship Him."

Worship is not introduced as a demand that drops from the sky. It is presented as a *conclusion*. As something that follows as inevitably as "come in" follows "the door is open." The logic is airtight: *If* He is the One who owns you, nurtures you, raises you from nothing to fullness — *then* the only coherent response is to give yourself back to Him. The **فَ** says: you don't worship Him because I'm telling you to. You worship Him because of what He already *is* to you. I'm just naming the consequence out loud.

Stay with this, because it reframes what worship even is. We tend to experience worship as an *imposition* — a tax, an obligation laid on top of life from outside. But the **فَ** says worship is not laid on top of anything. It is the *natural exhalation* of having understood who your Lord is. Recognize the *rabb*, and worship is simply what your recognition does next. It is the body of the truth you just accepted.

[PAUSE]

And look — the command is plural: *uʿbudū*, "all of you, worship." But remember Element One. He has already put himself inside the sentence with *rabbī* — "my Lord." So even though the imperative is grammatically pointed at them, the whole frame has already enrolled him in it. He is not standing outside the command shouting it in. He is standing inside it, and the **فَ** carries them into the same room he's already in.

#### Conclude the element

So the **فَ** does two things at once. It makes worship *rational* — the necessary consequence of Lordship, not an arbitrary tax. And it makes worship *shared* — flowing out of the "my Lord and your Lord" that came right before it, so that the worshippers and the one calling them to worship are all swept up in the same single response to the same single Lord.

One letter. *Therefore.* And everything hangs on it.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: هَـٰذَا صِرَٰطٌ مُّسْتَقِيمٌ — "*This* Is a Straight Path"

#### Teach the concept first

Two small features here, and I want to teach them before we open the words.

First — the demonstrative. **هَـٰذَا** — *hādhā* — "this." A demonstrative is a *pointing finger* made of language. And the most important question to ask of any pointing finger is: *what is it pointing at?* Because "this" only means something if you know what's at the end of the gesture.

Second — there is a family of verb-forms in Arabic, built by adding letters to the root, and one of them — what scholars call **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern — very often carries the sense of a thing *standing on its own*, *holding itself up*, *being self-sustaining*. We'll see why that matters in a moment.

#### Open the words

**هَـٰذَا صِرَٰطٌ مُّسْتَقِيمٌ** — *hādhā ṣirāṭun mustaqīm* — "this is a straight path."

- **هَـٰذَا** — "this," the pointing finger
- **صِرَٰطٌ** — *ṣirāṭ*, from **ص-ر-ط** — a road, a wide clear way. Notice: it's **indefinite** — *a* path, not "the" path. Hold that.
- **مُّسْتَقِيمٌ** — *mustaqīm*, from **ق-و-م** (*qāma*, to stand). This is the Form X participle — *mustaqīm*: that which **stands upright, holds itself straight, sustains its own erectness.**

#### Deepen — what is "this" pointing at?

Here is the first question. *hādhā* — "this" — points at *what*?

It does not point forward to something he's about to say. It points **backward** — back over the entire sentence we just walked through. *Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him* — **THIS.** This whole orientation. This is the straight path.

The classical commentators read it precisely this way. Al-Jalalayn pauses on *hādhā* and supplies the missing words: *"alladhī āmurukum bih"* — "**that which I am commanding you with**." "This" is not some object off to the side; it is the entire summons he has just made — the worship of the one shared Lord. The pointing finger lands on the call itself.

Let that reframe what *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* even means. We often imagine the "straight path" as a list — a set of rules, a code of behavior, a map with directions. But the pointing finger here doesn't land on a list. It lands on a *relationship*: the recognition that He is your Lord, and the giving of yourself that follows from it. **The straight path is not first a set of instructions. It is first a direction of the heart** — Lord recognized, self surrendered. Everything else is downstream of that.

#### Deepen further — the path that *stands you up*

Now the word *mustaqīm* itself. From *qāma* — to stand.

We translate it "straight," and that's fine, but the root isn't really about geometry. It's about *standing*. *Uprightness.* And in Form X — *istaqāma* — the sense deepens to *standing firm on its own, holding itself erect, being self-sustaining and unwavering.* The classical lexicons gloss *al-mustaqīm* as that which has no crookedness, no lean, no *ʿiwaj* — al-Muyassar uses exactly this phrase: *"alladhī lā ʿiwaja fīh"* — "the way in which there is no crookedness at all."

But sit with the *standing* imagery, because there's a gift hidden in it. A crooked structure cannot bear weight. A leaning wall, a bent pillar — it collapses under load. Only what is *mustaqīm*, only what stands truly upright, can hold something up.

So here is the quiet promise inside the word: **the straight path is not just the way you are supposed to walk. It is the thing that holds you up while you walk it.** A *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* is a road that doesn't sag, doesn't twist, doesn't give way beneath you. You can put your full weight on it. It will *stand* — and standing, it lets *you* stand.

[PAUSE]

#### And why is it "*a* path," not "*the* path"?

One more thing — that indefinite **صِرَٰطٌ**. *A* straight path. You'd almost expect "*the* straight path," *the* famous one we ask for in al-Fātiḥah. Why indefinite here?

What the indefinite invites us to consider is this: he is not, in this moment, naming a single famous highway and arguing his is the official one. He is making a more elemental claim. He is saying — *this orientation, here, the one I just described: it has the quality of straightness. It stands. It holds. Whatever else you've been told, weigh it against this: does it stand upright with no crookedness? Does Lord-recognized-self-surrendered describe it?* The indefinite keeps the focus on the *quality* — the straightness itself — rather than on a label to be claimed.

#### Conclude the element

So these last three words gather everything. The pointing finger sends us back over the whole sentence and says: *that — the shared Lord, the worship that follows — that is what straightness is.* And the word for straight is a word for *standing*: a path with no lean in it, that bears your full weight, that holds you up precisely because it does not bend.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge out of the language

So look at what these nine words have built. A pronoun order that bows the speaker first. A single letter, *fa*, that turns worship from a tax into a consequence. A pointing finger that defines the straight path not as a list but as a direction of the whole self — and names that direction with a word that means *standing upright under load*.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about how a human being is meant to live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Cure for Making Gods Out of People — and Out of Ourselves

#### Begin where we already live

Let me start nowhere near the seventh century. Let me start with your phone.

We are living through the largest experiment in human elevation the species has ever run. Every day, millions of people pour devotion into other human beings they have never met. We call it being a "fan," being a "follower" — and notice that the word *follower* is already a religious word. We memorize their lives. We defend them like family. We feel personally wounded when they fall. There is a name psychologists use for this — the *parasocial bond* — but the Qur'an named the underlying impulse fourteen centuries ago: the human heart is a worship-making machine. It will find something to bow to. If you do not give it the Real, it will manufacture a god out of whatever is nearest and most dazzling.

And the most dazzling thing is usually *a person*. Someone with a gift. A voice, a beauty, a brilliance, a power. The heart sees the gift and — exactly like that crowd watching a corpse stand up — it reaches for a category big enough, and reaches too far. It turns the gifted one into a destination.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now bring the ayah back.

Here is the most gifted human being most of those people had ever seen. A man who could undo *death*. If anyone in history had grounds to accept elevation, to let the adoration pool at his feet, it was him. The miracles were real. The crowd was ready.

And he says: **"Allah is my Lord and your Lord."**

Do you feel the force of it now? This is not a man modestly deflecting a compliment. This is the single most effective thing any human being can do to protect *other people from the disease of worshipping him.* He doesn't argue them out of it. He doesn't lecture them on theology. He simply *steps off the pedestal in the grammar of his own sentence* and stands down on the floor beside them. *My Lord and your Lord. I and you, equal in servitude.*

He is, in effect, refusing to be your god — out of love for you. Because he knows what we forget: that to worship a creature is to be betrayed by it eventually. Every human destination collapses. Every hero has a falling. Every idol has feet of clay — even, especially, the one who could make clay birds fly. He will not let them put their weight on him, because he is *mustaqīm* enough to know he is not the thing that stands. Only the Lord stands. So he points past himself.

And the deflection did not begin with this verse — it was already woven into the miracles themselves. Ibn Kathīr preserves the wording of the claims ʿĪsā made just before: he forms the bird from clay and it becomes a living bird *"by Allah's leave"* (*bi-idhnillāh*); he heals the blind and the leper, and he raises the dead — each, again, *"by Allah's leave."* The qualifier rides on every single miracle like a refrain. So when he reaches *rabbī wa-rabbukum*, he is not introducing a new humility at the end of the speech; he is naming out loud the dependence that every wonder had already confessed. The hand that worked the miracle had been pointing, the whole time, at the One whose leave made it possible.

And this is of a piece with everything else he tells them. In the verse just before, he says he came *confirming the Torah already in their hands* and to make lawful some of what had been forbidden to them (3:50). Al-Ṭabarī, parsing the grammar there, reads *muṣaddiqan* as a state attaching to "I came" — *"and I came to you confirming what is before me of the Torah."* Hear what that does: he is not erecting a brand-new religion with himself at its center. He is affirming the revelation they already had and turning them toward the same single Lord who sent it. The man who could make clay birds fly points, in the same breath, *backward* to the revelation given through Mūsā and *upward* to the one Lord of both. Nothing in the summons curves toward him.

[PAUSE]

#### The other direction — making a god of *yourself*

But there's a second face to this disease, and it's the one closer to home. Because most of us are not in danger of building a shrine to a celebrity. We are in danger of building a shrine to *ourselves.*

The modern self is *constantly* invited to be its own *rabb* — its own lord, sustainer, author, judge. "You are the only authority you need." "Trust your truth." "You are enough." And there is a sliver of beauty in that — but as a *theology* it is exhausting, because a self that has made itself its own lord can never rest. It has to generate its own worth, defend its own throne, sustain its own existence by sheer will. That is an unbearable weight for a creature to carry.

And *rabbī wa-rabbukum* is the cure for that too. Because the same sentence that stops you worshipping others stops you worshipping yourself. It says: *there is a rabb, and it is not you, and it is not me, and the relief of that is the whole gospel of it.* You were never supposed to be your own sustainer. You can set the throne down. There is Someone whose actual job it is to raise you from your beginning to your fullness — and the moment you let Him have the throne, you can finally exhale.

#### Conclude the theme — practical

So here is what changes tomorrow. Watch your devotions — the small ones. Watch where your attention pools, where your heart goes when it's idle: a person, a feed, an image of who you're trying to become, the throne of your own self-regard. And practice the ʿĪsā move. Not *deny* the gift — his miracles were real, the things you admire are often real — but *follow the gift upstream.* When you catch your heart stopping at a creature, say the correction *rabbī wa-rabbukum* trains: *this too has a Lord. Including me.* Let every admiration become a pointing finger instead of a destination.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Path That Holds You Up

#### Begin where we already live

Let me ask you something physical. Have you ever been *exhausted* — not in your body, but in your direction? That particular modern tiredness where you're not sure what any of it is *for*? Where you're moving, achieving, even succeeding, but there's a lean in you, a crookedness, like a building that's still standing but you can feel it's not quite plumb?

There's a reason we reach for spatial words when we describe a life going wrong. We say someone has "lost their way." We say a person is "bent," or "twisted," or has gone "off course." We say someone needs to "straighten out their life," to "get back on track." Every one of those is the language of *path* and *posture*. The soul, it turns out, has a *spine.* And it knows when it's out of alignment.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now — **هَـٰذَا صِرَٰطٌ مُّسْتَقِيمٌ.** "This is a straight path." And remember what *mustaqīm* actually means under the hood: not just *straight* like a drawn line, but *standing* — upright, self-sustaining, no lean, no *ʿiwaj*, no crookedness it has to fight.

We tend to hear "straight path" as a *restriction* — narrow, hemmed in, the no-fun option. But the root tells a different story. *Qāma* — to *stand*. The straight path is the one that lets you *stand up*. The crookedness — the lean, the exhaustion, the "what is any of this for" — that's what comes from building your life on something that can't bear weight. You leaned your whole self on a *rabb* that wasn't a real Lord: a career, an image, an approval, a person, your own willpower. And those things bend under load. So *you* bend.

[PAUSE]

And what does this particular *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* turn out to be made of? Go back to the pointing finger. *hādhā* — "this" — points at the sentence before it: *Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him.* That's the path. The straight path is not, first, a behavior chart. **It is an axis.** A single Lord recognized, a single self surrendered — and a life that hangs true off that one vertical line.

That's why it stands. A plumb line doesn't sag, because it's held from above. A life with one Lord at the top of it has something to hang from. It can bear weight. You can put your grief on it, your fear, your decisions, your death — and it does not collapse, because it is *mustaqīm*: it stands on its own, and standing, it lets you stand.

#### Build it further

And notice — this is the same path ʿĪsā walks *himself*. He didn't describe a road for them and stand off it. *Rabbī* — *my* Lord. He's on it. The straight path is not a route a leader maps for followers from the comfort of the side. It's the floor he's standing on while he speaks. When he says "this stands," it's because *he* is standing on it as he says it. The proof of the path is the uprightness of the one describing it.

This is why, at the very end of all things, the Qur'an shows us ʿĪsā saying nearly these exact words *again* — in Sūrat al-Māʾidah, on the Day of Judgment, asked whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods: *"I said to them only what You commanded me — that you worship Allah, my Lord and your Lord."* (5:117) The same axis. From the height of his mission to the Day of Resurrection, the line never bends. *That* is *istiqāma*: not a single straight moment, but a whole life — a whole *existence* — that holds its plumb.

And it is worth sitting, soberly, with how this call was actually received. Al-Jalalayn closes the verse with four flat words: *"fa-kadhdhabūhu wa-lam yuʾminū bih"* — **"so they denied him and did not believe in him."** The most self-effacing summons imaginable — a prophet refusing the very throne his people might have built for him — was met, by many, with rejection. There is something clarifying in that. The straightness of the path was never contingent on the crowd's accepting it. A *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* stands whether or not anyone walks it; ʿĪsā's uprightness held even when belief did not follow. The proof of the path was his standing on it — not their joining him there.

#### Conclude the theme — practical

So here's the shift for tomorrow, and it's almost architectural. Stop asking only "what should I *do*?" and start asking "what am I *hanging from*?" Find the lean in your life — the place you're tired in a way sleep won't fix — and trace it down to the *rabb* you've been leaning on there. An approval. An outcome. A self-image. A person. And then do the one move this ayah teaches: re-hang that part of your life from the top. *Allah is my Lord here too.* You will feel it the way a leaning thing feels when it's finally set plumb — not more restricted. *Lighter.* Held.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bring it together

Step back and look at what these nine words have done.

The **linguistic discoveries** and the **themes** turn out to be the same insight seen from two sides.

The pronoun order — *my* Lord before *your* Lord — is the cure for making gods of people, *built into grammar.* He bows first so that no one bows to him.

The little letter *fa* — "so worship Him" — is the cure for the exhausted, self-lording self: worship isn't a weight added on top of you, it's the natural exhale of finally recognizing there's a Lord who isn't you.

And the pointing finger — *this* is a straight path, *mustaqīm*, standing-upright — gathers both into one image: a life hung true off a single vertical axis, a Lord recognized and a self surrendered, a road that holds you up precisely because it does not bend.

A servant who bowed first, pointing past himself, at the one axis that stands. That's the whole ayah.

### Questions to carry

1. Where does my attention pool when it's idle — and have I quietly made that thing a *rabb*?
2. Is there a place in my life where I am trying to be my own lord — my own sustainer, my own judge — and exhausting myself doing a job that was never mine?
3. When I admire someone's gift, does my heart stop *at* them, or does it travel upstream to the One who gave it?
4. Where is the *lean* in me — the tiredness sleep won't fix — and what have I been hanging that part of my life from?
5. Could I, like him, point past myself — even when I have every reason to let the praise stay?

### One-sentence distillation

The one with the most reason to be worshipped insisted, in the order of his own words, on kneeling first — because the only thing that truly *stands* is the Lord we share, and a life hung true off that single axis is the one path that bends for no one and holds everyone up.

### The closing invitation

You don't have to overhaul your life tonight. The invitation is smaller and harder than that. It is simply this: the next time your heart reaches for a destination — a person, an outcome, an image of yourself — let it become a *pointing finger* instead. Follow the gift upstream. Say the correction this ayah was built to teach you: *this too has a Lord. Including me.* And feel what happens to your posture when you do.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You are our Lord — the Lord of the one who calls and the one who answers, level before You in our need of You. Teach us to point past every gift to the Giver, and past our own thrones to Yours. And hang our lives true off the one axis that stands, so that we, too, may stand. *Āmīn.*
