# When You Ask "How," He Answers "Who"

## Introduction: A Woman Alone With the Impossible

Let me give you the words first, and then let me take you to the room where they were spoken.

> قَالَتْ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِى وَلَدٌ وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِى بَشَرٌ ۖ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكِ ٱللَّهُ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ ۚ إِذَا قَضَىٰٓ أَمْرًا فَإِنَّمَا يَقُولُ لَهُۥ كُن فَيَكُونُ

*Qālat rabbi annā yakūnu lī waladun wa lam yamsasnī bashar. Qāla kadhāliki-llāhu yakhluqu mā yashāʾ. Idhā qaḍā amran fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu kun fa-yakūn.*

"She said, 'My Lord, how can there be a child for me when no man has touched me?' He said, 'Such is Allah; He creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it, "Be," and it is.'"

Now come with me into the moment.

Maryam is young. She has spent her life inside a sanctuary, raised under the guardianship of Zakariyya, set apart, devoted, protected. She is, in the language the Quran uses for her elsewhere, the one who *guarded her chastity* — her purity is not incidental to her story, it is the center of her identity. It is the thing everyone who knows her knows about her.

And into that life walks a figure — the angel — who tells her she will carry a son.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what that sentence does to her. This is not abstract theology being discussed in a study circle. This is a woman being told that the one thing that has defined her entire existence — her untouched purity — is about to produce the one thing that, in the eyes of every human being around her, could only mean its opposite. A child. With no husband. No man has come near her.

She is being handed, in a single sentence, both a miracle and a scandal. The honor of the ages and the accusation of a lifetime, fused together, indistinguishable from the outside.

And out of her comes a question. Three words in Arabic. *Annā yakūnu lī walad* — "How can there be a child for me?"

Here is the hook. Here is what I want you to hold onto for the next hour, because everything turns on it.

**Maryam asks a question about *how*. And the answer she receives does not explain the *how* at all.**

She asks for a mechanism. Allah gives her Himself.

She wants to know the process. The response erases the very category of process.

There is something happening in the *gap* between her question and the answer — a gap so precise, so deliberate, that once you see it, you will recognize it in every anxious "but how will this work out?" you have ever whispered to yourself at two in the morning.

Let's go find it.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Word That Means "How" and "From Where" at the Same Time — أَنَّىٰ

### First, the everyday thing

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small feature of how questions work — in any language, including yours.

When something stuns you, you have two different kinds of "how" available to you.

There is the *how* of method: "How does this machine work?" You're asking for the steps. Show me the gears.

And there is the *how* of astonishment: "How on earth did you get here?" — where you're not really asking for a travel itinerary. You're expressing that the thing in front of you doesn't fit into the world as you understood it. The "how" is carrying your shock, not requesting a manual.

English blurs these together. We use the same word for both and let tone do the sorting. Arabic does not blur them. Arabic has more than one tool, and the choice between them is meaningful.

### Now, the word

Maryam does not say *kayfa* — كيف — the plain, neutral "how," the "how does it work" word.

She says **أَنَّىٰ** — *annā*.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this word, because it is richer than any single English word can hold. The classical lexicographers — you find this in Lisān al-ʿArab and across the dictionaries — tell us that *annā* carries **two meanings braided together**: it means *kayfa* (how) **and** it means *min ayna* (from where, from what source, by what route).

Notice what that does. When Maryam speaks, she is not asking a clean technical question — "by what biological mechanism." She is asking something wider and more vulnerable. *From where would such a thing even come? By what road does a child arrive at a woman no man has touched? How, and from where, and along what path that I cannot see?*

It is the question of someone standing at the edge of a map. Not "explain the engine to me," but "I cannot locate the *direction* this could possibly come from. There is no road on my map that leads here."

### Deeper — this is wonder, not doubt

And here is where we must be careful and precise, because the difference matters enormously for who Maryam is.

The mufassirūn are clear on this point — al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both note it directly — Maryam's question is *taʿajjub*, **astonishment**, not *takdhīb*, denial. She is not Zakariyya's people doubting. She is not refusing the news. Al-Baghawi says plainly: *she said this out of wonder, because it was not the custom that a child be born with no father.*

She believes it. That is precisely why she is amazed. Her astonishment is the astonishment of faith meeting the unprecedented — not the resistance of disbelief meeting a claim.

Do you feel the difference? Doubt says "this cannot be." Wonder says "this *will* be — and I cannot fathom from where it comes." Maryam is entirely in the second posture. Her *annā* is the sound of a believing heart pressed flat against the wall of the impossible, saying: *I don't understand the road, but I am not stepping back.*

This is the dignity of her question. It is not weak faith. It is faith honest enough to admit its own bewilderment without letting the bewilderment turn into refusal.

### And then — the answer doesn't answer

Now watch what comes back. Because this is the heart of the whole ayah.

Maryam asks about the road — *from where, by what path*. And the reply is:

**كَذَٰلِكِ ٱللَّهُ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ** — *"Such is Allah; He creates what He wills."*

[PAUSE]

Read it again and feel what is *missing*. There is no road in the answer. There is no path described. There is no mechanism, no process, no "here is how it will happen to your body." The question asked *from where?* — and the answer does not name a "where" at all.

Instead, the answer changes the subject — but not evasively. It changes it *upward*. Maryam's question was pointed at a *process*: by what means? The answer redirects the entire question toward a *Person*: **such is Allah.** *Kadhāliki-llāh.*

It is as if the answer is gently saying: *You are asking the wrong kind of question, daughter of ʿImran. You are looking down the road for a cause. Look up instead, at the One who does not need roads.*

This is the first great movement of the ayah, and I want to name it as cleanly as I can: **When the human being asks "how," Allah answers "Who."** The reorientation from mechanism to Master — that is not a failure to answer. That *is* the answer. The deepest answer to "how can this be?" is "consider Who is doing it."

Let that settle, and we'll turn to a second word.

## Element Two: The Tenderness Hidden in "Touch" — وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِي بَشَرٌ

### The concept first: how the Quran handles delicate things

Every language has the problem of how to speak about intimacy with dignity. We reach for soft words — we say someone "passed away" instead of the blunt verb, we say a couple "is together." We choose gentle words to wrap difficult or private realities. The choice of a softer word over a harder one is never accidental; it tells you how the speaker wants you to *feel* about the thing.

Watch how the Quran does this here, on Maryam's own lips.

### The word

She does not use any clinical or explicit term for marital relations. She says:

**وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِي بَشَرٌ** — *wa lam yamsasnī bashar* — "and no human has *touched* me."

The verb is from the root **م-س-س**, *mass* — simply, **to touch**. The lightest possible word. Not "no man has married me," not anything anatomical — just *touch*. The faintest contact. She draws the line at the very outermost edge: not even a touch.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does. By choosing the gentlest word — *touch* — Maryam's statement becomes both more modest *and* more absolute at once. More modest, because she will not let a coarse word past her lips even while defending her own purity. And more absolute, because by denying even *touch*, she has denied everything: if not so much as a hand has reached her, then certainly nothing more. The softest word draws the strongest boundary.

The word choice *is* her character. The way she speaks about the matter is itself evidence of the purity the matter concerns. You learn who she is from the verb she selects under pressure.

### And the word for "human" — بَشَر

Now look at the other word in that phrase: **بَشَر** — *bashar*. We translate it "a human being," "a man." But hold the word a moment.

*Bashar* is connected in the Arabic to **بَشَرَة** — *basharah* — which means the **skin**, the outer surface, the visible flesh of a human being. *Bashar* names the human precisely as a creature *of skin and surface* — a being you can see, touch, who occupies a body.

This is exquisite, and here is why. Maryam denies that any *bashar* — any creature of skin, any embodied human — has touched her. And the answer that comes back is **kadhāliki-llāhu yakhluqu** — *thus does Allah create.* The contrast could not be sharper. On one side: *bashar*, skin, surface, the creature who must *touch* to bring about a child, who is bound to the physical mechanism. On the other side: **Allah**, who creates **without** touch, without surface, without contact of any kind — by sheer will.

Maryam frames her whole question inside the world of *skin* — the world where children require bodies meeting bodies. And the answer lifts the entire matter clean out of the world of skin. *No bashar has touched you? Of course not. This is not the work of bashar at all. This is the work of the One who needs no hands.*

[PAUSE]

So the second element deepens the first. The reorientation from "how" to "Who" is also a reorientation from *the realm of skin and surface* to *the realm of pure will.* Maryam's question lives in the only world she has ever known — the world where things come about through physical causes. The answer is teaching her that there is another order entirely, an order that the rules of skin do not reach.

## Element Three: The Two Letters That Hold Up the Universe — كُن

### The concept: the gap between wanting and getting

Let me ground this one in your own life before we open it.

For you and me, there is always a *distance* between deciding to do something and the thing being done. You decide to build a table — and then there are weeks of wood and tools and effort and mistakes. You decide to heal a relationship — and then there are months of difficult conversations. Between the *will* and the *result* there is always a stretch of labor, of process, of struggle, of *how*. Our whole lives are lived in that gap. Most of our anxiety lives there too — in the long, uncertain corridor between wanting something and watching it actually come to be.

Now watch the Quran collapse that corridor to nothing.

### The phrase

**إِذَا قَضَىٰٓ أَمْرًا فَإِنَّمَا يَقُولُ لَهُۥ كُن فَيَكُونُ**

*"When He decrees a matter, He only says to it: 'Be' — and it is."*

The whole machinery of creation, the entire distance between divine will and accomplished reality, is reduced to a single word: **كُن** — *kun* — "Be."

[PAUSE]

### Listen to it

Say it out loud. *Kun.* Two letters: kāf and nūn. It is, quite literally, one of the shortest utterances the Arabic language can form — a single closed syllable. You can barely begin to exert effort before the word is already finished.

And that brevity is not decoration. **The shortness of the word is the meaning of the word.** For us, big results require big effort — the size of the labor matches the size of the outcome. A house takes more than a chair. But Allah brings a living child into a virgin's womb — arguably the most staggering creative act imaginable — with a word so small it is over before your breath is. The effortlessness is encoded in the *sound*. The universe's most impossible event and its smallest possible word, set side by side, so you cannot miss the lesson: **there is no proportion between the difficulty of a thing and the ease with which Allah brings it about. For Him, there is no difficulty at all.**

Maryam stands at the edge of a map with no road on it. And the answer is: *He does not travel roads. He speaks.*

### The grammar that turns a command into a fact — فَيَكُونُ

Now here is the detail that the grammarians treasure, and it rewards attention.

Look at the last word: **فَيَكُونُ** — *fa-yakūn* — "and it is." Notice the final vowel — it ends in *u*, the *ḍammah*, the marker of the indicative mood (*marfūʿ*). 

Why does that matter? In Arabic, after a *fa-* like this, you might expect the verb to be *manṣūb* — to shift mood and read *fa-yakūna*, which would make it a *result clause*: "Be, *so that it may be*." A consequence hanging off the command, contingent, awaiting.

But that is not how it is recited. It is **fa-yakūn-u**, indicative — a fresh, independent, **declarative statement of fact**. Not "Be, in order that it then becomes." Rather: **"Be." — And it is.** A flat, finished assertion. The becoming is not presented as a hoped-for consequence of the command; it is presented as an accomplished reality, stated plainly, as though the gap between the command and the result is too small to even narrate.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what the grammar is doing? Even the *sentence structure* refuses to put any distance between the "Be" and the "is." A result clause would create a little space — a *so that*, a tiny corridor of waiting. The indicative slams that space shut. Command and existence are laid down as two facts side by side, with nothing in between. The grammar itself has no room for a "how."

This is the same lesson as *kun*'s shortness, now confirmed at the level of syntax: **for Allah, there is no corridor between the will and the world.** No process. No waiting room. No "how."

### Bridge: from words to reality

Step back and look at what these three elements have built together.

Maryam asked *annā* — "how, and from where?" — looking for a road.

She framed her question in the world of *bashar*, of skin and touch — the only world where children come.

And the answer dismantled both, gently, completely: *kadhāliki-llāh* — such is Allah — *kun fa-yakūn* — He says "Be," and it is. No road. No skin. No corridor. No how.

The linguistic construction has shown us something with surgical precision: **the question "how can this be?" assumes a universe of process — and that assumption is exactly what Allah's answer suspends.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the anxious human heart that lives inside it.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Tyranny of "How" — and the Freedom of "Who"

### Begin where you live

Let me start not with Maryam, but with you.

Think of the last thing that genuinely frightened you about the future. Not a small worry — a real one. The bill you don't see how you'll pay. The diagnosis. The marriage that feels beyond repair. The child who has drifted. The dream that the math says is impossible.

Now notice the *shape* of the fear. I want to suggest that almost all of our deepest anxiety has the exact same grammatical structure. It is the word **"how."**

*How am I going to get through this? How will this ever work out? How does someone in my situation get from here to there? I just don't see how.*

[PAUSE]

We are creatures who have been trained — by a lifetime of living inside cause and effect — to believe that if we cannot *see the road*, the destination is unreachable. We stand at our own edge-of-the-map moments and we say, with Maryam, *annā* — how, and from where? And when no road appears on the map, the heart concludes: then it cannot happen. Then I am trapped. Then there is no way.

The modern mind has this disease in an acute form. We are mechanism-worshippers. We trust only what we can trace, step by step, cause to effect. We have built a whole civilization on the premise that to *understand* a thing is to *map its process* — and we have quietly let that premise leak into our relationship with our own future, and even with our Lord. We will not feel safe about an outcome until we can see the road to it.

### Let the ayah in

And into that exact disease, this ayah speaks.

Maryam — the purest, the most beloved, the chosen of the women of the worlds — asks the most reasonable *how* in human history. There has *never* been a child without a father. Her "how" is not faithless; it is the most justified "how" anyone has ever asked.

And the answer does not honor the "how" with a road. The answer says: *kadhāliki-llāh.* Such is Allah.

Sit with what a mercy that actually is. Because if the answer to "how can this be?" were a *mechanism* — a road, a process — then your safety would always depend on whether a road exists. And sometimes, in your life, there will be *no road*. The math really will be impossible. The situation really will have no visible exit.

But the ayah relocates your security. It moves the foundation of your hope from **the existence of a road** to **the identity of your Lord.** And those are not the same thing at all. Roads run out. *Allah* does not.

[PAUSE]

### The movement deeper

Here is the turn, and it is everything.

When you ask "how will this work out?" and you cannot answer it — that is not a sign that it won't work out. It is a sign that you have been asking a question that was never yours to answer. **The "how" was never your department.**

Look again at Maryam. She is never told the mechanism. The Quran never has the angel explain to her *how* the child will form. She is simply redirected from the process to the Person — and then she is left to *trust*. Her peace does not come from finally understanding the road. Her peace comes from knowing Whose word is being spoken over her life.

This is the secret the anxious heart keeps missing: **you have been demanding a "how" from a situation, when your only real task was to recognize a "Who."** The relief you are looking for is not on the far side of finally figuring out the mechanism. It is on the near side of surrendering the mechanism altogether to the One who says *kun*.

### And the paradox — let it sit

Now, I will not tidy this up too quickly, because there is a real tension here and you deserve to feel it.

This does *not* mean you stop acting. Maryam still had to carry the pregnancy, still had to walk out and face her people, still — when the labor pains came in Surah Maryam — was told to *shake the trunk of the palm tree* so the dates would fall. She had to grip the tree. The God who says *kun* still told her to use her hand.

So the lesson is not "do nothing." The lesson is subtler and far more freeing: **do your part without the burden of having to author the outcome.** Shake the tree — but know that the dates do not come from the strength of your shaking; they come from the One who decreed them. Take your steps — but lay down the crushing weight of believing that the whole result rests on whether *you* can see and build the road.

The "how" is His. The shaking of the tree is yours. And the difference between those two is the difference between a life of exhausted, white-knuckled control and a life of active, peaceful trust.

### What changes tomorrow

So here is the practical thing. The next time you catch your heart saying *"I just don't see how,"* I want you to hear it as a signal — not of despair, but of a misplaced question. Let that exact sentence become a trigger that turns your face upward. Catch the "how," and answer it with a "Who": *kadhāliki rabbī — such is my Lord. He creates what He wills. He has done what has no road before, for someone far better than me. The 'how' is not mine to carry.*

Then go shake your tree, and leave the dates to Him.

## Theme Two: The Word "Be" — and What It Means That Your Lord Does Not Strain

### Begin in the human world of effort

Let me take you into a feeling you know intimately: the feeling of a thing being *hard*.

You know the weight of a difficult task before you even begin it. You feel it in your chest — the dread of the long effort ahead, the sheer *expensiveness* of what you're about to attempt. And the bigger the thing, the heavier the dread. A small favor costs you little. Rebuilding your life costs you everything. We live our whole lives inside this iron law: **outcomes cost effort, and big outcomes cost big effort.** It is so deep in us that we cannot imagine it being otherwise.

And then — without noticing — we project that law onto Allah.

We don't say it out loud, but we feel it. We feel that our *small* problems are easy for Him to fix and our *enormous* ones are, somehow, harder. We pray for the little things with quiet confidence and pray for the impossible things with a secret flinch, as though we are asking Him to *strain.* As though the size of our crisis registers as difficulty on His end.

[PAUSE]

### Let the ayah in

**كُن فَيَكُونُ.** "Be — and it is."

We already heard how short the word is. Now let's feel what the shortness *means for you.*

The most impossible thing in this passage — a child knit together in a womb no man has touched — is brought about by the smallest word in the language, spoken once. Not a long incantation for a great miracle and a short one for a small favor. **One word. For everything.** The conception of ʿIsa and the falling of a single leaf in a forest cost the *same*: a single *kun*.

Do you understand what this dismantles? It dismantles the entire iron law. It tells you that **the category of "hard for Allah" does not exist.** Your impossible and your trivial are identical in His sight — both equally answered by the same two letters. There is no problem in your life large enough to make Him strain, because straining is a thing creatures do, and He is not a creature.

[PAUSE]

### Deeper — the will behind the word

But go one layer further, because *kun* could sound cold if we stop there — like a machine that simply outputs whatever is requested. It is not cold. Look at the words that surround it.

**يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ** — He creates *what He wills.* **إِذَا قَضَىٰٓ أَمْرًا** — when He *decrees* a matter.

The *kun* is not random. It is the tip of a *will* — *mashīʾah* — and a *decree* — *qaḍāʾ*. Behind the effortless word stands a deliberate, intending, choosing Lord. He does not say *kun* by accident or reflex. He says it because He *willed* it, *decreed* it, *chose* it.

This is the warmth inside the power. The same *kun* that knit ʿIsa together in Maryam's womb is the *kun* standing behind every good thing that has ever reached you and every good thing you are now afraid will never come. It is not blind force. It is **willed** force — the deliberate word of a Lord who chooses, aimed precisely at what He has decreed.

So when you bring Him your impossible thing, understand the two truths you are leaning on at once: it costs Him *nothing* (the *kun* is effortless), and it is entirely *up to Him* (the *kun* flows from His will, not from your leverage). That is exactly the combination that should produce peace. The God who cannot be strained, and who chooses out of His own wisdom — that is the safest possible Being to entrust your impossibility to.

### The paradox of Maryam, held open

And here is where I want to leave the tension un-resolved, because Maryam herself had to live inside it.

The *kun* that honored her was also the *kun* that, from the outside, would look like her disgrace. The miracle and the scandal, remember, were the same event. Allah's effortless word gave her a son who would speak from the cradle to defend her — but it also gave her a walk back into her community carrying a child she could not explain.

The *kun* does not always arrive looking like rescue. Sometimes the decree that is pure gift, spoken by a Lord who does not strain and does not err, will *feel* in the moment like the hardest road of your life. Maryam's miracle came wrapped in something that looked like ruin — and was, in truth, her elevation above all the women of the worlds.

So when His *kun* lands in your life in a form you would never have chosen, hold Maryam's story against it. The word is effortless for Him and willed by Him and good in its end — even when, for a while, it asks you to walk out and shake a tree while the whole world misreads what is happening to you.

### What changes tomorrow

The practical shift here is about *how you pray.*

Stop sorting your du'ās into "realistic" and "impossible." Stop praying for the small things boldly and the enormous things with a flinch. That sorting is a quiet insult to *kun fa-yakūn* — it imagines a God who can be taxed. Bring Him your most impossible thing with the *same* confidence you bring Him your smallest, because to the One who says *kun*, they are the same size. And then surrender it to His will — *yakhluqu mā yashāʾ* — trusting that the same effortless, deliberate word that honored Maryam is more than enough for you.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole thing. This ayah is built as a perfect mirror — two halves facing each other across a single hinge.

On the **human** side stands Maryam's sentence:
- *annā* — how, from where? (the demand for a **road**)
- *yamsasnī bashar* — the touch of a creature of **skin** (the world of physical cause)

On the **divine** side stands the answer:
- *kadhāliki-llāh* — such is **Allah** (the **Person**, not the process)
- *kun fa-yakūn* — He says "Be," and it is (the **word**, not the mechanism)

Maryam's half is entirely about *means*: roads and skin and touch. Allah's half contains *no means at all*: only His name and His word. The ayah is structured so that the human reaches for a mechanism, and the divine answer is built — deliberately, at the level of grammar itself — to contain none. The empty space where the "how" should be is not an oversight. **It is the message.**

## Two Roads to One Truth

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the human themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammarians showed us *fa-yakūn* in the indicative — no corridor between command and reality. The anxious heart needed to hear: there is no corridor between your impossible situation and its resolution either, if He wills it — no road that has to exist first.

The lexicographers showed us *kun*, the shortest word, holding up the heaviest event. The frightened worshipper needed to hear: your largest crisis and your smallest wish cost Him exactly the same — nothing.

It is one truth, spoken once to the mind through morphology and once to the heart through Maryam's trembling courage: **your security was never meant to rest on seeing the road. It was meant to rest on knowing the One who needs no road.**

## Questions to Carry

Let these stay open. Don't answer them now — live with them.

1. What is the "how" you are currently demanding from a situation — the road you cannot see, and have therefore decided is closed?
2. Where in your life are you praying with a flinch — treating one of your problems as though it might make Allah *strain*?
3. When you say "I don't see how this works out," whose department have you wandered into that was never yours?
4. Maryam's miracle arrived disguised as her scandal. Is there a *kun* already at work in your life that you are currently misreading as ruin?
5. What is *your* palm tree — the small action that is genuinely yours to do, while the outcome remains entirely His?

## The One-Sentence Distillation

When you ask "how can this possibly be?", Allah does not hand you the road — He reveals Himself, the One who needs no road, who says to the impossible only "Be," and it is.

## The Closing Invitation

I am not asking you to stop thinking, or stop planning, or stop shaking your tree. Maryam did all of that. I am inviting you to lay down one specific, exhausting burden: the burden of believing that you must be able to *see the how* before you are allowed to feel safe.

Let the next "I don't see how" be the sound that turns your face upward instead of inward. Let it become, in your mouth, *kadhāliki rabbī* — such is my Lord — and feel the weight slide off your chest as the question returns to its rightful Owner.

## A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, You who create what You will and answer the impossible with a single word — teach my anxious heart to stop demanding the road and to rest in knowing You. When I cannot see *how*, let me remember *Who*. And when Your decree arrives in a form I would never have chosen, give me the trust of Maryam: to shake my tree, surrender the outcome, and wait in peace upon Your *kun*. Āmīn.
