# The Distance You Cannot Buy

### Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 30

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, the way they were given.

> **يَوْمَ تَجِدُ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَّا عَمِلَتْ مِنْ خَيْرٍ مُّحْضَرًا وَمَا عَمِلَتْ مِن سُوٓءٍ تَوَدُّ لَوْ أَنَّ بَيْنَهَا وَبَيْنَهُۥٓ أَمَدًۢا بَعِيدًا ۗ وَيُحَذِّرُكُمُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسَهُۥ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ رَءُوفُۢ بِٱلْعِبَادِ**

*Yawma tajidu kullu nafsin mā ʿamilat min khayrin muḥḍaran, wa mā ʿamilat min sūʾin tawaddu law anna baynahā wa baynahu amadan baʿīdan. Wa yuḥadhdhirukumu-llāhu nafsah. Wa-llāhu raʾūfun bi'l-ʿibād.*

> *On the Day every soul will find what good it did made present before it — and what evil it did, it will wish there were a vast distance between itself and that deed. And Allah warns you of Himself; and Allah is deeply tender toward His servants.*

### Rebuild the world

Before we open a single word, I want you to feel where this verse sits.

The verses just before this one are not about the Last Day at all. They are about *loyalty* — about whom the believers take into the inner circle of their hearts, about not handing the keys of your trust to those who war against your faith. And then, in verse 28, comes a strange ending: *"And Allah warns you of Himself."* Not "Allah warns you of the Fire." Not "Allah warns you of the enemy." **Of Himself.**

Then verse 29 lifts the roof off the human chest entirely: *"Say: whether you conceal what is in your chests or reveal it, Allah knows it."* Everything you have tucked away — the loyalty you faked, the grudge you nursed, the intention you never spoke aloud — He knows.

So picture the original listener in Madinah hearing this sequence. First: be careful where you place your heart. Then: I see inside your chest, all of it. And now — verse 30 — here is **the Day when everything inside the chest is taken out and set down in front of you.**

This is the morning after a life. The accounting room. And the air in that room is the strangest air a human will ever breathe, because in it, *nothing is hidden, nothing is past, and nothing can be undone.*

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to sit with as we begin.

This verse describes a wish. A single, desperate, universal wish that pours out of the human heart on that Day. Every soul will wish for one thing — and it is not Paradise, not forgiveness, not another chance.

It will wish for **distance.**

And the most devastating thing about that wish is that it is the one thing the universe can no longer give. [PAUSE]

Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مُحْضَرًا — *Made Present*

#### Teaching the idea first

Think about the difference between two sentences a manager might say to you.

The first: *"There's a record of your work somewhere in the system."*

The second: *"Your entire work history is printed out and sitting on the desk in front of you right now."*

Same facts. Completely different experience of those facts. The first is *information about* something distant. The second is *confrontation with* something present. One you can think about later. The other is already in your hands, demanding a response in this second.

Arabic has a word for that second state. Hold onto the feeling of it.

#### Opening the word

Allah says the soul will find its good deeds **مُّحْضَرًا** — *muḥḍaran*.

The root is **Ḥ-Ḍ-R**, and its base meaning is simply *to be present, to be there, to attend.* From the same family comes the everyday word *ḥāḍir* — "present," the word you'd say when your name is called from a roll. But Allah does not use the plain form here. He uses **Form IV** — the causative shape. The base *ḥaḍara* means "it was present." The Form IV, *aḥḍara*, means **"it was brought, it was made to be present, it was caused to attend."**

So *muḥḍar* is not a deed that merely *exists* somewhere in a divine archive. It is a deed that has been **fetched, carried in, and set down before you.** Brought present by a will that is not yours.

#### Sitting with it

Let's sit with this. The soul does not go *looking* for its record. Notice the verb: *tajidu* — "it will **find**." You find what is already there waiting for you. The deeds arrive first. You walk into the room and they are already on the table, already arranged, already *ḥāḍir*.

There is no filing cabinet to open, no hard drive to recover. The Arabic refuses the comfort of distance. *Muḥḍar* means it is **here.**

Al-Ṭabarī presses the arrival even further: he describes the good deed as *muwaffaran* — **brought in full measure, complete, with nothing withheld.** Not a partial ledger, not a curated highlight reel. Every grain of goodness, carried in whole. For the soul that tended its deeds carefully, this completeness is comfort beyond reckoning. But al-Muyassar notes that the evil deed waits with the same completeness — *fī intiẓārihā*, "attending, waiting for the soul" — so that when you arrive in that room, both records are whole and ready. There is nothing left to negotiate in the tallying. **Both sides of you will be fully there.** [PAUSE]

#### The absence that speaks

Now notice something, and notice it slowly, because this is where the verse does its quietest and most piercing work.

Read the two halves again:

*"what good it did **made present** (muḥḍaran)..."*

*"and what evil it did — **it will wish there were a vast distance...**"*

Here is the question. The good deed gets a word for its presence: *muḥḍar*, brought-near, made-present. **What word does the evil deed get for its presence?**

None.

The verse does not say "and the evil it did, made present too." The classical commentators noticed exactly this gap. Al-Baghawī records the discussion among the grammarians: some read *muḥḍar* as governing **both** halves — both the good and the evil are equally brought present, and he cites the parallel from Surah al-Kahf, *"and they will find what they did present before them"* (18:49). Others read the second half as a brand-new sentence whose entire predicate is the wishing.

Hold both of those readings together — because they are not rivals, they are two camera angles on one truth.

From the first angle: the evil is *just as present* as the good. Equally fetched, equally on the table. From the second angle: the verse **refuses to dwell on the evil's presence** and leaps straight to the soul's reaction. And why? [PAUSE]

Because for the good deed, presence is the whole story. You see it, and there is nothing more to feel but relief and joy. The verse can rest on *muḥḍar* and stop.

But for the evil deed, presence is unbearable — so the verse cannot stop at "present." The moment the evil is set down, the soul is already recoiling, already wishing, already trying to get away. **The grammar enacts the panic.** The good deed sits still on the page. The evil deed cannot — it immediately throws the soul into motion. [PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: on that Day, your deeds are not described to you. They are **delivered** to you. And the difference between the happy soul and the shattered one is not whether the record is present — it is present for everyone — but whether you can bear to look at what was brought.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: تَوَدُّ — *It Will Long*

#### Teaching the idea first

In English, "to wish" is a thin, cool word. *I wish it would rain. I wish I'd parked closer.* It carries no heat.

But imagine the difference between *"I'd prefer to be somewhere else"* and the cry of someone in the worst moment of their life: *"I would give anything — anything — to be away from this."* That second thing is not preference. It is the whole heart leaning, aching, yearning in one direction with everything it has.

Arabic keeps these two feelings in completely different words. And the word Allah chose here is not the cool one.

#### Opening the word

*"And what evil it did — **تَوَدُّ** law anna baynahā wa baynahu amadan baʿīdā."*

The root is **W-D-D**. And W-D-D is one of the warmest roots in the entire Arabic language. It is the root of *wudd* — deep affection, devoted love, the tender longing of one who *cherishes.* It is so saturated with love that Allah takes one of His own names from it: **al-Wadūd**, the Most Loving, the Source of all affection.

This is the root Allah places in the mouth of the soul standing over its own sin.

#### Sitting with it

Stay with me, because this is almost unbearably precise.

*Tawaddu* does not mean the soul *prefers* distance. It means the soul **longs** for distance with the full ache of love. The very faculty in us that was made to *cherish* — to yearn toward what we adore — is here turned entirely toward one object: **getting away from what we ourselves did.**

The warmest word in the language, describing the coldest desire a person can have: *to be far from a part of myself.*

And think about what that reveals about misdirected love. *Wudd* is the heart's reaching-engine. In this world, you spent it on something — on people, on pleasures, on a version of yourself you were building. On that Day, with the record on the table, the entire reaching-engine of the heart fires off in a single direction, and it is not toward Allah, and it is not toward Paradise. It is the pure, helpless yearning: *let there be distance.* [PAUSE]

Here's what is remarkable. The one created to be *wadūd* toward its Lord — to cherish Him, to lean toward Him — discovers on that Day that it spent its love so carelessly that now all its longing is reduced to a single hopeless wish to escape its own hands.

What this word reveals: regret in its final form is not cold. It is the heat of the heart, fully alive, fully yearning — and aimed at the one thing it can never reach.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: أَمَدًا بَعِيدًا — and the Distance That No Longer Exists

#### Teaching the idea first

When you want to escape something painful, you instinctively ask for one of two things: *space* or *time.* "I need to get away from here." "I just need this to be far behind me." Distance and time — these are the two great anesthetics. We move away in space, or we let the years carry the thing into the past until it stops hurting.

Now imagine a place where both have been switched off.

#### Opening the words

The soul longs that there be *baynahā wa baynahu* — "between it and that deed" — **أَمَدًا بَعِيدًا**, *amadan baʿīdan.*

*Baʿīd* you may know: *far, distant.* But the key word is **amad.** *Amad* in classical usage is not just "distance" in space — it is a **term, a stretch of time, a far horizon-point that you are traveling toward but have not reached.** It is time-as-distance. The soul is not only asking for miles. It is asking for **years, ages, an immense gulf of time** to open up between itself and its sin.

#### Sitting with it

Now feel the trap close. [PAUSE]

The soul asks for *time* — *amad.* But what Day is this? It is the Day described, just two verses earlier, as the Day when everything concealed becomes known; the Day the Qur'an elsewhere calls the moment the records are laid open. On that Day, **time itself has been folded up.** There is no "long ago" anymore. The deed it committed forty years before death is not forty years away. It is *muḥḍar* — present, on the table, now. The very anesthetic the soul reaches for — *amad*, the passage of time — is the one substance that no longer exists in that room.

And the deed it longs to be distant from — look closely — is described as *mā ʿamilat*: **"what it did."** Its own action. Its own work. You cannot put distance between yourself and your own hand. The thing it wants to flee is not an enemy outside it; it is **fused to it,** the way a fingerprint is fused to the finger.

So the wish is doubly impossible. It asks for *time* on a Day with no time. And it asks for *distance* from a deed that is *part of itself.*

The classical commentators push the impossibility one notch further. Al-Ṭabarī reads *amadan baʿīdan* not simply as "distance" but as *ghāyatan baʿīdatan* — **a far goal, a terminus at the horizon**, the kind of end-point toward which you travel without ever arriving. Al-Jalālayn crystallizes it with striking precision: *ghāyatan fī nihāyat al-buʿd falā yaṣil ilayhā* — "a goal at the extreme of distance, such that it cannot reach it." That final phrase — *it cannot reach it* — opens a third dimension of impossibility. The soul is not merely wishing for time that the Day has dissolved, nor for separation from its own self. It is wishing for an end-horizon so absolute, so infinitely remote, that **even if the universe still possessed distance, the soul could never arrive at the terminus it is imagining.** The wish is structurally helpless at every level: the medium (time) is gone, the gap (from oneself) is impossible, and the far point wished for is, by definition, unreachable. [PAUSE]

This is what makes the verse so quietly terrifying. Allah does not tell us the soul is punished here. He just lets us listen to its wish — and the wish is the punishment, because it is a wish for the two things the Day has abolished: distance, and time.

What these words reveal: in this life, time and distance are mercies — they let pain fade and let us walk away. The Day of Judgment is the removal of both. Which means the only place to deal with your deeds is **here, now,** while distance and time still work — while you can still walk *toward* repentance instead of wishing, uselessly, to walk away from the record.

[PAUSE]

### Element Four: يُحَذِّرُكُمُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسَهُ — the Turn, the Mirror, and the Self

#### The turn (iltifat)

Notice what just happened to *you* — to the reader.

The whole verse, up to this point, has been spoken in the **third person.** *Every soul... it will find... it will wish.* You have been an observer, watching some distant *nafs* in the accounting room from a safe seat in the gallery.

And then, with no warning, the camera spins around and points directly at you:

*"And Allah warns **you** (yuḥadhdhiru-**kum**)..."*

This sudden shift of address — Arabic rhetoricians call it **iltifāt** — is not decoration. It is the verse reaching out, grabbing your collar, and saying: *the soul in the gallery was never the subject. You were.* The "every soul" you were calmly watching — that is the one this is being said to. **You.** [PAUSE]

#### The verb

The verb is *yuḥadhdhir*, from **Ḥ-Dh-R**, the root of caution and wariness. And it is in **Form II** — the intensified, earnest, transitive shape. This is not a passing mention of danger. It is to warn *seriously, deliberately, repeatedly.* In fact, al-Jalālayn notes that this exact phrase already appeared in verse 28 — and that it is **repeated here for emphasis** (*karrarahu li't-taʾkīd*). Allah said it. Then He said it again. Like someone who loves you saying the same warning twice because they cannot bear for you to miss it.

#### The mirror

Now look at the most extraordinary feature of the whole verse — and it is hidden in plain sight.

The word **nafs** appears twice.

At the beginning: *kullu **nafsin*** — "every **soul**."

At the end: *yuḥadhdhirukumu-llāhu **nafsah***— "Allah warns you of His **Self**."

The same word. *Nafs.* The human soul that trembles over its deeds — and the divine **Self** that it must answer to. The verse opens with the small, exposed *nafs* finding its record laid bare, and closes with the infinite *Nafs* before whom that record is laid. **One word stretched between the lowest trembling and the highest majesty.**

And what is the warning? *"Allah warns you of **Himself.**"* Go back to verse 28 and 29: be careful whom you trust; know that I see everything inside your chest. The thing to be cautious of, in the end, is not the consequence. It is the *Reality* — Allah Himself, His seeing, His meeting. He does not point you toward a created danger. He points you toward Himself and says: *be aware of Me.*

#### The closing word

And then — after the accounting room, after the impossible wish, after "be aware of Me" — the verse lands on:

*"Wa-llāhu **raʾūfun** bi'l-ʿibād" — and Allah is deeply tender toward His servants.*

**Raʾūf**, from **R-A-F**, is not generic mercy. It is *raʾfah* — the gentlest, most tender, most protective form of compassion, the softness that flinches at the thought of the beloved being hurt. Al-Muyassar captures the staggering pairing: the very same passage that speaks of His grip and His punishment ends by declaring that *He is raʾūf with His servants.*

Sit with the impossibility of that ending. The verse that just showed you the worst moment a soul can face — the deed on the table, the love-engine of the heart firing uselessly toward a distance that no longer exists — that verse ends in **tenderness.** [PAUSE]

What this reveals — and this is the bridge to everything that follows — is that **the warning *is* the tenderness.** Read on.

---

### Bridge

The grammar has now shown us four things: that deeds are *delivered* (muḥḍar), that regret is *longing* (tawaddu), that the escape is *impossible* (amad on a Day without time, toward a horizon that could never be reached even if time remained), and that the whole scene is addressed, finally, to **you** by a *Self* who calls Himself *tender.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Cannot Outrun the One Doing the Running

#### Start where you already live

Everyone reading this has a moment they would give almost anything to undo.

A word said to a parent who is now gone, that can never be unsaid. A betrayal of someone who trusted you. A version of yourself you became for a season that you are now ashamed even to remember. You know the physical sensation of it — it visits you usually at night, and your whole body recoils, and your mind does a very specific thing: it tries to **get away.** You change the thought. You reach for your phone. You tell yourself *that was a different me, that was long ago.*

Notice what your mind reaches for in that moment of recoil. It reaches for exactly the two things this verse names: **distance** ("that's not who I am anymore") and **time** ("that was years ago"). We anesthetize our regret with distance and time. Every single human being does this. It is the basic machinery of living with yourself.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now hear the verse again: the soul *tawaddu law anna baynahā wa baynahu amadan baʿīdā* — it longs that there were a vast *span of time* between itself and its deed.

The Day of Judgment, this verse is telling us, is the day the anesthetic stops working. It is the day the two painkillers we have leaned on our whole lives — distance and time — are simply withdrawn from the universe. And what is left when you take away distance and time from a buried regret? **The raw deed, present, fused to you, and fully felt.**

#### Build it

Here is the second movement. Look again at what the soul is trying to get away from: *mā ʿamilat* — *what it did.* Not "the punishment." Not "the Fire." The thing it recoils from is **its own action.**

This is the deepest cut. The problem on that Day is not primarily an external enemy or an external flame. The problem is that **you cannot put distance between yourself and yourself.** The hand that did it is your hand. The longing to be far from the deed is itself happening *inside the very self that committed it.* The runner and the thing being run from are the same person. There is nowhere to go, because everywhere you go, you bring the doer.

And now the third movement, the turn that takes this from terror to mercy. Why is Allah telling us this *now*, in this life?

Because *right now*, distance and time still work. [PAUSE]

Right now, the deed is **not** yet *muḥḍar* and unalterable. Right now, you can do the one thing the soul on that Day cannot: you can actually *create* distance between yourself and your sin — not by fleeing it, but by **repenting** from it. *Tawbah* is the only real distance there is, because it doesn't move you away from the deed — it changes the deed, wipes it, and even (as the Qur'an promises elsewhere) transforms it. The soul on the Day of Judgment wishes for distance and is refused. You, today, are *offered* it for free. The wish that will be impossible then is *granted* now.

#### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical turn. The next time a buried regret surfaces and your instinct is to *flee* it — to change the thought, to reach for distraction, to whisper "that was long ago" — recognize that instinct as a rehearsal of the wish that gets refused. Instead, do the thing that *only works in this life:* turn toward it. Name it before Allah. Ask. Make even one small act of *tawbah* over it. You are using the window — distance and time — while it is still open. That is the entire point of being told this now, before the room with no clocks.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Warning Is the Embrace

#### Start where you already live

Imagine two people watching a child walk toward the edge of a cliff.

One says nothing. Smiles. Lets the child wander, because confronting the child would be unpleasant, and they'd rather be liked.

The other shouts — sharply, urgently, maybe frighteningly — *"STOP!"* The child cries. The shout was harsh. The shout was not "nice."

Which one loved the child? [PAUSE]

We have somehow absorbed the modern idea that love is always soft, always affirming, never alarming. That a warning is the opposite of warmth. But every parent knows the truth in their bones: **the sharpest warning can be the purest love,** and silence in the face of danger can be the deepest cruelty dressed up as kindness.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now look at the architecture of this single verse. It shows you the cliff — the accounting room, the deed on the table, the soul longing hopelessly for a distance it cannot have. It shows you the fall in full, unflinching detail.

And then it does not end on the fall. It ends: *"And Allah warns you of Himself; and Allah is deeply tender (**raʾūf**) toward His servants."*

The warning and the tenderness are in the **same breath.** Not "Allah warns you, *but* He is also merciful," as if mercy were an apology for the warning. The grammar puts them side by side as one act. The *raʾfah* — that flinching, protective tenderness — is *why* the warning exists. He showed you the cliff edge in verse 30 precisely because He is *raʾūf* and cannot bear for you to walk off it unawares.

#### Build it

Second movement. Remember the *iltifāt* — the sudden turn from "every soul" to "**you**." A distant, neutral lecturer describing the afterlife to a hall has no reason to suddenly point at one listener. But someone who *loves* you cannot keep talking in generalities when *your* safety is at stake. The grammatical lurch from third-person to "you" is the sound of love breaking through the description. He stopped describing souls in general the moment it became about *protecting you in particular.*

Third movement. *Why warn you of **Himself**?* Reflect on what an astonishing thing this is. He could have said "fear the Fire," "fear the angels of punishment," "fear the consequences." Instead: *"Allah warns you of Himself."* He puts *Himself* between you and your destruction as the thing to be conscious of — because to be truly aware of *Him* is to be saved from all of it. The warning points not at a tool of punishment but at the **Reality** that, if you keep it before your eyes, will reorder your entire life. To be warned *of Him* is, paradoxically, to be brought *near* Him. The warning is itself a door inward.

Al-Ṭabarī draws out one more thread in this direction: he reads the warning as fundamentally a declaration about *destination.* "Your return on that Day is to Him," he writes — *fa-inna maṣīrakum ayyuhā-l-qawm yawmaʾidhin ilayhi.* Which transforms the phrase entirely. "Watch out for *Himself*" is not only "be cautious of His power" — it is "remember where you are going." He is not simply the Judge installed at the end of the accounting; He is the **destination** toward which the whole journey of your life is already pointed. To be warned *of Himself* is to be reminded that you are, whether you feel it or not, traveling toward a meeting — and everything you do today is shaping the quality of that arrival.

And the fourth movement — the resolution of the paradox we left hanging in Part 1. We asked: how can a verse this severe end in *tenderness*? Now we can answer. **The severity was never the opposite of the tenderness. The severity was its proof.** Only someone who genuinely does not want you to suffer would describe the suffering so clearly. The *raʾūf* does not hide the cliff. The *raʾūf* shows you the cliff *in detail*, twice (*karrarahu li't-taʾkīd*), so that the showing might be the thing that saves you. [PAUSE]

#### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical shift. Stop hearing the warnings of the Qur'an as the cold voice of a judge and start hearing them as the urgent voice of the One who is *raʾūf bi'l-ʿibād* — who could not bear to watch you walk toward the edge in silence. The next time you encounter a verse of warning and feel yourself tighten or pull away, try receiving it as you would receive a frantic warning from someone who loves you: not *"He is against me,"* but *"He is trying to keep me."* That single reframe changes your entire relationship with the Book — from a relationship of fear-of-the-judge to a relationship of trust-in-the-One-who-warns-because-He-cares.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how the verse is *built* — because its shape is its message.

It opens with the small **nafs** — the trembling human soul — and ends with the infinite **Nafs**, the divine Self. Between those two *nafs* the whole drama unfolds.

Watch the movement:

- It begins in the **third person** (every soul, far away) and turns to the **second person** (you, here).
- It begins with deeds **delivered** and ends with a Lord **declaring Himself.**
- It begins with the soul's **impossible wish** for distance and ends with Allah's **possible nearness** — *raʾūf.*
- It begins inside the **closed accounting room** with no exit, and ends with an open hand: *tender toward His servants.*

The verse takes you all the way down — into the room where the deed is present and the wish is refused — and then, in its final four words, lifts you all the way back up into tenderness. **The structure is a descent into the worst, followed by the revelation that the One who showed it to you did so out of love.**

### Bringing it together

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes turn out to be the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a soul *longing* (tawaddu) for *time* (amad) on a Day where time is gone, recoiling from a deed *fused* to itself. The themes showed us that this is *us* — every regret we anesthetize with distance and time — and that the window to actually create that distance, through repentance, is open *only now.*

And the grammar showed us a sudden turn to *you*, a *Self* who warns, and a final word of *tenderness.* The theme showed us that this warning is not the absence of love but its highest expression — the One who is *raʾūf bi'l-ʿibād* refusing to let you reach that room unwarned.

Put them together and you have the whole verse in motion: **He shows you the day you'll wish for a distance you can't have — precisely so that, today, you'll seek the nearness you can.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the one deed I most instinctively reach to put "distance and time" between myself and — and have I ever actually brought it to Allah, or have I only ever fled it?
2. The soul's full power of longing (*wadd*) fires off on that Day toward escape. Where is my *wadd* — my deepest love and yearning — actually aimed right now?
3. When I read a verse of warning, whose voice do I hear: a judge looking for a reason, or the One who is *raʾūf* and cannot bear to see me fall?
4. If distance and time are mercies that let me deal with my deeds *now*, am I using the window — or assuming it will stay open?
5. What would change in my next twenty-four hours if I truly believed that everything I do today is being *brought present* for a room I will one day walk into?

### One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day when distance and time no longer exist, every soul will desperately wish to be far from its own deeds — and the God who warns you of this now does so only because He is too tender to let you arrive there unprepared.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to wait for the room with no clocks to deal with what you carry. The wish that will be denied to every soul on that Day — *let there be distance* — is being *offered* to you, freely, in this very moment, through a single turn of the heart back toward Allah. The verse is not trying to frighten you into despair. It is trying to walk you, gently and urgently, toward the door while the door is still open. Let it.

### Du'ā

*O Allah, You who are Raʾūf with Your servants — You warned us of Yourself out of tenderness, so let that warning reach our hearts before the Day reaches our deeds. Put a true distance between us and every wrong we have done, the only distance that is real: the distance of Your forgiveness. And on the Day every soul finds its record present, let what we find make us rejoice, not recoil.* Āmīn.
