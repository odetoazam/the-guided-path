# The Tireless Habit: Why Denial Repeats Itself Across Every Generation

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first, and then I want us to slow all the way down.

> **كَدَأْبِ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ وَالَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ ۚ كَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا فَأَخَذَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ**

*Ka-daʾbi āli firʿawna wa-lladhīna min qablihim — kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā fa-akhadhahumu-llāhu bi-dhunūbihim, wa-llāhu shadīdu-l-ʿiqāb.*

**"Like the way of the people of Pharaoh and those before them — they denied Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment."**

Fourteen words. And on the surface it reads like a history lesson — a warning that points backward, names a famous tyrant, and reminds us how his story ended.

But I want to suggest to you that this verse is not really about the past at all.

### Let's rebuild the world this landed in

To feel the weight of these words, you have to put yourself in Madinah in the months after the Battle of Badr. The young Muslim community had just survived something that should not have happened — a small, under-equipped band had stood against a far larger Quraysh force, and they had won. Imagine the air in the city. Relief. Disbelief. A sense that history had just bent.

And on the other side, there were those watching this and refusing to read the sign for what it was. Among the narrations preserved by the classical commentators — al-Jalālayn records this occasion — the Prophet ﷺ, after returning from Badr, called the Jewish community of Madinah to embrace Islam, pointing to what had just happened as evidence. And the response came back, in effect: *"Don't let it go to your head that you killed a handful of inexperienced Quraysh who knew nothing about war."*

Sit with that response for a moment. Something extraordinary had occurred right in front of them — and the human mind found a way to file it under "coincidence." To explain it away. To deny the sign while looking straight at it.

[PAUSE]

And it is *into that exact moment* that Allah says: **kadaʾbi āli firʿawn.** *Like the habit of Pharaoh's people, and those before them.*

In other words: this thing you are doing right now — this looking at a clear sign and refusing to see it — **you did not invent this.** You are not the first. There is a whole lineage of this behavior stretching back through history, and Allah is about to tell you exactly where that road leads, because He has watched it travel that road many times before.

### The Hook

Here is what I want us to chase today. The verse does not say *"like Pharaoh's people who disbelieved."* It uses a very specific word — **daʾb** — a word so rich that the classical scholars gave it four or five different meanings at once. It is a word the Arabs used for the sun and the moon. A word for relentless, tireless, never-stopping motion.

Why would Allah reach for *that* word to describe the act of denial?

What if the Quran is telling us that disbelief is not a thought you have — but a *machine you run*? Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word **دَأْب** — the tireless habit

#### First, the concept

Before we open the word, let me hand you a simple idea from your own life.

There is a difference between something you *do once* and something that has become *the way you are.* The first time you check your phone in the morning, that's an action. By the ten-thousandth morning, it's not an action anymore — it's a groove. It runs on its own. You don't decide to do it; it does itself. You reach for the phone before you've even decided to be awake.

We have a word in English for the small version of this: *habit.* But we don't really have a word for the *large* version — the kind of habit that defines an entire people, that gets passed down, that becomes the operating system of a civilization. The kind of pattern so deep it has its own momentum and pulls generation after generation into the same groove.

Hold that idea. Now let's open the Arabic.

#### The word itself

> **كَدَأْبِ** — *ka-daʾb* — "like the *daʾb* of…"

The root is **د-أ-ب (d-ʾ-b)**. Listen to what the lexicons actually preserve, because the range is stunning.

Al-Qurtubī, opening this very verse, tells us: *al-daʾb* is **al-ʿāda wa-l-shaʾn** — the habit, the characteristic affair. And then he gives us the verb beneath it: *daʾaba al-rajulu fī ʿamalihi* — a man does *daʾb* in his work — *idhā jadda wa-jtahada* — **when he strives and exerts himself relentlessly.** He adds: *adʾaba baʿīrahu* — he drove his camel to *daʾb* — meaning **he pushed it to its limit on the journey, on and on without rest.**

So at the root, *daʾb* is not lazy repetition. It is *driven, exhausting, relentless persistence.* Effort that does not stop.

[PAUSE]

And now al-Qurtubī gives us the image that I want to engrave on your heart. He says: **wa-l-dāʾibāni: al-laylu wa-l-nahār.** *The two dāʾib ones are the night and the day.*

Stay with this. Why are night and day called *al-dāʾibān*?

Because they never stop. They never tire. They never take a day off. Night follows day follows night follows day, and it has been doing this since before you were born and it will do it after you are gone. It is the most relentless, tireless, self-perpetuating pattern in all of creation. The Quran itself uses this exact root in Surah Ibrāhīm — *wa-sakhkhara lakumu-l-shamsa wa-l-qamara dāʾibayn* — "He made the sun and the moon, both *dāʾib*" — both ceaselessly, tirelessly running their course.

#### What this does to the verse

Now go back and read what Allah actually said.

He did not say their *disbelief* was like Pharaoh's people. He said their **daʾb** was like Pharaoh's people.

Do you feel the shift? Allah is not comparing one act to another act. **He is comparing one tireless engine to another tireless engine.** He is saying: the way denial *runs* in you — relentlessly, automatically, generation after generation, never stopping, like night chasing day across the centuries — *that* is what Pharaoh's people had, and the people before them, and now you.

Denial, the verse is telling us, is not a verdict you reach. **It is a treadmill you climb onto.** And once a people climbs on, the thing runs by itself. It becomes their *daʾb* — their tireless, defining, inherited habit. The Quraysh explaining away Badr were not thinking a fresh thought. They had stepped onto the oldest treadmill in human history, the one Pharaoh died running.

Al-Baghawī, beautifully, gives us not one meaning but a whole chorus of the early scholars on this single word — and I want you to hear them not as competing options but as camera angles on one reality. Ibn ʿAbbās, ʿIkrima, and Mujāhid read *kadaʾb* as **ka-fiʿl** — "like the *doing* of Pharaoh's people." ʿAṭāʾ and al-Kisāʾī read it as **ka-sunna** — "like the *established way* of Pharaoh's people." Al-Akhfash: **ka-amr wa-shaʾn** — "like their *whole affair and condition.*" Al-Naḍr ibn Shumayl: **ka-ʿāda** — "like their *habit.*"

Put those four together and you have the full anatomy of how evil entrenches itself: it begins as a *doing* (fiʿl), it hardens into a *habit* (ʿāda), it gets institutionalized as a *way* (sunna), and finally it becomes the people's *entire identity and condition* (shaʾn). That is the life cycle of denial in one word.

And this is no late refinement. Al-Ṭabarī, the earliest of the great commentators, already reads *kadaʾb* as **ka-sunnati āli firʿawna wa-ʿādatihim** — "like the *established way* of Pharaoh's people and their *habit*" — citing the early philologist Abū ʿUbayda. The reading that denial does not stay an act but hardens into a *sunna,* a transmissible institution that a whole people lives inside, sits at the very root of the tafsīr tradition.

**What this single word reveals:** disbelief is not an isolated decision — it is a self-perpetuating pattern that runs tirelessly through a soul and through history, and the question is never just "what did they think?" but "what *engine* did they climb onto?"

### Element Two: The doubled letter in **كَذَّبُوا** — denial with force behind it

#### First, the concept

In Arabic, you can take a simple verb and *intensify* it by doubling the middle letter. It is one of the most elegant features of the language. The single-letter version describes a plain action; the doubled version describes that action done with force, with repetition, with deliberateness, done *again and again and on purpose.*

Think of the difference in English between "he broke it" and "he *smashed* it to pieces." Same basic event — completely different energy. The doubling in Arabic is that extra energy, built right into the spine of the word.

#### The word itself

> **كَذَّبُوا** — *kadhdhabū* — "they denied."

The root is **ك-ذ-ب (k-dh-b)**, and the plain form of this verb, *kadhaba*, simply means "to lie." But that is not the form Allah uses here. Listen to the word: *kadh-dha-bū.* That doubled middle letter — the *shadda* sitting on the *dhāl* — moves the verb into its intensive, declarative form. This is **takdhīb.**

And *takdhīb* does not mean "they told a lie." It means something far more active and aggressive: **they pronounced the truth to be a lie. They cried lies against it. They actively, repeatedly, deliberately rejected something as false.**

#### What this does to the verse

Notice what they did this *to*: **bi-āyātinā** — "Our signs." The root here is *āya* — a sign, a proof, a marker pointing to something beyond itself.

So the full picture is not "they were ignorant" or "they hadn't heard." The intensive form slams that door shut. The signs *came to them.* They saw them. And then they did not merely overlook them — they took the truth and, with force and repetition, **stamped it FALSE.** Over and over. As a practice. As a *daʾb.*

Do you see how the two words we've studied lock together? **Kadhdhabū** is the engine of **daʾb** made visible. The tireless habit of the first word *expresses itself* as the relentless, deliberate stamping-false of the second. Pharaoh did not glance at Mūsā's miracles once and shrug. He saw the staff, the hand, the years of signs — and he *kept* stamping them false, again and again, as his settled policy. That is takdhīb. That is what becomes a daʾb.

And here is the quiet warning hidden in the grammar: you do not arrive at *kadhdhabū* by accident. The intensive form describes something practiced. Which means there were earlier, softer moments — small denials, little explainings-away — that they rehearsed until the rejection became forceful and automatic. **The smashing was built one tap at a time.**

Al-Muyassar adds a second word that completes the portrait, and it closes off the last excuse. The signs they stamped false, it stresses, were *āyāt Allāh **al-wāḍiḥa*** — the *clear,* unmistakable signs; there was no fog to hide behind, no honest room to mistake them. And the engine driving the denial was not confusion but **ʿinād** — sheer obstinacy. *Fa-ʿājalahum bi-l-ʿuqūbati bi-sababi takdhībihim wa-ʿinādihim:* He hastened the punishment to them *because of their denial and their stubbornness.* Takdhīb is what the mouth does; ʿinād is the will underneath it that refuses to bend even when the sign is plain in front of it. Put the two together and you have the full anatomy of a denial no one could ever call honest error.

**What this word reveals:** the people Allah seizes are not the merely mistaken. They are those who met the truth, recognized it as a sign, and made a sustained, forceful practice of calling it false.

### Element Three: The single letter **بِ** in **بِذُنُوبِهِمْ** — the most merciful word in a verse about punishment

#### First, the concept

Sometimes the most important word in a verse is one letter long.

In Arabic, the little prefix **بِ** (the letter *bāʾ*) has many jobs, but one of its most important is what grammarians call the *bāʾ of causation* — the *bāʾ al-sababiyya.* It means "by reason of," "because of," "on account of." It links an outcome to its *exact cause.* When you attach this *bāʾ* to something, you are saying: *this* happened *because of* precisely *that* — and nothing else.

Keep that in mind, because Allah is about to use it at the most charged moment in the verse.

#### The phrase itself

> **فَأَخَذَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ** — *fa-akhadhahumu-llāhu bi-dhunūbihim* — "so Allah seized them **by reason of their sins.**"

Two things to notice before the *bāʾ.* First, that **فَ** — the little *fāʾ* at the start of *fa-akhadhahum.* It is the *fāʾ* of immediate consequence — "and *so*," "and *then* at once." It bolts the seizing directly onto the denying with no gap in between. *They stamped the truth false — and so He seized them.* Cause, effect, no space to breathe between them. Al-Muyassar captures that same compression in a single verb: *ʿājalahum bi-l-ʿuqūba* — He **hastened** the punishment upon them. Once the daʾb had finished running its course, there was no drawn-out reprieve.

Second, the verb **أَخَذَ** — *akhadha* — "He took, He seized." This is the language of a sudden, decisive grip. Al-Muyassar and al-Baghawī both gloss it as *fa-ʿāqabahum* / *ahlakahum* — He penalized them, He destroyed them. It is the grip of someone seized by the collar when the running is finally over.

#### Now the one letter that changes everything

Allah does not say *fa-akhadhahumu-llāh* — "so Allah seized them" — and leave it there, as raw, arbitrary power. He adds two syllables: **bi-dhunūbihim.** *By reason of THEIR OWN sins.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the heart of it.

That little *bāʾ* does something profound. It tells you that the seizing was **not arbitrary.** Allah, who needs no justification and answers to no one, *chose to tell you the cause.* And the cause He names is not His will alone — it is **their sins.** Their *own.* Authored by their *own* hands, run by their *own* tireless *daʾb.*

Think about what this means. Allah could have simply said "He destroyed them" and we would have no right to ask why. Instead He attaches the outcome, by that single letter, to the precise thing they themselves produced. The punishment is not something that *fell on* them from outside. It is the exact weight of what they *carried in.* The grip closes on the collar of their own choosing.

This is why I called it the most merciful word in a verse about punishment. Because **bi-dhunūbihim** is a statement of perfect justice — and perfect justice is itself a form of honor. Allah is treating these people as *moral agents,* not as objects. He is saying: nothing happened to you that you did not write. You denied; the denial became your habit; the habit became your affair; and the affair, by reason of itself, became the grip. Every link in that chain was yours.

And read it the other way, and it becomes pure hope: if the seizing comes only *bi-dhunūb* — by reason of sins — then where there is no sustained, defiant sin, there is no such grip. The door that closed on Pharaoh's people closed on *their* hands. It is not waiting, arbitrary, for you.

**What this single letter reveals:** Allah's severity is never random. It is calibrated, caused, and self-authored — the exact return of what a people tirelessly sent ahead.

### Bridge

So look at what these words, standing together, have built.

**Daʾb** told us denial is a tireless, self-running engine. **Kadhdhabū** told us that engine expresses itself as the forceful, repeated stamping-false of clear signs. And the **bāʾ of bi-dhunūbihim** told us that what finally seizes such a people is nothing but the exact weight of that engine's own output.

The whole verse is a single closed circuit: *habit → denial → seizing → and the seizing is caused by the habit.* They authored every step.

And the circuit is not merely our inference — the verse draws it itself. Al-Jalālayn notes a quiet grammatical gift here: the sentence *kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā fa-akhadhahumu-llāh* stands as **mufassira** — an explanation of the daʾb that the word *kadaʾb* had named but not yet unpacked. The Quran does not leave "the tireless habit" hanging as an abstraction; in the very next breath it tells you exactly what that habit was *made of* (the denying of signs) and exactly where it *ended* (the seizing). The diagnosis and the prognosis arrive in one sentence.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Denial is not an event — it is a practice you rehearse

#### Begin where you already live

Let me start somewhere completely ordinary.

Think about how anyone actually changes — for the worse. No one wakes up one morning and decides, fully formed, to become a person who lies, or who cuts corners, or who is cruel. That is not how it works. It works in *taps.* A small thing the first time, and a flicker of discomfort. A slightly bigger thing the second time, and slightly less discomfort. By the hundredth time, no discomfort at all — and by then it isn't even a choice anymore. It's just *who you are now.* The groove is cut. The treadmill is running.

Psychologists have a name for the fading of that discomfort — they call it desensitization. Every repetition lowers the alarm. Eventually the alarm doesn't ring at all, and you mistake its silence for peace.

This is *exactly* what the word **daʾb** is describing. And here is the thing the verse adds that the psychology textbook cannot: it tells you this happens not just in a person but in a *people,* and not just in a lifetime but across *centuries.*

#### Let the verse illuminate it

> **كَدَأْبِ آلِ فِرْعَوْنَ وَالَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ** — "like the *daʾb* of Pharaoh's people, *and those before them.*"

Notice Allah did not stop at Pharaoh. He said *"and those before them."* He is deliberately drawing the line backward past the most famous tyrant in scripture, to a whole chain of nations stretching into the dark behind him. He is showing you that Pharaoh himself was not original. Al-Jalālayn names the nations the verse gestures toward — **ʿĀd and Thamūd** — peoples already turned to dust and proverb by the time Pharaoh rose to his throne. So even the great archetype of tyranny was a latecomer. Pharaoh, too, stepped onto a treadmill someone else had already built. He inherited the *daʾb.*

This is the terrifying and clarifying insight: **the pattern is older than any of its members.** Every generation that denies thinks it is reasoning freshly. The Quraysh after Badr thought they were being shrewd — "you just got lucky against some amateurs." They had no idea they were reciting Pharaoh's lines. Pharaoh, drowning, suddenly believed — and was told he was far too late, that he had been running an ancient script all along.

#### Build it forward

So what breaks the loop?

Here is the mercy hidden in naming the pattern. **You cannot step off a treadmill you don't know you're on.** The entire reason Allah tells you about Pharaoh's *daʾb* is so that when you feel yourself starting to explain away a clear sign — a truth you know in your chest but find inconvenient — you will hear a quiet voice say: *I have seen this exact move before. This is the first tap. This is how the groove begins.*

That recognition is the off-ramp. The person who can say "this rationalization I'm reaching for — this is Pharaoh's move" has already half-escaped it. The pattern's power depends entirely on you believing your denial is original, reasonable, *yours.* The verse strips that illusion away.

And notice the difference between you and Pharaoh's people in one crucial respect: **they didn't have this verse.** They couldn't read their own pattern from the outside. You can. The naming of the daʾb is a gift handed specifically to the one who comes after — so that the chain of "those before them" might finally have someone who steps off.

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Here is the practical heart of it. Tonight, or tomorrow, there will be a small moment — a truth you'd rather not act on, a wrong you'd rather rationalize, a sign you'd rather file under "coincidence." A tap.

The practice is simply this: **catch the first tap.** Not the hundredth. The first. The moment you feel yourself reaching for the explanation that lets you off the hook, name it: *this is the beginning of a daʾb.* Because the verse has taught you that the smashing — *kadhdhabū* — was built one tap at a time. Interrupt the first tap and the engine never starts. Let it run a hundred times and you will not even remember choosing.

### Theme Two: Nothing falls on you that your own hands did not write

#### Begin where you already live

Let me ask you about something we all carry: the fear that life is arbitrary. That bad things just *happen,* randomly, that the universe doesn't keep books, that a person can do everything wrong and walk free while another does everything right and gets crushed. That fear sits underneath a lot of modern anxiety — the sense of living in a world with no ledger, where consequences are detached from causes.

And underneath *that* fear is an even deeper one, the opposite one: the fear of a God who is severe *arbitrarily.* Who might seize you for no reason you could have known or prevented. A power so great that it doesn't need to be fair.

This verse speaks directly into both fears at once. And it does it with that one letter — the *bāʾ.*

#### Let the verse illuminate it

> **فَأَخَذَهُمُ اللَّهُ بِذُنُوبِهِمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ شَدِيدُ الْعِقَابِ** — "so Allah seized them *by reason of their sins.* And Allah is severe in punishment."

Read quickly, that last phrase — *shadīd al-ʿiqāb,* "severe in punishment" — sounds like the fearful, arbitrary God. The sounds themselves are heavy: that hard *qāf,* that strong *ʿiqāb.* It lands like a closing door.

But it does not stand alone. It comes *after* **bi-dhunūbihim.** And that order is everything. Before Allah tells you He is severe, He tells you His severity is *caused* — and caused by *their sins,* not His whim. The severity is real. The *qāf* is heavy on purpose. But it is **bound.** It is leashed to justice. Severe, yes — but never severe *for no reason.*

Notice, too, the word *ʿiqāb* itself. Its root, **ʿ-q-b,** is the root of *ʿāqiba* — the *end,* the *outcome,* what comes *after* a thing, the *heel* that follows the step. The very word for "punishment" here is built from the idea of *consequence* — of what trails behind an action as its natural sequel. The punishment is named, at the root, as the *end of a road they walked.*

#### Build it forward

Now feel what this does to both of your fears at once.

To the fear of an arbitrary universe with no ledger: the verse says *there is a ledger.* The most absolute Power that exists *chose to record the cause.* He did not need to. He told you anyway. The books are kept.

To the fear of an arbitrary God: the verse says *His severity is not loose.* Pharaoh was not seized because Allah was displeased on a particular morning. He was seized *bi-dhunūbihi* — by the exact weight of a lifetime of tireless, deliberate denial. Every gram of the consequence was authored by Pharaoh's own hand. The grip closed on a collar he had been holding out for years.

And notice what that grip cut straight through. The verse just before this one had declared that the wealth and the children of the disbelievers would avail them *nothing* against Allah — and both al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr read verse 11 as the living proof of verse 10. Pharaoh's people *had* it all: the wealth, the standing armies, the dynasty, the Nile itself bending to their command. And when the grip came *bi-dhunūbihim,* every gram of that worldly leverage stood useless before it. The *daʾb* drew a consequence that no amount of power or possession could buy off. That is precisely why the seizing of Pharaoh is summoned here — to show a generation counting its own advantages after Badr that the ledger the *daʾb* runs up is paid in a currency their wealth cannot touch.

[PAUSE]

And here is the paradox I want to leave unresolved, because it is meant to be sat with, not solved: **the same verse that frightens you should also be the one that frees you.** A God who punishes *bi-dhunūb* — strictly by reason of sins — is a God you can actually *stand before.* Because it means your turning back *matters.* It means the road has off-ramps. It means that where the tireless denial stops, the grip has nothing to close on. A God of arbitrary severity offers no door. A God of *caused* severity has built a door into the very structure of His justice — and that door is called *tawba,* the turning back.

The other side of *bi-dhunūbihim* is breathtaking when you let it land: if He seizes *only* by reason of sins, then He does not seize by reason of your weakness, your past, your fear, your stumbling. Only by reason of the sustained, defiant, unrepented *daʾb.* Everything short of that, He has left a way out of.

#### What this changes about tomorrow

So here is the practice for this one.

Stop reading your hardships as random verdicts from an arbitrary sky — and stop reading them as proof you've been seized. The verse re-trains the eye. When something difficult comes, the question is not "why is this happening *to* me, with no reason?" The question the believer learns to ask is: *"Is there a cause here I authored? Is there a daʾb of mine this is the ʿāqiba of?"* — not in self-torture, but in honest review, because a caused world is one you can actually navigate.

And then carry the mercy alongside the fear, the way the verse does. Let *shadīd al-ʿiqāb* keep you awake — but never let it convince you the door is shut. Because the *bāʾ* that makes His punishment severe is the very same *bāʾ* that makes it escapable. Stop the cause, and you have stopped the consequence.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of our journey turn out to be one truth seen from two sides.

The **linguistic** discovery was about *mechanism:* denial is a *daʾb,* a tireless engine; it runs as *kadhdhabū,* the forceful stamping-false of signs; and it is met by a seizing that is bound, by a single letter, to its own cause.

The **thematic** discovery was about *us:* that we become what we rehearse, generation after generation — and that nothing in the end falls on us that our own hands did not write.

They are the same insight. The *daʾb* of the first half *is* the *dhunūb* of the second. The tireless habit and the authored sins are one thing, traced once through grammar and once through the human heart. The verse is a closed circuit, and we are standing inside it: the habit produces the denial, the denial accrues the sins, the sins draw the grip — and at every single step, the hand on the pen is ours.

That is why Allah pointed to Pharaoh and *"those before them."* Not to tell us a story about the dead. To hold up a mirror to the living.

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life have I mistaken a *daʾb* — a deep, running habit — for a fresh, reasonable choice I make each time?
2. Is there a clear sign in my life right now that I am quietly, repeatedly filing under "coincidence" because acting on it would cost me something?
3. When I feel the first tap — the first small rationalization — do I catch it, or do I only notice the engine once it's already running?
4. Do I carry a secret fear of an *arbitrary* God? And what would change if I truly believed His severity is bound, by a single letter, to cause?
5. If He seizes *only* by reason of sustained, unrepented sins — what door has He left open for me that I have been too afraid to walk through?

### One-sentence distillation

**Denial is never a single event but a tireless inherited habit, and Allah's severity is never arbitrary but the exact, self-authored weight of that habit's own output — which means the same justice that should frighten us is also the open door that frees us.**

### The closing invitation

I am not asking you to walk away afraid. I am inviting you to walk away *awake* — awake to the treadmills running quietly beneath your reasonable-sounding choices, and awake to the mercy hidden inside a verse about punishment. Pharaoh's people couldn't see the pattern they were running. You just did. That seeing is the gift, and it is meant to become an off-ramp. Catch the first tap. Trust the door.

### Brief duʿāʾ

*O Allah, who seizes only by reason of what our own hands send ahead — let us catch the first tap of every denial before it becomes our daʾb. Open our eyes to the signs we are tempted to call coincidence, and let the door of turning back, which You built into the very justice of Your severity, never close on us. You are severe in punishment — and for that very reason, we run to You and not away.*

*Āmīn.*
