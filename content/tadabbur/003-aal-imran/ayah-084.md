# Say "We" — The Confession That Refuses to Fragment

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch the meaning.

> قُلْ ءَامَنَّا بِٱللَّهِ وَمَآ أُنزِلَ عَلَيْنَا وَمَآ أُنزِلَ عَلَىٰٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ وَإِسْمَٰعِيلَ وَإِسْحَٰقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَٱلْأَسْبَاطِ وَمَآ أُوتِىَ مُوسَىٰ وَعِيسَىٰ وَٱلنَّبِيُّونَ مِن رَّبِّهِمْ لَا نُفَرِّقُ بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِّنْهُمْ وَنَحْنُ لَهُۥ مُسْلِمُونَ

*Qul āmannā billāhi wa mā unzila ʿalaynā wa mā unzila ʿalā Ibrāhīma wa Ismāʿīla wa Isḥāqa wa Yaʿqūba wal-asbāṭi wa mā ūtiya Mūsā wa ʿĪsā wan-nabiyyūna min rabbihim. Lā nufarriqu bayna aḥadin minhum, wa naḥnu lahū muslimūn.*

"Say: We believe in Allah, and in what was sent down to us, and what was sent down to Ibrahim, Ismail, Ishaq, Yaqub, and the tribes, and in what was given to Musa, Isa, and the prophets from their Lord. We make no division between any one of them — and to Him we are submitted."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel why this ayah matters, you have to stand in Madinah in those early years.

This is a city of arguments. Not loud market arguments — deep ones, about whose lineage carries the truth. There are communities here who trace their faith through Musa, and a community newly forming around Muhammad ﷺ, and not far off, the memory and presence of those who follow Isa. And the air is thick with a particular kind of claim: *the truth came through* **our** *prophet. Yours is lesser. Yours is suspect. Yours was a deviation from ours.*

Aal-Imran is, in large part, a long and patient conversation with the People of the Book. And just before our ayah, the surah does something breathtaking. In 3:81 it tells us that Allah took a covenant from **the prophets themselves** — that each one, when the next messenger came confirming what he had, would believe in him and support him. The prophets are not rivals. They were bound, by covenant, to one another.

Then 3:83 asks the question that hangs in the air: *"Do they desire other than the religion of Allah — while to Him has submitted whoever is in the heavens and the earth, willingly or unwillingly?"*

[PAUSE]

And into that charged silence — into a world organized by *whose prophet is whose* — comes a single word of command. **Qul.** Say.

There is a hidden grammar to that command worth noticing. Al-Tabari points out that the verse opens on a swallowed condition: the unspoken sense is *so if they seek a religion other than Allah's, O Muhammad, then say to them…* The "if" clause is left off the page — but the **Qul** is its answer. So this confession is not a free-floating creed; it is a *reply*, placed in the believer's mouth as the response to a community that wanted a faith of its own making. You are being handed the words to say back.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin. In a world arguing about which messenger to belong to, Allah does not tell the believer to win the argument. He tells him to make a **confession** — and the confession is built in a way that quietly dismantles the entire argument from the inside.

It is one of the shortest creeds in the Quran. Thirty words. And almost every one of them is doing something. The command is singular but the answer is plural. Two different verbs are used for how revelation reached the prophets — and the difference is not random. And right at the heart of it sits a refusal — *we make no division* — followed by a strange, precise little word: *not between any* **one**.

Let's go slowly. There is far more here than a list of names.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The singular command and the plural answer — *Qul āmannā*

Let me teach you something simple about how commands work before we open the word.

When I give you an instruction, the grammar usually keeps you and me in the same number. "You — go." "You — speak." The command lands on a single person, and what follows stays with that single person.

Now look at how this ayah opens.

**قُلْ** — *Qul.* "Say." It is an imperative, and it is **singular**. One person is being addressed. We know exactly who: the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. Allah is speaking to one man.

But look at what He commands that one man to say.

**ءَامَنَّا** — *āmannā.* From the root *a-m-n*, the root of faith, of security, of trust. And the ending — that *-nā* — is **"we."** Not "I believe." **We** believe.

Sit with this for a moment. The command comes to **one**. The confession comes out as **many**.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah did not say *"Say: I believe."* That would have been the natural grammatical match — singular command, singular answer. He could have. He didn't. He took a singular mouth and put a collective heart inside it.

Here's why that matters. The Prophet ﷺ is not being told to give *his personal opinion* about the prophets. He is being told to voice **the creed of a community** — a community that includes him, and you, and everyone who will ever say these words after him. The moment the Prophet speaks this, he is not speaking as an individual with a view. He is speaking as the mouth of the *ummah*.

And think about what that does in that Madinan room full of *whose-prophet-is-whose*. Everyone else is saying *I, my lineage, my messenger.* The believer is taught to answer in the first person plural — a "we" so wide it is about to reach back and gather Ibrahim, Musa, and Isa into the same sentence.

The grammar is already the message. **Before a single prophet is named, the "we" has been built.** Faith here is not a private possession you defend. It is a belonging you step into.

And there is a tenderness in this you might miss. *Āmannā* is in the perfect tense — the form Arabic uses for something settled, accomplished, done. Not "we are trying to believe." Not "we will believe." **We have believed.** It is spoken as a thing already true. You are not auditioning for faith. You are declaring a home you already live in.

That is what the first two words give us. A command to one, a confession of many, spoken as already done. [PAUSE]

### Element Two: Two verbs, two prepositions — *unzila ʿalā* and *ūtiya*

Now I want to show you something that hides in plain sight, and once you see it you will never read this ayah flatly again.

There are two different verbs in this ayah for how revelation came to the prophets. Watch them.

First: **أُنزِلَ** — *unzila.* "Was sent down." From the root *n-z-l*, to descend. This is a Form IV construction — the form that takes a simple action and makes it transitive, makes something *come down* by an agent's act. And crucially, it appears here in the **passive**: not "He sent down," but "was sent down." The Sender is left unspoken — because everyone knows Who sends down. And notice the preposition that follows it twice:

*Wa mā unzila **ʿalaynā*** — "what was sent down **upon us**."
*Wa mā unzila **ʿalā** Ibrāhīma…* — "what was sent down **upon** Ibrahim…"

*ʿalā.* Upon. Onto. The word carries the image of something descending **from above onto a recipient below.** Revelation as something that lands on you, that comes *down upon* you from a height.

Now the second verb: **أُوتِىَ** — *ūtiya.* "Was given." From the root *a-t-y*, to come, in Form IV — to *bring*, to *give*. Again passive: "was given." But look — **there is no *ʿalā* here.**

*Wa mā **ūtiya** Mūsā wa ʿĪsā…* — "and what was **given** to Musa and Isa…"

No "upon." The gift simply *reaches* them. It is **handed** to them.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Why two verbs? Why does the Quran say revelation was "sent down upon" Ibrahim and his line, but "given" to Musa and Isa?

Here is one way to feel it. *Unzila ʿalā* — "sent down upon" — emphasizes the **direction and source**: from the heavens, downward, onto the prophet. Your eye is pulled upward to where it comes *from*. *Ūtiya* — "was given" — emphasizes the **arrival and possession**: it reached him, it became his to carry, it was placed in his hands. Your eye rests on the recipient, holding what he has received.

These are not two different kinds of revelation in their source — both are *min rabbihim*, from their Lord, as the ayah will say. They are two camera angles on the same act. One angle shows you the descent. The other shows you the delivery.

And do you see what this does? The Quran is being **scrupulously even-handed** while varying its language. To the line of Ibrahim — Ismail, Ishaq, Yaqub, the tribes — and to "us," the language of *sending down upon.* To Musa and Isa — the two figures most disputed in that Madinan room, the prophets of the two great communities of the Book — the language of *being given.* No one's revelation is described as lesser. Each is honored. The variety in the words is not a hierarchy; it is the richness of a single truth described from more than one side.

And those names carry histories worth pausing on. They are not a random list — they are a single bloodline drawn taut across generations. Al-Muyassar walks it for us: Ibrahim *the friend of Allah*, then his two sons Ismail and Ishaq, then Ishaq's son Yaqub — grandfather, father, grandson, three links of one chain. *Al-asbāṭ* — rendered here simply as "the tribes" — are not a vague crowd; the classical commentators explain them as the prophets raised up among the descendants of Yaqub, the prophetic line scattered through the twelve tribes of the Children of Israel. And what was *given* to Musa and to Isa the commentators name plainly: the Tawrah placed in the hands of one, the Injil in the hands of the other. So the confession is not gesturing at "religion in general." It is reaching, by name, into the precise scriptures the two great communities of the Book held as their own — and folding them, honored and intact, into the believer's own "we."

A small but real grammatical thing seals this: throughout, the verbs are **passive** — *unzila, ūtiya.* "Was sent down," "was given." Not one prophet is the author. Every one of them is a **recipient.** Whatever they hold, they hold because it was placed in their hands from above. [PAUSE]

And that, quietly, undoes the whole argument outside. If your prophet and my prophet were both *receivers* of the same Giver, then "my prophet versus your prophet" was always a category mistake. You cannot make the messengers compete when none of them owned the message.

### Element Three: The refusal at the center — *lā nufarriqu bayna aḥadin minhum*

Now we reach the heart. After the names, after the two verbs, the confession turns and makes a vow.

**لَا نُفَرِّقُ** — *lā nufarriqu.* "We make no division."

Let me teach you the word before we feel it. The root is *f-r-q*: to separate, to split, to part one thing from another. From this root come words you may know — *furqān*, that which separates truth from falsehood; *firqah*, a faction, a splinter-group; *tafarruq*, the scattering of a people into pieces.

But notice the **form** of the verb here. It is not the simple Form I, *faraqa* — "to separate." It is **Form II**, *farraqa* — and Form II in Arabic intensifies and multiplies the action. *Faraqa* is to separate. **Farraqa** is to separate thoroughly — to break into many pieces, to fragment, to drive wedges, to keep on dividing. It is the difference between cutting something and shattering it.

So when the believer says *lā nufarriqu*, he is not merely saying "we don't tell them apart." He is saying: **we do not do that violent work of fragmenting. We refuse to be the ones who shatter this into rival pieces.**

[PAUSE]

And now look at the object of that refusal — and this is the detail I promised you in the introduction.

He does not say *lā nufarriqu baynahum* — "we make no division **between them.**" That would have been smooth and natural. Instead:

**بَيْنَ أَحَدٍ مِّنْهُمْ** — *bayna aḥadin minhum.* "Between **any one** of them."

*Aḥad.* One. A single one. And it is indefinite — *aḥadin* — which in this context spreads to mean *any* one, *not a single* one. The word *aḥad* is the word of absolute oneness; it is, in another place, a name by which Allah Himself is described — *Allāhu aḥad.* It is the most singular word in the language.

Feel the precision. He could have rejected division "between them" as a group. Instead the refusal reaches down to the level of the **individual**: not between *any single one* of them. Pull out one prophet — any one — and try to set him below the rest, and you have already broken the vow. The exclusion of *even one* is the fracture.

Why does that matter so much? Because **this is exactly how fragmentation actually happens.** Nobody rejects all the prophets at once. They affirm the line — *and then carve out one.* "We believe in the messengers… except him." That single exception is the crack that splits the whole. So the confession is built to close the only door fragmentation ever enters through: it forbids the exclusion of even **one.**

The classical commentators land on exactly this point. When they gloss *lā nufarriqu bayna aḥadin minhum*, they read the division being refused as a division *of belief and denial* — affirming some of the messengers as true while branding others as liars. That is the precise fracture the verse forbids. It is not asking you to be blind to the differences between the prophets; it is forbidding the selective faith that says *yes* to one and *no* to another. To believe in the messengers, on this reading, is to believe in all of them — or you have not yet believed in the matter at all. The "except" is the disbelief.

[PAUSE]

Hold the three discoveries together for a second. A "we" wide enough to hold all the prophets. Two verbs that honor each one's revelation without ranking them. And then a vow — intensive, deliberate — *we will not be the ones who shatter this,* sealed at the level of the single individual so that not even one can be cut loose.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human heart holds its loyalties, and about what we are actually for.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The disease of *tafriq* — why we fragment, and the cure built into the creed

Let me start where you already live.

Think about how groups form around you. A team. A family. A community. A movement. They almost always begin around something true and good. And then watch what happens over time. The thing that was meant to *unite* people becomes the thing they *compete over.* "We are the ones who really understand the founder." "Our branch is the authentic one." "They drifted; we kept it pure." The shared good quietly becomes a flag for division.

This is one of the oldest patterns in human life. We take something whole and we *farraqa* it — we shatter it into factions, and then we pledge loyalty to our shard and call it loyalty to the whole.

The Quran names this and treats it as a spiritual disease elsewhere with the same root: *"those who divided their religion and became sects (shiyaʿan)"* — and the warning is severe. Fragmentation isn't a side-issue. It is one of the chief ways a true religion is hollowed out from within. The truth stays on the shelf; the people splinter beneath it.

Now bring that back to our ayah, and see how surgically it works.

The communities in Madinah had each taken a true revelation and turned it into a *firqah* — a faction defined by *its* prophet, against the others. The disease was not disbelief in revelation. It was **selective belief** — affirm mine, diminish yours. And the cure Allah gives is not an argument. It is a **confession that cannot be said in a factional way.** Try it. You cannot recite *āmannā billāhi wa mā unzila…* and remain a partisan of one prophet, because the very sentence makes you say all their names with one breath and then forbids you from cutting out even one.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to feel. Fragmentation works by *addition of an exception.* "Yes, and yes, and yes — but not him." The creed works by *removal of every exception.* "Not between any one of them." It meets the disease exactly where it lives.

And notice — this is not abstract for us. We are not in Madinah, but we carry the same nafs into our masjids, our online communities, our schools of thought. The same instinct that diminished a prophet now diminishes a brother, a scholar, a fellow believer who prays slightly differently. The root *farraqa* is alive in every comment section. The cure is the same posture: a heart trained to say "we" before it says "I," trained to honor before it ranks, trained to recoil from being **the one who shatters.**

So what changes tomorrow? Catch yourself at the moment of the exception — the *"yes, but not them."* That little word "but" is where *tafriq* enters. The discipline of this ayah is to notice that word and ask: *am I about to be the one who fragments?* You do not have to agree with everyone. But you can refuse to be the wedge.

### Theme Two: The destination is not a prophet — it is *Him*

Now the second theme, and it answers a question the first one raises. If we belong to all the prophets and elevate none above the rest — then what, exactly, is our faith *anchored* to? If not "my prophet," then what?

The ayah ends with the answer, and the word order is everything.

**وَنَحْنُ لَهُۥ مُسْلِمُونَ** — *wa naḥnu lahū muslimūn.* "And we — to Him — are submitted."

Let me teach you one thing about Arabic word order. Normally you would expect *wa naḥnu muslimūna lahū* — "we are submitted **to Him**," with "to Him" trailing naturally at the end. But the ayah moves it forward: *lahū* — "**to Him**" — comes *before* "submitted." In Arabic, when you pull a word forward out of its expected place, you are putting a spotlight on it. The fronting carries the force of **restriction**: to Him — *and to Him alone* — are we submitted.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened across the whole ayah?

We spent the entire verse on the prophets. Ibrahim, Ismail, Ishaq, Yaqub, the tribes, Musa, Isa, the prophets. We honored every one of them. We refused to rank them. And then, at the very end, the ayah lifts our face off all of them and points it in a single direction: **lahū — to Him.** Not "to them." To **Him.**

This is the resolution of everything. The reason we make no division between the prophets is that **the prophets were never the destination.** They are all signposts pointing to the same place. To argue over which signpost is greatest is to stand at the signpost arguing — and never walk to where it points. The believer reads every signpost with gratitude, and then **walks.** *Lahū muslimūn.* To Him we submit.

And look at the last word: **مُسْلِمُونَ** — *muslimūn.* This is the active participle of *aslama* — to surrender, to hand yourself over, to give your face entirely. It is the same root that names this entire way of life. It is also the word from 3:83 just before: *to Him has submitted (aslama) whoever is in the heavens and the earth.* The whole cosmos is already *muslim* in this deep sense — surrendered to its Lord. And the believer, at the end of this confession, simply joins what the stars and the mountains and the seas have been doing all along. *We, too — to Him — are submitted.*

And the classical commentators sharpen what kind of submission this is. Al-Jalalayn glosses *muslimūn* here with a single, piercing word: *mukhliṣūn* — those who are **pure, sincere, undivided in their worship.** So this is not a grudging, outward bending of the knee. The same word that began this verse — *farraqa*, to fragment — has its quiet opposite here at the end: *ikhlāṣ*, the gathering of the whole self into one undivided act of worship, with nothing held back and no rival admitted. Al-Muyassar unfolds the same closing phrase as a heart that confesses Him in *rubūbiyyah, ulūhiyyah, and ʿibādah* all at once — *no Lord for us but Him, no object of worship but Him.* Notice how that completes the journey. The verse refused to divide *among* the prophets; now it refuses to divide *the self* among any other master. Outward unity among the messengers, inward unity in the worshipper — both are *the refusal to fragment*, turned in two directions.

[PAUSE]

Feel the architecture of the human heart in this. We are built to attach — to a teacher, a leader, a tradition, a name. That instinct is not wrong; honoring the prophets is commanded here. But the instinct becomes idolatry the moment the signpost becomes the destination — the moment "I follow him" replaces "I submit to Him." Every community that fractured over a prophet had made exactly that slip. They loved the messenger more than they submitted to the One who sent him.

So here is what shifts tomorrow. Examine what your faith is actually resting on. Is it resting on a person — a shaykh, a scholar, a teacher you would defend past the point of truth? Is it resting on *belonging* to the right group? Or is it resting, finally, on **Him**? The test is simple and quiet: when the person you love disappoints you, when the group fractures, when the teacher errs — does your faith fall with them? If it does, your anchor was the signpost. The ayah is calling you to move the anchor. Honor them all. Submit to Him alone.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse as one built thing, because it has a shape.

It **opens** with the widest possible gathering — a singular command producing a plural "we," reaching back across centuries to gather every prophet into one sentence.

It **moves through** that gathering with perfect even-handedness — *sent down upon* this line, *given* to those prophets, every verb passive, no one the author, no one elevated.

It **turns** at the center with a vow against fragmentation — *we make no division between any one of them* — sealing the unity at the level of the single individual so not even one can be pulled loose.

And it **lands**, finally, by lifting our eyes off all of them and fixing them on the One they all pointed to — *and to Him alone we are submitted.*

Gathering. Honoring. Refusing to fragment. Submitting. The verse opens its arms as wide as all of prophecy — and then closes them around a single direction: **Him.**

### Bringing it together

See now that the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **plural "we"** built into a singular command is the grammar of belonging — and belonging is the cure for *tafriq.*

The **two verbs that honor without ranking** are the grammar of even-handedness — and even-handedness is what makes fragmentation impossible.

The **fronted "to Him"** at the end is the grammar of restriction — and that restriction is the whole point: the prophets are the way, but **He** is the destination.

The words and the meaning are one thing. This is a creed engineered so that you cannot recite it and remain a partisan, cannot recite it and worship a man, cannot recite it and shatter the family of faith. You can only recite it and **belong, honor, and submit.**

### Questions to carry

- Where in my life do I add the word *"but"* — *"yes, and yes, but not them"* — and become the one who fragments?
- Is my faith anchored to a person or a group I would defend past the truth — or to Him alone? What happens to my faith when that person disappoints me?
- When I speak of my community, does the word *"we"* come before the word *"I"* — or after?
- Do I read the prophets as signposts I walk past toward Allah, or as places I stop and stand and argue?
- The whole cosmos is already *muslim* — surrendered. In what corner of my life have I not yet joined it?

### One-sentence distillation

Honor every messenger, rank none, and refuse to be the one who fragments — because the prophets were never the destination; **He** is.

### The closing invitation

You will say *āmannā billāhi* many times in your life. Let this ayah change what happens in your chest when you do. Let the "we" be real — wide enough to hold every prophet and every believer who differs from you. Let the refusal be real — catch the *"but"* before it splits something whole. And let the last word be the truest: *lahū muslimūn.* Not to a name. Not to a faction. To Him. Let everything else in the verse be the road, and let Him be where you are finally going.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, who sent down upon the prophets and gave to the messengers from Yourself alone — make our hearts wide enough to honor every one of them, and keep us from ever being the ones who shatter. Anchor our faith not in any name but in Yours, and let our last word always be the truest one: *to You we are submitted.* Āmīn.
