# The Mercy He Sets Apart

## Surah Aal-Imran, Ayah 74

> [ayah:3:74] يَخْتَصُّ بِرَحْمَتِهِۦ مَن يَشَآءُ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ ذُو ٱلْفَضْلِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ

*Yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi man yashā'. Wa-llāhu dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm.*

**"He singles out for His mercy whom He wills, and Allah is the Possessor of immense bounty."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Words

Let us go back to Madinah, into a room we are not usually invited into.

There is a conversation happening — a quiet, strategic one. A faction among the People of the Book is talking among themselves about the new community of believers down the road. And the thing that gnaws at them is not, at its root, theology. Read the verses around this one and you will feel it. It is something older and more human than theology.

It is the question: *why them?*

Why should revelation come through *these* people? Why should prophethood land on an unlettered man from the Arabs, when we are the ones with the scripture, the lineage, the centuries of learning? We were the keepers of the Book. We had the credentials. And the gift went somewhere else.

And the resentment did not stay private. Ibn Kathīr, reading our verse against the ones just before it, describes the calculated form it took: in 3:72 a group among them agreed to make a public show of believing in the revelation at the start of the day and then openly renouncing it by the day's end — a piece of theater designed to plant doubt, to make the believers wonder whether the faith they had embraced was worth holding at all. *If even the People of the Book are walking away from it,* the staged retreat was meant to whisper, *what do they know that we don't?* Envy, here, had hardened into strategy.

The Quran has been answering this faction for several verses. And just before our ayah, in 3:73, it makes a sweeping statement: **"Say — indeed, all bounty is in the hand of Allah; He gives it to whom He wills."** All of it. In His hand. Given by His choice.

And then comes verse 74. The seal on the whole argument.

Now, here is what I want you to notice before we even open the words. You would expect a verse that is answering envy to be *defensive*. You would expect it to argue, to justify, to explain *why* the gift went where it went. To say: here are their merits, here is the wisdom, here is the reason you were passed over.

[PAUSE]

It does none of that.

Instead it does something almost startling. It refuses to defend the choice at all. It simply describes the *nature* of the One who chooses — and in doing so, it dissolves the entire premise of the resentment. By the end of eight short words, the question "why them and not us?" has not been answered. It has been **made unaskable.**

How does a verse do that? How do eight words take a wound as old as Cain and Abel — the wound of watching grace land on someone else — and quietly take the ground out from under it?

That is what we are going to sit with. Stay with me.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Yakhtaṣṣu* — The Verb That Sets Apart

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the whole verse turns on it.

In English, when we want to intensify or reshape a verb, we usually reach for another word. *Look* becomes *look carefully*. *Take* becomes *take for yourself*. We bolt on a second word to carry the new shade of meaning.

Arabic does not do that. Arabic reshapes the verb *from the inside*. A single three-letter root can be poured into different molds — the grammarians call them *forms* — and each mold bends the meaning in a precise, predictable direction. The same root, in one mold, means simply "to do." In another mold, it means "to make someone else do." In another, "to do it to yourself." The letters of the root stay; the shape around them changes the world.

Hold that idea.

Now, the root here is خ ص ص — *kh-ṣ-ṣ*. Its plain meaning is **specificity**. To distinguish one thing from the general mass. The simple Form I verb, *khaṣṣa*, means "to single something out, to particularize it." When you say a gift is *khāṣṣ*, you mean it is special, particular, set apart from the ordinary.

But the Quran does not use the simple form here.

It uses **يَخْتَصُّ — *yakhtaṣṣu*** — the eighth form. The *ifti'āl* mold.

And here is what the eighth form characteristically does to a verb. It folds the action back toward the doer. It carries a reflexive, self-directed sense — *to do something for oneself, to take it to oneself, to claim it as one's own.* The lexicographers capture this precisely: al-Rāghib, in his *Mufradāt*, lists *al-ikhtiṣāṣ* as the act of singling something out and drawing it specially to oneself. It is not just *to specify* — it is **to reserve, to appropriate, to set apart as exclusively one's own to give.**

Al-Ṭabarī, the earliest of the great commentators, grounds this in the plainest grammar. He notes that *yakhtaṣṣu* is the *ifti'āl* (Form VIII) built from the everyday phrase *khaṣaṣtu fulānan bi-kadhā* — "I gave so-and-so such-a-thing exclusively, as his particular portion, to the exclusion of all others." So even at street level, before any theology, the word already carries the picture of one giver, one chosen recipient, and one gift earmarked for him alone by the giver's own act. The Qur'an takes that ordinary picture and lifts it to the scale of God.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the difference.

If the verse had said *yakhuṣṣu* — the simple form — it would mean "He specifies His mercy for whom He wills." A statement about distribution. About where the mercy lands.

But *yakhtaṣṣu* says something deeper. It says: **the mercy is His to reserve.** It belongs to Him so completely, so internally, that the act of giving it is the act of releasing what was entirely His own to begin with. The mercy never left His hand. It was never on a shelf for the deserving to come and claim. It was *His*, held close, set apart — and bestowed, when bestowed, purely out of that closeness.

Do you feel what this does to the envious question?

The faction's resentment assumed something. It assumed the gift was a *prize* — something that exists out in the open, that the worthiest should win, that was wrongly awarded. But *yakhtaṣṣu* tells you the gift was never a prize in a competition. **It was the private possession of the Giver, released by His own hand, on His own terms.** You cannot be cheated out of something that was never yours to win.

And notice one more thing — the sound of the word itself. *Yakhtaṣṣu.* That doubled, emphatic *ṣṣ* at the end — it does not slide off the tongue. It *holds*. It is sustained, pressed, sealed. The very phonetics of the word enact what it means: a thing gripped, set apart, not let go of casually. The grammar tells you the mercy is reserved; the sound makes you *feel* it being held.

What this single verb reveals: **Divine mercy is not loot to be distributed by merit. It is a treasure held close, given as a gift — and a gift, by its nature, cannot be earned, and therefore cannot be unjustly withheld.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Man Yashā'* — The Door Left Open

Now look at what comes next. *Yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi* — He singles out for His mercy — **man yashā'.** Whom He wills.

I want to teach you to read an *absence* here, because this is one of those places where what Allah *does not* say is louder than what He does.

In Arabic, as in any language, you can be specific or you can be open. You can say "the people who pray," "those who believe," "the ones who strive" — and every one of those would have fit grammatically into this sentence. The verse could have told us the *criterion*. It could have said: He singles out for His mercy *the righteous*, or *the sincere*, or *those who turn to Him.*

It says none of these.

It says **man yashā'** — *whomever He wills.* The object of the choosing is left completely, deliberately open. No name. No category. No condition. The "whom" is a door left standing wide.

Now, your first instinct might be to feel unsettled by that openness. We *want* the criterion. We want to know the rule, so we can meet it, so we can secure our place. The undefined "whom" can feel like standing in a vast hall with no map.

But listen to what one of the early teachers said about exactly this openness. Al-Qurtubī, in his commentary, preserves a remark from Abū ʿUthmān. Why, he asks, was the statement left *general* — *ajmala al-qawl* — rather than specified?

His answer is breathtaking in its gentleness: **so that the hope of the one who hopes would remain alive, and the fear of the one who fears would remain alive.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land.

If the verse had named the deserving, two things would have died on the spot. The one who feels far — the sinner, the struggler, the one who is sure they have disqualified themselves — would have read the criterion, found themselves outside it, and given up *hope* forever. And the one who feels secure — the pious, the credentialed, the certain — would have read the criterion, found themselves inside it, and lost their *fear*, slipping into the very complacency that destroys.

By leaving the door open — *man yashā'* — Allah keeps both alive. **No sinner is so far gone that the door has closed on them. No saint is so secure that they may stop walking toward it.** The openness is not vagueness. It is *mercy in the grammar itself.* It is the structural refusal to let anyone despair and the structural refusal to let anyone presume.

And here, too, notice the tense. *Yakhtaṣṣu* — *yashā'* — both are in the present-continuous form, the *muḍāriʿ*. Not "He singled out" — past, finished, sealed in history. **He singles out. He wills. Now. Continuously. Still.** The choosing is not an event that happened to other people long ago. It is an ongoing reality, happening in this moment, with the door still open — and you are standing in front of it as you hear these words.

What this absence reveals: **Allah hid the list of the chosen not to torment us with uncertainty, but to keep every single heart in the only posture that saves it — between hope that never collapses and humility that never relaxes.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: From *Raḥmah* to *Faḍl* — The Word Climbs Higher

Now watch the verse's final move, because it is the most beautiful, and it is easy to miss.

The verse has been speaking of **raḥmah** — mercy. Root ر ح م, the same root as *raḥim*, the womb. *Raḥmah* is tender, relational, protective mercy — the mercy of the One who cares for what is weak and dependent on Him. That is the word in the first half of the verse: *bi-raḥmatihi.*

But the verse does not *end* on mercy. Listen to its closing words: *wa-llāhu dhu-l-**faḍli**-l-ʿaẓīm.* "And Allah is the Possessor of immense **faḍl**."

The word changes. *Raḥmah* climbs to **faḍl.**

What is *faḍl*? The root ف ض ل means, at its base, **surplus. Excess. The part left over. What overflows beyond the necessary.** When you have given everything that is owed and there is still more — *that* extra, that overflow, is *faḍl.* It is grace that is, by definition, *beyond all entitlement.* Mercy might still be imagined as something owed to the weak. But *faḍl* can never be owed. *Faḍl* is, in its very meaning, the gift that exceeds every claim.

Here is the physical picture buried in the word, the stepping stone Allah gives us: think of a cup being filled. *Raḥmah* fills the cup to meet its need. But *faḍl* is the water that keeps pouring *after* the cup is full — running over the rim, down the sides, onto the ground, far more than was asked for, far more than the cup could ever hold or claim.

[PAUSE]

And then the final word: **al-ʿaẓīm.** Immense. Vast. The root ع ظ م is the root of magnitude, of greatness that overwhelms scale. The overflow is not measured. It is *immense.* And this is not a flourish we are reading into the verse — it is how the plainest classical commentary unfolds it. Al-Muyassar glosses *dhu-l-faḍl al-ʿaẓīm* as *dhu-l-iḥsān wa-l-ʿaṭā' al-kathīr al-wāsiʿ* — the Possessor of beneficence and giving that is *abundant* and *vast.* Notice that last word, *al-wāsiʿ:* not merely large, but *expansive — never narrowing, never running short.* The early reading and the root meaning point to the same thing: a giving with no edge to it.

Now — bring this back to the envious faction we started with.

Their entire resentment was built on a hidden assumption: that grace is **scarce.** That if it goes to *them*, there is less for *us*. That the gift is a finite pie, and someone else getting a slice means our slice shrank. This is the logic underneath all envy, in every age, including ours: the zero-sum heart.

And the last three words of the verse simply… end that.

**Allah is *dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm*** — the Owner of *immense* overflow. There is no scarcity in the source. The cup that He filled for someone else did not empty the ocean. The mercy He singled out for another took *nothing* from the immeasurable surplus still in His hand. To envy someone else's portion of an *infinite* overflow is not just wrong — it is *incoherent.* You are grieving a loss that did not occur, in the presence of an abundance that has no floor.

What this final word reveals: **The verse does not answer envy by justifying the gift. It dissolves envy by revealing the Giver — One whose bounty is so vast that another's blessing has never, not once, made you poorer.**

---

### The Bridge

So look at what these eight words have built. A verb — *yakhtaṣṣu* — that tells us mercy is a held possession, not a won prize. An open door — *man yashā'* — that keeps every heart between hope and humility. And a climb from *raḥmah* to *faḍl* to *ʿaẓīm* that reveals a source with no scarcity in it at all.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of our own hearts, and the things we quietly resent in our own lives.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Ache of Watching Grace Land on Someone Else

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You scroll, and someone you know — someone no more deserving than you, maybe less — has the thing. The opportunity you prayed for. The ease you have been begging for. The marriage, the child, the recognition, the steadiness of faith that comes to them so naturally while you white-knuckle every day. And something in your chest tightens. You may be ashamed of it. You may bury it fast under a *mashā'Allāh*. But it was there for a second: *why them?*

This is not a modern disease. It is, perhaps, the *original* one. The first death in human history, the Quran tells us, came from exactly this — one brother's offering accepted, the other's not, and the resentment that could not bear it. And the faction in our passage, with all their scripture and learning, fell to the same ancient pull: *why did the gift go to them and not to us?*

Modern psychology has a clean name for the engine underneath it: the **scarcity mindset.** The unexamined belief that good things are a fixed quantity, so another's gain is automatically my loss. It is the lens that turns every blessing I witness into a subtraction from my own account. And it is exhausting, because under that lens you can never simply be happy for anyone — every joy in your field of vision becomes a small accusation against your own life.

Now let the ayah fall on this.

*Wa-llāhu dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm.*

[PAUSE]

The verse is not scolding the envious heart. It is *correcting its eyesight.* It is saying: the premise of your ache is false. You looked at your brother's blessing and saw a slice taken from a small pie. But there is no small pie. There is an *immense overflow* — *faḍl ʿaẓīm* — from a hand that does not empty.

Sit with the movements of this.

First: their blessing cost you nothing. Not a drop. The mercy singled out for them — *yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi* — was drawn from His private, infinite reserve, not from a shared pool you were both drinking from.

Second — and this is the turn — if His *faḍl* is that vast, then the very abundance you are *resenting in their life* is the proof of how much remains available for *yours.* Envy looks at another's gift and concludes *there is less for me.* The verse looks at the same gift and concludes *the Giver is unimaginably rich.* Same scene. Opposite reading. And only one of them lets you breathe.

Third: the door is still open. *Man yashā'*, in the present tense, *still.* The grace that landed on them is not a grace that has now passed you by. The same hand is still singling out, still willing, still pouring the overflow — and your name has never been struck from the possibility, because the openness of *man yashā'* is precisely what was never closed.

The paradox we must let sit here — without rushing to resolve it — is this: the cure for envy is not to *get* what the other person got. You could get it tomorrow and the scarcity-eye would simply find a new target. The cure is to stop believing the source is small. **Envy is healed not by acquisition, but by seeing the immensity of the One who gives.**

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time you feel that tightening in your chest at someone else's blessing — and you will, because the pull is ancient — do not fight the feeling with shame. Fight it with *theology.* Say the words: *dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm.* He owns immense overflow. Their cup full does not mean mine is empty. Then do the thing that breaks the spell entirely: make a sincere du'a *for* them, *for* the very blessing you envied. Envy cannot survive in a heart that is busy asking God to give the other person *more.* That is not pretending. That is correcting your eyesight.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: The Grace You Cannot Command

Now let me take you somewhere uncomfortable.

We are, most of us, builders of systems. We like to know the inputs and the outputs. Do the work, get the result. Eat well, sleep well. Study hard, pass the exam. We have organized our entire understanding of the world around the comforting machinery of *cause and effect, effort and reward.* And it serves us — until we turn that same machinery on God.

Because then, quietly, faith starts to feel like a transaction. I pray, therefore I am owed peace. I am consistent in worship, therefore I have *secured* my standing. I have done the inputs; I am entitled to the outputs. And the danger is not just spiritual pride — though it is that. The danger is that the moment you believe you have *earned* your nearness to God, you have made it into a wage. And a wage is something you can lose, something you must anxiously protect, something that turns the relationship into accounting.

Now hear the verse again, in its first word. **Yakhtaṣṣu.** He sets apart, for Himself, to give as *He* sees fit. And *man yashā'* — by His will. Not "He distributes His mercy according to the ledger of deeds." **He singles it out by His own choosing.**

The early commentators are precise about what this *raḥmah* refers to in context. Al-Ṭabarī, transmitting from Mujāhid, glosses it tersely as *al-nubuwwah* — prophethood, *which He singles out for whom He wills* — and in his own words widens it to *Islam and the Qur'an, together with prophethood.* Al-Muyassar reads it as *prophethood and guidance to the most complete of paths.* Al-Baghawī and al-Qurtubī, citing al-Ḥasan and Mujāhid, read it the same way; Ibn Jurayj, as al-Qurtubī reports, read it as *Islam and the Qur'an.* Notice what every one of those is. **Prophethood. Guidance. Islam. The Qur'an.** These are not wages. No one *earned* the Qur'an into existence. No one *qualified* for guidance the way you qualify for a paycheck. These are the supreme gifts — and the verse is naming them precisely as *the things He singles out by His will*, not the things He pays out by your merit.

And notice how surgically this lands on the scene we entered in that Madinan room. The faction's wound was *prophethood itself* — the gift of revelation passing to someone they judged unqualified. The classical reading does not dodge that. It says: yes, the *raḥmah* singled out here *is* prophethood — and that is exactly the kind of thing the verb *yakhtaṣṣu* governs. The very gift they were resenting is named, in the same breath, as a thing that was never theirs to win. The commentary and the grammar close the trap together.

[PAUSE]

And here is where it gets liberating, if you let it.

If your nearness to God were strictly a wage for your deeds, you would have every reason to be *terrified*, because your deeds are not enough. They never were. The most devoted worshipper among us, on honest reckoning, has not done enough to *purchase* a single breath, let alone Paradise. A relationship built purely on earning would crush every one of us under the weight of our own insufficiency.

But *yakhtaṣṣu bi-raḥmatihi* — He singles out by *mercy*, not by invoice — means the whole thing was never resting on your sufficiency in the first place. **Your standing with God is held up by His grace, not by your performance.** And grace, unlike a wage, cannot be exhausted by your failures, because it was never funded by your successes.

Does this mean deeds do not matter? Watch the verse carefully, because it refuses that conclusion too. The door is *man yashā'* — open — but it is still a door you must *walk toward.* Recall Abū ʿUthmān's insight: the openness keeps *fear* alive alongside hope. You do not stop striving, because you have no guarantee. But you do stop *strutting*, because you have no entitlement. Your deeds are not the *payment* that buys His mercy; they are the *turning of your face* toward the One who gives it freely. You walk to the door not to earn what is behind it, but because that is where the Giver is.

The paradox to sit inside: you must strive with everything you have, *and* you must know that everything you have could never be enough — and somehow both of these are not in tension but in harmony, held together by the single fact that the gift was always grace. The striving is real. The earning is an illusion. And the moment you accept that, the terror lifts, and what is left is not laziness — it is *gratitude that fuels the striving instead of anxiety that poisons it.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Examine the quiet ledger you keep with God — the one where you have logged your prayers and your fasts and feel, somewhere, that He now owes you ease. Tear it up. Not the deeds — keep every deed — but tear up the *entitlement.* Walk into your next prayer not as a creditor coming to collect, but as someone standing before *dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm*, asking for an overflow you could never deserve and being told the door is open. You will find the prayer feels completely different. Lighter. And, strangely, more sincere — because gratitude prays better than accounting ever could.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with deliberate care.

It opens with the Giver's act — *yakhtaṣṣu*, He sets apart. It moves to the open recipient — *man yashā'*, whom He wills. And it closes by naming the Giver Himself — *wa-llāhu dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm.*

Do you see the movement? The verse begins with an *action*, passes through an *opening*, and lands on a *Person.* It does not end on the gift. It does not end on the chosen. **It ends on God.** The whole architecture pulls your eyes off the question that started this — *why them?* — and fixes them where they belonged all along: not on the recipients of grace, but on the immeasurable Source of it. The verse answers a question about *people* by refusing to talk about people, and pointing instead at God. That is the design. That is the cure.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are one insight seen from two sides.

The grammar told us mercy is *held* (*yakhtaṣṣu*), the door is *open* (*man yashā'*), and the source is *overflowing* (*faḍl ʿaẓīm*). The themes told us that this is exactly why envy is incoherent and entitlement is an illusion. You cannot envy a portion drawn from an infinite reserve. You cannot earn a gift that was never a wage. **The same verse that disarms the resentful heart also humbles the self-satisfied one — because both diseases come from the same lie: that grace is small, and that we can control it.** It is neither small nor controllable. It is *His* — vast, freely given, and still being poured.

## Questions to Carry

- Whose blessing, right now, have I been quietly counting as a loss to myself — and what would it mean to believe it cost me nothing?
- In my relationship with God, am I behaving like a creditor coming to collect, or like someone standing before an immense overflow asking for grace?
- The door is *man yashā'* — open. Have I let myself believe it closed on me? On what basis, when even the verse refuses to name who is excluded?
- If His *faḍl* is truly *ʿaẓīm* — immense — what am I still treating as scarce in my life that He has never run short of?
- Could I make sincere du'a tonight for the very person whose blessing has bothered me — and watch what that does to my heart?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Allah's mercy is not a wage to be earned or a prize to be envied — it is a bounty He sets apart by a will so vast that no one's inclusion has ever been your loss, and no one's exclusion is ever final.

## The Closing Invitation

Let something shift. Not a resolution to *try harder* — that is the old machinery, the ledger, the wage. Let it be something gentler and deeper: a recalibration of your eyes. Begin to see the world as a place of overflow rather than scarcity, and your standing before God as grace rather than transaction. When you do, two heavy things fall off at once — the ache of comparison, and the terror of never being enough. What remains is a heart that can be genuinely glad at another's blessing, and genuinely hopeful about its own, because it has finally seen the size of the One who gives.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who single out Your mercy and possess immense overflow — *dhu-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm* — cleanse our hearts of the lie that Your grace is small. Let us never resent the cup You filled for another, knowing the ocean You drew it from has no floor. And since the door of *man yashā'* still stands open, do not let us turn away from it in despair or pass it by in pride — single us out, by Your will and not our worth, for a mercy we could never earn. *Āmīn.*
