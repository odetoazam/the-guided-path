# The Leash on Every Miracle

## Introduction: A Voice Given Before a Life Is Lived

Let me read it to you first in Arabic, then slowly in English.

> [ayah:3:49] وَرَسُولًا إِلَىٰ بَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ أَنِّى قَدْ جِئْتُكُم بِـَٔايَةٍ مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ ۖ أَنِّىٓ أَخْلُقُ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلطِّينِ كَهَيْـَٔةِ ٱلطَّيْرِ فَأَنفُخُ فِيهِ فَيَكُونُ طَيْرًۢا بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ وَأُبْرِئُ ٱلْأَكْمَهَ وَٱلْأَبْرَصَ وَأُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ وَأُنَبِّئُكُم بِمَا تَأْكُلُونَ وَمَا تَدَّخِرُونَ فِى بُيُوتِكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَـَٔايَةً لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ

*"And a messenger to the Children of Israel — 'I have come to you with a sign from your Lord. I fashion for you out of clay the form of a bird, then I breathe into it, and it becomes a bird by Allah's permission. And I heal the one born blind and the leper, and I give life to the dead by Allah's permission. And I inform you of what you eat and what you store up in your houses. Surely in that is a sign for you, if you are believers.'"*

Now I want you to notice something strange about *when* these words are being spoken.

A child has not been born yet.

Step back into the scene. Maryam is alone. The angels have just told her she will carry a son without a father. She has not held him. She has not heard him cry. And in the middle of this annunciation, Allah does something almost vertiginous: He hands us the speech this unborn child will one day deliver to a nation. We are hearing Isa's sermon — the miracles, the clay birds, the healed lepers, the raised dead — *before he has drawn a single breath of his own.*

Hold that. The most extraordinary catalogue of miracles given to any prophet is being recited to a frightened young woman as a kind of reassurance. As if to say: *do you see what this child will carry?*

### Rebuild the world

You have to understand what this passage is walking into.

This is Surah Aal-Imran. Much of this surah is a long, careful, sometimes tense conversation with the People of the Book — and especially with the way the figure of Isa would come to be understood. The community receiving this revelation lived in a world where a man's miracles had already, in some hearts, lifted that man into the place of God Himself.

So picture the danger. You are about to list, out loud, the most God-like things a human being has ever done. *I create. I give sight to the blind. I raise the dead.* Every single one of those sentences is a doorway. Walk through it the wrong way and you arrive at worship of a man.

And here is the wonder — the thing I want you to ache to understand by the end of our time together:

**The very ayah that lists the most divine-seeming powers ever given to a human being is also the ayah that most relentlessly chains every one of them to Allah.**

The miracle and its leash arrive in the same breath. Not in a later correction. Not in a footnote by the scholars. *In the sentence itself.*

How does the language do that? How does a single verse hand a man the power to create — and make it impossible to mistake him for the Creator?

That is what we are going to sit with.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word No Human Should Be Able to Say — *Akhluqu* (أَخْلُقُ)

Let me teach you something before we open the word, because the whole verse turns on it.

In Arabic — and really in the world the Quran builds — there is a vocabulary that belongs to Allah the way a name belongs to a person. Certain verbs are, in their fullest sense, His alone. The deepest of these is the root **خلق (kh-l-q): to create.** Not to assemble. Not to rearrange. To *originate* — to bring a thing into being.

Across the Quran, when *khalq* is spoken in its absolute sense — creation from nothing, the bringing-into-existence of what was not — the subject is Allah. *"Allah, the Creator of all things"* (al-Khāliq). It is one of His names.

Now open the word in Isa's mouth.

> **أَنِّىٓ أَخْلُقُ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلطِّينِ**
> *"that I create / I fashion for you out of clay..."*

**أَخْلُقُ — *akhluqu* — "I create."**

Stay with the audacity of that for a moment. A man — a prophet, but a man — stands before a nation and uses, in the first person, the verb of God. *I create.* If you were listening in that crowd and you stopped the sentence right there, where would your heart go? You would be one clause away from kneeling to him.

Here is what's remarkable, and the classical commentators are careful about it. Al-Jalālayn glosses **أَخْلُقُ** not as origination from nothing but as **أُصَوِّر** — *I shape, I form, I give a figure to.* Al-Muyassar reads it the same way: *I make for you, out of clay, the shape of a bird.* The lexical range of *khalaqa* genuinely includes this — to *measure out and proportion* a thing, to fashion a form. The Arabs spoke of a craftsman who *yakhluqu al-adīm* — who *cuts and shapes* the leather before sewing it.

So the verb itself is doing something extraordinary on two levels at once.

On the surface it dazzles — *I create.* It reaches for the most divine word available.

And underneath, the very same word quietly confesses its own limit. Because Isa does not create *from nothing.* Notice the next words: **مِّنَ ٱلطِّينِ** — *out of clay.* He needs material. He needs a substance already brought into being by Someone else. The most God-like sentence a human ever spoke still has to begin with a handful of dirt he did not make.

[PAUSE]

Let me sit with this, because it's easy to rush past.

Real creation — the creation that is Allah's alone — does not begin with clay. It begins with nothing. *Kun fayakūn.* But the highest creative act ever entrusted to a human being still requires raw material placed in his hands. The clay is the proof. The clay says: *even your most miraculous making is a re-making. You shape what He originated.*

So the word *akhluqu* is not a slip. It is the most precisely engineered word in the verse. It lets Isa reach as high as a creature can reach — and the single preposition that follows, *min al-ṭīn*, "from clay," marks exactly where the ceiling is.

One word. It both elevates the prophet and fences off the prophet's God. That is what this single verb reveals.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Breath That Echoes the First Breath — *Fa-anfukhu* (فَأَنفُخُ)

Now watch what Isa does *to* the clay.

> **فَأَنفُخُ فِيهِ**
> *"then I breathe into it..."*

The root is **نفخ (n-f-kh): to blow, to breathe.** And I need you to feel the echo here, because the Quran is doing something deliberate.

Where else does this exact root appear, in this exact gesture — breath given to clay?

It appears at the origin of the human being.

When Allah describes the making of Adam: *"When I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My spirit..."* — **وَنَفَخْتُ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِى** (15:29, 38:72). The same verb. *Nafakha.* Clay, then breath, then life.

And it appears in the conception of Isa himself — Maryam, into whom Allah *breathed* of His spirit (21:91, 66:12).

So feel the layering. Isa, who was himself brought to life by a divine breath into Maryam, now stands and breathes into clay shaped like a bird — and it lives. The miracle is not random. It is a deliberate, miniature, *permitted* re-enactment of the oldest scene in human existence: the breath that turns dust into a living thing.

But here is the thing the language will not let you forget. Look at the grammar of the two breaths.

When Allah breathes into Adam, the breath is *His own* — **مِن رُّوحِى**, *of My spirit.* It originates in Him.

When Isa breathes, the verb is in his mouth — *fa-anfukhu*, "then I breathe" — and the life that follows is immediately tagged: **بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ**, *by Allah's permission.* Isa supplies the gesture. He does not supply the life.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means. The breath is real. It comes out of Isa. You could have stood there and watched his chest rise and his lips move. And yet the thing that *matters* — the leap from lifeless clay to a heartbeat — does not travel out of Isa's lungs. It descends by permission from somewhere above him.

The breath is his. The life is borrowed.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah teaching the unseen through the seen. You can see a man blow on a clay bird. You cannot see the *idhn*, the permission, that is the actual cause. The visible breath is the stepping stone. The invisible permission is the destination. And the verse keeps your eyes from stopping on the breath by attaching, every time, the words that point past it.

That brings us to the word that is the true spine of this entire verse.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Heartbeat of the Verse — *Bi-idhni-llāh* (بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ)

Let me teach the concept first, then show you what the verse does with it — because this is the most important grammatical observation we will make today.

The word is **إِذْن (idhn): permission, authorization, leave.** It is the word for when a power that is not yours is *lent* to you for a moment. A guest does not own the house; he enters *bi-idhn*, by leave of the host. A servant does not own the treasury; he disburses *bi-idhn*, by the authorization of the master. *Idhn* always implies two parties: one who owns the power, and one who is permitted to exercise it.

Now I want you to read the verse again and simply *count.* Listen for the leash.

> *"...it becomes a bird* ***by Allah's permission.*** *And I heal the blind and the leper, and I give life to the dead* ***by Allah's permission.****"*

**بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ.** And again: **بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ.**

It is a refrain. It is the heartbeat. After the clay bird comes alive — *bi-idhnillāh.* After the dead are raised — *bi-idhnillāh.* The phrase is stitched to the verse the way a chorus is stitched to a song. You cannot recite the miracles without the leash pulling taut, twice, right after the two most dangerous claims.

And which two claims does the leash guard? Look closely. **Life.** *It becomes a bird* — the giving of life to clay. *I give life to the dead* — the giving of life to corpses. The two moments where a human being comes closest to the one act most exclusively divine — *the giving of life* — are the exact two moments the verse reaches out and chains him.

The danger is highest, so the leash is shortest.

[PAUSE]

Now — and this is where I need you to read *absence* as carefully as presence — let me show you something the verse does *not* do.

There is a third claim at the end. After the bird, after the healing, after raising the dead, Isa says:

> **وَأُنَبِّئُكُم بِمَا تَأْكُلُونَ وَمَا تَدَّخِرُونَ فِى بُيُوتِكُمْ**
> *"And I inform you of what you eat and what you store up in your houses."*

This is knowledge of the hidden — a window into the private interior of people's homes, into the meal on the table and the grain in the storeroom. It is a genuine miracle of the unseen.

And notice: it does **not** carry the words *bi-idhnillāh* immediately after it.

Why?

I don't want to over-read a silence, so let me be careful — this is an interpretive observation the structure invites, not a stated grammatical rule. But look at the pattern. The leash *bi-idhnillāh* is attached, twice, precisely to the claims that touch the giving of *life* — the most exclusively divine power, the place where a watching crowd is most likely to deify the man. The informing about food and hoarded stores, miraculous as it is, is *knowledge* — and the verse closes the whole list with a single comprehensive leash instead:

> **إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَءَايَةً لَّكُمْ**
> *"Surely in* ***that*** *— [all of it] — is a sign for you."*

The demonstrative *dhālika*, "that," gathers everything — bird, healing, resurrection, hidden knowledge — into one bundle and calls the whole bundle a single **آية**, a single *sign.* Not signs, plural, scattered to the credit of Isa. One sign, pointing in one direction.

So the architecture is this: the most dangerous powers get an individual leash, right on the spot. And then a final clause throws one net over all of them and names the entire performance a *pointer* — to the Lord, not to the man.

[PAUSE]

Let me state plainly what these three words have shown us, because they are the same insight from three angles.

*Akhluqu* — "I create" — reaches as high as a creature can reach, and the clay marks the ceiling.

*Anfukhu* — "I breathe" — gives the gesture of life, and *bi-idhnillāh* keeps the life itself in God's hands.

*Bi-idhni-llāh* — the refrain — is the leash that turns every miracle from a claim about Isa into a sign about Allah.

The verse hands a man the powers of God and makes it linguistically impossible to confuse the man with God. That is not an accident of phrasing. That is the verse protecting the one it honors.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Everything You Can Do Is Borrowed

Let me start where you already live.

Think of the most capable person you know. The surgeon whose hands can reopen a blocked artery and give a dying man another twenty years. The mother who can soothe a screaming infant in fifteen seconds when no one else can. The teacher who can take a child everyone gave up on and unlock him. We look at people like that and we say a telling word. We say they are *gifted.*

Listen to that word. *Gifted.* We use it without thinking, but it is exactly right. A gift is something that was *given.* It had an owner before you. It will have an owner after you. You hold it; you did not make it.

And yet — how easily we forget the giver and fall in love with the gift. The surgeon starts to believe the hands are *his.* The gifted child grows up and mistakes the gift for an achievement. We slide, so quietly, from *I have been given the power to do this* into *I am the power.* From steward to owner. It is the oldest drift in the human heart.

Now bring the verse back.

Here is Isa — who can do what no surgeon can dream of. He doesn't extend a life; he *restores* one. He doesn't treat blindness; he abolishes it. He doesn't comfort the dying; he calls the dead out of the grave. If anyone in human history had grounds to say *the power is mine*, it was this man.

And what does the verse put in his mouth, twice, right at the peak of the power?

**بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ.** *By the permission of Allah.*

He says it himself. He doesn't wait for a theologian to clarify it later. At the very summit of human capability, the most capable human says, out loud, in the same breath as the miracle: *this is on loan.*

[PAUSE]

Let me deepen this, because it cuts closer than it first appears.

We tend to think of *idhn* — permission — as something that limits the spectacular. The miracle is leashed. But turn it around. The same leash is on *you.* On everything you can do.

The breath you just took to follow this sentence — *bi-idhnillāh.* The heartbeat you have not had to think about once today — *bi-idhnillāh.* The fact that your eyes are decoding these shapes into meaning, that your memory is holding the start of this paragraph while you read its end — every one of those is a power placed in your hands by leave, and revocable. You did not install your own pulse. You cannot, by willing it, add one beat once it stops.

We notice the leash on Isa's resurrection of the dead because it is dramatic. We miss the identical leash on our own ordinary morning. But it is the same leash. The clay bird and your own beating heart are both alive *bi-idhnillāh.*

Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit rather than rush to resolve it: **this is the most humbling truth and the most freeing truth at once.**

Humbling, because nothing you are proud of is finally yours. The talent, the intelligence, the health, the years — all of it is lent.

But feel the freedom in it too. If your power is borrowed, then the *outcome* was never finally on your shoulders. Isa breathes — and whether the clay lives is not his burden to carry; it is *bi-idhnillāh.* You do your work — the studying, the parenting, the praying, the trying — and the result was never yours to manufacture. You supply the breath. He supplies the life. That is not a demotion. That is the lifting of a weight you were never built to carry.

**The practical shift:** Tomorrow, take one thing you are quietly proud of — one capability you privately consider *yours* — and say over it, deliberately, *bi-idhnillāh.* Not as a formula. As a correction of ownership. *I can do this — by leave.* Watch what it does to the pride, and watch, strangely, what it does to the fear. The same two words deflate the ego and disarm the anxiety, because both the boasting and the terror came from the same lie: that the power was yours to keep or lose.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Sign Is a Window, Not a Wall

Let me begin again where you live.

Have you ever received a gift so beautiful that it almost made you forget the person who gave it?

A child opens a present and is so absorbed in the toy that the grandmother who drove three hours to bring it stands in the doorway, unseen. The gift was meant to be a *message* — *I love you, I thought of you, I came.* But the gift was so bright it became a wall. The child stopped at the toy and never looked up to the giver.

This is the deepest spiritual danger in the entire verse, and it is the reason the verse is built the way it is.

A miracle is a gift like that toy. It is dazzling. It absolutely commands the eye. And precisely because it commands the eye, it is the easiest thing in the world to *stop at.* To stare at the healed leper, the living bird, the risen corpse — and never lift your gaze to the One the miracle was sent to point toward.

Now look at the word the verse uses for all of it.

**آية — *āyah* — a sign.**

Not a *trophy.* Not a *power.* A *sign.* And a sign, by its very nature, is the one kind of object you are forbidden to stop at. A road sign that says "Water 2 miles" is worthless to a thirsty man who admires the sign and sits down beside it. The sign is built to be *seen through.* Its entire dignity is that it points past itself to something it is not.

> **إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَءَايَةً لَّكُمْ**
> *"Surely in that is a sign for you."*

Isa is saying: *do not look at me. Look through me.* Every miracle I perform is a window. The clay bird is a window. The opened eyes are a window. Do not press your face against the glass and admire the glass. Look through it — to your Lord.

[PAUSE]

And now feel the tragedy that this verse was sent to prevent.

The very community Isa was sent to — *Bani Israel* — and the communities that came after, were the ones in whose midst this danger fully matured. The window became a wall. The man who said *bi-idhnillāh* with his own lips, who called his own miracles a *sign for you*, was lifted by some who came after into the very place he spent his ministry pointing away from. The gift was so bright that the Giver was lost behind it.

That is why this verse, recited to Maryam before the child is even born, is already armored. Allah does not wait for the misunderstanding to happen and then correct it. He builds the correction into the annunciation. The first time we hear Isa's voice, it is *already* saying *bi-idhnillāh, āyah, mir-rabbikum* — by permission, a sign, from your Lord. The defense against deifying him is woven into the words that introduce him.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this home, because it is not only about Isa, and it is not only about a community long ago.

You do this. I do this. We stop at gifts all the time and call it gratitude.

You love someone, and somewhere along the way the *person* became your wall — they stopped being a window through which you saw the Bestower of love, and became the destination of all your hope and fear. You are given health, success, a beautiful life, and it all becomes so absorbing that the very abundance designed to point you to Allah becomes the thing that makes you forget Him. The blessing meant to be a *sign* became a *wall.* This is not a failure of the wicked. It is the default drift of the grateful heart that forgets to look *through.*

And notice the verse's final, piercing condition:

> **إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ**
> *"...if you are believers."*

The sign is there for everyone. The bird flew for everyone. The blind saw for everyone. But the verse says it functions as a *sign* — it actually points, it actually delivers you to God — *only for the believer.* The unbeliever sees the identical miracle and stops at the spectacle. The believer sees the same miracle and is carried *through* it to the One who permitted it.

The miracle is the same. The window is the same. What differs is whether your eyes stop at the glass or pass through it.

**The practical shift:** Take the brightest blessing in your life right now — the person, the gift, the success you are most absorbed in. And practice looking *through* it. Let it become a sentence: *this is a sign — from my Lord.* Not a thing to be clutched, but a window to be looked through. The test of whether a blessing is a sign or a wall is simple: does it make you remember Him more, or remember Him less? Train yourself, with your brightest gift, to look up at the Giver in the doorway.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, let me show you how the pieces were fitted together — because the structure of this verse is itself the proof of its message.

Watch the shape:

- **Claim of power** → *akhluqu*, "I create" → **leash:** clay (you build from what He made) and *bi-idhnillāh.*
- **Claim of power** → *uḥyī al-mawtā*, "I give life to the dead" → **leash:** *bi-idhnillāh.*
- **All of it gathered** → *dhālika*, "that" → renamed: **āyah**, a sign → **final leash:** *mir-rabbikum*, "from your Lord."

Power, leash. Power, leash. And then the whole thing folded into a single sign that points home.

The verse is engineered so that you literally cannot recite the miracles without reciting their dependence. The grammar will not let you separate the gift from the Giver. Every time the human reaches up, a clause reaches back to attach him to God. *That* is the architecture — and the architecture *is* the theology. The form of the sentence enacts exactly what the sentence means: a creature lifted as high as a creature can go, and never once permitted to be mistaken for the Creator.

### Bringing It Together

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The word *akhluqu* leashed by clay, the breath leashed by *bi-idhnillāh* — these are the same truth as *everything you can do is borrowed.* The grammar of permission is the lived reality of the gifted hand.

And the word *āyah*, the sign that you must look *through* — that is the same truth as *the gift is a window, not a wall.* The rhetoric of the sign is the lived reality of the grateful heart that remembers to look up.

The most capable human who ever lived showed us both at once: he held the powers of God *and* he never stopped pointing past himself. He was the gifted hand that knew the gift was lent, and the bright window that begged you to look through, not at.

### Questions to Carry

1. Which of my abilities have I quietly stopped saying *bi-idhnillāh* over — which gift have I started believing I *own*?
2. When my power produces no result, do I carry the failure as if the outcome were ever mine to manufacture?
3. What is the brightest blessing in my life right now — and is it currently a window that makes me remember Allah, or a wall that makes me forget Him?
4. Do I admire the people and gifts I love, or do I look *through* them to the One who bestowed them?
5. The miracle is a sign only "if you are believers" — when I look at the signs all around me, do my eyes stop at the glass, or pass through it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The verse hands a man the very powers of God and, in the same breath, chains every one of them to *"by Allah's permission"* — teaching us that all power is borrowed and every gift is a window meant to be looked *through*, never a wall to stop at.

### The Closing Invitation

You are gifted. Genuinely, by leave of Allah, you can do things that would astonish you if you saw them clearly. Let this verse reorder how you hold them. Not as possessions to be proud of or terrified of losing — but as light coming through a window. Supply the breath. Leave the life to Him. And whenever a gift grows bright enough to absorb you, let that be your cue to look up — past the gift, to the doorway, where the Giver has been standing the whole time.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, every power in my hands is on loan from You — let me never mistake the gift for ownership, nor the sign for the destination. Teach my eyes to look through every blessing You give until I find You behind it, and let me supply only the breath, and leave all life to You — by Your permission, and from no one else. Ameen.*
