# A Portion of the Book: When the Truth Rules Against You

> أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ نَصِيبًۭا مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَىٰ كِتَـٰبِ ٱللَّهِ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَهُمْ ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّىٰ فَرِيقٌۭ مِّنْهُمْ وَهُم مُّعْرِضُونَ
> >  > *Alam tara ilā alladhīna ūtū naṣīban mina l-kitābi yudʿawna ilā kitābi llāhi li-yaḥkuma baynahum thumma yatawallā farīqun minhum wa-hum muʿriḍūn.*
>
> **"Have you not considered those who were given a portion of the Scripture? They are called to the Book of Allah, that it may judge between them; then a party of them turns away, and they are averse."**

---

## Introduction — The World Behind the Words

Picture the situation in Medina, only a few years into the new community.

The city is not a blank page. Long before the Prophet ﷺ arrived, Medina was home to communities who had lived for generations with a Book in their hands. They were the literate ones, the people of scrolls and schools, the ones the desert Arabs looked to and said: *they have knowledge, they have scripture, they know what a prophet is and how one is recognized.* When a farmer or a merchant in Medina wanted to know what God had said about a matter, he did not go to the illiterate tribes. He went to the people who could read.

So understand what kind of authority we are talking about. These were not people ignorant of revelation. They were its custodians. They taught it. They argued its fine points. Their entire standing in that society rested on one claim: *we are the people of the Book. We live by what God revealed.*

Now a dispute arises. The sources describe more than one occasion, but the shape is the same in each: a case comes up among them that already has a clear ruling in their own scripture — and the ruling is inconvenient. It touches someone important. It carries a penalty they would rather not apply. And so, when the Prophet ﷺ says, in effect, *let us settle this by what your own Book says,* something extraordinary happens.

The people whose entire identity is "we follow the Book" — turn away from the Book.

Not because they don't know what it says. Precisely because they do.

[PAUSE]

Feel the weight of that before we touch a single word of the verse. This is not a story about disbelievers who never had the truth. This is a story about people who had it, held it, taught it — and then, at the one moment it mattered, at the moment it asked something of them, they discovered that their loyalty had a limit. They would carry the Book as long as the Book carried them. The instant it turned and asked to carry *them* — to rule over them, to overrule what they wanted — they set it down and walked away.

This verse sits inside a larger conversation Aal Imran has been having. The surah has been building a single argument since its opening: that the true religion in the sight of God is *islām* — submission, surrender, the handing over of your will — and that the tragedy of the earlier communities was never a shortage of revelation. It was the withholding of surrender in the presence of revelation. Verse after verse, the surah keeps returning to people who *knew* and did not *yield*. This verse is the sharpest image of that whole strand. It freezes the exact instant where knowledge and submission part ways.

And here is the question the verse is going to place in front of you — not in front of them, in front of *you*: What would it look like if you did the same thing, and never once noticed you were doing it?

Because the frightening thing about this verse is not what it says about a community fourteen centuries ago.

It's how quietly it describes something you might have done this week.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### First: *naṣīban* — the portion that was never the whole

Let me show you something small in the Arabic that changes the entire temperature of the verse.

When we describe what these people were given, we might expect the language to say: *those who were given the Scripture.* The Book. All of it. That would fit the reputation — the people of the Book, the custodians of revelation.

That is not what the verse says.

It says they were given *naṣīban mina l-kitāb* — **a portion of the Scripture.** A share. A slice. Not the Book. A piece of the Book.

Sit with the word *naṣīb* for a moment, because the root is telling a quiet story of its own. The letters carry the sense of something *set up*, *allotted*, *fixed as your assigned share* — the same family of meaning that gives us the idea of a set portion divided out to each person, like an inheritance measured and handed over. Your *naṣīb* is your cut. Not everyone's — yours. It is bounded by definition. To call something a *naṣīb* is already to say: *this is a part, and there is a rest that is not in your hand.*

And notice — this is a documented grammatical feature, not a reading imposed on it — the word arrives **indefinite**. Not *the* portion. Not the specific, named, complete allotment. Just *a* portion. Unspecified. Undefined at its edges. A vague piece of a whole they do not hold.

Here is what that indefinite, partial word does to the story.

Think about how a person actually relates to a body of demanding truth over time. Nobody wakes up and rejects the whole thing at once. What happens is subtler. You keep the parts that fit you and quietly file away the parts that don't. You memorize the verses that comfort you and grow strangely forgetful of the verses that indict you. Over years, without a single dramatic decision, you assemble a private edition — a personal *naṣīb* — made only of the pieces that never ask you to change. And you call that edition "my faith."

That is what *naṣīban mina l-kitāb* has already diagnosed before the sentence even reaches the turning-away. These people did not lose their scripture in a fire. They *portioned* it. They took a share and left a share, and the share they left was — of course — the share that would have ruled against them. So that when they are finally called to *judge by the Book*, the ruling that surfaces is one from the part they had already, in their hearts, set down.

**They didn't turn away from a Book they never had. They turned away from the part of the Book they had always intended to leave.**

And the sound of the word plays along. *Naṣīb* — say it — is soft, open, almost generous in the mouth. It *sounds* like a gift being handed to you: *na-ṣīb*. Hold that gentle sound. We are going to hear it collide, at the end of the verse, with something much harder.

### Second: *alam tara* — the question that isn't asking

Now look at how the verse opens. *Alam tara* — **"Have you not seen?"**

At first glance it's a question. But notice who is being asked and what is really happening.

In Arabic, when a statement opens with this shape — the interrogative fused to a negation, *a-lam*, "have you not" — it very often is not seeking information at all. It is a way of taking someone by the arm and turning their face toward something: *look. Look at this. Are you seeing what I'm seeing?* We do the exact same thing in our own speech. When you grab a friend and say, *"Can you believe this guy?"* — you are not asking whether they can believe it. You are saying: *witness this with me. Register how astonishing it is.* The grammar of the question is carrying the emotion of astonishment.

That is the register the verse opens in. Not cool reporting. Astonishment. *Have you not seen — can you even take in — these people, of all people, doing this?*

But here is the feature worth slowing down for. **Who is grammatically being addressed?**

The "you" in *alam tara* is singular. Masculine singular. It is, in the first instance, the Prophet ﷺ himself. God is turning to His Messenger and saying: *look at them.*

And yet — you are reading this. The verse has been recited in the ear of every believer for fourteen hundred years. So there is a gap here, and the gap is doing something. The verse is spoken *to* one man, but its gaze is aimed at everyone who overhears it. When you read *"have you not seen,"* the finger that was pointing at the astonishing behavior of that community suddenly, quietly, has room to swing toward the reader. *You. Have you not seen? And — are you sure you are only the one watching, and never the one being watched?*

That is the discomfort built into the address. A verse that lets you stand beside the Prophet ﷺ, looking out at someone else's failure — and then leaves just enough space in the grammar for you to wonder whether you are standing on the right side of the pointing finger.

And the verb itself deepens it. *Tara* — to see — but the seeing here is plainly not with the eyes. Nobody is being told to physically watch a scene. It is the seeing of the heart: to perceive, to consider, to register the meaning of a thing. So the command underneath the question is not *look at them* but **understand them. Take in what this behavior actually reveals.** The verse is not asking you to observe a failure. It is asking you to comprehend one — which is a far more dangerous request, because to truly comprehend a failure is to become capable of recognizing it in yourself.

### Third: they are *called* — and the caller is left unnamed

Watch the next movement. *Yudʿawna ilā kitābi llāh* — **they are called to the Book of Allah.**

*Yudʿawna* is passive. They are *being called*. And notice what the passive does: it removes the caller from the sentence. The verse does not say *the Prophet calls them* or *the believers call them.* It says they *are called* — and leaves the one doing the calling unstated.

This is a grammatical gap, and it opens something. When the caller is dropped out of the sentence, the invitation stops belonging to any one person and starts belonging to the situation itself. It is as if the truth *itself* is calling them. As if the Book, lying there, is issuing the summons. The point of the sentence is no longer *who* extended the invitation. The point is that the invitation *came* — clearly, undeniably — and now everything hangs on the response.

And look which Book they are called to. A moment ago they were given a portion of *al-kitāb* — "the Scripture," named generically. Now they are called to *kitāb Allah* — the Book **of Allah.** The naming has shifted. And the shift is not decoration; it re-establishes who owns the thing.

They possessed a *portion*. He owns the *whole* — because it is *His.* When they are merely holding it, it's "the Scripture," a thing in their custody. But the instant it is invoked to rule over them, the verse renames it to remind everyone whose Book this actually is. You were given a share of it. You never owned it. And now the Owner's ruling is on the table.

That single change — from *the Scripture* they hold to *the Book of Allah* they are summoned by — is the whole drama in miniature. It is the difference between scripture as a possession you manage and scripture as an authority that manages you. They were comfortable with the first. They fled the second.

*Li-yaḥkuma baynahum* — **that it may judge between them.** And here is the exact hinge of the verse. They are not called to admire the Book. Not to recite it, not to display it, not to teach it. They are called to let it **judge.** To submit a live dispute — something they actually cared about, something with a real outcome — to its ruling.

That is the moment of truth for any relationship with revelation. Not *do you honor the Book* but *will you let it decide against you.* A judge you obey only when he rules in your favor is not a judge you have submitted to; he is a decoration you keep on the wall. Real submission is only ever tested at the point where the ruling and your desire pull in opposite directions. Everywhere else, "obedience" and "getting what you wanted" are indistinguishable.

They were brought to precisely that point. And now the verse tells us what they did.

### Fourth: *thumma yatawallā ... wa-hum muʿriḍūn* — the two turnings

*Thumma yatawallā farīqun minhum wa-hum muʿriḍūn* — **then a party of them turns away, and they are averse.**

Three things in this closing phrase are each carrying weight. Let me take them one at a time, because together they are the verse's verdict.

**First, the little word *thumma* — "then."**

Arabic has more than one way to say "then." There is a "then" of immediate sequence — one thing hard on the heels of another. And there is *thumma* — a "then" that carries **delay, distance, a gap.** Not *and then instantly*, but *and then, after all of that.* It puts space between what came before and what comes after.

Feel where that space falls in the sentence. They were given the Book. They were called to it. The invitation was clear, the Owner was named, the purpose was stated — *let it judge.* And *thumma* — then, after all of that, after the knowing and the summoning and the full clarity of the moment — they turn away.

The delay is the indictment. If they had turned away *immediately*, out of confusion, you might plead ignorance for them. But *thumma* says the turning came at the end of a process in which everything was made plain. This is not a reflex. It is a considered exit. They saw the ruling coming, they understood exactly what was being asked, and then, in full view of the truth, they turned.

**Second, the turning itself: *yatawallā.***

The root gives us the image of turning your back, pivoting your body away, showing someone your shoulders instead of your face. It is physical. Bodily. A man turning on his heel and walking off.

And this is where the Quran does what it always does — it hands you a thing you can *see* so that you will understand a thing you cannot. The verse shows you a body turning away because a body turning away is the visible shadow of something invisible: a heart that has already decided. The feet do not move until the will has moved. By the time you see someone physically walk away from the truth, the real turning — the inner one — is already complete. The body is just catching up, enacting in the open what the soul settled in private. The verse gives you the outward motion and trusts you to read the inward one beneath it.

**Third — and this is the feature I most want you to hold — the verse doesn't stop at the turning. It adds: *wa-hum muʿriḍūn.***

Now, on the surface this looks redundant. *They turn away* — and then, *and they are averse.* Isn't that the same thing said twice? Turning away, being averse — why both?

It is not the same thing twice. Look at the grammar. *Yatawallā* is a **verb** — an action, something they *do*, a single motion in time: they turn. *Muʿriḍūn* is a **participle** — not an action but a **state**, a settled condition, what they *are*. And the little *wa-* fastening it on is the circumstantial "while": *they turn away, while being — in themselves, all along — people who are averse.*

Do you feel the difference the grammar is drawing? The verb captures the moment; the participle captures the man. One is what they did on that day; the other is who they already were. The verse is saying: this turning was not an aberration, not a bad moment. The act of turning merely *revealed* a disposition that was there before the invitation ever arrived. They didn't become averse when they turned. They turned because they were already averse — and the single visible act pulled back the curtain on a permanent inner condition.

This is why the two words together are so devastating, and so precise. A verb alone would say *they made a mistake.* The verb-plus-participle says *the mistake exposed what they are.* One choice, and the whole disposition stands revealed.

And now — listen to the sound. Say it. *Muʿriḍūn.* Feel the machinery your mouth has to engage: the throat-tightening *ʿayn*, the heavy, resistant *ḍād* — one of the hardest sounds in the language, the one Arabic is famously named after. It is a word you have to *push* out against resistance. Set it beside the word we started with — *naṣīb*, the soft, open, gift-like sound of what they were given. The verse opens with a sound like an offering placed gently in your hands and closes with a sound like a door being shoved shut. The gift is smooth. The refusal is *hard*. You can hear the whole tragedy just in the journey from the first sound to the last.

[PAUSE]

**So what has the language shown us?**

Not two people arguing over a legal case. It has shown us the anatomy of a particular kind of failure. A gift received only in part (*naṣīban*). A summons that came from the truth itself and left no excuse (*yudʿawna*). A Book that stopped being a possession and became a judge (*kitābi llāhi li-yaḥkuma*). A turning that came *after* full clarity (*thumma*). A visible act that unmasked an invisible condition (*yatawallā ... muʿriḍūn*).

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not about their words, but about the human heart. About *your* heart.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The portion you assembled without noticing

Here is something you have almost certainly done, and probably this month.

You went to see a doctor. The doctor ran the tests and gave you two things: a diagnosis and a prescription. And you did a very human thing. You accepted the diagnosis — *yes, that explains the tiredness, that makes sense* — and you quietly did not follow the prescription. You didn't argue with it. You didn't declare it wrong. You just... didn't take the medicine. You kept the part of the appointment that named your problem and let go of the part that asked you to change your life.

Or think about how any of us handle advice from someone who knows us well. When a friend tells us something we wanted to hear, we say, *"You're so right, you really get me."* When the same friend, in the same conversation, tells us the hard thing — the thing about ourselves we don't want to face — we suddenly find reasons why *this* particular observation doesn't quite apply, why they don't have the full picture, why it's more complicated than they realize. We don't reject them. We just... edit them. We keep the affirming half and misplace the correcting half. And we walk away feeling we listened.

That editing — that quiet, unnoticed keeping-of-the-comforting-part and setting-down-of-the-demanding-part — that is *naṣīban mina l-kitāb* happening inside a single human life.

This is the thing the verse saw with such precision. The people it describes did not burn their scripture. They *curated* it. And the terrifying part is that curation doesn't feel like rejection. It feels like faith. You are still reading. Still quoting. Still defending the Book to others. You've simply, over the years, assembled a personal edition made only of the verses that never overrule you — the mercy without the accountability, the promises without the commands, the God who comforts without the God who judges. And you carry that private edition around and call it your religion, and you sincerely believe you are a person who follows the Book.

But there is a test that reveals, in one instant, whether you are following the Book or following yourself and using the Book when it happens to agree. The verse names the test exactly: *li-yaḥkuma baynahum* — **will you let it judge?** Will you bring it the one matter where the ruling cuts against what you want — the grudge you're enjoying, the money that isn't quite yours, the relationship you know it forbids, the anger you've decided is justified — and let it rule *against you*?

Because up until that exact point, there is no way to tell devotion apart from self-interest. As long as the Book only ever tells you what you already wanted to do, "I obey the Book" and "I do what I like" produce the identical life. They only separate at one place: the ruling you didn't want. That is the only moment your submission is ever actually measured. Everywhere else, it's untested — and untested submission has a way of imagining itself much stronger than it is.

[PAUSE]

And notice the cruelty of what the verse is describing, because it makes it worse, not better, that these were people of knowledge. They had a *naṣīb* — a real portion, genuine revelation, more than most. This is not a failure of information. They knew. The gap in them was never between not-knowing and knowing. It was between knowing and *yielding*. And that is the hardest gap in the human being to cross, because knowledge can actually make it *harder* — it gives you the vocabulary to justify yourself, the sophistication to build a case for why the ruling doesn't really apply to your situation, the fluency to turn away from the truth *in the truth's own language.*

So the practical thing this asks of you is uncomfortable and simple. Go find your left-out portion. Everyone has one. Ask yourself honestly: *which part of what I know God asked have I quietly stopped bringing to the table?* Not the parts you struggle with and keep trying — those are the parts you've submitted to and keep failing, which is an entirely different and far healthier thing. I mean the part you've stopped struggling with. The command you've made a settled, private peace with disobeying. The verse you skim past. That is your *naṣīb* — the portion you kept — and the shape of what you left out is the most honest portrait of your heart available to you.

Bring one piece of it back this week. Not all of it. One. Take it out of the "complicated" file and put it back under the ruling of the Book. That single act — dragging one avoided command back into the courtroom and letting it judge you — is the exact opposite of everything this verse describes. It is the *thumma* run in reverse.

### Theme Two: The gap between knowing and turning

There is a particular kind of morning that most people know.

You wake up already aware of the thing you need to do. It isn't a mystery. There's no missing information. The difficult conversation, the apology you owe, the habit you swore off, the prayer you keep pushing to its last minute, the message you need to answer honestly instead of avoiding — you *know*. You knew last night. You'll know tomorrow. And you lie there and you do the small, quiet, catastrophic thing: *not yet. Later. I'll deal with it later.*

And "later" — if you're honest about your own track record — has a way of never arriving. "Later" is often just the socially acceptable, self-respecting way of saying *no* without having to feel like you said it. You get to keep the self-image of someone who *will* do the right thing, while never actually doing it. You've turned away — but slowly enough, and with enough "I'll get to it," that it never feels like turning away. It feels like waiting.

The verse has a word for the whole of that. *Thumma.* The "then" of delay.

Remember what we saw in the grammar: these people did not turn away out of ignorance, in the first confused instant. They turned away *thumma* — after. After the Book was named, after the invitation was clear, after the ruling was on the table and there was nothing left to understand. The distance in *thumma* — that gap between the moment of full clarity and the moment of turning — is not the distance of *not knowing.* It is the distance of *knowing and still not moving.* It is the exact space you lie in on that morning. Everything is clear. Nothing is missing. And still — the feet don't turn toward the truth. They turn away.

This is why the verse should frighten us more than a verse about ignorant people ever could. It is easy to feel superior to someone who turned away because they never had the truth. But this is not that. This is people who had it fully, saw it clearly, and turned anyway. And if you've ever known exactly what you should do and gone and done the other thing — with full clarity, no excuse, in the bright light of the *thumma* — then you already know this failure from the inside. It isn't foreign to you. It's Tuesday.

[PAUSE]

But now hold the two words the verse ended with against your own life, because this is where it stops being a description and starts being a warning.

*Yatawallā* — the act. *Muʿriḍūn* — the state. The verse taught us that a single turning reveals a settled disposition. And that is the real danger of "later." Not that you fail once. Everyone fails once. The danger is that a thousand small "laters," a thousand individual acts of turning, quietly *harden into a state* — until you are no longer a person who occasionally avoids the truth but a person who is, at the core, *averse* to it. *Muʿriḍūn.* You didn't decide to become that. Nobody signs up for it. It accretes. Each small turning-away seems weightless in the moment — *it's just today, it's just this once, I'll get to it* — and none of them feels like the one that mattered. But the participle is watching the sum. At some point the verb has been repeated so many times that it has curdled into who you are, and the tragedy is you may be the last to notice, because from the inside a settled aversion feels exactly like being "busy," or "not ready yet," or "in a difficult season."

That is the trajectory the verse is holding up in front of you, still wet with warning: *watch how the act becomes the man.*

And yet — do not miss the mercy the grammar left open, because it is easy to miss and it changes everything.

Go back to the word *yudʿawna.* **They are called.** Present tense. Passive. Ongoing. The verse does not say *they were called once, and the door shut.* It says they *are being called* — the summons is depicted as still standing, still open, still issued. Even inside a verse that is describing people at the very moment of their turning, the invitation is grammatically kept *open.* The call has not been withdrawn. The Book is still lying there on the table. The Owner is still saying, *come, let it judge.*

Which means the *thumma* runs both directions. Yes — they were given, and called, and *then* turned. But the door the passive holds open says the story does not have to end at the turn. There is a *thumma* still available to them, and to you: called, and turned away, *and then* — turned back. The same gap of delay that let them walk away is wide enough to walk back through. As long as the calling is in the present tense — and for you, reading this, right now, it still is — the turning is never the last word unless you make it the last word.

So the practice here is almost embarrassingly concrete. Take the thing you have been "later"-ing. The specific one. You know which one it is; you thought of it three paragraphs ago and then let it slide back under the water. Do the smallest possible first piece of it **today** — not the whole thing, the first true inch of it — for one reason only: to break the present tense of your own turning before it sets. Because every day you leave it, the verb runs once more, and the participle is patient, and it is quietly totaling the count.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture

Now that we've walked through it, step back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with a symmetry that could not be an accident.

It opens with a **giving** — *ūtū naṣīban*, they were given a portion. It closes with a **refusing** — *yatawallā ... muʿriḍūn*, they turn away, averse. And watch how the two ends mirror each other with terrible precision. The giving was *partial* — a *naṣīb*, a portion, never the whole. And the refusing is *layered* — a single visible act (*yatawallā*) laid over a whole hidden state (*muʿriḍūn*). Partial receiving on one end; a partial act that exposes a total condition on the other. They took only a piece — and the piece they left is exactly the piece that comes back to judge them.

And running through the center, the hinge that turns gift into refusal: the renaming of the Book. It enters as *al-kitāb* — the Scripture, the thing they hold. It reappears as *kitāb Allah* — the Book *of Allah*, the thing that holds them. That single shift, from possession to authority, is the whole verse compressed into two words. Everything before it is a Book you can carry. Everything after it is a Book that asks to carry you. And the turning-away happens in the exact millimeter between those two.

There is one more piece of the architecture, and it is the mercy hidden in the justice. Read the subject of the turning again: *yatawallā **farīqun** minhum* — **a party** of them turns away. Not *they* turned away. Not *all of them.* A party. Even here — in a verse of rebuke, at the height of the astonishment — the language refuses to smear the whole community with the sin of some. It counts precisely. It condemns exactly as many as turned, and not one soul more. If you ever wondered whether the God who judges is also fair in how He judges, the answer is sitting in a single indefinite noun in the middle of a verse of condemnation. *A party.* Justice, even in the naming.

### What the two paths were always saying

The linguistic journey and the two themes were never two subjects. They were one insight approached from two sides.

The words showed us a *mechanism*: how a portion gets kept (*naṣīb*), how a summons stays open (*yudʿawna*), how an act hardens into a state (*yatawallā* into *muʿriḍūn*), how a turning comes only after full clarity (*thumma*). The themes showed us that same mechanism *running inside you*: the private edition you assembled without noticing, and the "later" that quietly becomes who you are.

And underneath both is the single question the whole verse exists to ask. Not *do you have the Book?* Most of us have far more of it than we live. The question is: **will you let it rule the one place you don't want it to?** Because that is the only place your faith is ever actually visible — to you, and to your Lord. Everywhere the ruling and your desire agree, you can't tell the two apart. They only separate at the ruling you didn't want. And at that separation, in that single millimeter, the whole worth of your submission is decided.

We began by watching someone else turn away, standing safely beside the Prophet ﷺ, looking out. We end, if the verse has done its work, no longer sure we were only watching.

### Questions to carry

- When you read the verses of the Quran that comfort you and the verses that indict you — be honest — do you spend the same energy on both? Which portion have you been quietly keeping, and which have you set down?
- Name, right now, the one command you have made private peace with disobeying. Not the one you struggle with and keep trying. The one you've stopped bringing to the table. What is the shape of it — and what does that shape say about your heart?
- Think of the thing you have been "later"-ing. How long has "later" been running? Long enough that it has stopped being an act and started becoming a state?
- If the calling is still in the present tense for you — *yudʿawna*, still open — what would it cost you to turn back today, and why does that cost feel larger than it is?
- When the ruling of the Book last cut against something you wanted, which one won?

### One sentence to keep

**To be handed a portion of the truth and then turn from it the moment it rules against your desire is to discover that what you called faith was only agreement.**

### The invitation

This verse does not come to shame you. It comes to hand you a mirror before the mirror is handed to you on a day when it can no longer be put down. The Book is still on the table. The calling is still in the present tense. The *thumma* — that gap of delay that let a party of them walk away — is exactly wide enough for you to walk back through. All it asks is that you bring the one portion you left out, and let it judge, in the one place you never wanted it to. Not all of it. One piece. Today, while the verb is still yours to redirect and the participle has not yet finished its count.

### A closing duʿā'

*O Allah, You who called and are still calling — do not let us be among those who were given a portion and turned from the rest. Make us of those who bring You even the ruling that cuts against us, and yield. Turn our turning-away back before it becomes who we are, while the calling is still open and the door is still Yours to hold. Āmīn.*
