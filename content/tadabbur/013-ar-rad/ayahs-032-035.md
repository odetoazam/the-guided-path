# Mockery Was Never New, Allah Stands Over Every Soul, and the Garden Does Not Fade

## Surah Ar-Ra'd (13:32-35)

[ayah:13:32] وَلَقَدِ ٱسْتُهْزِئَ بِرُسُلٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ فَأَمْلَيْتُ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ ثُمَّ أَخَذْتُهُمْ ۖ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عِقَابِ

[ayah:13:33] أَفَمَنْ هُوَ قَآئِمٌ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ نَفْسٍۭ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ ۗ وَجَعَلُوا۟ لِلَّهِ شُرَكَآءَ قُلْ سَمُّوهُمْ ۚ أَمْ تُنَبِّـُٔونَهُۥ بِمَا لَا يَعْلَمُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ أَم بِظَـٰهِرٍ مِّنَ ٱلْقَوْلِ ۗ بَلْ زُيِّنَ لِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ مَكْرُهُمْ وَصُدُّوا۟ عَنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ ۗ وَمَن يُضْلِلِ ٱللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُۥ مِنْ هَادٍ

## Introduction

These ayahs arrive as strength for the Prophet and exposure for the deniers at the same time.

Ar-Ra'd has just reminded us that even if mountains moved and the dead spoke, the command would still belong entirely to Allah. Now the surah makes that lesson more personal. If your people mock you, this is not new. Messengers before you were mocked too. Allah gave the deniers room. Then He seized them. The classical commentators read the ayah first as consolation: Ibn Kathir opens it as Allah "comforting" His Messenger in facing his people's denial — "so you have a good example in them" — and al-Muyassar hears in it the direct address, *so do not grieve*. Al-Tabari widens it further into a command: be patient over their harm, and carry on with your Lord's task of warning them.

From there the surah opens a devastating question: is the One who stands over every soul for what it has earned to be treated like fabricated partners? If these alleged rivals are real, then name them. Tell us what they are. Tell us what they know. Tell us what they do. But the challenge collapses immediately, because the whole thing turns out to be only outward speech beautified by delusion.

Then, after warning of punishment in this life and something even harsher in the Hereafter, the surah gives one of its most gentle turns: the likeness of the Garden promised to the God-fearing. Rivers, lasting fruit, enduring shade. What the deniers pursue is unstable. What Allah promises does not decay.

The hook of the passage is this: **Ar-Ra'd teaches that mockery does not threaten revelation, because Allah still stands over every soul and every false claim collapses before His knowledge, while the final contrast is between a fading worldly security and a Garden whose provision and shelter do not end.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Amlaytu ... thumma akhadhtuhum**: Delay Is Not Neglect

The first ayah says:

Messengers before you were mocked.

Then:

**fa-amlaytu**

So I gave respite.

And after that:

**thumma akhadhtuhum**

Then I seized them.

[PAUSE]

That sequence matters deeply. The mockers often misread delay as safety. The wounded messenger can be tempted to misread delay as abandonment. The ayah heals both misunderstandings at once.

**Imla'** is extension of time, lengthened room, a drawn-out stretch in which the denier may think he has outlasted the warning. Al-Tabari unfolds the word almost physically: *I lengthened for them the respite, and I stretched out for them the term* (aṭaltu lahum fi al-mahal, wa madadtu lahum fi al-ajal). Ibn Kathir reads it the same way — the judgment was deferred "for a term appointed," and when it came it came as encompassing punishment. So the extension is not indifference. It is part of the divine handling.

Then comes **thumma** - not simply "and," but "then," with real sequence and interval. There is a span between mockery and seizure. A span between rebellion and reckoning. But the span is not a cancellation of the end.

That is why the ayah closes:

**fa-kayfa kana 'iqab**

So how was My punishment?

Not as a request for information, but as a forced remembering. Al-Tabari paraphrases it as a summons to look: *did I not make them a lesson (ʿibra) for those who have understanding?* Read history by its endings, not by the length of the interval before them.

### 2. **Afaman huwa qa'imun 'ala kulli nafs**: Allah Is Described Here as Standing Over Every Soul

Then the passage turns to one of its greatest lines:

**afaman huwa qa'imun 'ala kulli nafsin bima kasabat**

Is then He who stands over every soul for what it has earned...

[PAUSE]

The answer is left unstated. Al-Tabari notices this deliberately: the second half of the comparison — *like one who is dead and powerless?* — is omitted (ḥudhifa al-jawab), because the listener already knows how the sentence must end. The silence is itself the argument.

The word **qa'im** here is powerful. Ibn Kathir glosses it as the One who "takes charge of every person," the guard and watcher over every living soul, from whom no deed, good or evil, ever escapes. Al-Tabari layers it richly: the Lord who is *eternal, who does not perish or pass away*, who stands guarding the provision of all creation, sustaining it, knowing every soul and everything it earns, watchful, with nothing hidden from Him wherever they are. This is not remote awareness. It is living supervision and sustaining authority over every soul and everything it earns.

And that is the exact hinge of the comparison. Against this Living Sustainer al-Tabari sets the idol: something *perishing and dead, that cannot hear or see or understand anything, that cannot repel harm from itself, let alone from the one who worships it, nor draw any benefit to either.* The question "is He like others?" is really: is the One who holds every soul the same as a thing that cannot even hold itself?

So against that reality the ayah places shirk:

**wa ja'alu li-Llahi shuraka'**

They assigned partners to Allah.

So the surah tells the Prophet:

**Qul sammuuhum**

Say: name them.

Name them how? As what? With what actual qualities? Al-Muyassar reads it precisely as a demand for their names *and their attributes* — and the point is that in those attributes they will find nothing that makes them worthy of worship. The challenge is almost surgical. If these partners are real in the way worship assumes they are real, then identify them in meaningful terms.

Then the ayah exposes the emptiness from two sides:

Are you informing Allah of something He does not know in the earth?

Or is it only an outward form of speech?

That last phrase is devastating:

**bi-zahirin mina al-qawl**

a surface statement, an outward verbal shell. Al-Muyassar spells it out: *you name them "partners" by mere outward wording, without their having any reality (ḥaqiqa) at all.*

The whole structure of shirk is reduced to ungrounded language. It is speech wearing the costume of reality without its substance.

Then the ayah tells us why this still feels convincing to the deniers:

their plotting was beautified to them.

They were turned away from the path.

Delusion is rarely ugly to itself. It usually arrives cosmetically arranged. The verb **zuyyina** is passive — the beautifier is left unnamed — and here the commentators differ. Al-Muyassar names the agent as Shaytan: *it was Shaytan who made their false speech seem fair to them and turned them from Allah's path.* Others let the passive stand as it is, hearing behind it the same divine decree that the next clause makes explicit — *and whoever Allah leaves astray, for him there is no guide.* Both readings meet in the warning: what feels most persuasive from the inside can be exactly what has turned a person away.

### 3. **Ukuluha da'imun wa zilluha**: The Garden Is Described Through What Does Not End

After the warning of worldly punishment and a harsher Hereafter, the surah says:

**mathalu al-jannati allati wu'ida al-muttaqun**

The likeness, or description, of the Garden promised to the God-fearing.

[PAUSE]

Then it gives a few features, but look closely at what kind of features they are.

Rivers flow beneath it.

Its fruit is lasting.

Its shade is lasting.

This is striking because the description does not only emphasize beauty. It emphasizes endurance.

**ukuluha da'im**

Its provision does not run out.

**wa zilluha**

and its shade.

Shade in this world shifts, shortens, disappears, or becomes unreachable. Fruit spoils, finishes, or is withheld by season. But the Garden is marked here by non-expiring nourishment and non-withdrawing shelter.

That is exactly the contrast this whole stretch of the surah has been building toward. The deniers rejoice in a dunya that is only temporary enjoyment. The Garden is described through what remains.

Then the closing pair:

**tilka 'uqba alladhina ittaqaw**

That is the final outcome of those who were mindful of Allah.

**wa 'uqba al-kafirina al-nar**

And the final outcome of the disbelievers is the Fire.

The surah keeps teaching us to judge by **'uqba**, by ending, by where a path finally lands.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Falsehood Often Survives for a While by Looking Plausible

One of the hardest experiences for a believer is to watch mockery, distortion, or confident falsehood seem socially stable for long enough that it begins to look permanent.

People laugh at truth, build systems on unreality, speak with certainty from empty foundations, and still appear to move through the world with momentum. That can shake the heart if it reads time badly.

Ar-Ra'd teaches us to read time differently.

[PAUSE]

The mockers of earlier messengers were given respite. The deniers in this surah have their plots beautified to them. Their language sounds substantial from the outside. They can demand names, slogans, claims, and rival objects of devotion. They can even feel intellectually secure.

But revelation keeps exposing the same secret: much of falsehood survives not because it has reality, but because it has surface. It keeps going for a while because it looks arranged, persuasive, repeatable, or culturally normal. It becomes **zahir min al-qawl** - outward speech without rooted truth.

That is why believers have to learn not to panic during the interval. Delay is real, but so is seizure. Beautification is real, but so is exposure. The path to Allah is not judged by who seems most socially confident in the middle of the story.

### Theme 2: What We Really Long For Is Lasting Nourishment and Lasting Shelter

The parable of the Garden is brief, but it reaches deeply into the human condition.

What do people actually want from the things they chase in this world? Beneath all the forms - money, reputation, security, ownership, relationships, power - there is usually some hunger for two things:

something that will keep nourishing us,

and somewhere under which we can finally rest.

[PAUSE]

But dunya keeps failing both tests. Its fruit is not permanent. Its shade is not permanent. Even its best moments come with expiry built into them.

So Ar-Ra'd answers not by condemning the desire for nourishment or shelter, but by relocating their fulfillment. The Garden promised to the God-fearing is described through enduring provision and enduring shade. The soul was not foolish to want food that keeps and shelter that holds; it was only mistaken about where such things could ever be found. The desire was true. The address was wrong.

And notice how carefully the surah keeps the promise honest. It does not say *this is* the Garden; it says **mathal** — this is the *likeness*, the description drawn near enough for a worldly heart to grasp. The reality is not being reduced to rivers and fruit and shade; those are the nearest words we have for a permanence we have never tasted. What the God-fearing are promised is not a better version of what fades, but a category of good that does not know how to fade.

### Theme 3: The Only Question That Settles Everything Is Whether He Stands Over the Soul

Underneath the mockery, the idols, the respite, and the two endings, the passage has been resting on a single claim: **qa'imun 'ala kulli nafsin bima kasabat** — He stands over every soul for what it has earned.

If that is true, then the mockers were never unwatched, the interval was never unattended, the beautified plot was never hidden, and the Garden is not a hope floated into an indifferent universe — it is a promise held by the One who holds everything.

[PAUSE]

This is why the commentators kept setting the Living Sustainer against the dead idol. Every counterfeit object of trust — whether a carved image, a fashionable certainty, or our own private strategies — shares the idol's defect: it cannot stand over a single soul. It cannot see us in the dark, cannot keep our provision, cannot repel harm from itself, let alone from us. To live as though something other than Allah stands over the soul is to lean on what is already perishing.

So the passage leaves the believer with a quiet reorientation. Do not measure truth by the confidence of those who mock it. Do not measure safety by the length of the respite. Measure everything by the One who stands over every soul — and then rest, because the ending (**'uqba**) belongs to Him.

[PAUSE]

## A Du'a From These Ayahs

O Allah, You who stand over every soul and know all that it has earned, let my heart not be shaken by the confidence of those who mock the truth, nor deceived by falsehood that only looks arranged and secure.

Do not let me mistake Your respite for Your neglect, nor my own delay in returning to You for safety.

Strip from me every attachment that cannot stand over my soul, and let me worship none but the One who sustains and sees.

Make me among the muttaqun to whom the Garden is promised — its provision lasting, its shade never withdrawing — and let my 'uqba, my final outcome, be with You.

Amin.

## Distillation

- **Delay is not neglect.** *Amlaytu thumma akhadhtuhum* — Allah lengthened the respite and stretched the term, then seized. Read history by its endings, not by the length of the interval before them.
- **These ayahs are first a consolation.** The mocked messengers before the Prophet are, in Ibn Kathir's words, "a good example" — so do not grieve, be patient, and keep warning.
- **Shirk collapses under one word: *sammuhum*.** Name them, with their real attributes — and nothing in those attributes qualifies them for worship. It is *zahir min al-qawl*, outward speech with no reality behind it.
- **He stands over every soul.** *Qa'im* is the Living Sustainer who guards, knows, and never sleeps over what each soul earns — the exact opposite of the dead idol that cannot even repel harm from itself.
- **The Garden is defined by what does not end.** Lasting fruit, unwithdrawing shade — the true home of every human hunger for nourishment and shelter.
- **Judge by *'uqba*.** The final outcome, not the middle of the story, is where truth and falsehood are told apart.
