# The Grammar of Permission and the Houses Where God Is Remembered

## Introduction

There are passages in the Quran that you cannot read casually. They sit in the text like load-bearing pillars — remove them, and the architecture of an entire worldview collapses. These two ayahs are exactly that.

> **أُذِنَ لِلَّذِينَ يُقَاتَلُونَ بِأَنَّهُمْ ظُلِمُوا ۚ وَإِنَّ اللَّهَ عَلَىٰ نَصْرِهِمْ لَقَدِيرٌ**
>
> *"Permission has been given to those who are being fought, because they have been wronged — and indeed, Allah is fully able to give them victory."*

> **الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا مِن دِيَارِهِم بِغَيْرِ حَقٍّ إِلَّا أَن يَقُولُوا رَبُّنَا اللَّهُ ۗ وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍ لَّهُدِّمَتْ صَوَامِعُ وَبِيَعٌ وَصَلَوَاتٌ وَمَسَاجِدُ يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا ۗ وَلَيَنصُرَنَّ اللَّهُ مَن يَنصُرُهُ ۗ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَقَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ**
>
> *"Those who were driven from their homes without right, only because they said, 'Our Lord is Allah.' Were it not for Allah's repelling of some people by means of others, there would have been torn down monasteries, and churches, and synagogues, and mosques — in which the name of Allah is much remembered. And Allah will surely support those who support Him. Indeed, Allah is All-Strong, All-Mighty."*

### Rebuilding the World

To understand what these words meant when they first descended, you have to feel the weight of thirteen years.

Thirteen years of being told: *do not strike back*. Thirteen years of watching companions tortured in the desert sun. Bilāl, his back on burning sand, a stone on his chest, refusing to renounce. Sumayyah, the first martyr of Islam, killed under torture. Yāsir. ʿAmmār. Khabbāb dragged across heated coals until the fat of his back extinguished them. Thirteen years of the Prophet ﷺ saying to people who came to him bleeding: **اصبروا** — *be patient* — *I have not been given permission*.

And then — finally — the Hijra. The forced migration. The sudden uprooting. Houses left behind. Property seized. Family members lost. They walked away from Makkah carrying nothing.

It is into *that* silence — the silence of a community that has bled for thirteen years without striking back — that this revelation falls.

**أُذِنَ.** *Permission has been given.*

Two short Arabic words. And the weight of more than a decade of patience.

### The Hook

Now hold this in your mind: this is the first ayah in the Quran allowing the believers to fight back. By the consensus of the classical commentators — Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Jubayr, al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr — *this* is where permission begins.

You would expect, then, an ayah of fire. An ayah of vengeance. An ayah that names the enemy, lists the wounds, calls down wrath.

Instead, what Allah gives is a verse that, before it finishes its second sentence, mentions **monasteries**. And **churches**. And **synagogues**. And places mosques *last* in the list.

Sit with that. The first verse of permission to fight in Islam climaxes not with the destruction of enemies, but with the protection of *all* places where God's name is remembered — and it places the Muslim's own house of worship at the end of the list, not the front.

What kind of permission is this? What kind of God speaks like this?

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Triple Passive — Who Is the Subject of This Story?

Before we open the words, let me teach you a feature of Arabic grammar that English barely registers.

In Arabic, the **active voice** says: *Zayd hit ʿAmr*. Zayd is the doer. ʿAmr is the recipient. The action belongs to Zayd. Power flows from him outward.

The **passive voice** — what Arabic calls **al-mabnī lil-majhūl**, "the construction built on the unknown" — flips this. It says: *ʿAmr was hit*. The doer is suppressed. The recipient is now the grammatical center. We are made to feel things from the side of the one to whom things were done.

Languages choose passive voice for many reasons. Sometimes to hide the actor. Sometimes to focus attention on the recipient. Sometimes — and this is rare and powerful — to convey a particular *moral* posture: *we are not the architects of this; we are receiving it.*

Now look at how this passage opens.

> **أُذِنَ** — *permission has been given* (passive)
>
> **لِلَّذِينَ يُقَاتَلُونَ** — *to those who are being fought* (passive)
>
> **بِأَنَّهُمْ ظُلِمُوا** — *because they have been wronged* (passive)
>
> **الَّذِينَ أُخْرِجُوا** — *those who were driven out* (passive)

[PAUSE]

Four passives in a row. **Four.**

Notice something. Allah is not saying: *We have given permission to those who fight, because the disbelievers wronged them, those whom the Quraysh expelled.* That is the active version. It would be grammatically simpler. It would name enemies. It would assign agency.

He chose, instead, to suppress every active subject and make the believers the *recipients* of every verb.

What does this do?

Stay with me. It does at least three things at once.

**First**, it tells you who the believers actually are at this moment in the story. They are not aggressors waiting for a green light. They have been the *object* — fought, wronged, expelled — for thirteen years. The grammar mirrors the moral reality. The passive voice is itself the truth-claim: *these people are not strikers; they are the struck.*

**Second**, it positions the permission itself as a gift, not a seizure. **أُذِنَ** — *it has been given* — leaves the Giver unnamed in the surface grammar but unmistakable in the meaning. Classical scholars from al-Ṭabarī to al-Rāzī all note this: the unnamed Subject is Allah. He is so present that He need not be mentioned. The gift descends from above, not seized from below.

**Third** — and this is what I want you to feel — the passive construction strips the believers of *enthusiasm* about what is being permitted. There is no joy in this grammar. No "now finally we get to fight." Just the quiet acknowledgment: *we have been wronged; we have been given permission.* That is not the syntax of vengeance. It is the syntax of reluctant duty.

Here is what this single morphological choice reveals: **in the Quran's vision, the moral status of an act is encoded in its grammar.** Defensive permission — even when granted — is never granted to people who *wanted* it. It is granted to people who endured without it for so long that the grammar itself records them as recipients, not initiators.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word *Idhn* — What Kind of Permission Is This?

Now the second word. **أُذِنَ** comes from the root **ʾ-dh-n** — the same root that gives us **udhun**, the ear. To listen. To hear.

In its base meaning, *idhn* is not just "permission" in our flat English sense. It is *hearing-and-allowing*. It is permission given by one who has been listening. Classical lexicographers note that the word implies attention, awareness, knowledge of the situation by the One granting it.

When you say in English "I gave him permission," you might mean: *I checked the box. I signed off.* Bureaucratic. Detached.

When the Quran says **أُذِنَ**, it carries the connotation: *the One who has been listening to every prayer, who has been hearing every cry of "how long?" — has now responded.*

The thirteen years of patience were not unheard. They were being listened to. The Companions who walked past Bilāl's torture site whispering *Allāhu Allāh* to themselves — they were not whispering into a void. The migration that severed people from their homes — was not unwitnessed.

**أُذِنَ** is the word of a God who heard everything, and finally said: *now*.

But notice what is *not* said. He does not say: **أُمِرَ** — *they have been commanded* to fight. Permission is not command. The classical scholars draw the line very sharply here. *Idhn* permits; it does not require. It opens a door; it does not push you through it.

This becomes load-bearing for everything that follows in Islamic ethics. Defensive war, in the Quran's grammar, is **permitted, conditional, and reluctant** — not commanded, unconditional, and eager. The permissive verb itself carries this weight.

[PAUSE]

And then — three words later — comes the explanation. **بِأَنَّهُمْ ظُلِمُوا**. *Because they have been wronged.*

The **bi-** here is the **bāʾ as-sababiyya** — the bāʾ of causation. The cause of permission is named, and it is a single word: **ẓulm**. Wrongdoing. Injustice.

This is the moral foundation. The permission is tethered to a cause. Remove the cause — remove the *ẓulm* — and the permission has no ground to stand on. The classical jurists, from the Ḥanafīs to the Mālikīs, anchor much of Islamic just-war theory in precisely this clause. **Idhn** without **ẓulm** as its cause is not the permission this ayah grants.

What this invites us to consider: there is no unconditional permission in this verse. The grammar itself ties the permission to the wrong, and where the wrong ends, so does the permission. *Bi-annahum ẓulimū* is not decoration. It is the load-bearing condition.

### Element Three: The Four Houses — and the Order They Come In

Now the move that has stopped readers cold for fourteen centuries.

After establishing that those who were given permission are those *who were driven from their homes without right, only because they said, "Our Lord is Allah,"* — Allah suddenly broadens the lens. The argument leaps from one community's wound to a cosmic principle.

> **وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍ**
>
> *Were it not for Allah's repelling of some people by means of others...*

Then comes the consequence. *...torn down would have been:*

> **صَوَامِعُ** — *ṣawāmiʿ* — monasteries
>
> **وَبِيَعٌ** — *wa-biyaʿ* — churches
>
> **وَصَلَوَاتٌ** — *wa-ṣalawāt* — synagogues
>
> **وَمَسَاجِدُ** — *wa-masājid* — and mosques

Stop. Read that list again, slowly.

Christian monasteries. Christian churches. Jewish synagogues. **And then** — at the *end* — Muslim mosques.

Classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī — discuss this list extensively. The dominant view: **ṣawāmiʿ** are the cells of solitary monks (often Christian, possibly some Sabian); **biyaʿ** are the larger churches of Christians; **ṣalawāt** is from the Hebrew/Aramaic word for prayer-house — that is, synagogues, the houses of the Jews; **masājid** are the places where Muslims prostrate. There are minor variations in identification — some place the Sabians among the *ṣawāmiʿ*, some debate whether *ṣalawāt* refers strictly to Jewish synagogues — but the consensus is firm: this list deliberately spans the Abrahamic communities.

Now let me name what is happening rhetorically.

**One**: the Muslim community has just been described, in the previous ayah, as victims of *ẓulm* and *ikhrāj* — wronged and expelled. You would expect the verse to climax with: *...and Allah's mosques would have been destroyed.* That would be the natural rhetorical close. The community of Muḥammad ﷺ defending its own places.

Instead, Allah lists three other communities' houses *first* — and only then mentions the Muslims' own.

**Two**: Allah does not say *"would have been torn down — though only the mosques are truly His."* He does not place the mosques in a separate category of value. He lists them as one item in a single list, ending with the relative clause: **يُذْكَرُ فِيهَا اسْمُ اللَّهِ كَثِيرًا** — *in which the name of Allah is much remembered.*

The classical grammarians ask: does this final clause modify *only* mosques, or does it modify *all four*? The dominant reading, held by al-Ṭabarī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, and many others, is that it modifies all four. The name of Allah — by whatever language, in whatever tradition — is remembered in monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques alike.

Now let me say what this is doing, and what it is not doing.

It is not collapsing all theology into one. The Quran elsewhere is clear about its own theological claims and its disagreements with other traditions. This is not a verse erasing distinctions.

But what it *is* doing is monumental: the very first verse permitting defensive warfare in Islam grounds that permission in the protection of religious life *as such* — including the religious lives of those whose theology the Quran disputes. The cosmic justification for *idhn* is not "to make Islam dominant." It is "so that the remembrance of God on this earth — wherever it is taking place — does not get torn out by the roots."

[PAUSE]

And there is something even more piercing here, when you sit with it. The Muslim is being told: *you are receiving permission to defend yourself, but the moral horizon of that permission is bigger than you. It is not really about your community alone. It is about a world in which God can still be remembered. Your defense is incidental to that larger purpose.*

That is the ego-deflating logic of this list. Mosques come last not because they matter least, but because the believer is being trained, in the very moment of being permitted to fight, not to make the fight about themselves.

This is the classical position. Al-Qurṭubī, in his tafsīr, dwells on this point: the permission to fight, even when granted, exists to preserve the *general* possibility of devotion to God on earth — and the believer is being asked to internalize that horizon, to fight, when fighting is necessary, *for* that horizon, not for tribal triumph.

What this single rhetorical ordering reveals: **the Quran's grammar of permission is also a grammar of humility.** It cannot be coopted into supremacism without rewriting the verse. The verse refuses, structurally, to put "us" first.

### Bridge

So we have looked at three things. The triple passive that positions the believers as recipients, not initiators. The word **idhn** with its history of being heard, and its careful refusal to become **amr** — command. And the architecture of the four houses, with mosques placed last.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Cosmic Principle of *Dafʿ* — Why a World Without Resistance Would Not Survive

There is a phrase modern people use when they are exhausted by the news cycle: *I just want everyone to leave each other alone.* It is the dream of a world without conflict. A world where the predator and the prey simply agree not to interact. A world without friction.

If you have ever felt that exhaustion — and almost everyone has — you are touching the edge of something the Quran wants you to think about much more deeply.

Allah here introduces a single word that classical theologians and modern Muslim philosophers have written entire treatises on. **دَفْعُ اللَّهِ** — *Allah's repelling*.

> **وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍ**
>
> *Were it not for Allah's repelling of people, some by means of others...*

The word **dafʿ** comes from the root **d-f-ʿ** — to push, to repel, to push away from oneself. But notice the construction: *Allah's pushing of people, some by means of others.* Allah is the one doing the pushing — but He is doing it *through* people pushing each other.

Sit with that.

What this is naming is a structural feature of creation itself. The world Allah made is not a world where unchecked power simply runs in one direction. It is a world where every concentration of force eventually meets a counter-force, and Allah is the one orchestrating that counter-balance — using human beings as His instruments without their always knowing.

You see this everywhere once your eyes adjust to it. The empire that grows too vast collapses under the weight of its own conquered. The corporation that monopolizes the market is undone by a startup it never saw coming. The ideology that sweeps a generation breeds its own dissident children. The body that lets one cell type proliferate without check becomes cancer; the body that maintains the *dafʿ* between cell types stays alive. **Life itself is the maintenance of resistance.**

The same Quran that earlier said **وَلَوْلَا دَفْعُ اللَّهِ النَّاسَ بَعْضَهُم بِبَعْضٍ لَّفَسَدَتِ الْأَرْضُ** in Surah al-Baqarah (2:251) — *were it not for Allah's repelling of people, some by means of others, the earth would be corrupted* — is now telling us, in the context of permission to fight: this is not just about the food chain or political balance. This applies even to *prayer itself*. Without resistance, the houses where God is remembered would be torn down. Remembrance of God, on this earth, is *contingent* on the cosmic mechanism of *dafʿ*.

[PAUSE]

This is uncomfortable. It means the dream of "let everyone leave each other alone" is not a vision Allah has built into His creation. He has built a creation where things hold together precisely because forces push back against forces.

Why? Why this design?

The classical theologians answer: because a world without *dafʿ* is a world in which the strong devour the weak unimpeded. A world without resistance is not peaceful — it is the peace of a single gravity well into which everything falls. The lion and the lamb do not lie down together by removing the lion. They lie down together when the lamb is *defended*.

Allah's *dafʿ* is the divine refusal to leave the weak undefended.

And now look back at what was said. **أُذِنَ لِلَّذِينَ يُقَاتَلُونَ بِأَنَّهُمْ ظُلِمُوا**. The permission to those who are *being fought*, because they have been *wronged*. This is not the permission of empire. This is the permission of *dafʿ* entering the human story through the door of the wronged.

What this changes about how you live tomorrow: when you find yourself exhausted by a world that will not stop having conflicts, remember that the alternative — a world without *dafʿ* — would be worse. Would be a world where the wronged stayed wronged, where the houses of God were torn down, where remembrance ended. The conflict you are tired of is, in some shape, the very mechanism by which Allah keeps the door of remembrance open. This does not mean every conflict is righteous. It means conflict, as such, is woven into the design — and what you can do is enter your own corner of it as one of those *through whom* Allah pushes back the wrong.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Whose Side Is God On? The Surprise Answer of This Passage

Here is a question every embattled community asks: *whose side is God on?*

The natural human answer is the tribal one. *Ours.* We are right, they are wrong, God is with us, the end.

If you read these two ayahs quickly, you might think they are giving the tribal answer. Allah gave permission to *our* side. Allah will support those who support *Him* — meaning *us*. Allah is *Qawiyy*, *ʿAzīz* — strong and mighty — and we have Him on our team.

But read the passage again, and notice what it actually does.

It tells the believers they have permission — and then it climbs up out of the believers' particular grievance into a list that includes monasteries, churches, and synagogues. It tells them they will be supported — and then names the support not as "the supremacy of your community" but as "the preservation of houses where God is remembered, *plural*." It uses the names of Allah **al-Qawiyy** and **al-ʿAzīz** — *the All-Strong, the All-Mighty* — but only after specifying that God's strength is exercised on behalf of *those who support Him*, not those who support themselves.

What this is doing is exquisite: it is taking the natural tribal answer — *God is on our side* — and bending it ninety degrees. *God is on the side of those who side with Him*. And siding with Him is not the same as siding with your own community. Sometimes they overlap. Sometimes they do not.

Consider the contrast hidden in the verse. The wronged community here — the early Muslims — was driven from its homes "only because they said, *Our Lord is Allah*." That is the moral test: *did you stand for God's side, or for your tribe's side?* They lost their homes for the first answer. They were not expelled because their tribe was unpopular. They were expelled because they refused to make tribe the highest loyalty.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this reaches into modern life. Every era invites you to take a side. Politics. Culture. Workplace coalitions. Family allegiances. The world is always organizing itself into camps.

This passage is asking a different question. It is asking: *are you on God's side, or on your camp's side?* And it is warning, by the very architecture of the verse, that those two are not automatically the same.

Look at the phrase: **وَلَيَنصُرَنَّ اللَّهُ مَن يَنصُرُهُ** — *Allah will surely support those who support Him.* The classical grammarians note the emphatic forms here — the **lām** of emphasis, the **nūn** of intensification. *Surely. Most certainly. There is no doubt.*

But who supports Allah? The verse has just told you. Those who, *when expelled from their homes for saying "Our Lord is Allah,"* did not abandon that statement. Those who, *when given permission to defend themselves,* still see their defense as part of a larger horizon in which monasteries, churches, and synagogues also stand. Those whose loyalty is not *to themselves first*.

This is a shockingly high bar. It refuses the tribal cheap version of "God is with us." It demands instead: *first show me you are with Him — even when He is asking something larger than your tribe.*

What this changes about how you live tomorrow: when the voice in you wants to pull God onto your side of an argument, pause. Ask the harder question. *Am I siding with Him, or am I trying to recruit Him to my side?* Those two prayers sound similar. They are nearly opposite. The first is *naṣr lillāh* — supporting God. The second is *istinṣār bi-llāh li-nafsī* — recruiting God for myself. The verse promises divine support to the first. It says nothing about the second.

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Look now at the structure these two ayahs make.

The passage opens with *permission* and ends with *power*. **أُذِنَ** in the first word of the first ayah — passive, soft, given. **لَقَوِيٌّ عَزِيزٌ** in the last words of the second ayah — active, hard, attributes of God Himself.

In between, an architecture of widening concentric circles:

- *Permission given* (the smallest circle: a legal act)
- *To those who are fought, wronged, expelled* (the second circle: this specific community)
- *Repelling some by others* (the third circle: a cosmic principle)
- *Monasteries, churches, synagogues, mosques* (the fourth circle: all communities of remembrance)
- *Allah will support those who support Him* (the fifth circle: the universal moral principle)
- *Allah is Strong, Mighty* (the final circle: the divine names that ground everything)

The verse begins in a courtroom — *permission has been given* — and ends in the throne room — *Indeed, Allah is All-Strong, All-Mighty*. From a single legal grant to the names of God Himself.

The journey is not from "small enemy" to "big enemy." It is from "your wound" to "God's purposes." The verse refuses to stay narrow. Every time it could close down on the believers' particular grievance, it opens outward.

That is the proof that the permission is not what it would have been if humans had drafted it. Humans, given permission to fight after thirteen years of suffering, would have written a verse about themselves. Allah wrote a verse about *Himself* — and folded the wronged community into the larger geometry of His remembrance.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **passives** told us the believers are *recipients*, not architects, of this permission. The theme of **dafʿ** told us why: because the believers are being folded into a cosmic mechanism larger than themselves, in which they are instruments of Allah's repelling, not authors of their own vengeance.

The **idhn** — permission, not command — told us this is conditional and reluctant. The theme of **whose side is God on** told us why: because permission is tied to *standing with God*, not to standing with yourself, and any drift from "Our Lord is Allah" toward "Our tribe is right" turns the permission into something else.

The **four houses** told us that mosques come last in a list of all remembrance. The cosmic horizon told us why: because *dafʿ* is in service of remembrance, plural, not in service of supremacy, singular.

It is one insight, repeated three times in three different keys: **permission to defend is granted on the condition that the defender does not make himself the center of the story**. The grammar refuses it. The vocabulary refuses it. The list refuses it. The names of God at the end refuse it.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you ask Allah for victory, are you asking Him to stand with you — or asking for the courage to stand with Him, even if it costs you the things you wanted to win?

2. The four houses are listed. Mosques come last. What in your own loyalties needs to be re-ordered, so that *the remembrance of God* sits higher than *the success of your tribe*?

3. *Dafʿ* — Allah's repelling some by others — is the principle that makes a world possible. Where in your own life is *dafʿ* operating, and have you been calling it suffering when it might be the very mechanism keeping something sacred alive?

4. Thirteen years passed before this permission came. Where in your life are you currently in the *thirteen years* — and would you be able to tell, from the inside, whether you were being asked to wait or being asked to act?

5. *Allah will surely support those who support Him.* Whose support are you actually living for — Allah's, or someone else's?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Permission to defend is given to the wronged not so that they may dominate the world, but so that the world may remain a place where the name of Allah is remembered — in every house where it is sincerely called.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this reflection with a position about war. That would be to read this passage as a manual when it is, in fact, a mirror.

Leave it with a question about *yourself*. About whether your defenses — verbal, social, political, personal — are being raised to protect what is actually sacred, or to protect what is merely *yours*. About whether your sense of "being wronged" is tied, the way the verse ties it, to *"only because we said, Our Lord is Allah"* — or to other reasons that the verse, very pointedly, does not validate.

The grammar of this ayah is offering you a posture. The posture of the *wronged who do not seize, but receive*. The posture of the *defender who lists other people's houses before his own*. The posture of *one who supports God before asking God to support him*.

Walk into your day with that posture, even just once, and this passage has done its work.

### Du'ā

*Allāhumma*, You who heard thirteen years of patience before You said *now* — make us among those who can wait until *You* speak, and not run ahead of Your permission. Teach us the difference between supporting You and recruiting You for ourselves. And let our defenses, when they must be raised, be raised for the houses where Your name is remembered — wherever they stand, in whatever language they pray. *Innaka anta al-Qawiyy al-ʿAzīz.*
