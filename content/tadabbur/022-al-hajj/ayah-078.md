# He Named You: The Identity You Did Not Choose

## Surah al-Hajj, Ayah 78 — The Final Word

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

> [ayah:22:78] وَجَـٰهِدُوا۟ فِى ٱللَّهِ حَقَّ جِهَادِهِۦ ۚ هُوَ ٱجْتَبَىٰكُمْ وَمَا جَعَلَ عَلَيْكُمْ فِى ٱلدِّينِ مِنْ حَرَجٍ ۚ مِّلَّةَ أَبِيكُمْ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ ۚ هُوَ سَمَّىٰكُمُ ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ مِن قَبْلُ وَفِى هَـٰذَا لِيَكُونَ ٱلرَّسُولُ شَهِيدًا عَلَيْكُمْ وَتَكُونُوا۟ شُهَدَآءَ عَلَى ٱلنَّاسِ ۚ فَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَٱعْتَصِمُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ هُوَ مَوْلَىٰكُمْ ۖ فَنِعْمَ ٱلْمَوْلَىٰ وَنِعْمَ ٱلنَّصِيرُ

*Wa-jāhidū fī Allāhi ḥaqqa jihādih. Huwa ijtabākum wa-mā jaʿala ʿalaykum fī al-dīni min ḥaraj. Millata abīkum Ibrāhīm. Huwa sammākum al-muslimīna min qablu wa-fī hādhā li-yakūna al-rasūlu shahīdan ʿalaykum wa-takūnū shuhadāʾa ʿalā al-nās. Fa-aqīmū al-ṣalāta wa-ātū al-zakāta wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-Allāh. Huwa mawlākum, fa-niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīr.*

> "And strive for Allah the striving that is His due. He has chosen you. And He has not placed upon you in the religion any constriction — the way of your father Ibrahim. He named you the Muslims, before, and in this — so that the Messenger may be a witness over you and you may be witnesses over humankind. So establish the prayer, give the zakāh, and hold fast to Allah. He is your Patron — what an excellent Patron, and what an excellent Helper."

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah, you have to inhabit a particular kind of moment.

The early Muslims in Madinah had spent thirteen years in Makkah being tortured, ridiculed, boycotted, and finally driven from their homes. They had carried something they could not put down — a faith that cost them everything — and they had no name for what they had become. They were not "Quraysh" anymore in any meaningful sense. They were not the ancient followers of Ibrahim in the way the Jews of Madinah claimed that lineage. They were not the People of the Book. They were something new, and yet — they were told — something very old.

Surah al-Hajj is being revealed at the seam between Makkah and Madinah, at the very moment Allah grants them permission to fight back against the people who expelled them (22:39-40). The whole surah has been pressing on a single nerve: *who are you, and on whose authority do you stand?* It has walked them through pilgrimage, through the terror of the Hour, through stories of destroyed nations, through the rites every community has been given. It has reminded them that disputation is endless and that every community has a *mansak*, a way of approach.

And now, at the very end, the surah names them.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

There is a sentence in this ayah that you have probably read a hundred times without stopping at it.

*Huwa sammākum al-muslimīn.*

He named you the Muslims.

Notice — He. Not you. He named you. You did not pick this name for yourselves. You did not workshop it. You did not brand yourselves. Long before you existed, before the Prophet ﷺ existed, before the Arabs spoke Arabic, **someone outside of you named what you would become.**

What does it do to a person, to a community, to receive its identity rather than construct it?

That is the question this ayah is going to open. And once it opens, it does not close again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ḥaqqa Jihādih* — The Striving That Belongs to Him

The ayah opens with a command:

> وَجَاهِدُوا۟ فِى ٱللَّهِ حَقَّ جِهَادِهِۦ
>
> *Wa-jāhidū fī Allāhi ḥaqqa jihādih.*

Most translations render this: "Strive in the way of Allah with the striving that is His due." That's not wrong. But it dilutes something the Arabic does that the English cannot.

Let me first teach you the structure, then we'll come back to the verse.

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, when you want to say "your house," you can say *baytuka* — *bayt* (house) attached to *-ka* (you). The pronoun ending makes it possessive. Now think about what that ending does. It assigns ownership. *Baytuka* — the house that belongs to you. *Kitābī* — the book that belongs to me.

There is also a special construction called *ḥaqq + iḍāfa*. *Ḥaqq* literally means "right" or "due" — what is owed, what is rightfully owed to a thing. So *ḥaqqu al-walidayn* — "the right of the parents" — means what is rightfully owed to them.

Now hold those two ideas together: a possessive pronoun assigns ownership, and *ḥaqq + something* assigns a debt to that something.

#### Now the verse

> *Ḥaqqa jihādih.*

Break it apart. *Ḥaqqa* — the right, the due, what is owed. *Jihādi-* — the striving. *-hi* — His. The pronoun *-hi* refers back to Allah.

So this is not "the striving that is owed to Him" — that would be *ḥaqqa al-jihādi lah*. This is something stranger. **The striving that is His own.**

The striving is grammatically being attributed *back to Allah*. As if Allah owns a particular kind of striving, and your job is to render to Him *His* striving.

Stay with this. Let's sit with what's happening.

When a soldier strives, the striving is the soldier's. When an athlete strives, the striving belongs to the athlete. The grammar of the world is: *I struggle, my struggle is mine.* My fatigue, my discipline, my output — mine.

And then this ayah comes and says: *jāhidū fī Allāh* — strive for Allah — *ḥaqqa jihādih* — the striving that is His.

Notice something. The word *fī* — "in" or "for the sake of" — already tells you the *direction* of the striving. *Fī Allāh* — for Allah. That's already complete; you could stop the sentence there. *Strive for Allah.* Done.

But the ayah doesn't stop. It adds *ḥaqqa jihādih* — and that addition does something specific. It pulls the striving itself out of your hands and assigns it back to its true owner.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The classical commentators — Ibn Kathir, al-Razi, al-Qurtubi — all sense something is being asked of believers that is bigger than ordinary effort. Some read it as: do not fear, in His path, the blame of any blamer. Others read it: be sincere, with no admixture. Others: give Him the maximum your capacity allows.

Each opinion is a camera angle on the same reality. Together they form the composite: *His* striving is the striving that does not contaminate itself with anything other than Him. Not your reputation. Not your tribe. Not even your sense of yourself as the strenuous one.

Because the moment you become aware of how hard *you* are striving, the striving has slipped back into your possession. It is no longer His.

#### What this single phrase reveals

The grammar is doing a quiet operation on the believer. It is saying: even your effort, even your exertion, even your fight — these too belong upstream of you. The striving that satisfies its real owner is the striving that knows it never belonged to the strider in the first place.

This is why the very next clause does what it does — and we'll feel why now.

> *Huwa ijtabākum.*
>
> He has chosen you.

The verb *ijtabā* is Form VIII of the root *j-b-y*, which carries the basic meaning of *gathering* or *collecting* — like collecting fruit, or like a tax collector gathering what is due. Form VIII often adds a sense of taking-for-oneself, drawing-in, selecting-out.

So *ijtabā* is not generic choosing. It is more like: *He picked you out and drew you in to Himself.* He sifted, and you were what He kept.

And notice the order. *Strive His striving.* Then immediately: *He chose you.* The demand is followed by the gift that makes the demand possible.

You were not asked to strive in order to earn being chosen. You were told to strive *because* you have already been chosen. The choosing is the basis. The striving is the response.

[PAUSE]

This is the rhythm of the entire faith. He does the verb first; you respond to a verb already done.

Hold on to that, because it is going to matter for everything else.

### Element Two: *Sammākum al-Muslimīn* — The Active, Completed Naming

Now we come to the sentence I asked you to feel at the start.

> هُوَ سَمَّىٰكُمُ ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ
>
> *Huwa sammākum al-muslimīn.*

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, there is a verb *samma* (سَمَّى) — to name, to give a name. It comes from the root *s-m-w*, which is the same root that gives us *ism* (name) and *samāʾ* (sky). The connection in the classical lexicons is interesting: a *name* is what *raises* a thing into recognition. To name a thing is to lift it out of the undifferentiated and let it be referred to.

*Samma* is in Form II — *fa''ala* pattern — which often carries an *intensifying* or *causative* meaning. Form I *samā* would be "to rise." Form II *samma* takes that and makes it transitive — *to cause something to have a name*, to fix a name on it.

Now there's also the question of who's doing the naming. Arabic has a structure where you can place a pronoun before the verb for emphasis. Normally you'd just say *sammākum* — "He named you." But this ayah says *huwa sammākum* — "**He** named you."

Why add *huwa* when the verb already encodes the subject? Because the *huwa* is doing rhetorical work. It is shutting a door. It is saying: *the agent of this naming, and no one else, was Him.*

#### Now the verse

> *Huwa sammākum al-muslimīn.*

He — and only He — named you the Muslims.

Notice three things, one after another.

**First**, the verb is in the perfect tense — *sammā*. Completed action. This naming has already happened. It is not an aspiration. It is not what you are working toward. It is done.

**Second**, the object — *al-muslimīn* — is **definite**. Not "Muslims" indefinite, not "some Muslims," not "a kind of Muslims." *Al-muslimīn.* The Muslims. The specific, identified, recognized group. As if the name was waiting for them.

**Third**, the temporal arc that follows: *min qablu wa-fī hādhā* — "before, and in this." The naming was already there *before* — in prior revelations, in Ibrahim's prayer, in the fabric of the prophetic tradition — and it is renewed *in this* revelation now.

Stay with what this is doing. Most of us, when we think about our identity, think of it as something we *constructed*. We chose our values. We chose our affiliations. We curated our beliefs. Even people who think of themselves as religious often relate to their religion as a chosen identity — *I picked this; I committed to this.*

This ayah does not let that be the whole picture.

[PAUSE]

It says: long before you decided anything, you were already being named. Ibrahim made a prayer (we read it in 2:128) — *rabbanā wa-jʿalnā muslimayni laka wa-min dhurriyyatinā ummatan muslimatan laka* — "Our Lord, make us Muslims to You, and from our descendants a Muslim community to You." And here, in 22:78, Allah is telling the Prophet's community — *that prayer was about you. The naming Ibrahim asked for, that name has settled on you. He named you. We named you.*

Some of the classical scholars — Mujahid, ʿAṭāʾ — read *huwa* as referring to Ibrahim ﷺ, since the previous clause is "the *milla* of your father Ibrahim." Others — Ibn Abbas, the majority — read *huwa* as referring back to Allah. The composite picture is what matters: the naming was set in motion by Ibrahim's prayer and confirmed by Allah's decree. Either way, the naming did not originate with you.

#### What this single sentence reveals

You did not name yourself. The community did not workshop the name. It was placed on you by someone outside of you, and your job is to grow into it.

This changes how you stand inside the word *Muslim*. It is not your trophy. It is not a label you wear because you decided to. It is a *trust* placed on you — a name that was waiting for you, that someone else asked for on your behalf, that you must now learn to inhabit.

Hold that, because we are about to see what the name is *for*.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Two phrases. *Strive His striving.* And: *He named you the Muslims.*

The grammar of both has done the same thing in two directions. The striving is *His*. The naming is *His*. Even the most active, most willed, most apparently-yours parts of your faith — your effort and your identity — are being grammatically returned to their true source.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Identity as Inheritance, Not Invention

Begin with where most of us actually live.

We are the first generation in human history that has been told, from childhood, that **identity is something you build**. You find yourself. You discover who you really are. You construct your brand. You choose your pronouns, your tribe, your aesthetic, your beliefs. The deepest virtue of late-modern personhood is *authenticity* — and authenticity, in this framing, means *whatever I have authored is the truest thing about me.*

The result is a peculiar kind of exhaustion. Because the self is now an unfinished project, you are never done. There is always more to refine, more to declare, more to update. And underneath the project is a quiet panic: *if I built this self, then I am responsible for whether it holds. If I assembled it, I can also lose the parts. If it is mine, it is also mine to lose.*

This is the world the listener arrives from. This is the air we breathe.

And then the ayah says: *Huwa sammākum al-muslimīn.*

[PAUSE]

You did not build this. You did not assemble it. The deepest fact about you — your standing as a Muslim before Allah — is not something you authored. It was placed on you. *Before*, and *in this*. Before you existed, and again now. The name was waiting.

What does this do to a person?

It does something the modern self cannot do for itself: it *grounds* the self in something that is not the self. The Arabic word for this is *thabāt* — firmness, being-rooted. When your identity is a project you constructed, it floats. When your identity is a name placed on you by Allah and asked for by Ibrahim ﷺ, it is anchored in a place that you cannot reach to undo.

This is why the ayah pairs the naming with *millata abīkum Ibrāhīm* — "the way of your father Ibrahim." *Milla* is a particular word. The classical scholars distinguish it from *dīn* (the binding code) and *sharīʿa* (the path of approach). *Milla* tends to denote a community's *traditional way* — the lived, inherited practice that passes from one to the next. To call you *millat Ibrāhīm* is to say: *you have a father. You have a lineage. You have a household you belong to that is older than the Arabs and older than the Romans.*

You are not, as the modern world told you, a free atom assembling a self. You are a *son* or *daughter of someone* — in the spiritual sense — and that someone made a prayer for you four thousand years ago.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the second movement.

If your identity is given, then the work of your life is *not to invent yourself but to grow into what you have been named*. This is profoundly different from self-construction. The self-constructor is asking *what should I be?* The named one is asking *what does this name require of me?*

The first question is exhausting because it has no anchor. The second question is demanding but it has a horizon.

When the Prophet ﷺ called his companions, the call was not "decide who you want to be." It was "you are the *muslimūn* — the ones who have surrendered. Live up to that name." The labor is not invention. The labor is faithfulness.

And here is the third movement, which is harder.

If the name is a gift, then **the name can also be a question**. If Allah has named you the Muslims, then every act of yours either honors the name or contradicts it. The name becomes a mirror. When you betray a trust, the name asks you, *is this who you were named?* When you forgive someone who hurt you, the name says, *yes, this is what your name means.*

This is what it means to live inside a given identity rather than a built one. The given identity is an ongoing call. The built identity is an ongoing performance. One has weight; the other has worry.

#### What this changes tomorrow

Notice, for one full day, every time you are about to say "I am the kind of person who…" Catch yourself. Try instead: "I have been named one who surrenders to Allah." Notice what changes inside you when the sentence is reframed from self-assertion to received naming. The first sentence is fragile because it depends on you to keep maintaining it. The second sentence is durable because someone else has spoken it over you.

This is the gift of *Huwa sammākum*. It removes the unbearable weight of being your own author.

### Theme Two: The Witness Sandwich — Being Seen and Seeing

The ayah does not stop at naming you. It tells you what the name is *for*.

> لِيَكُونَ ٱلرَّسُولُ شَهِيدًا عَلَيْكُمْ وَتَكُونُوا۟ شُهَدَآءَ عَلَى ٱلنَّاسِ
>
> *Li-yakūna al-rasūlu shahīdan ʿalaykum wa-takūnū shuhadāʾa ʿalā al-nās.*

So that the Messenger may be a witness over you, and you may be witnesses over humankind.

Begin with the human experience of being seen. There is almost nothing more vulnerable than being known. To be seen by someone who actually understands you is the deepest form of intimacy and the deepest form of exposure at once. A child performs differently when a parent is in the room. An employee writes a different email when the boss is cc'd. A friend behaves differently when an old friend is watching. *Being seen* is a force that organizes behavior at a level deeper than rules.

Now hear the ayah again. *The Messenger is a witness over you.* The Prophet ﷺ — in this life through his teaching, and in the next life as testimony before Allah — *is watching*. He is a witness over how the named ones inhabit the name. Every Muslim, in every generation, lives under his gaze.

This is not a threat. It is a structure. It is the structure that holds the community to the standard of the one who carried the trust. Without that gaze, the community drifts. With it, the community has a calibrating presence — even unseen — that pulls it back toward who it was named to be.

[PAUSE]

But the ayah does not stop there. It does something stunning.

*And you may be witnesses over humankind.*

The same structure — being witnessed over — is now turned outward. The community that lives under the gaze of the Messenger is then placed *as the gaze* over humankind.

This is not a status of superiority. It is a station of responsibility. To be a witness *over* a people, in Quranic usage, is to be the standard against which they are measured, the one whose testimony Allah accepts about how they responded to truth. The Muslim community is being told: *you carry the testimony. The way humankind is judged by what they did with the message — your living of it is part of that judgment.*

Stay with what's being asked here. The community is sandwiched. Watched from one side by the Messenger. Watching from the other side over humankind. There is no posture available except *to be true*. Nowhere to hide. The ayah constructs an architecture of accountability that runs in both directions.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the third movement, which is where this opens fully.

What does it mean to be a *witness* (*shahīd*) over a people? In the Arabic, *shāhid* shares its root with *shahāda* — testimony, presence, the act of being there to see something happen. A witness is not a judge. A witness is *the one who was there when it happened*.

If the Muslim community is to be a witness over humankind, then the Muslim community must *be there when it happens* — must be present in the world, visible, integrated, watching, testifying by its very life. A Muslim community that withdraws from humanity is a Muslim community that has abandoned its station. A Muslim community that becomes indistinguishable from humanity has nothing to testify.

The witness lives *with* the people but lives *as the named one* in their midst. They see you. You see them. And on the Day, the Messenger will testify about how you carried this, and you will testify about how they responded to what you carried.

Some of the classical commentators — Ibn ʿAshur prominently — note that this witness role is inseparable from the previous claim that there is "no constriction" in the religion. The community is given a religion *easy enough to live in front of the world*, so that the witness can be sustained across centuries. A religion that overwhelmed its people would not survive long enough to be a witness over humankind. The mercy of *no haraj* and the mission of *being witnesses* are interwoven by the same ayah.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes tomorrow

The next time you are tempted to perform your faith *for other Muslims* — to be seen as religious by people who already share the name — pause and remember: the witness is to be over *humankind*. Not over the in-group. Over the world.

What does it mean to live as a witness in your office, your apartment building, your neighborhood, your child's school? It means that the way you handle a small slight, the way you keep a small promise, the way you speak about an absent person, the way you manage anger when traffic surprises you — these are part of the testimony. The witness is not made in dramatic moments. The witness is made in the small ones, where humankind is watching whether the named ones live up to their name.

This is the call. The Messenger watches you, so that you may carry yourself in a way that makes the watching of humankind worth offering as testimony.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look back at the whole ayah, and see what was hidden in plain sight.

It opens with **a command**: *strive His striving.*
It grounds the command in **a gift**: *He has chosen you, and He has placed no constriction on you in the religion.*
It locates the gift in **a lineage**: *the way of your father Ibrahim.*
It explains the lineage with **a naming**: *He named you the Muslims, before, and in this.*
It explains the naming with **a purpose**: *so that the Messenger may be a witness over you, and you may be witnesses over humankind.*
It translates the purpose into **a practice**: *establish prayer, give zakāh, and hold fast to Allah.*
It seals the whole thing with **a reassurance**: *He is your Patron — what an excellent Patron, and what an excellent Helper.*

Read in this order: command → gift → lineage → naming → purpose → practice → patron.

The ayah moves from demand to grounding to reassurance. It demands the maximum striving and then immediately removes every basis on which the believer might collapse under the demand. *He chose you* (so you are not striving to earn what you don't have). *No constriction* (so the demand will never exceed your capacity). *Your father is Ibrahim* (so you are not alone in this). *He named you* (so your identity is secure). *To be witnesses* (so the work has meaning beyond yourself). *Establish, give, hold fast* (so the path is concrete). *He is your Patron* (so behind the whole thing stands the One who never abandons His own).

Notice that the final word of the surah is not a command. It is not a warning. It is not a threat. It is *naṣīr* — Helper.

A surah that began with the terror of the Hour ends with the assurance of the Helper. The same Allah whose Hour will undo the suckling mother (22:2) is the Allah who has named you and stands behind you. The terror and the tenderness belong to the same Source.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The two linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Ḥaqqa jihādih* told you that even your effort belongs to Him. *Sammākum al-muslimīn* told you that even your name belongs to Him. Together, they reveal the basic posture of the Muslim before Allah: **everything you bring to Him was already His.**

And the two themes — identity as inheritance and being a witness — show you what to do with this revelation. You receive who you are. And then you live so that humankind can see what the One who named you is like.

This is the entire architecture of Muslim selfhood, compressed into one ayah at the closing of one surah.

### Questions to Carry

1. What part of your identity have you been treating as *built* that the ayah is asking you to receive as *given*?

2. When you strive — in worship, in patience, in restraint — does the striving feel like yours, or like His? What would change if it consistently felt like His?

3. The ayah says the religion has no *ḥaraj* — no constriction — placed on you. Where in your practice are you adding constriction Allah did not add?

4. Who is watching you live the name? Not "watching to judge" — but "watching as humankind watches the witness." How does your daily conduct read to those who do not share the name?

5. The surah ends with *Niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīr.* When was the last time you actually *rested* in the fact that He is your Patron — not as an abstraction, but as a felt reality?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

You did not name yourself, you did not choose yourself, and you do not have to carry yourself — Allah has done all three, and what is asked of you is to grow into the name He placed on you, in the way of the father He gave you, under the watch of the Messenger He sent, in front of the humankind He has appointed you witness over, holding fast to the Patron who has never failed His own.

### The Closing Invitation

Stop trying to *be* somebody. You already *are* somebody. He named you.

The work is not to construct a self that earns the name. The work is to inhabit the name that has already been spoken over you. There is a difference between the exhausting labor of self-invention and the sustainable labor of faithful response. This ayah is asking you to put down the first and pick up the second.

And when you feel the weight of being a witness — when the gaze of humankind feels heavier than you can carry — return to the last words of the surah. *Huwa mawlākum.* He is your Patron. *Fa-niʿma al-mawlā wa-niʿma al-naṣīr.* What an excellent Patron, and what an excellent Helper.

You were not asked to do this alone. You were not even asked to do it by yourself. You were named, gathered, lineaged, located, watched, sent, and accompanied. The whole structure stands behind you.

### Du'aʾ

O Allah, You are the One who named us before we existed and who renewed the name in this revelation. Let us grow into what You have called us. Make our striving Your striving — uncontaminated by anything other than You. And as humankind watches, let what they see be worthy of the testimony the Messenger ﷺ will offer about how we carried Your name. You are our Patron — and there is no Patron but You, and no Helper but You.
