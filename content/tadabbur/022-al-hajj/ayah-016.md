# Clear Signs and the Mystery of Guidance — Surah Al-Hajj 22:16

> وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَنزَلْنَٰهُ ءَايَٰتٍۭ بَيِّنَٰتٍ وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَهْدِى مَن يُرِيدُ

> *Wa-kadhālika anzalnāhu āyātin bayyinātin, wa-anna Allāha yahdī man yurīd.*

> "And thus have We sent it down — as verses of perfect clarity — and [know that] Allah guides whomever He wills."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world of this ayah

Before we open this verse, we have to feel what just happened a few lines earlier in this surah.

Surah Al-Hajj has been painting human beings in extreme states. It opened with the earthquake of the Hour — nursing mothers forgetting their infants, pregnant women dropping their burdens, people staggering as if drunk. Then it pulled the camera in close, and showed us a particular kind of person.

Verse 11: **"And among people there is one who worships Allah on an edge."** *ʿalā ḥarf.* On the rim of a cliff. If good comes to him, he is content. If a trial strikes him, he turns on his face — losing both this world and the next.

Then verse 15. A figure so consumed by despair that he believes Allah will not help him — so he ties a rope to the ceiling of his house and cuts himself off. *Falyaqṭaʿ.* The verse leaves the image deliberately violent and ambiguous: cut his own breath, cut his own hopes, sever the connection.

Two portraits. **The waverer** and **the severed**. Both spiritually unstable. Both end in ruin.

And then — exactly here — verse 16 arrives. **"And thus have We sent it down — as verses of perfect clarity — and Allah guides whomever He wills."**

Stay with that pivot for a moment. After Allah has just shown us souls collapsing under doubt, after we have watched human beings unravel from instability — He turns and says: *the message We sent down is not the unstable thing. The signs are clear. They are bayyināt. The wavering was never a clarity problem.*

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Here is what I want you to feel as we begin.

There is a question buried in this ayah that almost every honest believer has asked at some point. **If Allah's signs are truly clear, why isn't everyone guided?** And on the flip side — if guidance is ultimately Allah's choosing, what does it mean to say the signs are clear at all?

Most readers of this verse pick one half and abandon the other. Either they emphasize the clarity and pretend the second half isn't there, or they emphasize divine will and act as if the first half were a formality.

But Allah said both, in one breath, joined by a single particle. **And thus We sent it down as clear signs — and that Allah guides whom He wills.**

Both are true. Both, simultaneously. And the secret of this ayah — the thing that, when you see it, changes how you read every page of the Quran — is **why both have to be true at once**.

That is what we are going to walk into.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three things to look at carefully. The opening word *kadhālika*. The construction *āyātin bayyinātin*. And the strange, deliberately incomplete ending: *yahdī man yurīd*.

### Element One — The backward-pointing word: *wa-kadhālika*

Let's start with the very first word. *Wa-kadhālika.* "And thus." "And likewise."

In English we use words like "thus" so casually that we barely register them. They feel decorative. But in Quranic Arabic, *kadhālika* is structurally important. It is a **demonstrative of comparison** — *kāf* (like) attached to *dhālika* (that). Literally: **"like that."**

Like what?

Here is the everyday version. Imagine someone telling a story, and they pause and say, "And in the same way..." You instinctively scan backward. *In the same way as what?* The phrase forces you to remember the previous moment to make sense of the next.

That is exactly what *kadhālika* does. It is a hand reaching backward from one ayah and grasping the one before.

So let us follow the hand.

What was just said? In verse 15, Allah described the despairing soul who concluded Allah would not help him. And before that, the waverer on the edge. And before that, the disputers about Allah without knowledge or guidance or an illuminating book (verse 8).

Notice that earlier verse — *bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr.* "Without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating book." That image of the missing book is still hanging in the air when we arrive at our verse.

And now, *kadhālika*: **"And in the same way" — in the same way as everything you have just been shown about disputers, waverers, and the despairing — "We sent it down as clear signs."**

The classical commentators read this in several converging ways. Some say *kadhālika* points to the manner of revelation: just as Allah has unfolded all of these ayahs with their sharp portraits of doubt and instability, in that same fashion has He sent down the rest of the Quran. Others say it points to the situation being addressed: just as humanity displays this desperate need for guidance, in that same proportion has the clarity of revelation been provided.

These are not competing readings. They are the same insight from two angles. **The clarity of the message is calibrated to the darkness of the human condition it answers.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The Quran is not arbitrarily clear. It is clear *in proportion* to how lost the human being is. The waverer needed clarity. The despairing one needed clarity. And so — *kadhālika* — clarity is what was sent.

That single word, the small word at the front of the ayah, has already done something. It has told us: **the clarity that follows is not an abstract property of the text. It is a response. To us.**

### Element Two — The signs that *separate*: *āyātin bayyinātin*

Now we step into the heart of the verse. *Anzalnāhu āyātin bayyinātin.* "We sent it down [as] clear signs."

Two words here that we need to open. *Āyāt* and *bayyināt*.

#### The word *āyāt*

The word *āyah* — plural *āyāt* — is one of the most loaded words in the Quran. We translate it as "verse" or "sign," but neither is quite right. An *āyah* is **a marker** — a thing that, when you see it, points beyond itself to a reality you could not see directly.

A road sign is an *āyah* in this sense. The sign that says "city center, 3 km" is not the city. It is something whose entire purpose is to direct you to something else. If you spend your time admiring the sign and never go to the city, you have missed what the sign is for.

Allah uses the same word for the verses of the Quran, for the rising of the sun, for the alternation of night and day, for the mountains, for your own self. **Everything is an āyah.** Everything points beyond itself.

So when Allah says *anzalnāhu āyātin* — He is not saying "We sent it down as text." He is saying: **"We sent it down as a series of pointers. Each one is a finger directed past itself toward Me."**

That changes how you read. The Quran is not, at its deepest level, a collection of statements. It is a collection of *signs*. And signs require something of the one looking at them — they require the willingness to follow where the sign points.

#### The word *bayyināt*

Now the qualifier. *Bayyinātin.* From the root *b-y-n*.

This root is doing something most translations miss. *Bayyana* does not just mean "to be clear." Its primary lexical sense, attested in classical lexicons, is **to separate**, **to distinguish**, **to make a distance between two things**. The space *between* two things is *bayn*. To clarify something — *bayyana* — is, etymologically, **to separate it from what it is not**.

So a *bayyinah* is not just a clear thing. **A bayyinah is a thing that separates truth from falsehood by its very presence.** It cuts. It distinguishes. It makes a line where there was confusion.

Think of how a flashlight in a dark room does not just illuminate — it *separates* the chair from the wall, the path from the obstacle. Light, by being light, makes things that were tangled into things that are distinct.

The Quran's signs are *bayyināt* in exactly this sense. They are not just "easy to understand." They are signs that, when they appear, **truth and falsehood can no longer be confused.** The two get separated.

This is why those who reject the Quran in the Quran's own descriptions are never described as "those who didn't get it." They are described as those who *covered* it, *turned away from* it, *substituted* something for it. The clarity was never the issue. The signs separated truth from falsehood. The question was always what the human being did with that separation.

[PAUSE]

#### Now sit with the construction together

*Āyātin bayyinātin.* **Pointers — that separate.**

The verses of the Quran do two things at once: they point past themselves toward Allah, and they cut a line between truth and what is not truth. They are simultaneously **directional** (pointing somewhere) and **discriminative** (separating).

This is what was sent down in response to the waverer on the edge. He didn't need more information. He needed signs that pointed clearly and that cut a line. **Allah is saying: I gave him exactly that.**

So the second half of our verse arrives with a question already burning. If the signs are pointers and they separate — if their clarity is this complete — **then how could anyone fail to be guided?**

That is exactly where Allah takes us next.

### Element Three — The unfinished sentence: *yahdī man yurīd*

The verse ends: *wa-anna Allāha yahdī man yurīd.* "And [know] that Allah guides whomever He wills."

In Arabic this construction has a peculiarity that English translations cover up. Let me show you what is actually there.

*Yahdī* — "He guides." Form I verb from *h-d-y*, the root behind *hidayah* (guidance) and *hādī* (guide). To guide is, in the Arabic sense, **to show the way and to deliver to the destination**. It is not a one-step act. It carries you.

*Man* — "whomever." A relative pronoun. It opens a slot — *the one whom* — and waits for that slot to be filled.

*Yurīd* — "He wills."

Now here is the thing. *Yurīd* in standard Arabic syntax expects an object. **He wills *what?*** In the natural grammar, you would say *yurīdu hidāyatahu* — "He wills his guidance." Or *yurīdu an yahdiyahu* — "He wills to guide him."

But Allah does not finish that thought.

The verse just says: *yahdī man yurīd.* **"He guides whomever He wills."** Wills *what*? The object is gone. The sentence ends with a verb whose target is unspecified.

This is not an oversight. Arabic does this when the object is **deliberately left to the listener's contemplation**. The classical scholars call this *ḥadhf* — strategic omission — and they identify it as a rhetorical device that opens space for meaning.

What does it open here?

Several things, simultaneously:

**Reading one:** *yurīd* takes its object from context — *yurīd [hidāyatahu]* — "He guides whomever He wills [to guide]." Allah's guidance flows from His will. This is the most common rendering.

**Reading two:** *yurīd* is left absolute — Allah guides whomever **He wills**, period. As in: **Allah guides whoever is the object of His willing**, full stop. The grammatical openness mirrors a theological openness. We are not told the criterion. The will of Allah is not reducible to a formula we can run.

**Reading three:** the omission is itself the lesson. *Yurīd* without a stated object echoes the truth that **divine willing is not something we can grasp on the other side of a verb.** Try to specify it and you will fail. So Arabic, in its precision, simply stops the sentence.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators — al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Zamakhsharī — all circle around this same observation, each from their own angle. None of them resolves the gap; they all recognize it as the point.

Notice what this means alongside the first half of the verse. Allah has just said: **the signs were sent down as separating, pointing clarities.** And immediately after: **Allah guides whomever He wills [—].** The unfinished verb at the end is not a contradiction of the clarity. It is a **completion of it from the other side.**

Here is the structure: clarity is fully on Allah's side. Guidance, ultimately, is also fully on Allah's side. **What is left for the human being is not the manufacturing of either, but the standing under both.** The signs descend. The will to receive them is also a gift. The whole apparatus of guidance, from end to end, is something Allah Himself does.

And yet — and this is essential — **the human being is still the one who turns toward or turns away.** The Quran never relieves us of that. The waverer in verse 11 is still responsible for his wavering. The clarity was sent to him; the guidance, when it comes, comes from Allah. Both are real. Neither cancels the other.

This is the deep grammar of the verse. **Bayyināt and yahdī man yurīd.** Clarity from Allah. Guidance from Allah. And in between — a soul that must be willing to be made willing.

### Bridge before Part Two

Look what these three words have given us. *Kadhālika* told us the clarity was a response, calibrated to our condition. *Āyātin bayyinātin* told us the signs both point and separate. *Yahdī man yurīd* told us that even with that pointing and separating, **understanding the sign and being guided are not the same thing.**

Now let us ask what this means not just about words, but about reality itself. About how we are made. About why a human being can hold a clear truth in his mind and still not be transformed by it.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The gap between knowing and being changed

Let me start where you already live.

Almost every adult has had the experience I am about to describe. You know — fully, factually, with every part of your rational mind — that something is true. And it makes no difference to how you live.

The smoker who knows. The procrastinator who knows. The person staying in a relationship that is harming them, who knows. The one who rehearses the same self-destructive thought every morning while knowing exactly what it is doing to him. The clarity is total. **The information is bayyinah.** And nothing moves.

Modern psychology has a thousand names for this gap. Cognitive dissonance. Akrasia. The "knowing-doing gap." Therapists encounter it every day in clients who articulate, with stunning precision, the dynamics they cannot escape. Educators encounter it in students who can recite a principle and violate it within the hour. **Knowing is not transformation.**

If a human being's problem were a clarity problem, then sending more information would solve it. We would have solved most of the world's spiritual problems by now, because we have never had more information than we have today.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that human reality next to our verse.

Allah does not say: "We sent it down clearly, *and that resolves everything.*" That would be the rationalist fantasy — that clarity is the whole work. Instead He says: **"We sent it down clearly — and Allah guides whom He wills."** The two halves of the verse correspond, exactly, to two halves of how a human being actually works.

The first half corresponds to your **mind**. The intellect can be reached by clarity. The Quran addresses your mind everywhere. Reason. Reflect. Look. Consider. Use your *ʿaql.* That work is real. Allah does not skip it.

The second half corresponds to something deeper than your mind. **Your heart.** The Arabic word for heart, *qalb*, comes from a root meaning *to turn.* The heart is the organ of orientation — what faces toward, what faces away. The mind sees clearly. The heart turns or refuses to turn. **And the turning of the heart is, ultimately, a thing of divine willing.**

This is what the Quran insists on, again and again. *Inna laysa ʿalayka hudāhum wa-lākinna Allāha yahdī man yashāʾ* — "It is not on you [O Muhammad] to guide them, but Allah guides whomever He wills" (2:272). To Muhammad ﷺ — the most clarifying messenger ever sent, with the most bayyināt anyone has ever held — Allah says: **even you cannot manufacture the turning of a heart.**

#### The depression analogy

Imagine someone in a profound depression. Tell them they are loved. They will say: *I know.* Tell them their family needs them. They will say: *I know.* Tell them this state will pass. They will say: *I know.* The clarity is so complete that they will sometimes recite their reasons for living before going through with the opposite.

What is broken in that moment is not the cognitive apparatus. **What is broken is the channel between clarity and conviction.** Between the mind that sees and the heart that turns. We do not yet, scientifically, fully understand that channel. We can only watch it open and close.

When it opens — when, after months or years of darkness, a person suddenly *feels* what they have known all along — we use words like "something shifted." We do not say "they got better information." Because the information was always there. **What changed was the heart's willingness to be reached by it.**

The Quran has a name for what opens that channel. **Hidayah.** And it has an attribution for who opens it. **Allah.**

#### What this changes about how we walk through life

If you have ever felt the weight of trying to convince someone — a sibling, a parent, a child, a friend — of something you knew with all your being to be true, and found yourself bewildered that they could not see it, this verse is speaking to you directly.

It is saying: **the clarity is not yours to manufacture.** And — listen carefully — **the turning of their heart is not yours to manufacture either.** Both belong to Allah. Your job is to be a sign. To live the bayyinah, to point clearly, to refuse to bend the truth out of the despair of feeling unheard. And then to leave the rest where it has always belonged — with the One whose will animates the heart.

The same is true in reverse. **When you find yourself unable to be moved by something you know to be true** — about your own salvation, about your own spiritual state, about a relationship you need to repair — you do not need more information. You need *hidayah.* You need to ask the One whose will turns hearts to turn yours.

This is why the very first surah of the Quran teaches us, in our daily prayer, to say *ihdinā aṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm.* **Guide us to the straight path.** Not "show us" — *show* would be a clarity request. *Guide* — *ihdi* — is a request for the channel between knowing and being to open. Every day, multiple times a day, the Muslim asks Allah for the very thing this ayah names. Because the Muslim, at every prayer, is admitting: **even I, who already know, need You to make me one who is moved by what I know.**

### Theme Two — The mystery of the will Allah does not explain

Now let us turn to the second half of the ayah and stay with what is hardest in it.

*Yahdī man yurīd.* "He guides whomever He wills."

For some readers, this clause is a comfort. For others, it is a stone. Both responses are honest, and the verse is large enough to hold both.

The stone version goes like this: *if Allah guides whomever He wills, then what control do I have? What is the use of my striving? Why does justice attach to my choices if my choices are downstream of His willing?*

These are real questions. They have been asked for fourteen centuries. The Quran does not pretend they are not asked. But it does something specific in response, and this verse is part of that response.

**It refuses to flatten either side.**

The Quran is filled with verses about human responsibility. *La yukallifu Allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā* — "Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity" (2:286). *Inna al-insāna lā yanālu illā mā saʿā* — "Man will have nothing except what he strives for" (53:39). *Bi-mā kasabat aydīkum* — "for what your own hands have earned" (42:30). The human is real. The choice is real. The accountability is real.

And the Quran is filled with verses about divine sovereignty. *Wa-mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa Allāh* — "And you do not will except as Allah wills" (76:30). *Innaka lā tahdī man aḥbabta* — "You [O Muhammad] do not guide whom you love" (28:56). The will is real. The sovereignty is real.

What our ayah does — and what almost every Quranic verse on this issue does — is **place both in the same breath without resolving them into a system.** *Bayyināt* — clarity that you can act on. *Yahdī man yurīd* — guidance that comes from a will beyond your manipulation.

The classical theologians spent centuries trying to organize these into a metaphysics. Some leaned toward predestination, some toward libertarian freedom, most landed in the middle position of *kasb* (acquisition) — that the human acts and Allah creates the act. None of those formulations *exhausted* the verse. The verse is wiser than any system we build around it.

#### Why the absence of an object on *yurīd* matters here

Remember from Part One — Allah did not say *whom He wills [to guide]* explicitly. He left the verb without a specified object. *Yahdī man yurīd.* He guides whomever He wills [—].

Why?

**Because the moment you complete that sentence with a specified criterion, you have given the human being a tool to manipulate.** "Allah guides whomever He wills [the sincere]" — and now I will perform sincerity. "Whomever He wills [the deserving]" — and now I will calculate desert. Any specified criterion becomes a back door to gaming the divine.

By leaving the will unspecified, Allah preserves what is most precious about *iradah*: **it cannot be reverse-engineered.** You cannot earn your way to it through a formula. You can only ask. You can only stand humbly before it. You can only do what you have been told to do — follow the bayyināt that have already arrived — and leave the rest with the One whose will is not a vending machine.

This is, paradoxically, **a mercy.** A guidance that could be manufactured by performance would be a guidance that the cunning would always win. By keeping it in His own hand, Allah keeps it safe from the manipulators. And He keeps the door open for the broken person, the simple person, the unschooled person, the person who has nothing to offer but a turn of the heart.

#### The story this clause makes possible

Think about the people in your life who have come to faith. Or to depth in faith. Almost none of them got there the way they expected to.

Some came through hardship. Some through love. Some through encountering a verse at exactly the moment they needed it. Some through a stranger's kindness. Some through their own failures. Some after decades of resistance. Some at the very end of their lives, in a single quiet moment.

If guidance were a formula, these stories would all look the same. They do not. Because *yahdī man yurīd* — Allah guides whomever He wills, by whatever means He wills, at whatever moment He wills. **The diversity of paths to guidance is itself a sign of the unspecified will.**

And this should produce in us **two simultaneous responses**, both of which the Quran trains us in.

The first is **fear** — *khawf*. Because if guidance is His will, I cannot take mine for granted. The companion who has been with the Prophet ﷺ for ten years can lose his way. The brilliant scholar can lose his way. None of us holds our guidance independently. We borrow it, breath by breath, from the One who keeps giving it.

The second is **hope** — *rajāʾ*. Because if guidance is His will, then no one is beyond it. The person you despair of — the family member who will never listen, the world that has gone dark — all of them are within reach of a will that is not bound by what they have been. *Yahdī man yurīd.* He guides whomever He wills. Including the one you have given up on. **Including the one who has given up on himself.**

#### What this changes about how you pray

When you ask Allah for guidance — for yourself or for someone you love — this verse changes how you ask.

You are not negotiating with a system. You are not laying out a case before a judge who must, by rule, rule in your favor if you have presented the right evidence. **You are turning toward a Will.** A Will that has shown you, throughout the Quran, that it loves to guide, that it has sent down bayyināt for that purpose, that it has placed in you the capacity to ask. But a Will, ultimately, that is its own.

So you ask the way you would ask someone who loves you and is also free. With humility. Without entitlement. Without despair. **Knowing that the asking itself is part of the gift.**

Because here is the secret last layer. When you find yourself wanting to be guided — when you find yourself, in the quiet of the night, asking for it — **that wanting is already a sign that something has turned.** The one whose heart is fully closed does not ask. The asking is the channel opening. The longing is the answer beginning.

This is why the people of knowledge have always said: **never trust the day you stop asking, and never lose hope on the day you cannot stop asking.** The asking is the work the will is already doing in you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architectural reveal

Now look at what we have walked through.

Verse 11: the waverer on the edge.
Verse 15: the despairing one who cuts himself off.
Verse 16: **and thus We sent it down as bayyināt — and Allah guides whomever He wills.**

The sequence is not random. It is a diagnosis followed by a description of the medicine. **The diagnosis: the human heart is unstable, prone to wavering and to despair.** The medicine has two ingredients: the descended clarity that points and separates, and the divine will that turns the heart to receive it. Neither ingredient alone heals. Both together do.

And notice what is missing from the medicine. **Self-rescue is not in it.** The waverer cannot stabilize himself by trying harder. The despairing one cannot find his way out by sheer effort. Both need the bayyinah from above and the hidayah from above. The cure descends. It does not ascend out of us.

### Bringing the linguistic and thematic together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are saying the same thing from two directions.

The linguistic side: *kadhālika* (clarity is a response to our condition), *bayyināt* (signs that point and separate), *yahdī man yurīd* with its open verb (guidance is a sovereign gift).

The thematic side: knowing is not transformation, and the will to be moved by what you know is itself a divine gift.

These are not two lessons. They are **one lesson** — that the human being lives between two descents. The descent of clarity, which is real. And the descent of guidance, which is also real. **And our work is to stand under both with our hands open.**

### Questions to carry

1. Where in your life have you been treating a heart problem like a clarity problem — assuming that more information will fix what is actually a willingness issue?
2. Whom have you given up on, who falls fully within the unspecified reach of *yahdī man yurīd*?
3. When was the last time you asked for guidance, not to escape a particular trial, but as a daily admission that even your faith is something Allah is keeping alive in you?
4. If clarity has already descended, what are you doing with the bayyināt you already have — the ones you can already see, but have not yet let cut a line through your life?
5. Can you sit honestly with the unfinished verb at the end of this verse, without trying to complete it with a formula that would let you control the outcome?

### One-sentence distillation

**The signs were sent with perfect clarity, and the heart's turning toward them is a gift we live by, not a result we achieve.**

### The closing invitation

Let nothing about this verse leave you passive. The bayyināt are real. They are in your hands. You are accountable for what you do with them.

But let this verse also relieve you of the burden you were never asked to carry — the burden of manufacturing your own guidance, the burden of forcing the hearts of those you love. **Lay that down.** Carry the bayyināt. Live them. Point others to them. And leave *yahdī man yurīd* in the hand it has always been in.

Be one who asks. Be one who keeps asking. Because the asking is the answer beginning to arrive.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You who sent down the signs as clarities that separate truth from falsehood — let them separate them in our hearts, and not just in our books. O You who guides whomever You will — will us. Will the ones we love. Will the ones we have despaired of. And keep us, every day, among those who are still asking. *Āmīn.*
