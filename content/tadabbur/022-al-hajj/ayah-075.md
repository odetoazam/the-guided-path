# Allah Chooses: The Selecting From Among Angels and Men

> [ayah:22:75] ٱللَّهُ يَصْطَفِى مِنَ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ رُسُلًا وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَمِيعٌۢ بَصِيرٌ
>
> *Allāhu yaṣṭafī mina-l-malā'ikati rusulan wa mina-n-nās. Inna-llāha Samīʿun Baṣīr.*
>
> *Allah selects from the angels messengers, and from mankind. Indeed Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.*

---

## Introduction — The Question No One Said Out Loud

Before we open the words, we have to feel the moment.

You are in Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ is being mocked daily — not for his message exactly, but for being who he is. *Him?* That orphan? That man who used to walk in our markets? That man whose tribe is not the wealthiest, whose voice is not the loudest, whose lineage carries no king? **Why him?**

The objection is recorded openly in another ayah. The chiefs of Makkah and Ṭā'if asked: *"Why was this Qur'an not sent down upon a great man from one of the two cities?"* (43:31). And underneath that complaint, in every century since, the same protest sits in the human chest: *Why was I not chosen? Why him? Why her? Why not me?*

Surah Al-Hajj has spent the previous ayahs taking apart what people worship instead of Allah. The idols cannot create a fly. Cannot rescue what a fly snatches from them. Cannot hear, cannot see, cannot answer.

And then, at the height of that demolition, the surah turns. It stops describing what is false. It describes how the real connection between heaven and earth actually happens.

In twelve words.

[PAUSE]

Listen. There is a word at the very beginning of this ayah whose root meaning is *the way a metallurgist draws pure silver out of stone.* And there are two divine names at the end whose presence here is not decoration — they are the courtroom verdict that closes the entire argument the surah has been making.

Let us slow down enough to see it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: يَصْطَفِي — The Verb That Means *To Draw Out The Pure*

Allah opens the ayah with His own Name and then a single verb: **يَصْطَفِي** (*yaṣṭafī*).

Most translations render it simply: "chooses." That is true, but it is the surface of a much deeper word.

Before we go in, let me teach you something about Arabic verb forms. Arabic verbs have ten major patterns. Each pattern bends the basic root toward a particular kind of action — intensification, causation, mutual action, reflexive doing-for-oneself. **Form VIII** — the pattern this verb is built on — typically carries the meaning of *doing the action upon oneself* or *taking the action's result for oneself.*

So Form VIII of "to write" doesn't just mean "to write" — it means "to inscribe for oneself, to copy out for oneself."

Now hold that and look at the root underneath this verb: **ص-ف-و** (*ṣ-f-w*).

In classical Arabic, this root means *clarity, purity, the state of being free of sediment.* The clearest documented use of *ṣafā* in the lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib) is **water that has stood still long enough that the silt has fallen to the bottom and only the clear part is on top.** Not water that was always clear. Water from which the muddy part has been separated out.

Now combine the root and the form.

*Yaṣṭafī*: Allah, in Form VIII, *takes for Himself the pure portion.*

[PAUSE]

This is not the verb you use for picking a name out of a hat. This is the verb of the metallurgist standing over the smelter, watching molten ore separate, drawing off the silver while the dross sinks. This is the verb of the diver who comes up holding the one pearl from a sea of empty oysters. The action presumes that there is something hidden inside the larger mass — something that the one selecting can see and the rest cannot.

Notice what this single word does to the question *"Why him?"*

The question assumes that everyone in the pile is equivalent and Allah just *picks*. That selection is arbitrary, like a lottery. The verb says: no. There is a quality being drawn out that the One drawing it knows, and the ones standing around objecting do not.

The complaint *"Why him?"* is the complaint of someone looking at the surface of the water. *Yaṣṭafī* is the verb of the One who can see all the way to the bottom.

[PAUSE]

And notice the present tense. Not *iṣṭafā* — past, "He selected." But *yaṣṭafī* — present-continuous, ongoing, repeated. **Allah is, always, constantly, drawing out the pure.** This is not a one-time event in history. The pattern of divine selection is a current that runs through all time.

---

### Element Two: The Word That Allah Did Not Repeat

Read the ayah again, slowly, and watch for what is *not* said:

> **مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا وَمِنَ النَّاسِ**
>
> *...from the angels messengers, and from mankind...*

Here is the structure in plain shape:

- *From the angels* — **messengers** (*rusulan*).
- *And from mankind* — [...].

Did you catch it?

The word *rusulan* — "messengers" — is mentioned **once**. It appears after "from the angels." After "from mankind," there is silence where the parallel grammatical word should be.

In Arabic, this is a documented rhetorical phenomenon. A word can be elided — left unspoken — when the listener is expected to carry it forward from the first half of the parallel. Translators, knowing this, almost always insert "messengers" again in the second half: *"and from mankind [messengers]."* That insertion is reasonable; the basic meaning carries.

But pause on what the silence is doing.

Allah could have said it twice. The ayah's rhythm would have allowed it. Instead, the word *rusulan* lives only on the angels' side of the sentence. On the human side, the noun is just left open. *And from the people...*

Some classical mufassirūn — Imām al-Rāzī notes this in his *Tafsīr al-Kabīr* — read the elision as deliberate breadth. Yes, *messengers* are selected from humanity. But the unspoken word leaves room for *more than messengers*: the prophets, the truthful, the witnesses, the righteous — the entire chain of *iṣṭifā'* that runs through human history. Allah does not select only the prophets from humanity. He is **always** drawing out the pure ones — and the verb's refusal to be repeated as just one noun preserves that breadth.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this.

When you imagine "Allah chooses from people," what comes to your mind? A handful of named prophets — Mūsā, ʿĪsā, Ibrāhīm, Muḥammad ﷺ. A list ending around twenty-five.

Now hear the ayah's silence. *And from mankind.* No closing noun. The set is not closed. The verb is in the present-continuous — *yaṣṭafī.* Allah is *still* drawing out.

The ayah doesn't say it explicitly, but its grammar leaves the door cracked open: every generation, somewhere, He is taking pure ones from among the people. The famous of them you may have heard of. The unknown of them are walking among you.

What this invites us to consider — and here we must be careful, because we have moved from grammatical fact to interpretive application — is that the closed-door image we sometimes carry of *the chosen* may be smaller than the open structure of the verse. Allah selects messengers as the most visible expression of selection. But the verb does not stop running.

---

### Element Three: سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ — The Two Names That Close the Case

The ayah ends with two of Allah's beautiful names placed deliberately together: **Samīʿun Baṣīr** — All-Hearing, All-Seeing.

Now, these two names appear together many times in the Qur'an. They are not unusual in pairing. So our question must be specific: **why these two, here, at the end of an ayah about selection?**

Teach yourself a small interpretive habit. When the Qur'an closes an ayah with a divine name, the name is almost never a closing flourish. It is the *because* of what was just said. The name explains why the verb makes sense. The name is the courtroom verdict on the matter just argued.

So: Allah selects from the angels messengers, and from the people. **Why does this work? Because — Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.**

[PAUSE]

Now think about what the rejectors of the Prophet ﷺ were doing. They were *speaking out loud* their objection — *why was this not sent to a great man from one of the two cities?* (43:31) They were comparing externals — wealth, lineage, social rank — *what they could see.*

In response, Allah names Himself *Samīʿ* — the Hearing One — first. **He has heard what you said.** Your objection has not been missed. He is naming Himself in answer to it.

And then *Baṣīr* — the Seeing One. **He has seen what you cannot see.** What your eye does for you is mostly catalogue surfaces — height, clothing, family, money. What His seeing does is reach interiors. He sees the heart that you cannot see. He sees the readiness, the purity drawn out by the smelter's fire of *yaṣṭafī*. He sees the qualification you are blind to.

Notice the architecture now: the verb at the beginning — *yaṣṭafī*, "draws out the pure" — and the names at the end — *Samīʿ, Baṣīr*, "Hearing, Seeing." They are the same statement said from two ends. **Selection is not arbitrary, because the One Selecting hears every objection and sees every interior.** The verb makes the names necessary. The names make the verb trustworthy.

---

### Bridge

The linguistic construction has shown us something. *Yaṣṭafī* tells us *what* Allah does — He extracts the pure. The elision tells us the door is wider than our list. *Samīʿ Baṣīr* tells us *why* this works — because He alone hears the objection and sees the interior.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the nature of being chosen, and the nature of being passed over.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Smelter's Eye — Why Selection Humbles the One Who Was Not Chosen

There is a kind of pain that has no name in our modern emotional vocabulary, and almost everyone has felt it.

You watched someone get the job, the marriage, the platform, the spiritual station — and underneath your congratulation was a quieter voice that said: *I would have done that better. I am as worthy as them. Why them and not me?*

That voice is older than you. It is the voice of the chiefs of Makkah saying *why this one, from one of the two cities*. It is the voice of Iblīs saying *I am better than him; You created me from fire and him from clay.* It is, in fact, the very first protest in scripture — and it was always a protest about *iṣṭifā'*, about being passed over for someone the protester saw as less.

Watch what happens to that voice when *yaṣṭafī* lands on it.

The protest assumes the chooser is operating with the protester's information. *I see myself, I see them, I see no good reason why them.* The verb says: there is information you do not have. There is a quality beneath the surface that the smelter's fire reaches and your eye does not.

This is why the ayah ends with *Samīʿ Baṣīr* — not with *ʿAlīm Ḥakīm* (Knowing, Wise), which would have also fit. Allah deliberately chooses the two sense-names — Hearing and Seeing — because the protest is fundamentally about *what you can hear and see versus what He hears and sees.* He is meeting the human eye and the human ear with His.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for the people in your life you have envied, even quietly.

The person who got the role you wanted: did you ever ask what they were carrying inside that you could not see? The grief, the discipline, the years of small unwitnessed obediences, the invisible refining? The smelter's fire that ran them at temperatures you never had to endure?

You were comparing surfaces. Allah was selecting from interiors.

This is not a consolation prize for the un-chosen. It is something stranger and more freeing. **It means the comparison was always wrong.** You were never being weighed against them on the same scale. Each interior is its own draw, its own pull of the pure portion. Your *iṣṭifā'* — if Allah wills it — looks like your life, not theirs.

What does this change tomorrow? It changes the way you scroll. It changes the way you sit in a room and quietly resent who gets the attention. It dissolves the question *why them* into a different question: *what is Allah drawing out of me, in the place He has actually put me?*

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Hidden Selected — When the Verb Doesn't Stop

Now stay with the elision in the middle of the ayah. *And from mankind* — and then no closing noun.

We treat *the chosen* as a closed historical class. Twenty-five named prophets. Their stories are over. The category is sealed.

But the verb is in present-continuous tense. And the second noun was withheld. The grammar refuses to seal the category.

Bring this into your everyday seeing.

Somewhere in your city right now, an old woman who no one knows is praying for the people on her street by name, and her prayers are being answered. Somewhere a child is forgiving a sibling in a way that adults cannot. Somewhere a young man, in the middle of a workplace that does not believe in any of this, is refusing a haram transaction at real cost. Somewhere a mother is whispering Qur'an over a sleeping child and nobody is filming it.

These are not prophets. The category of nubuwwah is closed; that is settled. But the larger structure of *iṣṭifā'* — the divine drawing-out of pure ones from the human mass — has never stopped. The verb is still running.

This means two things at once.

**First**, you have probably been in the room with someone Allah has selected, and you did not know it. The selection of the unseen righteous is the engine that holds the world together — the awliyāʾ whose names will never be spoken on any platform, whose tears in the last third of the night you will never witness. Walking among you. Sitting in the back of the masjid. Stocking shelves at your grocery store. The famous selected — the prophets — are the ones Allah lifted into history's spotlight. The unfamous selected are everywhere, and the verse refuses to close the door on them.

**Second** — and this is the harder one to receive — *you* are not excluded by definition.

The ayah does not promise you anything. It does not say you are chosen. But its present-tense verb and its withheld second noun together make a quiet structural statement: *the door has not been welded shut.* If you stop assuming you are unselectable, if you stop assuming the smelter's fire was finished a thousand years ago, you might begin to live differently. You might begin to live as someone who could, in some small way, be drawn out.

[PAUSE]

What does this change tomorrow?

It changes the way you treat your hidden actions. The midnight prayer no one will ever know about. The dollar slipped into someone's account anonymously. The harsh word swallowed for the sake of Allah. **These are not nothing. These are the kind of qualities the smelter's fire reaches.** They are the quiet metal beneath the noisy stone of your public life.

And it changes the way you treat the unimpressive people around you. The verb is still running. The people Allah is drawing out in your generation may not look the way you expect.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Revealed

Look at the whole ayah now, with everything we've seen:

> **اللَّهُ يَصْطَفِي مِنَ الْمَلَائِكَةِ رُسُلًا وَمِنَ النَّاسِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ بَصِيرٌ**

It opens with the Name **Allāh**.
It closes with two of His Names — **Samīʿ, Baṣīr**.
In between, the verb of selection, the open category, and the silent door.

The structure is a courtroom. Allah names Himself, declares the verdict — *yaṣṭafī*, I draw out the pure — and signs it with the two names that answer the objection: *the One who hears your protest and sees what you do not.*

Every accusation against every prophet — *why him?* — gets buried in the geometry of this single verse. The protest assumed surfaces. The verse names interiors. The protest assumed equivalence. The verse names a refining fire. The protest spoke aloud. The verse names a Hearer. The protest looked at outsides. The verse names a Seer.

The two themes converge into one insight: **the One who sees what you cannot is the only One qualified to choose, and He has not stopped choosing.** The first half of that sentence frees you from the bitterness of being passed over. The second half opens the door to a life that does not give up on being drawn out.

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose selection — by Allah, or by the world — have I quietly resented? What surface was I comparing, and what interior could I not see?
2. If *yaṣṭafī* is in the present tense and the second noun was withheld — what would change about my hidden actions if I lived as someone who has not been disqualified?
3. Have I been in rooms with people Allah is drawing out, and dismissed them because they did not look the part?
4. When I object to who Allah has placed in authority over a moment of my life — a teacher, a parent, a leader, a circumstance — am I hearing my own version of "why him, from one of the two cities"?
5. What is the smelter's fire in my own life right now? What is being burned off, that I have been treating as suffering rather than as refining?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah is always drawing the pure ones out from among His creation, and the only qualification you can trust is the one He alone can hear and see.

### The Closing Invitation

Let this ayah do two small things in you.

Loosen one resentment. Somewhere you have been quietly bitter that another was chosen. The bitterness assumed you and they were on the same scale. *Yaṣṭafī* tells you no scale you can read was ever the one being used. Set the bitterness down, even if only for an hour.

Take one hidden action seriously. Not for praise. Not so anyone will see it. Not even so Allah will choose you — that is His business, not the price of your obedience. Take it because the verb is still running and your interior is still being read.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who select from the angels messengers and from mankind — You who hear what we say and see what we hide — refine in us what is fit for You to draw out, and remove from us the protest of those who could not see beyond the surface. Place us, even unseen, among the ones Your verb is still gathering. Āmīn.*
