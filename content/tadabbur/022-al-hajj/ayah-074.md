# When the Scale Cannot Weigh Its Maker

## INTRODUCTION

Listen carefully to these eleven words.

> **وَمَا قَدَرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ حَقَّ قَدْرِهِۦٓ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَقَوِىٌّ عَزِيزٌ**
>
> *Wa mā qadarū llāha ḥaqqa qadrih. Inna llāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz.*
>
> **They have not appraised Allah with the appraisal that is His due. Indeed, Allah is Strong, Mighty.**

Eleven words. And they appear, in slightly varied form, three times in the Quran — once in Sūrat al-Anʿām, once here in al-Ḥajj, and once in al-Zumar. Each time, Allah is making the same diagnosis from a different angle: there is something the human mind keeps failing to do.

Not failing to *believe in*. Failing to *measure*.

### Where we are in the surah

To feel this ayah, you have to be standing where the listener was standing a moment before. The previous ayah — 22:73 — has just delivered one of the most devastating arguments in the Quran. Allah issues a challenge: *Yā ayyuhā l-nās* — O humanity. Listen. The things you call upon besides Allah — gather them all together — they cannot create a fly. And if a fly snatched something from them, they could not even take it back.

Then the ayah ends with five words that sit like a verdict: **ḍaʿufa l-ṭālibu wal-maṭlūb** — *weak is the seeker and weak is the sought*. The worshipper is weak. The thing being worshipped is weak. Both ends of this transaction collapse together.

You can almost feel the air go still after that line.

And then — *into that stillness* — comes this ayah. Not as a new argument. As the diagnosis behind the entire argument that just ended.

> They did not measure Allah with His true measure.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to feel before we open the words.

We live in a civilization that *measures everything*. We measure salaries, calories, distances, IQ scores, productivity, attention spans, follower counts, square footage, blood pressure, social capital. The modern person is a walking calibration instrument. We weigh, rank, compare, and price every object that crosses our field of vision.

So Allah comes — to a creature whose mind is built to weigh — and says:

**With all your weighing, you have not weighed Me truly.**

Not "you do not know I exist." Not "you have not heard of Me." But: *you have not given Me My weight.* You have placed Me on the scale of your mind, and the scale read a number — and the number was wrong.

[PAUSE]

What does it mean to mis-weigh God?

That is the question this ayah is going to open. And by the end, I hope you will see that the answer is not academic. It is the most practical diagnosis of the modern soul we have.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to open three things in this verse: a single root that does the heavy lifting; a small grammatical construction that turns a measurement into a *moral debt*; and the way the verse before this one is sonically *engineered* to crash into this one.

### Element One — The root *q-d-r*: where measurement, value, and decree are the same word

Before we look at what the verse says, I want you to feel how Arabic builds words.

Arabic operates on what scholars call a **trilateral root system**. Most words in the language are built from three consonants — and every word built from those three consonants shares a family of meaning. The root *k-t-b* gives us *kataba* (he wrote), *kitāb* (book), *kātib* (writer), *maktab* (desk), *maktaba* (library). All of them are about writing.

So when Arabic chooses one root to do the work of multiple ideas, it is making a claim. It is saying: *these things are not separate. They are facets of one reality.*

Now look at the root in the heart of this ayah: **ق - د - ر** — *q-d-r*.

What does *q-d-r* mean?

It means *to measure*. It means *to estimate the amount of something*. It means *to appraise*, *to value*, *to determine the worth*.

It also means *to have power over*. *Qādir* is a Name of Allah — *the One who has power*.

It also means *to decree*. *Qadar* is the Quranic word for divine decree, destiny.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. In Arabic, *to measure something*, *to have power over something*, and *to decree something*, **are all the same word**.

This is not coincidence. This is the language telling us something true. To truly *measure* a thing is to have a kind of cognitive *power* over it. A scientist who can measure a phenomenon precisely has, in some sense, captured it. And whoever has the power to set the measurement *decrees* what the thing is.

So when Allah says **mā qadarū llāha** — *they did not qadar Allah* — listen to how many doors that opens at once.

They did not measure Him correctly.
They did not value Him correctly.
They did not estimate His worth correctly.
They did not recognize the scope of His power correctly.
They did not honor Him with the weight that decreed reality requires.

**All of these are simultaneously true.** The root is doing all of them at once. Arabic does not force you to choose one meaning, because the failure is *all* of them.

And then — watch this — the very next word, the noun that completes the phrase, is *qadrihi*. **From the same root.**

> *mā qadarū llāha ḥaqqa **qadrih***
>
> they did not **qadar** Allah with the truth of His **qadr**

The verb and the noun come from the same letters. So the sentence is, in a sense, eating its own tail. *They failed to measure Him with the measure that was due to Him.* The failure and the standard against which the failure is being judged are spoken in the same sound.

It is one of the densest constructions in the Quran. Three uses, three different surahs, every time aimed at human cognition's relationship to the Divine.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means.

If *qadr* were only one of these things — only "measurement," only "value," only "decree" — then a person could potentially *fix* the problem by improving in that one dimension. Calibrate the scale. Adjust the price. Update the estimate.

But because *qadr* is **all of these at once**, mis-weighing Allah is not a technical error you can patch. It is a total stance. To mis-measure Him is to mis-value Him is to misunderstand His power is to misalign with His decree.

The single Arabic root has trapped the entire problem in one syllable.

### Element Two — *ḥaqqa qadrih*: when the verb meets a moral debt

Now I want to teach you a small grammatical structure, because this verse is using it in a way most translations cannot carry into English.

In Arabic, there is a construction called **al-mafʿūl al-muṭlaq** — sometimes translated "the absolute object" or "the cognate accusative." Here is how it works in plain language:

When you want to specify *the manner* or *the truthfulness* of an action, Arabic lets you take a noun related to the verb and put it in the accusative case as an intensifier. So instead of just saying "he loved her," Arabic can say "he loved her *with a love*" — meaning "he loved her in the way love truly demands." The word that carries the manner of the action sits beside the verb and qualifies how completely the action was done.

Here, the construction is built around the word **ḥaqq** — and *ḥaqq* in Arabic is one of the most loaded words in the entire Quran.

*Ḥaqq* means truth. But it also means *right*. As in, "this is your right." It means *due*. As in, "this is what is owed to you." It means *reality*, *that which actually is*. Allah is *al-Ḥaqq* — the Real, the True.

So **ḥaqqa qadrih** is not, strictly speaking, "the true appraisal of Him" the way English flattens it.

It is: ***the appraisal that is His due. The measure that is owed Him. The estimation that reality itself demands you give Him.***

Do you feel the difference?

If I told you, "you did not give the painting a fair appraisal" — that is a critique of accuracy.

If I told you, "you did not give the painting **the appraisal that was its due**" — that is a critique of *justice*. You owed the painting a certain measurement, and you fell short of that debt. The painting was *more* than what you wrote about it, and your underwriting was an injustice.

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah is saying. The failure to measure Allah correctly is not a math error. **It is an unpaid debt of cognition.**

Reality itself is owed an honest accounting of its Origin. And we have not paid that debt.

This is why translations like "they have not appraised Allah with true appraisal" — though grammatically defensible — carry only half the weight. The Arabic carries a moral charge: *you owe Him a recognition you have not given Him.*

That is what *ḥaqqa qadrih* is.

It is not a critique of your worldview. It is a debt notice from your Creator.

### Element Three — the engineered crash from *ḍaʿufa* to *qawiyy*

Now I want to show you something that will only be visible if you read the previous verse and this verse as a single sound event. Because that is how they were originally heard.

The previous ayah — 22:73 — ends with these words:

> **ḍaʿufa l-ṭālibu wal-maṭlūb** — *weak is the seeker and weak is the sought.*

The key word is **ḍaʿufa** — *was weak, became weak*. From the root **ḍ-ʿ-f**. The Arabic ear catches this: *ḍaʿufa* is a heavy, slow word. The *ḍād* — that fat, dragging consonant unique to Arabic — pulls the word down. *Ḍaʿufa* sounds like what it means. It is a word that has the gravity of failure inside it.

Then 22:74 begins. And what is the first attribute Allah names of Himself at the end of the new ayah?

> **inna llāha la-qawiyyun ʿazīz** — *indeed Allah is Strong, Mighty.*

**Qawiyy** — from the root *q-w-y*. Strong. Possessor of strength.

[PAUSE]

The Quran has just placed two words in opposition across the ayah boundary:

- **ḍaʿufa** — weak (end of 22:73)
- **qawiyy** — strong (end of 22:74)

This is not random. The two words are direct linguistic opposites. They are the antonym pair Arabic uses for the strength/weakness binary. Allah has staged the rebuke deliberately:

*The seeker is weak. The sought is weak. The whole transaction of false worship is weak.*
*And then the truth: I am the Strong One. The One you were supposed to be measuring.*

Now hear the architecture.

Verse 73 ends on **ḍaʿufa**. Verse 74 ends on **qawiyy**. **The same syllable count. The same syllable rhythm. The same final stress.** The Arabic ear hears them as twin words sitting at the end of two consecutive sentences — one weak, one strong — making a single statement together.

*You have spent your worship on weakness. The Strength was right in front of you.*

That is what these two ayahs are doing across their seam.

And what does the verse pair *qawiyy* with? **ʿAzīz**.

The root *ʿ-z-z* is one of the most psychologically rich roots in Arabic. It carries: *strength, but also rarity*. *Power, but also preciousness*. The land that is *ʿazīz* is land that is barren and inaccessible. The honor that is *ʿizz* is honor that cannot be touched, cannot be diminished by anyone else's opinion.

So **qawiyyun ʿazīz** together is not redundant. *Qawiyy* tells you He has internal strength — capacity, capability, force. *ʿAzīz* tells you that strength is *unreachable* — beyond your ability to diminish, contest, or access without His permission.

The verse closes by telling you the One you mis-measured is **strong in His own being and inaccessible to your measuring instruments** at the same time.

That is why your appraisal was wrong. *The Subject of measurement is, by His own nature, beyond what your scales can hold.*

[PAUSE]

Now let's bring the linguistic work together.

The root *q-d-r* told us that to measure is to value is to recognize power. Failing in any one dimension is failing in all three.

The construction *ḥaqqa qadrih* told us this measurement is owed — it is a debt of cognition reality itself imposes on you.

The crash from *ḍaʿufa* to *qawiyy* told us *who* it is owed to: the Strong, the Inaccessibly Mighty, in opposition to all the weak gods we run after.

So let's ask now what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart that keeps mis-weighing.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The mind as a scale that cannot weigh its own maker

Imagine you walk into a workshop and there is a scale on the bench. A precision scale, with calibration weights, a digital readout, the whole instrument. Beautifully made.

You ask the scale, "what is the weight of the engineer who built you?"

The scale gives you a number. Maybe it weighs the engineer's lab coat folded on the bench. Maybe it weighs his coffee cup. The number it gives is *some* number — but the number is for the *traces* the engineer left in the workshop. The scale cannot weigh the *engineer himself*, because the engineer is the one who *defined what weight even means in this workshop*.

The scale is doing its job. Its job is to weigh objects in its category. The engineer is not in its category. He is the cause of the category.

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah is naming.

The human mind is a magnificent instrument. It can weigh. It can value. It can rank, compare, model, predict. We measure galaxies. We measure neurons. We measure our own dreams in dollars.

But the mind was built *inside* a created order. Its categories — quantity, time, space, comparison, cause and effect — are themselves created. They are the rulers and the rubrics that came into existence when Allah said *kun*.

So when the mind turns toward its own Origin and tries to measure Him, **the mind is using categories that He authored to weigh Him.** The instrument is downstream of the One it is trying to measure.

What happens? It returns a number. The number is some number. And that number — whatever it is — is wrong. Not because the mind is broken. Because the mind is *categorically downstream*.

This is what *mā qadarū llāha ḥaqqa qadrih* names. **You used a calibrated instrument to measure the One who calibrated the instrument.** Of course the reading is off.

But here is the part that hurts: **we don't know it is off.** The scale gives us a number, and we trust the number, and we live our entire spiritual life as though that number were the truth.

We treat Allah as a thing somewhere on the scale. Sometimes a heavy thing. Sometimes a light thing. Often an item in our priority list — He sits at position one, or position three, or position twenty-seven, depending on the season. We *rank* Him.

The verse says: He is not on the scale. **The scale is in His hand.**

[PAUSE]

Now let me translate this into the texture of an ordinary day.

You wake up. You check your phone. You weigh — instinctively — the importance of what's on the screen. A message from your boss is *heavy*. A friend's text is *medium*. A push notification from a news app is *light*. A reminder for *Fajr* is — *what weight does it have on your scale?*

Be honest.

For most of us, the prayer reminder weighs less than the work message. Not because we don't believe in the prayer. Because *the weighing apparatus we use* — built by our culture, our economics, our ambitions — has assigned the prayer a low number.

This is the daily phenomenon of *mā qadarū llāha ḥaqqa qadrih*.

It is not that we have stopped believing. It is that our scale has miscalibrated, and we have not noticed.

The ayah comes as a system shock. **You have not given Me My weight.** Whatever the apparatus you are using — *recalibrate.* Because no item in the inbox, no number in the bank account, no ranking in the social order, can sit higher than the One who created the inbox, the bank, and the social order itself.

**The practical application:** before you act on the priority your scale hands you, *pause for one breath* and ask — *did this scale ever weigh its Maker correctly?* If the answer is no, then every reading it gives you is provisional. Especially the ones that demote prayer, demote remembrance, demote the next good action. Especially those.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two — The diagnosis is not unbelief. It is undermeasurement.

This is the second thing the verse reveals, and it is the harder thing.

When we hear a rebuke about people not appraising Allah correctly, our minds immediately picture *atheists*. Or *idol worshippers*. People who deny Him. People who pray to statues. *Those* people did not measure Him correctly.

But look at the verse again. *Mā qadarū llāha ḥaqqa qadrih.* They did not measure Him **with the measure that is His due**.

The grammar does not say they assigned Him zero. The grammar says they assigned Him *less than His due*.

[PAUSE]

This is a critical distinction.

Zero is a number that idolatry produces. The mushrik of pre-Islamic Mecca often did acknowledge Allah as the supreme creator — they just thought their idols were intermediaries, secondary measures of weight in the divine economy. They did not assign Allah zero. They assigned Him *less than His due*.

And here is the disturbing implication: **the diagnostic of this ayah does not stop at the boundary of unbelief.** It comes *into* the household of belief.

A believer who prays five times a day, fasts Ramadan, gives zakat — that believer can still under-appraise Allah. How? By treating Him as one priority among many. By trusting his bank account more than His decree. By fearing his boss's anger more than His displeasure. By feeling sorrow over a lost opportunity more than over a missed prayer.

The believer is not at zero. The believer is at *less than the due*. And the verse looks straight at that gap.

This is why the verse has been so terrifying to the great hearts of the tradition. They did not read it as being about *those people over there*. They read it as being about *me right now*.

Imam al-Ghazālī wept over it. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote that a person could spend a lifetime correcting their measure of Allah and still die in the act of correction. Because the gap between *what we have weighed Him as* and *what He truly is* — is infinite.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because this is the moment the ayah turns from a rebuke of others into an invitation to ourselves.

The invitation is: **stop assuming you are calibrated.**

Most spiritual stagnation is built on the quiet assumption that *my measurement of Allah is roughly correct, and now I just need to live by it.* That assumption is what the verse is dismantling. Because the moment you assume your measurement is correct, you stop *increasing* your appraisal. You stop adding to the number.

But the entire trajectory of īmān is the trajectory of *ever-rising appraisal*. Today you weighed Him heavier than yesterday. Tomorrow you should weigh Him heavier than today. Not because He is changing. Because *your scale is finally beginning to reach toward His true weight*, asymptotically, knowing it can never arrive.

The Companions had this. Abū Bakr did not stop increasing his appraisal of Allah at the moment of conversion. Forty years of the Prophet's companionship and his appraisal was still rising. ʿUmar's was rising. ʿĀʾishah's was rising. The mountain of their reverence had no summit, because the One they were reverencing had no top.

This is what the ayah invites: a life of *recalibration upward, forever*.

**The practical application:** at the end of every day — before sleep — ask one question. *Where today did I treat something as heavier than Allah?* Not in theory. In the actual decisions of the day. The moment I chose comfort over the prayer. The moment I chose silence over the truth. The moment I chose anxiety about an outcome over trust in His decree. Each of those moments is a place where my scale read low. Note them. Tomorrow, *recalibrate*. The recalibration is the worship.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

We opened three doors in this ayah.

**The root *q-d-r*** — where measuring, valuing, and recognizing power are not three things but one thing. So failing to measure Allah is also failing to value Him is also failing to recognize His decree. The failure cannot be partitioned.

**The construction *ḥaqqa qadrih*** — where measurement becomes a debt. Reality itself owes Allah a certain accounting, and the human mind has not paid it. This is not an opinion difference. It is an unpaid moral debt of cognition.

**The crash from *ḍaʿufa* to *qawiyy*** — where the previous ayah's verdict on false worship (*weak is the seeker and the sought*) is matched against the diagnosis of the human heart (*Allah is Strong, Mighty*) — and the contrast is engineered down to the syllable. Weakness on one side. Strength on the other. The ayah is asking: *which side has your weight gone to?*

And then the two themes:

**The mind is a scale that cannot weigh its own maker.** Every category we use to measure was authored by Him. So our reading is always categorically downstream. The remedy is not better measurement. The remedy is *humility about the measurement itself*.

**The problem is not unbelief. It is undermeasurement.** The verse does not stop at the boundary of īmān. It enters the household of the believer and says: even you, who pray and fast and give — your scale is still reading low. Recalibrate upward. Forever.

These are not two themes. They are one truth from two angles. The mind cannot weigh its Maker — *and yet the mind is asked to weigh Him as truly as it can, knowing it will always fall short*. The asymptote is the spiritual life.

### Questions to carry with you

1. When was the last time something on my scale outweighed Allah? Not in theory — in the actual decision of an actual hour?
2. What measurement category am I using right now to weigh Him — and is that category itself something He created?
3. If my appraisal of Allah were to *double* tonight, what would I do tomorrow that I am not doing today?
4. Where in my life am I treating Him as one priority among others, rather than as the Origin of all priorities?
5. When was the last time the gap between His true weight and my measurement of Him made me weep?

### One-sentence distillation

**The mind that measures everything has not measured the One who made measurement — and the spiritual life is the lifelong, never-completed work of correcting that one error.**

### The closing invitation

This is not an ayah you finish. It is an ayah you carry.

You will go back to your phone. The scale will start weighing things again. Messages, deadlines, opinions, ambitions. That is fine. That is what minds do.

The invitation is small. *Once a day*, look at the readings your scale is producing — and remember that the Maker of the scale was never on the scale to begin with. He is the One who decided what weight would be in the workshop. He is **al-Qawiyy al-ʿAzīz** — Strong in His being, Inaccessible to your measuring instruments.

When you remember Him this way, the readings of the scale do not change. But your relationship to the readings changes. They become provisional. Useful for life. Not ultimate. Never ultimate.

And in that small shift — that single breath of *recalibration upward* — you have begun to pay, in trembling installments, the debt of *ḥaqqa qadrih*.

### A closing du'ā

> O Allah, Strong, Mighty, Inaccessible —
> we have not measured You with the measure that is Your due, and we know we cannot.
> Forgive us for every priority we have placed above You,
> every comfort we have weighed heavier than Your call,
> and every day we have ended without our appraisal of You having grown.
>
> Recalibrate our scales toward You.
> And when our weighing fails — as it always will —
> let us at least be among those who knew it was failing,
> and trembled, and tried again.
>
> *Inna-ka la-Qawiyyun ʿAzīz.*
