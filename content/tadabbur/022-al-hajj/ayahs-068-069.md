# When They Twist the Rope: How Allah Teaches Us to End an Argument

## Surah Al-Hajj 22:68–69

> وَإِن جَادَلُوكَ فَقُلِ ٱللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ
>
> ٱللَّهُ يَحْكُمُ بَيْنَكُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ فِيمَا كُنتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ

*"And if they dispute with you, say: 'Allah knows best what you are doing.' Allah will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which you used to differ."*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah was spoken into

To feel what these two ayahs are doing, you have to imagine a very specific kind of pressure.

A man stands in Makkah, surrounded by people who refuse to leave him alone. Some of them want to convert him back. Some want to mock him out of his certainty. Some want to entangle him in an argument so circular that, by the time it ends, no one remembers what the original question was. They come to him at the Kaʿbah, in the markets, at the edges of his gatherings — and they argue. They twist words. They take his statements and braid them into knots, then hand him the knots and demand he untie them.

This is a man who is being asked, every day, to **defend reality itself**. To explain why the gods his people have worshiped for generations are not real. To justify, in conversation, what Allah has revealed to him in private. And he has to do this not in a seminar room — but in the streets, with people whose questions are not always sincere.

[PAUSE]

If you have ever been in an argument that you knew, somewhere inside you, was not really an argument — it was a performance, a trap, a way of making you waste your soul on noise — you know what I am describing. You know how it pulls at you. You know how, after twenty minutes, you have started to sound like the person you were arguing against. The shape of the argument has reshaped you.

Surah Al-Hajj has been moving through pilgrimage and resurrection, through the rites of Ibrahim and the trembling of the Hour. And right here, in the middle of all that grandeur, Allah pauses to teach His Messenger something almost embarrassingly practical: **what to say when someone is trying to drag you into a fight.**

### The hook

Two ayahs. Seventeen words. And inside them, Allah does something stunning. He gives the Prophet a way to end an argument without winning it. He gives him a sentence that pulls the entire dispute up out of this world and re-files it in a court that has not yet opened. And He embeds — into the grammar itself — a clue about the actual nature of what we call "religious dispute."

What is the argument *really*? When does it end? Who decides?

Stay with me. We are going to take this slowly.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: the word جَادَلُوكَ — and what a "rope" has to do with theology

Before we open this word, I want to teach you something about Arabic verb forms. Stay with me — it will pay off.

Arabic verbs come in shapes called *forms*. Form I is the basic stem: he did the thing. Form II often intensifies: he did it intensely or repeatedly. Form III, though — Form III has its own personality. Form III is the form of **mutual engagement**. When you take a verb and put it in Form III, you are saying: this is not one person acting on another. This is two people doing something *to each other, simultaneously*.

If Form I "kataba" means "he wrote," Form III "kātaba" means "he corresponded with someone" — letters going both ways. If Form I means "to fight," Form III means "to wage war with" — both sides swinging.

Form III is the form of the duel. The form of the dance. The form where the action only exists because two parties are co-creating it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at our word: **جَادَلُوكَ — jādalūka.** "If they jādal you." Form III.

The root is **j-d-l.** And here is where the word opens up.

In classical Arabic, the root j-d-l does not first mean "to argue." It first describes a physical action: **to twist a rope tightly.** *Jadl* is the way you take loose strands and braid them into something taut, something that does not come undone easily. A rope-maker is a *jaddāl*. A tightly-braided thing is *majdūl*.

Sit with that.

A *jadl* is what you do to strands of hemp to make them inseparable from each other. And the Arabs, watching this, named a certain kind of speech after it. Because there is a kind of arguing where what you are doing, with words, is *exactly* this: you are taking the other person's statements and braiding them around your own until neither of you can find where one began and the other ended. You are making a rope of language.

Notice what this implies. *Jadal* is not the search for truth. *Mudhākarah* is the search for truth — sitting together to remember and think. *Munāẓarah* is structured debate. But *jadal* — *jadal* is when speech becomes a rope. It is the kind of conversation where the goal is not to arrive somewhere together. The goal is to **bind**.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says, *"if they jādal you"* — and uses Form III, the form of mutual engagement — He is naming a very specific situation. He is saying: *if they come at you with that twisting, two-sided, knot-making mode of speech, where the argument is no longer about anything because the argument has become its own subject —*

And then watch what He does *not* say.

He does not say: *jādil-hum.* "Twist back." He does not put the Messenger into Form III in response. He does not give him a rope of his own.

He gives him something else entirely. He gives him a sentence that will not braid.

This is the first quiet miracle of the ayah. The instruction *breaks the symmetry*. They are in Form III; the response is not. The rope-makers are met with someone who refuses to make rope.

What does this tell us about what real prophetic speech sounds like? It does not match the energy of the disputant. It does not enter the shape of the dispute. It steps **sideways** — out of the argument's geometry — and points somewhere the argument does not go.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ — and the word that is missing where you would expect it

Look at the response Allah teaches: **اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ.** "Allah knows best what you do."

I want you to notice what is *absent* here. And to notice it, we need to think about what we would expect to be present.

If someone disputes with me about my faith — if they twist my arguments and try to expose what they think are my errors — what would I most naturally want to say back? *"You don't know what you're talking about."* Or: *"Allah knows best what you are saying."* Or: *"Allah knows the truth of what we are discussing."*

But Allah does not say: *bi-mā taqūlūn* — "what you say."
Allah does not say: *bi-mā tujādilūn* — "what you are disputing."
Allah does not say: *bi-mā tukadhdhibūn* — "what you are denying."

He says: **بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ — what you DO.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. This is doing something.

The disputants are operating at the level of speech. They are spinning words. They are performing arguments. And the response Allah teaches **completely refuses to engage at that level**. It says: *I am not going to talk to you about your words at all. I am going to redirect this entire conversation to something you forgot was being watched: your actions.*

Here is what this reveals. In a *jadal* — in a rope-twisting argument — both parties pretend that the only thing that matters is who can construct the better rope. Whose words win. Whose logic locks tightest. The whole argument is operating on the assumption that the contest is *verbal*.

And Allah, in one phrase, dismantles that assumption. He says: I am most knowing of *what you do*. Not what you say. Not what you argue. Not what you sound like when you make your case. **What your hands have actually done. What your body has actually moved toward. What your life has actually been.**

The argument suddenly has a different scoreboard.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. The verb is **تَعْمَلُونَ — taʿmalūna** — present tense, ongoing. *What you are doing right now, including this very disputation.* The arguing itself is a deed. The performance of the *jadal* is itself an action being recorded. So the redirection is also a quiet warning: even this argument you are having is being weighed not as language but as deed. You think you are sparring. You are actually living.

And one more thing. The phrase is **اللَّهُ أَعْلَمُ — Allāhu aʿlamu**, "Allah is most knowing." It is not "Allah knows" (*yaʿlamu*). It is the comparative-superlative form: **most** knowing. More knowing than anyone in this argument. More knowing than the disputant who is performing certainty. More knowing than the Prophet himself. The phrase pulls everyone present onto the same bench — under the same gaze of a knower whose knowing exceeds them all.

This is what real *de-escalation* sounds like. It is not negotiation. It is not appeasement. It is the calm, sovereign refusal to operate in the medium the disputant has chosen — and the redirection to the medium that actually counts.

What this invites us to consider: when someone argues with us about our faith, the question we have been trained to ask is "how do I win this verbally?" The question Allah is teaching is something else. *Whose actions, in this exchange, will look like Islam? Whose deeds will the argument testify to?*

That is the only scoreboard.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: كُنتُمْ فِيهِ تَخْتَلِفُونَ — and the strange tense of dispute

Now we move to ayah 69. And here Allah does something with **time** that I want you to feel.

The ayah is: *Allah will judge between you on the Day of Resurrection* **fīmā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn** — *concerning that over which you used to differ.*

Three pieces of grammar are doing serious work here. Let me lay them out.

**First:** the verb **تَخْتَلِفُونَ — takhtalifūna**. This is Form VIII. The root is *kh-l-f*, which means: to come after, to succeed, to differ, to be other than. Form VIII (*iftaʿala*) is the form of **reciprocal, mutual action you take on yourselves** — to mutually-do-the-thing-among-yourselves. So *takhtalifūna* is not "they differ from him" or "he differs from them." It is: *you all are mutually differing, among yourselves.*

This is important. Allah is not framing the dispute as *you (disbelievers) versus him (the Prophet).* He is framing it as something all of you — together — are doing among yourselves. Differing as a collective act. Like a hum that rises out of a crowd. The dispute belongs to the group, and the group is in it together.

**Second:** the construction **كُنتُمْ ... تَخْتَلِفُونَ — kuntum ... takhtalifūna.** This is the past tense of *kāna* (you used to be) joined to a present-tense verb. In Arabic this construction creates **past habitual** meaning — *you used to be in a state of differing.* It is the tense of something that was your continuous condition.

**Third — and this is the moment I want you to really feel** — when does Allah place this differing in the past tense?

He places it on the **Day of Resurrection.**

[PAUSE]

Listen carefully. From the perspective of the Day, all of this — all of our arguments, all of our *jadal*, all of our religious controversies, all the rope-twisting we are doing right now — is already **kuntum**. Already *used to.* Already past tense.

Right now, in this life, our disputes feel like the most present thing imaginable. They feel current. They feel ongoing. They feel like they will never end. Two people on opposite sides of a religious question feel like they are inside a verb that has no past tense.

But Allah, speaking to us now, narrates our disputes from the standpoint of after-they-are-over. He uses a tense that places us already on the other side of resurrection, looking back. *Fīmā kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn.* That which you **used to** differ in.

Used to.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what this does?

It pre-archives the argument. While we are still inside it, sweating, bracing, twisting, Allah has already given it a past-tense form. The *jadal* you are in right now — Allah is naming it, before you finish, with the grammar of something that has ended. He sees the conclusion of the argument before the argument has concluded.

This is the discipline of the believer who internalizes 22:69. You realize that the disputation pulling at your sleeve, demanding a verbal response right now, is — from the standpoint of the Court that actually matters — already over. It is already in the pluperfect. It is already a thing you *used to* be in.

And what is the verb of the Day itself? **يَحْكُمُ — yaḥkumu**, present tense. *Allah judges.* On the Day of Resurrection, the disputing is past, and the **judging is present**. The active verb belongs to Him, not to us. We had our verb in the world. He has His verb on the Day. And the two are not the same magnitude of verb.

[PAUSE — major]

### A bridge before we move into the themes

So we have three movements in the language.

**One:** they twist a rope (jādalūka — Form III, the form of mutual entanglement); the Messenger is told not to twist back, not to enter their geometry, but to step sideways.

**Two:** he is told to redirect from words to deeds (*bi-mā taʿmalūn*, not *bi-mā taqūlūn*) — to deny the disputant the medium of language and pull the entire question down onto the ground of actual lived life.

**Three:** the matter is then re-framed as something *kuntum* — already, in the deepest reality, past tense — to be settled in a court that is opening on a Day already drawing nearer.

These three moves — *don't braid back, redirect to deeds, place the dispute in past tense* — are not three separate teachings. They are one teaching, seen from three angles. The Prophet is being given a way to inhabit dispute without being deformed by it.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how to actually live with people who oppose us.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The discipline of not matching the energy

Let me start here, where you live.

Imagine you are online. Someone has quoted you out of context. They have built a thread. They have screen-captured your words and put them next to a deliberately unflattering interpretation. The thread is performing. It is not asking a question. It is **building a rope.**

What is the standard response? You feel it physically. The chest tightens. The fingers move. You start drafting. *Actually, what I meant was —* and you are already three sentences into the rope, with them, weaving.

Twenty minutes later, you have spent your afternoon. You have produced six paragraphs of clarification. You have argued back. You have, in some sense, won. And yet — when you put the phone down, why do you feel hollowed out? Why does winning feel like losing?

Because **you became the shape of the thing you were arguing against.** Form III pulls you in. The mutual engagement reshapes both parties. Stand long enough in front of a mirror that distorts you, and you start moving the way the mirror demands.

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah is naming. *Jadal* is not just bad arguing. *Jadal* is a **mode of speech that deforms whoever enters it.** And the instruction Allah gives to the most articulate human being in history — the man who could have demolished any argument — is: **don't enter it.**

Sit with how strange that is. The Prophet ﷺ could speak. The Quran is the most articulate text in existence; the man delivering it could clearly handle a verbal challenge. And yet, when the *jadal* comes, his instruction is not "use your superior eloquence to win." His instruction is: *redirect to deeds, refer to Allah's knowledge, end the argument by leaving its medium.*

This is a teaching about **what eloquence is for.** Eloquence is not for winning rope contests. Eloquence is for delivering truth. The moment speech becomes a rope, even the truest tongue should fall silent and point to something the rope cannot bind.

[PAUSE]

There is a movement here in three steps, when you find yourself being pulled into *jadal*:

*First*, recognize the shape. Not every argument is a *jadal*. Some questions are sincere, and you should answer them. *Mudhākarah* and *munāẓarah* and *naṣīḥah* are real things — sitting together to think, structured debate, sincere advice. The ayah is not telling you to become silent in the face of every theological question. It is teaching you to **recognize when speech has stopped serving truth and started serving the rope.**

*Second*, refuse the medium. If the question has become performance, do not perform back. If the words have become weapons, do not arm yours. The redirection is not avoidance — it is precision. You are saying: *what you are asking is not the question, and I am not going to pretend it is.*

*Third*, point to the only court that matters. Not as a way of dismissing the person — but as a way of locating the conversation in its actual jurisdiction. *Allah knows best what you do.* Including this. Including me. Including the heat in my chest right now. *Allah will judge.*

That last redirection — *Allah will judge* — is not a threat. Listen carefully. Most of us hear it as a threat: *just you wait.* That is not what is happening. What is happening is that the believer is **letting go of a court he was never qualified to preside over in the first place.** He is saying: I am not going to render the verdict. I do not have the bench. I do not have the jurisdiction. The case goes up.

And in letting go, he is freed.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow? Specifically:

When the next argument arrives — and it will — try this. Before you respond, ask one question: *am I being invited into thinking, or into a rope?* If it is thinking, engage. If it is a rope — even if you would win the rope contest — refuse the medium. Say less. Say something that the rope cannot braid around. Redirect to deeds, to lives, to the only Knower whose verdict is binding.

You will be accused of dodging. Let that happen. Some of the highest discipline of the believer is to be accused of cowardice for refusing a fight that was beneath the truth being defended.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The argument has an expiration date — and you are not the judge

Now I want to push us toward the second teaching. It is, in a way, the foundation of the first.

You can only refuse to engage in *jadal* if you really, viscerally, know one thing: **the argument will end.** Not philosophically. Not eventually. *Specifically.* On a specific Day. With a specific Judge. Whose verdict will be the only one that counts.

If you don't believe that — if, somewhere in your bones, you suspect that this argument right here is the only forum in which the truth will ever be adjudicated — then of course you have to win it. Of course you have to twist back. Of course your tongue is the last line of defense.

But the Prophet ﷺ, in this ayah, is being grounded in a very different reality. He is being told: *this argument is not final.* It only feels final because you are inside it. From the standpoint of the Day, this whole disputation is **kuntum** — past tense, archived, awaiting verdict. Your job is not to win it. Your job is to not become deformed by it while it runs its course.

[PAUSE]

Let me put this in a shape you can feel.

Imagine you are arguing with someone about who broke a vase. Twenty minutes in, both of you have built elaborate cases. You have witnesses. They have witnesses. The argument is ferocious. It feels like the most important thing in the world.

And then someone walks in and says: *the entire thing was on camera.*

What happens to the argument?

It does not so much end as **collapse**. It is revealed to have been, the entire time, *already settled.* The truth of who broke the vase was not pending the outcome of your verbal contest. It was already recorded. The argument was just sound waves over a fact that had been determined the moment the vase fell.

[PAUSE]

This is what *kuntum fīhi takhtalifūn* names. Right now, in this dunya, our religious arguments feel like they are determining truth. They are not. They are sound waves over a recorded reality. The camera has already captured everything. The Knower already knows. The arguments do not change the verdict; they just are themselves more footage being recorded.

And once you really feel that — once it is not theory but bone — *jadal* loses its grip on you. Because what was its grip? Its grip was the secret belief that the argument was ultimate. That if you lost the rope contest, you lost the truth. That your tongue had to defend reality.

But the truth is not defended by your tongue. The truth is **known** — by Him — and **judged** — by Him — and **disclosed** — by Him. Your tongue's only job is to not betray the truth, and to not be deformed in its service.

[PAUSE]

There is something else here, and I want you to hear it carefully because it is humbling.

Notice that 22:69 says: *Allah will judge **between you**.*

Between **whom**? In the immediate context, between the Prophet ﷺ and his disputants. But the grammar is plural — *baynakum* — and it puts everyone on the same side of the courtroom: the Messenger and his opponents are together the parties between whom Allah will judge.

This is striking. The Messenger of Allah is not framed as the judge in this scene. He is one of the parties. He, too, stands before Allah's judgment. He, too, has the verdict pending — though we know, from elsewhere, what his verdict is.

What does this imply for the rest of us?

It implies that **none of us are the judge.** Not the most learned scholar. Not the most pious worshiper. Not the brother who is right. The judgeship belongs only to Allah. The ones in dispute are *all* parties in front of Him.

Which means: when you find yourself in religious dispute, even when you are right, you are not the judge of the other party. You are a witness, possibly. A caller, possibly. A defender of something true, possibly. But not a judge. The judge is in another court, and that court has not yet opened.

[PAUSE]

What does this change tomorrow?

Two things, very practically.

*First*: it lifts a weight off your shoulders that was never yours to carry. You do not have to win every argument because you are not the judge. You do not have to render the verdict because the verdict is not yours to render. You can speak truth as a witness — calmly, accurately — and then **release the case.** It goes up. It is not yours to decide.

*Second*: it changes how you treat the person you are disputing with. If you had been the judge, you would treat them as a defendant. But if you and they are both *baynakum* — both parties under the same coming verdict — then you treat them as someone whose ultimate fate is in the same hands as yours. You become more careful. More gentle. More aware that *Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā taʿmalūn* applies to you also. He knows what you do, including how you spoke to this person who differed with you.

The discipline of the ayah is, in the end, not just about ending arguments. It is about **standing in dispute as someone who knows they are being watched, alongside the one they are disputing with, by the same Eye.**

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now let us look at the whole shape of what these two ayahs do.

Ayah 68 addresses the **horizontal**: how you stand in this world when someone disputes with you. *Don't twist back. Redirect to deeds. Refer to His knowledge.* Allah is **most knowing** — *aʿlamu*.

Ayah 69 addresses the **vertical**: where the dispute ultimately resolves. *On the Day. Between all of you. Concerning what you used to differ in.* Allah **judges** — *yaḥkumu*.

The two together form a complete teaching:

> **Knowledge** governs the present. **Judgment** governs the future.
> Knowledge is the floor on which judgment will stand.
> The believer aligns himself with both: with the One who knows now, and the One who will judge then.

And the architectural inversion: the **disputants** are using verbs in the present tense — they are arguing right now. The **Prophet** is given a sentence anchored in Allah's knowledge — outside the argument's tense. The **Day** then re-narrates the dispute itself as past tense — *kuntum* — and assigns the present-tense verb to Allah's judging.

So the verbs of the world flip. What feels present-tense to us (our arguing) is, in the deepest sense, already past. What feels future-tense to us (the Judgment) is, in His reality, already locked. The believer who absorbs this lives in the **right tenses.** He treats his own disputes as already-archived. He treats the coming Judgment as already-real. And in doing so, he stops being the prisoner of the present argument.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic discovery: *Form III jadal* is mutual entanglement; the response refuses Form III; the dispute is placed in *kāna + imperfect* — the past habitual; the verbs of the Day are present-tense.

The thematic discovery: don't match the energy of the disputant; the argument is not final; you are not the judge; Allah knows now and will judge then.

Both are saying: **stand inside dispute without being deformed by it, because the medium of dispute is not where truth is decided.**

### Questions to carry

1. When was the last time I entered a *jadal* — a rope-twisting argument — and walked away feeling deformed by it? What did I learn about the medium that should change how I respond next time?

2. In my disputes, am I behaving as if I am the judge of the other party? What would change if I treated us both as parties standing under the same coming verdict?

3. *Bi-mā taʿmalūn* — what I am doing. If my words right now were translated into deeds and weighed not as language but as actions, what would my arguing look like?

4. Do I really, in my bones, believe that this argument has an expiration date? Or am I behaving as if my tongue is the last line of defense for the truth?

5. What does it mean for me, today, to release a case to the only Court that can rule on it?

### The whole ayah in one sentence

> *When they twist a rope with their words, refuse the medium, redirect to the only Knower of deeds, and let the case go up to the only Judge — because what feels like a present-tense argument to you is already past tense in the courtroom that actually decides.*

### The closing invitation

This is not about becoming a person who never speaks up for the truth. The Prophet ﷺ spoke, taught, and called — endlessly. This is about becoming a person who **knows the difference** between speech that serves truth and speech that has become rope. And, when the rope appears, having the discipline — and the trust in Allah's knowledge and Allah's judgment — to step out of the rope's shape and into the open air.

Tomorrow, an argument will find you. Some of you, before this day ends, will be invited into a rope. The invitation will not announce itself. It will look like a conversation, a comment, a thread, a brother who wants to "just clarify something."

Test it gently. Is this thinking, or is this rope?

If it is rope: try saying less. Try the redirection. Try, even quietly to yourself, the words: *Allāhu aʿlamu bi-mā naʿmal — Allah knows best what we are doing.* And let the case go up.

You will feel something loosen in your chest. That is the loosening of a burden you were never qualified to carry.

### Du'a

> O Allah, You who know our deeds more than our words, who will judge between us on a Day when our arguments are past tense — teach us to stand inside dispute without being deformed by it. Save our tongues from rope. Save our hearts from imagining ourselves as judges. And on the Day You judge between us, in that which we used to differ in — let us be among those whose deeds, not whose disputes, testified for them. Āmīn.
