# The Two Blind Spots and the Return of All Matters

## Surah Al-Hajj, Ayah 76

> [ayah:22:76] يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ ۗ وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ
>
> *Yaʿlamu mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum, wa ilā Llāhi turjaʿu l-umūr.*
>
> **He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them — and to Allah all matters are returned.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

Surah Al-Hajj is one of the strangest, most far-reaching surahs in the Quran. It begins with a tremor — *yā ayyuha n-nāsu ittaqū rabbakum, inna zalzalata s-sāʿati shayʾun ʿaẓīm* — "O mankind, fear your Lord; surely the convulsion of the Hour is something terrible." And from that opening shock, the surah moves through resurrection, through the rites of pilgrimage, through Abraham's stand at the Kaʿba, through permission to fight, through the catastrophic helplessness of false gods who cannot even create a fly. It is a surah that keeps asking the same question from different angles: **who actually has power, and who is just pretending?**

By the time we reach the passage that contains our ayah — verses 73 through 76 — we are deep in that argument. Allah has issued a thunderous challenge: gather all the false gods you call upon, and let them try to create a fly. They cannot. Even if a fly snatches something from them, they cannot reclaim it. *ḍaʿufa ṭ-ṭālibu wa l-maṭlūb* — "weak is the seeker, and weak is the sought." Then Allah declares: they have not measured Allah with the measure due to Him. He is the Strong, the Mighty.

And then, in verse 75, He says something that lands differently: *Allah chooses messengers from among the angels and from among people. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.* He is the One who selects. He picks His representatives. He hears. He sees.

And then comes our verse — the seal on the whole passage:

> **He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them — and to Allah all matters are returned.**

### Reconstruct the Moment

Try to inhabit the world of the original listener. The Prophet ﷺ has been chosen — *muṣṭafā* — and chosen ones in this world are not loved. They are mocked. They are plotted against. People conspire while they sleep. Tribes meet in the night to discuss how to silence them. Promises are made and broken. Allies hesitate. Family members turn cold.

The chosen one walks forward into hostility he cannot fully see. The future is opaque to him in the ordinary human way: *what will tomorrow hold? Who will betray? Who will believe? When will this end?*

And behind him, there is also fog. The whispered meetings he never witnessed. The decisions made in rooms he was never in. The grudges nursed for years before he ever spoke a word of revelation. The mockery passed around at night. The ridicule that travels faster than truth.

Two blind spots — front and back. The chosen one is squeezed between them, walking forward, defenseless to what is unseen.

Now hear the verse again:

> *He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them.*

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Pay attention to two things, because they are about to change how you read this ayah.

First — Allah did not say "He knows everything." He said something much more specific. He named **two blind spots** — and those two are not random. They map exactly onto the human experience of fear. We will see why.

Second — Allah did not say at the end "all matters return to Him." He said something different in Arabic. The verb is **passive**. *Turjaʿu l-umūr* — the matters **are returned**. By whom? Allah doesn't say. And that absence is the whole point.

These two stones — the named blind spots and the hidden hand — together build a verse that does something rare in the Quran: it gives the messenger, and us, **a place to put our anxiety**.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to walk slowly through three features of this verse. Each one earns the next. By the end you will see that what looks like a simple statement of divine knowledge is actually a precisely engineered piece of comfort.

### Element One: *Mā Bayna Aydīhim wa Mā Khalfahum* — The Merism of Front and Back

#### Teach the concept first

In every language that humans speak, there is a way of saying "everything" by naming **two opposites**. We do it in English without thinking. "From cradle to grave" — we don't mean only those two moments; we mean the whole life in between. "From sea to shining sea" — not just the coastlines; the whole nation. "Heaven and earth" — not two specific places; everything that exists. This is called a **merism**. You name the two extremes, and the totality between them is implied.

Arabic uses this device constantly. *Al-mashriq wa l-maghrib* — east and west, meaning everywhere. *Al-awwal wa l-ākhir* — the first and the last, meaning the whole stretch.

Now hold that idea, because Allah has chosen a very specific merism here. Not east and west. Not first and last. Not heaven and earth.

He chose: **what is in front of them, and what is behind them.**

#### Open the phrase

> *مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ*
>
> *mā bayna aydīhim wa mā khalfahum*

The first part literally reads: "what is **between their hands**." This is the Arabic idiom for what is in front of you — the field of vision, the stretch of road ahead, what is approaching. When you walk forward, what is "between your hands" is what you are walking toward.

The second part: "what is **behind them**" — *khalfahum*. The root *kh-l-f* is the root of being left behind, of what comes after, of what is at your back.

Front and back. Two directions. And between them — supposedly — the totality.

But sit with this for a moment. **It's not a totality.** It's incomplete on purpose.

#### Stay with this

When Arabic wants to say Allah knows everything, it has plenty of ways. *Wāsiʿun ʿalīm* — vast in knowledge. *ʿAllāmu l-ghuyūb* — knower of all unseen things. *Bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm* — knowing of every single thing. The Quran uses these phrases hundreds of times.

Here, Allah didn't say any of those.

He named **specifically front and back**. He could have said "above them and below them." He could have said "their open and their hidden." He could have said "their inside and their outside." Each of those is a real Arabic merism. Each of those would have meant "everything."

Why front and back?

Because **front and back are the two directions you cannot see**.

You can see what is to your right — turn your head. Left — turn your head. Above — look up. Below — look down. Inside yourself — close your eyes. But what is in front of you — fully? You can see *toward* the front, but you cannot see *through* the front. You cannot see what is on the other side of the next moment. The future is a wall.

And behind you — you literally cannot see behind you without turning around. Even when you turn, what is now behind you was a moment ago in front. The back is the structural blind spot of the human body.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah names *what is between their hands and what is behind them*, He is not making a comprehensive claim about His knowledge in general. He is making a **targeted** claim. He is saying:

**The two places where you cannot see — I see.**

The two regions of your fear — I cover.

Because we don't actually live in fear of "everything." We live in fear of **two specific things**: what is coming that we cannot prepare for, and what is already done that we cannot undo. The future and the past. The wall and the rear.

Notice something. The future haunts us through anxiety — *what if they reject me, what if I fail, what if the next moment brings collapse?* The past haunts us through guilt and grievance — *what was said about me, what was decided about me, what was done that I cannot now reach to fix?*

These are the two doors through which suffering enters the human mind. Front and back.

And Allah, with surgical precision, said: **I know what is in front of you, and I know what is behind you.**

He didn't say "I know everything" — though He does. He said: **I cover your two blind spots.**

#### Deepen — who is this addressed to?

Now look at the pronoun. *Aydīhim* — "their hands." *Khalfahum* — "behind them." Plural masculine. **Whose** them?

Go back one verse. Verse 75: *Allah chooses messengers from among the angels and from among people.* The most immediate antecedent is the messengers — *al-rusul*. Allah is saying: He knows what lies before His messengers and what lies behind them.

So this verse is, first and most directly, **a word to the chosen.** To the Prophet ﷺ standing in Mecca, surrounded by mockery, walking into a future of exile and battle. To Mūsā walking into Pharaoh's court without knowing whether the staff will work this time. To Ibrāhīm walking with his son toward a hill, not knowing the ram is coming.

The messengers are uniquely exposed. They walk forward into hostility they cannot quantify. They walk away from a past full of plots they were never told about. Of all human beings, the messenger has the largest blind spots — because the largest forces in the world are arrayed against him.

To the messenger, Allah says: *I see your front and your back.*

But of course — the messenger is not the only one. The principle applies to anyone who walks forward into the unknown carrying a calling. To anyone who has tried to do the right thing and felt afraid of what was coming, or haunted by what had been done. To **you**, when you sit in the worry of the future you cannot see, or the memory of the past you cannot retrieve.

The verse is structured first for the prophets, but it leaks outward to every soul that has ever feared in those two directions.

#### Concluding statement

When Allah said *He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them*, He was not making a general theological claim about omniscience. He was naming, with care, the **two specific regions where a human being is blind** — the unfolding future and the buried past — and placing Himself there.

This is not information about Allah. This is **comfort offered through information about Allah**.

### Element Two: *Yaʿlamu* — The Tense That Doesn't End

#### Teach the concept first

Arabic verbs work differently from English verbs. In English, when we say "He knows," we are using a present-tense statement, but it sounds like a snapshot — a fact in this moment. In Arabic, the equivalent form — what grammarians call the *muḍāriʿ*, the imperfect — does not just describe **this moment**. It describes an ongoing, continuous, unfolding action that does not stop.

Think of it this way: the Arabic *yaʿlamu* doesn't mean "He knows right now." It means "He is knowing — and continues to know — without interruption."

When the Quran wants to talk about something that happens once and is done — Allah created, Allah said, Allah sent — it uses the perfect tense, the *māḍī*. When the Quran wants to talk about something that is **always happening, never finishing, eternally in motion** — it uses the imperfect.

This is one of the most quietly powerful features of Quranic Arabic. The choice of tense is doing theology.

#### Open the word

Here Allah did not say *ʿalima mā bayna aydīhim* — "He knew" (perfect, complete, finished). He said:

> *يَعْلَمُ*
>
> **yaʿlamu** — He knows. He keeps knowing. He has never stopped knowing. He will never stop knowing.

Now apply this back to the merism we just unfolded. Allah's knowledge of what lies before you and behind you is not a pre-recorded snapshot taken at the beginning of time. It is a **live presence**.

This matters. Imagine the difference. A photograph of you knows you. So does a person watching you. But the photograph is dead — it captured one moment and then it stopped. The person watching is alive — they are seeing you **now**, and now, and now, adjusting their understanding as you move.

The Arabic *yaʿlamu* belongs to the second kind. Allah is not a photograph that captured your future and is holding the print. He is a witness who is with you in the unfolding. He is knowing your future not by remembering it, but by **being there when it happens, and now, and now**.

#### Deepen

Here's why this matters for the ache of being human.

When we worry about the future, what we are really worrying about is **being alone in it**. We imagine ourselves stepping into a moment we have not yet stepped into, and we know that no one we love will fully be with us there. The friends will not be there. The parents will not be there. The certainty we have today will not be there.

The verse tells us something else.

Whatever is in front of you that you fear — Allah is **already there, knowing**. Not in a passive way. Not in a "He'll find out when you find out" way. He is there now, before you arrive. Already aware. Already with you in the moment that has not yet happened from your side.

And what is behind you — the past you cannot now reach, the moment you wish you could go back to and change, the conversation you replay at 3 a.m. — Allah is **still there, knowing**. The past is not gone for Him. He still sees what you cannot see. The grace He extended in moments you didn't notice. The mercies you walked past unaware. The crimes against you that He saw even when no one else did.

[PAUSE]

The combination of *yaʿlamu* (continuous) with the merism of front and back is doing something specific:

**He is permanently stationed in the two places where you cannot go.**

Not "He visits them." Not "He has a record of them." He is *living* in them, in the present-continuous tense of His own knowledge. Permanently.

This is what it means for a human being to actually be **seen**.

#### Concluding statement

The choice of *yaʿlamu* — present-continuous — is what turns this verse from a fact about Allah into a refuge. He is not knowing your blind spots from far away. He is knowing them as a living witness, in the same eternal "now" that contains your past, your present, and your future at once.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Wa Ilā Llāhi Turjaʿu l-Umūr* — The Passive Voice and the Hidden Hand

#### Teach the concept first

In every language, there is a difference between active voice and passive voice.

Active: *He drove the car.* The doer is named.

Passive: *The car was driven.* The doer is hidden — or absent, or unnamed on purpose.

In Arabic, the passive is built morphologically. The active form *yarjiʿu* means "he/it returns" — the subject is doing the returning. The passive form *yurjaʿu* — or here, *turjaʿu* in the feminine to match the plural feminine *al-umūr* — means "he/it is returned." Something is making the return happen, but the agent is not named.

When the Quran uses the passive deliberately — and it does, with great care — it is usually because **the agent is so obvious that naming it would diminish the effect, or the absence of the agent is doing meaningful work.**

#### Open the phrase

> *وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ*
>
> *wa ilā Llāhi turjaʿu l-umūr*

Three things to notice, in order.

First: the prepositional phrase *ilā Llāhi* — "to Allah" — is **fronted**. In normal Arabic word order, a verb comes first, then its subject, then its complements. The default would be: *turjaʿu l-umūru ilā Llāh* — "the matters are returned to Allah." But Allah inverted it. He put *ilā Llāh* first.

In Arabic rhetoric, fronting a phrase like this carries the meaning of **restriction or exclusivity**. It transforms the statement from "the matters are returned to Allah" into "**to Allah specifically, and to no one else, are the matters returned**."

Second: the verb is passive. *Turjaʿu* — "are returned." Not "they return." The matters are not running back home of their own accord. **They are being driven back.** Something is moving them. Some force, some hand, some pull, is bringing every matter that has been set in motion back to its origin point.

Allah does not name the agent. He uses the passive. Why?

Because to name the agent — to say "Allah returns all matters to Allah" — would be tautological and clumsy. By using the passive, Allah is letting the **whole machinery of the cosmos** — every cause, every effect, every movement of every atom — be the unnamed mover. *Everything* is returning the matters. The angels, the laws of physics, the unfolding of time, the consequences of choices, the silent corrections, the visible eruptions — all of them, together, are doing the work of **return**.

And it all has one direction: *ilā Llāh*. To Allah.

Third: *al-umūr* — "the matters." Definite plural. Not "some matters." Not "important matters." Not "religious matters." **The matters** — meaning the totality of all matters that exist in any sense at all.

Stack these three together: an exclusive direction, an unnamed force, and an unlimited scope. The verse is saying:

**Every single matter, of every kind, by every means, is on its way back — and it is going to one place.**

#### Stay with this

Why is this comfort?

Because the messenger — and you — live in a world where **the matters look like they are getting away**. Plots happen. Mockery succeeds. The wicked seem to prosper. The good are humiliated. Lies travel faster than truth. Wealth flows to the unjust. Reputations are ruined by people who never have to face the person they ruined.

Look at the matters from inside the moment, and they look loose. Unaccountable. Drifting. As if the world is a leaky boat where things slip out the bottom and nobody is keeping count.

The verse says no.

There is no leak.

Every matter — the public ones and the private ones, the celebrated ones and the buried ones, the matters of empires and the matters of broken hearts — every one of them is **being returned**. The passive voice tells you: *you do not need to identify the mechanism*. There are a thousand mechanisms. There is gravity, there is consequence, there is the angel writing, there is the body remembering, there is the soul that cannot rest, there is the historian who eventually writes, there is the witness at the Hour. **All of them, working together, returning the matters.**

And the fronted *ilā Llāh* tells you: **only one destination**. Not to nothing. Not to forgetfulness. Not to the universe. Not to history. **To Allah.**

[PAUSE]

#### Deepen — the paradox of the passive

There is a strange tenderness in the passive voice here.

Allah could have said: *"I will return all matters to Myself."* That would have been an assertion of power, and it would have been true. But it would have made the listener feel **judged**. Like every action they took was being dragged back to a tribunal where Allah was sitting with a list.

Instead He said: *the matters are returned to Allah*. The verb has no actor named. The matters are simply on their way home, the way water finds the sea. The way light, after being scattered, eventually returns to its source. The way every breath you exhale is on its way back to the air it came from.

The image is not of a tribunal. The image is of a **return** — *rujūʿ* — which is the same root as *raj-ʿah*, return, and *rajʿah*, the going back. Everything that came from Him is going back to Him. The matters are not being seized. They are being **gathered**.

You came from Him. You are going back. The matter you are dealing with right now — the one that is consuming you, the one you cannot resolve, the one that feels like it is just bleeding out into the world without an answer — it is not bleeding out. It is on its way home. The current is invisible from inside the moment, but the current is real.

#### Concluding statement

The combination of fronted *ilā Llāh* (exclusive direction), passive *turjaʿu* (hidden mechanism), and definite plural *al-umūr* (total scope) builds a single sentence that says: *everything you are watching unfold, however chaotic, is on a precise trajectory back to one specific place.*

Nothing escapes. Nothing leaks. Nothing is forgotten.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have now seen three features of this short verse:

The **merism of front and back** — Allah names the two directions in which the human being is blind, and stations Himself there.

The **continuous tense of *yaʿlamu*** — His knowledge is not stored, it is lived; He is in the eternal present of every moment we cannot reach.

The **passive *turjaʿu* with the fronted *ilā Llāh*** — every matter, by every mechanism, is on its way back to Him alone.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of human suffering, and what Allah has placed inside this verse to meet it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Geography of Anxiety, and the Map That Covers It

#### Begin where we live

Modern psychology has a term that sits beneath most of our suffering: **anticipatory anxiety**. It is the fear of what has not yet happened. Studies on chronic worriers find that the overwhelming majority of the things people fear never come to pass — and yet the suffering is real, sometimes more real than the actual events when they finally arrive. The body cannot tell the difference between a wolf and the imagined wolf. It floods with cortisol either way.

There is also a backward-facing version of this. Therapists call it **rumination** — the loop of replaying the past. The conversation you wish you'd handled differently. The moment you missed. The decision that turned out to have been wrong. The room where someone said something about you that you were not in.

If you map a human life under stress, you find that the stress almost always lives in two zones: **forward** — what is coming — and **backward** — what cannot now be reached.

The present moment, oddly, is rarely where suffering lives. The present moment is mostly fine. You are sitting somewhere right now, breathing, with a roof over you. The pain is almost never in *here* and *now*. It is in *what is coming* and *what already happened*.

Therapists work hard to bring people back into the present. *Notice your breath. Feel your feet on the floor. Five things you can see, four you can touch.* These techniques work because they pull us out of the future and the past — the two regions where we suffer — and place us in the present, which is usually peaceful.

But there is a problem. The present is small. We cannot stay in it forever. The mind keeps flying out — forward, backward, forward, backward. And every time it does, the suffering returns.

Now hear the verse again.

> *He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them.*

#### Let the ayah illuminate

The Quran is not telling you to ignore the future or forget the past. It is doing something else. It is saying: **the two places where your mind cannot help going are the two places Allah has stationed Himself**.

You go forward in fear. He is there.

You go backward in regret. He is there.

The mind that flies into the future does not fly into emptiness — it flies into a region where Allah is already living. The mind that drifts into the past does not drift into a void — it drifts into a region where Allah is still present, still seeing, still holding.

This is a different kind of healing than secular therapy can offer. Secular therapy says: *the past and the future are not real, only the present is real, so come back to the present.* The Quran says: *the past and the future are real — Allah is in them.*

You do not have to evacuate them. You can let your mind go there, and find Him already there.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepen

Think about what this means for the prophet who first heard this verse. The Prophet ﷺ in Mecca was not anxious about a single specific thing. He was anxious about a whole **structure** of things. The future of his community. The fate of those who had been killed. The mockery of those who had not yet been guided. The plots being made tonight, in houses he could not enter. The fate of his uncle, who had not accepted. The wives of his Companions being tortured.

These anxieties did not have one location. They were *everywhere* — in front, behind, above, below. But the verse zeroed in on the two that map to time itself: front (future) and back (past).

Because all anxiety, in the end, is **temporal**. It is anxiety about a moment that is not now. The Prophet ﷺ was not anxious about the room he was sitting in. He was anxious about a moment he was not yet in, or a moment he could no longer reach.

Allah's response: *I am in those moments. I know them. I am living in them.*

The remarkable thing is what this implies about Allah's relationship to time. The verse is not saying *He has a record of the past and a forecast of the future*. The verse, with its present-continuous *yaʿlamu*, is saying that Allah is **simultaneously present in all of those moments**. The future is not an unwritten page for Him; it is an unfolding present. The past is not an archive; it is also an unfolding present. He stands in a kind of **eternal now** that contains every moment, and from inside that *now*, He sees everything.

We cannot live there. We are bound to one moment at a time. But we can know that He is there, and that our blind spots are not blind to Him.

#### Practical application

Try this. The next time your mind flies forward into a fear about something that has not happened yet, do not fight it. Do not force yourself back to the present. Let your mind go to the feared moment. And then say, quietly, inside that moment:

*Allah is here too.*

Whatever I am imagining. Whatever the worst case is. Whatever the catastrophe is. **Allah is already there. Knowing. Present. Living in that moment with His full attention.**

Then let your mind drift backward to the regret. The thing you cannot now fix. The conversation that ended badly. Say:

*Allah is still there. He saw what no one else saw. He knows what was actually happening, even what I did not know I was doing.*

The verse is not asking you to leave the future and the past. It is offering you the truth that **you have never been alone in either of them**.

This is not therapeutic technique. This is **theology applied to the ache of being a creature in time**.

### Theme Two: The Current Beneath the Surface — Why "Returning" Matters

#### Begin with the human experience

Have you ever felt that the world is leaking?

I do not mean a bad day. I mean the larger feeling — that the bad things slip away, that injustice gets buried, that the people who hurt you go on with their lives unbothered, that the cruelty of history is forgotten by history, that the cosmos is not actually keeping score.

Every honest person has felt this. The Prophet ﷺ felt it when he stood over the graves at Uḥud. The mother of Yāsir felt it as she was being killed. Every person who has watched a tyrant die comfortable in his bed has felt it.

There is a theological term for this feeling: **the sense that the moral order has detached from the physical order**. That the world's machinery does not, in fact, sort the matters into their right places. That what happens just happens, without consequence, without weight, without return.

Many people lose their faith in this place. Not in arguments. Not in philosophy. In **the felt sense that the world is leaking**.

#### Let the ayah illuminate

The second half of our verse is built precisely against that feeling.

> *وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ*
>
> *And to Allah the matters are returned.*

The passive voice — as we saw — does not name the mechanism. It says: by some means, *the matters are being returned*. They look loose from inside the moment, but they are not loose. The current is invisible at the surface but real underneath.

There is a way of seeing the world that this verse forces on you, if you take it seriously. Imagine standing on a beach watching the tide go out. From the shore it looks like the water is leaving. *Where is it going? Will it come back?* You feel a small panic. The horizon is empty.

But there is a current under the water. The water is not leaving. It is being **drawn back** to the moon's gravitational pull. The going-out is part of a coming-back. The whole ocean is on a leash you cannot see.

The verse is telling us this is the structure of reality. **Every matter** — every plot, every kindness, every word, every silence, every moment of suffering, every moment of joy — looks like it is drifting away into the world. It is not. It is on a leash. It is being drawn back to Allah.

The passive voice is the leash. The fronted *ilā Llāh* is the destination. There is no ambiguity. Nothing leaks.

#### Deepen — what about my matter?

Your matter is on the leash too.

The thing that happened to you that no one ever acknowledged. **It is being returned.**

The kindness you did that nobody saw, that did not even seem to register on the person you did it for. **It is being returned.**

The wound you carry that has shaped you in ways no one will ever know about. **It is being returned.**

The injustice you watched and could not stop. **It is being returned.**

The part of your story that has felt buried, irrelevant, off the books. **It is being returned.**

The verse does not specify which mechanism will do the returning. That is the genius of the passive. It might be a Day of Judgment. It might be a quiet correction in the next moment. It might be a long arc of consequence that you will never personally see. It might be a memory that surfaces in the heart of someone who wronged you and changes them. It might be a verse that finds someone else who needed it. It might be nothing visible at all in this world. **All of those count as the return.**

Allah does not say *how*. He says **that**. The matters are being returned. To Him.

[PAUSE]

#### The strange comfort of not knowing the mechanism

This is one of the most freeing aspects of the passive construction. You do not have to track the mechanism.

When you have suffered, the temptation is to **demand the mechanism**. *Tell me how this will be made right. Show me the punishment of the one who hurt me. Show me the recognition of the kindness no one saw. Tell me when, and how, and through what means.*

The Quran rarely answers that question. It tells you instead: *the matters are being returned*. By a thousand means you cannot trace. The whole machinery of the cosmos is bringing the matters home.

This is the difference between the believer's relief and the non-believer's despair. The non-believer demands to see the mechanism, and when the mechanism is not visible, despairs. The believer trusts that the mechanism is operating beneath the surface — *as the current is real even when the surface is calm* — and is freed from having to engineer it personally.

You do not have to make the matter return. You do not have to seize the consequence. You do not have to extract recognition. You do not have to chase the one who hurt you. **The matter is on the leash already.**

This is also why the verse can sit comfortably alongside the Quran's frequent commands to *do* justice in this world. *We work for justice in our own moment because that is part of how the matters return.* But the work of full return is not on our shoulders. It is happening, by His arrangement, with or without us.

#### Practical application

When something happens to you that feels unaddressable — when a wound is buried, when an injustice is invisible, when a kindness goes unrecognized — try this practice.

Speak the verse to yourself: *wa ilā Llāhi turjaʿu l-umūr*.

Then list the matter you cannot resolve: *the conversation that ended without apology; the sacrifice no one named; the praise that went to the wrong person; the harm done to my child; the years of effort that nobody saw.*

After each one, say: **this too is being returned.**

You are not asking Allah to do anything new. You are reminding yourself that He has already designed the cosmos so that it returns to Him. The matter is already on its way. You are simply choosing to stop demanding to see the mechanism.

This is a kind of *tawakkul* — reliance — that operates not on the future, but on the **structure of reality itself**. You are trusting that the world is shaped this way. That Allah has built the return into the bones of existence.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the verse whole, and notice what it is doing.

> **He knows what lies before them and what lies behind them — and to Allah all matters are returned.**

The verse has two halves, and they correspond to each other with surgical precision.

The **first half** is about Allah's gaze. It moves outward from the human being into the two regions of blindness — front and back, future and past — and says: *He sees there.*

The **second half** is about the matters' motion. It moves from the world back to Allah. *The matters are returned.* From everywhere, back to Him.

So the verse is doing two things simultaneously:

**His knowledge goes outward** to where we cannot see.

**The matters come inward** to where He is.

Two arrows. One pointing out; one pointing in. Together they form a complete circuit. There is no place we can fear that He has not already entered with His knowledge. There is no matter we can lose that is not already on its way back to Him.

Front, back, and the great current beneath. We are surrounded.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing the Linguistic Discoveries Together with the Themes

The two themes — the geography of anxiety and the current beneath the surface — are the same insight from two directions.

The **first half** of the verse heals you in the dimension of **time** — the past you cannot reach and the future you cannot foresee.

The **second half** of the verse heals you in the dimension of **consequence** — the matters you cannot track, the outcomes you cannot enforce, the justice you cannot deliver.

Time and consequence. The two great anxieties of the conscious creature. Allah covers both in eleven words.

And the linguistic features we walked through — the merism, the continuous tense, the passive voice, the fronted prepositional phrase — are not decorative. They are the **machinery** by which the verse delivers its comfort. Without them, the verse would be a flat statement. With them, it is a refuge.

This is what tadabbur reveals: that Allah is not just telling us things. He is **constructing places to live** in the architecture of His sentences.

### Questions to Carry

Carry these into the week. Do not answer them quickly. Let them work.

1. **Where is your mind going when it leaves the present?** Is it going forward into a fear, or backward into a regret? Today, when you notice it leaving, can you say to yourself: *Allah is already in the place my mind is going*?

2. **What matter in your life feels like it is leaking out?** What injustice, what sacrifice, what wound, what kindness, has gone unaddressed for so long that you have begun to doubt it counts? Can you let yourself believe — not as a hope, but as a fact about the structure of the world — that *it is being returned*?

3. **What would it mean to live as someone whose blind spots are not blind to Allah?** How would you walk into a difficult conversation, a feared meeting, an uncertain medical result, if you genuinely believed that Allah was already living in the moment you were walking toward?

4. **What mechanism are you demanding to see, that you might be allowed to release?** The Quran's passive voice frees you from having to track how the return happens. What would change if you stopped trying to engineer the consequence yourself?

5. **Whose front and back are you trying to control that you cannot actually see?** The verse is about Allah's knowledge of His messengers. Who in your life are you trying to protect by knowing things about their future or past that are not yours to know? Can you release them to the One who *does* see?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> **The two regions where you cannot see — Allah lives there; and the matters you cannot track — He is already drawing them home.**

### The Closing Invitation

The Prophet ﷺ heard this verse in Mecca, surrounded by enemies he could not see, walking forward into a future he could not predict. The verse did not promise him that the future would be easy. It did not promise him that every matter would be resolved in his lifetime. It promised him something else, and something larger:

**You are not alone in your blind spots. And nothing is leaking out of the world.**

That is what is on offer to you right now. Not a guarantee that life will be comfortable. Not a forecast that everything will work out the way you want. Something deeper. The conviction that the geography you live in — the future ahead, the past behind, the matters all around you — is not unwitnessed and not unaccounted for.

You can stop straining to see your blind spots. You can stop demanding to track every matter to its conclusion. The architecture of the world has built the gaze into the front and back, and built the return into the bones of every event. **You can rest into a structure that is already operating.**

This is not passivity. The Prophet ﷺ heard this verse and went on to do extraordinary work in the world. The point of the verse is not to make you stop. The point is to make you **stop carrying weight that was never yours**. The weight of seeing your own future. The weight of redeeming your own past. The weight of enforcing your own consequences. None of that is your job. **You can do your work in the present moment, with full attention, because the rest is being handled.**
