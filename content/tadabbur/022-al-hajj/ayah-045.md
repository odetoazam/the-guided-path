# The Abandoned Well and the Towering Palace

## Surah 22, Ayah 45 — Al-Hajj

> [ayah:22:45] فَكَأَيِّن مِّن قَرْيَةٍ أَهْلَكْنَـٰهَا وَهِىَ ظَالِمَةٌ فَهِىَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا وَبِئْرٍ مُّعَطَّلَةٍ وَقَصْرٍ مَّشِيدٍ
>
> *Fa-kaʾayyin min qaryatin ahlaknāhā wa hiya ẓālimah, fa hiya khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā, wa biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa qaṣrin mashīd.*
>
> "And how many a town have We destroyed while it was committing wrong — so it lies fallen upon its roofs — and how many an abandoned well, and how many a towering palace."

---

## Introduction — The Camera Pan

Stay with me for a moment.

Imagine the surah you've been listening to has been a courtroom — opening with the violent shaking of the Hour, walking through the wombs and the dust and the trumpets, building a single argument: *the world is structured around accountability.*

Now, just before this ayah, Allah has done something unusual. He's listed names. Nuh's people. ʿAd. Thamud. The people of Ibrahim. The people of Lut. Madyan. Musa. Seven civilizations. Each given a messenger. Each given a chance. Each gone.

The list of names ends.

And then — instead of moralizing, instead of explaining — Allah does something a poet would do. He stops naming. He starts *showing*. He pans the camera across the wreckage.

[PAUSE]

Here's the strangeness I want you to feel before we even open the words.

When you describe a destroyed civilization, what do you describe? You describe *people*. The bodies. The screams. The fall of the palace that day.

Allah doesn't.

In this ayah, Allah does not mention a single human being. Not a body. Not a name. Not a survivor. Not a victim.

He mentions a roof. A well. A palace.

That's it.

The absence of human beings in this ayah is not laziness. It is the most precise rhetorical choice. We are walking, in the Quran, through the silence *after* the civilization. We are not witnessing the destruction. We are arriving long after the dust has settled.

We are tourists in our own future.

Hold that. Because by the time we get to the abandoned well, you're going to feel something the people of Mecca felt when this came down — and something we, with our drone footage of empty malls and Soviet hospitals and Detroit factories, are uniquely positioned to feel right now.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *Fa-kaʾayyin*: The Word That Means "How Many" But Doesn't Mean "How Many"

Arabic has a perfectly ordinary word for "how many." It is *kam*. You use it when you ask, "How many books do you have?" *Kam kitāb*. It's neutral. It's countable. It does its job and goes home.

That is **not** the word Allah uses here.

He uses *kaʾayyin*.

Now, you can translate *kaʾayyin* as "how many" — and most translators do. But that's like translating "*innumerable*" as "many." Technically related. Emotionally a different planet.

*Kaʾayyin* is a compound word. It's built from two pieces fused together — the comparative *kāf* (like, as) and *ayyin* (which) — and the fusion creates an exclamation that linguists call **takthīr** — amplification. It is the Arabic of someone throwing up their hands.

Let me give you an everyday version.

If a friend tells you their startup failed, and you say, "How many startups fail?" — you sound like an actuary.

But if you say, "Oh — how *many* startups fail!" — that's the same words, but now there's grief in your voice. Now you're not asking a number. You're naming an avalanche.

*Kaʾayyin* is the second one. It is not a count. It is a sigh.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah opens this ayah with *fa-kaʾayyin min qaryah*, He is not informing us that some quantity of cities was destroyed. He is having us stand in front of a horizon and say: *look at how many.* The word itself has the texture of staring at a graveyard that doesn't end.

And here's what's load-bearing about this choice. The list of named civilizations Allah just gave us — Nuh, ʿAd, Thamud, Lut, Madyan — is finite. Seven names. You can hold seven names in your hand.

But *kaʾayyin* says: *those were the ones we named for you.* The actual number? Past counting.

The seven were the headlines. The graveyard is much, much wider.

What this single word does is shrink the listener. It tells you: you are not standing at the beginning of human civilization wondering what will happen. You are standing late in a long, long story, surrounded by the unnamed dead of cultures whose names no one ever recorded. You are not the first. You are not even the hundred-thousandth.

That alone, before we touch the rest of the ayah, is meant to do something to your sense of your own importance.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā*: The Inversion of a Building

The next phrase is one of the most quietly brutal images in the Quran. Most translators render it "fallen on its roofs," and that sounds odd in English. Roofs go on top. How does a building fall *on* its roofs?

To answer that, we need to slow down on two words.

The first is *khāwiyah*. The root is *kh-w-y*, and it carries a beautifully terrible double meaning. It means **empty** — a hollow stomach, a vacant house, a womb that bore no child. And it also means **collapsed inward** — the way a chest caves when the breath leaves, the way an old building sags into itself, the way a campfire ring of stones sinks after the flames are out.

So *khāwiyah* is not "destroyed." It is not "smashed." It is the specific look of something that has *fallen in on its own emptiness*. The fall is from the inside.

Now hold that, and listen to the sound. *Khāwiyah*. The breathy *kh*, the long open vowel, the whisper at the end. The word itself sounds like an exhale. It sounds like the sigh a building makes when it gives up.

[PAUSE]

The second word is *ʿurūsh*. Plural of *ʿarsh*. The roof, the canopy, the thing held up overhead.

And here is what classical commentators sit with — Ibn Kathir, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī all walk through it. When a building collapses in the way Allah is describing, the sequence is precise: **the roof falls first**. Then the walls, with nothing left to hold up, topple inward — and they come to rest *on top of* the fallen roof.

So when you arrive at the ruin, what you see is a structure inside out. The walls are on top. The roof is at the bottom. The thing that should be on top of you is now under everything.

Allah is describing not the moment of destruction but the **resting position** — the final, frozen geometry of what's left.

A building that has fallen on its roof is a building whose order has been reversed. What protected is now buried. What was framed is now framing nothing. The shape says: *something was here, and the shape it left behind makes no sense.*

[PAUSE]

This is one of those moments where Quranic Arabic refuses to be efficient. Allah doesn't say "the city was destroyed." He gives you the freeze-frame: walls on roof, roof on ground, hollow inside the heap. He makes you walk around the geometry of the silence.

And notice, again, what's missing.

There are no people in this picture.

When a building collapses on inhabitants, that's a tragedy. When a building collapses on no one — when the collapse happens late, *after* everyone is already gone — that's something else. That's the universe quietly adjusting the furniture in a room nobody lives in anymore.

The walls aren't burying people. They're burying *the floor plan*.

### Element Three — *Biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa qaṣrin mashīd*: The Working Well and the Standing Palace

Now we come to the line that, when you sit with it, breaks something.

*Wa biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa qaṣrin mashīd.* "And how many an abandoned well, and how many a towering palace."

Two images. Two structures. They don't look like a pair, but they are. Let me show you the precision.

#### The Well

*Biʾr* is a well. The water source.

The word that follows it is the one I want you to feel: *muʿaṭṭalah*.

This is a passive participle from Form II of the root *ʿ-ṭ-l*. Form II in Arabic — the *faʿʿala* pattern — typically intensifies or actively *causes* the meaning. The base verb means to be free of work, to be unoccupied. Form II — *ʿaṭṭala* — means to actively *render* something inoperative, to suspend it, to put it out of service.

So *muʿaṭṭalah* is not "broken." It is not "destroyed." It is **suspended.** Made inactive. Taken out of service.

This is a critical distinction. Listen to it.

The well is not broken.

The water is still there.

The walls of the well are still standing.

The bucket — if there were anyone left to drop it — would still come up wet.

What's missing is **the hand that draws.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a second. Because this is where the ayah opens up.

Allah is describing a well that *still works*. It is fully functional. The provision is intact. The infrastructure of mercy — the cool water in a desert, the difference between life and death — is sitting right there, waiting.

And it is *muʿaṭṭalah*. Suspended. In service to no one.

Why? Not because the well failed. The well never fails. Wells don't disqualify themselves. **The drinkers disappeared.**

The well is a monument to the fact that what God provided was sufficient. The lack was never on God's side.

Now I want you to feel where this image lives in our world. Because Allah didn't pick "abandoned well" because it was poetic. He picked it because every listener — then and now — has felt this.

A parent dies, and the recipes they would have taught you are still in their head, still teachable, except there's no longer a teacher. The recipes weren't destroyed. The student lost the chance.

A teacher offers you years of guidance, and you don't take it, and one day you realize the teacher has moved on, the door is closed, and the wisdom is still wisdom — it just isn't *yours*.

The Quran sits on a shelf in a Muslim home. The water is still cool. The pages still hold what they always held. *Muʿaṭṭalah* — suspended. Out of service. Not because the words failed. Because nobody opened it.

A grandparent's stories. An ʿālim's lectures. A khutbah you slept through. A dream you wrote down once and never read again.

The well is not the tragedy. **The well being fine is the tragedy.**

What this ayah is saying — quietly, devastatingly — is that the destruction of these civilizations was not the failure of God's provision to them. The provision was always sufficient. They walked away from a well that still had water.

[PAUSE]

#### The Palace

Now the palace.

*Qaṣrin mashīd.* A palace, *mashīd*.

The word *mashīd* comes from the root *sh-y-d*, which means to construct firmly, to plaster with hard mortar, to build *up* — to raise something high and solid. *Mashīd* is the passive participle: a palace that has been firmly built, plastered, raised.

In the lexicons it carries two related senses: **lofty** (raised high) and **solid** (built well, plastered against weather). Both are operative. The palace is tall. The palace is sturdy. The palace is engineered to last.

And it does last.

This is the second devastation in the line, and you have to feel it next to the first.

The well: small, useful, common, *still functional*.

The palace: large, ornate, exceptional, *still standing*.

Both are perfectly fine. Both are completely empty.

[PAUSE]

Allah is showing you the full range of human infrastructure — from the humblest provision (a well in the village square) to the proudest achievement (the king's palace on the hill) — and He is saying: *all of it outlives you.*

The civilization left. The civilization is gone. The wickedness that made *qaryatin… wa hiya ẓālimah* — "the town while it was wronging" — that wickedness ran its course and was answered.

But the things they built? The things they built are still here.

Have you seen Roman aqueducts? Two thousand years old. Still standing. Still carrying water occasionally. The Romans are gone. Their language survives mostly in mass and law school. But the aqueducts are *mashīd*. Solidly built.

Have you seen the abandoned cities of the Soviet collapse — empty hospitals, empty pools, hammers and sickles still mounted on walls of buildings nobody enters? The ideology is dust. The plumbing works.

Have you seen Detroit's empty factories? The Pyramids surrounded by a Cairo that has nothing to do with them? Pompeii, frozen in plaster, walked through by tourists who can read the menus on the walls?

[PAUSE]

The palace stands. The well still has water. **Civilizations become their own eulogies.**

And here is the structural payoff. Hold the two halves of this ayah together.

The first half — *khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā* — shows you the building inverted: walls fallen onto the roof. The geometry of collapse.

The second half — *biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa qaṣrin mashīd* — shows you the building still standing. The geometry of *endurance.*

Both are forms of the same horror. Whether the city is rubble or whether the city is intact, **the people are gone**. The state of the architecture is irrelevant. The point is the absence of the inhabitant.

A heap of rubble can mourn. A standing palace, empty, mourns louder.

### Bridge

So we have walked through three pieces of language.

*Kaʾayyin* — not "how many" but the sigh of a graveyard without end.

*Khāwiyatun ʿalā ʿurūshihā* — the inverted building, the geometry of collapse from inside, the walls resting on what was supposed to protect.

*Biʾrin muʿaṭṭalatin wa qaṣrin mashīd* — the working well no one draws from, the towering palace no one enters. The infrastructure of mercy and the infrastructure of pride. Both intact. Both alone.

We've seen what the words say.

Now let's ask what this is really about. Because the ayah is not actually about cities.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The Civilization You Belong To Is Already a Ruin (You Just Don't Know Yet)

Let me start somewhere you've already been.

Have you ever walked through an abandoned mall?

In the United States, in particular, we now have hundreds of them. Shopping centers built in the 1970s and '80s, anchored by department stores that no longer exist. The roof is mostly intact. The fountains are dry but the marble is still there. The escalators stand frozen mid-flight, like museum pieces, and you can still read the directory: *JCPenney — Wing B. Sears — Wing C.*

You walk through, and you feel something specific. It's not horror. It's not sadness. It is a quieter feeling — a kind of vertigo. Because you remember when this place was full. You remember the smell of pretzels and the noise of the food court. You remember thinking this is what the world looks like.

And now it is *muʿaṭṭalah*. Suspended. The escalators still work, technically. The lights still come on in the half of the wing the security company maintains. The infrastructure is largely intact.

The people simply are not here anymore.

[PAUSE]

The reason this feeling is important is because every confident civilization, while it is being a civilization, *cannot believe it will be that mall*. It is not built into the human imagination to walk through your own streets and see them as future archaeology. The 7-year-old in 1985, eating soft pretzels with his mom, did not see ruins. He saw permanence.

This is what the people of ʿAd saw. This is what the people of Thamud saw. This is what the people of Pharaoh saw, walking past pyramids built by their *grandfathers* and assuming the empire that built such things was eternal.

The pyramids are still there. Pharaoh is dust.

What this ayah is doing — and this is where it stops being history and starts being personal — is teaching you to see your own civilization the way Allah sees it. Not because Allah delights in collapse. But because the fastest way to break the spell of permanence is to walk through what's left when permanence ends.

There's a phrase in classical tafsīr literature that I want to give you. The scholars, when discussing this ayah and its companions, ask the question: *why does Allah keep returning the listener to ruins?* And the answer, across multiple commentators, is the same. Ruins are the only argument that **the present is not exempt.**

If a civilization with more wealth, more technology, more confidence than yours has already become rubble — then the question is not *whether* yours will. The question is only *whether you live as though you know it will.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Look at the next ayah, the one that follows this — *afalam yasīrū fī l-arḍ — fa-takūna lahum qulūbun yaʿqilūna bihā.* "Have they not traveled the earth, so that they might have hearts that reason?"

Allah is connecting two things people don't usually connect. **Travel through ruins. Hearts that work.**

The argument is: a heart that doesn't walk through ruins doesn't think. A heart that has not stood in front of *biʾrin muʿaṭṭalah* and felt the silence has not yet learned the basic fact about its own situation.

So the practical application of this theme is almost embarrassingly simple. Go look at ruins. Real ones. Walk through Pompeii if you can. Walk through an abandoned mall if you can't. Drive past the closed factory in your hometown. Stand in the empty church that's now an apartment building. Read about civilizations that thought they were eternal.

Not as a tourist. As a citizen of the next ruin.

When you stand in front of *qaṣrin mashīd* — a tower someone built so it would last forever — and you realize that it did last forever and the builder still didn't — something snaps in you. The spell breaks. You start to suspect, for the first time, that *you* are also temporary. That the things you build — the career, the reputation, the home, the institution — are all *mashīd*. Solidly built. Designed to last. And like every other tower, you will leave them.

That's not a depressing thought. That's the thought that frees you to live for what *isn't* mashīd. For what isn't a structure at all. For the heart, the deed, the relationship to your Lord — the only things that travel with you when the building empties.

### Theme Two — The Well Doesn't Fail. The Drinker Disappears.

This is the harder theme. Stay with me.

The image of *biʾrin muʿaṭṭalah* — the abandoned well that still has water — is, if you really sit with it, almost unbearable. Because it shifts the entire moral question.

In our default imagination of divine punishment, we think of God as withdrawing. The water dries up. The blessing leaves. The harvest fails. The mercy is lifted away.

That is not what this ayah says.

This ayah says: **the well kept its water.** *The mercy stayed.* What changed was not the supply. What changed was the appetite for the supply. The civilization grew to a point where the water of guidance, the water of revelation, the water of accountability — all of it sitting right there, fully drinkable — was simply *not drawn*.

The well is still doing its job. Nobody is using it.

[PAUSE]

I want to say this very carefully, because it has personal application that I think is important.

The most dangerous spiritual condition is not when the water dries up. It's when the water is fine and you've stopped drinking.

Almost every Muslim listening to this knows the experience. There was a year — maybe a phase — when reciting the Quran wept you. When ṣalāh wasn't long enough. When dhikr was the most natural thing in the world. The well was full. The water was cool. You were drinking.

And then somewhere — without any single decisive moment — the drinking stopped. The Quran is still on the shelf. The masjid is still around the corner. The five times a day still arrive. Nothing has changed about the well. *Muʿaṭṭalah*.

That's the spiritual condition this ayah is naming.

This is why I want you to feel the precision of *muʿaṭṭalah* and not let the translators flatten it to "ruined." Because if the well were ruined, the question would be: how do we rebuild the well? But the well is not ruined. The well is fine. The question is: *why isn't anyone drawing?*

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is something almost frightening. The civilization Allah destroyed in this ayah was not destroyed by a cosmic withdrawal. It was destroyed by a slow human disinvestment from the gifts already given. The water was sufficient. The wickedness was not in the well's failure. The wickedness — *wa hiya ẓālimah* — was in walking past the well.

And here's where the ayah quietly indicts you and me, because we live in a moment of surreal access to the well. We have the Quran in our pocket. We have tafsīr in our bookmarks. We have lectures from every classical school on YouTube. We have translations in fifty languages. We have masājid we don't enter and copies we don't open and surahs we cannot recite and duas we have never made.

The well of revelation in our generation is less *muʿaṭṭalah* and more like a fire hydrant blown open on a summer street. The water is *everywhere*. And our civilization is dying of thirst because we are too distracted to bend down.

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is, I think, the most concrete thing this ayah can give you.

Pick one well that you know, in your life, is functional and unused.

Maybe it's a copy of the Quran you bought during a high moment of īmān and have not opened since. Maybe it's a relationship with a parent or a teacher who is still alive, still willing, still able to teach you, and whom you have not asked anything in years. Maybe it's the dawn prayer that you still believe in, that the alarm still rings for, that nothing prevents — except you.

Identify one well. Don't pick all of them. Pick one.

And tomorrow morning — or the morning after — go draw from it. Not as a project. As an answer to this ayah. Because the worst thing you could take from this reflection is the knowledge that wells become *muʿaṭṭalah* and to do nothing about the one in your own house.

The civilization you belong to is going to leave behind palaces. That's already in motion; you can't stop it. The only question is whether *you* leave behind a well someone is still drawing from. Or whether you leave behind one that nobody used while you were here.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

I want to now show you something that I held back, deliberately, until you had walked through the linguistic and the thematic work. Because the architecture only lands if you've earned it.

This ayah — 22:45 — does not stand alone. It is one half of a pair. The next ayah, 22:46, asks: *afalam yasīrū fī l-arḍ fa-takūna lahum qulūbun yaʿqilūna bihā aw ādhānun yasmaʿūna bihā — fa-innahā lā taʿmā l-abṣāru wa lākin taʿmā l-qulūbu llatī fī ṣ-ṣudūr.* "Have they not traveled the earth, so that they might have hearts to reason with, or ears to hear with? For indeed, it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts inside the chests."

Now look at what Allah has done structurally.

22:45 shows you the **outer** ruin: the fallen city, the abandoned well, the empty palace. The infrastructure of a civilization that walked away.

22:46 shows you the **inner** ruin: the blind heart in the chest of someone walking past the outer ruin and not seeing it.

The outer ruin is meaningless without the inner one. *Many* people walk past abandoned wells. The question is whose heart registers what the well is saying.

The pair is one argument. **The reason civilizations end as ruins is the same reason individuals do — a heart that has gone *muʿaṭṭalah*. Suspended. Functional, but not drawing.**

The well of the body still works. The eyes still see. The ears still hear. But the heart — the actual instrument of meaning — is out of service.

So 22:45 is not a history lesson. It is a mirror. The cities Allah destroyed are an image; what they image is the human heart that was given a well of revelation and would not draw.

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah did not destroy these civilizations by drying up His provision — He let them destroy themselves by walking past a well that still had water, until all that was left of them was infrastructure no one used.

### Questions to Carry

- Which well in my life is *muʿaṭṭalah*? What gift, fully present and fully functional, am I currently not drawing from?
- When I walk past the visible decay in my own civilization — empty churches, abandoned masājid, families that broke apart, neighborhoods that emptied — what does my heart register? Does it travel, in the way Allah commanded the heart to travel, or does it look away?
- If somebody walked through the building of my life two centuries from now, what would they find? A heap of *khāwiyah* — collapsed inward on its emptiness? Or a well that, even after I'm gone, is still cool, still drawn from, still serving thirst I never met?
- What palace am I currently building — what *qaṣrin mashīd* — that I have begun to mistake for permanence? And what would change about today if I remembered it will outlast me, empty, and no one will care that I built it?
- Of the gifts Allah has put in my immediate, no-effort reach — the Quran in the next room, the parent on the other end of the phone, the prayer mat in the corner — which one will I draw from before this week is out?

### The Closing Invitation

The invitation in this ayah is not to mourn dead civilizations.

The invitation is to pick up a bucket.

You are alive in a window — short, narrow, closing — when the wells around you are still working. Allah has not yet declared yours a *qaryatun ẓālimah*. The infrastructure of mercy in your life is, by the standards of any civilization in human history, almost unimaginably abundant.

You don't need a new well. You don't need to wait for a new revelation. You don't need a perfect spiritual director or a more conducive environment.

You need to lower the bucket.

That's all this ayah is asking. Lower the bucket — once, today — into one well you've been walking past. And then, tomorrow, lower it again.

A civilization is built one drawn bucket at a time. It is destroyed one walked-past well at a time.

You are choosing, today, between *biʾrin muʿaṭṭalah* and *biʾrin maʿmūrah* — the well in service.

### A Brief Du'a

Yā Rabb, You have placed wells in our reach we have never bent to. You have given us a Quran we have not opened, hearts You shaped to receive You, and time we have not used. Do not let us be the ones who leave behind a *qaṣrin mashīd* — a tower we built that no one was watered by.

Yā Allah, before our hearts go *muʿaṭṭalah* — before the water remains and the drinking stops — pull us back to the well. Make us a people who drew while drawing was possible.

Yā Lord of every fallen city and every standing one — let our days end with our buckets wet, not our towers tall.

Āmīn.
