# Al-Hajj 22:38 — The Hidden Defender

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُدَٰفِعُ عَنِ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ خَوَّانٍ كَفُورٍ
>
> *Inna-llāha yudāfiʿu ʿani-lladhīna āmanū. Inna-llāha lā yuḥibbu kulla khawwānin kafūr.*
>
> **Indeed, Allah actively defends those who have believed. Indeed, Allah does not love any habitual betrayer, deeply ungrateful.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World This Ayah Walked Into

Imagine, for a moment, what the early Muslim community in Madinah was carrying when this surah descended.

For thirteen years in Makkah, they had been told one thing: **don't strike back.** When stones cut your forehead, walk away. When boycotts starve your children, endure. When your uncle dies under torture, bury him and pray. The Quran in those years was a Quran of *ṣabr* — patience, restraint, the long inner stillness of a people who had no political body to defend them.

Then they arrived in Madinah, and something shifted. Not all at once. But slowly, the air changed. Caravans came hunting them. Tribes plotted with their old enemies. The men who had spent thirteen years pulling their hands back now lay awake at night wondering if the time had finally come to lift them.

And then, in the verses just before our ayah, the heavens opened with something the believers had been waiting their whole lives to hear:

> *Permission [to fight] has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged. And indeed, Allah is competent to give them victory.* (22:39)

Most scholars say this is the very first ayah of permission to defend. The first crack in thirteen years of restraint.

[PAUSE]

But before that permission could be acted upon — before a single sword was drawn — Allah inserted **this ayah**, the one we are about to enter. Right between the trauma and the response. Right between the wound and the action.

He says something you would not expect Him to say to a community about to fight.

He does not say: *Allah will give you weapons.*
He does not say: *Allah will make you strong.*
He does not even say: *Allah will give you victory.*

He says something stranger, and far more profound. He says — *I am already defending you. I have been defending you the whole time. The fight you think is about to begin is a fight I have been engaged in on your behalf, while you were sleeping, while you were praying, while you were grieving, before you ever knew there was something to grieve about.*

### The Hook

There is a verb here — one verb — that is built on a grammatical pattern Arabic uses when two parties are locked in sustained, reciprocal engagement. Not a single rescue. Not a one-time intervention. Something more like an ongoing wrestling match where one of the wrestlers is invisible.

And then, in the second half, the ayah tells you the only thing that can break the wrestler's grip.

It is not your weakness.
It is not your failure.
It is not your sin.

It is something much more specific — and much more chilling. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yudāfiʿu* — The Verb Built for Two

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we open this word.

Most languages have one form of a verb — *to defend.* You either defend or you don't. The grammar is flat.

Arabic is not flat. Arabic verbs come in patterns, and the **pattern** itself carries meaning that goes beyond the root. The base form (Form I) is simple action: *I defended.* But there is a higher form — what grammarians call **Form III**, the *mufāʿala* pattern — which is reserved for actions that involve **two parties engaged with each other over time.** It implies sustained, reciprocal, ongoing engagement.

Here's an everyday example. In Arabic, *qatala* (Form I) means *he killed.* But *qātala* (Form III) means *he engaged in mutual combat with* — the back-and-forth of battle, where blows go in both directions over time. The pattern itself tells you: *this is a contest, not a single act.*

Now hold that in mind, and look at our verb.

The root is **د-ف-ع** *(d-f-ʿ)*, which means *to push, to repel, to thrust away.* The base form *dafaʿa* would mean *He pushed away* — a single act. A door slammed. A blow blocked once.

But Allah does not say *dafaʿa* here. He says **يُدَافِعُ — *yudāfiʿu*** — Form III.

The same pattern as *qātala.*

[PAUSE]

What is Allah saying?

He is saying that His defense of the believer is not a single intervention. It is **an ongoing, sustained, two-sided engagement.** There is something pushing toward the believer. And Allah, like a wrestler locked in a match, is pushing back — continuously, reciprocally, while the believer goes about his day.

Notice something. The classical grammarians point out that *mufāʿala* implies sustained back-and-forth — and the lexicographers note that this pattern can also intensify the action. Either way, the picture is the same: this is not one hand stretched out once. This is **a continuous defensive engagement on your behalf, going on right now, while you read these words.**

Let's sit with what this changes.

You woke up this morning. You drank tea. You answered an email. You felt the small heaviness of a problem in your chest. You did not know — because you could not see — that *yudāfiʿu* had been happening. That something had been moving toward you, and Allah had been moving against it. That a thing was supposed to land in your life today, and it didn't, because of a parry you did not witness.

The believer who reads this ayah and understands its grammar can never again look at his own life the same way. Because the question is no longer *"Why is this hard?"* The question becomes *"What would this be like if* yudāfiʿu *stopped?"*

Here is what is remarkable. The verb is in the **imperfect** (*muḍāriʿ*) tense — which in Arabic does not just mean "present." It means *ongoing, habitual, not-yet-completed.* So it's not "Allah defended" (one time, in the past). It is *Allah defends, is defending, will continue defending* — in one breath, all of it.

This is not a story about a battle. It is a description of a permanent state.

[PAUSE]

One more thing, and then we will move on.

The preposition that follows is **عَن** — *ʿan* — which means *away from* or *on behalf of.* Allah does not just defend. He defends *ʿani-lladhīna āmanū* — "*off* of those who have believed." The image is precise. Something is coming *toward* the believer. Allah's defense moves it *off, away, around.* The believer is the destination the attack never reaches.

You have been protected from things you do not even know existed.

That is the first word.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Asymmetry of "Defends" vs "Loves"

Now look at the structure of the whole ayah. There are two halves, and they are linked by a parallelism that is hiding a strange asymmetry.

First half: *Allah **defends** those who have believed.*
Second half: *Allah **does not love** every habitual betrayer, deeply ungrateful one.*

Stay with me here.

If you were writing this verse, what would you put in the second half? Most of us would write the obvious symmetry: *Allah defends believers; Allah does not defend betrayers.* Or perhaps: *Allah loves believers; Allah does not love betrayers.* That would be balanced. That would be neat.

But Allah does not write either of those.

He says **defends** the believer. And **does not love** the betrayer.

These are not the same axis. Defense is a *protection-axis* word. Love is a *relationship-axis* word. So why mix them?

[PAUSE]

Here is what this invites us to consider. The first half is about what Allah is *doing for* the believer in this specific moment of attack and threat. The believer is under siege. He needs *defense.* Allah does not say "I love you" here — although that is true elsewhere — because that is not the word the wounded heart needs in the middle of a fight. The wounded heart needs to hear: *something is standing between you and what is coming for you.*

But the second half pivots.

Allah does not say "I do not defend the betrayer." He says **I do not love** him. Why?

Because the believer's question, in the moment of being attacked by the treacherous, is not *"Will Allah defend that man?"* — it is *"What is my relationship to Allah, while this man is harming me?"* And the answer is: *Allah loves you, and Allah does not love him.*

The line shifts mid-verse. The first half tells you what Allah is *doing.* The second half tells you what Allah is *feeling.* These are not the same. The first protects you. The second positions you.

There is a tafsir reading — found in the classical commentaries — that adds another layer. They note that Allah did not say "*Allah loves the believers*" in symmetry with "*does not love every khawwān kafūr.*" Some say this is because the love of Allah for the believer is *implied and obvious* — the very fact that He is engaged in *yudāfiʿu* on your behalf is the evidence of His love. You don't sustain a wrestling match for someone you don't love. The love is the verb, not a separate clause.

[PAUSE]

Each tafsir lens here is a different angle on the same architecture: **the asymmetry is intentional, and it is doing pastoral work.** It is meeting the believer where he stands — under siege, looking for shelter — and it is giving him both: protection in the moment, and relational standing for eternity.

That is the second movement.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Khawwān* and *Kafūr* — The Architecture of Disqualification

Now look at the words that close the ayah. Two words, both adjectives, both intensives, both nominated together as the only thing Allah will not love.

**خَوَّان — *khawwān*** — from the root *kh-w-n,* meaning *betrayal, breach of trust, treachery.*
**كَفُور — *kafūr*** — from the root *k-f-r,* meaning *concealment, denial, ingratitude for grace received.*

But notice the **forms** of these words. They are not the regular adjectives.

*Khā'in* would be "a betrayer" — someone who has done an act of betrayal. That's the simple participle.
*Khawwān* is something else entirely. It is a **mubālagha** — an *intensive* form, the *fa''āl* pattern. This pattern is reserved in Arabic for **habitual, professional, characterological** descriptors. *Khawwān* is not someone who betrayed once. It is someone who has become **a betrayer by trade, by habit, by character.**

Same with *kafūr.* The simple adjective *kāfir* means a denier. *Kafūr* is the *faʿūl* intensive — *deeply, repeatedly, characteristically ungrateful.* Someone for whom denial of blessing is the texture of their inner life.

[PAUSE]

So Allah is not saying *Allah does not love anyone who betrayed once.* That would be terrifying — that would disqualify almost every human being who ever lived, because we have all betrayed something or someone at some point.

He is saying something far more specific: **Allah does not love the one who has made betrayal a profession of the soul. The one for whom ingratitude has become a way of being.**

This is enormously merciful. The line is not crossed by an act. The line is crossed by a *character.*

There is room — vast, oceanic room — for the slipping believer, the falling believer, the believer who betrayed a trust last week and is full of shame this morning. That believer is not *khawwān.* That believer has done *khiyāna,* an act, but has not become *kha-w-w-ā-n,* the doubled letter that signals a settled identity.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the pairing. *Khawwān kafūr.* Why these two together?

Stay with me. Treachery and ingratitude are spiritual cousins. Treachery breaks horizontal trust — between you and other people, between you and obligations, between you and contracts. Ingratitude breaks **vertical trust** — between you and Allah, between you and the gifts you have received.

The one who has made both a habit — who has trained himself to break covenant *with people* and to deny grace *from his Lord* — has constructed an inner world where there is nothing left to defend. The defense Allah extends to the believer flows through the channels of trust and gratitude. Block both channels, and the defense has no path in.

This is not Allah refusing to defend. This is the soul having sealed off the ports through which defense enters.

[PAUSE]

And now hear how it is sonically written. *Khawwān-in kafūr-in.* Both end in the *tanwīn* — the indefinite nunation. *Some habitual-betrayer, some deep-ingrate.* Indefinite. Not a name. Not a person. **A type.**

You can become this type. Anyone can. The ayah does not name names. It names a shape of the soul. And it warns: this shape is the only shape Allah's love does not enter.

That is the third word.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridging from Words to World

We have looked at three things: the verb that says *Allah is defending you right now in a fight you cannot see;* the asymmetry that says *He defends you, and does not love what attacks you;* and the intensives that say *the only thing that disqualifies you is not sin but settled character.*

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Battles You Do Not See

Let me start here, far from the ayah, in a place you already live.

You have had days that should have gone wrong, and didn't. A near-miss on the highway you didn't even notice was a near-miss until you got home. A conversation that almost ruined a friendship, but somehow didn't, and you can't quite explain why. A diagnosis that was almost given, but the test came back clean for reasons no one on earth could fully articulate.

Most of your protection in this life is invisible to you.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most sobering and beautiful realities of being a creature. You are not the protagonist of a movie where the camera shows you every threat in slow motion. You are a small soul moving through a world full of forces you cannot see, cannot count, cannot understand. And the **vast majority** of what would have ended you, has been parried for you.

When the ayah says *Allah yudāfiʿu* — Allah is *engaged in defensive combat on your behalf* — it is naming a reality that was already true. The grammar didn't make it true. The grammar **revealed** what was already true.

And here is what shifts. When you understand this, your relationship to gratitude changes shape.

We tend to thank Allah for the things we *received* — the meal, the home, the relationship, the success. But the believer who sits with *yudāfiʿu* learns to thank Allah for **the things that did not happen.** For the harm that didn't reach. For the betrayal that didn't land. For the door that closed before you walked through it. For the person you didn't end up with. For the job you didn't get.

Most of what Allah does for you, you will never know about. *Yudāfiʿu* is happening, and you are largely unaware. This is not a defect. It is a mercy. To know every threat that has ever been moving toward you would crush you. So Allah parries, and lets you walk through life thinking it was a calm day.

[PAUSE]

Now hear how this connects to the surrounding ayahs in the surah.

The believers were granted permission to fight in 22:39. They were told *Allah is competent to give them victory.* But before they could even mobilize, Allah said **wait — before you lift your arms, understand that the war you think is starting has already been underway, and I have been the one fighting it.**

The point of permission to fight was never that the believer was finally going to defend himself. The point was that the believer was being invited into **a defense that was already being conducted on his behalf, by Someone else.** His role was not to be the protector. His role was to be the *trustworthy ally* of the protector. To do his small portion. To stand where he was placed. To not become khawwān or kafūr.

The fight is not your fight in the way you thought it was.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

When you wake up tomorrow and feel the weight of what is moving against you — at work, in your family, in your own soul — pause before you mobilize. Recognize that the engagement is already happening. *Yudāfiʿu* is in the imperfect tense; it does not require your activation. Your job is not to win the war. Your job is to *not betray the One who is winning it for you.*

And here is the practical application: build the habit of thanking Allah for the unseen parries. End your day not just with *thank you for the meal* but with *thank you for the harms I never knew were aimed at me. Thank you for the version of today that did not happen.* Train your tongue to praise the invisible defense. Because it is the bigger half of your life.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: What Actually Disqualifies You

Now let me ask the harder question. The one most of us are afraid to ask.

*If yudāfiʿu is real — if Allah is actively defending those who believe — then what would it take to lose that defense?*

Most of us, when we are honest, fear that our sins will revoke it. That every slip we take, every fall, every moment of weakness chips away at the protection. We build a quiet theology in our chests where Allah's defense is conditional on our perfection — and since we are not perfect, we live always slightly braced, always slightly afraid that the parry has stopped and we just don't know it yet.

The ayah is here to dismantle that theology.

[PAUSE]

Allah does not say *Allah does not love the sinner.* He does not say *Allah does not love the one who fails.* He does not say *Allah does not love the one who falls and rises and falls again.*

He says **Allah does not love every khawwān kafūr** — every **habitual** betrayer, every **deeply settled** ingrate.

The grammar is the mercy.

The intensives are the boundary, but they are also the breathing room. *Khawwān* is not a verb you commit. It is a **noun you become.** A character that has hardened. A pattern that has been chosen, repeated, and integrated into the architecture of your soul.

You do not become khawwān by failing. You become khawwān by *making peace with failure as identity.* By saying, in the quiet of your heart: *this is who I am now. I betray. I deceive. This is my mode.*

That is the line. And it is much, much further out than most of us fear.

[PAUSE]

Imam al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī — one of the great classical lexicographers — defined *khiyāna* not just as breaking a contract, but as **the violation of trust where trust was due.** It is a relational sin, not just a behavioral one. It is what happens when someone who was supposed to be on your side, became against you while pretending to remain on your side.

That is what Allah does not love.

The believer who falls into sin and weeps for it has not become *khawwān.* The very weeping is the evidence that the trust is intact. The believer who feels the weight of his ingratitude and says *yā Rabb, forgive me, I have not lived up to what You have given me* has not become *kafūr.* The very weight is the evidence that the gratitude is alive.

*Khawwān* is the soul that has stopped weeping for its betrayals. *Kafūr* is the soul that has stopped feeling the weight of its ingratitude. These are not events. These are settlements.

[PAUSE]

And here is the connection back to the first half of the ayah.

*Yudāfiʿu* — the defense — flows on the channel of *trust* between Allah and the soul. The believer is in a covenant with Allah. *I am yours. You are my Lord. I am trying. I am imperfect, but I am Yours.* That covenant is what the defense is being conducted on behalf of.

When you become *khawwān*, you have not just sinned — you have **withdrawn from the covenant.** You have said, *I am no longer in trust-relationship with my Lord. I am operating on my own.* And so the defense, which flowed through the channel of covenant, no longer has a recipient.

It is not that Allah pulls His defense away. It is that you walked out of the territory that was being defended.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes the meaning of **return.** *Tawba* — repentance — is not earning back Allah's favor. It is *re-entering the territory of His defense.* Walking back into the covenant. Saying, with whatever broken voice you have left: *I am still Yours. I betrayed, but I am not a betrayer. I forgot, but I am not forgetful.*

The ayah is telling you: the door back in is open, **as long as you have not made the betrayal your home.** The intensive forms are warnings, but they are also reassurances. *You are not yet what you fear you have become.* The very fact that the question troubles you is proof that you are not the one Allah does not love.

So tomorrow, when you sin and the whisper comes — *you are no longer worth defending* — you will hear in your chest the answer the grammar gives you:

*Khawwān is not the one who fell. Khawwān is the one who built a house in the falling.*

You have not built a house there. You are still in transit. The defense is still running.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

The verb *yudāfiʿu* is in **Form III** — the form Arabic reserves for sustained, reciprocal, ongoing engagement. Allah is not a switch that flips on when you call. He is a defender whose engagement on your behalf has the texture of a continuous wrestling match with what is moving against you.

The ayah's two halves are **structurally asymmetric** — *defends* the believer, *does not love* the betrayer. Defense is what the wounded heart needs in the moment; love is what positions the soul for eternity. Allah gives you both, in two clauses, calibrated exactly to where you stand.

And the disqualification is not what you feared. It is not your falls. It is not your failures. It is *settled character* — the *fa''āl* and *faʿūl* intensives that name not what you did, but who you have become. The line is much further out than the whispering voice in your chest told you it was.

These are the same insight from two directions. The first says: **the defense is real, ongoing, and runs on the channel of covenant.** The second says: **only the abandonment of the covenant — not the failures within it — closes the channel.**

You are inside an active defense. Stay inside it. That is most of what is being asked.

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my day today was a parry I did not witness — and have I thanked Allah for the harms that did not reach me?

2. Where in my soul am I confusing *fell into sin* with *became sin*? Have I been treating myself as *khawwān* when I am only *khā'in fī laḥẓa* — a betrayer in a moment, not in identity?

3. What is the specific attack I am bracing for — and have I let myself feel the relief of knowing I am not the one defending against it?

4. Whom have I been pretending to be on the side of, while quietly working against? Where is the *khiyāna* I haven't yet acknowledged in myself?

5. If Allah is engaged in *yudāfiʿu* on my behalf right now, what is my one job — and am I doing it?

### One Sentence

The believer's safety is not the absence of attack but the active presence of a divine defender — a defense forfeited only by the soul that has settled into habitual betrayal and characterological ingratitude.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not the protagonist of your own protection. You never were. The fight you wake up to was already underway when you opened your eyes, and the One conducting the defense is more competent than you ever imagined and more committed to you than you ever dared believe.

Your invitation is small, and it is enormous. **Stay in the covenant.** Fall, if you must — but rise. Sin, if you slip — but weep. Forget, if you do — but remember. Do not build a house in your worst moments. Do not let the betrayal harden into character. Do not let the ingratitude settle into identity. The defense will continue running as long as you stay on the territory. The territory is the trust. The trust is the covenant. The covenant is — *yā Rabb, I am Yours, even when I am bad at being Yours.*

That sentence is enough. It keeps you on the field where *yudāfiʿu* still finds you.

### Du'a

*Yā Allāh, You have been defending me from things I do not know to fear. Make me a soul whose covenant is intact, even when my actions are not. Keep me from the hardening — from becoming khawwān, from becoming kafūr — and when I fall, return me before the falling becomes home. Let the parries of Your defense continue to find me on the territory of trust. Āmīn.*
