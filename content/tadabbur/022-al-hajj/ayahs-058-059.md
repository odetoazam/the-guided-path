# The Promise to the Migrant Who Did Not Finish

## Surah Al-Hajj 22:58–59

> [ayah:22:58] وَٱلَّذِينَ هَاجَرُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ثُمَّ قُتِلُوٓا۟ أَوْ مَاتُوا۟ لَيَرْزُقَنَّهُمُ ٱللَّهُ رِزْقًا حَسَنًا ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَهُوَ خَيْرُ ٱلرَّٰزِقِينَ
>
> [ayah:22:59] لَيُدْخِلَنَّهُم مُّدْخَلًا يَرْضَوْنَهُۥ ۗ وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ

*Wa-lladhīna hājarū fī sabīli-llāhi thumma qutilū aw mātū la-yarzuqannahumu-llāhu rizqan ḥasanan, wa-inna-llāha la-huwa khayru-r-rāziqīn. La-yudkhilannahum mudkhalan yarḍawnahu, wa-inna-llāha la-ʿalīmun ḥalīm.*

> "And those who migrated in the way of Allah, then were killed or died — Allah will surely provide for them a beautiful provision. And indeed Allah — He is the best of providers. He will surely enter them into an entrance with which they will be pleased. And indeed Allah is Knowing, Forbearing."

## INTRODUCTION: The Migrant Who Died in Bed

Picture Medina, sometime around year three or four of the Hijrah.

A man left Mecca with everything he owned on a camel. He left behind family, an inherited home, a profession, a name his ancestors had built over generations. He arrived in Medina poor — shockingly poor, by his old standards. He moved into the home of an Anṣārī brother. He learned new trades. He buried friends at Badr. He kept showing up. He kept hoping.

And then he caught a fever. Or his heart simply gave out. Or old age took him. He died in his bed, under a borrowed roof, in a city that was not his.

His widow buried him. The community prayed over him. And then — there is a question hanging in the air of that grieving room. **Did he get what he came for?**

The martyrs who fell at Badr — they were promised paradise in language that had been flowing through the surahs for years. *Do not consider those killed in the way of Allah dead. Rather they are alive with their Lord.* That language is for them. **But this man? This man who left Mecca for Allah and then just… died?**

What does Allah say to that grieving widow?

[PAUSE]

This is the moment our two ayahs answer.

Surah al-Hajj has spent its long passages doing demolition work — exposing the emptiness of idols, the absurdity of clinging to inherited lies, the spiritual disease that makes a heart unable to see what eyes can see. By the time we reach 22:58, the surah turns. It begins to rebuild. And the first thing it rebuilds is the dignity of the migrant whose journey did not end the way the heroic stories ended.

In two short ayahs, Allah will use one of the most emphatic grammatical constructions Arabic possesses — He will use it twice. He will pair the word "killed" with "or died" in a way that flattens the distinction between them. He will leave one word indefinite when we expect Him to specify it. He will name the gates of paradise after the migrants' pleasure, not His own. And He will close with two of His names that almost no one expects in a verse about reward — but which, when you sit with them, are the only two names that could possibly belong here.

**Here is the hook.** When you and I imagine paradise, we imagine a place that pleases Allah, and we hope to fit ourselves into it. This ayah suggests something quietly staggering: paradise is not built around what pleases Allah of us. It is built around **what pleases us** — by an Allah who knows what we never told Him, and who is forbearing with everything we never finished.

Let's see how the words actually do that.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "Hājarū" — A Form That Means Leaving and Becoming

Before we open the word, we need to teach a piece of Arabic.

Arabic verbs come in patterns called *forms*. Form I is the base form — the simple action. *Kataba* — he wrote. *Qatala* — he killed. Forms II through X modify the meaning in patterned ways: Form II intensifies, Form IV makes causative, Form V makes reflexive, and so on.

Form III is special. Form III takes a verb and makes it **mutual** — an action that requires another party. *Qātala* (Form III of *qatala*) doesn't just mean "he killed" — it means "he fought," because fighting requires two combatants. *Kātaba* (Form III of *kataba*) means "he corresponded" — writing back and forth, requiring another writer. The grammatical name for this pattern is *mufāʿalah* — the form of mutual exchange.

Now look at the verb Allah uses for the migrants here:

**هَاجَرُوا — *hājarū***.

This is Form III, from the root *h-j-r*. The Form I verb *hajara* means simply "he left." But Allah does not say *taraka* (he abandoned), and He does not even use the simple Form I *hajara*. He uses Form III — *hājarū*.

What does it mean to migrate in a Form III sense?

Listen to this carefully. In Form III, the migrant is not just leaving one place. He is **engaging in mutual separation** — leaving one home *while bonding to another*. The form itself encodes that hijrah is not subtraction; it is exchange. You are not just walking out of Mecca. You are walking into Medina. You are not just losing one thing. You are entering a new relationship — with a new city, a new community, a new identity that will be forged in the crucible of arrival.

This is why the muhājir is not called the *tārik* (the one who left) but the *muhājir* — the one engaged in the bilateral movement of hijrah. **Hijrah is a relationship, not a departure.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Why does this matter for our verse?

Because the very next thing Allah will say is *thumma qutilū aw mātū* — "then they were killed or they died." And the question hanging over the verse is: did the hijrah count if it was interrupted? Did the relationship begin?

The Form III answers that. *Hājarū* means the relationship had already begun the moment they left. The bond with the new home was already forming as their feet crossed the city limits of Mecca. They didn't need to arrive at some imagined destination called "completed migration" before the relationship was real. The migration *was* the relationship.

So when death comes — whether the violent death of the battlefield or the quiet death of the sickbed — it does not interrupt anything. It marks the moment when the migrant's relationship with this dunya ends and the next begins. **The hijrah was already complete the moment it began, because hijrah was never about arrival. It was about the bond of leaving for Allah.**

That is what Form III is doing here. One form. One word. And the entire anxiety of "I started but I didn't finish" dissolves.

### Element Two: "Thumma Qutilū aw Mātū" — The Particle of Delay and the Equalizing "Or"

Now we come to the most quietly stunning construction in the verse.

Arabic has several connecting particles. *Wa* means "and" — simple addition. *Fa* means "and then" — immediate sequence, with no gap. **Thumma** means "then, after a delay." It implies time has passed. It is the particle of waiting.

Allah does not say *hājarū fa-qutilū* — "they migrated and were immediately killed." He says *hājarū **thumma** qutilū aw mātū* — "they migrated, **and then, after time passed,** they were killed or died."

This particle, *thumma*, opens the verse to every kind of muhājir. The one who died at Badr a year after arriving. The one who died at Uḥud two years later. The one who lived in Medina for ten years and finally died of old age. The one who fell ill on the road and died before he reached the city. *Thumma* is wide. It holds them all.

But the real wonder is what comes after. **Qutilū aw mātū** — "were killed or died."

Now notice something. Listen carefully.

In a culture that valorized the warrior's death, in a community that had just been told *do not consider those killed in the way of Allah dead* (Aal ʿImrān 3:169), in a moment when martyrdom carried unmatched prestige — Allah pairs *qutilū* (were killed) with *mātū* (died). And He joins them with *aw*.

*Aw* in Arabic is the particle of equivalence in some constructions and disjunction in others. Here, it is functioning as **the equalizer**. Not "they were killed *or, alternatively, perhaps,* they merely died." Not "they were killed, and if not, then they died." It is: **killed or died — same outcome, same promise, same provision.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The Arabs of that era would have heard the asymmetry that *should* have followed. They would have expected something like: "those who migrated and were killed get *this*; those who migrated and died get *that, but lesser*." The ranking of deaths was something they understood deeply.

Allah refuses the ranking.

**The one who took an arrow at Badr and the one who caught a fever in Medina — Allah names them with one breath, joins them with one particle, and promises them one provision.**

This is what Form III's "the relationship was already complete" cashes out to. If hijrah was the bond, then the manner of dying is no longer the qualifying event. The qualifying event was the leaving for Allah. Death — however it came — is just the door at the end of the hallway. Some doors open with a sword stroke. Some open with a slow breath. Allah does not weigh which door you walked through.

He weighs **why you walked the hallway in the first place.**

### Element Three: The Triple-Stacked Emphasis and the One Word Left Open

Now we come to what Arabic grammarians call *taʾkīd* — emphasis. Arabic has many ways to emphasize a statement. Allah deploys several of them at once here, stacking them on top of each other in a way that is almost grammatically excessive — except that nothing in revelation is excessive.

Watch:

**لَيَرْزُقَنَّهُمُ ٱللَّهُ رِزْقًا حَسَنًا ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَهُوَ خَيْرُ ٱلرَّٰزِقِينَ**

Layer one: the **lām of emphasis** (لَ) prefixed to the verb — "verily, surely."

Layer two: the **nūn of emphasis** (ـنَّ) suffixed to the verb — a doubled, emphatic *n* sound that drives the verbal action like a hammer.

Together, *la-yarzuqanna* means: "He will surely, absolutely, beyond any doubt, provide." The doubled emphasis cannot be intensified further within the verb itself.

Layer three: **rizqan ḥasanan** — a *cognate accusative*. This is when Arabic repeats the root of the verb as its object. *He provided a providing.* English can't quite render the music of it. It is like saying in English, "He will gift them a gifting" — the noun and verb share a root, and the repetition itself is emphatic. The provision is so real, the verb has to swallow itself.

Layer four: a new sentence introduced by **inna** (إنّ) — "indeed."

Layer five: **la-huwa** — the lām of emphasis returns, this time on the pronoun. "Verily, He."

Layer six: **khayru-r-rāziqīn** — "the best of all providers." Superlative comparison, comparing Him to all other providers and ranking Him above them.

Six layers of emphasis on a single promise: **He WILL provide. He WILL provide a providing. And indeed He, He Himself, is the best of all who provide.**

[PAUSE]

Why so much emphasis? What is being insisted against?

It is being insisted against the **doubt of the bereaved.** The widow looking at her dead husband and thinking, "He left everything. He died with nothing. Was it for nothing?" The community burying a brother who never reached the heroic ending and asking quietly, "Did Allah see him?" Allah is not just answering. He is **drowning the doubt** — burying it under so many layers of grammatical emphasis that there is no air left for it to breathe.

But notice what happens at the end of the construction. After all that emphasis, Allah comes to the noun — *rizqan ḥasanan* — "a beautiful provision."

**Indefinite.**

Not "the beautiful provision" (*al-rizq al-ḥasan*). Not "a specific reward you can imagine and quantify." **A beautiful provision.** Open. Unmeasured. Whatever beauty means to the one who left — Allah will give them that.

And here is what is exquisite. When Arabic leaves a noun indefinite in a context this charged, it functions as what classical rhetoric calls *taʿẓīm* — magnification through indefiniteness. As if to say: "a provision — but a provision so beautiful you cannot define it." The indefiniteness becomes a hand gesture toward something too vast to name.

So the construction goes like this: He stacks six layers of emphasis on the certainty that He will provide — and then, when He arrives at the provision itself, He leaves it open. **Certainty about the giver. Spaciousness about the gift.**

Here is what this invites us to consider: every other dimension of the promise is locked down with grammatical iron. But the gift itself is left to the imagination, because the imagination of the bereaved is too small to contain it. He cannot tell you what it is, because what it is exceeds what you can hold.

### Bridge

We have looked at three things: a verb form that says hijrah is a bond not a destination; a particle and a conjunction that flatten the death of the warrior and the death of the sleeper into one promise; and a six-layered emphasis that dissolves doubt while leaving the gift indefinite.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of how Allah meets the human soul that tried.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Sanctified Incomplete Journey

Almost everyone you know is living an incomplete journey.

The student who started a degree but had to drop out when a parent got sick. The convert who entered Islam at sixty-two and feels they will never make up the years they lost. The mother who promised herself she would memorize the Qur'an by forty and is now forty-five with three juzʾ under her belt. The young man who left his hometown to study in another country and ended up working at a job that has nothing to do with what he came for. The woman who began to wear hijab and found it harder than she expected, who is still working her way back. The father who started reading bedtime ayahs to his children and slowly let it slip. The seeker who began praying tahajjud and slowly stopped.

All of us, all the time, are starting things and not finishing them. Often through no fault of our own. Sometimes through our own weakness. And we carry, deep underneath the surface of our days, **the quiet conviction that what counts in the eyes of Allah is what we completed.**

This is one of the most subtle disorderings of the religious heart. We treat completion as the proof. We assume that if we did not finish, Allah did not count it.

**Surah al-Hajj 22:58 reaches into that exact wound.**

Look at who Allah is praising here. He is not praising the warriors of Badr — they have their own ayahs elsewhere. He is praising those who *migrated* and then *died.* The people who set off toward something for Allah and did not arrive at the heroic ending. Their journey was, by every measurable standard, **incomplete.**

And Allah does the unthinkable. He flattens the difference. *Killed or died.* He does not say: the warriors get a beautiful provision and the rest get a lesser one. He says they all get *rizqan ḥasanan* — a single, indefinite, vast provision.

Why?

Because in Allah's economy, **the act being measured is not the completion. It is the leaving.** The moment your foot left the door for His sake, you were already inside the gate of the reward. What happened next — whether you arrived at the heroic story or fell asleep on the way — was simply the form your ending took. Not the substance of what you did.

This reframes everything.

The mother who began to memorize the Qur'an and got to three juzʾ — Allah is not weighing the eighty-seven juzʾ she did not finish. He is weighing the moment she sat down with the muṣḥaf for the first time and said, "Bismillāh." The convert at sixty-two — Allah is not adding up the years he was not Muslim. He is weighing the moment he said the *shahādah* with a heart that meant it. The father who slowly let bedtime ayahs slip — Allah is not auditing the nights he failed to read. He is preserving the nights he did, and waiting patiently for him to begin again.

[PAUSE]

There is something important to add here. This is not a license for sloth. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the most beloved deeds are those done consistently, even if small. Allah's mercy toward the incomplete journey is not a permission to refuse the journey. It is a comfort for the journey you took as far as you were able to take it.

But for the heart that is genuinely trying — that is starting, and falling, and starting again — this ayah is **a load taken off the chest.** You do not have to die a martyr to die a Muhājir. You just have to be the one who left. Allah will count the leaving. He counts it now. He will count it on the day He provides for you.

What changes about how you live tomorrow? **Begin things you are afraid you will not finish.** The fear of incompletion has kept many of us from starting at all. *La-yarzuqanna* — He will surely provide — does not require you to complete. It requires you to leave.

### Theme Two: An Entrance That Pleases You

Now we come to the second ayah — and the quietly extraordinary phrase at its heart.

**لَيُدْخِلَنَّهُم مُّدْخَلًا يَرْضَوْنَهُۥ**

"He will surely enter them into an entrance — *they* will be pleased with it."

Let me ask you something. When you imagine paradise, who is pleased?

Most of us, if we are honest, imagine paradise as a place that **pleases Allah** — and we hope to be admitted into it because it pleases Him. We imagine ourselves fitting into a divinely-pleasing aesthetic. We picture Allah's palace, Allah's garden, Allah's design, and we hope to be welcomed inside.

Now look at the pronoun in this ayah. *Yarḍawnahu* — "they will be pleased with it." The pleasure is **theirs**, not His. The pronoun is not on Allah. The pronoun is on the migrants.

This is staggering when you sit with it.

[PAUSE]

Allah does not say, "He will enter them into an entrance that pleases Him." He says, "He will enter them into an entrance that pleases **them**." The architecture of paradise is **shaped to the soul of the one who enters it.**

Here is an analogy that might help — though no analogy will be sufficient.

Imagine you have been working for a great king for many years. You served Him faithfully. You sacrificed for Him. The day comes when He calls you to His palace to give you your reward. There are two possible scenarios.

Scenario one: He hands you a generic gift — beautiful, expensive, the same gift He gives to everyone. You receive it gratefully. You appreciate it. But you also notice that it does not quite fit you. It is a beautiful thing for someone, but you are someone in particular, and the gift was made for someone in general.

Scenario two: He opens a door, and behind that door is a place built **for you specifically**. The colors are colors that have always moved you. The light comes in at an angle that has always made you breathe more easily. The room contains the books, the smells, the textures, the views, the company that, had He asked you, you would have asked for. He did not ask you. He knew. He built it because He knew.

Which gift makes you feel known?

The second one. Because the second one is not just generous. It is **personal.**

The verse says: paradise is the second one.

Not a generic palace into which you are admitted. **An entrance shaped to your pleasure.** A paradise made *for you* — your specific you, your particular ache, your singular thirsts, the home you would have built if you had had the power to build it.

This is what *yarḍawnahu* means.

Now hold this against the closing names:

**وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ**

"And indeed Allah is **Knowing, Forbearing.**"

These two names. Why these?

Almost every other verse about paradise closes with names like *Ghafūr Raḥīm* (Forgiving, Merciful) or *ʿAzīz Ḥakīm* (Mighty, Wise). But here — for these specific people — it is *ʿAlīm Ḥalīm.*

*ʿAlīm* — the Knower. **He knows.** He knows what you wanted but never said. He knows what you would have asked for if you had thought to ask. He knows the small private things that bring your soul home — the smell of a certain rain, the sound of a certain voice, the angle of a certain light. He knows you better than you know you. And so when He builds the entrance you will be pleased with, He builds it from the data of who you actually are, not who you tried to be in public.

*Ḥalīm* — the Forbearing. **He is patient.** He is patient with the gap between what you tried to do and what you finished. He is patient with the fact that you took a fever in Medina before you could fight at Uḥud. He is patient with the years it took you to migrate at all. He is patient with all the moments you should have done more and didn't. *Ḥilm* in Arabic is not just patience — it is the dignity of holding back judgment when judgment was warranted, choosing instead to wait, to forbear, to give time.

Put them together.

**ʿAlīm Ḥalīm** — He knows everything about you that no one else knew, *and* He is patient with everything about you that you were ashamed of. So when He builds your *mudkhal yarḍawnahu* — your entrance you will be pleased with — He builds it knowing the real you, and forbearing with the imperfect you, and the entrance is shaped by both. **Known, and forborne, and welcomed.**

This is why the closing names are not Ghafūr Raḥīm. *Forgiveness* is the name of erasure — the wrong is wiped away. *ʿAlīm Ḥalīm* is the name of **integration** — the imperfect self is not erased, it is held, and the paradise is built around the held person.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes the question that organizes your worship. Most of us ask, "Am I pleasing Allah?" That is a good and necessary question. But this ayah reveals a question Allah is also asking on your behalf, beneath the surface of your striving: **"What would please you?"** Not what you think should please you. Not what the pious aesthetic of your community says should please you. **The real you. Your particular soul. The unfashioned hunger inside you that no one else has named.**

Allah is keeping a record. He is paying attention to what makes you light up. He is paying attention to the silent loves you never spoke aloud. And He is going to use them as the blueprint of the place He will enter you into.

This is the most personal promise in the verse. Paradise is not a one-size-fits-all reward. It is a tailored homecoming.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at what these two ayahs do together.

Ayah 58 ends with **خَيْرُ ٱلرَّٰزِقِينَ** — *the best of providers.*
Ayah 59 ends with **عَلِيمٌ حَلِيمٌ** — *Knowing, Forbearing.*

The first closing name addresses a public anxiety: "Will I be provided for? Will my needs be met? Did my hijrah pay out?" Answer: He is the best of providers. Of all who could have provided for you, He is the one who provides best.

The second closing name addresses a private anxiety: "Does He know me? Does He know what I really wanted? Will He hold my failures against me?" Answer: He knows everything you never said. And He is forbearing with everything you wished you had done differently.

Public provision and private knowledge. The two anxieties of every soul that ever left home for Allah and feared they had not done enough.

And underneath both: the verb *hājarū*, in Form III, telling us that **the bond was complete the moment the leaving began.** You do not have to finish to belong. You only have to leave for the One worth leaving for.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

**From the linguistic side:** A Form III verb of mutual bonding, a particle of delay that opens the door wide, a conjunction that equalizes martyr and sleeper, six layers of emphasis on the giver and an indefinite gift, a pronoun on the migrants' pleasure rather than Allah's, and two closing names that integrate the imperfect.

**From the thematic side:** Allah counts the leaving, not the arriving. Allah builds paradise around your specific soul. Allah meets your incomplete journey not with audit but with provision and personalized welcome.

Two directions. One promise. **Whoever leaves home for Allah — however that leaving ended — will be received as someone known, with a provision they cannot picture, into an entrance shaped by their own pleasure.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What journey have I started for Allah and quietly stopped grieving over because I did not finish it? What if the leaving itself was already counted?

2. If Allah is *ʿAlīm* — knowing what I never said — what is the unsaid hunger in me that He already knows about? What would my paradise be built from, if He built it from the real me?

3. When I imagine standing before Allah, am I imagining a generic admission, or an entrance built for me? Which version reflects the *tawḥīd* of His knowledge?

4. *Ḥilm* — His forbearance — is not the absence of seeing my failures. It is the dignified holding of them without indictment. What in my life am I most afraid He has indicted me for? What would change if I knew He was forbearing with that, not erasing it?

5. The widow burying her husband in Medina — is there a version of her grief living inside someone I love, or inside me? What does this ayah say to that grief?

### One-Sentence Distillation

For the one who left home for Allah and did not finish, He will surely provide a beauty too vast to name and welcome them into an entrance shaped by their own pleasure — known by the Knower and forborne by the Forbearing.

### The Closing Invitation

Begin the journey you have been afraid to begin because you fear you will not finish.

The fear of incompletion is not a sign of humility. It is a sign that we have misread the economy of the One we are migrating toward. He counts the leaving. The arriving belongs to Him.

And if you are already on a journey that has not gone the way you hoped — if your hijrah has not produced the heroic ending you envisioned — *thumma qutilū aw mātū* is for you. Allah does not weigh the form of your ending. He weighs the bond of your beginning. He has been counting since the moment your foot crossed the threshold for His sake.

Whatever happens next — whether you fall in some dramatic battle, or quietly fade away in a borrowed bed in a city that is not yours — He has already prepared the entrance. He knows what would please you. He is patient with everything you wished you had done differently.

You will walk through a door He built for you specifically. And you will be pleased.

### A Du'ā

O Allah, You who knew me before I left and will know me when I arrive — count my leaving, even when it does not end the way I hoped. Build me an entrance my soul will recognize as home. And meet the parts of me I am most ashamed of with Your *ḥilm*, the way You met the muhājirūn who died in their beds with the same provision You gave the martyrs of Badr. You are *Khayru-r-Rāziqīn*. You are *ʿAlīm, Ḥalīm*. *Āmīn.*
