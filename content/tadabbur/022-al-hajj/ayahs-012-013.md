# When Harm Arrives Before Benefit
## Surah Al-Hajj 22:12–13 — The Two Stages of False Worship

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

> يَدْعُو مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ مَا لَا يَضُرُّهُۥ وَمَا لَا يَنفَعُهُۥ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلضَّلَـٰلُ ٱلْبَعِيدُ
>
> *Yadʿū min dūni-llāhi mā lā yaḍurruhu wa-mā lā yanfaʿuh. Dhālika huwa-ḍ-ḍalālu-l-baʿīd.*
>
> **He calls, instead of Allah, upon what cannot harm him and cannot benefit him. That — that is the far-gone misguidance.**

> يَدْعُوا۟ لَمَن ضَرُّهُۥٓ أَقْرَبُ مِن نَّفْعِهِۦ ۚ لَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَوْلَىٰ وَلَبِئْسَ ٱلْعَشِيرُ
>
> *Yadʿū la-man ḍarruhu aqrabu min nafʿih. La-biʾsa-l-mawlā wa-la-biʾsa-l-ʿashīr.*
>
> **He calls upon one whose harm is nearer than his benefit. How wretched the protector, and how wretched the companion.**

---

### Rebuilding the World

To hear what these two ayahs are doing, you have to picture the man in 22:11 — the one we just left behind on the previous page. He is the worshipper *ʿalā ḥarf* — on an edge. He worships Allah on the rim. If good comes to him, he relaxes. If a trial brushes him, he tips off the edge. He is not an unbeliever. He is something more frightening — he is a believer whose belief is contingent on outcomes.

[PAUSE]

Now imagine that man losing a child. Or losing his livelihood. Or watching the future he expected dissolve. The edge he was standing on cracks. He falls.

But where does he fall *to*?

That is the question this passage answers. The Quran does not leave the man on the edge in suspense. It traces, with terrifying precision, the two stages of his descent.

**Stage one — ayah 12:** he begins calling upon something that cannot harm him and cannot benefit him.

**Stage two — ayah 13:** he ends up calling upon something whose harm is *nearer than* its benefit.

Two stages. A progression. And then — just on the far side of these ayahs, in 22:14 — the Garden, for those who believed and did good. The architecture of the passage is the architecture of two roads diverging at the moment of trial.

The original audience in Mecca knew this descent intimately. They had watched relatives stand on the edge — Muslim when business went well, retreating to Hubal when caravans were lost. They had seen a man begin by saying *"this stone is just a habit, my heart is with Allah"* and end, twenty years later, with the stone running his life.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Most of us, if asked to describe shirk, would describe ayah 12 — bowing to something useless. A stone, a tomb, an idol that does nothing.

But what the Quran does in ayah 13 is more disturbing. It reveals that **the things we worship do not stay neutral**. Whatever we treat as a god begins, eventually, to harm us faster than it benefits us. The neutrality of stage one collapses into the destructiveness of stage two.

The question we are about to sit with is this: *what changes between ayah 12 and ayah 13?* The idol is the same. The man is the same. So what shifts?

And once you see the shift — you will not be able to unsee it in your own life.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Quiet Shift from *Mā* to *Man*

Before we open the words, let me teach you something about Arabic that English flattens.

In Arabic, when you say "that which," you have two options. **Mā** (مَا) — used for things, objects, non-rational beings. The wind, the stone, the table. **Man** (مَن) — used for rational beings, persons, agents with will.

If I say *daʿawtu mā lā yusmaʿ* — "I called upon what does not hear" — I am referring to a thing.

If I say *daʿawtu man lā yusmaʿ* — "I called upon one who does not hear" — I am referring to a person who happens not to hear me.

The two words look almost identical on the page. One letter's difference. **But the universe inside that one letter is enormous.** *Mā* assumes the thing has no agency. *Man* assumes the thing is an agent.

[PAUSE]

Now look at our two ayahs.

In 22:12: *yadʿū min dūni-llāhi* **mā** *lā yaḍurruhu wa-mā lā yanfaʿuh* — he calls upon **what** cannot harm him and **what** cannot benefit him. **Mā.** The non-rational. The thing.

In 22:13: *yadʿū la-***man** *ḍarruhu aqrabu min nafʿih* — he calls upon **one** whose harm is nearer than his benefit. **Man.** The rational agent. The personality.

The same act of calling. The same worshipper. But between the two ayahs, the object of worship has been promoted — or rather, has revealed itself — from a thing into an agent.

Stay with this. Notice what Allah is showing you.

In stage one, the man thinks he is worshipping something inert. A stone. A statue. A neutral object. *"It can't hurt me. It can't help me. It just sits there. I keep it as a tradition, a comfort, a habit."* That is the worshipper's self-image. He is calling upon a *mā*.

But by stage two, **the same act of devotion has put a personality on the other end of the line.** Whatever he thought was a stone has begun to *behave* — to act on him, to harm him, to draw closer to him. The grammar itself has shifted from object to subject.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the classical commentators noticed and what the grammar quietly confirms: **idols never stay idols.** What you give your heart to acquires agency over you. The thing you treat as a god begins to act like one — not in giving you what you want, but in taking from you what you have. The stone was never just a stone. The career was never just a career. The image was never just an image. *Mā* becomes *man* the moment you start praying to it.

Most of us protest, in stage one, *"this is harmless — it's just a hobby, just a comfort, just a small thing."* We are arguing with the *mā*. We do not realize that the very act of returning to it, defending it, structuring our day around it, is converting the *mā* into a *man*. Promoting the thing into a person. Giving an inert object a vote inside our soul.

That is what the linguistic shift is showing us.

What this invites us to consider is uncomfortable: **anything you cannot stop calling upon is no longer a thing. It is a someone.** And someones have wills. And wills harm.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Aqrabu* — "Nearer," Not "Greater"

Now look at the heart of ayah 13: *ḍarruhu aqrabu min nafʿih* — *his harm is **nearer** than his benefit.*

Stop on the word *aqrabu* (أَقْرَبُ). Comparative form, root *qaraba* (ق-ر-ب) — to draw near, to approach, to be close. The same root as *qurb* (closeness), *qarīb* (relative, near one), and the divine *qarīb* — *He is nearer than the jugular vein* (50:16).

In English we would have expected: *his harm is **greater than** his benefit*. That is the natural comparison. We weigh harm against benefit on a scale. Whichever is heavier wins. That is how a calculator thinks about gods.

**But Allah does not say "greater." Allah says "nearer."** *Aqrabu*. Closer in space. Closer in time. Closer in arrival.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. *Aqrab* is a spatial word. It is a word about proximity, about how soon something gets to you, about distance in time and reach.

What is Allah telling us about the way false gods harm?

**They harm faster than they benefit.** That is the secret of the false god. It is not that its harm outweighs its benefit on some final ledger — though sometimes it does. It is that its harm *arrives sooner*. Its damage is already in the room while you are still waiting for the gift.

This is the most accurate description of every modern attachment I have ever heard.

The career promises wealth, but the exhaustion is already in your body. The wealth is years away.

The image promises validation, but the anxiety of maintaining it is in you tonight. The validation is fleeting and arrives only sometimes.

The relationship-as-savior promises completion, but the desperation it generates is already eating you. The completion never quite arrives.

The substance promises peace, but the dependency is in the next dose. The peace was the first dose, never again.

The ideology promises certainty, but the rage is in your daily feed. The certainty is always one more victory away.

**Notice in every case: the harm is *aqrab*. The benefit is *baʿīd* — far.** That is the structural signature of the false god. It withdraws what it promises into the future and pushes what it costs into the present.

[PAUSE]

The true God reverses this. *Wa-naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi min ḥabli-l-warīd* — *We are **nearer** to him than the jugular vein.* In the case of Allah, what is *aqrab* is the help. The protection. The presence. The harm — if you call it harm — is in the test that refines you, and even that is mercy delayed into recognition. With Allah, the *qurb* runs in the direction of benefit. With the false god, the *qurb* runs in the direction of harm.

This is why the Quran does not make us calculate net benefit. It makes us check the **timing** of arrival. Whatever you serve, look at what shows up first when you serve it. If anxiety arrives before peace, exhaustion before reward, shame before recognition — you are calling upon a *man whose harm is nearer than his benefit*. The grammar of your life is telling you what you are worshipping, regardless of what your tongue says.

Here is what is remarkable: we never use this metric in our actual decisions. We use the calculator metric. *On balance, is this good for me?* And the calculator metric is exactly the metric the false god can survive. *On balance*, the career is fine. *On balance*, the addiction has its perks. *On balance*, the validation feels good. The Quran's metric — *aqrab* — is the metric the false god cannot survive. Because false gods always front-load their cost.

### Element Three: *Mawlā* and *ʿAshīr* — The Two Failures of Companionship

The ayah closes with two words that, taken together, exhaust the categories of human relationship:

*La-biʾsa-l-**mawlā** wa-la-biʾsa-l-**ʿashīr*** — *How wretched the **protector**, how wretched the **companion**.*

Before we explain what these two words mean, notice the structure: not one rejection but two. Not "how wretched it is" but "how wretched as X, and how wretched as Y." Two relational roles. Two failures.

[PAUSE]

**Mawlā** (مَوْلَى), root *waliya* (و-ل-ي) — to be near, to take charge of, to be the patron of. From this root come *walī* (guardian, protector, ally), *wilāya* (authority, guardianship), and *mawlā* itself, which carries an entire spectrum of meaning: master, patron, protector, freed slave's former master, ally, supporter. In pre-Islamic Arabia, your *mawlā* was the powerful person you attached yourself to for protection. If you were a freed slave, your former master was your *mawlā*. If you were a weak tribesman, you sought a *mawlā* among the strong. **The *mawlā* is the one above you who has agreed to defend you.** Vertical relationship. Power flowing downward to cover you.

**ʿAshīr** (عَشِير), root *ʿashara* (ع-ش-ر) — to be the tenth, to live with, to associate intimately. From this root come *ʿashīra* (clan, the people you live among), *muʿāshara* (intimate companionship), and *ʿashīr* — the close friend, the one you spend your days with, the intimate associate. **The *ʿashīr* is the one beside you who shares your daily life.** Horizontal relationship. Presence flowing alongside you.

[PAUSE]

So the Quran is saying: **the false god fails as protector from above, and fails as companion from beside.**

Stay with this. These are the two roles every human being looks for in something larger than themselves. We want a power that covers us when life is too big. And we want a presence that sits with us when life is too lonely. *Mawlā* and *ʿashīr*. Strength above, intimacy beside.

When we choose what to worship, we are usually trying to fill one of these two roles. Sometimes both.

Money becomes our *mawlā* — *"if I have enough, I am protected."*

Image becomes our *mawlā* — *"if enough people respect me, I am safe."*

A relationship becomes our *ʿashīr* — *"this person will keep me from being alone with myself."*

Distraction becomes our *ʿashīr* — *"the noise will sit beside me so silence can't reach me."*

And the ayah's verdict on all of it: *la-biʾsa* — *how wretched.* The intensified verb of dispraise, doubled, with the emphatic *lām* before it. The Quranic equivalent of slamming the table. **Wretched as protector. Wretched as companion. Wretched twice.**

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to see is that the false god does not fail in only one way. It fails in *both* ways. The career-as-mawlā does not protect — it exposes you to anxieties you would not otherwise have. The image-as-mawlā does not cover you — it makes you naked to every gaze. The relationship-as-ashīr does not accompany — it abandons the moment you need it most. The distraction-as-ashīr does not sit with you — it isolates you from yourself.

And then look at how Allah describes Himself elsewhere. *Allāhu walīyu-lladhīna āmanū* — *Allah is the **walī** of those who believed* (2:257). *Wa-huwa maʿakum aynamā kuntum* — *and He is **with you** wherever you are* (57:4).

Allah claims both roles. *Walī* (the same root as *mawlā*) — protector from above. *Maʿakum* — companion alongside. The two relational hungers that the false god fails to satisfy are the two relational realities Allah announces He fulfills.

The bridge between this and Element Two becomes visible now. The false god's harm is *aqrab* — nearer. Allah's nearness — *naḥnu aqrab* — is the protection itself. **The false god is near in its harm. Allah is near in His mawla-hood and His companionship.** Same word — *qarīb* — pointing in opposite directions. One closeness destroys. The other defends.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge to the Themes

The grammar has shown us three things.

The thing you worship does not stay a thing — it acquires agency over you (*mā* becomes *man*).

What the false god offers arrives in the wrong order — its harm is closer in time than its benefit (*aqrab* not *aktharu*).

And what the false god is supposed to be — your power above and your presence beside — it cannot be (*biʾsa-l-mawlā wa-biʾsa-l-ʿashīr*).

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the structure of every human attachment.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Promotion You Did Not Notice

I want you to think about something you have done every day for the last five years. Not anything dramatic — something small. The phone you check first thing in the morning. The cup of coffee you cannot start the day without. The validation you scroll for when no one is watching. The replay of a conversation you cannot let go of.

Pick one.

Now ask: *when did this become a person?*

Because that is what has happened. You started it as a thing — *mā*. A tool. A habit. A small comfort. And somewhere, without your noticing, it became someone — *man*. It now has opinions about how your day should go. It withdraws from you and you feel its absence. It rewards you and you feel its approval. It punishes you with its silence. It has, in every functional sense, **agency over you**.

[PAUSE]

This is the descent ayah 12 to ayah 13 is describing. It is not the dramatic descent of a believer renouncing Allah at Hubal. It is the quiet descent of a *mā* becoming a *man*. The promotion of an object into a personality. The day you started serving the thing instead of using it.

The Prophet ﷺ said: *taʿisa ʿabdu-d-dīnār, taʿisa ʿabdu-d-dirham* — *miserable is the slave of the dinar, miserable is the slave of the dirham.* He did not call them owners of the dinar. He called them **slaves**. The dirham had been promoted from object to master.

Notice he used the same word as 22:13's *biʾsa* — words of misery, of wretchedness — to describe the same condition. Wretched is the slave. Wretched the protector. Wretched the companion. The Prophet ﷺ heard the warning of these ayahs and translated it for us.

[PAUSE]

The dangerous moment in any attachment is **not when you start it but when you stop noticing it.** When the thing has been around so long it disappears into your mental furniture. When you no longer ask "is this serving me?" because you have begun, without realizing it, to ask "am I serving it?"

Here is a test, drawn directly from the language of these ayahs.

Take whatever you suspect you may have promoted from *mā* to *man*. And ask yourself: *if it disappeared tomorrow, what would I lose?* If the answer is "a thing I used" — it is still a *mā*. If the answer is "a part of myself" — it has become a *man*. The day a habit becomes a piece of your identity is the day the grammar in your soul has shifted. The day the thing on the other end has acquired agency.

The believer's ongoing work is to **demote** these things. To move them back from *man* to *mā*. To unmake the personality you accidentally built and put a thing back in its place. This is what *dhikr* does. This is what *ṣalāh* does. Five times a day, the believer interrupts every attachment and turns to the only One who is supposed to be a *man* — *Allāhu lā ilāha illā huwa* — *Allah, there is no god but Him.* The verse insists on the personal pronoun. *Huwa.* He. **He is the only legitimate "He" in your life.** Everything else is an "it." Everything else is a *mā*.

[PAUSE]

The application: tonight, before you sleep, name one *mā* that has become a *man* in your life. Do not rush to remove it. Just see it. Just acknowledge that the grammar has shifted. The first work of *tawḥīd* in the modern soul is **noticing the personalities you have accidentally built.**

### Theme Two: The Order of Arrival

There is a question every honest person asks at some point in their life, usually in their thirties or forties. *Why does this thing I poured myself into hurt so much when it was supposed to make me happy?*

The career was supposed to bring stability. It brought exhaustion first, stability never quite arrived.

The image was supposed to bring respect. It brought surveillance first, respect was thin and short-lived.

The relationship was supposed to bring completion. It brought dependency first, completion was a moving target.

The accomplishment was supposed to bring rest. It brought the next anxiety first, rest never came.

We feel this and we blame ourselves. *I must have done it wrong. Maybe a different career. A different image. A different relationship. A different accomplishment.* And we set off to do it again, expecting the order of arrival to change next time.

[PAUSE]

But ayah 13 told us, fourteen centuries before our self-help industry, that **the order of arrival is the property of the god, not the property of the worshipper.** *Ḍarruhu aqrabu min nafʿih.* Whatever you worship — that is not Allah — its harm will arrive nearer than its benefit. Always. By design. Not because you are doing it wrong. Because that is what false gods are.

This is shattering and liberating at the same time.

Shattering: you cannot fix the order of arrival by trying harder. The career will always exhaust you before it secures you. The image will always surveil you before it protects you. The harm is built into the structure of treating a *mā* like a god. No effort of yours rearranges the sequence.

Liberating: **the misery is not your failure. It is the false god's signature.** What you have been mistaking for "I am bad at this" is actually "this is what serving this is." The exhaustion is information. The anxiety is information. The hollowness after each victory is information. They are all telling you: *you are calling upon a man whose harm is nearer than his benefit.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at what worship of Allah does. *Alā bi-dhikri-llāhi taṭmaʾinnu-l-qulūb* — *truly, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest* (13:28). The very first thing that arrives is rest. *Taṭmaʾinn*. Tranquility. Settling. **The benefit of remembering Allah is *aqrab* — the benefit is what reaches you first.** The work — the patience, the sacrifice, the discipline — that is real, but it is woven into a sequence where the rest is already in your chest while the work is happening.

This is the inversion. The false god's harm is *aqrab*. The true God's *qurb* is the benefit itself.

Practical application: stop tracking the *eventual* outcome of what you serve. Track the **first thing that arrives**. When you scroll for an hour, what arrives first? When you obsess over the deal, what arrives first? When you replay the slight in your head, what arrives first? When you turn to *dhikr*, what arrives first? **The first arrival tells you the kind of god you are calling upon.**

If the first arrival is anxiety, exhaustion, restlessness, shame, or the urgent need for more — you have found your *man whose harm is nearer than his benefit*. The verdict is not on the activity. The verdict is on what your soul does the moment you turn to it.

[PAUSE]

The believer's revolution is not to renounce all activity. It is to put each activity in its right grammatical category. Use what is *mā* as *mā*. Worship only the One who is *huwa*. Stop expecting things to do what only Allah can do — protect from above, accompany from beside, give peace as the first arrival.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at what these two ayahs do together with the ayahs around them.

**22:11** — *the man who worships Allah on an edge.* The doubter. The contingent believer. He has not yet renounced. He is still officially with Allah. But his stance is ready to fall.

**22:12** — *he calls upon what cannot harm him and cannot benefit him.* He has fallen — but he tells himself it is harmless. *Mā.* A thing.

**22:13** — *he calls upon one whose harm is nearer than his benefit.* The thing has become a person. The harm has begun arriving first. The relationships — *mawlā* and *ʿashīr* — have failed.

**22:14** — *Allah will admit those who believed and did good to Gardens beneath which rivers flow.* The other road. The benefit that arrives now in the form of belief and continues into eternity.

The descent of the unbeliever and the rise of the believer are mapped against each other across four ayahs. **The believer who held his ground in 22:11 is in the Garden by 22:14. The believer who fell off the edge has, by the same number of ayahs, ended up worshipping a destroyer.** The geometry of trial. The geometry of the hour of testing.

Both men begin in 22:11 with their faith on the edge. **What separates the Garden from the destroyer is what they did when the edge cracked.**

[PAUSE]

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed: *mā* becomes *man*. The theme showed: every modern attachment quietly acquires personality over us.

The grammar showed: *aqrab* not *aktharu*. The theme showed: false gods front-load harm because that is what false gods are.

The grammar showed: failure as *mawlā* and as *ʿashīr*. The theme showed: the two relational hungers — for protection above and presence beside — that only Allah can fulfill.

One insight: **anything you treat as god begins to behave like one — but only in the way of harm. It cannot give the protection or presence you seek, and the cost arrives before the reward.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in your life is no longer a *mā*? When did it stop being a thing you used and start being a personality that uses you?

2. When you turn to your usual comforts — phone, food, image-checking, replaying conversations — what arrives first? Peace? Or anxiety? What is your soul reporting about what you are worshipping?

3. Where have you assigned the role of *mawlā* — protector — to something that cannot protect? Money? Reputation? A particular outcome you are sure will save you?

4. Where have you assigned the role of *ʿashīr* — companion — to something that does not actually accompany you? A scroll, a substance, a noise, a relationship?

5. If you genuinely believed that the harm of your attachment was *nearer* than its benefit — that the cost was already in the room while you were waiting for the reward — what would you do differently tomorrow morning?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Whatever you treat as god that is not God will harm you faster than it benefits you, fail you as protector and as companion, and slowly turn from a thing you use into a personality you serve.**

### The Closing Invitation

The invitation is not to renounce the world. The world is full of *mā* — things — and *mā* are fine as *mā*. The invitation is to **return each thing to its grammatical category.** Demote what you have promoted. Use what you have begun to serve. Reserve the personhood — the *huwa* — for the only One who is, in fact, a person worthy of worship.

And the test for whether the demotion has happened is simple: when the thing disappears for a day, does your soul ache as if a friend has left, or does it function as if a tool is in the drawer? If the ache is too sharp, you have not yet demoted. If you can return to it without the surge of need — you have begun to.

[PAUSE]

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, return every false personality in our hearts back to its proper category. Let no created thing be our **mawlā** or our **ʿashīr** — be You alone the Protector above us and the Companion beside us. And when we are tested on the edge, do not let us fall toward what harms before it benefits. Let our **first arrival**, in every turning of our hearts, be the rest that only Your remembrance gives.*

*Āmīn.*
