# The Verse That Grants Permission to Retaliate — and Ends With the Names of Pardon

## Surah Al-Hajj, Ayah 60

> [ayah:22:60] ۞ ذَٰلِكَ وَمَنْ عَاقَبَ بِمِثْلِ مَا عُوقِبَ بِهِۦ ثُمَّ بُغِىَ عَلَيْهِ لَيَنصُرَنَّهُ ٱللَّهُ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌ
>
> *Dhālika. Wa man ʿāqaba bi-mithli mā ʿūqiba bihi thumma bughiya ʿalayhi la-yanṣurannahu Llāh. Inna Llāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr.*
>
> **"That [is so]. And whoever responds [to an injury] with the like of what he was injured with, and then is transgressed against — Allah will surely aid him. Indeed, Allah is Pardoning and Forgiving."**

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Words

Before we open this verse, you have to feel what it landed into.

Surah Al-Hajj is the surah of the wronged. A few ayahs back, in 22:39, Allah grants — for the first time in Islam — permission to fight. *"Permission has been given to those who are being fought, because they have been wronged."* For thirteen years in Mecca, the believers had been beaten, starved, boycotted, tortured. Some had been killed. Most had been driven from their homes. They were told to be patient. To turn the other cheek. To bear it.

And then — finally — in this surah, the gates open. *You may defend. You may strike back.*

The ayahs just before our verse (58–59) speak about those who emigrated in Allah's path and were killed or died — *Allah will provide for them a noble provision.* The martyrs and the displaced. The dead and the dispossessed. Allah is settling their account with His own hand.

Then verse 60. The living wronged. The ones who survived. The ones who can still act.

And what do they hear?

[PAUSE]

They hear permission. Proportional permission. *Whoever responds with the like of what he was struck with…* You may. You have the right.

But then — at the end of this verse — Allah names Himself with two names that should not be there. After a verse about retaliation, you expect: *Indeed, Allah is Mighty, Avenger.* You expect: *Indeed, Allah is Powerful, Strict in punishment.*

Instead, you hear: ***Inna Llāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr*** — Indeed, Allah is **Pardoning** and **Forgiving**.

[PAUSE]

Why? Why these names? Why here? Why does Allah grant the right to retaliate and then close the verse with the names of release?

That is the door we are about to walk through.

## PART ONE: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Grammar of Grievance — Active in Action, Passive in Suffering

Before we open this word, sit with a small grammatical truth that you already know but have probably never noticed.

When you tell the story of a wrong done to you, the grammar of your sentence reveals what you've made of it.

You can say: *"I was hurt."* That's passive. Something happened *to* you. You are the recipient.

Or you can say: *"They wronged me. They humiliated me. They destroyed me."* That's active — but with someone else as the actor. You are still the recipient, but you have given them the power of the verb.

Or you can say: *"I'm going to make them pay. I'm going to teach them a lesson."* Now you are the actor. You take the verb back.

Notice how the same event can be told three ways, and each telling shapes a different self.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the verbs in this verse. Read them slowly:

> *Wa man **ʿāqaba** bi-mithli mā **ʿūqiba** bihi thumma **bughiya** ʿalayhi…*

Three verbs. Watch which voice each one is in.

**ʿāqaba** — "responded / retaliated." This is **active**. The wronged person is the doer. *He* retaliated.

**ʿūqiba** — "was injured / was punished." This is **passive**. The verb has been turned around. The wronged person is the recipient.

**bughiya ʿalayhi** — "was transgressed against." This is **passive** again. The recipient.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah gives the wronged believer the active voice **only at the moment of his proportional response**. *He responded.* Active. The verb belongs to him.

But the original wrong — the thing that gave him the right to respond in the first place — is presented in the **passive**. *He was struck.* Not "they struck him." Just: "he was struck."

And the second wrong — the wrong that comes *after* his proportional response, the wrong that triggers Allah's promised aid — is also in the **passive**. *He was transgressed against.* Not "they transgressed against him." Just: "he was transgressed against."

Stay with this. Why?

Here's what's remarkable. When the original aggressor is named in the active — *"they hurt me, they wronged me, they did this to me"* — something shifts in the wronged person's heart. The wrong becomes a story with a villain. The villain becomes the center of the universe. The wronged person organizes his life around the one who hurt him.

But Allah, in this verse, refuses to give the original aggressor the active voice. The wrongdoer is **erased from the grammar**. He doesn't even get to be a subject. The harm just *happened.* The transgression just *occurred.* The wronged believer is the only person in the verse with a face.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the deepest mercies of the Quran's grammar. Allah will not let the wrongdoer live rent-free in the wronged person's sentence.

You retaliate? Yes. *You.* Active. Named.
You were hurt? Yes. But the one who hurt you doesn't get to be the actor. *It was done.*

Watch what this does psychologically. The believer is invited to keep his agency. He acts. But he is also released from the obsession that the wrongdoer's identity must be the gravitational center of his story. The wrongdoer is in the passive. He is a hand that swung but not a face that owns the moment.

This is what the believing heart sounds like in grammar: *I act. The harm came. I respond. The harm came again. Allah will help me.* The aggressor never quite materializes as a self in the sentence. He is a weather pattern. Not a god.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing in this small grammatical fact. Look at the verb chosen for the active retaliation: **ʿāqaba** — Form III of the root *ʿ-q-b*. The root meaning is *to follow, to come after.* Form III in Arabic carries the meaning of **reciprocity** — doing something back, mutually, in pairs.

So **ʿāqaba** doesn't just mean "punish." It means *to do back* — to mirror, to match, to follow up someone's strike with a strike that follows from his. It is built into the verb that the response is **a reflection, not an originating force.** The wronged person is not initiating violence. He is *following* someone else's violence, in equal measure.

This is the grammar of *qiṣāṣ* — proportionality — built into the morphology of the word itself. You don't strike. You *strike back.* And the verb itself remembers that you didn't start it.

[PAUSE]

What this single grammatical fact reveals: even in granting permission to retaliate, Allah is shaping the believer's inner life. The verbs choose what your heart organizes around. *You* act. The aggressor is not a subject. The retaliation itself is a reflection of someone else's strike, not the opening of a new account.

This is permission given without arming the ego.

### Element Two: *Bi-Mithl* — The Tiny Word That Saves the World

Now look at the small phrase tucked between the verbs:

> *…ʿāqaba **bi-mithli** mā ʿūqiba bihi…*
>
> "…responded **with the like** of what he was injured with…"

**Mithl** in Arabic means *like, similar to, equivalent.* The preposition **bi-** here means "with." So *bi-mithl* = "with the like of."

Before we open this word, teach yourself the concept first.

In every legal system in human history, the question of retaliation has been one question: *how much?* When someone strikes me, may I strike back? Yes. May I strike back harder? Most ancient systems said yes — and that's how feuds began. He hit me with a stick, so I hit him with a stone. He hit me with a stone, so I burned his field. He burned my field, so I killed his son. He killed my son, so I killed his entire family. *And so the desert ran red for generations.*

The pre-Islamic Arabs lived inside this. A single insult could birth a war that lasted a hundred years. Once a retaliation exceeded the original wound, the original wound was forgotten and now *the retaliation itself* became the new wound. Which had to be retaliated against. Which had to be retaliated against. Forever.

This is what humanity is, when left to its own grievance.

[PAUSE]

Now look at this two-word phrase: **bi-mithl** — "with the like of."

This phrase does what no human society had managed to do: **it caps the response at the size of the wound.** Not bigger. Not "and a little extra to teach them a lesson." Not "and now everything you love." Just — *the like.* What was done to you. Not more.

Notice how dangerous this is to hold in the heart of someone who has just been hurt. Because the heart, when wounded, doesn't want **mithl**. The heart wants **akthar** — *more.* The heart wants the wrongdoer to feel what you felt and *then some* — because no retaliation feels like enough. The heart wants the universe to balance the books *with interest.*

And Allah says: no. **Bi-mithl.** *Like.* Equal weight. The exact size of what you carried.

[PAUSE]

Stay with how astonishing this is. The verse has just granted you the right to act. But the moment it grants the right, it also caps the right. *You may strike back, but you may not strike back larger.* The permission and the limit arrive in the same breath.

And here is the secret tucked inside that limit: anyone who has ever been hurt knows that **mithl is almost impossible**. The wronged person almost never stops at *the like.* The wounded heart, given permission, will swing past proportion. It is its nature. Pain seeks compounding interest. Grievance accrues.

So Allah, in giving permission, is also handing the believer **a discipline.** Yes — you may. But the right itself is bounded. And to actually **stop at *bi-mithl*** is one of the hardest acts of the human soul. It requires the believer to be more measured at the moment of striking back than he was at the moment of being struck.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this small phrase reveals: in the Quranic worldview, the right to retaliate is not the right to release your rage. It is the right to **restore balance.** Once balance is restored — *bi-mithl* — the account is closed. To go further is no longer retaliation. It is now a new wrong. And now you, who were once the wronged, have become the wrongdoer.

The cap *protects the wronged from becoming what wronged him.*

This is something only a Lawgiver who knows the human heart from the inside could write. *Yes, strike back. But not even one inch further. Because the moment you do, you have lost.*

### Element Three: The Names That Should Not Be There

Now we come to the heart of this verse. Read the closing phrase:

> *Inna Llāha la-**ʿafuwwun ghafūr***
>
> "Indeed, Allah is Pardoning and Forgiving."

Before we open the names, teach yourself what to listen for.

In the Quran, the divine names that close a verse are not decoration. They are doing **structural work.** They tell you what attribute of Allah is most relevant to what just happened. When a verse ends with *ʿAzīzun Ḥakīm* (Mighty, Wise), Allah is signaling: *what I just told you stands on My might and My wisdom.* When a verse ends with *Samīʿun ʿAlīm* (Hearing, Knowing), Allah is reminding you that nothing of what was discussed escapes His attention.

So the names at the end of a verse are a kind of caption. They tell you the lens through which to read what came before.

[PAUSE]

Now ask: what names should we expect at the end of a verse about retaliation?

Walk through the Quran's other retaliation verses and see what names cluster there. *Strict in punishment. Severe in retribution. The Avenger. The Mighty. The Powerful.* These are the names that match a verse about striking back.

You expect: *"…Allah will surely aid him. Indeed, Allah is **Mighty, the Avenger.**"*

That would make complete sense. That would feel coherent. That would feel *satisfying* to the wronged believer who has just been told he may strike.

[PAUSE]

But Allah does not say that.

Allah says: ***ʿAfuwwun Ghafūr***. **Pardoning and Forgiving.**

Stay with how strange this is. The verse has spent its breath on the right to retaliate. The verse has promised divine aid to the one who was wronged twice. And then — at the very moment we expect the divine names of justice and victory — Allah names Himself with the names of release.

What is this?

[PAUSE]

Let's open each name.

**ʿAfuww** comes from the root *ʿ-f-w.* The base meaning of this root, documented in the classical lexicons, is *to erase, to efface, to wipe clean, to leave no trace.* Picture wind blowing across sand and erasing footprints — that is *ʿafw.* The trace is gone. As if no one walked there.

The form **ʿAfuww** is the intensive pattern *faʿūl* — meaning *the One who does this abundantly, repeatedly, characteristically.* So *ʿAfuww* is not "the One who occasionally pardons." It is *the One whose nature is erasing.*

**Ghafūr** comes from the root *gh-f-r.* The base meaning is *to cover, to conceal, to clothe over.* The same root gives us *mighfar* — the helmet that covers the head in battle. *Ghafr* is to cover an offense so that it cannot be seen.

**Ghafūr** is also the *faʿūl* intensive — *the One whose nature is covering.*

[PAUSE]

So the two names work together but they are not the same. **Maghfira** — covering — leaves the offense underneath. It hides it from view. It protects the offender from exposure. But the trace remains beneath the cover.

**ʿAfw** goes further. It does not just cover the offense. It **erases** it. As if it never happened.

Maghfira hides the footprint.
ʿAfw erases the footprint.

Both are mercy. But ʿafw is the deeper mercy — it doesn't just protect from consequence; it removes the act from the ledger entirely.

[PAUSE]

And these are the names Allah chooses to seal a verse about retaliation.

Here is the asymmetry that should stop us cold. Allah has just **given the believer permission to act on the principle of *bi-mithl* — proportional response.** And then Allah names Himself by the principle of ***ʿafw*** — erasing. By the principle of *ghafr* — covering.

These are not the same principle. *Bi-mithl* is exact accounting. *ʿAfw* is the dissolution of the account.

What is Allah doing?

[PAUSE]

He is doing what only the Real Lawgiver can do. He is saying: *I have given you the right. I have not commanded you to use it.*

The verse permits. The names invite higher.

You may retaliate proportionally — that is your right, and I will defend it. But know this about Me: **I am the One who erases. I am the One who covers.** When you stand before Me, you will need that. And the closer your character is to My names, the closer you will be to Me.

The permission and the names are in tension on purpose. The tension is the lesson. The believer is being told: *you have the legal right; here is the higher path.*

[PAUSE]

Look at how this fits with what the Quran does elsewhere. In Surah Ash-Shūrā (42:40), Allah says: *"And the recompense of an evil is an evil like it. But whoever pardons (ʿafā) and reconciles — his reward is upon Allah."* The same architecture. The right is named. Then the higher path is named. *Whoever pardons.*

Or 16:126: *"And if you punish, punish with the like of what you were punished with. **But if you are patient, it is better for those who are patient.**"*

Same shape. Permission, then a quiet door held open above it.

[PAUSE]

Here is what these two names at the end of this verse reveal, that the rest of the verse does not say in words: **the permission to retaliate is real, but the character of Allah leans toward release.** The verse gives the believer his right *and* shows him the One whose nature is to forgive — and lets him decide which one to be more like.

This is divine pedagogy in the smallest possible space. Two names. And in those two names, an entire ethic.

### Bridge

The grammar refused to let the wrongdoer be the subject of the sentence.
The two-word phrase *bi-mithl* capped the response at the size of the wound.
The two names at the end pointed past the response, toward release.

Three small linguistic features. Each one shaping the believer's heart while the believer thought he was just learning his rights.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the wronged self before God.

## PART TWO: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Permission That Points Past Itself

You know what it feels like when someone gives you permission for something they hope you will not do.

A parent says to a teenager: *"You're seventeen now. You can stay out until midnight if you want."* The permission is real. The teen can absolutely use it. But the way the parent says it — the slight pause, the open door, the trust extended — communicates more than the permission. It communicates *I am giving you this so you can show me you don't need it.*

Or imagine a colleague who has wronged you publicly. Your manager pulls you aside and says: *"Look — if you want to file a formal complaint, you have every right. HR will back you. You'd win."* The right is real. The path is open. But your manager's tone tells you something else: *I'm telling you this because I think you're bigger than this.*

This is **permission that points past itself.** The permission is genuine. It is not a trick. You can use it without sin. But the way it's framed — what surrounds it, what it ends with, what it opens onto — shapes you toward something the permission alone would not.

[PAUSE]

This is what verse 60 is.

Allah grants the wronged believer the legal right to strike back proportionally. That right is **real.** It is not a setup. The believer who exercises it has done nothing wrong. He has done what is just. *La-yanṣurannahu Llāh* — Allah will surely aid him. Allah's help is promised to the one who acts within his right.

But the verse closes with **ʿAfuwwun Ghafūr** — the names that describe the One who erases.

The verse does not say: *do not retaliate.* It says: *here is what I am like.*

And the believer is left in front of this. Holding two truths at once. *I have the right. And the One who gave me the right is the One who erases.*

[PAUSE]

Now think about your own wounds. Think of someone who hurt you. Someone you have the right to be angry with. Someone you could legitimately confront, or expose, or cut off, or reciprocate.

Here is the question this verse asks you: **are you using your right because justice requires it — or because it lets you stay angry?**

There is a kind of righteousness that we use to hide behind. *I'm not bitter, I'm just standing up for myself. I'm not vindictive, I'm just holding them accountable. I'm not consumed, I'm just refusing to let it go.* All of these can be true. *Bi-mithl* is real. The right exists.

But this verse, with the names it ends on, asks: **even in your right, are you turning toward Allah's character — or away from it?**

[PAUSE]

Here's what's hardest. The wronged person who exercises his right does nothing wrong. But the wronged person who *releases* his right — who pardons when he could retaliate — does something that resembles Allah Himself.

The legal floor is *bi-mithl.* The character ceiling is *ʿafw.* The verse hands you both, and asks you to live somewhere on the line between them.

[PAUSE]

This is why this verse is one of the most subtle ethical texts in the Quran. It does not command pardon. It does not forbid retaliation. It does the harder thing: **it gives you the right to retaliate and shows you what the One who gave you the right is like — and lets the rest happen between you and Him.**

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes the question. The question is no longer: *was I right to be angry?* The question is: *given that I am right, what kind of person do I want to be?*

The right to be angry settles nothing. The character of God is the real ceiling. And every grievance you hold is, in the end, an opportunity to either hold your ground at *bi-mithl* — or step closer to *ʿafw.*

This is why the verse refuses to choose for you. The choosing is the point.

### Theme Two: The Wronged Person's Quiet Battle

There is a battle inside the wronged person that nobody sees.

When you have been hurt — really hurt, not inconvenienced, but *hurt* — your inner life reorganizes around the one who hurt you. Their face appears unbidden in your prayers. Their voice plays over your morning coffee. You rehearse arguments with them while you drive. You imagine their downfall while you fall asleep. Without choosing it, you have given them something close to worship: the constant attention of your inner life.

This is the secret cost of being wronged that nobody warns you about. It isn't just the original wound. It is what the wound *becomes* when it lives in you. The wrongdoer takes up residence. The wound becomes a shrine. And without realizing it, the wronged person's life starts to revolve around the one they cannot stop thinking about.

[PAUSE]

This is what the grammar of verse 60 was protecting against.

Remember: the original wrongdoer is **never given the active voice in the verse.** Not once. He is erased from the sentence. The wrong was *done* — but the doer doesn't get a face. He doesn't get a verb. He doesn't get to be the subject of the wronged person's story.

Why?

Because Allah knows what wrongdoers do to wronged people if you let them stay as subjects. They become idols. Quiet, hateful idols, but idols nonetheless. They become the center around which your inner life orbits.

[PAUSE]

So the verse is doing two surgeries at once.

**The first surgery is on the rights.** It says: *you may strike back, but only bi-mithl, only the like.* That cap protects you from becoming what you hate.

**The second surgery is on the inner life.** It says: *the one who hurt you is a passive voice in your story. He is not the subject. He is not the doer. He is something that happened — and Allah is the One who acts in your defense.*

And then — the names: *ʿAfuwwun Ghafūr.* Pardoning. Forgiving.

These are not just names of Allah's character. They are also a quiet door for **you** — for the wronged person — to walk through. *Look how I am with sinners. Look how I erase. Look how I cover. If you become like Me, the wrong stops living inside you.*

[PAUSE]

Because here is the thing nobody tells the wronged: **the deepest wound is not the original injury. The deepest wound is what you carry afterward.** The original injury heals. The body forgives. The bruise fades. But what stays, sometimes for years, sometimes for decades, sometimes forever — is the *meaning* of the wound. The story you tell about it. The face that haunts your prayers. The vengeance that warms you on cold nights.

This residue is what *ʿafw* is for. ʿAfw is not naive forgetting. ʿAfw is **erasing the trace** — releasing the wound from being the gravitational center of your inner life. Letting the wrongdoer sink back into being a passive voice. A weather pattern. Not a god.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah closes this verse with *ʿAfuwwun Ghafūr,* He is not just naming Himself. He is offering a way out for the wronged. *I am the One who erases. I can erase what is done to you. And I can erase what you carry.*

Pardon, in the Quranic vision, is not a gift the wronged gives the wrongdoer. It is, much more often, **a gift the wronged gives himself.** The wrongdoer may never know. The wrongdoer may never deserve it. Pardon is between you and Allah — and it releases *you* from the prison the wrongdoer built when he hurt you.

[PAUSE]

So: who are you in this verse?

If you are the one who has been wronged — the verse is handing you a mirror. *Look at what you carry. Look at whose face lives in your prayers. Look at how often this wrong has the active voice in your story.* The verse gives you your right. And it shows you Allah's character. And it asks you, gently, *which one do you want to live as?*

And what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes how you read your own anger. The next time you feel that familiar, righteous fire of grievance — the next time you rehearse the argument or imagine the downfall — ask yourself: *am I exercising my right, or am I worshipping my wound?*

The right is real. The verse stands behind it. But the names at the end of the verse stand behind something larger. They stand behind a self that has handed the wound back to Allah. They stand behind a heart that has decided not to let the wrongdoer take up residence.

And that handing back — that quiet release — is what Allah names Himself with at the end of this verse.

[PAUSE]

Allah is not telling you what to do. He is telling you what He is like. And waiting to see what you do with that knowledge.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

The grammar said one thing. *The wrongdoer doesn't get to be the subject of your story.*

The phrase *bi-mithl* said one thing. *Your response is capped at the size of the wound, not the size of your rage.*

The two names at the end said one thing. *I, the One who gave you the right, am the One who erases. Become like Me.*

Three small linguistic features — and they are the same insight from three angles. **The verse refuses to let the wrong own you.** It refuses to let the wrongdoer be the subject of your inner life. It refuses to let your rage write the size of your response. And it refuses to let the right to retaliate be the highest thing you reach for.

The right is a floor.
The character of Allah is the ceiling.
You live somewhere between them, and the choosing is the worship.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

1. Is there someone whose face has become the active voice in my inner life — someone who, without my choosing it, has taken up residence in my prayers, my drives, my insomnia? What would it mean to return them to the passive voice?

2. When I exercise my right against someone who has wronged me, am I stopping at *bi-mithl* — or am I quietly going further, telling myself it is justice when it is something else?

3. Am I confusing the right to be angry with the obligation to stay angry? The verse gives me the right. It does not require me to use it. What would it cost me to release it?

4. Allah names Himself ***ʿAfuww*** — the One who erases. When I look at my own ledger of grievances, what would it mean to let some of them be erased — not because they didn't happen, but because I no longer want them to be the center of my life?

5. If Allah, who has been wronged by me far more than anyone has been wronged by anyone, names Himself by erasing and covering — what does that say about the wrongs I am still keeping on my own books?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah grants the wronged believer the legal right to retaliate proportionally — and then closes the verse with the names of pardon, so that the believer holds both his right and his Lord's character at the same time, and chooses.**

[PAUSE]

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked, by this verse, to abandon your rights. The verse stands behind your right. It promises Allah's aid to the one who was struck and then was struck again. *La-yanṣurannahu Llāh* is a real promise, and it is for you.

But the invitation, after the right has been named, is this: *look at the One who gave you the right.* Look at how He describes Himself when He is finished defending you. He does not call Himself the Avenger. He does not call Himself Strict in Punishment. He calls Himself **the One who erases. The One who covers.**

And He waits.

He waits to see whether you will use the floor He has given you, or reach toward the ceiling He has shown you.

Both are valid. The floor is righteous. The ceiling is closer to Him.

This is what tadabbur of this verse is for: not to make you abandon your rights, but to make sure that when you exercise them — or choose not to — you are doing it consciously, in front of the One whose names you are now seeing more clearly than you saw them before.

### Du'ā'

*O Allah, You are ʿAfuww — You erase. You are Ghafūr — You cover. We have been wronged, and You know what we carry. Free us from letting the ones who hurt us live inside us as gods. Cap our retaliation at* bi-mithl *and never an inch beyond. And when our rights are defended and our hearts are tired of carrying the wound — make us the kind of people who choose Your character over our right. Because we, who have wronged You far more than any have wronged us, need You to be exactly what You named Yourself at the end of this verse.*

*Āmīn.*
