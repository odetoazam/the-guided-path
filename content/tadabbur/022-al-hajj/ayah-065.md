# Yumsiku al-Samāʾ: Why the Sky Has Not Fallen

> أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَخَّرَ لَكُم مَّا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَٱلْفُلْكَ تَجْرِى فِى ٱلْبَحْرِ بِأَمْرِهِۦ وَيُمْسِكُ ٱلسَّمَآءَ أَن تَقَعَ عَلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِۦٓ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِٱلنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ
>
> *A-lam tara anna-Llāha sakhkhara lakum mā fī-l-arḍi wa-l-fulka tajrī fī-l-baḥri bi-amrih, wa-yumsiku-s-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā-l-arḍi illā bi-idhnih. Inna-Llāha bi-n-nāsi la-raʾūfun raḥīm.*
>
> "Do you not see that Allah has subjected to you whatever is in the earth, and the ships running through the sea by His command? And He holds the sky back from falling upon the earth — except by His permission. Indeed, Allah, to mankind, is Most Kind, Most Merciful."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

Surah Al-Ḥajj is one of the most architecturally strange surahs in the Quran. It begins with the earthquake of the Hour — *zalzalat al-sāʿati shayʾun ʿaẓīm* — a cosmos coming apart. It moves through arguments with the deniers of resurrection, the rituals of pilgrimage, the permission to defend oneself, the opposing parties at the Throne, the ones who emigrated and were killed, the Sabians, the Magians, the Christians, the Jews, the believers — all converging at the seat of judgment.

By the time we arrive at verse 65, the surah has already done extraordinary work. It has talked about the embryo in the womb. It has talked about the crops drying up. It has talked about Shayṭān casting into the recitations of every prophet. It has turned over almost every theme it possesses.

And then, in this stretch, the surah pivots. It stops arguing. It stops warning. It says, in effect: **stop. Look around you. Look up.**

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine the first listener in Makkah or early Madīnah hearing this verse. They live in a world without a ceiling of theory. They have not been told that the sky is held up by atmospheric pressure differentials and the gravitational equilibrium of celestial bodies. They look up, and they see exactly what we see — a vault of blue, sometimes filled with cloud, sometimes with stars — and they have no automatic explanation for why it stays where it stays.

For them, the question is alive in a way it is not alive for us.

*Why doesn't the sky fall?*

We have been trained, over centuries, to feel that this is a childish question. The sky doesn't fall because there is no "sky" in that sense — there is air, there is space, there is gravity. We are told the question dissolves once you understand the physics.

[PAUSE]

But notice what has happened. The question has not been answered. It has been *renamed*. We have given new names — gravity, atmosphere, equilibrium — to a phenomenon we still cannot generate, control, or guarantee. We have replaced the word "miracle" with the word "mechanism" and we have called this progress.

The first listener of this ayah is not bound by that move. When they hear *yumsiku al-samāʾa* — "He holds the sky" — the verb does not strike them as primitive. It strikes them as honest.

### The Hook

There is a verb in this ayah — *yumsiku* — that is not in the past tense. It is not "He held the sky once." It is "He is holding the sky now." Right now. As you breathe. As you read this. Continuous, present, ongoing.

And there is one word in this ayah, tucked between *the sky falls* and *His permission*, that quietly inverts everything we think we know about how the world works.

That word — when we get to it — will tell us that what we call "stability" is not the world's natural state. It is the world's interrupted state. The default is collapse. What we are living in is a withholding.

Let's open the text and see how Allah builds this.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will move through three linguistic anchors. Each one is doing real work.

### First Anchor: *Sakhkhara* — The Verb of Subjection

#### Teach the concept first

In Arabic, verbs do not just sit on a shelf. They live in *forms* — patterns that intensify, soften, cause, or reflect the basic meaning of the root.

Take a simple root: *k-s-r*, "to break." Form I — *kasara* — means "he broke something." Form II — *kassara* — means "he broke it to pieces, repeatedly, intensely, completely shattered it." Same root. Different intensity.

The doubling of the middle letter — that *shadda* you hear when an Arab speaker leans into the word — is the carrier of the intensification. It tells you: this is not light action. This is action with weight, with thoroughness, with force.

Hold that pattern in your mind.

#### Open the word

Now look at the verb Allah uses for what He has done with the earth: *sakhkhara*. Not *sakhara*. *Sakh-kha-ra*. The shadda is sitting right there in the middle, audible, demanding to be pronounced.

سَخَّرَ لَكُم مَّا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ

*Sakhkhara lakum mā fī-l-arḍ.*

"He has subjected to you whatever is in the earth."

#### Sit with this

The root *s-kh-r* in its base form has the meaning of compelling something into service. It is not the word for "giving" something to someone. It is the word for *bending* something toward someone — overriding its natural orientation so that it now serves your purpose.

When the Arab linguists describe *taskhīr* — the noun form of this verb — they describe it as *taskhīr al-dābba li-rākibihā*: the bending of the riding animal to its rider. The animal does not naturally agree to be ridden. Something has been done to it. Its natural will has been turned. It now goes where the rider directs.

Notice what this does to your reading of the verse.

The earth is not described here as a passive resource Allah handed to you like a gift on a tray. The earth is described as something that *had to be bent* to your purpose. The seas had to be made cross-able. The animals had to be made tame. The crops had to be made grow-able. The minerals had to be made find-able. The fields had to be made plow-able.

Every one of those is a *taskhīr*. A subjection. A bending of a will that did not have to bend.

#### Deeper

Now stay with the form. Form II is intensive and causative. Why does Allah use Form II here, rather than Form I?

Because Form I would say: He has put these things in your path. Form II says: He has *made them serve you*. He has not just placed the river near the village. He has made the river *carry your boat*. He has not just put the cow in the field. He has made the cow *yield her milk*.

Form I describes proximity. Form II describes obedience.

[PAUSE]

There is a thought experiment that may make this land. Imagine for a single moment that the iron in the soil refused to be smelted. Just refused. Stayed inert no matter what fire you brought to it. Imagine that wheat decided it would no longer feed humans — that the moment it entered a stomach it would simply pass through unconverted. Imagine the wind decided not to fill sails anymore.

None of those is physically impossible in any deep metaphysical sense. They are simply, right now, being held in a posture of service.

That posture is *taskhīr*.

#### Conclude

The first verb of the ayah, then, is not telling you that the world is full of useful stuff. It is telling you that the world is full of things that have been *bent toward you*, against what you might call their natural inertia, by an act of divine will.

Every spoonful of food, every breath of breathable air, every step on solid ground — *sakhkhara*. He bent it.

[PAUSE]

### Second Anchor: *Yumsiku* — The Verb of Holding

We come now to the heart of the ayah.

#### Teach the concept first

Arabic distinguishes very precisely between verbs that describe a completed action and verbs that describe an ongoing action. The grammarians call this the difference between *al-māḍī* (the perfect, completed) and *al-muḍāriʿ* (the imperfect, continuous).

In English, we have something similar — "He built the house" versus "He is building the house" — but in Arabic the distinction is built into the verb itself. You cannot say it without choosing.

Now read the ayah and watch the tenses shift:

*Sakhkhara* — past tense, perfect, completed. He **has subjected** the earth. The bending was done.

*Yumsiku* — present tense, imperfect, continuous. He **is holding** the sky. The holding is happening.

#### Open the word

وَيُمْسِكُ ٱلسَّمَآءَ أَن تَقَعَ عَلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ

*Wa-yumsiku-s-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā-l-arḍ.*

"And He holds the sky from falling upon the earth."

The root *m-s-k* means to grip, to grasp, to take hold of, to retain in the hand. It is the verb you would use for closing your fingers around something so that it does not slip. It is also the verb used in another famous ayah — *fa-imsākun bi-maʿrūf* — "then a holding-on with kindness," about the husband who chooses to retain his marriage.

It is a verb of *active retention*. Not passive containment. Not "the sky happens to remain up there." Active holding. Allah's hand — figuratively — closed around the sky.

#### Stay with me

Now read it again, and feel the tense:

*Yumsiku.* He **is holding**. Right now. As your eyes move across this line. As you take a breath. As your heart beats. As the second hand on a watch sweeps another second. He is holding.

This is not a one-time event in deep time. This is not "He set up the universe and now physics runs it." This is not the watchmaker God of the Enlightenment philosophers, who built a clock and walked away.

This is *active, present-tense, moment-by-moment retention*.

[PAUSE]

If, for a single instant, He let go — what then?

The ayah tells us. *An taqaʿa ʿalā-l-arḍ* — "from falling upon the earth."

#### Notice what this implies

This is the most dangerous word in the ayah, and it is hidden in plain sight: *an taqaʿa*. "That it would fall."

Read it carefully. The structure is *yumsiku al-samāʾa* + *an taqaʿa*. He holds it back from falling.

In Arabic, this construction tells you what *would happen* if the holding stopped. It tells you the *default behavior* of the thing being held.

If you say in Arabic, "I held the dog from biting the visitor," the structure tells you: the dog's natural inclination was to bite. The biting was not an exception. The not-biting was the exception.

Apply that to the sky.

The default behavior of the sky, on its own, with no one holding it, *is to fall*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment. Because everything in our modern training screams against this. Our entire scientific world-picture begins from the assumption that the cosmos is *naturally stable* — that things move, but on average they hold their patterns; that gravity, atmospheric pressure, orbital mechanics keep the heavens roughly where they are; and that catastrophe is the exception, the rare disturbance.

The Quran inverts this. The Quran says: stability is the exception. Catastrophe is what is being prevented. Every moment that the sky does not fall is a moment in which a hand has not opened.

The question we ask — "why do disasters happen?" — is exactly backwards.

The Quranic question is — "why does anything *not* happen?"

#### Deeper

Now look at one more word: *bi-idhnih*. "Except by His permission."

Even the falling, when it does occur — even the meteor, even the storm, even the day the rain becomes ruin — even *that* is by permission. Disasters do not slip past Him. They do not happen because the holding briefly failed. They happen because the holding was deliberately, momentarily, by His command, eased.

This is enormous. It means there is no event in the cosmos — no collapse, no breakage, no falling — that is outside the system of His decree. The hand that holds is the same hand that, when it acts, releases.

#### Conclude

So when we read *yumsiku al-samāʾ*, we are not reading a piece of pre-modern science. We are reading a metaphysical claim that has not been falsified, and cannot be falsified, by any amount of physics. The claim is: the apparent self-sufficiency of the cosmos is a courtesy. What you call "the laws of nature" is the consistent name we give to a consistent grip.

When the grip is constant, we call it gravity. When the grip eases for a moment, we call it disaster. Both are by His permission. Both are *idhn*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Third Anchor: *Bi-n-nās* — To Mankind, Not to the Believers

#### Open the closing

The ayah ends with a phrase that, on first reading, sounds like a standard Quranic closing — but is doing very specific work.

إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِٱلنَّاسِ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Inna-Llāha bi-n-nāsi la-raʾūfun raḥīm.*

"Indeed Allah, to mankind, is Most Kind, Most Merciful."

#### Notice what is missing

Look at the addressee. *Bi-n-nās*. **To mankind.**

Not *bi-l-muʾminīn*. Not "to the believers."
Not *bi-l-muttaqīn*. Not "to those who fear Him."
Not *bi-ʿibādih*. Not "to His servants."

*Bi-n-nās*. To people. All people. Believer, denier, hypocrite, idolater, wanderer, lost — all of them.

This is significant because in the Quran, divine names attached to attributes are rarely scattered carelessly. When Allah closes a verse with *raʾūf raḥīm*, the indirect object — *who is He kind to* — is chosen with care.

So why mankind here? Why not the believers?

#### Sit with this

Because the mercy described in this ayah is *cosmic mercy*, and cosmic mercy is universal.

The atheist's sky does not fall on him because he is an atheist. The murderer is not crushed by gravity malfunctioning the moment he commits the crime. The blasphemer breathes the same held-up air as the saint. The denier drinks the water from the same subjected sea. The cosmos does not discriminate.

This is *raʾfah* — a tenderness that pours out without asking who deserves it.

The Quran is not afraid to make this point. Allah's relationship to the cosmos as Sustainer is not a reward for belief. It is the canvas on which belief and disbelief, virtue and vice, gratitude and ingratitude all play out.

You and the person you most disagree with are both alive right now because of the same single reason.

*Yumsiku.*

He is holding.

#### Deeper

Now look at the two names: *raʾūf* and *raḥīm*.

*Raḥīm* you have heard a thousand times. It is from the root *r-ḥ-m* — the womb, the womb's mercy, the mercy that nurtures and provides. It is encompassing.

*Raʾūf* is rarer. The lexicographers describe *raʾfah* as a more delicate, more tender form of compassion than *raḥmah*. It is the compassion that *aches* for the recipient. The Arabs use it for the gentleness of a person who cannot bear to see suffering — who removes a thorn from the path because they cannot stand the thought of someone's foot finding it.

The two are paired here, with *raʾūf* placed *first* — which the linguists note is unusual, and significant. The deeper, more intimate name leads. The encompassing name follows.

What this tells you about the holding of the sky: it is not bureaucratic mercy. It is not the impartial enforcement of a contract. It is a tenderness that does not want you to be hurt. It is His hand staying closed around the sky because He cannot bear what would happen if it opened.

#### Conclude

The ayah's closing is not a flourish. It is the answer to the question the ayah has been building.

Why is the earth subjected to you? Because He is *raʾūf*.
Why is the sky held back from falling? Because He is *raḥīm*.
Why does this apply to mankind without distinction? Because the mercy is in His nature, not in your worthiness.

The cosmos is the visible body of an invisible tenderness.

### Bridge

Three words. *Sakhkhara* — He bent the earth toward you. *Yumsiku* — He is holding the sky away from you. *Bi-n-nās* — and this is for everyone, not just the deserving.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### First Theme: The Default Is Collapse

#### Begin where you live

There is a way of looking at the world that almost everyone alive in the modern era has been trained into. It goes like this:

The world is fundamentally stable. Stuff works. Cars start. Bodies digest. Bridges hold. Stars stay in the sky. *Of course they do — that's what they do.* Disasters happen, but they are aberrations, statistical outliers, the price of living in a probabilistic universe. Most of life is the system functioning.

This worldview is so deep we don't even notice we hold it. We notice it only when it cracks. When the body that always digested suddenly cannot. When the bridge that always held suddenly does not. When the loved one who always woke up suddenly will not.

In those moments, a question rises that has no good place in the modern frame: *why did it work all those other times?*

We assume disasters need explanation. We don't assume non-disasters need explanation.

The Quran flips this.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

*Yumsiku al-samāʾa an taqaʿa ʿalā-l-arḍ.*

He holds the sky from falling.

If you take this seriously — and the ayah is asking you to take it seriously — then the picture changes completely.

It is not that disasters are rare interruptions of an otherwise self-running cosmos. It is that the cosmos has no self-running mechanism. There is a hand. The hand holds. When the hand keeps holding, you call this Tuesday. When the hand opens for a moment, you call this catastrophe. The difference between an ordinary day and the worst day of your life is a single decision in a will you cannot see.

[PAUSE]

This is unbearable to contemplate for very long. Most of us cannot live with it. We translate it back, almost immediately, into something more comfortable: "Yes, of course, Allah holds everything together — but, you know, through laws of physics and so on."

But that is not what the ayah says. The ayah does not mention laws. It mentions a verb. *Yumsiku.* And it puts that verb in the present tense, not the past. The holding is not delegated. The holding is direct.

#### Deeper

Now bring this into your own life.

Think about the moments today when something *did not go wrong*. You walked across a room and your knees did not buckle. You swallowed and the swallow went down. You drove and the steering responded. You spoke and the words came out in approximately the order you intended. You loved someone and they were still alive at the end of the conversation.

For each of these, there is a possibility — a perfectly real, perfectly available possibility — in which the same act could have ended in ruin. Knees do buckle. Swallows do enter the wrong tube. Steering does fail. Words do come out wrong. People do die mid-conversation.

*Why did they not, this time?*

The Quranic answer is not "because the system was working." The Quranic answer is: *yumsiku.* He was holding. Each of those non-disasters was a held grip.

If you take this seriously, every ordinary day is the result of a thousand silent acts of restraint, none of which were owed to you, none of which were guaranteed, none of which were caused by your worthiness.

#### Notice what this asks of you

There is a posture this opens.

In the ordinary frame, gratitude is reserved for noticeable gifts. Someone gives you something, you thank them. The roof of the world is not a gift in this sense. It is just there. So we do not think to thank for it.

In the Quranic frame, gratitude is a response not just to gifts, but to *withholdings*. To every moment that did not become disaster. To every collapse that was not permitted.

This is why the Quran connects *taḥmīd* — praise — with the realization of being held. The one who has noticed *yumsiku* cannot help but say *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh*. Praise be to the One who, while I was busy thinking I was self-sustaining, was holding the sky over my head.

#### Practical application

Tonight, before you sleep, name three things that did not go wrong today. Just three. Not three blessings — three *non-disasters*. Three things that, on a different day, in a different decree, could have unraveled, but did not.

Then, for each one, do not say "thank You for blessing me with this." Say something stranger. Say: *thank You for not letting go.*

You may find that this small reframe does something to your heart that no amount of generic gratitude prayer ever has.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Second Theme: The Two Faces of Mercy — What Is Bent Toward You, What Is Held Away From You

#### Begin in the everyday

There are two kinds of love a person experiences in their life.

There is the love that gives. The mother who feeds. The father who provides. The friend who shows up with the meal. This is the love we recognize and celebrate. It is mercy in the giving direction.

But there is another kind of love — quieter, almost invisible, and in many ways more difficult to give. It is the love that *withholds*. The parent who restrains themselves from saying the cutting thing during the argument. The friend who does not gossip when they could. The leader who chooses not to use the power they hold.

This is mercy in the *holding-back* direction.

We almost never thank people for this kind of mercy, because we never see it. The most we ever feel is the absence — the wound that did not happen, the betrayal that did not occur. And because the absence is invisible, we don't write it down on the list of things we owe gratitude for.

#### Let the ayah illuminate

Read the ayah again, and watch the structure.

*Sakhkhara lakum mā fī-l-arḍ* — He has bent the earth **toward** you.
*Wa-yumsiku-s-samāʾa* — and He is holding the sky **away from** you.

Two different directions. Two different verbs. Two different tenses. One mercy.

The first half of the ayah is mercy in its giving form. He bent things toward you. The earth's resources, the sea's pathways, the ships that cross it. These are gifts in motion. You feel them. You eat them. You ride on them. They are visible.

The second half of the ayah is mercy in its restraining form. He holds something *back*. The sky, with all its weight, with all its potential energy, is right there above you, and the only reason it is not on you is that He is keeping it from being on you.

You will never feel this mercy in the way you feel the first kind. You will never wake up and notice that the sky did not fall on you. You will simply wake up.

But the ayah is teaching you that both are mercies, and the unfelt one may be the larger one.

#### Sit with this

Now bring this back to your relationship with Allah.

Most of our spiritual life is shaped by the first kind of mercy. We notice the things we have. We notice when we get a new opportunity, a healing, a child, a livelihood. We thank Allah, and we should.

But we rarely notice the things we don't have. The illness that never came. The accident that did not happen. The slander that was never spoken about us in a room we never knew was full. The person who almost wronged us severely but, for reasons known only to Allah, changed their mind at the last second.

These are *yumsiku* mercies. Mercies of holding-back.

The Quran is, in a sense, training your eye to see them. Once you start noticing them, you cannot stop. You begin to suspect — and this is one of the marks of the people of *baṣīrah*, real spiritual sight — that the harms that did not happen vastly outnumber the goods that did.

The visible life is one small island in a sea of withheld catastrophes.

#### Deeper

Now consider what this means for the person walking through pain.

It is one of the great difficulties of faith: when something terrible happens, the heart says, *where was the mercy?* And the easy answer — that the mercy is somewhere on the other side, in the next life — can feel hollow. It can feel like a deferral.

The ayah offers something different.

It says: even on the day of your tragedy, look around. Look at all the parts of you that are still intact. All the parts of your world that did not also collapse. The same hand that allowed *this* to happen *held* a thousand other things. Even your tragedy is bordered by mercies.

This is not minimization. The Quran does not minimize human pain — it has whole surahs about the weight of it. But the Quran also will not let you forget that no event in the cosmos happens in isolation. Every calamity sits inside a vast field of withheld calamities. The hand that opened, on this one matter, did not open on the others.

To live with faith is, in part, to learn to see both: the wound, and the unwounded periphery.

#### Practical application

Pick a difficult area of your life — something where you feel Allah has withheld something good or allowed something hard. Do not minimize it. Do not pretend it doesn't hurt.

But once a week, for the next four weeks, sit with this question:

*In this same area of my life, what has He held back from happening?*

Not "what blessings has He given me elsewhere." That is a topic-change. Stay in the same area.

What further harm has not come? What deeper loss has been spared? What worse version of this story is not the version I am living?

If you cannot find anything, that is information. But most of the time, if you sit long enough, you will start to see the contours of *yumsiku* in your own life — the held grip you had not noticed because the grip never broke.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Look now at the verse with everything we have done in mind.

*A-lam tara* — Have you not seen.
*Sakhkhara lakum* — He bent toward you.
*Wa-yumsiku* — And He is holding back from you.
*Inna-Llāha bi-n-nāsi la-raʾūfun raḥīm* — Indeed He is to mankind Most Kind, Most Merciful.

This is a perfect cosmic chiasmus. The verse is built like a sandwich:

> **Have you not seen** — the question
>   **Sakhkhara** — what He gave (past tense, completed)
>     **The ships** — moving image, in between
>   **Yumsiku** — what He withholds (present tense, ongoing)
> **Most Kind, Most Merciful** — the answer

Notice what sits in the middle. The ships running through the sea. *Wa-l-fulka tajrī fī-l-baḥri bi-amrih.* This image is the hinge. Why?

Because the ship is the perfect image of the human condition under both verbs. The ship is *given* to you — the wood of the trees, the wind, the buoyancy of the water. Sakhkhara. But the ship is also a thing you cling to in the middle of an ocean that, at any moment, can rise up and swallow you. Yumsiku. The sea is also being held.

The believer's life is the ship. Made possible by what He bent toward you, and not destroyed by what He holds away from you.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The verbs — *sakhkhara* and *yumsiku* — are the grammatical bones of the two faces of mercy. One bends, one holds. The first is mercy as gift. The second is mercy as restraint.

The names — *raʾūf* and *raḥīm* — are the divine attributes that ground both. Raʾfah is the tenderness that gives. Raḥmah is the comprehensive mercy that includes the giving and the withholding.

The address — *bi-n-nās* — is the universality that says: this holding, this bending, applies to all of you, believer or not.

And the unspoken implication, the one the ayah does not state because it does not need to, is this: if He is doing this much for mankind without their belief — *bi-n-nās*, not *bi-l-muʾminīn* — then what is He prepared to do for the one who turns toward Him?

### Questions to Carry

1. What did not go wrong today, and have I noticed?
2. What does it change to see "the world is stable" replaced with "Allah is holding"?
3. Where in my life am I confusing a *withheld harm* for an *absence of mercy*?
4. If the sky is held by His permission, is there any "small" matter in my life beneath His attention? Or have I drawn that line myself?
5. The same hand that held the sky over me today — am I willing to let it hold the things I am most afraid of?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The cosmos is not a stable system you live inside; it is a continuous act of restraint by a hand whose default mode is mercy.

### The Closing Invitation

Most of your gratitude has been pointed at the visible gifts. That is good and right.

But there is a whole other field of mercy that you have probably never thanked Him for, because you cannot see it. The sky that did not fall. The disease that did not come. The grief that was deferred. The catastrophe that was permitted to remain catastrophe-shaped only in your imagination, never in your reality.

The invitation of this ayah is to learn to see *yumsiku*. To start noticing the held grip. To start including, in your prayers, the things that did not happen.

The day you can sincerely say *al-ḥamdu li-Llāh* not just for what He gave you, but for what He held back from happening to you, is the day this ayah has done its work.
