# Allah Is the Truth — and the Hour Is Coming, No Doubt in It

## Surah Al-Hajj 22:6-7

> ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلْحَقُّ وَأَنَّهُۥ يُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ وَأَنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ قَدِيرٌۭ
>
> وَأَنَّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ ءَاتِيَةٌۭ لَّا رَيْبَ فِيهَا وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَبْعَثُ مَن فِى ٱلْقُبُورِ

*Dhālika bi-anna Allāha huwa-l-Ḥaqq, wa-annahu yuḥyī-l-mawtā, wa-annahu ʿalā kulli shay'in Qadīr. Wa-anna-s-Sāʿata ātiyatun lā rayba fīhā, wa-anna-llāha yabʿathu man fī-l-qubūr.*

> "That is because Allah — He is the Truth, and because He gives life to the dead, and because He is over all things All-Powerful — and because the Hour is coming, no doubt in it, and because Allah will raise whoever is in the graves."

---

## INTRODUCTION

Stop for a moment and consider what just happened in the ayah before this one.

Allah took the listener on a journey. He started with a sperm drop — the smallest, most overlookable thing — and walked you through it becoming a clot, a chewed-looking lump, then bones, then flesh, then a person who is born, who grows, who reaches the peak of strength, who is taken back to the most fragile age "so that after knowledge he knows nothing." Then He pivoted — without warning — to the earth. The earth too: dry, dead, lifeless. Then the rain comes. And the earth shudders, swells, and brings out every kind of beautiful pair.

That entire journey — the womb, the body, the grave, the earth, the seasons — is one extended demonstration. One enormous proof, stretched across the human lifetime and the agricultural year.

And now, in 22:6, comes the conclusion.

The conclusion begins with a single word: **dhālika**. *That.*

That demonstration. That whole accumulated weight of evidence. That entire visual argument that began with a drop and ended with a flowering field — all of it is **because of something**. It was leading somewhere. It had a verdict.

The Quran is now going to deliver the verdict.

And the verdict is not one statement. It is five. Five declarative truths stacked on top of each other, each opened with the same emphatic particle, each presented not as a possibility but as a finished fact.

[PAUSE]

Here is what's stunning. The first listeners of this surah were people who looked at the same world we look at. They saw wombs, they saw graves, they saw rain, they saw spring. They had been seeing it their entire lives. The Quran is not adding new data. It is telling them: **you have been reading the text of creation without knowing what it says.** All of it has been pointing at one conclusion, and you've been missing the conclusion.

Today's listener has even more data. We have ultrasound, we have embryology, we have soil science, we have time-lapse footage of seeds bursting open. We see more than they saw. And we miss it more than they missed it.

So before we open these two ayahs, let me ask you the question they're going to answer:

If a God exists who can do all of *that* — the full chain from drop to person, from dust to garden — then what kind of being is He? And what is He preparing for next?

That is the question. The next two ayahs are the answer.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Pronoun That Won't Let You Hide — *huwa al-Ḥaqq*

Before we open the first declaration, we need to learn one feature of Arabic grammar. It will unlock the whole verse.

In English, when you want to say "Allah is the Truth," you put a verb between the subject and the predicate: *is*. In Arabic, you don't need that verb. You just say *Allāhu al-Ḥaqq* — Allah, the Truth — and the listener understands.

But Allah does not say it that way here.

He says: **Allāhu *huwa* al-Ḥaqq**.

He inserts a pronoun. *Huwa.* He.

"Allah, **He**, is the Truth."

In Arabic grammar this inserted pronoun has a name. It is called **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — *the pronoun of separation.* And it does a very specific job. It does not just connect subject to predicate. It **restricts** the predicate to the subject. It says: this attribute, in its full and absolute sense, belongs to *this one* and to no one else.

Let me give you an everyday example. Imagine someone tells you, "My brother is a doctor." Fine. Many people are doctors. But now imagine they say, "My brother — *he* is the doctor." That extra "he" — said with weight — communicates something different. It says: when it comes to *the* doctor, the one we have been looking for, the one who can actually treat this — it's him. Not anyone else.

That is the work *huwa* is doing here. Allah does not say "Allah is true" — many things are true in some sense. He says: "Allah, *He*, is the Truth." When it comes to *the* Real, *the* Actual, *the* one whose existence does not depend on anything else — it is Him. Only Him.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the word itself. **al-Ḥaqq.** With the definite article *al-*.

The root *ḥ-q-q* in Arabic carries the meaning of something being established, fixed, fitting, weighty, undeniable. It is the opposite of *bāṭil* — the empty, the void, the thing whose appearance exceeds its substance. *Ḥaqq* is what is genuinely there. *Bāṭil* is what looks like it's there but isn't.

When you put *al-* in front of it, you don't just have "truth." You have *the* Truth. *The* Real. The one whose reality is the source of every other reality.

Notice something. The Quran could have called Allah *al-Ṣādiq* — the Truthful, the One who tells the truth. That's a different word. *Ṣādiq* is about the correspondence between speech and fact. *Ḥaqq* is about something more fundamental. It is about *being itself*. It is the difference between "what He says is true" and "*He* is what is real."

Let's go deeper still.

Consider what it means for something to be real. You and I think of ourselves as real. The chair you're sitting on is real. The phone in your hand is real. We move through a world thick with realities.

But ask yourself — is your reality *necessary*? Could you not have existed? Could the chair have not been built? Could the phone not have been invented? The answer to all of these is yes. Your existence, the chair's existence, the phone's existence — none of these *had* to happen. They are real, but they are real in a borrowed way. They depend on something else for their reality. Your parents had to meet. The factory had to operate. The materials had to be mined. Cut any of those threads and your "real" thing simply isn't there.

There is a word for this kind of reality in classical metaphysics: *contingent*. Real, but contingently real. Real because something else made it real.

For there to be any contingent reality at all, philosophers and theologians from many traditions have noticed, there must be at least one *necessary* reality — one being whose existence does not depend on anything else. Otherwise the whole chain of contingencies has nothing to hang from. Like a chandelier with no ceiling.

That is what *al-Ḥaqq* names. The one Reality that is not borrowed. The one Being whose existence is not contingent on anything. The ceiling on which every other reality hangs.

And then the *huwa* — the pronoun of separation — closes the door. **He, and only He, is that.**

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, in his commentary, draws the antithesis that the passage context demands with considerable precision: everything they were worshipping besides Allah — the idols, the constructed gods — is *bāṭil*, and the proof is simple. Not one of them could do anything from what the preceding ayah described. Not one of them gave a single person a nervous system. Not one of them brought a dead field back to life. Their inability to create is the measure of their emptiness.

This is the negative side of *al-Ḥaqq* the passage requires. Allah does not claim to be the Real merely as a metaphysical assertion about Himself — He is delivering a *verdict* on everything else. The entire creative chain (womb, life, death, soil, rain, growth) was evidence. The verdict: only the One Being with the substance to account for all of it deserves the name.

And the classical commentators note that the name *al-Ḥaqq* here carries a specific dimension of worship alongside its ontological weight. Al-Muyassar reads it as: Allah is *al-Rabb al-maʿbūd bi-ḥaqq* — the Lord who is worshipped by right, the one for whom worship is constitutively fitting. The *ḥaqq* in that phrase is not merely "rightfully." It means: the fit between His nature and worship is as established, as fixed, as real as anything in existence. Directing worship elsewhere is not just a moral failure. It is a category error — a real act aimed at an empty object.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this means, practically.

Every time you've said "this is real" — about your job, your fears, your accomplishments, your suffering, your relationships, your nation, your bank account — you have been speaking about a contingent reality. A reality that exists only because something else is sustaining it. A reality that, traced back far enough, hangs from the only Real that doesn't hang from anything.

Allah is not one real thing among many. He is not the most important real thing in a list of real things. **He is the source of the realness of every real thing.** Every other reality is a leaf. He is the ground.

That is the first declaration.

### Element Two: The Stack of Five — Why Allah Repeats *anna* Five Times

Now look at the structure of these two ayahs as a whole. Read them again, this time listening for a single word.

> *bi-**anna** Allāha huwa-l-Ḥaqq*
> *wa-**anna**hu yuḥyī-l-mawtā*
> *wa-**anna**hu ʿalā kulli shay'in Qadīr*
> *wa-**anna**-s-Sāʿata ātiyatun lā rayba fīhā*
> *wa-**anna**-llāha yabʿathu man fī-l-qubūr*

Five clauses. Each one opened with **anna** — the Arabic particle of emphatic assertion, meaning "that." It is not a casual word. In Arabic grammar, when you want to assert something as a settled fact — not a possibility, not a likelihood, not your opinion, but a *fact* — you open with *anna*. The first clause is preceded by *bi-*, which is where the causal force enters: *because that*. The four clauses that follow are joined by *wa-*, continuing that grammatical weight through the chain. The "because" belongs to the *bi-*, not to *anna* itself — *anna* simply hammers each clause shut as certain.

It is the difference between saying "I think the meeting is at three" and saying "*the meeting is, in fact, at three.*" The second has a different structural weight. It is closing a question, not opening one.

Five times. *Anna. Anna. Anna. Anna. Anna.*

[PAUSE]

Notice that this is not the Quran arguing. The Quran has *just finished* arguing — the entire previous ayah was argument. Look at what We made you out of. Look at what We do to the earth. Look at the chain. The argument is finished. What follows now is the **verdict**.

This is a courtroom moment. The evidence has been presented. The judge stands and reads the findings.

> *Finding one*: Allah, He, is the Truth.
> *Finding two*: He gives life to the dead.
> *Finding three*: He has power over every single thing.
> *Finding four*: The Hour is coming. No doubt in it.
> *Finding five*: He will raise whoever is in the graves.

Each finding is anchored with *anna* — the linguistic equivalent of pounding the gavel. *This is so. This is so. This is so. This is so. This is so.*

Stay with the architecture. Notice what the five findings actually do.

The first three (in 22:6) describe **who Allah is**. He is the Real. He is the Reviver. He is the All-Powerful.

The last two (in 22:7) describe **what therefore is true about your future**. The Hour is coming. The dead are getting up.

Do you see what just happened?

The metaphysics of who Allah is — His Realness, His life-giving, His power — *necessarily* produce the eschatology of what is coming. If He is al-Ḥaqq, then His promises are real. If He gives life to the dead, then resurrection is not a stretch. If He is over every single thing All-Powerful, then nothing in the resurrection is beyond Him.

The Hour is coming **because of who He is**.

That is what the five *anna*s are doing. Each one is a column. Together they hold up a building. And the building is your future.

Notice also the literary movement within the last two clauses. The fourth makes a vast cosmic announcement: the Hour is coming. The fifth immediately descends to the particular — not "the dead will rise" in the abstract, but *whoever is in the graves*. Every person. Each name. Every buried body. The fifth clause is not a rephrasing of the fourth. It is the *specification* that makes the fourth concrete. Al-Tabari reads the two together as a deliberate movement: from the announcement of the great event to the naming of its mechanism — the graves open, the dispatched go forth. The announcement lands. You know what it means.

### Element Three: The Doubt That Lives in You, Not in It — *lā rayba fīhā*

Look at the fourth declaration. It contains a phrase you may recognize.

> *wa-anna-s-Sāʿata ātiyatun **lā rayba fīhā***
>
> "And the Hour is coming — **no doubt in it**."

Anyone who has read Surat al-Baqarah will hear an echo. The opening of al-Baqarah, after the disconnected letters, reads: *Dhālika al-Kitābu lā rayba fīh* — "That is the Book, no doubt in it." Same construction. Same key phrase.

Let's open it carefully.

*Rayb* in Arabic is not just *doubt* in the casual sense. *Rayb* is uneasy doubt. Doubt mixed with suspicion, with discomfort, with the feeling that something is off. It is the kind of doubt that makes you turn the thing over in your hand and squint. *Shakk* is intellectual doubt — uncertainty about whether something is true. *Rayb* is more visceral.

So Allah is not just saying "the Hour is undoubted." He is saying: "the Hour is not the kind of thing that produces uneasy suspicion *in itself*." The Hour, considered properly, is not a flickering candle of a claim. It is steady.

But that is not the deepest thing here.

Look at the preposition. **Lā rayba *fīhā***. *No doubt **in** it.*

Stay with the preposition for a moment. What does it mean to locate doubt *inside* a thing?

In English we usually treat doubt as something *we* have *about* a thing. "I have doubts about this plan." The doubt belongs to me. It is in my head, in my chest. It is something I bring to the object.

The Quran's grammar suggests something subtle and devastating. It says: there is no doubt **in it**. Inside the Hour itself. The doubt is not located there.

[PAUSE]

Here's an analogy. Think of the sun. People can deny the sun exists. They can shut their windows. They can refuse to look up. They can convince themselves that the light filling the room is from some other source. They can argue, demand evidence, mock the believers in the sun. None of that affects the sun. The sun is not less bright because of their denial. The doubt is in *them*, not in the sun. The sun is **lā rayba fīhā** — there is nothing inside it that warrants doubt.

The Hour is the same. Whether or not human beings are uncertain about it, whether or not they argue, whether or not they have nightmares about it or laugh about it on their podcasts — none of that touches the Hour. The Hour does not contain any feature, any property, any internal weakness, that justifies doubt about it.

If you have doubts about the Hour, the Quran is saying very gently — **the doubt is in you.** Inspect *yourself*. Don't inspect *it*.

This is one of the most important pivots in Quranic epistemology, and what it demands is not more argumentation but a different kind of looking. The Quran is not inviting you to debate the Hour. It is inviting you to ask: what is it in *me* that makes this feel uncertain? What attachment, what investment, what fear of implications is producing the fog?

Al-Tabari adds a reading of the active participle *ātiyatun* that deepens this further. He glosses the Hour's coming as *lā maḥāla* — *without fail*, inevitably, with no conceivable path around it. The participle does not carry the feel of a future event that depends on conditions being met. It carries the feel of a thing already set in motion, whose arrival is as fixed as anything that has ever arrived. Not "the Hour might come" or even "the Hour will come" — but the Hour is a thing that cannot *not* come.

[PAUSE]

And this is what makes *lā rayba fīhā* so arresting when you hear it against that backdrop. If the Hour is *lā maḥāla* — without fail — then doubt about it is not neutral intellectual uncertainty. It is a misreading of the structure of existence itself. The question is not "will the Hour come?" — *ātiyatun* closed that question. The question is only: how are you going to meet it?

### Element Four: The Specification That Makes It Personal — *yabʿathu man fī-l-qubūr*

The fifth and final clause is the one most likely to be read quickly.

> *wa-anna-llāha yabʿathu man fī-l-qubūr*
>
> "And that Allah will raise whoever is in the graves."

After the metaphysical architecture of clauses one through three, and after the cosmic announcement of clause four — the Hour is coming, inevitably, without fail — this final clause might feel like a footnote. A rephrasing. A closing summary.

It is not.

Look at the verb. **Yabʿathu.** The root *b-ʿ-th* in Arabic means to send, to dispatch, to set in motion from a state of rest. It carries the image of something lying still and then being activated, sent out, propelled. This is not a word for gradual awakening. It is the word for a sudden, directed dispatch — something or someone sent from one state toward another.

And who is dispatched? **Man fī-l-qubūr.** *Whoever is in the graves.* Not "the dead" in the abstract. The Arabic is precise: whoever is *in* them. Present tense. Right now. In them. People who are, at this moment, buried.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the movement of the camera?

Clause four pulled back to the horizon: the Hour, the final cosmic event, vast and structurally inevitable. Clause five zooms in: to the soil. To the graves you have walked past. To the bodies in them. *Those* people. *Each* of them. Dispatched.

Al-Muyassar notes that this raising is purposive — for *ḥisāb wa-jazāʾ*, for reckoning and recompense. The word *baʿth* is not merely a biological event, a body waking up. It is a sending *toward* something. Al-Tabari specifies: toward the station of reckoning. Every person raised is raised *toward* an accounting — toward the resolution of every unresolved story, every injustice that went unnamed, every suffering that ended in the dirt before its explanation arrived.

Think about what this means. You have known people who died without justice. Every culture, every generation, has them — the ones who suffered and were not heard, the ones who were wronged and no one knew, the ones whose lives ended with loose threads hanging. The Quran is not offering you a vague hope that "it will somehow work out." It is telling you: there is a mechanism. There is a verb — *yabʿathu* — that describes exactly what will happen. The graves will release their contents. The station of reckoning is where they are being sent. Nothing stays buried.

[PAUSE]

Notice one more thing: the agent of this clause is not the Hour. It is **Allah**. *Wa-anna-llāha yabʿathu.* "And that *Allah* raises." The Hour in clause four was described without a named divine agent — it is simply *coming*, like a weather front arriving. But in clause five the divine name returns: *Allāh*. He, specifically, is doing the raising. This is not an impersonal cosmic mechanism that carries the dead upward like a tide. This is a sovereign act — *al-Ḥaqq*, the same name the passage opened with, exercising His power over the specific, named contents of specific, located graves.

The circle closes. The one who is the Real raises the ones who were buried. His Realness guarantees their resurrection.

## PART 2: WHAT YOUR BODY KNOWS

There is a reason this passage is addressed to the whole of humanity — *yā ayyuha-n-nās* at 22:5 — and not to believers alone.

Because every human being has a body that was made from dust, will return to dust, and has been watching the earth do exactly what the Quran describes, season after season, since the first breath. The rain comes. The dead field responds. You have seen this. Some part of you already knows.

The five *anna*s of 22:6–7 are not asking your intellect to accept a proposition it has never encountered. They are asking your intellect to *catch up* to what your whole life has been showing you.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari makes the structure of the argument explicit: all of what was presented in 22:5 — the womb, the stages of life, the barren earth and the rain — was placed before you so that you would believe and affirm that the One who did it is al-Ḥaqq, and so that you would know that the power which brought those wonders into being cannot possibly be hindered by the task of raising the dead after their dissolution in the earth. The argument is not from scripture to belief. It is from existence to recognition.

You live inside the proof. Every birth you have witnessed is a proof. Every spring is a proof. Every scar that healed, every hunger that was met, every breath that continued after you stopped thinking about breathing — each of these is one more column, one more *anna*, asserting that Something is operating here that is not *bāṭil*, not empty, not borrowed from anywhere.

The invitation of these two ayahs is this: let the five verdicts land. Not as information to be filed. As realities that carry weight.

He is the Truth — the only non-borrowed Real.

He gives life to the dead — and you have watched Him do it in the earth every year.

He is over every single thing All-Powerful — nothing in the resurrection exceeds Him.

The Hour is coming — inevitably, without fail, *lā maḥāla*.

He will raise whoever is in the graves — each one, toward reckoning and recompense, by name, by sovereign act.

## DISTILLATION

**Three things to carry:**

The *ḍamīr al-faṣl* — the pronoun *huwa* inserted between Allah and al-Ḥaqq — does not just connect. It restricts. It closes the door on every other candidate. When it comes to *the* Real, the one whose existence is the source of every other existence, the one before whom everything else is either *ḥaqq* (borrowed) or *bāṭil* (empty) — it is this One. Every object you have ever treated as ultimate was real only in a contingent, borrowed way. He alone is the original.

The *lā rayba fīhā* on the Hour does not say "no one doubts it." It says the doubt is not *in it*. The Hour does not carry the burden of your skepticism. If you find the Hour uncertain, the uncertainty is in you, not in the coming event. That is a different kind of question to carry — not *is it real?* but *what in me makes it feel distant?*

The fifth *anna* — *yabʿathu man fī-l-qubūr* — is not a rephrasing of the fourth. It is the specification that makes the cosmic announcement concrete. The verb *baʿth* means dispatched from stillness — sent toward reckoning and recompense. Every person in every grave is being dispatched toward an accounting. Nothing stays buried.

## DU'A

*Allāhumma* — You are al-Ḥaqq. Let every reality I pursue be tested against whether it traces back to You. Loosen my grip on the things that are *bāṭil* — the things I have treated as real that depend on nothing more solid than my own need.

Grant me the courage to carry the certainty of the Hour not as a weight that paralyzes but as a compass that orients. And when doubt arises — about the Hour, about resurrection, about Your promise — let me ask the right question: not *is it real?* but *what in me makes it feel far?*

And let me never forget the graves. The people in them. The unresolved stories. *Wa-anna-llāha yabʿathu man fī-l-qubūr.* You will raise each one. You will dispatch them toward Your justice. Nothing stays buried.

*Āmīn.*
