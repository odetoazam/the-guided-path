# Two Guidances — The Hidden Reward Beneath the Gold and Silk of Paradise

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُدْخِلُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ جَنَّـٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ يُحَلَّوْنَ فِيهَا مِنْ أَسَاوِرَ مِن ذَهَبٍ وَلُؤْلُؤًۭا ۖ وَلِبَاسُهُمْ فِيهَا حَرِيرٌۭ

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before the Ayah

To enter these two ayahs you have to first walk through the room they are sitting next to.

Just before this — only four ayahs before — Allah described another scene. Not gardens. A different kind of room. Two parties at war over their Lord. For one party: garments cut for them out of fire. Boiling water poured down from above their heads, melting whatever is in their bellies and their skins. Hooks of iron. Every time they try to escape from the grief, they are returned. *Taste the punishment of the Burning.*

You are still feeling the heat of that scene when Allah says one word — **inna** — *indeed* — and turns the camera.

The same surah. The same page. But suddenly you are inside a garden. Rivers flow beneath. Bracelets of gold and pearls. Silk on the body. The two scenes are placed side by side on purpose. The Quran is not painting two pictures for variety. It is showing you a fork.

And then — when your eyes are still adjusting to the brightness, when you are still cataloguing the gold and the pearls and the silk — Allah says one more thing. One sentence. So quiet you could miss it if you read fast.

*And they were guided to the good of speech, and they were guided to the path of the Praiseworthy.*

### The Hook

[PAUSE]

Stay with me on this. The people of paradise have already arrived. They are inside the gardens. The bracelets are already on their wrists. The silk is already on their bodies.

So why — at the moment of arrival — does Allah mention guidance?

Guidance is for someone walking. Someone choosing. Someone still on the road. These people are no longer on the road. They are home.

Here a note from the classical commentators is essential to hold alongside this question. *Hudū* — the past passive — in the dominant reading is pointing backward, not inward. Al-Tabari's gloss is explicit: *their Lord guided them in this life* — in the dunya, before any garden gate — to the testimony that there is no god but Allah. The guidance was the cause; paradise is the destination it delivered them to. The verb looks behind.

Yet al-Muyassar opens a second fold the structure permits: in the akhirah, they are also guided — to *praising Allah for the good outcome,* to seeing, without distortion, where all that halting dunya-guidance was aimed all along. The thread does not snap at the gate; it simply becomes transparent, visible from one end to the other all at once. Both readings are permitted; the classical majority reads the cause completed in dunya, and what follows reads what the arrival reveals about that cause.

What is Allah showing them — and us — in this final line that comes *after* the gold?

This is what we are going to sit with for the next hour. Because what Allah does in this small sentence is something I want to suggest most of us have never noticed. He places, at the end of paradise, a mirror. And what He shows His servants in that mirror is the answer to a question they have been carrying their entire lives — even though they may not have known they were carrying it.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to look at three things in this passage. Three small grammatical features. Each of them is doing more than you would imagine.

### Element One: The Voice That Tells the Story

Before we open the ayah, I want to teach you something about Arabic — and really about every language.

In any language, there is a difference between saying *I built this house* and *this house was built for me*. The first centers you. The second centers the house. The grammar is doing different psychological work.

Arabic, like English, has two voices. *Al-mabnī lil-maʿlūm* — built upon the known — what we call **active voice**. The doer is named. *Allah admits them.* And *al-mabnī lil-majhūl* — built upon the unknown, where the doer is veiled. The **passive voice**. *They are admitted.*

When the Quran chooses one over the other, it is never accidental. The choice of voice is the choice of where to point the light.

Now look with me at this passage. Read it again slowly and watch the verbs.

> *Inna-Llāha **yudkhilu**...* — "Indeed Allah **admits**..."

Allah names Himself. Active voice. *Allah is the One who admits them.*

Then —

> ***yuḥallawna** fīhā...* — "**They are adorned** therein..."

Did you catch the shift? In one breath the voice has changed. The verb *yuḥallawna* is **passive**. The form is *yuḥalla-* with the *u-a* vowel pattern that marks the passive. They are not adorning themselves. *They are being adorned.* By whom? Allah does not say. The agent is veiled.

And then the next ayah —

> ***wa hudū** ilā-ṭ-ṭayyibi mina-l-qawli, **wa hudū** ilā ṣirāṭi-l-ḥamīd.*
>
> "And **they were guided** to the good of speech, and **they were guided** to the path of the Praiseworthy."

Passive again. Twice. *Hudū* — they were guided. They did not guide themselves. They were guided. And again — they were guided.

Notice something. **Allah is named once, at the very beginning.** "Indeed Allah admits..." After that, every action that happens to the people of paradise — being adorned, being guided, being guided again — is in the passive. The doer is hidden. The doer is the same doer named at the start. But His name is now under a veil.

[PAUSE]

Why?

Here is what I want to suggest. In dunya, the believer is constantly being told *you must do.* Pray. Fast. Give. Strive. Walk. The grammar of dunya is **active.** *You did this. You earned that. You must do more.* And this is correct — this is how the road works.

But the believer who walks that road faithfully often arrives at a private fear. *Did I really do enough? Was my prayer accepted? Was my striving sincere?* The active voice of dunya tells you *you did,* but it can never tell you *whether what you did was enough.*

Then comes paradise. And the moment you enter, Allah switches the voice.

You are not adorned because you adorned yourself. **You are adorned.**
You are not guided because you guided yourself. **You were guided.**

The one who is doing the adorning, the one who did the guiding — He is the same One named once at the gate. *Inna-Llāh.* But once you are inside, He places His hand under the silk. Under the bracelets. Under your feet on the path. He is the doer of every blessing, but He hides Himself under the passive voice — so that the entire room can fill, instead, with the awareness of *receiving.*

This is what I mean. The passive voice in paradise is not a grammatical accident. It is a **theological gift.** Allah is allowing the people of paradise to feel, in their bones, that everything they have was given. Nothing earned. Nothing self-made. *They were adorned. They were guided. They were guided again.*

And here is the strange thing. This is not a punishment. The recognition that everything was a gift is itself the deepest joy of paradise. Because it means: there is no fear left of losing it through inadequacy. *I did not put myself here. The One who put me here is the One who keeps me here.*

And the classical reading deepens this still further: al-Tabari's *hudū* points to a sentence that was already being completed in the dunya, not a new instruction announced at the garden entrance. What the passive voice is therefore doing is marking the moment of recognition — that every step was guided, including the ones that felt like wandering. Every time a believer returned to prayer when they did not feel like praying, every time they said *lā ilāha illa-Llāh* in exhaustion or doubt — that was not self-navigation. That was being led. Paradise is where the full trajectory becomes visible, from the first trembling testimony to this last arrival. *They were guided.* Past tense. Completed. And the completion is standing here.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Hold that. We will return to it. There is more.

### Element Two: The Strange Construction "The Good *Of* Speech"

Look at this phrase with me:

> *al-ṭayyibi **mina**-l-qawl* — "the good **of** the speech"

In English you would expect *good speech.* Adjective + noun. Simple.

But Arabic does not say that. It says **al-ṭayyibi mina-l-qawl** — *the good [portion] of speech.* This is what grammarians call the **partitive *min.*** It separates one part from a larger whole. Like saying *the gold of the earth* — implying earth has many things in it, but you are pointing to the gold specifically.

So this is not "good speech." This is **THE pure portion, drawn out, from among all speech.**

Sit with that.

The Quran is implicitly acknowledging — *most speech is not this.* Most of what comes out of human mouths is not this. There is speech that wounds. Speech that gossips. Speech that boasts. Speech that lies. Speech that fills time. Speech that performs. There is even, the Quran reminds us elsewhere in this very surah, *qawl al-zūr* — false speech (22:30).

Out of this entire ocean of human talking, there is one stream. **Al-ṭayyib min al-qawl.** The pure portion. The fragrant portion. The wholesome portion. The root *ṭ-y-b* in Arabic does not just mean *good* — it means *pure, fragrant, wholesome,* the kind of fruit you can eat without fear, the kind of perfume that doesn't fade.

And the people of paradise — Allah is telling us — *were guided to that one stream.*

Here is the question. **What is *al-ṭayyib min al-qawl*?** What is this purest portion of speech that they were guided to?

The classical commentators give you several pictures, and I want to put them together as one composite — not a multiple choice. Each is a camera angle.

The first angle: *lā ilāha illa-Llāh.* The testimony. There is no god but Allah. The Prophet ﷺ called this *aḥsanu mā qultu anā wa-l-nabiyyūna min qablī* — the best of what I and the prophets before me have said. Out of all human speech across all the centuries, **the purest sentence ever spoken** is *lā ilāha illa-Llāh.* They were guided to that sentence.

Al-Tabari's chain through Ibn Zaid expands what *al-ṭayyib min al-qawl* holds: not *lā ilāha illa-Llāh* alone, but *Allāhu akbar* and *al-ḥamdu lillāh* alongside it — three pillars standing together, the tongue's full posture of submission. And Ibn Zaid ties this to the Quran's own image of where the good word travels: *"To Him ascends the good word, and righteous deeds raise it"* (35:10). The ṭayyib word does not merely leave the mouth — it rises. And it is the deeds that accompany it that give it its lift. The tongue and the limbs are doing the same work.

The second angle: *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* Praise be to Allah. The first speech of the people of paradise, as we are told elsewhere, is *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* They were guided to a speech whose entire orientation is upward — toward the Giver, away from the self.

The third angle: the Quran itself. *Al-qawl* in some readings refers to revelation, to scripture, to the speech of Allah. They were guided to recognize, among all the books and all the words of humans, the pure portion — the words that came from above.

Each angle is true. The composite is this: in dunya, the believer is constantly sifting. They are inside an ocean of words — their own words, others' words, the noise of opinions and ideologies and gossip and content — and they are trying to find the stream that is pure. They are trying to find the sentence that is true. They make mistakes. They speak words they regret. They listen to words that poison. **But underneath all of it, they were being guided.** Toward one sentence. Toward one praise. Toward one Book.

And on the day they enter paradise, Allah lets them see it. *You were not just speaking. You were being led, by your speech, here.*

Al-Muyassar captures one more dimension: in the akhirah, the same guidance that carried them through dunya turns their tongues toward *praising Allah for the good outcome* — toward seeing, clearly and completely, where all that dunya-speech was aimed. The first words of the people of paradise, as 10:10 tells us, are *al-ḥamdu lillāh Rabb al-ʿālamīn.* They were guided to this speech in dunya, haltingly and imperfectly, without yet knowing where it was pointing. They arrive and find it is the only speech left. The journey ends where the tongue was always rehearsing.

[PAUSE]

This is what the partitive *min* is doing. It is honoring the fact that **most speech is not pure** — and praising the believer not for never speaking the impure, but for being guided, again and again, **back** to the pure portion. The story of their tongue was the story of return. And they were guided.

### Element Three: Why "Path of the Praiseworthy" — and Not Another Name?

Now the third feature. The one that, when you see it, seals the architecture.

> *wa hudū ilā ṣirāṭi-**l-ḥamīd*** — "and they were guided to the path of **the Praiseworthy.**"

I want you to notice the name. Allah has ninety-nine names. Each one is precise. Each one is chosen for the moment it is invoked. So when Allah ends this passage — when He places one name at the destination of the path — He is making a choice.

He could have said *ṣirāṭi-l-Raḥmān* — the path of the Most Merciful. We would have heard mercy at the destination.

He could have said *ṣirāṭi-l-Karīm* — the path of the Generous. We would have heard generosity at the destination.

He could have said *ṣirāṭi-l-Ghafūr* — the path of the Forgiving. We would have heard forgiveness at the destination.

He says — *ṣirāṭi-l-**Ḥamīd**.* The path of the Praiseworthy. The One worthy of all praise.

Why this name?

Look back. *Wa hudū ilā-l-**ṭayyibi mina-l-qawl*** — they were guided to the good of speech. *Wa hudū ilā ṣirāṭi-l-**Ḥamīd*** — and they were guided to the path of the Praiseworthy.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it?

The two ayahs are knit together by a thread you only notice when you stand back. The first guidance is **a speech.** The second guidance is **a path.** What is the purest speech? *Al-ḥamdu lillāh.* All praise belongs to Allah. And what is the destination of the path? *Al-Ḥamīd.* The Praised. The Praiseworthy.

**The speech they were guided to and the destination of the path they were placed upon — they are the same point.**

Their tongues were saying *al-ḥamdu lillāh* in dunya, often without fully understanding what they were saying. They were being placed on a path in dunya, often without seeing where it led. And on the day of arrival, Allah lets them see: every time you said *al-ḥamd,* the path you were on was bending you toward the One whose name is *al-Ḥamīd.* Your speech was rehearsing your destination. Your destination was the home your speech was always pointing to.

Al-Muyassar makes this architecture explicit: *ṭarīq al-Islām al-maḥmūd al-muwaṣṣil ilā-l-jannah* — the praised path of Islam that delivers to paradise. The name *al-Ḥamīd* at the path's end and the *ḥamd* on the tongue at the path's beginning are not coincidence. They are the design of a journey that was praised at every point: the walker praised Allah, and the destination is the Praiseworthy Himself.
