# The Body Language of Empty Argument
## Surah Al-Hajj 8-9

> [ayah:22:8] وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ مَن يُجَـٰدِلُ فِى ٱللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَلَا هُدًى وَلَا كِتَـٰبٍ مُّنِيرٍ
> [ayah:22:9] ثَانِىَ عِطْفِهِۦ لِيُضِلَّ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ لَهُۥ فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا خِزْىٌ ۖ وَنُذِيقُهُۥ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ عَذَابَ ٱلْحَرِيقِ

*Wa min al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr. Thāniya ʿiṭfihi li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīl Allāh. Lahu fī al-dunyā khizyun wa-nudhīquhu yawma al-qiyāmati ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq.*

"And among the people is one who disputes about Allah without knowledge, without guidance, and without an illuminating Book — twisting his side [in arrogance] to mislead [others] from the path of Allah. For him in this world is disgrace, and We will make him taste on the Day of Resurrection the punishment of the Burning."

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Behind These Words

You are in Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling for years, and a particular kind of opposition has emerged. It is not the opposition of the openly hostile soldier — that comes later. It is the opposition of the salon. The opposition of the man who stands in the marketplace and gathers a circle around himself and starts asking questions. Loud questions. Confident questions.

He is not asking to learn. He is asking to perform. He has no answers — but he has a posture. He has a way of turning his neck. He has a way of dismissing without engaging. And he is influential. Other men listen to him because he sounds certain, and certainty in a marketplace is a currency.

This man — and we know from the early commentators that there were specific historical figures behind this verse, men like al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith — would dispute about Allah. About resurrection. About prophethood. He had no scripture. He had no inner illumination. He had no knowledge. But he had volume, and he had body language, and he had followers.

And Allah, watching from above seven heavens, names him. Not by his name. By his anatomy.

### The Strand This Ayah Sits Inside

Earlier in this surah — at ayah 3 — Allah described another figure: *"And among the people is one who disputes about Allah without knowledge and follows every rebellious devil."* That was the follower. That was the disciple of doubt. He disputes, but he does so because he is being led.

Now, five ayahs later, Allah returns to the same opening — *wa min al-nās* — but completes the picture differently. This time it is not the follower. This time it is the one who **leads others into the disputation**. The recruiter. The architect of doubt.

Same disease. Two different roles. Surah Al-Hajj is taking us behind the scenes of unbelief and showing us its sociology. There are foot soldiers and there are generals. And in these two ayahs we meet the general.

### The Hook

Allah is about to name this man. But notice what He uses to identify him. Not his ideas. Not his arguments. Not his school of thought.

His posture.

The way he holds his neck.

[PAUSE]

There is something almost shocking about this. The Quran — a book of cosmic scope, dealing with the origin of the universe and the end of time — pauses to describe the angle of a man's shoulder. Why?

Stay with that question. We will return to it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Three Empty Hands — *bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa-lā hudan wa-lā kitābin munīr*

Before we open this phrase, let's teach a small grammatical concept.

In Arabic, when you want to negate one thing, you say *bi-ghayr* — "without." When you want to add another negated thing to it, you have a choice. You can simply repeat *bi-ghayr*. Or you can say *wa-lā*, which carries an additional sense: "and not even this either."

The repetition of *wa-lā* is emphatic. Each *wa-lā* hits like a separate blow. It's the difference between saying "he had no money or food or shelter" and saying "he had no money — and no food — and no shelter." The second version makes you feel each absence individually.

Now look at how Allah builds the description of this man:

> *bi-ghayri **ʿilmin** wa-lā **hudan** wa-lā **kitābin munīr***
> *without **knowledge**, and not **guidance**, and not an **illuminating Book***

Three negations. Three separate blows. He is empty-handed three times over.

Notice something. Allah could have said simply, *"He disputes about Allah without knowledge."* That would have been enough. But Allah instead opens up the category of "knowledge" into three distinct sources of evidence and shows us that this man has none of them.

Let's sit with what these three are.

**ʿIlm** is the first — knowledge. In its broadest sense, this is what a person can come to through reason, observation, careful thinking. The accumulated certainties of a thinking mind. He has no *ʿilm* — no rationally established conclusions. He has not done the work of thinking carefully.

**Hudan** is the second — guidance. This is something different from *ʿilm*. *Hudan* is what comes from inside. It is the inner light that orients a person — the fiṭrah pointing the needle of the heart toward the qiblah of truth. *Hudan* is the experiential knowing that does not need to be argued for because it is already lived. He has no *hudā* — no inner orientation. The compass inside him is dead or covered.

**Kitāb munīr** is the third — and notice the adjective. Not just *kitāb*. Not just "a book." But *munīr* — an **illuminating** book. *Munīr* comes from *n-w-r*, which is light. It is form IV — the active participle of "to give light." A book that does not just sit there, but emits.

Why specify *munīr*? Because there are books that don't illuminate. There are pages that contain sentences but produce no light. By saying *kitāb munīr*, Allah signals: he doesn't even have a borrowed light — not a single revealed source he could be deriving his disputation from.

So now stand back. Look at the architecture.

He has no rational knowledge. ʿIlm — gone.
He has no inner guidance. Hudan — gone.
He has no revealed scripture. Kitāb munīr — gone.

Three empty hands. And yet he is disputing.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. Allah is showing you the structure of evidence itself. There are three sources from which a human being can legitimately speak about ultimate reality: what you have figured out by careful thought, what you have come to know through inner orientation, and what has been revealed to you by the One who actually knows. These three doors. And this man stands outside all three of them — and still talks.

Notice also: Allah did not say he has *bad* knowledge or *false* guidance or a *corrupted* book. He simply says he has nothing at all. The most dangerous disputants of religion, the Quran is teaching us, are not those who hold a competing system. They are those who hold no system at all and are simply performing.

What this invites us to ask: when I dispute about Allah — even quietly, even in my own head — which of these three doors am I standing inside? Or am I standing outside all three of them, like this man, and merely performing?

### Element Two: *Thāniya ʿIṭfihi* — The Anatomy of Arrogance

Now we come to the phrase that should make us stop the whole reflection.

> *thāniya ʿiṭfihi*
> *twisting his side / bending his neck*

Let me explain what this phrase actually says in Arabic, because it is one of the most precise pieces of bodily description in the entire Quran.

*Thāniya* is the active participle of *th-n-y* — to fold, to bend, to turn aside. This is the same root from which we get *ithnayn* — "two," because two is what you get when you fold one back on itself. *Thāniya* describes the action of bending or folding something away from its natural straight position.

*ʿIṭf* — and now listen carefully — is the side of the body, specifically the area between the shoulder and the neck. The shoulder-neck region. The classical lexicons say it is the part that turns when you turn your head with attitude. It is not just the neck (*ʿunuq*). It is the *ʿiṭf* — the part that swings when you swivel your shoulder away from someone you are dismissing.

So *thāniya ʿiṭfihi* is, in the most literal anatomical sense: **the bending of his shoulder-neck axis**.

Now ask yourself — when does a human being do this physically?

Picture it. Picture a man being told something he does not want to hear. He doesn't argue. He doesn't refute. He just turns. The shoulder pivots. The neck folds. The face is now in profile, half-turned away, no longer engaging.

That is *thāniya ʿiṭfihi*.

[PAUSE]

You have seen this. Every single one of us has seen this. It is the body language of dismissal. It is the gesture of the one who refuses to face. It is the posture of pride.

Now — here is what is remarkable. Allah, in describing the spiritual condition of this man, **did not describe his beliefs**. He described his neck.

Stay with this.

Why? Why does the body get used to diagnose the soul?

Because the body cannot lie the way the mouth can.

The mouth can claim humility. The mouth can say "I am open to evidence" while the *ʿiṭf* is already turning. The mouth can say "I am just asking questions" while the shoulder is already pivoting away from the answer. The body knows what the heart actually believes — and Allah, who created both, names the heart's reality through the body's gesture.

There is a parallel in Surah Luqmān, ayah 18: *"wa-lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka lil-nās"* — "do not turn your cheek aside from people in arrogance." Same motif. Same anatomical precision. The Quran is consistent: arrogance is not a mood. **Arrogance is a posture.**

Notice something else. The phrase appears as a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial clause describing the state of the disputant. He is *yujādilu* — disputing — *while* his side is twisted. The disputation and the twisted neck are happening at the same time. They are the same act seen from inside and outside.

Inside: he is disputing.
Outside: his neck is folded away.

The disputation IS the twisting of the neck. The intellectual posture and the bodily posture are one motion.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this invites us to consider. Real engagement with truth requires the body to be straight. Face forward. Neck unfolded. Shoulders square to the speaker. When you are willing to be persuaded, your body shows it. You lean in. You face the source.

When you are not willing — when you have already decided, when you are only here to perform certainty — your body shows that too. You turn. You fold. You half-listen.

The Quran is teaching us, with anatomical precision, that to dispute about Allah requires a *bent neck*. You cannot dispute about Allah while standing straight and facing forward. Anyone who is genuinely facing forward — genuinely open — does not end up disputing. They end up **inquiring**. They end up asking. They end up listening.

Disputation is a body posture before it is a position.

### Element Three: *Li-yuḍilla* — The Recruitment of Doubt

The third element is short but devastating. After describing the man's empty hands and his twisted neck, Allah names his purpose:

> *li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīl Allāh*
> *to mislead [others] from the path of Allah*

Let's open the verb *yuḍilla*.

The root is *ḍ-l-l* — to be lost, to stray, to wander off the path. In its basic form (Form I), *ḍalla* means simply "he went astray." That would describe someone who is lost himself.

But this verb is **Form IV** — *aḍalla / yuḍillu*. Form IV in Arabic is the **causative** form. It is the form that says: not only does the subject do the action, but he *makes someone else* do it. *Aḍalla* doesn't just mean "he went astray." It means "**he caused others to go astray.**"

This is a critical distinction. In ayah 3 of this same surah, the figure who disputes is described as one who *follows* — *yattabiʿu*. He is led. In ayah 8, the figure disputes *li-yuḍilla* — to **lead others astray**.

Same disease. Two different roles. The follower and the recruiter.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means. A person who is merely lost is one kind of soul. A person who, having no knowledge, no inner guidance, and no scripture, decides nonetheless to **organize a movement of others into his lostness** — that is a different kind of soul.

The *lām* before *yuḍilla* — *li-yuḍilla* — is what Arabic grammarians call *lām al-taʿlīl*: the lām of purpose. It tells you the **purpose** of the disputation. Why does he twist his neck and dispute? **In order to** mislead others.

The disputation is not a private failure. It is a public mission.

This is what makes ayahs 8-9 so much heavier than ayah 3. The follower in ayah 3 is in error. But the recruiter in ayah 8 is using his error as a platform. He is leveraging his lostness into a kind of social authority.

You know this figure. You have seen this figure. The one who, having read nothing seriously, is nonetheless certain that the religion is wrong. The one who, never having sat with a single scholar of any tradition, can lecture for an hour on what the Quran "really" means. The one whose argument is not arguments — it is performance, posture, and the recruitment of others into his confidence.

Allah names this figure with three pieces of evidence:
- Empty hands.
- Twisted neck.
- Recruiting purpose.

That is the diagnostic.

### The Bridge

We have looked at three things. The three sources of evidence the man does not have. The bodily posture that betrays him. The recruiting purpose that defines him.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about a particular kind of person, but about reality itself — about how truth actually works in the world, and about what each of us must guard against in our own posture.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When the Body Tells the Truth the Mouth Won't

Think about the last time you watched a debate. Not a serious one — one of those public ones where two people are talking at each other and neither is listening. Watch the bodies. Notice that one of them is not waiting to understand. He is waiting to speak. You can see it. The shoulders are already turning. The eyes are already disengaging. The hand is already lifting to interrupt.

You did not need to read his mind. The body told you everything.

Now think about a different scene. You are sitting with a friend who has just said something that contradicts what you believe. You feel the choice rise up in your chest. There is a moment — a split second — where you decide whether to face them or turn away. To stay with them in the question, or to retreat into a posture.

In that moment, your *ʿiṭf* makes a decision. It either stays straight, or it twists.

Allah is telling us, in these ayahs, that **the most reliable diagnostic of where a person stands with the truth is not what they say. It is what their body does in the moment of encounter.**

This is profound — and humbling. Because it means that the diagnostic for arrogance is not internal. It is observable. We can watch ourselves doing it. We can catch ourselves in the act of folding the neck away from a Quran ayah we don't want to absorb. We can catch ourselves in the act of pivoting our shoulder away from a piece of evidence that would require us to change.

The body knows. And Allah, who created the body, watches the body.

[PAUSE]

Now think about prayer.

What is the body of one who is praying? Standing straight. Hands folded. Eyes lowered to the place of prostration. Neck unfolded. Shoulders square to the qiblah. Then bowing — the neck still in line with the back. Then prostrating — the highest part of the body placed on the lowest point.

The entire posture of ṣalāh is the **opposite** of *thāniya ʿiṭfihi*. The body of the worshipper is straight, then bent forward in submission, then placed on the ground in surrender. The neck never twists away. The shoulders never pivot in dismissal. Every angle is an *opening*, never a closing.

Subhan Allah. The Quran describes the body of the disputant — and the Sunnah gives us a body of the worshipper that is its precise inversion.

This is one of the deep secrets of why Muslims pray with their bodies. It is not because Allah needs the gesture. It is because **we** need the gesture. The body teaches the soul. When you put your *ʿiṭf* in the position of submission five times a day — when you literally cannot dispute in that posture — you are training the body to refuse the geometry of arrogance.

What this changes about how we live tomorrow: pay attention to the body. When you encounter a verse, a ḥadīth, a piece of knowledge that disturbs you — notice what your shoulder does. Notice whether you lean in or pivot away. The shoulder is the truth-teller. Stay straight. Face forward. Even if you don't yet agree, **don't twist**. The willingness not to twist is the beginning of being teachable.

### Theme Two: The Lonely Doubter Becomes the Recruiting Doubter

Here is something we don't talk about enough. Doubt is rarely content to stay private.

When a person begins to lose their footing in faith, there is an early stage where the doubt is internal. It is a question whispered to the self. It is an unease in the chest at certain ayahs. It is a quiet unwillingness to commit to certain practices. At this stage, the doubt is lonely. It belongs to one heart.

But doubt does not stay lonely for long. There is something inside the doubting heart that wants company.

Why?

Because lonely doubt is unbearable. If I am the only one doubting, then perhaps I am the one who is wrong. The presence of millions of believers around me becomes a kind of pressure — every one of their faces is implicitly asking me, "Why are you the exception?" To live alone in doubt is to live as the anomaly.

But if I can recruit others — if I can bring even three or four people into my doubting — suddenly I am not the anomaly. I am a movement. I am a perspective. I am a school of thought. The recruitment of others is not just a project. It is a **psychological necessity** for the lonely doubter.

This is what Allah is naming with *li-yuḍilla*. The disputant in ayah 8 is not a calm intellectual. He is a man under pressure to prove that his lostness is justified, and the only way to prove it is to make others lost too.

[PAUSE]

You have seen this in our own time. Watch the people who leave the religion publicly. Notice that very few of them leave quietly. They leave with a podcast. They leave with a book. They leave with a campaign. The leaving is not enough — they need others to leave with them.

This is not because they have suddenly become evangelists for truth. It is because their doubt is unbearable in solitude. They need company. The size of the platform is proportional to the loneliness of the doubt.

And this is a warning to us — but it is also a mercy. Because it means that the next time you encounter the loud, confident voice trying to pull you out of faith, you can ask yourself a different question. Not "Are his arguments good?" — but "**Why does he need me to agree with him?**"

Truth does not need recruits. Truth is what it is, whether or not anyone agrees. The man who is genuinely confident in his understanding does not require you to validate it. But the man whose understanding is hollow — the man with the three empty hands and the twisted neck — that man **needs** you. He needs you because his confidence is borrowed from your assent.

This is why Allah describes the punishment as *khizy* — disgrace, public humiliation. It is the perfect inversion of his crime. He sought public influence — he gets public disgrace. He sought to elevate himself by gathering followers — he gets lowered before everyone. The dunya consequence matches the dunya ambition.

Then in the ākhira: *nudhīquhu ʿadhāba al-ḥarīq* — "We will make him **taste** the punishment of the Burning."

Notice the verb. To **taste**. The most intimate of the senses. The one that brings the substance into the body itself. He spent his life in performance — in the theater of disputation, in the public square of recruitment. The fire will not be public. The fire will be on his tongue. Intimate. Personal. Tasted.

What this changes about how we live tomorrow: **pay attention to who is recruiting you.** When someone tries to pull you toward a position about Allah, ask not just whether they are correct — ask whether they have *ʿilm*, *hudan*, or *kitāb munīr*. Ask whether their neck is straight or twisted. Ask whether their need for your agreement is suspicious. The people who genuinely possess truth do not chase you. They simply stand straight and let truth do its work.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now let's stand back and see what these two ayahs actually are.

Ayah 3 of this surah opened with the same words: *wa min al-nāsi man yujādilu fī Allāhi bi-ghayri ʿilm*. But ayah 3 ended with *yattabiʿu* — "he follows." Ayah 8 ends with *li-yuḍilla* — "to mislead."

The two ayahs are a pair. The follower and the leader of misguidance.

But notice — the punishments are different. Ayah 3's follower has his fate written: he will be guided to the punishment of the Blaze. Ayah 8's recruiter gets two punishments: *khizy* in this world AND *ʿadhāb al-ḥarīq* in the next. The leader pays in two currencies. Why? Because his crime had two components. He sought to elevate himself in this world (so he is disgraced in this world) AND he sought to misguide others (so he tastes the burning in the next). Each ambition gets its mirror.

The architecture is exact. Not a single piece is decorative.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two angles.

Linguistically, we found three empty hands, a twisted neck, and a recruiting purpose. Thematically, we found that the body tells the truth and that lonely doubt seeks company.

These are the same finding. The body that twists is the body of someone who has nothing in his hands and needs you to agree to compensate. The empty hands and the twisted neck and the recruiting mouth are one organism. They appear together because they belong together. They are the anatomy of a single condition.

And the cure — the implicit cure, the inverse — is also a single condition. The hands that hold *ʿilm* and *hudā* and *kitāb munīr*. The neck that stays straight. The mouth that does not chase recruits. This is the believer. Not because the believer has all answers, but because the believer's posture toward truth is **open**. Hands holding what they have, neck unfolded, willing to receive what they don't yet have.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time my *ʿiṭf* twisted away from a verse, a piece of advice, a correction I needed to hear? What did I do in that moment?

2. When I argue about religion — even silently in my own head — which of the three doors am I standing inside? *ʿIlm*? *Hudan*? *Kitāb munīr*? Or am I standing outside all three and merely performing?

3. Who in my life is trying to recruit me into their doubt? What do they need from my agreement that they cannot get from the truth itself?

4. When I share my own beliefs with others, am I sharing them because they are true, or because I need company to bear my own uncertainty?

5. What would it mean, in my body, to stop twisting? To face every encounter with truth — even uncomfortable truth — with my shoulders square to the source?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The most reliable diagnostic of how a person stands with truth is not what they argue, but the angle of their neck — and Allah, who created the neck, watches.

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this is not your ideas. It is your posture.

Tomorrow, when you next encounter something — an ayah, an admonition, a piece of evidence, a correction — that you do not want to absorb, notice what your shoulder does. Notice the millimeter of pivot. Notice the quiet folding of the *ʿiṭf*.

And then, by the will of Allah, **don't twist**.

Stay straight. Face forward. Let what comes, come.

This is not a small thing. This is the geometry of the believer. Five times a day in ṣalāh you train the body in the opposite of *thāniya ʿiṭfihi*. The training is for the rest of life — for every moment outside the prayer when truth presents itself and asks whether you will face it or turn.

### A Du'ā From These Words

*Yā Allāh, the One who watches the body and reads what the heart will not say —*

*Straighten our necks before You. When evidence comes to us, do not let our shoulders pivot in dismissal. When Your verses speak, do not let our ʿiṭf fold in arrogance. Fill our hands with ʿilm, with hudā, with the light of Your illuminating Book — so that when we open our mouths about You, we open them from fullness and not from emptiness.*

*And protect us, yā Rabb, from being either the recruiter of doubt or the recruited. Make our certainty quiet, our learning straight-backed, and our final standing before You — when we taste what we will taste — be a standing of those whose hands were full and whose necks never twisted away from Your light.*

*Āmīn.*
