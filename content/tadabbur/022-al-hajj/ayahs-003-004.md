# Disputing Without Knowledge — and the Shaytan Who Guides to Fire

## Introduction: After the Earthquake, the Argument

> **وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ مَن يُجَـٰدِلُ فِى ٱللَّهِ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ وَيَتَّبِعُ كُلَّ شَيْطَـٰنٍ مَّرِيدٍ ۝ كُتِبَ عَلَيْهِ أَنَّهُۥ مَن تَوَلَّاهُ فَأَنَّهُۥ يُضِلُّهُۥ وَيَهْدِيهِ إِلَىٰ عَذَابِ ٱلسَّعِيرِ**

> *Wa mina-n-nāsi man yujādilu fi-llāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa yattabiʿu kulla shayṭānim-marīd. Kutiba ʿalayhi annahu man tawallāhu fa-annahu yuḍilluhu wa yahdīhi ilā ʿadhābi-s-saʿīr.*

> "And among people is one who disputes about Allah without any knowledge, and follows every rebellious devil. It has been decreed concerning him that whoever takes him as an intimate ally — he will lead him astray and guide him to the punishment of the Blaze."

Step into the world this ayah enters.

The surah has just opened with two of the most devastating verses in the Quran. *O mankind, fear your Lord. Indeed the convulsion of the Hour is a terrible thing.* Then ayah 2: nursing mothers forgetting the infants at their breast. Pregnant wombs releasing what they carry. People drunk — but not drunk. The whole frame of human life — motherhood, gestation, sobriety — is dissolving in a single cosmic tremor.

That is the world Allah has just placed before us.

And then — the very next breath — ayah 3 begins.

You would expect the next sentence to describe how *people respond* to this warning. You would expect terror. Repentance. Trembling. Some posture of receiving what has just been said.

Instead, Allah says: *and among people is one who disputes about Allah without any knowledge.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that juxtaposition. The Hour has just been described in cosmic terms. The reader has barely had time to feel his own pulse. And the response of a certain kind of human being is — to argue. Not to ask. Not to investigate. Not to fall silent. To *dispute*.

The classical tradition gives this disputer a historical face. Al-Tabari transmits from Ibn Jurayj that the ayah was revealed concerning al-Nadr ibn al-Harith, one of the Quraysh's most persistent opponents of the Prophet. And al-Tabari makes the content of the dispute precise: it was not an abstract theological debate. The specific claim was that Allah could not revive those who had "decayed and become dust" — a denial of resurrection itself. The surah opens with the earthquake of the Hour. The very next figure to appear is a man disputing the possibility of that Hour. The two were always one conversation, aimed at the same crisis of belief.

About what does he dispute? About Allah Himself.

Equipped with what? *Bi-ghayri ʿilm.* Without any knowledge.

There is something here that the surface cannot hold. Why does Allah place this portrait *here* — at the threshold, before the believers are even introduced? What is it about this particular disorder that it must be addressed before anything else? And what does it mean that this disputer doesn't just dispute — he *follows every rebellious devil*?

We are about to discover that this ayah is drawing a portrait of one of the most dangerous configurations the human soul can take. And when we trace the verb-forms, we will find a striking pattern — a structural asymmetry in how this soul relates to truth and to falsehood — that rewards careful attention.

Stay with me. We are going to open this slowly.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### Element 1: *Yujādilu* — The Form of the Verb is the Diagnosis

Before we even translate this word, let me explain something about how Arabic verbs work.

In Arabic, the same three-letter root can be poured into different verb-shapes (forms), and each shape changes the *kind* of action being described. Form I is the basic action — *to do something*. Form II intensifies it. Form III takes the same action and makes it **reciprocal** — back-and-forth between two parties. *Qatala* (Form I) means "he killed." *Qātala* (Form III) means "he engaged in mutual fighting" — a two-sided contest.

Hold that grammatical fact. We will need it.

The root here is **ج-د-ل** *(j-d-l)*. The basic noun *jadl* means a tightly twisted rope — strands wound around each other so tightly that you cannot separate them.

Form I would mean simply *to twist*. But Allah does not use Form I. Allah uses Form III: **يُجَادِلُ** *yujādilu* — to engage in mutual twisting. To enter a contest of intertwined rope-pulling. To wrestle, where two sides each grip the same cord and pull.

That is the picture. *Jidāl* is not "disagreement." It is not "asking a question." It is a **contest**. Two parties, each gripping their position, twisting the rope of the argument back and forth, neither releasing.

Now hear what Allah says next: *yujādilu **fi-llāhi** bi-ghayri ʿilm.*

He enters this back-and-forth contest. *About Allah.* And he enters it — with no rope to grip.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with that. Form III demands two equipped parties. *Mufāʿala* — the very name of this verb-form — means a mutual exchange. You cannot have a wrestling match with empty hands. You cannot enter a tug-of-war without rope.

But this person has chosen the verb-form of *contest* — and arrived to the contest with *bi-ghayri ʿilm* — without any knowledge. Notice the indefinite: not "without sufficient knowledge," not "without the right knowledge." Without **any** knowledge. The Arabic indefinite *ʿilmin* in this construction means he is empty.

So the structure of his action is itself a contradiction. He is performing the *form* of someone who has something to say, while having nothing to say.

Here's what this reveals. The pathology Allah is naming is not ignorance as such. Ignorance is a passive state — a person can be ignorant and silent. Al-Tabari characterizes this action precisely as *jahl* — the ignorance of a person who speaks without knowing what he says. The pathology is **the choice to enter combat about Allah while empty of equipment**. The hands closing into fists where there is no rope to hold.

You have seen this. We all have. The person on the comment thread, on the timeline, at the dinner table, who has read nothing — who has not opened the Quran with serious attention, has not studied a single verse with a teacher, has not even read a translation carefully — who nevertheless, with full chest, **enters the contest** about who Allah is, what He commands, what He intends. The verb is *yujādilu*. The shape of the action requires a shape of preparation that he does not have.

And notice: Allah does not say "and among people is one who *speaks* about Allah without knowledge." Speaking without knowledge is a smaller crime. Allah names the harder one — the one who *contests*. Who treats his ignorance as a position to defend.

[PAUSE]

The diagnosis is in the verb-form itself. *Mufāʿala* with no equipment.

### Element 2: *Yattabiʿu* — When the Will Commits Where the Intellect Won't

Now read the second half of ayah 3: *wa yattabiʿu kulla shayṭānim-marīd.*

He follows every rebellious devil.

Stay with the verb. The root is **ت-ب-ع** *(t-b-ʿ)*. Form I, *tabiʿa*, means "he followed." A simple act — to walk behind someone.

But again, Allah does not use Form I. Allah uses Form VIII: **يَتَّبِعُ** *yattabiʿu*.

What does Form VIII do? In Arabic morphology, Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*) takes the basic action and makes it **internal — taken on as one's own, adopted, embraced**. It is the verb of *appropriation*. Form VIII turns "to follow" into "to take as one's path." Not just walking behind — *making this one's own direction*.

Now place that next to what we just saw.

In ayah 3, Allah pairs two verbs:
- *yujādilu* — Form III (mutual contest about Allah)
- *yattabiʿu* — Form VIII (internal appropriation of every rebellious shaytan)

Look at the asymmetry.

When it comes to *Allah* — the disputer enters the verb-form of contest *while empty*. He cannot *commit* his intellect to knowing. He stands at arm's length. He fights, but he does not study.

When it comes to *shaytan* — the same person enters the verb-form of **internalization**. Total appropriation. Made his own.

[PAUSE]

This is the pattern that emerges from the two verb-forms placed side by side. The verb of engagement with Allah is left in the mode of contest-without-equipment — arm's length, nothing committed. The verb of following shaytan has been taken entirely inward, adopted as one's own direction.

And notice the qualifier: *kulla shayṭānim-marīd* — **every** rebellious devil. Not "a particular shaytan he has weighed and chosen." *Every* one. The *kull* is devastating. Whatever rebellious voice arrives — be it from a person, a culture, an inner whisper, an algorithm — it is given uptake. The mind that demanded combat-level resistance to the truth about Allah accepts every rebellious whisper without scrutiny.

One way to describe what these two verb-forms are mapping — in contemporary terms — is what psychologists call **motivated reasoning**: we do not evaluate evidence neutrally, but have often decided where we wish to land, and the mind constructs its position afterward. Reading the two verbs this way, the *jidāl* about Allah becomes theater — the real action is the *yattabiʿu*. The will has already committed downward. The mouth is decorating that commitment. Whether that psychological reading fully captures what Allah is naming here, or whether it is one modern lens on an older reality, the pattern in the Arabic remains: the soul that demands proof of Allah accepts shaytan with no proof at all.

Here's what makes this so penetrating. We tell ourselves that doubt is a sign of intellectual seriousness. That the person who refuses to commit to claims about Allah without ironclad proof is the rigorous one. But the verb-forms press the honest question: **what does the same person believe without proof?** If the soul that enters *jidāl* about Allah enters *ittibāʿ* toward every rebellious voice — then it was never about proof. It was about the direction of commitment.

### Element 3: *Marīd* — The Stripped One

Now look at the kind of shaytan he follows: *kulla shayṭānim* — **marīd**.

The root is **م-ر-د** *(m-r-d)*. The classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt of al-Rāghib) document its core meaning: **stripped bare, smooth, having had every covering removed**. A *shajarah marīdah* is a tree whose bark has been stripped — exposed, unprotected, naked wood. A *ṣarḥ mumarrad* — used in Surah An-Naml about Bilqis's palace — is a building so smoothly polished that nothing clings to it; even water slides off.

From this physical sense, the word came to mean — in a moral register — **one who has stripped himself of every restraint**. Not merely disobedient. Not merely sinful. *Stripped* — having peeled off every covering of submission, every layer of shame, every restraining bark. Bare defiance. Defiance with nothing on it.

Now hear: *kulla shayṭānim-marīd*. Every shaytan whose insolence has reached this terminal stage. Not a tempted shaytan. Not a fallen-but-conflicted shaytan. The shaytan who has stripped away every last filament of restraint and stands as pure rebellion.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir makes an important widening here that is easy to miss. These shayāṭīn are not only the unseen rebellious from among the jinn — they include shayāṭīn among *men*: the human leaders of misguidance who call people away from what Allah has revealed to His Prophets. Al-Muyassar describes the disputants themselves as *ruʾūs al-kufr* — the heads and chiefs of disbelief, not merely private doubters but social figures around whom deviation organizes itself. The stripped rebel the disputer follows may be a whisper from within, or a living human being who has peeled off every restraint and now stands before others as a voice of authority.

Notice something remarkable. The disputer is *empty of knowledge* — a kind of nakedness. The shaytan he follows is *stripped of restraint* — another kind of nakedness. Two emptinesses meet. Two unclothed parties. They recognize each other.

This is what the Arabic is showing us through pure morphology: like calls to like. The soul that has stripped itself of *ʿilm* finds its native companion in the entity that has stripped itself of *ḥayāʾ*. They fit.

And now we are ready for the second ayah.

### Element 4: *Kutiba ʿAlayhi* — The Decree Is on the Shaytan, Not on the Disputer

Read ayah 4 carefully:

*Kutiba ʿalayhi annahu man tawallāhu fa-annahu yuḍilluhu wa yahdīhi ilā ʿadhābi-s-saʿīr.*

Whose pronoun is *ʿalayhi*? Who is "him"?

Most casual readers assume the *him* is the disputer — that *it has been decreed upon the disputer* that he will be misled. But the Arabic syntax pulls in the other direction. The nearest antecedent is *shayṭānim-marīd*. And the classical mufassirūn confirm this reading — not as one option among several, but explicitly. Al-Tabari states it plainly: "*kutiba* here means *quḍiya* — it was decreed — and the *hāʾ* in *ʿalayhi* refers to the shaytan." He then transmits this reading through two independent chains: via Qatada — "it was written on the shaytan, that whoever from Allah's creation follows the shaytan" — and via Mujahid — the pronoun is the shaytan, who was followed. Both hold that the decree has been written upon the *shaytan*, not yet upon the disputer who still has time to turn.

This is a startling shift. Allah is not (here) decreeing the disputer's punishment. Allah is describing **the ontological function of shaytan**. It is a cosmic statement about what shaytan *is*. Misguidance is his job description. He has been *written* as the misguider — *kutiba* in the passive — by a Hand that need not be named because there is only one Hand that writes such things.

And notice the form: **kutiba** — passive perfect. Not "Allah will write upon him." Not even "Allah wrote upon him." The passive removes the agent and renders the decree as fact. As reality. As what is.

This is what shaytan *does*. Not what he might do. Not what he chooses sometimes to do. **It is his function.**

Why does this matter? Because the disputer in ayah 3 is treating shaytan as if shaytan were a neutral source of input — a voice among voices, an opinion among opinions, an algorithm among algorithms. The whole framing of *yattabiʿu kulla shayṭānim* is that the disputer doesn't understand what he is following. He thinks he is following ideas. He thinks he is following arguments. He thinks he is following his own preferences.

Allah corrects the picture: *kutiba ʿalayhi* — it has been written upon him. Whoever takes this voice as ally, this voice **will** misguide. Not might. Will. Because that is what this entity has been written to do.

### Element 5: *Yuḍilluhu wa Yahdīhi* — The Most Dangerous Phrase

And now we arrive at what is, for me, the most piercing phrase in these two verses.

*Fa-annahu **yuḍilluhu wa yahdīhi** ilā ʿadhābi-s-saʿīr.*

He misguides him and guides him to the punishment of the Blaze.

Two verbs. Look at them.

*Yuḍillu* — from the root **ض-ل-ل** *(ḍ-l-l)*: to go astray, to be lost, to wander from the path. Form IV causative — *yuḍillu* means to *cause* another to go astray. To take someone who had a path and remove them from it. To make them lost.

That verb is expected. Of course shaytan misguides. That is his function, as we have just established.

But then Allah uses the second verb. And it is devastating.

*Yahdī* — from the root **ه-د-ي** *(h-d-y)*: **to guide**. The root of *hidāyah*. The root of *hādī*. The root of the supreme prayer in Al-Fatiha: *ihdina al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* — **guide us to the straight path**.

Shaytan *guides* his follower.

[PAUSE]

But to where? *Ilā ʿadhābi-s-saʿīr.* To the punishment of the Blaze.

This is not a slip of language. This is a deliberate, precise, devastating choice. Allah uses the most blessed word in the Quran — the word for divine guidance, the word that is the object of every Muslim's most repeated prayer — and places it in the mouth of shaytan. Shaytan *guides*. He has a destination. He leads his follower toward it. He does not merely push them away from truth and abandon them in darkness. He *takes them somewhere*.

The root **ه-د-ي** means, at its most physical level, to make a path clear, to show the way through terrain. A *hādī* was one who walked ahead on a difficult journey, showing others where to step. Shaytan is doing this. He is walking ahead. He is clearing the path. He is showing where to step.

The destination is fire.

And notice the word for fire here: **al-saʿīr** — from the root **س-ع-ر** *(s-ʿ-r)*: to kindle, to set ablaze, to stoke. A fire that has been deliberately lit and is being actively fed. Al-Muyassar renders it as "جهنم الموقدة" — the kindled, stoked fire of Jahannam — and uses the verb *yasūquhu*: he *drives* him toward it, herding him forward the way a shepherd drives livestock, not merely pointing in a direction but actively pushing from behind. Not a fire that happens upon you. A fire that has been prepared. And a driver who will not let you stop.

[PAUSE]

Now hold both verbs together: *yuḍilluhu* **and** *yahdīhi*. They are simultaneous, not sequential. Shaytan does not first misguide and then, separately, guide to punishment. The two are one continuous action. In the act of leading someone away from the truth, he is already leading them toward the fire. There is no neutral ground between them. The path away from *ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* does not leave you standing still — it is itself a path, with a destination, and that destination is al-saʿīr.

This is the sharpest edge of these two verses. The disputer in ayah 3 thinks he is standing in open ground — rejecting the Hour, refusing the Quran, following his chosen voices — with nowhere in particular to land. The two ayahs together say: there is no such ground. The verb for following shaytan (*yattabiʿu*, Form VIII — internalized, taken as one's own path) terminates in the verb for shaytan's activity (*yahdī* — active guidance to a prepared fire). There is a destination. The path has already been laid. The driver is already behind.

## Part Two: What the Portrait Asks of Us

### The Mercy Inside the Warning

We should pause at something that is easy to miss in the force of this language.

Allah *warns*.

He did not have to. He could have simply allowed the disputer to dispute, the follower to follow, the path to reach its end without prior notice. But ayah 3 is placed here — before the disputer reaches that end, before the judgment has fallen — as a word of prevention. This is what the Quran consistently does: it shows the interior of a path while you are still near enough to the entrance to turn around.

The disputer of ayah 3 is still in the state of *man yujādilu* — present tense, active, ongoing. He has not yet arrived. The ayah is meeting him in the middle of the action. And Allah says: look at what this is. Look at the verb-forms. Look at who has been decreed to misguide whoever follows him. Look at where the path you have taken leads.

That is mercy. Confrontational mercy — but mercy.

### The Cure Named Inside the Disease

The diagnosis names its own antidote. The disputer disputes *bi-ghayri ʿilm* — without knowledge. The *ʿilm* that is missing is not described in detail, but the emptiness is named precisely.

Al-Tabari specifies what the disputer refused to know: the reality of resurrection, of Allah's power over the dead, of the Hour that the surah's first verse had just named. He had access to this knowledge — it was being recited in his city, by the Prophet, in the Quran itself. He chose the contest over the inquiry. The *jidāl* was not what he had exhausted, having tried *ʿilm* and found it wanting. The *jidāl* was what he chose *instead* of *ʿilm*.

The question each reader must sit with: in what area of my own life am I performing *jidāl* — entering the verb-form of contest — while refusing the *ʿilm* that would actually settle the matter? Where have I adopted the form of argument while avoiding the burden of study?

This is not only about theological dispute. The pattern *yujādilu bi-ghayri ʿilm* can appear in how one debates a parent, defends a habit, or dismisses a Quranic injunction that would require inconvenient change. The verb-form is the same. The emptiness beneath it is the same.

### The Word *Tawallā* — Choosing Your Walī

Look at ayah 4 again: *man tawallāhu* — whoever **takes him as a *walī***.

The root **و-ل-ي** *(w-l-y)* is one of the most loaded in the Quran. A *walī* is not merely a companion or a preference — a *walī* is an intimate protector, an ally bound to you by loyalty, whose affairs are inseparable from yours. The same root gives us *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū* — "Allah is the *walī* of those who believe." And *man yatawalla-llāha wa-rasūlahu wa-lladhīna āmanū fa-inna ḥizba-llāhi humu-l-ghālibūn* — those who take Allah and His Messenger as *walī* belong to the party that prevails.

The word *tawallā* means: to turn toward, to adopt as your protector, to place yourself under the care of. It is not passive. It is a turning. A *walāʾ* is a loyalty-structure, a belonging.

When the disputer *yattabiʿu kulla shayṭānim-marīd*, he is doing something deeper than merely following a voice. He is making a *walī* choice. He is entering the *walāʾ* of the rebellious. And ayah 4 names the consequence of that turning with the full weight of a decree: *kutiba ʿalayhi*. This is what has been written for the one who chooses in.

The antithesis is implied in the very vocabulary. The believer who reads these verses and asks: who is my *walī*? — is being invited by the language itself to the other *walāʾ*, the one named in Al-Baqarah: *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū yukhrijuhum mina-ẓ-ẓulumāti ilā-n-nūr* — Allah brings His allies out of the darknesses and into the light. One path has a *walī* who guides to fire. The other has a *Walī* who leads to light. Both use the verb *yahdī*. The destination is everything.

### The Second Portrait — and What It Warns

We should note, before closing, that the Quran returns to this figure.

At 22:8-9, the same disputer reappears: *wa mina-n-nāsi man yujādilu fi-llāhi bi-ghayri ʿilmin wa lā hudā wa lā kitābin munīr — thāniya ʿiṭfihi li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīli-llāh.* He disputes about Allah without knowledge, without guidance, without an illuminating book — **turning his neck in arrogance** to lead others away from the path of Allah.

The movement between 22:3 and 22:8-9 is arresting. In these verses, the disputer follows. By 22:8-9, he leads. By 22:9, arrogance has calcified into the posture of the neck — *thāniya ʿiṭfihi* — the turned head, the body language of contempt directed at those he would misguide.

One literary reading of the surah's structure sees these two moments as progressive: at 22:3-4 the portrait is of a follower, empty of knowledge but still in the receiving position; at 22:8-9 the same type has become an axis, now actively calling others to deviation. Whether the Quran intends a single figure's deepening or two related portraits, the trajectory the Arabic maps is sobering: the follower of every rebellious shaytan may, in time, become one of the *ruʾūs al-kufr* — the chiefs of disbelief — that al-Muyassar describes: social figures around whom deviation organizes, whose turned necks define the direction others look.

The ayah does not tell us this trajectory is inevitable. But it places the warning early — at the first stage, when the disputer is still following, still in the verb-form of contest-without-knowledge — because it is at the first stage that the path can change.

## Closing: The Hour, Still Trembling

We began with an earthquake.

*Inna zalzalata-s-sāʿati shayʾun ʿaẓīm.* The convulsion of the Hour is a tremendous thing.

The surah opened with that tremor and placed, immediately in its wake, the figure who responds to the tremor by arguing about whether the tremor is possible. Al-Nadr ibn al-Harith, or whoever history has produced in his shape in every age, who reads the signs of the universe and concludes that his preferred position is worth defending. The nursing mother of ayah 2 is already forgetting her infant. The pregnant woman is already releasing what she carries. And this person has his arms folded, his position ready, his mouth already opening for the contest.

But the Quran's addressing him — right here, right now, in the first words after the earthquake — is itself a form of rescue. The Hour has not yet arrived. The decree is written on the shaytan, not yet executed on the follower. The *ʿilm* that is missing is still available to be acquired. The *walāʾ* that has been extended toward the stripped rebel can still be redirected.

These two ayat are simultaneously a warning to the disputer and an invitation to everyone reading who recognizes, in the portrait, something of themselves. Where have I entered the verb of contest while avoiding the burden of knowledge? Where have I appropriated a rebellious voice as my own path? Whose *walī* am I, truly — and toward whose fire am I being guided?

The earthquake has happened. The question is only: what kind of person stands up when the trembling stops?

*Allāhumma ihdina-l-ṣirāṭa-l-mustaqīm.* O Allah, guide us to the straight path — not the path of those who have followed every stripped rebel to his prepared fire, but the path of those who turned toward You, while the warning was still ringing, while there was still time to turn.
