# The Fly Parable: Weak Are the Seeker and the Sought

> [ayah:22:73] يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ ضُرِبَ مَثَلٌ فَٱسْتَمِعُوا۟ لَهُۥٓ ۚ إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ لَن يَخْلُقُوا۟ ذُبَابًا وَلَوِ ٱجْتَمَعُوا۟ لَهُۥ ۖ وَإِن يَسْلُبْهُمُ ٱلذُّبَابُ شَيْـًٔا لَّا يَسْتَنقِذُوهُ مِنْهُ ۚ ضَعُفَ ٱلطَّالِبُ وَٱلْمَطْلُوبُ

> *"O people, a parable is struck, so listen to it. Indeed, those you call upon besides Allah will never create a fly, even if they all gathered together for it. And if the fly should snatch something from them, they could not recover it from it. Weak are the seeker and the sought."*
> — Al-Hajj 22:73

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world we have to enter to hear this

Before we can hear what Allah is doing in this ayah, we have to walk into the courtyard around the Ka'bah in the seventh century.

The original sanctuary — the house Ibrāhīm built — was not empty. By the time of the Prophet ﷺ, scholars and sīrah-narrators report something close to **three hundred and sixty idols** arranged in and around it. Some carved from wood, some from stone, some imported from Syria, some inherited from Yemen. Hubal, the largest, stood inside the Ka'bah itself. Lāt, ʿUzzā, Manāt — the famous three — had their own sanctuaries nearby. Tribes from across Arabia would arrive during the pilgrimage months bringing **offerings**. Honey poured into bowls. Dates piled on plates. Cuts of meat. Sweet milk. Grains soaked in oils.

And around every offering, **flies**.

You have to picture this concretely. The Arabian sun. The smell of honey and meat in open air. And the small, dark, ubiquitous insect that lives wherever sweetness meets warmth. Flies on the offerings to Hubal. Flies on the offerings to Lāt. Flies landing on the dates piled before stone faces that did not blink, did not move, did not chase.

The pilgrims watched this every day for generations. They watched their gods sit motionless while flies stole from them.

And they had **never let themselves think about what they were seeing**.

[PAUSE]

### The strand running through this surah

Al-Hajj has been building toward this moment from its opening earthquake. It has shown us Allah's power in the **womb** (22:5), in the **resurrection** (22:6-7), in **the cosmos and the kingship** (22:65), in **the rise and fall of cities** (22:45-46). It has named the worshipper of false gods as someone who has **"lost the world and the next"** (22:11). And now, in the closing movement, the surah does something different.

It does not argue.

It does not philosophize.

It points at a fly.

### The hook

What you are about to hear is not a clever metaphor invented by Allah. It is **the description of what was happening on the temple grounds while the worshippers refused to look**. Allah is not constructing an analogy. He is naming what they had already seen — for years — with their own eyes.

And what He places at the end of the ayah is one of the most surgically devastating sentences in the entire Quran. Six words in Arabic. A verdict so total that classical scholars debated for centuries who, exactly, was being called weak — and the debate itself is part of the meaning.

Stay with that. **Weak are the seeker and the sought.** Whoever the seeker is. Whoever the sought is. The verdict applies.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Why a fly? — The choice of *dhubāb*

Before we open the word, let me teach the principle.

When Allah constructs a parable in the Quran, the **object** chosen is never random. He could have said: those you call upon besides Allah will never create **a star**. Or **a mountain**. Or even **a sparrow**. Each of those would have made the theological point. None of them would have done what the word He chose actually does.

He chose **dhubāb (ذُبَاب)** — "fly."

Open the word with me.

In classical Arabic dictionaries — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — *dhubāb* is the small, common house-fly. The word itself is sonically dismissive. Listen: *dhu-bāb*. The opening *dh* is a soft, almost contemptuous sound. The doubled *b* in the second syllable lands like a swat. It is a word that **sounds like the thing it names** — small, repetitive, beneath notice.

Now ask: of all creatures, why this one?

Let's sit with this. The fly is the creature you **dismiss without thinking**. You wave it away. You don't even notice yourself waving it away. It is so far beneath your attention that you have automated the gesture of ignoring it.

And it is the creature that, for the original audience, **was already on their offerings**.

Notice what Allah is doing. He is not picking a creature so majestic that the failure to create it would seem distant — *of course* false gods cannot create an eagle, an elephant, a star; the mind hears that and feels the gap is large. He is picking the creature **closest to the ground of their actual religious experience**. The fly that was, at that very moment, walking across the date offered to Lāt.

Here's what's remarkable. By choosing *dhubāb*, Allah accomplishes three things in a single word:

**He brings the comparison down to the level of the absurd.** Not "these gods cannot create what Allah creates." But: "these gods cannot create the smallest, most ignorable, most contemptible thing in their own temples."

**He makes the parable visible to anyone, in any era, in any place.** A child who has never read philosophy can understand "a fly." There is no human society that does not have flies. He chose the universal animal.

**He reveals what they had already seen.** The flies were on the altars. He is not introducing an image. He is **forcing them to look at what they had been refusing to look at**.

And there is a fourth layer. In Lisān al-ʿArab, *dhubāb* also carries a secondary meaning — **the sharp tip of a sword's edge**. The word that names the most ignorable creature also names the sharpest part of a blade. Allah's parable, named with this word, is the sword-tip pressed against the chest of every false religion that has ever existed.

[PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: **Allah is not arguing with idolatry on its own terms. He is naming the most ignorable creature in the room and using it to collapse the entire structure.**

---

### Element Two: *Lan yakhluqū dhubāban walaw ijtamaʿū lahu* — The absolute denial across coordinated effort

Let me teach the construction first.

In Arabic, there are several ways to negate a future action. The mildest is *lā* — "they will not." Stronger is *mā* — depending on context. But the most absolute, the most categorical negation of the future is **lan (لن)**. *Lan* doesn't just mean "they will not." It means **"they will never, under any circumstance, in any imaginable scenario, do this."** It closes the door and locks it.

Now hear what Allah says: **lan yakhluqū dhubāban**. They will *lan*-create a fly. Not "they cannot create" — that would be a description. **"They will never create"** — that is a metaphysical sentence. The door is closed across all possible futures.

But Allah does not stop there. He adds: **walaw ijtamaʿū lahu**. "Even if they all gathered together for it."

Stay with what this is doing.

In ordinary human reasoning, weakness is something cooperation overcomes. One person cannot lift the boulder. Ten people can. One ant cannot move the leaf. A colony can. **Coordination compensates for individual weakness** — this is the foundational logic of every human civilization, every team, every army, every economy. If you cannot do something alone, you gather, and what was impossible becomes possible.

Allah is taking this assumption and demolishing it.

He is saying: the false gods you call upon are weak in a category where coordination *does not help*. **Even if every false god ever worshipped — Hubal and Lāt and ʿUzzā and Manāt and Baal and Zeus and every household idol and every river-spirit and every star-deity — sat down together and pooled their effort, they would not produce one fly.**

This is not a quantitative claim. This is a **categorical** claim. There is a kind of weakness that no amount of cooperation overcomes — the weakness of **not being the Creator**. The lack is not a lack of strength. It is a lack of *being the kind of thing that can create at all*.

And notice what classical scholars like al-Rāzī observed about the verb *yakhluqū* itself. *Khalq* in its most precise sense is not "to make from existing materials." That would be *ṣanaʿa* or *jaʿala*. *Khalq* is **to bring into existence what was not there**. To originate. To give being.

So the ayah is not even saying: "they cannot manufacture a fly from raw materials." It is saying something far more devastating: **they cannot give existence to a fly**. They cannot do the one thing that would make them, in any sense, "gods." Because to be God is to give being. And they don't.

[PAUSE]

Now let's add the next layer. Allah continues:

**wa-in yaslubhumu adh-dhubābu shay'an lā yastanqidhūhu minhu**
"And if the fly should snatch something from them, they could not recover it from it."

Two verbs to open here.

**Salaba (سلب)** — to forcibly seize, to snatch, to plunder. In classical usage, it is the verb used for what a victor strips from a defeated enemy. The fly is described with the language of a **conqueror**. The fly *plunders* the false god.

**Istanqadha (استنقذ)** — Form X of the root *n-q-dh*. The base meaning is to rescue, to save, to recover. Form X (*istafʿala*) intensifies and adds the connotation of **earnest, desperate effort**. *Istanqadha* is not "to take back." It is **"to desperately try to rescue."** The verb itself contains the strain of the effort.

So hear what this sentence is now saying. **If a fly — the conqueror — plunders something from the false god, the false god cannot even desperately try-to-rescue it.** The two verbs have created a small drama: one beat of conquest, one beat of failed rescue. And the failure is total.

Notice also what Allah leaves **unspecified**. *Yaslubhumu shay'an* — "snatch from them *something*." What did the fly take? A grain of honey? A drop of perfume? A speck of food? Allah does not say. The vagueness is the point. **It does not matter what the fly took. Whatever it was, it's gone.** The unspecified object is itself a comment: the loss is not significant enough to even name. And yet the false god could not recover it.

[PAUSE]

What this construction reveals: **The two halves of the ayah have placed the false god between two equally crushing failures. They cannot create even the smallest creature — that is the failure of generative power. And they cannot recover even the smallest stolen thing — that is the failure of defensive power. Powerless to add. Powerless to defend. There is nothing left for "god" to mean.**

---

### Element Three: *Ḍaʿufa aṭ-ṭālibu wal-maṭlūb* — The double weakness and the deliberate ambiguity

We arrive now at the most rhetorically charged six words in the ayah.

**ḍaʿufa aṭ-ṭālibu wal-maṭlūb** — "weak are the seeker and the sought."

Let me teach the form first.

*Ṭālib* and *maṭlūb* are an **active and passive participle from the same root** — *ṭ-l-b*, "to seek." *Ṭālib* is "the one seeking." *Maṭlūb* is "the one being sought." In Arabic, when you place an active and passive participle from one root side by side, you create a small, compressed universe — both ends of the action, simultaneously named.

Allah does this here. *Ṭālib* and *maṭlūb*. Seeker and sought. Both. Together. Weak.

Now here is where the ayah opens into one of the most discussed ambiguities in classical tafsīr. Who, exactly, is the seeker? Who is the sought?

The classical scholars offered several readings, and we are going to honor them as **camera angles on the same reality**, not competing alternatives.

**Reading One: The seeker is the false god, the sought is the fly.**
The false god is trying to recover what the fly took. The false god is the *ṭālib* — earnestly seeking. The fly is the *maṭlūb* — what is being chased. Both are described as weak. The false god is weak because it cannot recover. The fly is weak because it is, after all, just a fly.

**Reading Two: The seeker is the fly, the sought is the false god (or what was taken from it).**
The fly was seeking food. It found the offering. From its angle, the fly is the *ṭālib*, and what it sought — the offering, or the false god from whose altar it took — is the *maṭlūb*. Both weak. The fly because of its inherent smallness; the false god because what was taken cannot be recovered.

**Reading Three: The seeker is the worshipper, the sought is the false god.**
The human being who calls upon a false god is the seeker — they are seeking refuge, blessing, intercession. The false god is what they sought. **And the verdict is: both are weak.** The worshipper is weak because they have placed their need on something that cannot meet it. The false god is weak because it cannot meet anything.

Notice what happens when you let all three readings sit on the page together.

**Every possible pairing in this transaction is named weak.** 

The false god vs. the fly: both weak.

The fly vs. the false god: both weak.

The worshipper vs. the false god: both weak.

The ambiguity is not a flaw in the ayah. **The ambiguity is the verdict spreading**. Allah has constructed a sentence where, no matter which way you parse the seeker and the sought, **everyone in the picture except Him is weak**. The only one absent from this list of the weak is the One the worshippers refused to call upon.

[PAUSE]

And listen to the sound of the verdict itself. **Ḍaʿufa**. Three letters: *ḍ-ʿ-f*. The word for weakness in Arabic is itself sonically slack. It opens with *ḍād* — a heavy, slumped letter. The mouth has to fall to make it. The classical phoneticians described *ḍād* as the heaviest letter in Arabic, the one that slows the breath. **The word for "weak" sounds like collapse.**

And the verb is in the **perfect tense** — *ḍaʿufa*, "has been weak," "is weak as a settled fact." Not "may be weak." Not "becomes weak." **Has been, is, and will be — weak.** The verdict is past, present, and future at once.

What this six-word sentence reveals: **Allah has constructed the most efficient demolition of polytheism in the Arabic language. Three readings of the seeker-sought pair, and in every reading, the verdict holds. Everyone in the picture is weak. The only one not in the picture is the only one not weak.**

---

### Bridge

Look at what these three linguistic moves have done together.

The choice of **fly** brought the parable into the dust of actual life — the temple grounds, the offerings, the everyday creature anyone in any era can see.

The construction **lan yakhluqū walaw ijtamaʿū** closed the door on every possible escape — no future, no coordination, no cumulative effort can produce what false gods cannot produce, because the lack is categorical, not quantitative.

The closing verdict **ḍaʿufa aṭ-ṭālibu wal-maṭlūb** spread the weakness across every party in the transaction — false god, creature, worshipper — leaving only Allah outside the verdict.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the things we, today, secretly call upon besides Him.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The idols of our age — what we worship that cannot create a fly

Let me start where you live.

You don't pray to Hubal. You probably haven't bowed before a stone idol in your life. So when you hear an ayah about polytheism, there's a quiet voice inside you that says, *this is for someone else. This is for the seventh-century Meccan, not for me.*

Stay with that voice for a second. Because the Quran's understanding of *ilāh* — what gets called upon as a god — is not narrow. The classical scholars defined *ilāh* as **whatever the heart turns to in fear, in love, in need, in hope, expecting that thing to deliver.**

Now ask yourself, honestly: in a moment of fear at 2 a.m., what does your heart turn to first?

Your bank balance? Your career trajectory? The opinion of a particular person? Your followers? The next deal? The doctor? The lawyer? The political party? The algorithm?

We don't carve our gods anymore. **We subscribe to them.** We download them. We post for their attention. We refresh to see if they have noticed us today.

Now ask: can your career create a fly?

Sit with that question literally, not metaphorically. Your job, your title, your LinkedIn, your portfolio — can it bring into existence one small, ignorable, walking-on-the-windowsill creature? Could every employee at your company, gathered together in a coordinated effort, **give being** to one fly?

[PAUSE]

The honest answer is no.

Can your reputation protect you from one mosquito bite? Can your savings cure one toothache? Can the algorithm console you when your child is sick? Can the political movement you have placed your hope in answer the prayer you make in the dark?

These are not rhetorical questions. **They are the same questions Allah is asking the Meccan, transposed.**

And here is what the parable opens up. The Meccan thought their false gods were *something*. They had elaborate stories about Hubal's powers, about Lāt's intercession, about ʿUzzā's blessings. **Every age develops elaborate stories about its idols.** Our age has them too. The story that money is security. The story that career is meaning. The story that the right relationship will complete you. The story that being known is being loved.

Allah's parable is the universal solvent for all such stories. **Take any idol. Test it against the fly. Can it create one? Can it defend itself against one?** If the answer is no, then whatever else it is, it is not God. It is *min dūnillāh* — a thing besides Allah, a thing whose entire defining identity is **not being Him**.

[PAUSE]

But notice — and this is where the parable cuts deepest — Allah is not telling you to despise your career, hate your reputation, or stop using money. He is telling you to **see what you have placed it as**. The career is fine. The career as your *ilāh* is the fly trying to create a fly.

Here's what's remarkable. The original Meccans had a defense the modern person doesn't have. They could say: *we know Lāt didn't make the world. She is just an intercessor. We worship Allah through her.* The Quran rejects that defense (39:3) — but at least it was an articulated theology.

The modern person has no defense. **We have not even articulated to ourselves what we are doing.** We have placed our deepest hope in things and have not even noticed that we placed it there. We have made flies our gods without ever pronouncing their names.

The parable's mercy is that it gives us a test we can apply at home, today, alone. The fly test. *What in my life have I been calling upon that cannot create a fly? What in my life have I been turning to first that cannot recover what a fly takes?*

Practical application: **For the next week, when you feel the reflex of fear or hope, name what your heart turns to in that first second.** Not what you think it should turn to. What it actually does. Then, before doing anything else, place that thing — silently, in your mind — next to the fly. Ask: *can it create one?* Let the answer settle. Then turn, deliberately, to the only One who can.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The mercy hidden in the parable — Allah meeting people where they look

There is a question the ayah quietly raises that we should not move past.

**Why a parable at all?**

Allah, who created the heavens and the earth, who needs no rhetorical strategies, who could simply declare the truth and leave it — chooses, here, to "strike a parable." *Ḍuriba mathal* — "a parable has been struck." Notice the passive voice. The striker is Allah, but the framing is impersonal, ceremonial, like a coin being minted in the divine treasury and placed into the public hand.

Why? Why not just say, *the false gods are weak; abandon them*?

The answer is one of the most tender things in the Quran, and it doesn't get talked about often.

Allah is **meeting His creature where the creature is looking**.

The Meccans looked at flies every day. Their entire religious environment was full of flies. Allah, in His pedagogy, did not call them up to the abstract heights where philosophical proofs of monotheism live. **He came down to the level of their feet, where the dust and the flies were, and said: look here. The proof is here.**

This is the same pattern that runs through the entire Quran. He does not say "consider the metaphysics of contingent being." He says "consider the camel — how it was created" (88:17). He does not say "reflect upon the doctrine of providence." He says "look at your food" (80:24). **The Quran's pedagogical genius is that it never asks the listener to climb. It descends to where the listener already stands and shows them that the proof is there.**

Stay with this. Because it tells you something about how Allah works with you.

When you are far from Him, He does not require that you first ascend. He sends His signs to where your eyes already rest. **Whatever you are looking at right now is already a place He could meet you in.** The fly on the windowsill. The breath in your chest. The child's hand. The way light moves through the room. The Quran is teaching you that the divine signature is not hidden in the high abstractions. It is on the surface of your daily seeing — but you have to be willing to look.

[PAUSE]

And there is a second mercy in the parable form.

A direct command — *abandon your idols* — would have shamed the listener. It would have created a confrontation. It would have made the Meccan defensive.

A parable does something different. **A parable invites the listener to render the verdict themselves.** Allah does not say "your gods are weak." He says: "they cannot create a fly. They cannot recover what a fly takes. Now you tell me what to call them."

The verdict — *ḍaʿufa aṭ-ṭālibu wal-maṭlūb* — comes at the end, but by the time the listener hears it, they have already arrived at it. The conclusion has been earned in their own minds. **Allah has not lectured them. He has handed them the materials and let them build the verdict themselves.**

This is the model for how the Quran does *daʿwah*. Not by overpowering. By inviting. By placing a fly between the worshipper and the worshipped and letting the worshipper see what they had refused to see.

[PAUSE]

Practical application: **When you see something true that someone you love is refusing to see, do not lecture them. Find their fly.** Find the small, ordinary thing in their life that, if they really looked at it, would render the verdict for them. Then place it gently in front of them and step back. The Quran's pedagogical method is also a model for how to speak to anyone in difficulty. Don't tell. **Show — at the level of where they are already looking — and let them name it themselves.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Look at the structure of the ayah now that we've walked through it:

**Opening:** *O people* — universal address, not just believers
**The frame:** *A parable is struck, so listen* — divinely minted, demanding attention
**The first impossibility:** *Cannot create a fly* — failure of generative power
**The intensifier:** *Even if they gathered together* — failure across all possible coordination
**The second impossibility:** *Cannot recover what the fly snatches* — failure of defensive power
**The verdict:** *Weak are the seeker and the sought* — collapse spreading across every party

The ayah is a **chiasm of weakness**. It opens by addressing humanity, narrows down to the false god's incapacity, reaches its tightest point at the fly, then expands back outward to the verdict that catches everyone — fly, false god, worshipper — in its net.

Allah moves the camera from the cosmos (*O people*) down to the smallest creature (*the fly*) and back out to the universal verdict. The geometry of the ayah is itself the argument: **everything from the cosmos to the insect bears witness, and only Allah stands outside the verdict.**

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The choice of *dhubāb*, the closure of *lan walaw ijtamaʿū*, the spreading verdict of *aṭ-ṭālib wal-maṭlūb* — they are not three separate observations. They are one motion: **Allah, with surgical precision, naming what the human heart has been refusing to look at, in the language of the most ignorable creature in the room.**

And the themes — the modern idols and the divine pedagogy of the parable — are the same insight applied: **what the Meccan refused to see in the temple courtyard, we refuse to see at our desks and on our screens.** And the same mercy that came down to the level of the fly to teach the Meccan is still coming down — to the level of whatever you are looking at right now — to teach you.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. **In a moment of fear at 2 a.m., what does my heart turn to first? Be specific. Name it.**

2. **Can what I just named create a fly? Can it recover from a fly what the fly takes?**

3. **What is the modern offering on which the modern flies are walking — and what would it mean for me to actually look at it?**

4. **When was the last time I let Allah speak to me through the small, ordinary thing right in front of me — instead of waiting for some grand sign?**

5. **Where in my own daʿwah, my own teaching, my own conversations with people I love, have I been lecturing instead of placing a fly between them and what they refuse to see?**

### One-sentence distillation

**Every false god, in every age — from stone idol to career to algorithm — is weaker than the smallest creature it cannot create or defend itself against; only Allah stands outside the verdict of weakness.**

### The closing invitation

This is not an ayah that asks you to do anything spectacular. It does not call you to sacrifice or struggle or strive. **It calls you to look.**

To look, with honesty, at what your heart actually turns to. To look at the small things you have stepped over for years. To look at the divine signature on the fly on your windowsill. To look at the elaborate stories you have told yourself about what your idols can do, and to test those stories against the smallest creature you can find.

Allah is not asking you to ascend. He is asking you to stop refusing to see what is already in front of you.

The fly was on the offering. The Meccan looked away. **Don't look away.**

### Du'a

*Yā Allāh — You who created the fly and the cosmos, You who do not need parables but mint them in mercy for hearts like ours: open our eyes to what we have refused to see. Show us the small idols we have built without naming. Strip from us the false gods our hands have carved out of careers and reputations and screens. Let us look — really look — at the fly, and at every small thing You have placed in our lives as a sign, until our hearts say only Your name when the dark hour comes. You alone are not weak. You alone create. You alone defend. To You alone we turn.* Āmīn.
