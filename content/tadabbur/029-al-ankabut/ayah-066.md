# The Ingratitude That Wears the Mask of Enjoyment

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 66

### Arabic

> [ayah:29:66] لِيَكْفُرُوا۟ بِمَآ ءَاتَيْنَـٰهُمْ وَلِيَتَمَتَّعُوا۟ ۖ فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ

### Transliteration

> *Li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum wa-li-yatamattaʿū — fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.*

### Translation

> "So let them deny what We have given them, and let them enjoy themselves — for soon they will know."

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we sit with these eleven words, we need to know where they land.

The surah is called Al-Ankabut — the Spider. The image governing the whole chapter is a fragile web. Houses people build for themselves that look like shelter but, when the wind comes, hold nothing. Affiliations, dependencies, gods, schemes — all woven out of the same gossamer.

In the ayah just before ours, Allah paints a scene the original listener in Makkah would have recognized instantly. He says: *when they board the ship*, and the waves rise around them like dark mountains, *they call upon Allah, making their religion sincerely His.* In that moment, all the partners disappear. All the idols. All the negotiations. There is one Owner of rescue and they know His name and they cry it out with everything they have.

Then He delivers them. Their feet touch the harbor. And the moment the salt water dries from their sandals — *they associate partners with Him again.*

That is the scene.

This ayah — our ayah, 66 — is what Allah says next. It is His response to that turning of the face. And what makes it shattering is not anger. It is something far more unsettling than anger.

Stay with me. Because the first time you hear this ayah, it might sound almost casual. It might sound like Allah is shrugging. *Fine. Let them. Let them deny what I gave them. Let them enjoy themselves. They'll find out.*

That is not what is happening.

There is a grammatical construction in these eleven words that is doing something so specific, and so devastating, that once you see it you will not be able to un-see it. There is a verb form in the middle of this ayah whose entire morphological shape is a warning. And there is a single missing word — a word your Arabic ear *expects* and Allah deliberately withholds — whose absence is louder than anything He could have said.

Let's go slowly.

---

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The *Lām* That Is Not Permission

Before we touch the ayah, I want you to think about a construction we use in English all the time, and we don't notice we're doing it.

A parent tells a child: *don't touch the stove, it's hot.* The child reaches for it anyway. The parent sees they cannot stop them in time. And what does the parent say?

*"Fine. Touch it. You'll see."*

Did the parent *want* the child to touch the stove? No. Did the parent *forbid* the child from touching it? They tried, and the child ignored them. So what is "fine, touch it" doing grammatically?

It looks like permission. It is shaped like permission. But it is not permission. It is what Arabic grammarians call *al-amr lit-tahdīd* — a command-form used for **threat**, or *takhliyyah* — **withdrawal**. The speaker has stopped intervening. They are letting consequence become the teacher.

Now open the ayah.

> لِيَكْفُرُوا۟ بِمَآ ءَاتَيْنَـٰهُمْ وَلِيَتَمَتَّعُوا۟

That *lām* at the beginning of *li-yakfurū* and *li-yatamattaʿū* — the *lām al-amr*, the command-*lām* — is grammatically an instruction. Translated flat, it reads: *let them deny, let them enjoy.*

A listener with no ear for context might think Allah is **permitting** kufr and **permitting** indulgence. As if He were saying: *go on, this is allowed.*

[PAUSE]

But notice where this *lām* falls. It comes **immediately after** the scene of the people on the ship, who called Him sincerely, were saved, and then walked back into associating partners. The *lām* is not opening a door for them. The *lām* is **stepping back** from a door they have already kicked open.

It is the divine equivalent of *"fine. Touch the stove."*

Classical grammarians read this *lām* here in two related registers, and we don't need to choose between them — both are working at once. One register reads it as **threat** — Allah is daring them in the way you dare someone who is already committed to ruin: *go ahead, see what happens.* The other reads it as **withdrawal** — Allah is announcing that He is no longer arguing with them, no longer pleading, no longer sending the storm to wake them. He is letting them have what they keep grabbing for.

Both readings converge on a single feeling: **the most frightening thing in the universe is not Allah's punishment. It is Allah's silence after warning.**

The *lām* is that silence in grammatical form.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment. What does it mean for the One who made you to say, in effect, *go ahead* — and mean it not as endorsement but as the **end of the conversation**?

It means the warning phase is closing. It means the alarms have already rung. It means we have arrived at the part of the story where the speaker is no longer trying to change the listener's mind.

That *lām* — that one letter — carries all of that weight.

### Element Two: The Verb *Yatamattaʿū* — Enjoyment with a Stopwatch

Now move to the second verb. *Wa-li-yatamattaʿū.*

This is the verb that the listener should pause on the longest. Because of all the verbs Allah could have used for "enjoy" — and Arabic has many — He chose this one. And the choice is not decorative. It is a definition.

Let me teach you something about Arabic verb forms first, because this is load-bearing.

Arabic builds verbs in patterns called *abwāb* — Form I, Form II, all the way up to Form X. Each form takes a basic root meaning and twists it in a specific direction. Form II often intensifies a meaning. Form V — the form on the pattern *tafaʿʿala* — typically does something very particular: it makes the verb **reflexive** and **gradual**. It is something the subject does **to themselves**, often through a process, often involving **acquisition** or **immersion**.

The verb here, *yatamattaʿū*, is Form V from the root *m-t-ʿ*.

The root *m-t-ʿ* in classical Arabic lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib) is built around a very specific concept: enjoyment that is **bounded by time**. The lexicographers note that the noun *matāʿ* refers to goods, provisions, things you carry with you — things that, by their very nature, you will leave behind. The Quran itself uses *matāʿ* repeatedly for the goods of this world: useful, real, enjoyable, and *temporary*.

So the root is not about pleasure in the abstract. The root is about **provision-with-an-expiry-date**.

Now apply Form V to that. *Tamattaʿa* — to give *oneself* the experience of *matāʿ*. To wrap oneself in temporary enjoyment. To absorb oneself, immerse oneself, in pleasures the verb already knows are running out.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has done here. He has not said *let them be happy.* He has not said *let them have joy.* He has chosen a verb whose **morphological architecture** has an expiration clock built into it.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* working through a verb. The physical, sensible concept of *matāʿ* — the worn-out carpet, the cracked bowl, the worldly goods that will be left behind when the caravan moves on — is being used to point at a spiritual reality: that any happiness pursued in denial of the One who gave it is, by its grammatical nature, **already on a timer**.

When Allah says *wa-li-yatamattaʿū*, He is not granting them a long indulgence. He is naming the indulgence for what it is. He is saying: *let them sink themselves into what was always going to end.*

There is no Arabic word for "enjoy" that does not carry baggage. *Yafraḥū* would have carried connotations of giddy delight. *Yastalidhdhū* would have carried connotations of relishing. But *yatamattaʿū* carries connotations of **using up provisions**. Like someone eating from a sack of grain — every spoonful enjoyable, every spoonful one spoonful closer to empty.

That is the verb Allah selected.

And He selected it right next to *yakfurū* — let them **cover up**. Because the root *k-f-r* in Arabic does not primarily mean "disbelieve" in the modern academic sense. The lexicons attest that *k-f-r* originally describes a farmer who **buries seed** in the soil. The *kāfir* is one who **covers** something. To deny Allah's blessing is to take what He has given you and *put dirt over it* — to bury it under your own forgetfulness, your own attribution, your own *I-did-this-myself*.

So put the two verbs side by side.

*Li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum wa-li-yatamattaʿū.*

Let them **bury** what We have given them, and let them **use up** what's left.

[PAUSE]

That is what the ayah is actually saying. The translation "let them deny and let them enjoy" is honest, but it is wearing a flat coat over a structure that is doing two precise things at once: an act of covering, paired with an act of consuming.

The denial *enables* the enjoyment. You cannot fully give yourself over to *matāʿ* unless you have first put dirt over the question of *who provided this.* The kufr is the lid you have to close before the indulgence can happen. The two verbs are not separate sins. They are a single mechanism: **bury the Giver, then enjoy the gift.**

### Element Three: The Missing Object — *They Will Know* What?

Now I want to ask you to do something subtle. I want you to read the last three words and listen for what is **not** there.

> فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ
> *Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.*

"For soon they will know."

Stop. Breathe. And ask: *they will know* — **what?**

In Arabic, the verb *ʿalima* — "to know" — is what grammarians call a *fiʿl mutaʿaddī*, a transitive verb. It naturally takes an object. *I know* — what? You know — what? The sentence expects something to follow. *They will know — the punishment. They will know — the truth. They will know — that I was right.*

Allah does not provide it.

He has used what classical balāghah calls *ḥadhf al-mafʿūl* — the deliberate omission of the object. The verb is left hanging into open air. And this is not poetic carelessness. This is a precision instrument.

Let me explain why. When a speaker omits the object of a verb of knowing or seeing or hearing, classical Arabic rhetoric understands this as a signal that the object is **too vast to name, or too dreadful to name, or too varied to enumerate.** The omission is the very thing that creates the dread. By refusing to fill in the blank, Allah forces the listener to fill it in themselves — and the listener's imagination, primed by the rest of the surah, fills it in with everything.

What will they know?

They will know who actually gave them their breath. They will know who answered them on the ship. They will know what the *matāʿ* was worth when the *matāʿ* ran out. They will know what they buried, and what was buried under it. They will know what they should have known. They will know — *everything.*

[PAUSE]

The English translation "they will know" is impossible to do justice to, because in English the verb feels small when its object is missing. In Arabic, the missing object is what makes the verb **enormous**.

And there is one more thing about these three words.

The particle *sawfa* — "soon" — is the longer of two Arabic future particles. There is *sa-* (a prefix), and there is *sawfa* (a free-standing word). Grammarians have long noted that *sawfa* is used for a future that is more **distant** or more **certain** — sometimes both. Some classical lexicographers say *sa-* is the closer future and *sawfa* the farther one; others say *sawfa* simply carries more emphatic certainty. Either way, what is being signaled here is not "in a moment." It is something more like: *this is coming, and it does not need to hurry.*

Combine this with the omitted object. Combine it with the *lām* of withdrawal. Combine it with the verb whose morphology has an expiration clock.

What Allah has built in these eleven words is a single, slow, calm sentence whose entire architecture says: **the consequence is not in a hurry, because the consequence is already certain.**

### Bridge

We have looked at three things: a command-*lām* that is not really a command; a Form V verb whose morphology contains its own ending; and a verb of knowing whose object is missing because the object is everything.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about a pattern in the human heart that almost all of us carry.

---

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Ship We Forget We Were On

Think about the last time you were genuinely afraid for someone you love.

A scan. A phone call at the wrong hour. A child's fever that wouldn't break. A test result you were waiting on. A relationship you thought was about to end.

If you have lived long enough to know fear like that, then you know what your duʿāʾ sounds like when fear strips you down. There is no eloquence. There is no ego. There is just: *Yā Allah.* Just: *please.* Just a name and a breath and everything inside you pointing in one direction.

The ayah before ours describes exactly that. The waves are like mountains. The ship is groaning. The merchants who, on dry land, had a dozen idols and a dozen schemes and a dozen names to call on — in *that* moment, they have one name. *Mukhliṣīna lahu al-dīn.* Sincerely. Wholly. Only Him.

This is not a flaw in human nature. This is human nature *working correctly.* Pressure burns away the lies we tell ourselves about who is sustaining us. Fear is sometimes the only honest weather we ever experience.

Then the ship docks.

And here is the part that should make every one of us pause. Allah does not say *some of them.* He does not say *some who are weak in faith.* The pronoun is plural and unqualified. When they reach shore — they associate partners.

Why is this universal?

Because the moment safety returns, something in the human heart reaches automatically for **partners**. Not always idols of stone. Sometimes the partners are a doctor we are praising more than the One who gave the doctor skill. Sometimes the partners are our own effort: *I beat that diagnosis because I was tough.* Sometimes the partners are our network, our luck, our timing, our smarts. The associates change. The reflex doesn't.

And this is where our ayah lands. Allah, having just described this reflex, says: *li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum.* Let them bury what We gave them.

Notice: He does not say *what We will give them.* He says *what We have given them — āṭaynāhum,* perfect tense, completed action. The blessings are already in their hands. The deliverance is already in their lungs. The denial isn't a denial of some future favor. It is a burying of the rescue that just happened.

[PAUSE]

This is the haunting part. We are not the people on the shore in some other story. We **are** the people on the shore. Every one of us has been pulled out of something — a financial crisis, a depression, a fear, an illness, a relationship — and within months, sometimes within days, we have authored a new narrative about how we got out, and Allah's name has slid out of the story.

That is the burying. That is the *kufr* in the lexical sense. Not philosophical atheism. *Forgetfulness as practice.*

What does the ayah ask of us, then? It asks one specific thing. It asks us to keep a **list of ships**. To remember, by name, the storms we were brought through. To resist the autobiographical pull that wants to make us the hero of our own deliverance.

The practical move: tonight, before you sleep, name three storms Allah brought you through that you have stopped thanking Him for. Not the ones from this week. The ones from five, ten, fifteen years ago. The ones whose rescue you have now built a non-Allah story around.

That is the unburying. That is the opposite of *li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum.*

### Theme Two: The Joy with the Stopwatch Inside It

Now move to the second verb. *Wa-li-yatamattaʿū.*

I want to bring this one through an image you already know.

You know the experience of eating something delicious — and the very last bite. The way the last bite is different from the others. The way, even mid-chew, you are mourning that there isn't more. The way the pleasure of the last bite is mixed with the smallness of the plate.

That is *matāʿ.* All worldly enjoyment, by its lexical nature, carries the last-bite quality. Every joy in this world is being consumed even as you experience it.

This is not a pessimistic claim. This is a structural claim. The Quran is not telling us *don't enjoy.* The Quran is telling us *don't be deceived about what kind of enjoyment this is.*

There is a kind of enjoyment that is layered on top of gratitude — where the pleasure of the food sits *on top of* awareness of the Giver. That kind of enjoyment expands. It opens into something larger. It connects.

And there is a kind of enjoyment that is layered on top of forgetfulness — where the pleasure of the food is consumed *as a way of not thinking* about who provided it, or about what is missing, or about what waits. That kind of enjoyment closes. It shrinks. It demands more next time to reach the same place.

The ayah is describing the second kind. *Li-yakfurū... wa-li-yatamattaʿū.* The burying first; then the enjoyment that requires the burying.

Modern psychology has a name for this without knowing it has a name for it. The hedonic treadmill. The phenomenon where pleasure-seeking, divorced from meaning, requires escalating doses to produce the same effect. Researchers describe it as if it were a glitch in human reward circuitry. The Quran describes it as the **predictable result** of severing enjoyment from acknowledgment of the Giver.

You cannot fully *taste* what you have not first *thanked.* Because tasting without thanking turns the gift into a possession, and possession is anxious, and anxiety is hungry, and hunger needs more.

[PAUSE]

This is why Allah's verb here has an expiration clock built into its morphology. Not because pleasure is bad. Because pleasure unlinked from its Source is, by structure, **already running out**.

The practical move on this one is harder than the first. The practical move is: the next time you are about to enjoy something — a meal, a piece of music, an embrace, a sunset, anything — say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* **before** you enter the enjoyment, not after.

Not as a transaction. Not to "earn" the pleasure. Just to refuse to let the *kufr* — the burying — get installed underneath the *tamattuʿ.* To enjoy from a place where the Giver is still in the room.

That is the inversion of the ayah. The ayah describes burying-then-consuming. The Muslim's life is meant to be acknowledging-then-receiving. Same act of pleasure. Entirely different architecture underneath.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have, now, in eleven words.

The *lām* of withdrawal — Allah stepping back from a conversation the listener has refused to enter.

The verb *yakfurū* — burying the Giver under the gift.

The verb *yatamattaʿū* — a Form V whose morphology contains an expiration clock.

The omitted object after *yaʿlamūn* — a knowing so total that no single noun could carry it.

The particle *sawfa* — a future that does not need to hurry, because it is already certain.

Read together, these five elements draw a single picture. A picture of a moment in which the divine pedagogy has shifted from **argument** to **letting**. Not because Allah has stopped caring. Because the only teacher left is the experience itself.

And here is what makes the ayah profound rather than merely terrifying: the ayah is not aimed at the doomed. The ayah is aimed at *us*, the readers, the listeners, the ones who still have time, the ones who can hear the *lām* of withdrawal and step back from the door before it closes behind us.

The ayah is a description of a process so that we can interrupt the process in ourselves.

### Questions to Carry

1. What was the last storm Allah pulled me through — and how long after the rescue did I rewrite the story to remove Him from it?

2. When I enjoy something today, am I enjoying it *on top of* my acknowledgment of the Giver, or *instead of* it?

3. Are there areas of my life where Allah has gone quiet — and have I mistaken His silence for His absence, when it might be the *lām* of withdrawal, the silence after warning?

4. What am I currently burying — what gift, what mercy, what answered prayer — under a story in which I am the hero?

5. If the verb *yatamattaʿū* contains its own expiration, what would change about how I hold the pleasures of this week if I held them with that ending visible?

### One-Sentence Distillation

To bury the Giver in order to fully consume the gift is to inherit a joy that is, by its own grammar, already running out — and Allah, after warning, eventually lets that lesson teach itself.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before sleep, do two things. First: name three storms. Storms Allah brought you through whose rescue you have stopped thanking Him for. Speak His name back into those stories. Second: tomorrow, before your first pleasure of the day — the first sip of coffee, the first bite of breakfast, the first moment of warmth — say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* **before** the pleasure, not after.

Those two small acts are the structural opposite of *li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum wa-li-yatamattaʿū.* They are the unburying, and they are the enjoyment that does not require forgetfulness. They are, in eleven words of practice, the answer to eleven words of warning.

### A Brief Du'a

*Yā Allah, do not let me be among those who bury what You have given. Let me name Your gifts before I consume them, and let me remember the ships I rode through storms while their wood was still wet beneath my feet. Save me from the joy that requires forgetting You, and grant me the joy that knows You are in the room.*
