# The Parables and the People Who Can See Them

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 43

> [ayah:29:43] وَتِلْكَ ٱلْأَمْثَـٰلُ نَضْرِبُهَا لِلنَّاسِ ۖ وَمَا يَعْقِلُهَآ إِلَّا ٱلْعَـٰلِمُونَ
>
> *Wa tilka al-amthālu naḍribuhā lin-nāsi, wa mā yaʿqiluhā illā al-ʿālimūn.*
>
> **"And these parables — We strike them for mankind, but none truly grasps them except those who know."**

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Verse

Before we touch a single word of this ayah, we have to step back and see *where* it is placed. Because in the Quran, position is meaning.

Two verses earlier, Allah had given humanity one of the most quoted images in His entire Book:

> *"The likeness of those who take other than Allah as protectors is the likeness of the spider, who takes for itself a house — and indeed, the frailest of houses is the house of the spider — if only they knew."* (29:41)

A spider's web. Geometrically perfect. Visible only when the light catches it just right. So strong it can hold a struggling insect, so weak that the lightest breath can dissolve it. That image is the structural heartbeat of this entire surah. The surah is even *named* after it — *al-ʿankabūt*, "The Spider."

So picture the listener in the first audience. They have just been handed this image. Some of them are nodding — *yes, I see it, the false gods, the false alliances, the false securities of my tribe, all of it is a web*. Others are sitting with arms crossed. *A spider? What does a spider have to do with anything? This is poetry, not guidance.*

And in that *exact* moment — when half the room has seen it and half the room has not — Allah does not move on. He turns *toward* the listener. He stops talking *about* the parable and starts talking *about parables themselves*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. The verse that follows the spider is not another image. It is Allah pulling back the curtain on what just happened. Allah is, in a sense, narrating His own pedagogy. He is telling you, in the very moment you are receiving a parable, what a parable *is*, who it is *for*, and — most unsettling of all — who actually *receives* it.

Eleven words. A verse about every other verse. A mirror placed in front of the listener that asks, with absolute calm: **Did you see it? And if you didn't — what does that say about you?**

That is what we are sitting with tonight.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three linguistic features to open. Each of them, taken alone, looks small. Taken together, they remake the verse.

---

### Element One: *Tilka* — The Word That Points Far Away

The verse opens with one word that almost every translation flattens: **tilka**.

We read it as "these." *"And these parables..."* But that is not what *tilka* actually means in Arabic.

Let me teach the concept first, because in English we have lost this distinction.

In English we have two demonstratives: "this" and "that." *This* is near, *that* is far. We have the bare minimum.

Arabic is more precise. Arabic has at least three layers of demonstrative:

- **hādhā** — *this*, the thing right here, within arm's reach
- **dhālika** — *that*, the thing over there, at some distance
- **tilka** — the *feminine* counterpart of *dhālika*, also pointing to something at distance, but often something elevated, something held at a certain ceremonial remove

Think of how we point at things in real life. If I am sitting at a table with a cup of tea in my hand and I want you to know about it, I say *this cup*. But if I am standing at a museum, gesturing at a Persian miniature painting twenty feet away on the wall, I say *that one* — or in more elevated speech, *that masterpiece over there*. The grammar changes because the *relationship* between me, the object, and you has changed. Distance creates reverence. Distance creates gaze.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah does.

He has *just* given the parable of the spider. It is the most recent thing the listener has heard. By any normal logic, He should say *hādhihi al-amthāl* — *these* parables, the ones right here, the one I just gave you.

He does not.

He says **tilka al-amthāl** — *those* parables. The far ones. The elevated ones. The ones held at a distance.

Stay with me. Why would Allah point to a parable He just gave as if it were far away?

Here is what this invites us to consider. The parable, the moment it leaves Allah's speech and enters the air, is no longer a casual conversational object. It rises. It is elevated. It is now part of *the body of divine speech*, and divine speech is something the listener does not stand level with — the listener stands beneath it, looking up.

Notice something else. *Tilka* is feminine plural, but the grammar is doing something the English translation cannot show. By using *tilka*, Allah is *not* just referring to the spider parable. He is gesturing at the whole *corpus* — every parable, every *mathal*, that has ever been or will ever be in His Book. The spider was one star. *Tilka* opens the night sky and says *all of these*. The lightning, the rain, the fly, the gnat, the donkey carrying books, the dog panting, the seed, the tree, the ant, the hoopoe — all of them. The entire constellation of Quranic parables, held at the proper distance so you can see the whole pattern.

One word. *Tilka*. And it has already done two things: it has dignified the parable, and it has expanded it from one image to a whole tradition.

### Element Two: *Naḍribuhā* — Allah Does Not Tell Parables. He *Strikes* Them.

The next word is one that absolutely cannot be flattened, though almost every translation flattens it.

We render it: *"We give them"* or *"We set them forth"* or *"We coin them."* All of these are too polite.

The Arabic is **naḍribuhā**. From the root *ḍ-r-b*. And the primary, physical, undeniable meaning of *ḍaraba* is **to strike**. To hit. To impact with force.

Now — Arabic verbs, like English verbs, can extend. We can say *strike a deal*, *strike a chord*, *strike a match*. *Ḍaraba* extends similarly in Arabic — *ḍaraba fī al-arḍ* means to travel (to *strike out* in the land). And one of its most well-documented extensions is **ḍaraba mathalan** — to coin, to set forth, to put forth a parable.

But here is the question every classical commentator on the Arabic tongue has asked: when an extended meaning is used, the *physical core* never fully disappears. It echoes. It colors. It bleeds through.

So why this verb? Of all the verbs Allah could have chosen for the act of giving a comparison — *qāla* (to say), *bayyana* (to clarify), *jaʿala* (to make) — why this verb of *impact*?

Let me ask you to live inside this for a moment.

A parable is not a piece of information that is *handed* to you. A parable is *struck against* you. You are the surface. The parable is the hammer.

Think of how a bell works. A bell, sitting silent, is just a piece of metal hanging in the air. It contains no sound. The sound is *latent*. It only becomes sound when something *strikes* it. The striker does not insert sound into the bell — the striker *releases* what the bell was already capable of.

[PAUSE]

This is what Allah is doing.

The parable is the strike. *You* are the bell. The sound — the meaning — is not in the parable alone, and it is not in you alone. It happens at the *moment of contact*. And whether any meaning rings out at all depends entirely on what kind of bell you are.

Hit a cracked bell — and nothing. A dull thud. The strike was the same; the surface was wrong.

Hit a full, intact, ready bell — and the air fills with resonance that has nothing to do with the size of the hammer.

This is why the very next clause of the verse becomes inevitable. Because if a parable is a *strike*, then Allah is going to have to tell us something about which surfaces actually ring.

There is one more layer here. *Naḍribuhā* — *We strike them*. The verb is in the first person plural, the royal "We" of divine majesty. And it is present-tense, continuous. *We are striking. We continue to strike.* This is not a one-time event. This is the ongoing posture of Allah toward humanity. He is, right now, in this hour, in your living room, *striking* parables against the hearts of mankind. Some are ringing. Some are dull.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Asymmetry Between *Al-Nās* and *Al-ʿĀlimūn*

Now we come to the structural heart of the verse. And to see it, we need to look at *who* is mentioned and, more importantly, *who is missing*.

The verse splits into two clauses:

1. *naḍribuhā lin-nāsi* — *We strike them for mankind*
2. *wa mā yaʿqiluhā illā al-ʿālimūn* — *but none truly grasps them except those who know*

Two groups are named. **Al-nās** — humanity, all of them, the broad sweeping totality. And **al-ʿālimūn** — those who know, those with knowledge.

The structure looks symmetric at first. Allah strikes parables *for* one group, but only *one subset* receives them. Fine. We have seen this kind of thing before — many are called, few are chosen, the standard pattern.

But here is what is asymmetric, and you will miss it if you read too fast.

When Allah names the first group — *mankind* — He does not modify them. He does not say *the righteous of mankind*, or *the believers of mankind*, or *the wise of mankind*. He says just **al-nās**. All of humanity. The whole species. The parables are *thrown wide* — across the whole field — to every human being who has ears.

But when He names the second group, He does *not* say *the believers* (*al-muʾminūn*). He does *not* say *the pious* (*al-muttaqūn*). He does *not* say *the prophets* or *the scholars in turbans* or any social category at all.

He says **al-ʿālimūn**. *Those who know*.

Stay with this. The qualifier is not religious affiliation. The qualifier is not piety. The qualifier is not credentials. The qualifier is a relationship with *knowing*.

[PAUSE]

And there is a second asymmetry, even more striking, hiding inside the verb.

*Wa mā yaʿqiluhā* — *and none reasons through them*, *none truly grasps them*. The verb *yaʿqilu* comes from the root *ʿ-q-l*. The *ʿaql* — the faculty of binding, of tying together, of restraining. In Arabic, the original sense of *ʿaql* is the rope that ties the camel's leg so it does not wander. The *intellect*, then, is the faculty that *binds* — that holds two things together long enough to see how they connect.

A parable is a comparison. *A is like B*. The spider's house is like the false god. The act of seeing this requires a specific cognitive movement: you must *bind* the image of the spider's house to the idea of false worship and hold them together until they fuse into a single insight. That binding — *that act* — is *ʿaql*.

So when Allah says *mā yaʿqiluhā illā al-ʿālimūn*, He is saying something extremely specific. *None binds these (parables) to their meaning except those who know.*

The asymmetry is now visible. The strike goes out to everyone. The capacity to bind the strike to its meaning belongs to a particular kind of person.

And notice what is *not* said. Allah does not say the parables are *hidden* from some people. He does not say the parables are *encoded*. The parable is right there, plain, in the open. The spider's house is a perfectly ordinary image. A child could see it. The failure is not in the encoding. The failure is in the *binding*.

[PAUSE]

Now — who are *al-ʿālimūn*? The classical commentators looked at this with care. Some, like al-Ṭabarī, took the broad lexical reading: those with sound knowledge, in the most general sense. Others, like al-Rāzī, narrowed it: those who know *Allah*, specifically — those whose knowledge has produced *taqwā* in them. And a third strand, found in Ibn al-Qayyim and elsewhere, brought the two together: the *ʿālim* is the one whose knowing has *moved* — the knowing has descended from the head into the heart and out into the limbs.

Do not pick one and discard the others. Each is a camera angle on the same person. The *ʿālim* of this verse is the one whose intellect is awake enough to bind, whose knowledge of Allah is real enough to want to bind, and whose life is responsive enough to be changed by the binding.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### Bridge Out of the Linguistic Journey

Look at what these three words have done, when we hold them together.

*Tilka* — these parables are elevated, plural, part of a whole sky of divine images. Hold them at the proper distance and you will see the constellation.

*Naḍribuhā* — Allah does not whisper parables. He strikes them. You are the bell. The meaning lives in the contact, not in the parable alone.

*Illā al-ʿālimūn* — the strike goes wide, but only certain surfaces ring. Not because the parable is hidden, but because the binding faculty must be alive.

Three words. And we have not yet asked what this reveals about us — about how human beings actually move through revelation, and why two people sitting next to each other can hear the same ayah and walk away living in two different universes.

Now let us ask what this verse reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Hierarchy of Listening

Imagine two students sitting in the same lecture hall. The same professor. The same words. The same hour.

One walks out and says, *that was a waste of time*. The other walks out shaking, and says, *I think my life just changed*.

You have seen this. Maybe you have *been* both of them, in different rooms.

We tend to chalk it up to attention, or mood, or the chemistry of the moment. But there is something deeper going on, and we know it. The information was identical. The reception was not. Something inside one of those students was *ready* to receive what was said. Something inside the other was not. And readiness, it turns out, is not a function of intelligence. It is a function of *what you have been doing with yourself*.

This is the thing the ayah is naming.

Allah is not describing a system in which some people are gifted with secret access codes and others are not. He is describing something far more sober. He is describing the *fact* that human beings do not all bring the same self to the same words.

[PAUSE]

The parable hits the air. *Naḍribuhā*. The strike is universal — *lin-nās*. Everyone in earshot is hit by the same wave. But the wave, when it lands, finds different surfaces. It finds a heart busy resenting its neighbor. It finds a mind exhausted from chasing a promotion. It finds a soul half-numb from a year of unprayed prayers. And it finds, somewhere in the same room, a person who happened — by grace, by struggle, by repetition of small acts of remembering — to be *awake* in that moment.

The wave does not change. The surface determines the sound.

Here is the part that should rearrange us. We tend to think of *understanding* as something that happens *during* the words. *I will pay attention this time, I will concentrate, I will get it.* But this verse tells us the truth: understanding is decided *before* the words ever arrive.

Whether you grasp the spider parable today was determined by what you did with your attention, your appetites, your prayer life, your relationships *yesterday*. The parable strikes. What rings is *what you built to be struck*.

Now connect this to *al-ʿankabūt* itself — the spider. The spider builds a house. Day after day, thread after thread. The parable about the spider is, on its surface, about the *fragility* of what the spider builds. But there is a quieter truth here. *Every one of us is also building a house*. Every habit is a thread. Every choice is a strand. We are weaving, every day, the structure that will or will not be capable of holding the meaning when Allah's parable lands.

You cannot decide, in the moment a parable arrives, to suddenly be the kind of person who can hear it. You can only *be* such a person, or not, when the moment comes.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow morning?

It changes the question we ask before we open the Quran. The usual question is *what will I learn today?* The deeper question, the one this verse pushes us toward, is: *what kind of surface am I bringing today?*

If the answer is, *honestly, a dull one* — then the work before the Quran is not more Quran. It is the work of restoring the surface. A walk. An istighfār. A genuine sajdah. A phone call to someone you have wronged. *Then* the Quran. Because *naḍribuhā* — He is striking. He is always striking. The only question is what He is striking against.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Quiet Architecture of *ʿAql* and *ʿIlm*

The second theme is harder. It is about two words this verse braids together that English collapses into one: *ʿaql* and *ʿilm*. *Intellect* and *knowledge*.

In English, these feel like cousins. Intellect is what you have; knowledge is what you accumulate. We tend to think of them as roughly the same kind of thing.

In the Quranic vocabulary, they are doing very different work, and this verse places them in a very specific order.

Look again. *Wa mā **yaʿqilu**hā illā al-**ʿālim**ūn*. The act is *yaʿqilu* — to bind, to reason, to grasp by tying together. The actor is *al-ʿālim* — the one who knows.

So the verse is saying: *the act of binding the parable to its meaning belongs to the one who already knows.*

Stop and feel how strange this is. Normally we would expect the opposite. We would expect: *those who reason come to know.* You think hard, you connect dots, you arrive at knowledge. That is the modern, post-Enlightenment story of cognition. Reason produces knowledge.

Allah inverts it. *Knowledge produces the capacity to reason through the parable.*

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete with something everyone has lived.

Have you ever read a book at twenty, found it boring or confusing, and then re-read the same book at forty and been *destroyed* by it? Same words. Same sentences. Same paragraph breaks. But at twenty you had no *prior knowledge* — no losses, no betrayals, no quiet sufferings — for the book to *bind to*. At forty, you bring a self that has lived. And suddenly every line is a hook that catches on something real.

The book did not change. *You became someone who could be struck by it.*

Now extend that. Imagine a person who has never once seriously considered their own death. They read *every soul shall taste death* (3:185), and they nod. They *understand* the sentence. But they cannot *yaʿqil* it. The sentence cannot bind to anything inside them, because they have not yet built the inner referent. They have the words. They lack the *interior knowledge* the words are reaching for.

Now imagine the same person, years later, sitting at the bedside of a dying parent. They read the same ayah. And it does not even feel like the same verse.

That second moment is what Allah is naming when He says *illā al-ʿālimūn*. Not those who memorized the most. Those whose interior has been *furnished* — by living, by struggling, by remembering Allah through the seasons — with enough *real referents* that the parable has somewhere to land.

[PAUSE]

This has a hard implication, and we should not dodge it.

It means that *more information will not save you*. You cannot brute-force your way into *taʿaqqul* by reading more books. The faculty that binds parables to their meaning is built upstream of reading. It is built in *how you live with what you already know*. The person who knows three ayahs deeply, who has let three ayahs eat into their behavior, will *yaʿqil* the fourth one when it comes. The person who knows three hundred ayahs at the surface will not.

There is a saying among the early generations: *kāna al-ʿilmu yusta'tā wa lā ya'tī* — *knowledge is something you go to; it does not come to you*. But there is a deeper version of this. Knowledge does not come to those who do not already, in some real sense, *know*. The parables are everywhere. They are striking everyone. They only reverberate inside the soul that has done the prior work of becoming a place where reverberation is possible.

So what does this change about how we live tomorrow?

It changes the goal. The goal is not *to know more*. The goal is *to live more deeply with what you already know*. One verse — really, truly inhabited — opens fifty more. Fifty verses, lightly skimmed, open nothing.

If you read this whole ayah tonight and only sat seriously with the spider parable for an hour — let your eye walk over your own house, your own attachments, your own webs that you mistake for walls — you would have done more for your *capacity to yaʿqil* than reading a hundred more pages.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at where this verse sits in the surah, and what it does to the spider.

The spider parable is in 29:41. The verse about parables is in 29:43. In between, in verse 42, Allah says: *"Indeed, Allah knows whatever they invoke besides Him. And He is the Exalted in Might, the Wise."*

Look at the structure:

- v. 41: **The parable** (the spider)
- v. 42: **Allah's knowing** (He knows what you really worship)
- v. 43: **The parable about parables** (and only the *knowers* can grasp it)

Three verses. And what is the word that runs through the middle one and reappears in the third? *Knowing*. *ʿAlima*. *Al-ʿālimūn*.

The architecture is silently saying: *Allah knows. Now — do you?* The parable is offered. Allah's own *knowing* is asserted. And then the listener is told that the parable only opens up to those who *also* are characterized by knowing — by a knowing that, however small, is of the same kind as His.

The spider, the divine knowledge, and the parable about parables — these are not three separate verses. They are one motion. Allah hands you an image. Allah names His own omniscient knowing. And Allah tells you that to *receive* the image, you must yourself become a knower.

You see the spider's web because you have done the slow work of refusing to live in one.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight from two sides.

The words told us: *tilka* (the parables are elevated), *naḍribuhā* (they are struck like a bell), *illā al-ʿālimūn* (only certain surfaces ring).

The themes told us: *understanding is decided before the words arrive*, and *knowledge is the soil that makes reasoning possible — not the other way around*.

Both are saying the same thing. Allah is offering Himself, constantly, through images that surround us — spiders, mountains, rain, lightning, gardens, ashes, smoke, fly-wings, ants. The offer is universal. The reception is not. And the difference is not in *how clever* you are. The difference is in who you have been *becoming*.

### Questions to Carry

1. When the Quran strikes me this week — and it will, in some ayah, some image, some line — what kind of surface will it find?

2. Am I trying to *yaʿqil* the Book on the strength of intellect alone, when the verse is telling me intellect needs *ʿilm* of a deeper kind first?

3. What house am I building, thread by thread, with my daily habits? Is it a house that could hold the weight of a parable when one lands?

4. Of all the parables of the Quran I have read — the seed, the lightning, the mountain, the gnat, the spider — which one have I *actually let strike me*? And which ones have I let pass over me like background music?

5. If I knew that Allah were *striking* a parable at me right now, through some event in my life this week — would I be able to read it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah is constantly striking parables against the surface of every human being, but only those who have already, in some real way, become knowers can hear the bell ring.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

You are not asked, after this verse, to read more parables. You are asked to *become someone who could receive one*.

That work happens between the ayahs. In how you spoke to your spouse this morning. In whether you returned a glance or lowered it. In whether you remembered Allah while waiting in line, or scrolled instead. In whether, when something small went wrong today, you cursed it or you whispered *al-ḥamdu lillāh*.

These are the threads of the surface. These are what determine, when the next parable lands, whether anything sounds at all.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who strike Your parables against the hearts of mankind — make our hearts among the surfaces that ring. Grant us a knowing that lives in us, not one that merely passes through us. And when You send us an image — a spider, a seed, a sudden loss, a quiet mercy — open in us the eye that binds it to You.*

*Āmīn.*
