# The Catalogue of Ruin — What Allah Did Not Do

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 40

---

### The Arabic

> فَكُلًّا أَخَذْنَا بِذَنبِهِۦ ۖ فَمِنْهُم مَّنْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِ حَاصِبًا وَمِنْهُم مَّنْ أَخَذَتْهُ ٱلصَّيْحَةُ وَمِنْهُم مَّنْ خَسَفْنَا بِهِ ٱلْأَرْضَ وَمِنْهُم مَّنْ أَغْرَقْنَا ۚ وَمَا كَانَ ٱللَّهُ لِيَظْلِمَهُمْ وَلَـٰكِن كَانُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

### Transliteration

*Fa-kullan akhadhnā bi-dhanbihi, fa-minhum man arsalnā ʿalayhi ḥāṣiban wa-minhum man akhadhat-hu al-ṣayḥatu wa-minhum man khasafnā bi-hi al-arḍa wa-minhum man aghraqnā. Wa-mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn.*

### English

So each [of them] We seized for his sin: among them were those upon whom We sent a violent storm of stones; and among them were those whom the Blast overtook; and among them were those whom We caused the earth to swallow; and among them were those whom We drowned. And it was not Allah who wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before you sit with this ayah, you have to sit with the strangeness of *where* it appears.

Surah Al-Ankabut opens with one of the most disquieting questions in the entire Quran: did the people think they would be left alone to say *we believe* without being tested? The surah is named after a creature — the spider — and after this catalogue of ruin you are about to enter, Allah will describe the worship of anything besides Him as a spider's web. The frailest house in existence.

But before He shows you the web, He shows you the wreckage.

The middle of the surah moves through prophet after prophet. Nuh and his people. Ibrahim and his confrontation. Lut and the city flipped upside down. Shu'ayb and Madyan. And then a sentence later: Qarun, Fir'awn, Haman — each one with his rebellion and each one with his end. By the time we arrive at our ayah, the listener is exhausted. Story after story. Generation after generation. The same shape of refusal, the same shape of destruction.

[PAUSE]

And here, Allah does something extraordinary. He does not give us another story. He gives us the audit. A single sentence that catalogues, in one breath, the four methods by which entire civilizations were taken — and then, immediately, in the same breath, He removes Himself from any accusation of injustice.

Imagine listening to this for the first time in Makkah. You are part of a small, persecuted community. You have just heard the names of nations who were powerful, established, who had cities and walls and gods and wealth. And every one of them is gone. And the One narrating is telling you not just *that* He did it, not just *how* He did it — but that He owes you, before you even ask, an answer to the question your heart has not yet dared to form: *was this fair?*

Here is what is stunning. The verse spends most of its breath listing destructions — and then spends its weight, its final architectural emphasis, on something Allah did *not* do. The catalogue is the setup. The denial is the climax.

What does it mean that Allah will not let the ledger close until He has told you He did not wrong them?

That is the question we are about to live inside.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word *Fa-kullan* — and the Strange Geometry of "Each"

Let me first teach you something about Arabic that will let this opening word land.

In English when we say "each of them," it lands flat. It is a distributive — it spreads attention across a group equally. But Arabic has a precise grammatical shape for what is happening here, and that shape is called *taqdīm* — fronting. Putting a word in front of where you would expect it, because the meaning lives in the *position*, not just the word.

The expected order would be: *akhadhnā kullan bi-dhanbihi* — "We seized each one for his sin." Verb first, then object.

What Allah does instead is: *fa-kullan akhadhnā bi-dhanbihi.* He puts the *kullan* — the "each" — out front. And the *fa-* at the beginning means *so*, *therefore* — it is the consequence-marker. The verse before has just listed the cast: ʿĀd, Thamūd, Qārūn, Firʿawn, Hāmān. And then comes the verdict word: *so each.*

Notice something. The *tanwīn* — that *-an* sound at the end of *kullan* — marks it as indefinite-but-comprehensive. It does not mean "each in general." It means *every single one* of the ones just named, individually, without exception, without remainder.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that for a moment. This is not a casual sweep of history. Allah is going down a list, name by name, and stamping *taken* next to each one. The fronting of *kullan* before the verb means: before you even hear what was done, you hear that *no one slipped through.*

And here is what is remarkable. The phrase that follows — *bi-dhanbihi* — uses the singular pronoun *hi*, "his." Not "their sin." Not "the sin of nations." *His sin.* Each one for *his* sin. The grammar refuses to let destruction be a collective fog. It particularizes. Every person seized was seized for something *individually his*.

This is the first thing the ayah does to the listener. It compresses a sweep of history into a courtroom, and inside the courtroom there is no anonymous defendant. The geometry of the sentence is: collective announcement, individual indictment.

What this invites us to consider is that Allah's accounting does not flatten people into nations even when His action levels nations. The destruction is collective in scale, but the reckoning is singular in texture. *Each — for his.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Four Verbs — and the Asymmetry Hidden Inside Them

Now look at the four destructions. The ayah lists them in a parallel structure: *wa-minhum man... wa-minhum man... wa-minhum man... wa-minhum man.* And among them was one... and among them was one... You can feel the rhythm in Arabic — it has the cadence of a roll call.

But here is the thing. Listen to *who* does the action in each one. Because Arabic grammar will tell you something English translations smooth over.

**Number one:** *arsalnā ʿalayhi ḥāṣiban* — "We sent upon him a *ḥāṣib*." Active. *We* sent. The pronoun "We" is Allah Himself, directly. The verb is in the first-person plural of majesty. Allah names Himself as the agent.

**Number two:** *akhadhat-hu al-ṣayḥatu* — "The *ṣayḥah* took him." Look at this carefully. The subject of the verb is not "We." The subject is *al-ṣayḥah* itself — the Blast. The Cry. The phenomenon, named with the definite article *al-*, as though it were a known force, takes hold of him. Allah has stepped out of the syntactic foreground.

**Number three:** *khasafnā bi-hi al-arḍa* — "We caused the earth to swallow him with him." Active again. *We* caused. Allah back in the syntactic foreground.

**Number four:** *aghraqnā* — "We drowned." Active. *We* drowned.

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened. Three of the four verbs put Allah in the agent position — *We* sent, *We* sank, *We* drowned. But the second one — the *ṣayḥah* — pulls Him out. The Cry is the subject. The phenomenon does the seizing.

This is not random. The *ṣayḥah* — etymologically rooted in *ṣ-y-ḥ*, the root for *crying out*, for a piercing sound that splits the air — is associated in the Quran most often with the people of Thamud and the people of Shuʿayb. It is a sound. A scream from the sky. And when Allah narrates it, He lets the sound itself be the actor, because part of the horror of the *ṣayḥah* is that you do not see who sends it. You only hear it. And then you are gone.

So the grammar mirrors the experience. When the destruction comes by storm of stones, you can imagine the storm. When the earth opens, you can imagine the opening. When the flood rises, you can imagine the water. But the Cry — there is nothing to look at. There is only a sound, and the sound is the end of you.

Even *more* than this, look at the four verbs as a set. *Ḥāṣib* — stones from above. *Ṣayḥah* — sound from the air. *Khasf* — collapse from below. *Ighrāq* — water surrounding from all sides.

[PAUSE]

Above. Around. Below. Surrounding.

This is not a list. This is a sealing-off of every direction a creature could try to escape from. There is nowhere a created being can move that is not already a possible instrument of his accounting. The four directions are not four punishments; they are the four walls of a cell that the disobedient never knew they were already standing inside.

What this reveals is that Allah did not invent new methods for the disbelievers. He used the elements that were sustaining them. The same air they breathed. The same ground they walked on. The same sky they slept under. The same water they drank. The very world that had been their home was always also the instrument of their reckoning, and they could not see it until the moment it turned.

That is a terrifying thought. But notice — it is also, on the other side of itself, a tender one. Because if the world becomes the instrument of reckoning only at the moment of refusal, then every moment before that, the same air, ground, sky, and water have been holding you in mercy.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa-mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum* — The Grammar of Denial

We now arrive at the architectural climax. After listing four destructions, Allah turns and says: *wa-mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn.*

Let me teach you the structure first, because in Arabic this is one of the strongest forms of negation that exists.

The phrase *mā kāna li-* in Arabic does not just mean *did not.* It means *it would not befit*, *it is not in the nature of*, *it is not for*. When Allah says *mā kāna Allāhu li-* — He is not just denying an action. He is denying the very *possibility* that the action could come from Him. It would be against His essence. It would contradict who He is.

Think about the difference. If a friend asks, "did you steal my book?" and you answer "no, I didn't steal it" — that is a denial of action. But if you answer "I would never do that to you" — that is a denial of *capacity within character*. The second is heavier. The second appeals to who you are, not just what happened.

That is the construction here. *Wa-mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum.* It would not befit Allah to wrong them. The act is excluded not just from His history but from His nature.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the placement. Allah has just finished telling you, in graphic, parallel, four-fold detail, that He sent stones from the sky, made the earth swallow people, and drowned entire populations. And the very next clause says: *and it would not befit Allah to wrong them.*

Stay with the audacity of that sequence. Most teachers, most narrators, most authority figures, when describing punishments they themselves have inflicted, will spend more breath on *why* the punishment was deserved than on the punishment itself. Allah does the inverse. He gives us four direct, vivid, unblinking images of His own seizing — and then, before we can even form the question, He answers it: *I did not wrong them.*

This tells you something about how Allah understands the human heart. He knows. He knows that when human beings hear of mass destruction, even of those they would intellectually agree deserved it, something in the heart hesitates. Something wants to ask. And Allah does not wait for the question to be asked. He answers it before it has fully formed.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the contrast. *Wa-lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn.* But it was their *own selves* they were wronging.

The grammar here is doing extraordinary work. Look at the placement of *anfusahum* — "their own selves." In a normal sentence, the object follows the verb: *yaẓlimūna anfusahum*, "they wrong their selves." But Allah fronts the object: *anfusahum yaẓlimūn.* "Their own selves — they were wronging."

This fronting in Arabic creates *qaṣr* — restriction, exclusivity. The meaning becomes: it was *specifically their own selves* — not anyone else — that they were wronging. Not Allah. Not creation. Not even, in the deepest sense, each other. *Themselves.*

And the verb *kānū... yaẓlimūn* — using *kāna* with the imperfect — gives you continuous past tense. *They used to keep on wronging themselves.* It was not one act. It was a habit. A posture. A direction of life.

[PAUSE]

What this single closing clause does is invert the entire moral geometry the listener has been holding. We came into this ayah expecting to hear about Allah's action upon them. And we did. But the ayah closes by telling us that the real story — the *actually consequential* story — was their action *upon themselves*. Allah's seizing was the outer event. Their self-wronging was the inner one. And the inner one is what determined the outer.

The destruction did not begin when the stones fell. The destruction began long before — in every moment they chose to walk against their own *fiṭrah*, their own nature, their own clearest knowing. The stones were the visible part. The self-wronging was the actual ruin, and the ruin had already happened.

[PAUSE — major]

### The Bridge

Three things this linguistic construction has revealed. *Fa-kullan* — that no one is missed in Allah's audit, and no one is collectivized either. *The four directions* — that the very world holding us is also the instrument of accountability, which means both: it can turn, and it has not yet turned. And *mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum* — that Allah, even when narrating His own seizing, places a denial of injustice into the same sentence, because He knows the human heart will hesitate, and He refuses to let the hesitation stand unanswered.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Quiet Self-Destruction That Precedes the Loud One

Here is something almost every adult has watched happen, either to someone they loved or, more uncomfortably, to themselves.

Someone begins drifting. At first no one can quite name it. They are still showing up. Still answering messages. Still functioning. But something has shifted in the interior. A small lie told to themselves and accepted. A boundary they always held that they have quietly stopped holding. A relationship they keep neglecting that they tell themselves they will return to next month. A practice that used to be central — prayer, study, kindness — that has become decorative.

And on the outside, nothing visible has happened. They are not in crisis. There is no event.

Then, one day — sometimes years later — there is an event. A marriage collapses. A career implodes. A reputation evaporates. A health crisis. A loss that seems to come out of nowhere.

And everyone who watches says: *what a sudden change.*

But it was not sudden. The visible event was just the moment the invisible accumulation finally surfaced. The actual destruction had been happening in slow motion for a long time, in private, in places no one was looking. The outer collapse was the last domino. The inner collapse had been falling for years.

[PAUSE]

That is what this ayah is telling you about the nations Allah destroyed. The stones, the cry, the earth, the flood — these are the *visible* moments. Spectacular, undeniable, in the historical record. But Allah's grammar tells you the actual story was happening invisibly long before. *Kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn* — they *used to keep on* wronging their own selves. Continuous. Habitual. A direction of life that had been compounding interest in private for generations.

The Quran is teaching us a counterintuitive principle: *the loud destruction is the receipt, not the transaction.* The transaction happened in countless quiet moments where a soul refused what it knew, lied to itself a little more, drifted a little further, and called the drifting *normal*.

This is why Allah, in another place, says of human beings: *Allah does not wrong people in the least, but it is the people who wrong themselves.* It is one of the most repeated theological signatures in the Quran. The Quran refuses to let the human being externalize the location of his ruin.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with this — because it is the door to something tender, not just terrifying.

If the destruction begins in the inner self-wronging long before it becomes outer, then *so does the rescue*. The reverse must also be true. If a thousand quiet moments of self-betrayal compound into a visible collapse, then a thousand quiet moments of returning — small ones, unwitnessed ones, ones no one will give you credit for — compound into a visible mercy.

The same mathematics. Just running in the other direction.

This is what *tawbah* means. Not a single dramatic event of repentance. *Tawbah* in Arabic is from *tāba* — to *return*. To re-orient. It is not a moment. It is a direction.

The nations were destroyed because they chose, over and over, in private, the direction *away*. We have the same choice running in our own lives right now. Not in some abstract way. In the next small decision. The next conversation we are tempted to have dishonestly. The next prayer we are tempted to skip. The next reflexive cruelty we are tempted to indulge. The next truth about ourselves we are tempted to bury.

[PAUSE]

The practical application: tonight, before you sleep, ask one question. Not "am I a good person?" — that question is too large to be useful. Ask instead: *what is one place in my life where I am currently wronging myself, and pretending it is something else?*

That is the place the ayah is pointing at. Not the catastrophe you fear. The quiet self-betrayal you have not yet named.

Because the ayah is saying: by the time the stones fall, it is too late. The work was supposed to happen long before. And the mercy Allah is showing you, *right now in this moment of reading*, is that you are still in the *long before*.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The God Who Defends Himself Before You Accuse Him

Here is something you have probably felt and may not have given language to.

When something terrible happens — to you, to someone you love, to a community, to the world — there is often a delay before the theological question arrives. First there is the shock. Then there is the grief. And then, sometimes weeks or months later, in the dark, the question forms: *was this fair? Did God do this? And if He did, what does that mean about who He is?*

Most religious traditions hand you the answer to that question later. After you have stewed. After you have struggled. After you have, sometimes, walked away in pain because no one would meet the question directly.

What this ayah does is unprecedented. Allah answers the question *before you have asked it.*

He has just told you, in cinematic detail, that He sent stones from the sky and made the earth open and drowned entire populations. The natural human response — and Allah knows the human He created — is the question. *Was this just?* Even if intellectually we would say yes, something in the heart wants to ask.

And before that something can finish forming the question, Allah says: *and it would not befit Allah to wrong them.*

[PAUSE]

Think about who does this in human relationships.

A friend who, when you express a frustration, says "I know, you don't even have to say it, I've already thought about it." A parent who, before you can complain about a decision, explains why they made it. A teacher who, before you can ask, says "I know this seems harsh; here is the reason."

This is the move of someone who has *already considered your interior*. Who knows what you are about to feel before you feel it. Who is not waiting for the accusation to be filed before mounting the defense.

Now translate that to the Divine. Allah does not need to defend Himself. He owes no one an explanation. He is the *Mālik*, the Owner. If destruction had been arbitrary, He would still be Allah. The denial of injustice in this ayah is not a defense He owes; it is a courtesy He extends.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what this means about His relationship to you, personally.

When something hard happens in your life, and the theological question begins to form, Allah is not waiting on the other side of your accusation with His arms crossed. He has already, in His book, answered the question before you raised it. He has placed, in the Quran, dozens of versions of this same sentence: *Allah did not wrong them, but they wronged themselves.* He has put it in your hands long before the crisis arrived, so that when the crisis arrives and the question forms in the dark, the answer is already there — placed in advance, like a lamp left on in the hallway before you walked into the house.

The theological insight is this: *Allah's mercy is not only what He gives. It is also what He pre-empts.* Some mercies are positive — a blessing arriving. Other mercies are pre-emptive — a doubt that never gets to take root because the answer was already provided. This ayah is the second kind. He is doing protective work on your faith before you knew you would need the protection.

[PAUSE]

Now there is a difficult corner of this theme that we need to walk into.

What about the people who were destroyed? Yes, the ayah tells us they had wronged themselves. Yes, the prophets had come to them. Yes, the evidence had been delivered. But there is still — in the modern reader's heart — sometimes a flicker of *that is harsh*.

The ayah is not asking you to feel nothing about the destruction. It is asking you to relocate the source of your discomfort. If the destruction disturbs you, the ayah says, *that disturbance is not properly aimed at Me. Aim it at the self-wronging. Aim it at the long, slow refusal to return. Aim it at the prophets they killed, the messengers they mocked, the truth they buried. Allah's seizing came at the end of a process they ran for generations. The seizing did not initiate. The seizing concluded.*

This is theologically precise. Divine justice in the Quran is never disconnected from a long, patient, *istidrāj*-bearing process in which the human being is given time, then more time, then signs, then warnings, then prophets, then opportunities for return. The seizing is what happens after the door has been held open for so long that holding it open any longer would itself be a kind of cosmic dishonesty.

[PAUSE]

The practical application: when something difficult arrives in your life, before the theological question takes root, do what Allah did in this ayah — answer it pre-emptively. Tell yourself, out loud if you need to: *Allah does not wrong. Whatever the meaning of this is, it is not that.* Hold that as the floor of the room. Then, from inside that floor, ask the real questions — what is being shown to me, what return is being invited, what self-wronging am I being asked to see.

This is not denial. It is theological grounding. The ayah is teaching you the order of operations. First, anchor that Allah does not wrong. Then, search for the meaning. If you flip the order, you will spend years interrogating Him instead of yourself, and the years will not yield anything.

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Look now at the shape of the entire ayah. It has a structure you may not have seen on the first hearing.

It begins with a *single statement of comprehensive seizing*: *fa-kullan akhadhnā bi-dhanbihi.* Then it opens into a *fourfold parallel*: storm, cry, earth, flood. Then it closes with a *single statement of theological precision*: Allah did not wrong, they wronged themselves.

One — four — one. A frame opened. A catalogue inside. A frame closed.

And notice: the opening frame and the closing frame both deal with the *moral* status. The opening: *each for his sin*. The closing: *they wronged themselves*. The middle is the *physical* catalogue. The architecture is: moral, physical, moral. The physical is sandwiched inside the moral, because the physical is the *consequence*, not the cause. The moral is the actual story.

This is the surah's signature. Al-Ankabut is the surah of the spider's web — the frailest house. And this ayah is the ledger explaining why the houses fell: not because Allah pushed them, but because they were built on a self-wronging that could not bear weight.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the same insight from two directions.

The grammar told us: each person is individually indicted, the four directions are sealed, and Allah pre-empts the question of His justice before we can ask it.

The themes told us: the loud destruction is the receipt of a quiet self-destruction that preceded it, and Allah defends Himself in advance because He knows our hearts will hesitate.

Both are saying the same thing: *the real action is in the inner life of the human being, and Allah is more interested in giving you mercy about that interior than in winning an argument about His justice.*

The ayah is not really about *them*. It is about *you*. The four nations were the case studies. The audit is yours.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life is the *quiet self-wronging* currently compounding, in a place I have not yet named?

2. If destruction had been my portion, would I have been able to identify the moment it became inevitable — or would I, like them, have been surprised?

3. What is one place where I am still in the *long before* — where the door is still open — and what would it look like to walk through it this week?

4. When difficulty has arrived in my life, what has been my reflex: to interrogate Allah, or to anchor in *mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlim* first and then ask the real questions?

5. If Allah pre-empts the question of His justice before I can ask it — what does that tell me about His patience with my doubt, and how should that change the tone of my prayers?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah's seizing is the visible end of a long invisible self-wronging — and the same sentence that names the seizing also pre-empts the question of His justice, because He cares more about anchoring your heart in His mercy than about defending Himself against an accusation you have not yet made.

### The Closing Invitation

Something should shift after this. Not a feeling of fear at the four destructions — that is the surface reading. What should shift is a *re-location of moral attention*. Away from the spectacular and toward the quiet. Away from interrogating God and toward auditing the self. Away from waiting for crisis to course-correct and toward course-correcting in the unspectacular present.

The ayah is offering you the most generous thing it can offer: it is showing you the receipt before you finish the transaction. You can still close the account. You are still in the *long before*.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who have told me — before I dared ask — that You do not wrong, anchor my heart on that floor when the difficult things come. Show me the quiet self-wronging in my life while the door is still open, before the door closes. And let me be of those who return to You in small moments, in countless small moments, so that when the final seizing of every soul comes, You find me already turned toward You.

Āmīn.
