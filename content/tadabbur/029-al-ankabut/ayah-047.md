# And Thus We Sent Down to You the Book

## The Quiet Confidence of a Revelation That Recognizes Itself

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَنزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ ۚ فَالَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِهِ ۖ وَمِنْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ مَن يُؤْمِنُ بِهِ ۚ وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا الْكَافِرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa kadhālika anzalnā ilayka al-kitāb. Fa-alladhīna ātaynāhum al-kitāba yuʾminūna bih. Wa min hāʾulāʾi man yuʾminu bih. Wa mā yajḥadu bi-āyātinā illā al-kāfirūn.*

**Translation:**
"And thus We have sent down to you the Book. So those to whom We gave the Scripture believe in it. And among these [people] are those who believe in it. And none reject Our signs except the disbelievers."

---

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine for a moment that you are a man in Mecca, perhaps fifty years old, and for nearly a decade you have been telling your people something they will not accept. Your closest companions are mocked in the street. Your uncle, who used to shield you, has died. Your wife, who used to comfort you, has died. You walked to a city called Ṭāʾif hoping for sanctuary and came back bleeding. The leaders of your tribe have offered you wealth and kingship if you will only stop. They have offered you medical treatment if you will only admit you are mad. Some nights you wonder if anyone besides the small handful around you will ever understand what you are carrying.

This is the world into which Surah Al-Ankabut descends. The surah opens with a question that hits like cold water on the face: *Did the people think they would be left alone to say, "We believe," and not be tested?* And then it walks through the trials of prophet after prophet — Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, Lūṭ, Shuʿayb, Mūsā — as if to say to the Prophet ﷺ: *you are not the first to feel this alone.*

Then comes the famous image. The people who take protectors besides Allah are like the spider who built a house. *And surely the flimsiest of houses is the house of the spider, if only they knew.* The whole surah is a long meditation on what is real and what is gossamer.

And then — right before our ayah — Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ: *Do not argue with the People of the Book except in the way that is most beautiful, except with those of them who do wrong, and say: "We believe in what was sent down to us and what was sent down to you. Our God and your God is One, and to Him we submit."*

This is the moment our ayah arrives. The Prophet ﷺ has just been told how to speak to the People of the Book. And now, before he goes to do it, Allah leans in — and this is the ayah He speaks.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

There is something strange about this ayah. Allah is comforting His Prophet ﷺ. But notice how He does it. He does not say, *"I am with you."* He does not say, *"Your enemies will be defeated."* He does not promise victory, or wealth, or numbers.

He says something much quieter. He says: *the people who already have scripture — they recognize this Book.*

What kind of comfort is that?

Stay with that question. Because by the time we are done with this ayah, you will understand that this is the most precise comfort Allah could have given a Messenger who was being told, every day, *you made this up.* And you will see that the same comfort is being offered to anyone today who has felt utterly alone in what they know to be true.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa kadhālika* — "And thus"

Before we look at our ayah, let me ask you about something you do every day.

When someone asks you, *"How do you know your friend?"* you might say, *"I met her the way you meet most people — through a mutual friend."* Notice what you just did. You explained a specific event by gesturing at a pattern. You said: *this is part of how things normally work.* You did not justify it. You simply located it inside something larger.

This is what the Arabic word *kadhālika* does. It is built from two pieces: *ka-*, meaning "like" or "in the manner of," and *dhālika*, meaning "that." Literally: *like that.* Functionally: *and in this same manner.* It is a word that gestures backward at something already established and says: *this new thing belongs to the same pattern.*

Now look at how the ayah opens.

**وَكَذَٰلِكَ أَنزَلْنَا إِلَيْكَ الْكِتَابَ**

*"And thus We have sent down to you the Book."*

Notice something. The ayah does not begin with an argument for the Quran. It does not say *"Here is why the Quran is from Us."* It does not say *"We have sent down to you the Book."* It says: ***thus*** *We have sent down to you the Book.*

Like that. In that same way. The way We always do this.

Let's sit with this. To whom is *kadhālika* pointing? The entire surah before this ayah has been a tour through prophetic history. Nūḥ for nearly a thousand years. Ibrāhīm thrown into fire. Lūṭ ridiculed in his own home. Shuʿayb dismissed by his people. Mūsā chased by Pharaoh. And what did Allah do for each of them? He sent guidance. He sent scripture. He sent signs.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah is not introducing the Quran to the Prophet ﷺ as if it were a strange new thing. He is locating it. He is saying: *this Book you are carrying — it is not an anomaly. It is the latest expression of something I have been doing since the beginning. The same way I sent guidance to those before you, the same way the previous Books came down — that is the way this Book has come to you.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what that does to the Prophet ﷺ in this moment?

The Quraysh have been telling him for ten years: *you are alone. You are making this up. No one before you ever said anything like this. You are an aberration.* And Allah's response — His comfort — is the single word *kadhālika.* **You are not an aberration. You are a continuation.**

This is *istidlal* by precedent. The argument is not in what the word says explicitly. The argument is in the pattern it points to. And the pattern is the whole of revelation history.

There is one more thing to notice. The verb *anzalnā* — *We sent down* — is in the perfect tense, the completed form. It is not "We are sending" or "We will send." It is *We have already sent.* By the time this ayah is recited, the act is — from Allah's perspective — done. Even though the Quran was still being revealed piece by piece over years, Allah speaks of it as a single completed sending. The Book exists. The work is done. The Prophet ﷺ is not waiting on it; it has already been placed in his hands.

What this single word *kadhālika* reveals: **the comfort Allah offers His Messenger is not novelty but lineage.** You are not alone because you are not first. The line behind you is long, and you stand at its end carrying its full weight.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Yuʾminūna bih* — They believe in it

Now I want you to think about a moment you may have lived through.

You are at a dinner. Someone is telling a story about a city you have never been to. They are describing the streets, the smell of the markets, the way the light falls in the afternoon. The whole table is nodding along. And then, in the middle of all this, you happen to glance at one other person at the table — and you can tell, from the look on their face, that they have *been there.* They are not nodding because they trust the storyteller. They are nodding because they recognize what is being described.

That recognition is a different kind of confirmation than agreement. It is the testimony of someone who already knows.

Now look at what the ayah says:

**فَالَّذِينَ آتَيْنَاهُمُ الْكِتَابَ يُؤْمِنُونَ بِهِ**

*"So those to whom We gave the Scripture believe in it."*

Notice the verb. *Yuʾminūna* is the present-tense, ongoing form of the verb of belief — from the root *ʾ-m-n*, which carries the meanings of safety, security, and trust. *They are believing in it.* Not *they will believe*; not *some of them once believed*; but *they are, in an ongoing way, affirming it.*

And notice who is doing the believing. *Alladhīna ātaynāhum al-kitāb* — *those to whom We gave the Scripture.* This is a phrase Allah uses for the People of the Book — Jews and Christians who received the Tawrāh and the Injīl.

Stay with this. Allah is not saying *some Arabs in Mecca believe in this.* He is saying *the very people who already received scripture — they are affirming this.* Why does that matter? Because they are not naïve. They are not people who would believe just any claim of revelation. They have a baseline. They know what divine speech feels like. They know what a Book from Allah sounds like. They have the Tawrāh in their hands; they have the Injīl in their hearts. And when they hear this — they recognize it.

There is a verse elsewhere that says of these very people: *those to whom We have given the Book recognize it as they recognize their own sons* (2:146). The Arabic word there is *yaʿrifūnahu* — they *know* it, the way a father knows the face of his child in a crowd. There is no mistaking it.

So when our ayah says *yuʾminūna bih,* it is the same recognition expressed as ongoing trust. They are not converting from one religion to another in shock. They are looking at something and saying: *yes. This. I know this voice.*

[PAUSE]

Now let's notice something even more subtle. The verb is plural and present-tense, but Allah does not say *kulluhum* — *all of them.* He says *alladhīna ātaynāhum al-kitāb yuʾminūna bih* — *those to whom We gave the Book believe in it.* In Arabic this construction does not mean every single recipient of previous scripture, but rather it functions to identify the class of person being described — *the kind of people who actually received scripture; the kind who actually know what scripture is — they affirm this.*

This is why the next phrase becomes so important.

**وَمِنْ هَٰؤُلَاءِ مَن يُؤْمِنُ بِهِ**

*"And among these [people] are those who believe in it."*

Notice the shift. From the plural *yuʾminūna* — *they believe* — to the singular *yuʾminu* — *he believes.* From *alladhīna* — *those who* — to *man* — *whoever, the one who.*

Among the People of the Book, the language is plural and confident: *they believe.* But among *hāʾulāʾi* — *these ones,* meaning the Meccans, the Arabs in front of the Prophet ﷺ — the language becomes singular and selective: *among these, the one who believes.*

This is not an accident. This is the texture of the moment. Among the People of the Book, belief is already an established phenomenon — a recognizable pattern, a community of recognition. Among the Meccans, in the year this ayah was revealed, belief is still rare. It is individual. It is one person at a time slipping into Islam, often in secret, often at great cost. Allah's grammar captures the demographic of belief in that very year.

But here is what is breathtaking. He does not say *and from these, none believe.* He says *and from these, the one who believes.* The singular is not a complaint. It is an honoring. Each one who believes among the Meccans is named in the singular because each one is precious. Each one is a story. Each one matters enough to deserve their own pronoun.

[PAUSE]

And consider what this means for the Prophet ﷺ in that moment. He looks around Mecca and sees a few dozen believers. He has watched his uncle die outside the faith. He has watched his daughter buried by a hostile city. And Allah is saying to him: *do not measure the truth of this Book by the number of Meccans who accept it. Measure it by the fact that those who already know what revelation is — they recognize this. And as for these in front of you — count them one by one. Each one is enough.*

What this construction reveals: **belief that is forced into the singular is not weaker belief — it is more visible belief. When few are believing, every believer becomes legible.**

### Element Three: *Mā yajḥadu* — They do not reject

Now we come to the strangest word in the ayah. And in some ways, the most precise.

The ayah ends:

**وَمَا يَجْحَدُ بِآيَاتِنَا إِلَّا الْكَافِرُونَ**

*"And none reject Our signs except the disbelievers."*

The English translation says *reject.* But the Arabic word *yajḥadu* — from the root *j-ḥ-d* — does not just mean "to reject." Let me teach you this word, because once you understand it, this ayah opens up.

Arabic has many words for refusing to believe. There is *kafara* — to cover, to conceal. There is *kadhdhaba* — to call something a lie. There is *aʿraḍa* — to turn away. Each of these describes a different posture toward truth.

But *jaḥada* is special. *Jaḥada* means **to deny something while inwardly knowing it to be true.** It is the word used in Arabic for a man who denies a debt he knows he owes. It is the word used in legal contexts for false testimony. Classical lexicographers explain *jaḥada* as the speech-act of one who recognizes and refuses at the same time. The mouth says no while the heart says yes.

This is a profoundly specific word.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah just said. He has spent the first part of the ayah describing the people who *recognize* the Book — the People of the Book, the believing Meccans. And then He says: *and no one rejects Our signs except the disbelievers.*

But notice: He does not say *no one fails to know Our signs.* He does not say *no one is confused about Our signs.* He says *no one* yajḥadu *Our signs.* No one **denies what they have seen and known to be true.**

This is one of the quiet thunderclaps in this ayah. Allah is making a claim about the inner state of those who reject the Quran. He is saying that rejection of His signs is not, at its root, an intellectual failure. It is not that the signs are unclear. It is not that the evidence is missing. The signs of Allah are clear enough that to reject them, one has to *jaḥada* — one has to look at them, recognize them, and turn away anyway.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment. The Quraysh of Mecca were not stupid. They were merchants, poets, navigators of the most sophisticated oral culture of their world. They knew the Prophet ﷺ. They had called him *al-Amīn* — the Trustworthy — for forty years before he ever spoke a word of revelation. They knew this Book was beyond his unaided power. There is a story of one of their leaders, ʿUtbah ibn Rabīʿah, returning from a meeting with the Prophet ﷺ saying: *I have heard speech that is not poetry, not soothsaying, not the speech of men. I do not know what it is.* He went back and told his people: *let him be — there is something here.* But his people refused. They knew. And they refused anyway.

That is *jaḥada*.

And so when Allah says *no one* yajḥadu *Our signs except the disbelievers,* He is making a statement that cuts in two directions at once.

First, it is a statement about the signs: **they are so clear that to refuse them, you have to refuse with your eyes open.** This is not a faith for people who lack evidence. This is a faith whose evidence is loud enough that you have to *suppress* it to remain outside.

Second, it is a statement about the disbeliever: **the one who rejects the Quran is not someone who could not see. He is someone who would not.** The word *kāfir* itself, from *k-f-r,* means *one who covers over.* You cannot cover something you cannot see. To cover requires that the thing be there.

[PAUSE]

There is a final point about this word that I want you to hold. *Yajḥadu* is in the present tense, which in Arabic carries the meaning of ongoing, habitual action. Not *they rejected once.* But *they are, continually, rejecting what they know.* It describes a life-state. A posture sustained over time.

What this reveals: **the deepest rejection of Allah is not ignorance — it is knowledge held in the dark.**

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

We have walked through three words now. *Kadhālika* — *thus.* The single word that placed this whole revelation inside a lineage. *Yuʾminūna bih* and *man yuʾminu bih* — believers among the People of the Book in the plural, believers among the Meccans in the singular. And *yajḥadu* — the word for rejection-with-recognition.

These three movements are doing one thing together. They are telling the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, us — that the truth of revelation does not depend on its popular reception. It depends on its recognition by those qualified to recognize it, and on the fact that those who refuse it are refusing what they have already seen.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Comfort of Being Recognized

I want to start somewhere uncomfortable.

There is a particular loneliness that almost everyone has tasted at some point in their life. It is the loneliness of *carrying something true that no one else can see.* You see what is wrong with the relationship; everyone else thinks it is fine. You sense what is rotten in the company; everyone else is enthusiastic. You feel the meaning in a piece of music; everyone else changes the station. You watch your friend slipping toward something destructive; no one else is alarmed.

This is one of the heavier weights a human being can carry. To know, and to not be believed.

Psychologists call it *epistemic loneliness* — the isolation of being right, or believing one is right, in the absence of confirmation. Children experience it when adults dismiss them. Whistleblowers experience it inside corrupt institutions. Trauma survivors experience it when their experiences are minimized. Anyone who has ever said *I am telling you what I saw* and had it waved away knows this loneliness.

Here is what's interesting. The brain seems to be wired such that this kind of loneliness is not just sad — it is destabilizing. When no one confirms what you are perceiving, you begin to doubt your own perception. Your grip on what you know loosens. You become uncertain not just of the world but of yourself.

Now imagine being the Prophet ﷺ.

For ten years he has been telling the people of Mecca that the words coming through him are from Allah. For ten years they have told him he is lying, or mad, or possessed. They have not been gentle about it. They have thrown stones. They have boycotted his family. They have buried his daughter. Every single day is another day in which the truth he is carrying is denied by the world around him.

And then Allah descends this ayah.

[PAUSE]

He does not say *I see you.* He does not say *trust Me.* He gives the Prophet ﷺ something more structural. He says: *the people who already have scripture recognize what you are carrying. The pattern of revelation is a pattern they know. And among the very people who reject you, there are some who believe — and those who reject are rejecting what they have already seen.*

This is the comfort of being recognized. Not by everyone. Not even by most. But by those who are qualified to recognize. And that, Allah is teaching, is the only confirmation that should matter.

Let me say this directly. There is a kind of validation everyone wants — universal validation. We want the people we love, the people we work with, the people in our city, the strangers on the internet, all to agree with us. And when they don't, we feel ourselves dissolving.

But Allah is teaching the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every person who carries a difficult truth — that this is not how validation actually works. The validation that matters is the validation of those who *know.* The kind of people who can recognize what is real.

Think about how this lands in your own life. When you are uncertain whether you are doing the right thing in your marriage, whose opinion do you weigh? The cousin who has been divorced four times, or the elder who has been married for fifty years? When you are uncertain about a financial decision, whose advice do you take seriously? The friend who is in debt, or the man who has built and protected wealth for decades? When you are uncertain about your iman, whose witness comforts you? The stranger online who scoffs, or the teacher whose words you can feel coming from a deep well?

The pattern of human wisdom in every domain is: **the recognition of those who know carries more weight than the dismissal of those who don't.**

[PAUSE]

And here is where the ayah cuts deeper. The Prophet ﷺ is being told: *the people who are dismissing you are not those who know. The people who know are recognizing what I have given you. Do not let the loud voices of the unqualified drown out the quiet recognition of the qualified.*

This is medicine for any one of us who has ever felt isolated in faith. If you have walked into a room and felt embarrassed about your prayer. If you have hesitated to mention Allah at a workplace dinner. If you have ever doubted yourself because the secular world rolled its eyes at what you believe. The Prophet ﷺ stood in a city of mockers and was told by his Lord: *the qualified see what you are carrying. The unqualified are rejecting what they already know. Walk on.*

**The practical movement:** When you next feel that isolation — the feeling of carrying something true in a world that does not see it — pause. Ask: *who am I letting confirm or deny what I know? Am I weighing the recognition of those qualified to recognize, or am I drowning under the dismissal of those who never had standing to judge?*

Often the loneliness is built not from the absence of recognition but from listening to the wrong audience.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Knowing and Refusing

Now I want to take you somewhere even harder.

The word *jaḥada* is in this ayah because Allah wants you to understand something deeply uncomfortable about the human heart. Disbelief, at its root, is rarely an information problem. It is almost always a will problem.

Let me give you a picture from ordinary life.

A man is told by his doctor — kindly, clearly, with charts and tests — that he has high blood pressure and that he must change his diet. He nods. He goes home. He continues to eat what he was eating. Three years later he has a heart attack. Did he not know? He knew. He was told. He was shown. He understood. But knowing and acting are not the same thing. Between knowledge and action there is a third thing — *will.* And that will can refuse what the mind has accepted.

This is the everyday version of *jaḥada*. The refusal of what you know.

Now multiply this. A woman is in a relationship that everyone — including she herself, in her quiet moments — knows is wrong for her. Does she lack information? No. She has more information about this relationship than anyone else alive. She is not confused. She is *refusing.*

A businessman is doing something ethically corrosive in his work. Does he not know it is corrosive? He knows. He has felt his own conscience name it. But he keeps going.

The Quran is identifying, in this single word *yajḥadu,* a particular human pattern — and then naming, with terrifying clarity, that this same pattern is the pattern of *kufr.* The Meccans were not failing to see Muhammad ﷺ. They were refusing what they had seen.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter for us?

Because most of us, when we think about why some people accept Islam and some don't, frame it as an *information* problem. *If only they knew. If only they read. If only they understood.* And so we pour our energy into producing more information. More books. More videos. More arguments.

But Allah is telling us, in this ayah, that the gap between belief and disbelief is not usually an information gap. It is a *will* gap. The signs are clear. The Quran is clear. The world itself, with its order and beauty, is clear. What is hard is not *seeing* it. What is hard is *yielding* to what one has seen.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ was told, in another place: *you do not guide whom you love; rather Allah guides whom He wills* (28:56). Because guidance is not the delivery of information. It is the softening of a will. And only Allah can do that.

[PAUSE]

But there is a second, deeper movement here. And this is the movement that should make us tremble.

If *jaḥada* is the disease of the disbeliever, then it is also a disease that can creep into the believer.

Every one of us, sometimes, knows what Allah wants from us in a given moment and turns away. We know we should pray now; we delay. We know this conversation is gossip; we keep listening. We know we have wronged someone; we will not apologize. We know our wealth has a right on it; we keep tightening our fist.

In each of those moments, we are doing — in miniature — what the Meccans did. We are *jāḥidūn* about a small ayah of Allah, even if we are *muʾminūn* about the larger ones. The Quran is telling us, *the deepest spiritual disease is not failing to know — it is knowing and refusing.*

And the good news in that bad news is this: if your knowing is intact, the work is mostly the work of *yielding.* The work is not gathering more facts. The work is bending the will toward what you already see.

[PAUSE]

How does this become practical?

Here is one move. When you feel resistance to something you know is right — pause. Don't reach for an argument against what you know. Don't recruit your intellect to find reasons not to do it. Recognize the resistance for what it is — the same resistance the Meccans had — and turn toward it rather than away from it. Say: *I see the resistance. I am going to do the thing anyway, because the knowing is older and truer than the resistance.*

This is how you keep yourself out of the camp of those who knew and turned away. Every small yielding — every small *I will do this even though I don't want to* — builds the habit of *iman.* Every small refusal — every small *I know, but* — builds the habit of *jaḥada.*

**The practical movement:** Identify one thing in your life right now that you *know* is true about how you should live, and that you are *refusing* to act on. Just one. Don't fix the list — fix the one. Because the structure of refusal is the same whether the refusal is large or small, and so the structure of yielding is the same too.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Shape of the Ayah

Now look back at what Allah has built.

The ayah moves in four motions. First: *and thus We have sent down to you the Book* — locating this revelation inside a lineage. Second: *those to whom We gave the Scripture believe in it* — naming recognition by the qualified. Third: *and among these are those who believe in it* — naming the precious singular believers in front of him. Fourth: *and none reject Our signs except the disbelievers* — naming refusal-with-recognition as the only kind of rejection that actually exists.

Notice the structure. The first half of the ayah is about *belief.* The second half ends with *kufr.* But the bridge between them is the question: *what does it mean to see?* The believers — both the People of the Book and the Meccan singles — see and yield. The disbelievers see and refuse. The ayah is not actually about two groups. It is about two responses to the same recognized reality.

This is why Allah did not need to argue for the Quran in this ayah. He simply described what happens in front of the Quran. Some look at it and yield. Some look at it and refuse. And the ones who refuse are not refusing in ignorance — they are refusing in *jaḥd.*

---

### Bringing the Threads Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are saying one thing from two directions.

The word *kadhālika* tells the Prophet ﷺ he is part of a lineage. The theme of recognition tells him he is being recognized by those qualified to recognize.

The word *yajḥadu* tells him his opponents are refusing what they know. The theme of knowing-and-refusing tells him that the human disease is not ignorance but will.

Together, these are the structure of consolation Allah offers the Prophet ﷺ — and offers us. **You are not alone in carrying truth. The qualified see it. And the ones who deny it are denying what they have seen.**

---

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose recognition am I waiting for in order to feel that what I believe is real? Are they qualified to recognize?
2. Where in my life am I letting the dismissal of the unqualified drown out the recognition of the qualified?
3. What do I already know about how I should live — and how am I refusing it?
4. If *jaḥada* — knowing-and-refusing — is the structure of *kufr,* how often does it appear in small forms in my own day?
5. When I feel isolated in my faith, am I willing to remember that the Prophet ﷺ felt this too — and that Allah's answer to him was not numbers, but lineage?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

The truth of what Allah has revealed does not depend on how many accept it, but on the fact that those qualified to recognize it do — and that those who reject it are rejecting what they have already seen.

---

### The Closing Invitation

If you have ever felt alone in carrying something true, this ayah is for you. Allah does not promise the Prophet ﷺ a majority. He promises him *recognition by the qualified* and *evidence of refusal in the unqualified.* That is the deepest comfort revelation offers anyone who has ever walked against the grain.

And if you have ever caught yourself knowing what Allah wants and refusing it anyway — this ayah is also for you. The word *jaḥada* is in the ayah to name a disease and to invite you out of it. Each small yielding to what you know moves you toward the camp of those *to whom We gave the Book* — those whose hearts had grown spacious enough to recognize what was always there.

Let the ayah do its quiet work. Let *kadhālika* place you inside a lineage. Let *yuʾminūna bih* remind you that recognition is what matters. Let *yajḥadu* be the word you never want said about you.

### Du'a

Allahumma, You who sent down this Book in the same way You sent down every Book before it — let us be among *those to whom You gave the Scripture* in our hearts, who recognize Your speech when we hear it. Soften our wills toward what we already know to be true. And protect us from the deepest disease of the heart: the disease of *jaḥada* — of seeing and refusing what we have seen. Āmīn.
