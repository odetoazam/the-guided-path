# When Shaytan Decorates the Road
## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 38

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:29:38] وَعَادًا وَثَمُودَا۟ وَقَد تَّبَيَّنَ لَكُم مِّن مَّسَـٰكِنِهِمْ ۖ وَزَيَّنَ لَهُمُ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ أَعْمَـٰلَهُمْ فَصَدَّهُمْ عَنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ وَكَانُوا۟ مُسْتَبْصِرِينَ

**Transliteration:**
> Wa ʿĀdan wa Thamūda wa qad tabayyana lakum min masākinihim, wa zayyana lahumu sh-shayṭānu aʿmālahum, fa-ṣaddahum ʿani s-sabīli wa kānū mustabṣirīn.

**Translation:**
> And [We destroyed] ʿĀd and Thamūd — and it has become clear to you from their dwellings. Shaytan made their deeds seem beautiful to them, and so turned them away from the path — though they had been capable of seeing clearly.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Picture for a moment the Arabian Peninsula in the seventh century. The Prophet ﷺ is speaking, and the people of Makkah listening to him are not strangers to the names *ʿĀd* and *Thamūd*. These were not foreign legends. These were local memory.

The trade caravans of Quraysh ran north every summer. The road to Syria passed directly through al-Ḥijr — the rock-carved cities of Thamūd, the people of Ṣāliḥ. You can still go there today. The tombs are still cut into the cliffs. The doorways are still there. The carved facades, the geometric details, the empty windows looking out over the sand.

The merchants of Makkah had **walked past these ruins their entire lives.**

And the people of ʿĀd — their territory in the south, in al-Aḥqāf, the sand dunes near Hadramawt. Their pillared cities, the *Iram dhāt al-ʿimād* the Quran will later describe. Again, not myth. Geography.

So when Allah says in this ayah, "*qad tabayyana lakum min masākinihim*" — "it has already become clear to you from their dwellings" — He is not asking the Makkans to imagine something. He is asking them to remember the last time they rode past those silent stones and thought, *somebody used to live here.*

This whole surah, Al-ʿAnkabūt, is built around one image: the **spider's house**. The flimsiest structure in nature. Whoever takes anything other than Allah as a refuge — their refuge is like that web. Beautiful when the morning light catches it. Useless in the slightest wind.

And here in this ayah, Allah is going to show us something **even more uncomfortable** than the destruction of a city. He is going to show us *how* it happens. Not the lightning. Not the wind. The mechanism *inside* the human chest that walks a clear-sighted people, with their eyes open, off the edge of the cliff.

### The Hook

This ayah ends with a phrase that should stop us in our tracks. Allah says they had been **mustabṣirīn** — possessing clear sight. They could see. They knew. They were not ignorant.

And yet they walked the road to destruction anyway.

How? How does that happen? How does a person who knows better, who can see clearly, who has every faculty intact — *still* end up at the bottom of the canyon?

Allah gives us the answer in one verb. One verb sitting in the middle of this ayah, which — once you see it — explains every shocked headline you have ever read, every "how could they have done that" moment in your own life, every time you have looked back at a decision and asked yourself, *what was I thinking?*

Stay with me. We are going to find that verb.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Masākinihim* — "Their Dwellings"

#### The Concept Before the Word

Before we touch this word, I want you to do something. Think of a place you knew well as a child. The home of a grandparent, maybe. A neighborhood you grew up in. Now imagine going back, years later, and the building is still there — but empty. The furniture gone. The voices gone. Just walls.

What do those walls do to you?

There is a particular kind of silence in a building that used to be lived in. It is louder than a building that was never inhabited at all. Because the *absence* is shaped like a presence that used to be there.

In Arabic, the root for a place where someone lives is **S-K-N**, the same root as *sakīnah* — stillness, tranquility, the settled heart. A *maskan* is not just a building. It is the place where motion came to rest, where a family settled, where life *quieted down* into a daily rhythm.

#### Open the Word

Allah does not say "*min āthārihim*" — from their *traces*. He does not say "*min qubūrihim*" — from their *graves*. He says **min masākinihim** — **from their dwellings.**

Their *settled places*. Their *homes*.

#### Sit With This

Notice what this word does. The Quran could have shown us their bones. Could have shown us their punishment. Could have shown us the storm or the cry. Instead Allah points us to the place where they **lived**. The kitchens. The courtyards. The bedrooms. The places where children laughed and meals were eaten.

Why?

Because the lesson is not "look how they died." The lesson is "**look how they lived.**" Look at the *normality* of it. Look at the architecture, the ambition, the carved facades, the engineering. These were not depraved people in dark caves. These were sophisticated civilizations with mortgages and dinner plans and weddings to attend.

And the word *maskan* — the *settled place* — carries inside it the irony that destroyed them. Because *sakīnah*, that settledness, was exactly the thing they should never have felt in this dunyā. The moment a people becomes too *settled* in this world, too at home, they have already forgotten the appointment that is coming.

The Prophet ﷺ would later describe a believer in this world as **like a stranger or a traveler.** Don't get too settled. The ruins of Thamūd are still there because Thamūd got too settled.

[PAUSE]

#### What This Single Word Reveals

Allah did not preserve their ruins as a tourist attraction. He preserved them as a **mirror**. The mirror does not show you a stranger's death. The mirror shows you a stranger's *life* — which looked a lot like yours.

### Element Two: *Zayyana* — "He Beautified"

#### The Concept Before the Word

In Arabic morphology, there is a verb pattern called **Form II** — *faʿʿala*. It takes a simple verb and *intensifies* it, *causes* it, *makes the action happen to something else.*

Take the root **Z-Y-N**, which simply means *to be beautiful, adorned*. In Form I you would say something **is** beautiful. In Form II — *zayyana* — you don't say something is beautiful. You say *somebody made it look beautiful.* Somebody *applied* the beauty to it. Somebody *decorated* it.

Think of a stage set. The wood is plain pine. Cheap, splintery, ugly. Then the set designer comes in with paint and lighting and props, and suddenly that pine is a Venetian palace. **Z-Y-N** in Form II is the work of the set designer. It is the *applied* beauty, the *cosmetic* layer, the *decoration* that hides what is actually underneath.

#### Open the Word

**Wa zayyana lahumu sh-shayṭānu aʿmālahum.**

"And Shaytan **decorated** for them their deeds."

Not: their deeds were beautiful.
Not: they thought their deeds were beautiful.
**Somebody applied beauty to their deeds.** A set designer came in and made the ugly look gorgeous.

#### Sit With This

Here is what I want you to feel. The shocking thing about this verb is not that Shaytan exists. The shocking thing is *what Shaytan actually does.*

He does not, in this ayah, force them to do anything. He does not whisper a command. He does not threaten them. He does not even lie to them about consequences.

He just **decorates the action.**

He takes the same act — the same exploitation, the same arrogance, the same denial — and he changes the *lighting*. He adds the soundtrack. He frames it differently. He gives it a name that sounds dignified. *Ambition. Tradition. Strength. Realism. Just being smart. Just protecting my family. Just how the world works.*

The deed didn't change. The *aesthetic of the deed* changed.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because this is where you and I live. Right here.

Have you ever noticed that you almost never do something while thinking, "I am being a terrible person right now"? Almost never. In the moment, the action has a *story* attached to it. A justification. A frame. A *decoration*. And the decoration is so convincing that you cannot even see the underlying act for what it is.

That gossip wasn't gossip — it was "*processing*."
That extravagance wasn't extravagance — it was "*self-care*."
That cruelty wasn't cruelty — it was "*setting boundaries*."
That betrayal wasn't betrayal — it was "*being honest*."

I am not saying these categories never apply. I am saying that Shaytan's primary technology is not the lie about the consequences. **His primary technology is the makeover on the act itself.**

#### The Composite Sketch from the Tradition

The classical scholars, when they encountered this verb *zayyana*, did not all read it the same way. Some emphasized that the decoration is in the **whisper** — the suggestion that makes the bad look good. Others emphasized that the decoration is in the **environment** — the cultural conditions, the peers, the prevailing winds that make the deed feel normal. Others said the decoration is the **internal lens** — the nafs that wants the deed and so the eye that looks at it goes soft.

These are not three different opinions. These are three angles on one operation. The whisper plants the seed. The environment waters it. The nafs welcomes it. By the time the action happens, the person is no longer choosing to do evil. They are choosing to do *what they sincerely believe is reasonable*.

#### The Sonic Texture

Listen to the verb: **zayyana**. Two soft syllables. Soft "z," then "yyana" — almost gentle, almost a lullaby. There is no harshness in the sound. The word itself is *cosmetic*. The word itself is *beautified*. And then it sits next to **al-Shayṭān** — a name that sonically *expands*, the "ay-tān" stretching out — and the contrast tells you something. The agent is enormous and ancient and terrible. His method is soft and quiet and **pretty**.

[PAUSE]

#### What This Single Word Reveals

Shaytan does not usually come for you with horns. He comes for you with **interior decoration**. The deed stays the same. The frame around the deed changes. And you, looking at the frame, mistake the frame for the deed.

### Element Three: *Mustabṣirīn* — "Capable of Seeing"

#### The Concept Before the Word

Arabic has, again, those morphological forms — and one of them is **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern. Form X often carries the meaning of *seeking* or *being in a state of having acquired* the basic action.

Take the root **B-Ṣ-R**, which is sight, perception, *insight*. In Form I, *baṣura* — to see. In Form IV, *abṣara* — to make sight happen, to be able to see. In Form X, **istabṣara** — to *seek out* sight, to *possess and exercise* insight, to be *equipped with* clear vision.

So **mustabṣir** is not a passive seer. It is somebody who has *gone after* clarity. Somebody who has the *capacity*. Somebody whose vision is *active and informed*.

#### Open the Word

**Wa kānū mustabṣirīn.**

"And they had been **mustabṣirīn**" — possessors of clear sight, capable of perception, equipped with the faculty to see.

#### Sit With This

Notice what Allah does NOT say. He does not say *they were ignorant*. He does not say *they didn't know any better*. He does not say *the message hadn't reached them*.

He says **the opposite.** They could see. They had the faculty intact. They were *mustabṣirīn*.

And this is the most terrifying word in the ayah. Because every comfortable explanation we want to give for the destruction of past peoples — *"oh, they didn't have a prophet yet," "oh, the message wasn't clear," "oh, they were primitives"* — Allah strips away. No. **They saw.** They had warnings. They had Hūd. They had Ṣāliḥ. They had a she-camel coming out of a rock. They saw it.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question this word forces on us:

If clear sight is not enough to save you — *what is*?

Sight is necessary, but it is not sufficient. You can see the cliff perfectly and still walk off it, because Shaytan did not blindfold you. Shaytan **decorated the cliff.** He made the edge look like a balcony. And your sight — the sight you spent a lifetime cultivating — was looking at the **decoration**, not the **drop**.

#### The Asymmetric Absence

Look at how the ayah is constructed. Allah gives us two things and **withholds** a third.

He gives us: clear ruins (the external evidence).
He gives us: clear sight (the internal faculty).
He withholds: the link between them.

What is the link between *external evidence* and *internal sight* that is *missing* here? It is the **willingness to apply the sight to oneself.** The Thamūd could see the truth perfectly clearly when it was about somebody else. They could see Hūd's people clearly — *those* fools, *those* deniers. What they could not see was their **own** face in that mirror.

The absence in the ayah is not knowledge. It is **self-application.**

[PAUSE]

#### What This Single Word Reveals

The most dangerous person in the world is not the ignorant one. It is the **clear-sighted person whose clarity has been turned on everyone except themselves.** Sight without self-application is a flashlight pointed forever outward, while you stand in the dark.

[Major PAUSE]

### Bridge

So we have three pieces.

**Masākinihim** — the lesson is in the normality of their *lives*, not the spectacle of their deaths.
**Zayyana** — the mechanism is **applied beauty**, not raw evil.
**Mustabṣirīn** — they had every faculty needed to see. And it didn't save them.

The text has shown us *how the machine of self-destruction actually runs* in a clear-sighted person. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about ʿĀd and Thamūd — but about the way our own days, right now, are quietly being decorated.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Marketing of the Self to the Self

#### Start Where You Live

You are walking through a grocery store. You are tired. You are vaguely hungry. You pass an end-cap display: chocolate, on sale, beautifully lit, three for one. Two minutes ago you had no thought of chocolate. Now you are reaching for it.

What happened?

A marketing team — somewhere, months ago — sat in a room and designed that display. Color, lighting, font, placement, scarcity ("3 for 1!"), positioning at eye-level. They did not invent your hunger. They did not lie about the price. They simply **arranged the experience of the chocolate** so that your hand reached out before your judgment could catch up.

We accept that this happens. We even joke about it. *"Don't go to the store hungry."*

Now: what if I told you that your **moral life** has end-caps?

What if I told you that there is a marketing team for every wrong decision you have ever rationalized, and that marketing team is **inside your own head**, working with material supplied by Shaytan, working in a language you yourself speak, working with your own values, your own self-image, your own preferred story about who you are?

Welcome to **zayyana lahumu sh-shayṭānu aʿmālahum.**

The marketing of the self to the self.

#### Let the Ayah Illuminate

Here is the move I want you to make. Stop imagining ʿĀd and Thamūd as ancient bearded idol-worshippers shaking fists at the sky. They were *people who got out of bed in the morning* and made breakfast and went to work and built things and loved their kids — and along the way, did things that destroyed them. And every one of those destructive things came with a **story attached.** A reason. A frame. A decoration.

Maybe the story was: *we are the strongest people on earth* (ʿĀd's actual boast in the Quran). And from inside that story, oppressing the weak isn't oppression — it is **the natural order of things.**

Maybe the story was: *we built these mountains, we are master craftsmen* (Thamūd's pride). And from inside that story, killing the prophet's miracle isn't sacrilege — it is **protecting our way of life.**

Notice what neither story sounds like: "We are evil and we love being evil." That story would have woken them up. That story would have failed.

The decoration **must look like a good story** or it doesn't function.

#### Build It Further

Now turn the camera around.

When was the last time you did something you later regretted? Not a small thing — a thing you actually winced at, later. I want you to remember not the deed, but the **story** you were telling yourself in the moment.

Was the story:
- "I deserve this"?
- "They had it coming"?
- "It's just this once"?
- "Nobody will know"?
- "If I don't, somebody worse will"?
- "I'm just being honest"?
- "I'm protecting myself"?

Each of those is a piece of furniture in the room Shaytan decorated for you that day. He didn't drag you into the room. He just **made the room look like a place you wanted to be.**

And here is the merciful and terrifying fact: the moment you can *name the decoration*, it loses power. The end-cap stops working the second you say to yourself, *I see what they're doing here.* The same is true of the moral end-caps inside your head. **Naming is half of escape.**

The first practical question this ayah asks you to carry into every choice today:

*What is the story I am telling myself right now to make this feel okay?*

And then a follow-up:

*If I subtracted the story, would I still do the action?*

If the answer is yes — proceed, the action stands on its own. If the answer is no — Shaytan just got caught at the end-cap. Walk away.

#### The Paradox to Sit With

This is the paradox we must not rush past. The verse says they were *mustabṣirīn* — clear-sighted. *And* it says Shaytan decorated their deeds.

How can both be true at the same time? How can someone have *sight* and *also* be deceived?

Because **sight is not the same thing as honesty.** A person can have 20/20 vision and refuse to look in one specific direction for thirty years. A person can be a genius at reading other people and a complete stranger to themselves. A person can be a brilliant diagnostician of *somebody else's* marriage and the worst possible diagnostician of their own.

Sight is the *instrument*. Honesty is the *willingness to point the instrument at yourself.* And **Shaytan's whole project is to convince you that the instrument is already pointed correctly** — that *of course* you have examined yourself, *of course* you have considered the alternatives, *of course* you have weighed it fairly — when in fact the instrument has been pointed at the wall for years.

The ruins of Thamūd are not a warning about being stupid. They are a warning about being **smart and unexamined.**

#### Practical Application

Tonight, before sleep — five minutes. Pick the **single most uncomfortable decision** of your day. The one with the most justification attached to it.

Then ask: **What was I selling myself?**

Just that. Don't fix anything. Don't repent yet. Just *see the marketing.* See the end-cap. See the decoration.

That five minutes is the application of *istibṣār* — active sight — to your own life. Without it, you remain a person of clear vision and unexamined deeds. Which is exactly the description of the people whose homes you can still tour in the Saudi desert.

### Theme Two: *Al-Sabīl* — The Path That Was Always Yours

#### Start Where You Live

There is a particular kind of grief that does not get talked about much. It is the grief of looking back at a road not taken — not because you didn't know it was there, but because **you were walking past it for years, and somehow you never turned.**

You knew you should call your father more. You knew you should leave that job. You knew you should fix that habit. You knew you should pray. You knew. You **saw the path.** And every day, for some reason, you kept walking parallel to it instead of onto it.

When the ayah says **fa-ṣaddahum ʿani al-sabīl** — "and so He turned them away from **the path**" — what is "the path"?

#### The Word *Al-Sabīl*

In Arabic, *sabīl* is a path, a way, a road. But notice — Allah doesn't say "*a* path." He says **al-sabīl. *The* path.** With the definite article. *The* known path. *The* established path. *The* path that everyone already understood was there.

This is enormous. It means the path was not hidden. It means the path was not invented by Allah after the fact and held against them. The path was *known to them*. They were *mustabṣirīn* about it. They had Hūd. They had Ṣāliḥ. They had everything they needed to recognize it.

And Shaytan did not destroy the path. Read the verb again: **ṣaddahum** — he *turned them aside.* The path was still there. They were the ones who got redirected.

Picture a highway with a clearly marked exit. Shaytan does not blow up the exit. Shaytan stands at the exit holding a sign that says **"Construction ahead — detour."** The exit is still there. Anyone who looks carefully can see it is still there. But the sign is so confident, so professional-looking, that drivers just *follow it* — without ever stopping to verify.

The decoration **redirected** them. It did not delete the path.

#### Let the Ayah Illuminate

This is one of the most merciful sub-points in the ayah, and you can miss it. Allah is not saying *the path was hard to find.* He is not saying *you had bad luck.* He is saying — with terrible tenderness — *the path was right there. The whole time. You walked past it.*

And underneath that, there is a quiet message for *us*, the listeners:

**The path is still right there. For you. Right now.**

ʿĀd and Thamūd are over. Their decision is sealed. But you are not yet a ruin in the sand. You are still on the highway, and the exit is still ahead of you, and Allah has just — through this ayah — shown you that **somebody is standing at the exit with a sign.**

The whole point of reciting this ayah is to make the sign-holder *visible*. Once you can see the sign-holder, the sign loses its authority. You can read the actual highway signs again. You can take the actual exit.

#### Build It Further

There is something extraordinary about how the verse describes the act of being turned aside. It doesn't say "*fa-aḍallahum*" — and led them astray. It doesn't say "*fa-akhraja-hum*" — and removed them. It says **fa-ṣaddahum ʿani al-sabīl** — and *blocked them from* the path. Diverted them. Steered them around it.

You see what this implies?

The path was their **birthright.** The default. The thing they were already on. *Sadd* means to block or obstruct something that *was already going somewhere*. You don't block what wasn't moving. You obstruct what was *en route*.

In other words: **every human being is, by default, on the path.** The fiṭra is on the path. The natural disposition is on the path. The thing that has to happen for a person to end up destroyed is not the *creation* of a new direction — it is the **obstruction** of the one they were already walking.

This is hopeful and terrifying at once. Hopeful because *the path is your default.* Terrifying because *the diversion is happening to you constantly* and the only way back is not to *find* the path, but to **stop being diverted from it.**

#### The Paradox to Sit With

So here is the strange architecture of the ayah:

— You were given clear sight.
— You were placed on the path by default.
— The ruins of those who failed are *visible*.
— And still, the diversion works.

How? Because the diversion does not have to be powerful. It only has to be *constant*. Every day, a small turn. Every choice, a small decoration. Every story, a small redirection. **Nobody walks off a cliff in one step.** They walk off a cliff in ten thousand quarter-degree corrections, each one of which felt utterly reasonable in the moment.

The destruction of ʿĀd took generations. The destruction of you and me takes years. And the mechanism is the same: not a single dramatic apostasy, but a **slow elegant decoration** of one deed after another, until the path is somewhere off to the left and we can no longer see it.

#### Practical Application

One question to carry into tomorrow morning:

**What is the exit I have been walking past for years?**

You know the answer. You have always known it. Some part of you knows exactly which path you have been blocked from. Maybe it's a prayer you haven't been making. Maybe it's a person you haven't been forgiving. Maybe it's a relationship you haven't been ending. Maybe it's a relationship you haven't been starting. Maybe it's a truth you haven't been telling.

**Name it.** Just name it.

Then, tomorrow, take **one step** that the decorated story has been telling you not to take. Just one. The path is still there. *Fa-ṣaddahum* describes what happened to them. It does not have to describe what happens to you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now stand back and look at the whole thing.

> *And ʿĀd, and Thamūd — and it has become clear to you from their dwellings — and Shaytan decorated their deeds for them, and so turned them away from the path — and they had been clear-sighted.*

Look at the structure. It is a sandwich. On the **outside** of the ayah, two layers of *clarity*:

— *Qad tabayyana lakum* — it has become clear to you (from outside, from the ruins).
— *Kānū mustabṣirīn* — they had been clear-sighted (from inside, from their faculties).

And on the **inside** of the ayah, the engine of destruction:

— *Zayyana lahumu sh-shayṭānu aʿmālahum, fa-ṣaddahum ʿani s-sabīl* — Shaytan decorated their deeds, and so blocked them from the path.

The architecture is showing you something. **Clarity from outside + clarity from inside is not enough.** The verse is sandwiching the deception between two layers of sight, as if to say: *you can have evidence on the outside and faculties on the inside, and still — if the decoration in the middle goes unnamed — the diversion completes itself.*

The visible ruins on the outside.
The visible faculty on the inside.
The invisible decoration in between.

The whole spiritual life is the work of making **the invisible middle visible.**

### Bringing It Together

So let's gather what we have found.

The word **masākinihim** told us: the lesson is in their *lives*, not their deaths. Look at how normal they were.

The word **zayyana** told us: Shaytan's primary tool is not force, not lies — it is **applied aesthetic.** Decoration. Re-framing.

The word **mustabṣirīn** told us: sight is necessary but not sufficient. Clear-sighted unexamined people can walk off any cliff if the cliff has good lighting.

The two themes were the same insight from two angles. **The marketing of the self to the self** is what *zayyana* feels like from the inside. **The path you were already on** is what *al-sabīl* looks like from above. The first describes the deception. The second describes what the deception is keeping you from.

And the whole ayah, sandwiched as it is, is showing you that the entire war is fought in the **interpretation of your own deeds.** Not in whether you have information. Not in whether you have sight. In whether you let *yourself* see what you are actually doing — or whether you let the set designer keep working.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one deed in my life right now that I have *decorated* — given a flattering name, dressed in a noble frame — that I would not be able to defend if I stripped the decoration off?

2. Where is my own *istibṣār*, my clear sight, working brilliantly — on others — while my own actions go unexamined?

3. Which exit on the highway have I been walking past for years, with the sign-holder smiling at me, holding up *"detour"* — when I know perfectly well the road is still open?

4. If a stranger walked into my life and watched me for a week without hearing any of my justifications, what would they say I am actually building?

5. The ruins of Thamūd are still there. **In a hundred years, what will the ruins of my life look like to someone who walks past them?**

### The Distillation

The most dangerous thing about evil is not that it is ugly — but that the most dangerous evils arrive **beautifully dressed**, and the clear-sighted person who cannot see *that* will walk past every exit on the way to the cliff.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't need a new faculty. You already have the sight. You don't need a new path. You are still standing on the default road.

You just need to **catch the decorator at work.**

Tonight, before you sleep — name one decoration. Just one. The frame around one act. *I was doing it because… I told myself it was… I called it…*

Strip the frame. Look at the deed.

That five minutes is the difference between a person who walked past the ruins of Thamūd, and a person who **read** them.

### Du'a

> *O Allah — You who preserved the ruins of ʿĀd and Thamūd as a sign for those who would come after, preserve in us the clear sight that they had, and give us what they did not have: the willingness to turn it upon ourselves.*
>
> *Strip from our deeds the decoration that hides them from us. Show us our actions as they truly are, before the day comes when every veil falls and we see them anyway.*
>
> *And place us, again and again, back on **al-sabīl** — the path that was always Yours, the path that was always ours, the path that no decorator can erase as long as we are still willing to look.*

Āmīn.
