# The Appointed Time That Is Already Coming

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 5

> مَن كَانَ يَرْجُو لِقَاءَ ٱللَّهِ فَإِنَّ أَجَلَ ٱللَّهِ لَآتٍۢ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ

> *Man kāna yarjū liqā'a Llāhi fa-inna ajala Llāhi la-ātin. Wa huwa as-Samīʿu l-ʿAlīm.*

> **Whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah — indeed, Allah's appointed term is surely coming. And He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

To feel this ayah, you have to first feel the four ayahs that just preceded it.

Surah Al-Ankabut opens in Makkah, in a moment of terrible compression. The believers are few. The pressure is enormous. The Quraysh have moved past mockery into something darker — economic boycott, social exclusion, physical torture for the weakest among the believers. Bilāl is on hot sand with a stone on his chest. Sumayyah will soon become the first martyr. Some of the Companions have already fled to Abyssinia. The Prophet ﷺ himself is grieving — Khadījah is gone, his uncle is gone, the year is called *ʿām al-ḥuzn*, the Year of Sorrow.

And in this moment Allah opens the surah with a question that must have felt like being shaken awake:

> *A-ḥasiba n-nāsu an yutrakū an yaqūlū āmannā wa hum lā yuftanūn?*
> "Do people imagine they will be left alone for saying 'we believe' — without being tested?"

The next ayah states it plainly: We tested those before you. So Allah will surely know — *fa-la-yaʿlamanna Llāhu* — who is truthful and who is lying. Then a warning to those running from trial: do they think they can outrun Us? Bad judgement.

And then — ayah 5. Just when the listener has been pressed all the way into the corner of the question — *how long? when does it stop? what holds me here?* — the surah opens a window.

### The Strand

There is a strand running through this opening passage. It's not about trial in the abstract. It's about **what holds a person inside a trial without breaking**. Ayahs 1-4 establish that trial is the curriculum of faith. Ayah 5 names the fuel. Ayah 6 will name the muscle (*man jāhada* — whoever strives). Ayah 7 will name the result.

Ayah 5 is the fuel.

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah that, on the surface, looks like a small encouragement. *La-ātin* — "is surely coming." It sounds like a normal future-tense reassurance: don't worry, it's on the way.

But the Arabic isn't doing what English thinks it's doing.

The word Allah chose is **not a verb**. It is something stranger. Something more present. Something that, once you see it, will make you wonder how a meeting that hasn't happened yet can already be *arriving*.

And there is something else. Allah names Himself at the end with two names. Not three. Not four. **Two.** And one of them — once you see why it is there — will quietly take your breath.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Is Not a Verb

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, there is a difference between a verb and what is called an *ism fāʿil* — an active participle. A verb tells you about an *action that happens*. An active participle tells you about *the one doing the action* — and it carries the action like a quality, like a state, like something stuck to the doer.

Think of it in English. "He runs" is a verb. "He is a runner" is a participle. The verb tells you about an event. The participle tells you about *what he is*. The first can stop. The second has settled into him.

Now move that into Arabic. *Yaʾtī* means "he comes" — a verb, an action that will happen. *Ātin* means "one who is coming" — a participle, a description, a state currently underway.

Hold that distinction. Because Allah did not use the verb.

#### Opening the word

Look at the phrase:

> *fa-inna ajala Llāhi **la-ātin***

The appointed term of Allah — *la-ātin*.

A translator must render this in English as "is surely coming" because English has no other gear. But what Allah actually said is closer to:

**"Allah's appointed term is one-already-on-its-way."**

Let's sit with this.

If Allah had wanted to say "it will come," the Arabic would have been *sa-yaʾtī* or *la-yaʾtiyanna*. Either was available. Either is a perfectly normal way to make a future promise. The Quran uses both, often, when promising future events.

But here — at the precise moment when a believer is being crushed by *now*, when the trial feels like it will never end — Allah does not say "it will come." He says **it is in the state of coming.** Right now. As you read this. As Bilāl lies on sand. As the boycott tightens. As the loved one you buried is still warm in your memory.

The meeting is not a future event. The meeting is **already in motion**.

#### Notice something

A verb makes a promise. A participle reports a reality.

When someone tells you "your delivery will arrive tomorrow," you might believe them, you might not. It is a claim about a future they have not yet made happen.

When someone tells you "your delivery is on the truck right now, the driver is three streets away" — something different has occurred. The future has become physical. The future is no longer being *promised*; it is being *located*.

This is what *la-ātin* does. It refuses to leave the meeting in the abstract bin of "someday." It places it on the road. Moving toward you. With the *lām* of emphasis welded onto the front — *la-* — which in Arabic functions like an oath, a guarantee: *truly, unmistakably, no doubt about it.*

Here's what's remarkable: the trial is happening to you in the present tense. The pain is present tense. The injustice is present tense. The grief is present tense. Every ordinary fact in your life is in the present tense — and the meeting is described as a future event, which makes it feel less real than the pain.

So Allah refuses the future tense. He puts the meeting in the same tense as the pain.

**The pain is here. The meeting is also here — just hasn't unwrapped itself yet.**

[PAUSE]

#### Going deeper

There is a second layer to this word that the classical lexicographers note. The participle *ātin* — "one who is coming" — describes the *ajal*, the appointed term, as if it were itself a traveler. A messenger on the road. Something with intention. Something *aimed*.

The ajal of Allah is not drifting. It is not random. It is not a generic endpoint waiting for you to arrive at it. **It is moving toward you on purpose.**

This changes the geometry of your life.

A person walking through trial usually imagines themselves moving forward through time toward a distant finish line. They are the active party. The finish line is passive. They must reach it.

Allah inverts this. The ajal is the active party. You are not walking toward the meeting. **The meeting is walking toward you.**

You do not have to reach it. It is reaching you.

One specific statement of what this single word reveals:

> *La-ātin* tells the trapped believer that the meeting with their Lord is not a destination they must struggle to arrive at — it is a guest who has already left His house and is presently on the road to theirs.

### Element Two: The Asymmetry of *Yarjū*

#### Teaching the concept first

In English we use the word "hope" for two completely different psychological states. "I hope it doesn't rain tomorrow" — that is wishful uncertainty. And "I hope to see my child graduate" — that is anticipatory longing toward something I expect.

Arabic separates these. *Tamannā* is wishful hope — what you wish for without basis to expect. *Rajāʾ* is anchored hope — longing toward something you have grounds to anticipate. *Rajāʾ* is the word a sailor uses when he sees the harbor lights. It is hope with weight. Hope with direction.

Allah chose *yarjū*.

#### Opening the word

> *Man kāna **yarjū** liqā'a Llāh*
> Whoever was — and continues to be — *hoping for* the meeting with Allah.

Notice the construction. Not "whoever hopes" alone. **"Whoever was hoping"** — *kāna yarjū*. This compound tense in Arabic does something specific: it describes a state that has been ongoing. A habit. A standing orientation of the heart.

So Allah is not addressing the person who, in this one terrible moment, manages to summon a flicker of hope. He is addressing the person whose heart has been **leaning toward Him** for a while.

#### Stay with this

Now read the absence.

Allah says: *whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah*. The condition is about your hope. But the second half of the ayah — the answer to that condition — does not say "their meeting is coming." It says **"Allah's appointed term is surely coming."**

Notice the asymmetry. The believer hopes for *liqāʾ* (a meeting). Allah responds with *ajal* (an appointed term).

These are not the same word.

*Liqāʾ* is the soft word. It is the word of two faces turning toward each other. The lover's word. The reunion word. From the root *l-q-y*, which carries the sense of two things meeting from opposite directions and resolving into one place. When the Quran wants to speak of the moment of seeing Allah, it uses *liqāʾ*.

*Ajal* is the hard word. It is the legal word. It is the word used for the term of a debt, the lifespan of a body, the deadline of a contract. From the root *ʾ-j-l*, which carries the sense of a fixed, non-negotiable point. *Ajal* is what makes life end on schedule. *Ajal* is what makes death arrive on time.

#### Notice what just happened

The believer brings their soft word — *liqāʾ*. The longing word. The word of the heart that wants to see its Beloved.

Allah does not answer with the soft word. He answers with the hard word.

Why?

Because the believer in pain does not need a poet's promise. The believer in pain needs a **lawyer's guarantee**.

Hope alone is fragile. Hope can wobble. Hope can ask, "but what if it never comes?" So Allah does not match the believer's hope with more hope. He matches it with **decree**. He takes the longing of the heart and welds it to the iron of the appointed term.

It is as if a child says, "I hope you come home soon, Father." And the father answers, "The contract is signed. The flight is booked. The arrival time is fixed. I am not negotiating with the meeting. I am bound by it."

Hope, in this ayah, is not asked to support itself. Hope is being bolted onto something that does not move.

[PAUSE]

#### Going deeper still

There is one more layer. The believer hopes for *liqāʾ Allāh* — the meeting **of Allah**. Allah responds with *ajal Allāh* — the appointed term **of Allah**.

The repetition of the name *Allah* in both halves is not redundant. It is doing structural work. The meeting belongs to Allah. The term belongs to Allah. Both ends of the rope are in His hand.

Your hope is not floating in space, looking for something to attach to. Your hope is one end of a rope, and the other end is **held by the One you hope to meet**. You are not pulling toward Him. He is pulling you toward Him.

A clear statement of what this element reveals:

> The hope of the believer is matched by Allah not with sympathy but with certainty — the soft longing for *liqāʾ* is answered with the iron deadline of *ajal*, so that hope can rest on something that cannot move.

### Element Three: The Two Names That Close the Ayah

#### Teaching the concept first

In the Quran, when Allah closes an ayah with His names, the names are not decorative. They are the *seal* of the meaning. They tell you which of Allah's attributes is specifically being engaged by what He just said. They are also instructions to the heart: *this is the lens you should be looking through right now.*

When you see two names paired, ask: why these two? Why these two together? Why not three? Why not different ones?

#### Opening the words

> *wa huwa **as-Samīʿu l-ʿAlīm***
> And He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

Two names. *As-Samīʿ* — the One who hears all things. *Al-ʿAlīm* — the One who knows all things.

A casual reader passes over this as a pious closing. But place it back in the ayah's emotional context. A believer is being tested. They are crushed. They have been told their meeting with Allah is on the way. And then Allah closes with: *and He is the Hearing, the Knowing.*

Why these names?

#### Sit with the structure

When a human being suffers in silence, two terrors visit them. The first is: **"Does anyone hear me?"** The second is: **"Does anyone understand?"**

These are not the same fear.

You can be heard without being understood. The neighbor hears your crying through the wall — but does not know why you cry. They have the sound of your pain but not its meaning. This is a particular kind of loneliness.

You can also be understood without being heard. Someone in another city knows your situation in theory — but they are not present to your voice, not present to the sound of you breaking down in real time.

The human soul needs both. The actual sound of its pain, registered. *And* the meaning of its pain, known.

So Allah closes with both.

**As-Samīʿ** — your sound, your whisper, your unspoken sigh inside the trial, every "*yā Rabb*" said with no audience, every prayer you couldn't finish because you were crying too hard, every dua you didn't say out loud because you didn't want anyone to know how much you were struggling — *He hears it.*

**Al-ʿAlīm** — and not just the sound. The *meaning* of it. The why. The story behind the tear. The history compressed into that one moment of breaking. The thoughts you did not even let yourself articulate. He knows *all* of it. From the inside.

[PAUSE]

#### Here's what's remarkable

These two names are not closing the *whole* ayah. They are closing the specific architecture Allah just built.

He told the believer: *the meeting is on its way; the term is fixed.* That handles the **future**. The believer now knows where they are going.

But the future-handling does not handle the present. The believer is still inside the trial. Still suffering. Still alone in the moment.

So Allah seals the ayah with the names that handle the **present**. *I have told you about the meeting that is coming. And while you wait — know that you are heard. Know that you are known.*

The future is sealed by *ajal*. The present is sealed by *Samīʿ ʿAlīm*.

You are not left to wait in silence. You are waiting *inside* the awareness of the One who is on His way.

A clear statement:

> *As-Samīʿu l-ʿAlīm* tells the believer that while the appointed meeting travels toward them, the One who has set out is already in the room — hearing every sound of the waiting and knowing every meaning of it.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. *La-ātin* — the meeting that is already on its way, in motion, not waiting for the future to start. *Yarjū / ajal* — the soft hope of the heart being welded to the iron deadline of decree. *As-Samīʿu l-ʿAlīm* — the One who is coming is, simultaneously, the One who is hearing you wait.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Architecture of Waiting

Begin here, in the world you already live in.

You have probably waited for something difficult. A diagnosis. A flight after a family emergency. A reply that would determine whether you got the job or didn't. The phone call from the hospital. The verdict.

Notice what waiting does to a person.

Waiting is not a passive activity. Waiting *consumes*. It eats your attention. It loops back on itself. The clock seems to run slower. The mind invents possibilities — most of them dark. You replay the past, scanning for the moment when things went wrong. You rehearse the future, scripting catastrophes you have not yet experienced.

This is what makes trial so heavy. Trial is not just *pain*. Trial is **pain plus waiting**. Pain alone, you could bear — at least in theory. But pain that has no known end, pain whose duration is unknown — this is what breaks people.

This is why modern psychology distinguishes between **acute** and **chronic** suffering. Acute suffering is intense but bounded — surgery, childbirth, an injury. The mind can endure enormous pain if it knows the pain has an edge. Chronic suffering is suffering whose end is invisible. And it is chronic suffering, not acute, that produces despair, depression, the slow collapse of the will.

The technical term in suffering research is **temporal ambiguity** — not knowing *when* it ends. Temporal ambiguity is what turns pain into agony.

Now read ayah 5 again.

> Whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah — indeed, Allah's appointed term is surely coming.

Allah is doing precision surgery on temporal ambiguity.

He cannot — in the wisdom of this life — remove the trial. The trial is the curriculum. Ayahs 1-3 just established that. To remove the trial would be to remove the very thing that makes faith real.

But He can do something else. He can name the **end**.

He cannot tell you *when*. The exact moment of the meeting is among the things only He knows — and there is mercy in not knowing it. But He can tell you **that** it is coming. He can convert your chronic suffering, which has no visible edge, into something that does have an edge — even if you cannot see exactly where the edge is.

This is the architecture: **the trial keeps its intensity, but loses its infinity.**

[PAUSE]

#### Going deeper

Here is the strange thing about hope in this configuration. Hope, as the world understands it, is a kind of guessing. It is a low-confidence prediction about the future — *I hope this works out*. It is fragile, because it can be falsified by events.

But the hope Allah is asking the believer to carry — *rajāʾ liqāʾ Allāh* — is not a prediction. It is **a relationship with a fact.**

The meeting is already a fact. It is already on its way. *La-ātin*. The ajal is set. The hope is not predicting whether the meeting will happen — the hope is the believer's *orientation toward a meeting that is not in doubt*.

This re-architects waiting itself.

The mother who paces the airport waiting for her son to arrive on a flight whose status board says "DELAYED — arrival uncertain" — that is one kind of waiting. The mother who paces the airport waiting for her son to arrive on a flight whose status board says "ON FINAL APPROACH" — that is a completely different kind of waiting.

The pacing looks the same. The internal state is not the same.

A believer in trial paces. A non-believer in trial paces. The bodies look identical. The hearts are in different worlds.

#### What changes about tomorrow

If this ayah is true, then every act of waiting in your life — every commute spent wondering whether you should have taken a different career, every night spent wondering whether your child will turn out okay, every hospital visit, every prayer that feels unanswered, every long stretch of life that feels like it is going nowhere — every one of those is **a waiting inside a known meeting**.

Concrete application: tonight, before you sleep, name one thing you are currently waiting on. One source of temporal ambiguity in your life right now — something whose end you cannot see. And then, before sleep, say to yourself: *whatever the end of this thing is, the meeting that matters is already on the way. The ajal is already moving toward me. The pacing inside this trial is happening inside an arrival.*

That single repositioning, done nightly for a week, will change how you wake up.

### Theme Two: Being Heard While You Wait

Now I want to go to a different door.

There is a particular pain in being unseen during your hardest hours. Anyone who has ever cried at three in the morning knows it. You are awake, alone, the world is asleep, and the world's sleep feels like proof that your suffering does not register on the cosmic radar. The universe is going about its business. And you are here, breaking, with no audience.

This is one of the deepest griefs of human life — not the suffering itself, but the **invisibility of the suffering**. The feeling that you could disappear and the air would not even rearrange itself.

Modern research on what makes pain bearable has a consistent finding: **co-presence**. Not removal of the pain. Not solution of the pain. Just the presence of another consciousness that knows the pain is happening. Patients in hospitals who have someone holding their hand during procedures report lower pain scores even when nothing else changes. The hand does not solve the surgery. The hand makes the surgery non-solitary.

What humans need, in their deepest moments, is **a witness**.

Now read the seal of the ayah again.

> *wa huwa as-Samīʿu l-ʿAlīm*
> And He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

Allah is not promising — at the end of this ayah — to remove your trial. He is not promising to shorten it. He is not promising to send you a sign. He is doing something more fundamental than all of that.

He is naming Himself as **the witness in the room**.

#### Stay with this

The two names work together with terrifying precision.

*As-Samīʿ* is the witness of your **expression**. The sound you make. The whisper. The dua you whispered into your pillow at 3:17 a.m. The "*Allāhumma*" you said when nobody was listening. *He heard it.* Not metaphorically. Literally. The sound was registered. The audio file exists. The witness was in the room.

*Al-ʿAlīm* is the witness of your **interior**. The thoughts behind the whisper. The doubts you did not say out loud because you did not want to seem ungrateful. The questions you have not been able to articulate even to yourself. The hidden grief that does not have a name. *He knows it.* From the inside. Better than you do.

You have, in the middle of your hardest moments, the most complete witness conceivable.

#### The paradox that does not need to be resolved

Here is a paradox the ayah leaves sitting on the table:

If Allah hears every sound of my pain, and knows every meaning of it — **why does He not lift it?**

The ayah does not answer this question. It does not even try to.

It just sits the believer next to the paradox.

But notice what the structure of the ayah implies. The trial is held inside two facts: *the meeting is coming* (future certainty) and *He hears, He knows* (present co-presence). The trial is not being made meaningless. The trial is being made **witnessed** and **temporary**.

The classical scholars say that what hurts a believer is not the trial — it is the *fear that the trial is for nothing*. That the suffering is unobserved, unaccounted, untransmitted. That you are pouring your life into a void.

Ayah 5 closes this fear with two names. **None of it is being poured into a void.** The void is not a void. The void is a Presence — *Samīʿ, ʿAlīm*.

#### Connect to the universal

Every human being, religious or not, eventually asks: *is anyone seeing this?* It is the cry of the child who falls and looks immediately for the parent's face. It is the cry of the grieving widow who keeps her husband's clothes because somewhere she needs his witness to her continuing existence. It is the cry of the artist, the activist, the unknown worker, the parent who has poured a life into children who do not yet understand it.

*Is anyone seeing this?*

Ayah 5 is the Quran's answer.

**Yes. Every sound of it. Every meaning of it. And the One who is seeing it is already on His way.**

#### What changes about tomorrow

Concrete application. The next time you are alone with a difficulty — even a small one, a worry on the commute, a frustration with a child, a private grief that no one knows about — pause for ten seconds and say, silently: *Samīʿ. ʿAlīm.* Just the two names. Not a request. Not a complaint. Just a naming of the witness in the room.

This is not a magic formula. It is a re-orientation of attention. It is the moment in which the loneliness of the difficulty is broken — not by anyone arriving, but by the recognition that someone was never absent in the first place.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Beneath

Look at the ayah now from above. It has three movements:

1. **The condition** — *whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah*
2. **The promise** — *indeed, Allah's appointed term is surely coming*
3. **The seal** — *and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing*

But notice how these three movements sit in time.

The condition is about the **heart in the present** — its standing orientation, *kāna yarjū*.

The promise is about the **future** — but a future that has been pulled into the present by the participle *ātin*. The future is on its way.

The seal is about the **present** itself — Allah's hearing, Allah's knowing, now.

So the ayah holds all three tenses of the believer's life inside twelve words. The heart's posture. The arriving future. The witnessing present. None left dangling.

This is why this ayah lands the way it does in moments of trial. It does not give you one thing — it gives you the whole geometry of how to be a believer in time.

### Bringing the Discoveries Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two directions.

Linguistically: Allah refuses the future tense for the meeting (*ātin*, not *sa-yaʾtī*), refuses the soft word for the hope (matches *liqāʾ* with *ajal*), and refuses to leave the present unwitnessed (*Samīʿ ʿAlīm*).

Thematically: the believer's hope is given a deadline, the believer's waiting is given an end, and the believer's loneliness is given a witness.

The same insight, twice.

**What Allah is doing in this ayah is removing every place where a believer could fall through the cracks of their own trial.**

The crack of doubt about the future — sealed by *la-ātin*. The crack of fragility in hope — sealed by *ajal*. The crack of feeling unseen in the meantime — sealed by *Samīʿ ʿAlīm*.

You cannot fall through this ayah. There is no gap.

### Questions to Carry

1. What am I currently waiting on whose end I cannot see — and what would change if I named, tonight, that the ajal of Allah is already in motion regardless of when that waiting ends?

2. Where in my life am I treating hope as a prediction (*I hope it works out*) rather than as a relationship with a fact already decreed (*the meeting is coming*)?

3. What are the sounds I have been making in private that I have never named to anyone — and what does it mean that *Samīʿ* has been in that room with me the whole time?

4. If the meeting with my Lord is already on its way, what would it look like to prepare for a guest rather than to brace for a deadline?

5. The believer's hope was answered not with sympathy but with certainty. Is there a place in my dua life where I have been asking Allah for sympathy when He is offering me certainty?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The meeting you long for is not waiting for you in the future — it is already on its way to you, and the One who is coming has been hearing every sound of your waiting and knowing every meaning of it.**

### The Closing Invitation

What might shift after this is not a doctrine. It is a posture.

The posture of someone who has stopped reaching for an uncertain future and started receiving an arriving guest. The posture of someone who has stopped fearing they are unseen and started recognizing the witness who has always been in the room. The posture of someone whose hope is no longer a fragile guess but a rope held at both ends by the One they hope to meet.

This is not a posture to be achieved once. It is a posture to be returned to — every morning, every difficulty, every long night.

The ayah is twelve words. The architecture is enough for a lifetime.

### A Brief Du'a

*Yā Allāh, You are As-Samīʿ — You have heard every sound I have made in my hardest hours, the ones I forgot and the ones I never spoke aloud. You are Al-ʿAlīm — You know what those sounds meant, even when I did not. Make me of those whose hearts continue to hope for Your meeting — and let me wait for that meeting not as one who fears the future, but as one who knows the appointed term is already on its way.*

*Āmīn.*
