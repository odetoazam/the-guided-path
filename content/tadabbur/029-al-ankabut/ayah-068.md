# The Two Faces of Injustice — Surah Al-Ankabut 29:68

## INTRODUCTION

**The Ayah**

> [ayah:29:68] وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا أَوْ كَذَّبَ بِٱلْحَقِّ لَمَّا جَآءَهُۥٓ ۚ أَلَيْسَ فِى جَهَنَّمَ مَثْوًى لِّلْكَـٰفِرِينَ

*Wa man aẓlamu mimman iftarā ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban aw kadhdhaba bil-ḥaqqi lammā jā'ahu. Alaysa fī jahannama mathwan lil-kāfirīn.*

> "And who is more unjust than one who fabricates a lie against Allah, or who rejects the truth as a lie when it has come to him? Is there not in Hell a dwelling-place for those who deny?"

### The World Before the Words

Before we step into this ayah, we have to feel where it sits.

Surah Al-Ankabut — *The Spider* — opens with a question that I want you to hear as if it is being asked of you now: *"Do people think they will be left to say 'we believe' without being tested?"* The surah was revealed during one of the most punishing seasons of the early Meccan community. People were being tortured for their faith. Some were leaving Mecca for Abyssinia. Some were holding on. Some were breaking.

And in the middle of this surah — at ayah 41 — Allah gives one of the most quietly devastating images in the Quran. He says that those who take protectors other than Him are like a spider that built a house, and the spider's house is the most fragile of houses — *if only they knew.*

The whole surah is about **houses.** About what you build your life on. About what you trust as shelter. About what feels solid until the test arrives and the wind blows the silk away.

And now, near the end of the surah — second to last ayah — Allah turns from the spider to the human being and asks: *who is the most unjust person of all?*

### The Hook

There are two ways the human heart goes wrong with God. Two opposite, mirror-image ways. And in this single ayah, Allah names both of them — and tells us they are the same crime.

One is **manufacturing.** The other is **rejecting.**

One puts something into Allah's mouth that He did not say. The other rips something out of Allah's mouth that He did say.

And then the surah's last great image returns — but inverted. The spider chose a web. The one who does this chooses a different kind of dwelling. Stay with that.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Elative That Closes the Door — *Aẓlamu*

Look at how Allah opens:

> وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ...
> *Wa man aẓlamu mimman...*
> "And who is more unjust than the one who..."

In English we read this as a question. *Who is more unjust?* And our minds want to start listing candidates.

But this is a specific Arabic rhetorical structure, and I want to teach it before we use it.

#### The grammar first, in everyday speech

In Arabic, when you want to say something is the most extreme example of a quality, you can use the *elative* form — the *af'al* pattern. *Aṭwal* means "taller." *Akbar* means "greater." *Aḥsan* means "more beautiful." It's a comparative-superlative shape.

And there is a particular construction the Quran loves:

*Man aẓlamu mimman...* — literally, "Who is more unjust than the one who...?"

Now in English we read this as an open question. In Arabic, this is what's called an *istifhām inkārī* — a rhetorical-denial question. It is not really asking. It is asserting through the *shape* of a question.

When a teacher says to a child, *"Is there anyone more careless than you?"* — they are not asking. They are stating: *there is no one more careless than you.* The question form is louder than a statement would be.

That is exactly what Allah is doing here. *Man aẓlamu mimman iftarā ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban* — is not asking us to find candidates. It is sealing the verdict.

**There is no one more unjust than this.** Full stop.

#### What does *aẓlamu* mean?

Let's sit with this word. The root is ẓ-l-m. In its most basic, oldest sense in Arabic, *ẓulm* means **to put something where it does not belong** — to displace. The same root gives us *ẓulmah* — darkness — because darkness is light displaced, light put where it does not belong, or absent from where it should be.

So when Allah calls something *aẓlam* — the most ẓālim, the most displacing — He is not just calling it "wrong." He is saying: this is the deepest possible **misplacement** in the moral order. This is putting a thing in the most extremely wrong place that a thing can be put.

Notice something. The Quran uses this exact phrase — *man aẓlamu mimman* — about fifteen times across the entire Book. Every single time, it identifies a *specific* crime. Hiding a testimony from Allah. Concealing knowledge. Preventing people from the mosques of Allah. Forging revelation. Each time the structure is the same: *who is more unjust than the one who...*

Scholars like al-Rāzī and Ibn ʿĀshūr noticed something about this: each one of these "supreme" injustices, when you look closely, is some form of **displacing the relationship between the human being and his Creator** — falsifying it, hiding it, blocking it, distorting it. The supreme ẓulm is always *vertical* — between the slave and his Lord — not horizontal between two human beings.

Why? Because to displace something between you and a creature is to misplace it within creation. But to displace something between you and Allah is to misplace it at the source. The error is at the root.

So Allah opens this ayah by saying: I am about to name something at the root.

### Element Two: The Two Opposite Hands of the Same Crime — *Iftarā* and *Kadhdhaba*

Now the verdict is named. And here is what's astonishing: Allah doesn't name *one* crime. He names *two*.

> ...مِمَّنِ افْتَرَىٰ عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا أَوْ كَذَّبَ بِالْحَقِّ لَمَّا جَاءَهُ
> ...the one who **fabricated a lie against Allah** *or* **rejected the truth as a lie when it had come to him.**

Look at the two verbs:

- **افْتَرَىٰ** — *iftarā* — fabricated, invented
- **كَذَّبَ** — *kadhdhaba* — declared false, rejected

Both are connected to the root ك-ذ-ب — the root of *lying*. But they move in opposite directions. And the difference between them is the heart of this ayah.

#### Teaching the forms first

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns — *forms* — and each form does something specific to the meaning. Form I is the basic action. Form II makes it intensive or causative. Form VIII often makes it reflexive — something the actor does *to themselves* or *for themselves*, something they actively take on.

*Iftarā* is Form VIII from the root ف-ر-ي. Now here is something stunning about this root. In old Arabic, before it was used about lies, *farā* meant: **to cut, to slice, to tear**, especially the working of leather. A *farī* was a leatherworker — someone who would take a hide and cut it, shape it, *manufacture* something out of it.

When you apply that to speech — *iftarā* doesn't mean "told a lie." It means **fabricated** in the literal sense. *Manufactured.* Took raw material and *fashioned* a lie out of it, the way a leatherworker takes a hide and fashions a sandal.

Imam al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* makes this point clearly: *iftirā'* is the **creation of falsehood**, not the mere reporting of it. It is craftsmanship. Cold, deliberate, productive work.

And then look at what is fashioned: *kadhiban* — "a lie" — and it's *indefinite*. *A* lie. Not *the* lie. Any lie. Any manufactured thing.

And the preposition: عَلَى اللَّهِ — *ʿalā Allāh* — **against Allah**, **upon Allah**. The lie is *laid on top of* Allah. Placed at His door. Attributed to Him.

So this first crime is: **the human being takes raw material, fashions a lie out of it, and pins it on Allah.**

[PAUSE]

#### Now the second verb

*Kadhdhaba* is Form II from the same root ك-ذ-ب. Form II in Arabic intensifies, and very often makes the verb *declarative* — to actively, repeatedly *call* something the thing the root names.

So while *kadhaba* (Form I) means simply "he lied," *kadhdhaba* (Form II) means **"he called something a lie. He declared it false. He rejected it as a lie."**

And what does he reject? *Bil-ḥaqq* — **the truth**, with the definite article. *The* truth. The specific, arrived, present truth.

Notice the opposition now:

- *Iftarā* takes a **lie** and attributes it **to Allah**.
- *Kadhdhaba* takes **Allah's truth** and attributes to it **the label of lie**.

One puts a false thing where Allah is. The other puts the label *false* on the thing that came from Allah.

Both are displacements. Both are *ẓulm* — both put something where it does not belong. One imports falsehood into the divine. The other exports the label of falsehood onto the divine.

This is the **two opposite hands** of the same disease.

#### And then: *lammā jā'ahu*

Here is the small phrase that I do not want us to miss:

> ...كَذَّبَ بِالْحَقِّ **لَمَّا جَاءَهُ**
> ...rejected the truth **when it had come to him.**

*Lammā* in this construction means "when, at the moment that." And *jā'ahu* — *it came to him.* Past tense. Completed action. The truth has *arrived.*

Allah is not condemning ignorance. He is not condemning someone who never had access. He is condemning the rejection of truth **after it has shown up at the door.**

The classical commentators — Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Ṭabarī — all underline this. The *takdhīb* (denial) being named here is not the takdhīb of someone who never heard. It is the takdhīb of someone who heard, who was reached, who *had it brought to him*, and then said: *no, this is false.*

Why does this matter? Because it tells us what the supreme injustice actually is. The supreme injustice is not failing to find the truth. It is **closing the door after the truth has knocked.**

And here is something worth pausing on. The verb *jā'a* — to come — is the verb of arrival. Not the verb of being shown. Not the verb of being demonstrated. The truth *came*. It moved. It traveled. It *arrived.* The structure suggests the truth has agency — it is the one doing the coming. The human being is the one standing at the door.

What does the human being do when the truth comes? That is the question.

[PAUSE]

#### Putting the two together

Let's stop for a moment and feel the symmetry the ayah has just built.

| First crime | Second crime |
|---|---|
| **Iftarā** — fabricated | **Kadhdhaba** — declared false |
| About Allah | About the truth |
| Creates a lie | Rejects a truth |
| Adds what isn't there | Subtracts what is there |
| Falsehood put *in* | Truth thrown *out* |

These are the **two opposite errors of the human heart in relation to God.**

One person says: *"God is like this, God said this, God wants this"* — when in fact God did not say it, and is not like that, and did not want it. They have manufactured a god.

Another person says: *"What God actually revealed — that's not real. That isn't true. Reject it."* They have evicted the real God.

And here is the chilling thing the ayah makes us see: these are not two different people. They are often **the same person**, doing both. The person who manufactures a self-serving image of God and pins it on Him will, when the *actual* truth arrives — when the messenger comes, when the verse comes, when the reminder comes — be the *first* to reject it as a lie. Because the actual truth contradicts the manufactured one.

This is one mechanism. **The lie you fabricated about God becomes the wall against which the truth of God breaks.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Final Word — *Mathwā*

Now look at how the ayah closes:

> أَلَيْسَ فِي جَهَنَّمَ **مَثْوًى** لِّلْكَافِرِينَ
> "Is there not in Hell **a dwelling-place** for those who deny?"

The word is *mathwan*. And of all the words the Quran could have used for the destination of those who reject, this one is doing very specific work.

#### What does *mathwā* mean?

The root is ث-و-ي — *thawā*. In its core sense, *thawā* means **to remain in a place, to settle, to take up residence permanently.** Lisān al-ʿArab gives the basic meaning as *aqāma* — to stay, to reside. *Mathwā* is the noun of place from this verb: **the place where one settles, one's lodging, one's residence.**

Now Arabic is rich with words for "place." The Quran could have used *qarār* (a place of stability), *manzil* (a place where one descends), *dār* (a house), *maskan* (a dwelling). Each has its flavor.

*Mathwā* carries a particular weight. It is the place you **come to rest in.** The place your traveling ends. The place that absorbs you. *Aqāma* — the verb at the root — is the same verb used for someone who has stopped moving, who has *settled*, who has made a place their own.

When Allah says *mathwan lil-kāfirīn* — He is not saying the disbelievers will *visit* hell. He is saying hell is their **address.** Their *settled* address. The place their motion ends.

[PAUSE]

#### And here is what makes this word terrible

In Surah Yusuf, when Yusuf is welcomed into the Egyptian household, the master says to his wife: *akrimī mathwāh* — **"Honor his lodging" — make his stay good.** *Mathwā* there means: the place you are housing him in.

In several other places in the Quran, *mathwā* is used for **hospitality** — the place where a guest is received and made to stay.

So *mathwā* carries a flavor of: *this is where they are housed.* Hell, the ayah is saying, is where they are **received and made to stay.** It is their accommodation. It is the room prepared for them.

There is a terrible inversion here. Because what is the surah about? Houses. The spider builds a house. The spider's house is the frailest of houses. The disbeliever chose to dwell somewhere other than with Allah. They picked their house in this world.

And now, at the end, the ayah says: *there is a dwelling for them.* They wanted a house. They will be given one.

Notice that scholars like Ibn ʿĀshūr have specifically pointed to the resonance between the spider parable in 29:41 and the closing imagery here. The whole surah is structured around *dwellings* — the frail dwelling chosen, and the permanent dwelling arrived at. The ayah closes the architecture of the surah.

#### The closing question

> أَلَيْسَ فِي جَهَنَّمَ مَثْوًى لِّلْكَافِرِينَ
> "**Is there not** in Hell a dwelling-place for those who deny?"

The ayah began with a rhetorical question. It ends with a rhetorical question.

*Alaysa* — "is there not?" — is another *istifhām taqrīrī* — a question that confirms. Like a teacher who has finished proving a point and turns to the class and says: *"Now, is this not clear?"* It is not asking. It is sealing.

So the ayah opens by sealing the verdict — *no one is more unjust than this* — and closes by sealing the consequence — *and is there not for them a place to be housed?*

The whole ayah is two rhetorical questions framing two named crimes. The questions are not openings. They are closures. They are doors being shut.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge to Theme

So what have the words shown us?

That the supreme injustice is not horizontal — not what we do to each other. It is vertical. It is what we do with **God's name** and **God's truth.** That this supreme injustice has two opposite faces — *manufacturing* a lie about Him and *rejecting* the truth that comes from Him. And that the human being who does either or both has, by his own choice, picked the house he will be housed in.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the actual life of the human heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two-Directional Disease of the God-Picture

Let me start somewhere very human.

You have probably noticed, in your own life, that two of your friends can hear the same advice and one of them will manufacture an exaggerated version of it — *"so what you're saying is..."* — while the other will dismiss it entirely — *"that's not really what you mean."* Both have done something to the actual thing you said. One has added to it. One has subtracted from it. Neither has *received* it.

There is a name for this in modern psychology. It's sometimes called **filtering.** The mind, when it receives information that touches our self-image or our comfort, very rarely receives it as it is. It either inflates it into something I can resent or rejects it into something I can dismiss. The one thing it almost never does is sit still and let the information be what it is.

Now scale that up. Apply it to Allah.

Every human being who thinks about God forms some kind of image. We can't help it. We are meaning-making creatures. The question is never *do you have an image of God?* The question is *whose image is it?* Is the image you have of Allah **the one He gave of Himself** — through His names, His speech, His revelation? Or is the image you have **one you have manufactured** — assembled out of your hopes, your fears, your culture, your father, your wounds, your need?

This ayah names the first half of the disease. *Iftarā ʿalā Allāhi kadhiban.* **Fabricated a lie about Allah.**

We tend to hear that phrase and think: oh, this is about pagan idolatry. About people saying Allah has a son, or that Allah has daughters, or that Allah is three. And yes — the classical mufassirūn (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī) name all of these as paradigm cases.

But the ayah is wider than that. *Iftirā' ʿalā Allāh* is **any** manufacturing about Allah. Any attribution to Him of something He did not say. Any "God is" or "God said" or "God wants" that is sourced not from revelation but from my own projection.

Here is where it gets close to home.

#### The God I made and the God He revealed

If you ask most people, *"what is God like?"* — and listen carefully — you will hear, very quickly, a description that fits the speaker's own wounds. A person abandoned by their father will describe a distant God. A person crushed by religious authority will describe an angry God. A person who never had limits placed on them will describe a permissive God. A person who needs God to validate them will describe a God who validates them.

This is not always *iftirā'*. We are limited. We see in part. We grow.

But it becomes *iftirā'* — fabrication — at the moment we **insist on the manufactured god instead of the revealed God.** When Allah, through His own speech, has *told us who He is* — and we say: *no, but my God is —* and finish the sentence with a description Allah did not give.

The moment we close our Qur'an and walk out with a god *we* assembled rather than the God *He* described, we have done the first half of the ayah.

[PAUSE]

#### And then the second half

Now here is the mechanism that should terrify us, because it explains why people who *seem* religious can become hostile to revelation.

The manufactured god feels safer. It feels familiar. It looks like the person who made it. And so when the *actual* God speaks — through the Qur'an, through a teacher we did not want to listen to, through a verse that contradicts the comfortable theology we had assembled — the manufactured god is **threatened.**

And the heart that has been protecting the manufactured god *has only one move left.* It must reject the real thing. *Kadhdhaba bil-ḥaqqi lammā jā'ahu.* **Declared the truth false when it came.**

This is not the rejection of someone who never heard. This is the rejection of someone who heard, recognized the contradiction with their manufactured god, and chose the manufactured one.

Watch this in yourself. Not in some imagined hardened *kāfir*. **In yourself.** When you read a verse and feel a little sting — that small recoil, that small *no, that can't be right* — that is the moment. That is the moment of *kadhdhaba bil-ḥaqq lammā jā'ahu* in miniature. The moment of choosing the made god over the real one.

Most of us live our religious life with a low-grade version of this. Allah's actual speech keeps arriving, and we keep softening it, blurring it, ignoring the parts that don't suit the manufactured image, amplifying the parts that do. It is a slow, daily *iftirā'* paired with a slow, daily *takdhīb.*

The ayah is saying: **at the supreme end of that spectrum, this is the most unjust thing a human being can do.**

So our work — and this is the practical move I want to leave you with — is to **let Allah describe Himself.** When you read His names, let them stand. When you read His promises, let them stand. When you read His warnings, let them stand. Do not soften them into what you wish He had said, and do not dismiss them as someone else's God. Receive Him as He has revealed Himself. That is the cure for both halves of the disease.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Question of Where You Are Building Your House

Now let me bring us back to the spider.

Earlier in this surah — in ayah 41 — Allah said:

> *"The likeness of those who take protectors other than Allah is as the likeness of a spider that took a house — and surely the frailest of houses is the spider's house, if only they knew."*

When we read that ayah on its own, we tend to think the lesson is about *strength*. The web is fragile. The shelter you are choosing is fragile.

But that's not the deepest cut of the parable. The deepest cut is this: **the spider chose to dwell there.** Of all the places the spider could have settled, of all the shelters available, the spider built — and *lived in* — the most fragile. The injustice in the parable is not the web's weakness. The injustice is *the choice to make it home.*

Now look at how Surah Al-Ankabut closes. Ayah 68. *Alaysa fī jahannama mathwan lil-kāfirīn?* **Is there not in Hell a dwelling-place for those who deny?**

The word is *mathwā* — the settled abode, the place of rest, the lodging. And it is the **counter-image** to the spider's house.

The spider chose its house in this world.
Allah will give it its house in the next.

The whole surah is built around this question: **where are you choosing to settle?**

#### Where do we settle?

Think about your own life. What is the place you keep coming back to? Where does your mind go when it has nowhere it has to be? What is the thing you reach for in the empty moment?

That is your *mathwā*. Your dwelling. Where you live when you think no one is watching.

If your dwelling is His name — His remembrance, His speech — you are dwelling somewhere that does not collapse. *Mathwā ṣāliḥ.* A good dwelling.

If your dwelling is the image of how others see you, or the comfort of distraction, or a fantasy life you replay, or a self-image you are constantly polishing — that is a web. It is exquisite work. It can be very beautiful. The spider's web *is* beautiful. But it is *the frailest of houses*. And one wind, one test, one death — and the web is gone, and you are without shelter.

The surah opened by saying: *do you think you will not be tested?* It closes by saying: *do you not see where the untested-but-rejecting heart ends up?*

[PAUSE]

#### The two questions the surah leaves you with

So the two questions of the surah, the questions this final ayah seals, are:

**First:** *What is the image of God you live with — and is it His, or yours?*
**Second:** *What is the dwelling-place you are quietly making for yourself — and is it the kind of thing that can hold weight?*

Notice that both questions point to the same crisis. Because the image of God you live with **determines** where you build your house. A god you fabricated will tell you to dwell wherever feels comfortable. A god you let reveal Himself will tell you to dwell where He told you to dwell.

The cure for both halves of the disease — the manufacturing and the rejecting — is **reception.** To stop manufacturing. To stop rejecting. To sit, and let Allah's actual words come to you, and let them be what they are.

The practical move here is small and daily: when a verse arrives, before you react, **pause.** Don't immediately soften it. Don't immediately resist it. Let it land. Let it say what it says. Receive the truth *as it comes to you* — *lammā jā'aka.* Because *that* — *that moment of reception* — is the opposite of every single thing this ayah condemns.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Look at the ayah one more time, now that we've walked through it.

> وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ افْتَرَىٰ عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا أَوْ كَذَّبَ بِالْحَقِّ لَمَّا جَاءَهُ ۚ أَلَيْسَ فِي جَهَنَّمَ مَثْوًى لِّلْكَافِرِينَ

It is built like a courtroom verdict.

- *Who is more unjust than the one who...?* — **The charge is sealed by rhetorical question.**
- *...fabricated a lie about Allah —* **The first crime: importing falsehood.**
- *...or rejected the truth when it came to him —* **The second crime: exporting truth.**
- *Is there not in Hell a dwelling for those who deny?* — **The sentence is sealed by rhetorical question.**

Two questions. Two crimes. And in the middle, the precise word — *lammā jā'ahu* — that tells us this is the rejection of the truth that **arrived**, not the truth that was hidden.

And the whole structure sits inside a surah whose central image is the choosing of a house. The spider made one. The one this ayah names will be given one. Both chose dwellings. Only one of those dwellings stands.

### Bringing the Threads Together

The linguistic work and the thematic work were the same insight from two sides.

The linguistic side told us: *iftarā* and *kadhdhaba* are the two opposite hands of the same disease — *ẓulm*, displacement, putting things in the wrong place. One puts falsehood where Allah is. The other puts the label *falsehood* on Allah's speech.

The thematic side told us: every human heart does both. We fabricate an image of God to live with, and then we reject the actual God when He speaks. The cure is reception — letting Allah describe Himself, letting His truth come to us *as it comes*, without manufacturing and without dismissing.

Both sides arrive at one place: **what kind of dwelling are you building?** The disease of fabricating and rejecting is the disease of refusing to settle in His house and choosing to settle in your own. And the spider, the surah told us, will not enjoy the house she chose.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I describe God to myself or others — am I describing the God *He has revealed*, or am I describing a God I have *assembled* from my own needs?
2. When a verse arrives that contradicts my comfort — what is my first move? Reception, or recoil?
3. What is the small daily *iftirā'* I have made my peace with — the small lie about Allah I have stopped noticing?
4. What is the *truth that has come to me* — through reading, through teaching, through correction — that I am quietly still calling false?
5. Where, honestly, am I dwelling? When my mind has no task, where does it settle? Is that my *mathwā*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The supreme injustice is to fabricate a god you find comfortable and then reject the God who actually speaks — and the human being who does this is, by that very choice, picking the dwelling in which he will be housed forever.

### The Closing Invitation

What I am inviting — not commanding — is this. The next time you read a verse, before you do anything with it, **let it arrive.** Let it cross the threshold. *Lammā jā'aka* — *when it has come to you* — receive it as it came. Not as you would have written it. Not as your manufactured god would have approved of. As it is.

That posture — that small daily reception — is the antidote to both halves of this ayah. It refuses the manufacturing. It refuses the rejection. And it begins, slowly, to make the heart a place where Allah's truth is the dwelling, instead of a place where Allah's truth is the visitor we keep turning away.

### Du'ā

O Allah — let me not fashion You into the shape of my comfort, and let me not turn away when Your true word arrives at my door. Make my heart the dwelling for Your speech, and not a web of my own assembling. And on the day every soul finds the house it built, place me in Yours — not in the one I made for myself.
