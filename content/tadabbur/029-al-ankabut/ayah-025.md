# The Affection That Becomes Enmity

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 25 — Ibrahim's Final Diagnosis

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

وَقَالَ إِنَّمَا ٱتَّخَذْتُم مِّن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ أَوْثَـٰنًا مَّوَدَّةَ بَيْنِكُمْ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ ثُمَّ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ يَكْفُرُ بَعْضُكُم بِبَعْضٍ وَيَلْعَنُ بَعْضُكُم بَعْضًا وَمَأْوَىٰكُمُ ٱلنَّارُ وَمَا لَكُم مِّن نَّـٰصِرِينَ

**Transliteration:**

Wa qāla innamā ittakhadhtum min dūni-llāhi awthānan mawaddata baynikum fi-l-ḥayāti-d-dunyā, thumma yawma-l-qiyāmati yakfuru baʿḍukum bi-baʿḍin wa yalʿanu baʿḍukum baʿḍan, wa maʾwākumu-n-nāru wa mā lakum min nāṣirīn.

**Translation:**

"And [Ibrahim] said: 'You have only taken, besides Allah, idols — [as a means of] affection among yourselves in the life of this world. Then on the Day of Resurrection, you will disown one another and curse one another, and your refuge will be the Fire, and you will have no helpers.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Step back into the scene the listener almost never sees. Ibrahim, peace be upon him, has just been pulled out of a fire. Not a metaphor. A literal pyre, built by his city, fed by his community, lit by people who knew his name. He smashed their idols. They built a furnace. They threw him in. And Allah said to the fire: **kūnī bardan wa salāman** — *be coolness and peace.*

Now he stands before them again. Smoke still in the air. Their plot has failed. Every theological argument has been answered. The miraculous has happened in front of their eyes.

What does Ibrahim say to them now?

You'd expect rebuke. You'd expect vindication. You'd expect: *"Did you see what your gods couldn't do?"*

He says something stranger. Something quieter. Something more diagnostic.

He says: *I know why you really worship these idols. And I know what's going to happen to that reason.*

### The Strand Running Through Al-Ankabut

This surah is called **Al-Ankabut** — *The Spider*. A few ayahs later, Allah will give the surah's iconic image: those who take protectors besides Allah are like a spider building a house. **Wa inna awhana-l-buyūti la-baytu-l-ʿankabūt** — *and indeed the frailest of houses is the spider's house.*

[PAUSE]

The spider's web looks like architecture. It catches things. It seems to work. But it's spit and air. One breath dissolves it.

Ibrahim's ayah, twenty verses earlier, is the **sociology** of that spider's house. Before Allah shows us the metaphor, He shows us the human reality the metaphor is naming: a community held together by something that looks like love but is actually structural fragility waiting to collapse.

### The Hook

What if the most painful loss on the Day of Judgment isn't losing Paradise?

What if it's discovering that the people you loved most in this world — the family, the tribe, the circle you organized your entire life around — only loved you because of something both of you were pretending was true?

And the moment the pretense falls, the love falls with it. And what's left isn't neutral. It isn't strangers. It's something worse.

Ibrahim names it. In thirty-two words. Stay with him.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ittakhadhtum* — The Verb of Taking-As

#### Teaching the form first

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns called *forms* — Form I, Form II, Form III, all the way to Form X. Each form does something to the base meaning. Think of forms like grammatical gears. The root might mean "to take" — but shift it into a different gear and now it means "to take *as*," or "to take *for oneself*," or "to make something *into* a possession."

Here's the everyday version. In English, "I took a book" and "I adopted a book as my guide" are doing different things. The second one isn't just acquisition — it's *appointment*. You've assigned the book a role. You've conferred status on it.

Arabic does this kind of work with morphology rather than extra words. Form VIII of a verb — called *iftaʿala* — often carries a sense of *taking-for-oneself*, of *deliberate self-implicating action*. It's not passive reception. It's active appointment.

#### Opening the word

The word Ibrahim uses is **ittakhadhtum** — *you (all) took as.* It is Form VIII of the root **أ-خ-ذ** (to take). This isn't the simple verb "you took." Allah could have said **akhadhtum**, the Form I — *you took*. He didn't.

**Ittakhadhtum** means: *you took for yourselves, you adopted, you appointed, you conferred status upon.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment.

Ibrahim is not accusing them of *finding* idols lying around. He's not accusing them of being passive victims of inherited religion — even though they will later try to plead exactly that excuse. He is using a verb that locates the agency in *them*.

*You appointed these stones to a role. You raised them. You commissioned them. The idols didn't make themselves into gods. You made them gods.*

#### Deepening

Notice something. The Quran uses this same Form VIII — *ittikhādh* — in some of its most theologically loaded moments. Whenever a created thing is being elevated to a status it doesn't deserve, this gear gets engaged.

*They took (ittakhadhū) the calf as a god.*

*Do not take (lā tattakhidhū) the Jews and the Christians as awliyāʾ in place of believers.*

*They took (ittakhadhū) their rabbis and monks as lords besides Allah.*

It's the verb of **appointment to false office**. The verb of *promoting* something to a rank it cannot bear.

Now hear what Ibrahim is saying: *You appointed stones to bear the weight of your community's love.*

The stones didn't ask for the job.

#### The first thread closes

Here's what this single morphological choice does to the ayah. It removes every escape. It says: whatever happens next — the disowning, the cursing, the Fire — is not happening *to* you in some random catastrophe. It is happening because of an appointment *you* made. A role *you* assigned. A throne *you* built.

The collapse on the Day of Judgment is not arbitrary. It is the resignation letter of every false god you ever hired.

### Element Two: *Awthān* — The Sound of Heaviness

#### Teaching the principle first

Arabic words sometimes *sound like what they mean*. Linguists call this phonosemantic correspondence — the texture of the word echoes the texture of the thing. A soft thing gets soft consonants. A heavy thing gets heavy consonants. A thing that drags gets a word that drags.

The Quran uses this with extraordinary precision. Light, joyful things get light open vowels. Dense, burdensome things get clustered, weighted sounds.

#### Opening the word

The word Ibrahim uses for idols is **awthān** — singular **wathan**. The root is **و-ث-ن**.

Now, Arabic actually has multiple words for *idol*. There is **ṣanam**, which is the common word — sculpted, shaped, often human-form. There is **nuṣub**, a stone set up as a marker. There is **jibt** and **ṭāghūt** for objects of false worship more broadly. The Quran picks among these deliberately.

Here, Ibrahim says **awthān**.

#### Deepening

Classical lexicographers note that **wathan** carries a specific connotation: a heavy, set, immovable object — typically a stone, not necessarily shaped into human form, just *there*, planted, weighted.

Say it aloud. **Wathan.** The opening **w** is a slow, rolling consonant. The **th** is a heavy fricative — the tongue has to push against the teeth to make it. There's no light vowel in the word. It is dense from start to finish.

Now contrast it with **ṣanam** — *ṣ-n-m* — which has a brighter, more open quality. **Ṣanam** sounds like something *made*. **Wathan** sounds like something *that just sits there*.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter?

Because Ibrahim is not making a theological complaint about *artisanship*. He's not saying *you should have picked better-looking gods*. He's naming the **inert, lifeless, weight-without-life** character of what they're worshipping.

These things are stones. They are heavy. They cannot move. They cannot answer. They cannot help. They cannot even step aside on the Day of Judgment to lighten the load they helped create.

The very sound of **awthān** is the sound of dead weight. And Ibrahim is telling them: *the thing you placed in the center of your community to hold it together — it has no life in it. It has only weight. And weight, without life, is what drags you down.*

#### The second thread closes

When a community organizes itself around a dead thing, the community looks alive only because the people are still moving. The center is inert. Take away the people, and you don't have a religion — you have a quarry.

The sound of the word **awthān** is the sound the heart makes when it discovers, too late, that what it was kneeling toward was never breathing.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Mawaddatan Baynakum* — The Hidden Subject

#### Teaching the principle first

In Arabic, when a noun follows a verb without a preposition and takes a particular case ending, it often functions adverbially — it tells you *why* or *how* the action was done. This is called **mafʿūl li-ajlih** — the *object of purpose*. It answers the question: *for the sake of what?*

The everyday version: if I say in English, "She bought flowers, *as a gesture of friendship*" — that last phrase isn't *what* she bought. It's *why*. It names the underlying social function.

#### Opening the construction

Ibrahim says: **ittakhadhtum... awthānan, mawaddatan baynakum fi-l-ḥayāti-d-dunyā.**

*You took idols — [as] affection among yourselves in the life of this world.*

The word **mawaddatan** is in the accusative — it is not *what* they took. They took *idols*. The **mawaddah** — the *affection*, the *bondedness*, the *warmth between people* — is **the reason**. The function. The purpose.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most psychologically devastating diagnoses in the Quran. And almost every translation softens it.

Ibrahim is not saying, *you took idols, and you also happened to love each other.* He is saying: **the idols ARE the love. The idols are the medium of your social cohesion. You worship them because shared worship is what holds you together.**

#### Deepening

The root **و-د-د** gives us **wudd**, **mawaddah** — a particular kind of love. Classical lexicographers distinguish it from **ḥubb**, which is the broader category of love. **Mawaddah** has a specific texture: it is *affectionate cordiality*, *warmth of relation*, the love that *binds people together socially*. Allah uses this same root when He describes the love between spouses — *wa jaʿala baynakum mawaddatan wa raḥmah.*

So **mawaddah** is the **glue word**. It is love-as-bond, love-as-social-fabric.

And Ibrahim is naming it — with surgical precision — as the *real* function of the idol system.

Stay with what he is revealing.

He is saying: *You don't actually believe in these stones. You believe in each other. The stones are the meeting place. The stones are the ritual that gives you something to do together. The stones are the reason the festival happens, the reason the tribe gathers, the reason your father and your son are on the same side. Take the stones away — and you don't lose a god. You lose the only thing that made you feel like one people.*

[PAUSE]

This is why your people tried to burn me, Ibrahim is saying. Not because they love Marduk. Because they love **each other** through Marduk. And I am the threat to that **each other**.

#### The composite picture from classical commentary

Classical mufassirūn — Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī — all converge on this reading from different angles. Some emphasize that the idols were the *cause* of their mutual affection (they loved each other *because* of shared worship). Some emphasize that the affection was *temporary*, lasting only in the dunya. Some emphasize that the idols served as a *common ground* — a shared object of devotion that made strangers feel like kin.

These are not three different opinions. They are three camera angles on the same reality:

The idol is the **excuse** for the bond.
The bond is **shallow** because the excuse is hollow.
And when the excuse is removed — the bond is removed with it.

#### The third thread closes

Ibrahim has named the deepest mechanism of false religion: **it is rarely about the god. It is almost always about the people you get to be a "we" with by sharing the god.**

And the moment Allah strips away the shared object of pretense on the Day of Judgment, the "we" doesn't soften into neutrality.

It hardens into hate.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Three words — *ittakhadhtum*, *awthān*, *mawaddatan baynakum* — and Ibrahim has built an entire sociology.

You appointed dead weight to be the center of a community whose only real cargo was each other.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about a Day none of us has yet seen but all of us will.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Affection That Was Never Really About You

#### Beginning where the listener lives

Think of a friendship you had that ended — not in a fight, but in *evaporation*. You were once close. You spent hours together. You laughed at the same things. And then something changed in the conditions — you left the job, you left the city, you stopped going to that gym, you stopped believing what they believed — and within months, you realized: *we were never actually friends. We were colleagues. We were neighbors. We were co-believers. The friendship was the shape that the shared situation made.*

It's a particular kind of grief. It's the grief of discovering, retrospectively, that what you thought was a bond between *you and them* was really a bond between *you and them and a third thing* — and the third thing was doing all the work.

Now expand that. Imagine an entire community organized that way. A whole tribe. A whole civilization.

Ibrahim is telling his people: *that is what you have.*

#### Letting the ayah illuminate

The terror of Ibrahim's diagnosis is that it's not *cynical*. He's not saying *you don't love each other*. He's saying *you do — but the love is brokered. There is a middleman. And the middleman is fake. And when the middleman is exposed, the brokerage collapses.*

This is why **mawaddah** is the precise word — not **ḥubb**. **Ḥubb** can survive its causes. **Mawaddah** is bond-love, situational love, the warmth of being-on-the-same-side. **Mawaddah** needs a *baynakum* — a "between you" — and the *between* has to be made of something.

If the *between* is Allah — the bond is eternal. The Quran will later say of the righteous, *al-akhillāʾu yawmaʾidhin baʿḍuhum li-baʿḍin ʿaduww, illa-l-muttaqīn* — *every close friend will be an enemy to the other on that Day, except the God-fearing.* The exception is precise. Friendships built on taqwa survive the unveiling. Every other friendship is rented furniture.

[PAUSE]

#### The deeper movement

Here is where this ayah turns into a mirror.

Ibrahim's people had stone idols. We mostly don't. But the *mechanism* he is naming — using a shared object of devotion to manufacture social bondedness — is one of the most universal human strategies for community-building. It has not gone away. It has just changed materials.

What is the "shared object" that holds your circle together? Is it a political position? A grievance? A nostalgia? A status game? A consumption pattern? An ideology? An aesthetic? A tribe of taste?

If you removed it — what would remain?

Test it honestly. If you stopped agreeing about *that thing* tomorrow, would the love survive? Or would you become slowly invisible to each other, the way old friends do when they no longer have the meeting room?

[PAUSE]

#### Where this lands

The point is not to despair of every friendship. The point is to ask: **what is the *baynakum* — the between — actually made of?**

Ibrahim's people built their *between* out of stone. Stones don't last in the heart's economy. They are heavy enough to weigh you down. They are not alive enough to hold you up.

The believers' *between* is made of Allah Himself. *Innama-l-muʾminūna ikhwah* — the believers are brothers — and the brotherhood is real because the third thing in the room is real.

#### The application

One specific practice for this week: think of the three or four most important relationships in your life. For each one, identify the *baynakum* — the "between." What is the love actually organized around?

If the answer is *Allah, mutual taqwa, sincere wishing of good* — protect that bond. It is one of the few things going with you through the grave.

If the answer is *a shared opinion, a shared resentment, a shared lifestyle* — don't end the friendship. Just *don't trust it with your soul*. Don't make it the reason you compromise your dīn. Don't let a temporary *between* veto your eternal one.

The relationships built on Allah will survive everything — including each other's disappointment. The others will survive only as long as the prop holds.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Disowning — Why the Day of Judgment Bond Flips to Hatred

#### Beginning where the listener lives

There is a particular kind of anger that only people who *were close* can feel toward each other. Strangers don't hate. Strangers are indifferent. The deepest betrayals — the ones that produce the most poisonous, sleepless, decades-long resentments — happen between people who *used to love each other*.

Why?

Because love that was real-feeling but false-foundationed leaves a debt the heart cannot forgive. You gave yourself to someone on terms that turned out to be a lie. And when you discover the lie, the love doesn't return to zero. It inverts.

Ibrahim names this with brutal precision: **yakfuru baʿḍukum bi-baʿḍin wa yalʿanu baʿḍukum baʿḍan** — *some of you will disown others, and some of you will curse others.*

#### The vocabulary of the rupture

Notice the two verbs Allah pairs.

**Yakfuru** — from the root **ك-ف-ر**, the same root as *kufr*. But the base meaning of *k-f-r* in Arabic is *to cover, to conceal, to disown, to disavow*. When Allah uses *kafara* about belief, it means *to cover the truth, to refuse to acknowledge*. Here, in this ayah, it carries its older, sociological flavor: *to disown, to renounce association.*

*Yakfuru baʿḍukum bi-baʿḍin* — *each of you will disown the other.* You will pretend you didn't know them. You will say *I never met this person. I had nothing to do with him.*

Then the second verb: **yalʿanu** — from **ل-ع-ن**, *to curse, to invoke distance, to push away.* *Laʿn* in Arabic is not just verbal abuse — it is a *prayer for the person to be removed from divine mercy*. It is the most active form of dissociation. Disowning says *I don't know you*. Cursing says *I want Allah to send you further from me.*

[PAUSE]

#### The architectural pattern

Look at what's happening structurally. Ibrahim said the idols functioned as **mawaddata baynikum** — affection between you. And what does the Day of Judgment produce? Verbs of **baʿḍukum baʿḍan** — *some of you against others of you*.

The exact same **mutual structure** — but the content has flipped from love to curse.

The *baynakum* doesn't disappear. The structure of being-bonded-to-each-other doesn't dissolve. What dissolves is the **content** of that bond — and what rushes in to replace the affection is the rage of having been deceived together.

This is the deepest horror Ibrahim is exposing. It isn't that you'll be strangers to each other on that Day. It's that you'll still be **bonded** — but the bond will be made of mutual blame.

#### The Quran echoes this elsewhere

Surah Al-Baqarah, ayah 166-167: **idh tabarra'a-lladhīna-ttubiʿū mina-lladhīna-ttabaʿū wa ra'awu-l-ʿadhāba wa taqaṭṭaʿat bihimu-l-asbāb. Wa qāla-lladhīna-ttabaʿū law anna lanā karratan fa-natabarra'a minhum kamā tabarra'ū minnā** — *When those who were followed disown those who followed, and they see the punishment, and all ties between them are severed. And those who followed will say: "If only we had a return, so that we could disown them as they have disowned us!"*

The same pattern. The same disowning. The same wish — too late — to do the disowning first.

[PAUSE]

#### What this exposes about the present

This is not just a future event. It is a **forensic preview**. Ibrahim is telling them — and us — what is *already true* about their bonds, what will simply become *visible* on that Day. The Day of Judgment doesn't *create* the curse. It *uncovers* it.

Which means: the relationships in your life that will turn to curse on that Day are *already* secretly that. The disowning happens then because the love was *never about you* now. It was about the prop you both agreed to honor.

And here is the mercy in this ayah, buried but real: **Ibrahim is telling them this *while they still have time to change it.*** This whole speech is delivered in the dunya. The fire has been cooled. The dialogue continues. The diagnosis is given so that the diagnosis can be *escaped*.

#### The application

One specific practice: examine the *kufr-ready* relationships in your life. Where, if you stripped away the shared performance, would the love evaporate? Don't sever those relationships. Instead, **try to convert them**. Introduce something real into the *baynakum*. A shared dhikr. A shared sadaqah. A shared prayer for each other. A conversation about Allah where there used to only be a conversation about the prop.

If the relationship can metabolize that — Allah has been quietly added to the *between*, and the bond becomes one that survives the grave.

If the relationship recoils from it — you've discovered, gently, what Ibrahim was telling his people loudly. And you have time, still, to know it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Look at how this ayah is built.

**First movement (the dunya):** *You appointed idols as affection between you in the life of this world.*

**Pivot word:** *Thumma* — *then.* A word that means *afterwards, sequentially, with consequence.* The hinge of the ayah.

**Second movement (the akhirah):** *On the Day of Judgment, you will disown one another and curse one another.*

**Third movement (the verdict):** *And your refuge is the Fire, and you will have no helpers.*

[PAUSE]

The structure mirrors the spiritual reality. The *first* movement names what was *built*. The *second* movement names what that building *becomes*. The *third* movement names where the rubble *ends*.

And notice the final detail: **wa mā lakum min nāṣirīn** — *and you have no helpers*. The word **nāṣirīn** — *helpers* — is plural. Ibrahim is closing the door on every alternative source of rescue. Not just the idols. Not just the tribe. Not just the leaders. *No helpers, of any kind.*

Why plural? Because in the dunya, you had *many* — your community, your gods, your fathers, your patrons, your alliances. The plural was the comfort. *I have so many people on my side.*

The ayah ends by quietly removing every single one. The plurality of helpers in the dunya becomes the **absence of all helpers** in the akhirah. The fullness of the social *baynakum* becomes the emptiness of *mā lakum*.

It is the same pattern again. What looked like abundance is shown, in the unveiling, to have been nothing at all.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are saying the same thing from two directions:

- **Ittakhadhtum** taught us: false bonds are *appointed*. They are not accidents. We choose them.
- **Awthān** taught us: the centers of false community are *dead weight*. They have no life to give back.
- **Mawaddatan baynakum** taught us: the love was *brokered* through a hollow middleman.

And the thematic depths showed us:
- The *baynakum* — the between — is the most important architectural question in any relationship.
- The structure of bondedness *outlasts* its content — which is why fake love becomes real hate on the Day of revelation.

It is one insight, viewed twice.

### Questions to Carry

1. In the most important friendships of my life — what is the *baynakum* actually made of? If I took the prop away, would the love survive?

2. Am I currently appointing something to a throne it cannot bear? A reputation, a role, a relationship, an opinion I've made central to my identity?

3. Is there a relationship in my life that I sense, quietly, is held together by something hollow? What would it cost me to introduce Allah into the *between*?

4. When I imagine the Day of Judgment, do I imagine being alone — or do I imagine being *bonded in mutual blame* with the people I once organized my life around? Which is worse?

5. Whose **mawaddah** in my life is built on Allah — and have I told that person, lately, that I love them for His sake?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**The communities we build around hollow centers will not just collapse on the Day of Judgment — they will invert into the very enmity our love was hiding.**

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to become a hermit. It is not asking you to suspect every friend. It is asking you to look at the *architecture* of your bonds with honest eyes — and to begin, gently, the slow work of *introducing Allah into the between*.

Where you find friendships built on Him — protect them, water them, thank Allah for them.

Where you find friendships built on something else — don't sever them. Convert them. Bring one small piece of real *baynakum* into them: a prayer for each other, a moment of dhikr, an honest word about the Beloved.

What is built on Allah, lasts. What is built on stones, will one day curse you for ever building it.

Choose the first.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who placed *mawaddah* between hearts that turn to You — let the love in our lives be love that survives the unveiling. Be Yourself the *between* in every bond we carry, so that on the Day when every false foundation collapses, we find that the people we loved are loving us still, because we loved them only for You. Save us from the *mawaddah* that becomes *laʿn*, and gather us with those whose affection was made of nothing but Your remembrance.

آمين.
