# The Brother Who Asked Them to Hope for Judgment

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 36

> [ayah:29:36] وَإِلَىٰ مَدْيَنَ أَخَاهُمْ شُعَيْبًا فَقَالَ يَـٰقَوْمِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَٱرْجُوا۟ ٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَ وَلَا تَعْثَوْا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ مُفْسِدِينَ
>
> *Wa ilā Madyana akhāhum Shuʿaybā, fa-qāla yā qawmi-ʿbudū-Llāha wa-rjū-l-yawma-l-ākhira wa lā taʿthaw fī-l-arḍi mufsidīn.*
>
> **"And to Madyan, their brother Shu'ayb. He said: O my people, worship Allah, and *hope for* the Last Day, and do not run wild in the earth as corruptors."**

---

## Introduction — A City on the Caravan Road

Before we go anywhere near the text, I want you to close your eyes and walk with me into Madyan.

Madyan was not a sleepy desert village. Madyan was a thriving commercial city sitting along the trade route between the Hijaz, Egypt, and Syria — a junction. Caravans rested there. Weights and scales were unboxed there. Spices, grains, oils, fabrics changed hands there. Madyan was, in modern language, a financial center. A place where money was being made.

And Madyan was beautiful. The Quran tells us elsewhere that Shu'ayb's people had been "increased in number," that they had been given prosperity. They were not poor. They were not desperate. They were comfortable. They were succeeding.

And what Shu'ayb came to confront was not a temple full of idols. It was something far more familiar to us. It was a marketplace where the scales had been quietly tilted. Where the merchant gave you slightly less than what you paid for. Where contracts had hidden teeth. Where the powerful negotiated from positions you couldn't refuse. Where everyone, when they bought, demanded full measure — and when they sold, gave a little less. A whole society had agreed to a small lie, and the small lie had become the architecture of the economy.

Now, why is Allah telling Mecca about this?

Because Mecca was *also* a trade city. Mecca was a junction. The Quraysh were merchants. The wealthy of Mecca who were rejecting the Prophet ﷺ were people whose hands knew the feel of a scale. So when this surah turns to Shu'ayb, the early Muslim community would have leaned in. *This is about us. This is about the people next door.*

[PAUSE]

And here's the hook. The hook that will pull us through this whole reflection.

When most prophets address the Last Day, the Quran uses the language of warning. *Fear it. Beware of it. Be conscious of it.* But Shu'ayb, addressing this comfortable, prosperous, cheating merchant city, uses a verb that no English translation quite captures. He doesn't tell them to *fear* the Last Day.

He tells them to **hope for it.**

Stay with that. Why would a prophet tell people to *hope for* the day of reckoning? And why would Allah give that exact word to *this* community in particular?

Let's find out.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: أَخَاهُمْ — "Their Brother"

Before we touch the verbs, look at the framing. *Wa ilā Madyana akhāhum Shuʿayban.* "And to Madyan, their brother Shu'ayb."

I want to teach a concept first, before we use it. In Arabic, when you describe someone's relationship to a community, you have many options. You can say *minhum* — "from among them." You can say *rasūluhum* — "their messenger." You can say *al-rajulu min ahli Madyan* — "the man from the people of Madyan." Each carries a different weight.

But Allah uses none of those. Allah uses **akh** — brother. And He uses it with a possessive pronoun attached to *them*, not to Shu'ayb. *Their* brother. Not "Shu'ayb whose people were these," but "the people whose brother was him."

Notice something. The grammar puts the *people* first and the prophet second. The community is the noun, the prophet is described in relation to them. He belongs to them before they belong to him.

This is the Quranic signature for the prophets sent to specific peoples. Hud is *their brother*. Salih is *their brother*. Lut is *their brother*. Shu'ayb is *their brother*. Allah does not send a stranger to confront a people. He sends one of their own. Someone whose mother they knew. Someone whose accent matched theirs. Someone whose face had eaten with them at weddings.

Here is what this invites us to consider. The hardest message in the world — *you are corrupting yourselves and you will be destroyed* — is delivered not by an outsider who can be dismissed, but by a brother who cannot. You can ignore a stranger's warning. You can build a wall against a foreigner's critique. But when your *brother* tells you the family business is built on a lie, something inside you has to either accept it or murder the part of you that loves him.

Madyan chose the second option. They did not just reject Shu'ayb's message. They threatened to drive him out. They mocked him. And eventually they were destroyed by a sound from the sky.

But notice what Allah preserved in the telling: even after they rejected him, even after they threatened him, even after they were destroyed — the Quran still calls him *their brother*. The brotherhood was not retracted by their rejection. He remained, eternally, *theirs*.

[PAUSE]

There is a tenderness in this. A heartbreak. The prophet is not someone above his people looking down. He is one of them, who came back to them with a lamp, and they put the lamp out.

### Element Two: وَٱرْجُوا۟ — "And Hope For"

Now we come to the heart of this ayah. The word that should stop us in our tracks.

*Wa-rjū-l-yawma-l-ākhir.* "And hope for the Last Day."

Let me first teach the concept. The Arabic verb here is from the root **r-j-w** (ر-ج-و). And in classical Arabic lexicography — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — *rajāʾ* sits in a very specific semantic field. It is not the same as *khawf* (fear). It is not the same as *iʿtiqād* (mere belief). And it is not even the same as English "expect," which is morally neutral.

*Rajāʾ* is what a thirsty traveler feels when he sees the outline of a well in the distance. *Rajāʾ* is what a mother feels in the ninth month. *Rajāʾ* is what a farmer feels when the first cloud appears after a dry season. It is anticipation tilted toward something *good*. It is longing braided with confidence. It is the heart leaning forward into a future it wants.

That's the verb Allah puts in Shu'ayb's mouth.

Now, sit with this for a moment. Look at how every other prophet, in this same surah and across the Quran, addresses the Day of Judgment. *Fear it. Be conscious of it. Be warned of it.* The default mode of the Last Day in scripture is *taqwa* — protective awe. It is something to be guarded against.

But to *Madyan* — to the people of the marketplace, the people of the tilted scale — Shu'ayb says: **hope for it.**

Why?

Here is what classical commentators noticed and what the morphology confirms. The verb *rajāʾ* in this construction implies that whatever you are hoping for, you are *acting in accordance with the hope of receiving it*. You are arranging your life around its arrival. You are preparing the house for the guest you are expecting.

So when Shu'ayb tells these merchants to *hope for* the Last Day, he is saying something devastating about their current state.

He is saying: **you have been arranging your life as if it will never come.**

[PAUSE]

The man who tilts the scale by half a gram does not believe — *in his bones, in his behavior* — that there is a day coming when every gram will be weighed back. The economy of cheating is built on the secret denial of accountability. You can say with your tongue that you believe in the Akhirah. But if you cheated your customer this morning, you did not, in your *acting* self, hope for the Day.

And here is the second devastation. *Hope for it.* Hope for the day that will tear open every contract, expose every weight, replay every transaction. Hope for it.

Why would you hope for the day that exposes you?

Only if you have nothing to hide. Only if you have arranged your life so that the unveiling will be your vindication, not your humiliation. Only if you have lived in such a way that the Day, when it comes, comes as the *fulfillment* of what you spent your life building, not as the *cancellation* of it.

Stay with this. Shu'ayb is doing something extraordinary. He is not telling them to be terrified of the Day. He is telling them to *long for* it. And the only people who can long for it are the people who have made the daily choice to stop cheating, to give the full measure, to honor their word — so that when the books are opened, the books will smile back at them.

The verb *rajāʾ* turns the Last Day from a guillotine into a wedding. From an audit into a homecoming. But only — only — for the one who has been preparing the house.

[PAUSE]

And one more layer. *Rajāʾ*, in the lexicons, also carries the sense of *lengthening one's view*. The opposite of *rajāʾ* is short-sightedness. The merchant who shaves a gram off the scale today is making a calculation against an *immediate* benefit. He sees only this transaction, this hour, this profit. *Rajāʾ* is the cure for that disease. It says: *lift your eyes. There is a Day at the end of the road. Run your scale according to that Day, not according to this hour.*

The cheater is a man with no horizon. The believer is a man whose horizon includes the Day. Shu'ayb is not asking Madyan to add a religious belief to their lives. He is asking them to *lengthen their gaze*.

### Element Three: تَعْثَوْا۟... مُفْسِدِينَ — The Doubling That Is Not a Repetition

Now the closing of the ayah. *Wa lā taʿthaw fī-l-arḍi mufsidīn.* "And do not run wild in the earth as corruptors."

Here is something that Arabic readers feel immediately and English readers must be helped to see. The ayah uses *two* words that both relate to corruption. *Taʿthaw* and *mufsidīn*. On the surface this looks redundant. *Do not corrupt as corruptors.* Why both?

Let me first separate them.

The verb **ʿathā** (root ع-ث-و) is documented in the classical lexicons as carrying a very specific image: to run loose, to act with abandon, to behave wildly without restraint — and the lexicons specifically note that ʿathā is used for *fasād*, for corruption-causing wildness. Imagine an animal that has slipped its tether and is now crashing through a garden, trampling, eating, breaking — not with malice, but with *unrestraint*. That is *ʿathā*. The wildness of an unbridled creature.

The word **mufsidīn** (root ف-س-د, in Form IV active participle plural) is the agent noun. *Those who actively spread corruption.* Where *fasād* is the disease, *mufsid* is the carrier. *Mufsidīn* names them by their function. They are no longer people who *do* corruption. They *are* corruption-spreaders. The verb has become an identity.

Now look at the grammar. *Mufsidīn* sits as a **ḥāl** — a circumstantial accusative. The ḥāl in Arabic describes the *state* of the subject *while* the verb is happening. So the literal architecture of the sentence is: "Do not run wild in the earth — *while being corruption-spreaders*."

What is the doubling doing?

Here is what this invites us to consider. Allah is showing us, in two beats, the *anatomy* of moral collapse.

**Beat one: the action.** *Taʿthaw.* The wildness. The slipping of restraint. The first cheat at the scale. The first shaved gram. The first lie told because it was easier than the truth. *Taʿthaw* is what you *do.*

**Beat two: the identity.** *Mufsidīn.* The state you become *while* doing it. You did not just cheat — you became a cheat. You did not just lie — you became a liar. The action curdled into the noun. The verb hardened into who you are.

The Arabic is showing us that *fasād* is a two-stage disease. First it is something you do. Then it becomes something you are. And the most dangerous thing about the second stage is that you can no longer feel the first one. The cheat who has become a cheater no longer feels the moment of cheating. It is now simply how he conducts business. The wildness has been domesticated into normalcy.

This is why Shu'ayb does not just say *don't do fasād*. He says *don't run wild in the land while being corruption-spreaders* — naming both the *act* and the *identity* — because by the time the act becomes the identity, the warning has come too late.

[PAUSE]

And notice the *location*. *Fī-l-arḍ.* "In the earth." Not *in your homes*, not *in your contracts*, not *in your marketplaces* — even though those are the actual venues. *In the earth.*

Why does Allah expand the venue?

Because corruption in the marketplace is not local. The shaved gram on Tuesday in Madyan rebalances the moral weight of the whole earth. Cheating is not a private sin. It is a wound to the order of creation itself. The earth is the witness. The earth is the location. The earth is what your fasād damages.

When a merchant tilts the scale, the *earth* tilts.

### The Bridge

So we have three things from the language alone. We have a brother who came from inside the family. We have a verb that turns the Last Day from a threat into a longing. And we have a doubled construction that exposes how an act becomes an identity.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the kind of life Shu'ayb was inviting Madyan to leave behind, and the kind of life he was inviting them to enter.

## PART 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Day You Hope For Is the Life You Are Living

Begin where you live.

You have something on your calendar — a meeting, an appointment, a conversation, a moment — that is coming. And depending on what you have done in the days leading up to it, you either *dread* it or *look forward to* it.

The student who studied looks forward to the exam. The student who didn't, dreads it.

The employee who did the work looks forward to the review. The one who slacked, dreads it.

The friend who has been honest in the relationship looks forward to the hard conversation. The one who has been lying, dreads it.

There is a perfect, almost mechanical correspondence between *what we have done* and *how we feel about the day of accounting for it*. We do not need to be taught this. Every human being knows it in their body.

Now bring this knowledge to the ayah.

Shu'ayb is telling Madyan: *the Day is coming. You will stand before the books. The scales of every transaction will be reweighed.* And then he gives them the diagnostic: **how do you feel about that Day?**

If your stomach drops at the thought of it, that is data. That is your own life telling you that you have been arranging your hours in a way that the Day will not vindicate. If your heart leans forward toward it — if there is even a small *rajāʾ*, a small longing — that is also data. That is your life telling you that something in your hours has been pointed in the right direction.

[PAUSE]

The Last Day, in Shu'ayb's framing, is not primarily a threat. It is a *mirror*. It reflects back to you the truth about how you have been living. The man who hopes for it has, by the very fact of his hoping, given evidence that his life is in some kind of order. The man who dreads it has, by the very fact of his dread, given evidence of where the corrections need to be made.

And here is the spiritual practice this opens up.

Stop trying to *decide* whether you believe in the Last Day. Belief is too easy to fake to yourself. Instead, ask: *do I look forward to it?* Because the looking-forward cannot be faked. *Rajāʾ* arises only when the life beneath it is already aligned. You cannot manufacture *rajāʾ* out of nothing. It is the perfume that rises from a particular kind of life.

So the question to carry from this theme is not *do I believe.* It is *can I hope?* And if I cannot — what would have to change in my Tuesday afternoon, in my contracts, in my words to my spouse, in the way I close out my workday — for the Day to become something I could lean forward into?

The practical application: pick one transaction, one relationship, one habit this week where you have been *shaving the gram*. Not the big sins. The small unrestraint. The little wildness. And stop. Not because you fear the Day. Because you want to hope for it.

### Theme Two: There Is No Worship Without Justice in the Marketplace

Look at the trio Shu'ayb gives them. *Worship Allah. Hope for the Last Day. Do not corrupt the earth.*

In the modern religious imagination, these are three separate categories. Worship is what happens in the masjid. Hope for the Last Day is theology, what you affirm in your creed. Don't corrupt the earth is ethics, what you do at work.

But Shu'ayb does not separate them. He puts them in one breath. One sentence. *Connected by the simple conjunction wāw.* Worship-Allah-and-hope-for-the-Last-Day-and-do-not-run-wild-as-corruptors. *One thing.*

Why?

Because in the prophetic vision, these are not three commands. They are one command from three angles.

**Worship Allah** is the *vertical* dimension. Your relationship up.

**Hope for the Last Day** is the *temporal* dimension. Your relationship across time, oriented toward the horizon.

**Do not corrupt the earth** is the *horizontal* dimension. Your relationship out, with creation and other people.

And Shu'ayb is telling Madyan — telling *us* — that these three dimensions are not separable. You cannot have one without the others. The man who claims to worship Allah while shaving the scale has not actually worshipped Allah. The man who claims to believe in the Last Day while cheating his customer has not actually believed. The man who is just to his customer but does not turn his face to Allah is missing the vertical that makes the horizontal coherent.

[PAUSE]

This is why Madyan is such a frightening case study. Madyan was not a city of *unbelievers* in the usual sense. They were not idol-temple builders like Quraysh. They were not running blood-festivals like the people of Lut. They were respectable. They were religious in some surface sense. They believed in many of the right things.

But their *marketplace* was corrupted. And Shu'ayb came and said: that one corruption is enough to bring this whole civilization down. The shaved gram in the morning unmakes the prayer in the afternoon.

This was a terrifying message for Madyan and it remains a terrifying message for us. Because most of us are not idolaters. Most of us are not running cosmic rebellions against Allah. Our temptation is not paganism. Our temptation is the small, professional, polite cheat. The contract written with a hidden clause. The product sold knowing it will not last as long as we implied. The hour billed that was not fully worked. The signature on the form that we know is not quite true. The negotiation in which we used a power asymmetry we knew the other party could not refuse.

These do not feel like *idolatry*. They feel like *business*.

But Shu'ayb came to people who also called it business. And Allah called it *fasād fī-l-arḍ.* Corruption in the earth.

[PAUSE]

Here is where the modern bridge becomes most painful. We have arranged a culture in which *separating the dimensions* is the default. We worship in one room. We do business in another. We affirm the Last Day in our credo and we run our quarterly numbers without ever mentioning it. We have made the trio into three separate things.

Shu'ayb came to undo that separation.

The practical application: take one place in your professional or financial life where you have made peace with a small wildness. A contract clause you would not want read aloud on the Day. A representation you make to clients that is not quite true. A way you measure your own labor that you would not accept if measured against you. Name it. Then stop it. That single act of returning the gram to the scale is a more authentic worship than the one offered by the man who prays five times and shaves the gram in between.

Because the prayer of the cheater, Shu'ayb is telling us, does not reach the Day. It dies somewhere along the way.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look back now and see how the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The word *akhāhum* — *their brother* — established that the message comes from inside the family. It is not foreign. It is one of us, telling us the truth about us. The first theme of *hope as diagnostic of life* has the same shape: the Day is not a foreign threat being imposed from outside. It is the inside truth of how we are already living, made visible. Both the prophet and the Day are *intimate*.

The verb *warjū* — *and hope* — established that the Last Day is not primarily a fear-object but a longing-object for the one whose life is in order. The second theme of *worship and justice as one thing* completes the picture: only when the vertical, the temporal, and the horizontal are integrated can the *rajāʾ* arise. Hope is the symptom of integration. Dread is the symptom of fracture.

And the doubled *taʿthaw...mufsidīn* — *do not run wild as corruptors* — exposed how an act becomes an identity. This is the *engine* of why Shu'ayb's whole sermon mattered. If the act stayed an act, you could repent it. But once it has hardened into the identity, you can no longer see it. The brother had to come *now*, before the wildness completed its journey into the noun.

### Questions to Carry

1. If the Day came tomorrow, would I lean forward into it, or would my stomach drop? And what does the answer reveal about the life I have been arranging?

2. Where in my professional or financial life have I shaved the gram so consistently that I no longer notice the shaving?

3. Have I separated my worship from my marketplace? And if so, when did the separation happen, and what would it cost me to dissolve it?

4. Whose *brother* is being sent to me — what voice from inside the family of my life is trying to tell me a truth I have been refusing to hear?

5. What single transaction this week could I conduct in a way that I would be willing to have read aloud on the Day?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The marketplace is the masjid you cannot escape, and the Last Day is the mirror that will tell you which of the two you have actually been worshipping in.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to overhaul your whole life this week. Shu'ayb did not ask Madyan for a revolution. He asked them for *one thing*: stop running wild. Restore the scale. Honor the contract. Give the full measure.

The invitation from this ayah is not to add a new act of worship. It is to subtract a small act of cheating. To find the place where the wildness has slipped its tether, and to put the tether back on. Not because you fear the Day — but because you want, when it comes, to be the kind of person who can *hope* for it.

That is what Shu'ayb came to give Madyan. They refused. The offer is still on the table.

### Du'a

O Allah, we ask You to make us of the people who *hope for* Your Day, not the people who dread it. Restore the scales of our lives — in our marketplaces, in our contracts, in our words, in the small wildness that has slipped past our notice. Make us brothers and sisters to one another, the way You sent Shu'ayb as a brother to Madyan, and let us hear the warning before the wildness becomes who we are. Āmīn.
