# Every Soul Will Taste Death — Then To Us You Will Be Returned

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 57

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:29:57] كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَآئِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ ۖ ثُمَّ إِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Kullu nafsin dhā'iqatu-l-mawt; thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn.

**Translation:**
> Every soul will taste death. Then to Us you will be returned.

---

## Introduction: The World Before The Verse

Before we open this ayah, we have to feel the room it was first heard in.

Imagine you are a Muslim in Makkah. Late period. The pressure has become unbearable. Your tribe has turned on you. The shops won't sell to you. Your cousin won't look at you. You walk through streets you grew up in, and they no longer recognize you as theirs. Some of your friends have already been tortured. Some have died. Some have left for Abyssinia and you have not seen them since.

And then a word comes down from the Prophet ﷺ that you have been quietly dreading: **leave**. Leave your home. Leave the graves of your parents. Leave the well your grandfather dug. Leave the only city your family has ever known. Walk into a desert toward a town you have never seen, to live among people who are not your blood.

The Surah we are inside — *Al-Ankabut* — has been preparing this moment from its first verse. It opened by saying: *Did people think they would be left to say "we believe" and not be tested?* It gave you the prophets — Nuh whose people drowned, Ibrahim thrown into a fire, Lut whose city was overturned, Shuʿayb whose people were destroyed — one after another, men who lost their homes, their kin, their cities, for the sake of the One who sent them.

And in the middle of the surah, an image was placed that the whole surah is named after: the spider. **The frailest of homes is the home of the spider** — and that is the image of anyone who clings to something other than Allah. Your house, your wealth, your status, your safety — all of it, a spider's web. Beautiful in the morning light. Gone with a single breath.

And now, right before this ayah, Allah has just commanded the believers to migrate. *"O My servants who believe — indeed My earth is wide, so worship Me alone."*

The believer hears this command and feels everything inside him tighten. *Leave?* Everything in him is rooted here. His mother is buried here. His children sleep here. His tongue formed itself on these streets.

And into that tightening — into that grip the world has on his heart — Allah drops seven words.

[PAUSE]

What if I told you that these seven words were designed to do one thing? To loosen — gently, completely, finally — every grip the world has on your heart. Not by frightening you. Not by shaming you. But by reminding you of something you have known since you were a child, but somehow forgotten.

Let's open the ayah.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Kullu nafsin* — "Every soul"

Before we look at how Allah opens this ayah, let me ask you a question.

In English, when we want to talk about death's universality, what word do we use? We say "everyone." *Everyone dies.* It is a population statement. A demographic fact. It catches all the bodies in a net.

But notice what English does *not* do. It does not reach inside. It treats death as something that happens to a *crowd*, to a *number*, to a *species*. You can hear "everyone dies" a thousand times and never once feel that *you* are inside the word "everyone."

[PAUSE]

Now look at how Allah opens.

He does not say *kullu insān* — every human being.
He does not say *kullu ḥayy* — every living thing.
He does not say *kullu aḥad* — every single one.

He says: **كُلُّ نَفْسٍ** — *kullu nafsin* — every **soul**.

The word **nafs** in Arabic does not mean "person" as a body-count. It means the inner self — the self that wants, that fears, that loves, that schemes, that hopes, that wakes up at three in the morning and cannot fall back asleep. It is the self that says *I*. The self that is reading these words right now.

So when Allah says *kullu nafsin* — He is not making a population statement. He is reaching inside the chest of every single reader, across all time, and addressing the *one* who is in there. He is bypassing the demographic and going straight to the *I*.

Stay with this.

There is a version of this ayah that He could have revealed which would have landed as a fact. *Every human will die.* True, but distant. The kind of truth you nod at and forget. But by choosing **nafs**, Allah refused to let death be a statistic. He made it personal. He made it interior. He made it *yours*.

Notice something else: **nafsin** here is indefinite — *a* soul, *any* soul, *every* soul. In Arabic, when an indefinite noun follows the word *kull*, it sweeps. It catches everything that could possibly be called a soul. Rich and poor. King and slave. Prophet and disbeliever. Strong and weak. Old and infant. Believer and rejecter.

There is no soul that escapes the reach of this word.

And here is what is remarkable: this ayah appears three times in the Quran — here in Al-Ankabut, in Aal Imran 3:185, and in Al-Anbiya 21:35. Three different surahs. Three different contexts. And in all three, the opening is identical: *kullu nafsin*. As if Allah is saying: *however the conversation began, however the surah opened, however the test was framed — this is the line you cannot escape. This is the truth that closes every door of denial.*

[PAUSE]

So before we have even reached the second word, Allah has done something to you. He has reached into your chest, found the *I* that lives there, and quietly placed it inside the sentence.

You are now inside this ayah. There is no spectator seat.

### Element Two: *Dhā'iqatu-l-mawt* — "Will taste death"

Now we come to a word that should stop us cold.

If you wanted to say "every soul will die" in Arabic, the natural phrasing would be: *kullu nafsin tamūt* — every soul dies. Or: *kullu nafsin sa-yamūt* — every soul will die. Both are clean, accurate Arabic. Both communicate the fact.

Allah chose neither.

He chose: **ذَائِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ** — *dhā'iqatu-l-mawt* — *taster of death.* A soul that *tastes* death.

Let me teach a small grammatical point before we go further. The word **dhā'iqah** is not a verb here — it is an active participle. In Arabic, an active participle names the *one who does the action* as a quality of who they are. So *kātib* is "one who writes" — a writer. *Ḥākim* is "one who judges" — a judge. *Dhā'iq* is "one who tastes" — a taster.

When Allah says *kullu nafsin dhā'iqatu-l-mawt*, He is not saying *every soul will die at some moment in the future*. He is naming the *soul itself* as **a taster of death**. As if it is part of what you *are*. As if the soul carrying you through this life is, by its very design, a soul on its way to a tasting.

But that is the grammar. The deeper question is: **why "taste"?**

[PAUSE]

The root of **dhā'iq** is ذ-و-ق — the root for the sense of taste. It is the sense that requires the *closest possible contact*. You cannot taste from a distance. You cannot taste through a screen. You cannot taste by hearing about something. To taste, the thing must enter you. It must pass your lips, touch your tongue, mix with you. The mouth is the most intimate threshold of the body.

So when Allah describes death as something the soul will **taste** — He is telling us several things at once.

First: this is not something that will happen *to* you from the outside. This is something that will *enter* you. Death will not stand at the doorway of your life and knock. It will sit at your tongue. Closer than any external event could be.

Second: tasting is *personal* in a way that nothing else is. Two people can hear the same sound, but they cannot share the same taste. The taste is *had* by one person at a time. When Allah says every soul will *taste* death, He is reminding you: **no one will taste your death for you.** Your mother cannot taste it for you. Your wealth cannot taste it for you. The crowd of "everyone dies" dissolves — and you are left at the table with a single plate.

Third — and this is where the word opens up — *to taste* something is to *experience* it consciously. It is not annihilation. The dead do not become nothing. The dead *taste*. The soul does not switch off; it has a moment of meeting, a moment of contact, a moment of knowing. Classical scholars noticed this and said: Allah did not say *every soul will be killed by death* or *will be erased by death*. He said *every soul will taste death*. The soul survives the tasting. It is the body that ends, not the *one who tastes*.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with what this does to your imagination of death.

Most of us, when we imagine death, imagine a *thing happening to a body*. The body stops. The lights go out. The end.

Allah's image is different. The soul leans forward and *tastes*. The taste is sharp. The taste is undeniable. The taste passes — and the *one who tasted* is still there, on the other side of the taste, more awake than ever.

What is remarkable is the texture of this word. *Dhā'iq.* It is a soft word in Arabic. It is the same root we use for "taste" in everyday speech — *dhuq*, taste this food, taste this drink. Allah did not pick a brutal word for what death will do to us. He picked the gentlest sensory word in the language. And this gentleness is itself the message: *you, soul, will not be smashed by death. You will taste it, and pass through it, and arrive at what comes next.*

For the believer about to leave Makkah and head into the desert, do you see what this does? He is afraid of dying on the road. Allah does not deny that he might die. Allah re-describes death itself. *It is not an erasure. It is a tasting. You — the* nafs *that fears — will still be there after the taste. The only question is: what comes after?*

And that brings us to the third element.

### Element Three: *Thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn* — "Then to Us you will be returned"

I want to start with the small word that hinges the whole ayah: **ثُمَّ** — *thumma*.

In Arabic, there are several words for "and then." There is *fa* — which means "and then immediately, right after." There is *wa* — which is just "and," with no time order at all. And there is **thumma** — which means "and then, after some time." It introduces a pause. A gap. A space between two events.

Allah did not say *kullu nafsin dhā'iqatu-l-mawti **fa**-ilaynā turjaʿūn* — "every soul tastes death and *immediately* is returned to Us." He said **thumma** — *then, after some time, you will be returned.*

That small word *thumma* opens up the grave. It is the space between the tasting of death and the standing before Allah. It is the *barzakh* — the realm of waiting. It is the long sleep that is not really sleep, the time that is not really time, the silence between the last breath and the first trumpet.

So already, in three letters, Allah has stretched the ayah from this life, through death, through the long quiet of the grave, and out the other side.

Now the next word: **ilaynā** — "to Us."

Notice He did not say *ilā Allāh* — to Allah. He did not say *ilā rabbikum* — to your Lord. He said *ilay**nā*** — **to Us**. The pronoun. The royal *We* that Allah uses in the Quran when He is speaking with majesty.

Why this word at the end of this ayah?

[PAUSE]

Look at the geometry of what Allah has built. The ayah opens with **every soul** — the widest possible word, sweeping in everyone who has ever breathed. And it closes with **to Us** — the narrowest possible destination. *Every* soul. *One* return.

The whole human story is compressed into this arc. We come from countless places — different mothers, different cities, different centuries, different languages, different sins, different victories — and we all converge on a single point. **To Us.**

And there is something more. The word *turjaʿūn* — "you will be returned" — is in the **passive voice**. Allah did not say *tarjiʿūn*, "you will return," as if you are the one walking. He said *turjaʿūn* — **you will *be* returned.** Something will return you. The matter is taken out of your hands.

In this life, the human being is the one who moves. You decide where to go. You buy the ticket. You pack the bag. You choose the door. The illusion of agency is total.

But the last journey is not one you take. It is one that is taken with you. **You will be returned.** Whether you want to be returned or not. Whether you are ready or not. Whether you packed for the trip or not. The agency that you spent a lifetime exercising — the choosing, the controlling, the resisting — collapses into a single grammatical fact: passive voice. *Turjaʿūn.*

[PAUSE]

And one more thing — the word **return** itself. *Ruju.* To go back. To come back to the place you started.

This is not the language of a *new* destination. It is the language of *coming home*. The ayah does not say *to Us you will be sent* or *to Us you will be brought* — it says *to Us you will be **returned**.* The implication, sitting quietly inside the verb, is that you *came from Us in the first place*. The soul did not begin in the body. The soul began in the knowing of Allah, when He took the covenant from the children of Adam — *am I not your Lord?* — and the soul answered, *yes*. That is the home you are returning to. The body was the journey. The grave is the doorway. The standing before Allah is the *coming back*.

So sit with the architecture now. Allah opens with **every soul**. He moves through **the tasting**. He pauses through **thumma** — the long quiet. And He closes with **a return to the original home**.

Seven words. A whole human story.

---

### Bridge

Notice what the linguistic construction has done. It has taken the largest fact of human existence — *we will all die* — and refused to let it be abstract. *Kullu nafsin* placed *you* inside it. *Dhā'iqah* made death personal and survivable — not an erasure, but a tasting. *Thumma* stretched the line into the unseen. *Turjaʿūn* — passive — removed the illusion of control. And the word **return** quietly told you: this is not exile. This is going home.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How To Hold The World Loosely

Before we touch the ayah, I want you to think about your hand.

When you were born, your hand was a fist. That is the first posture of human life. We arrive clenched. And we spend the first years of life learning to *grab*. The grab of the bottle. The grab of the toy. The grab of the parent's finger. The grab of the chest, of the wealth, of the lover, of the title, of the home.

By the time we are adults, our hands have learned a single skill better than any other: **the grip**.

We grip our reputations. We grip our routines. We grip the people we love, terrified of losing them. We grip our positions at work, our place in the family, our square of land. We even grip the things we *hate* — old hurts, old resentments, old failures — because at least they are *ours*.

[PAUSE]

And here is what nobody tells you about a grip: a hand that is gripping something cannot receive anything. A closed fist is closed in both directions. You cannot hold what is in front of you, and you cannot accept what is being offered. The grip costs you both.

The Muslims of Makkah, on the eve of hijrah, were a community of clenched hands. Hands gripping the houses they grew up in. The mosque they prayed in. The streets where their parents walked. The shops they had built. Their hands were clenched around Makkah — and Allah was asking them to leave.

The first instinct of anyone in that moment is *no*. *I cannot leave. I cannot let go. This is mine.*

And into that moment, Allah revealed seven words.

*Every soul will taste death.*

Read it again, but read it as the Muslims of Makkah heard it. They were not afraid of *dying* — they were afraid of *losing*. Losing the house. Losing the property. Losing the network. Losing the sense of belonging that had defined their entire lives.

And Allah said: **you were going to lose all of it anyway.**

[PAUSE]

That is what this ayah does. It does not say *don't be attached to your home*. It does not lecture you about materialism. It simply tells you the truth that you have always known but always pushed away: **the thing you are gripping was never going to stay in your hand**.

The house? You will let go of it.
The wealth? You will let go of it.
The position? You will let go of it.
The body itself — the very hand that is doing the gripping? You will let go of even that.

So the question Allah is gently placing in your chest is not *should I let go?* It is *when?* Will I let go now, willingly, while there is still time to use my hand for something better? Or will I let go on the last day, with nothing to show for the grip?

The believer who hears *kullu nafsin dhā'iqatu-l-mawt* before he leaves Makkah does not leave with a broken heart. He leaves with an *open hand*. He has not lost his home — he has simply given it back early. Everything he leaves behind, he was always going to leave behind. The only question was whether he would leave it behind *for Allah* or *to the worms*.

[PAUSE]

This is the practical work of this ayah in your life right now. Not theoretical. Not "someday." Today.

Look at what you are gripping. The job you are too afraid to leave even though it is destroying you. The relationship you keep returning to even though it dims your light. The status you are bending your worship to protect. The wealth you cannot give from because *what if I need it*. The grudge you are holding against your sibling because *they were wrong*.

You will let go of every one of those things. The only question is whether the letting go will be *worship* or whether it will be *defeat*.

The Companions chose worship. They opened their hands in Makkah, walked into the desert, and arrived in Madinah as the freest people on the earth — because they had pre-died. They had already tasted the loss death would one day require. And once you have tasted that loss willingly, *nothing can be taken from you anymore*. Everything has already been given back.

This is the secret the ayah carries in its first half. *Every soul will taste death* is not a threat. It is a key. It is the key to the closed fist. It is the way the human hand learns, finally, to open.

### Theme Two: The Direction Of A Life

There is a question that almost no one asks themselves until it is too late: **which way am I facing?**

Not literally. Spiritually. Existentially.

A human life is, in its truest description, a *direction*. Every choice you make today either turns you slightly more toward something, or slightly away from it. Over a year, those small turns compound. Over a decade, they become the shape of your whole self. By the end of a life, you are pointing in a direction you have been pointing in for so long that you no longer feel yourself pointing.

The cruel thing is that most people are *pointing*, but they have never named *toward what*.

Toward more comfort? Toward a higher salary? Toward being liked? Toward proving someone wrong? Toward never being hurt again? Toward something — but what?

[PAUSE]

Allah's words at the end of this ayah answer this question with a precision that should silence us.

*Thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn.* **Then to Us you will be returned.**

Whatever you *think* you are walking toward, the real direction of your life — the direction that is fixed, the direction nothing can change — is *toward Allah*. Every breath is a step. Every heartbeat is a step. Every meal, every conversation, every silence between conversations — a step. You are returning. You have been returning since the day you were born. You will return whether you intend to or not.

This is what makes the passive voice — *turjaʿūn* — so devastating and so liberating at the same time.

It is *devastating* because it strips you of the illusion that you are in charge of where your life is going. You are not driving. You are a passenger. Whether you face forward or backward, whether you sleep through the journey or stare out the window, the destination is fixed. *You will be returned.*

But it is *liberating* — and this is what the believer feels — because once you know that the direction is fixed, you can finally stop *fighting it*. You can stop pretending you have a thousand directions to choose between. There is one direction. Everything else is decoration.

[PAUSE]

Look at how this transforms a life.

Before this ayah, a person wakes up and faces a hundred questions. *What should I do today? What matters? What is worth my time? Who should I please? What should I chase?* The questions are exhausting because they assume the answers are not given.

After this ayah, a person wakes up and the question collapses into one. *I am being returned. What is the version of today that, when I review it from the standing before Allah, I will be glad I lived?* That question is not exhausting. That question is *clarifying*. It cuts through ninety percent of life's noise like a knife.

The believer who internalizes *thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn* is not depressed by it. He is *oriented* by it. He has been given the rarest gift a human being can receive in this life: **a fixed point on the compass**. He may still get lost. He may still wander. But he can never be *disoriented*, because the direction of his life has been named by the One who set him in motion.

And here is the most beautiful part. *Return.* Not *destination*. Not *arrival*. *Return.*

You are not being sent into a strange land. You are being sent **home**. The discomfort you have always felt in this world — the sense that something is not quite right, that you do not quite fit, that the joys are too small and the sorrows too sharp — that discomfort is *correct*. You were not made for this place. This place was a journey. You are returning to where you came from.

The believer's death is not a leaving. It is a *coming back*. The grave is not an exile. It is a doorway through which the soul finally steps back into the home it was made for.

[PAUSE]

So when you face anything today — a difficult conversation, a tempting sin, a moment of fear, a moment of pride — let this ayah be the question you ask. *Does this action point me toward the return, or away from it? Does this open my hand, or close my fist? Does this prepare me for the tasting, or does it bury the tasting under noise?*

And then act.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what this ayah has done in seven words.

The linguistic journey showed us:
- **Kullu nafsin** — death is not a population fact; it is reaching into *your* chest right now.
- **Dhā'iqatu-l-mawt** — death is not an erasure; it is a tasting. The *one who tastes* survives.
- **Thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn** — and after the tasting, after the long quiet, you will be *returned home*.

The thematic depths showed us:
- The clenched hand of the world must learn to open — and this ayah is the key.
- The direction of a human life is not chosen by the human. It is fixed by Allah. The wise one orients to it.

These are the same insight from two directions. The linguistic work tells you what *is true*. The thematic work tells you what to *do about it*. The truth: you are a soul on its way to a tasting and a return. The action: open your hand now, face the return now, *live* now as someone who has already accepted both.

This is what the believer leaving Makkah did. He stopped gripping. He turned his face toward the return. He walked into the desert holding nothing — because he had already given everything back.

And the world has never seen a freer human being than that.

### Questions To Carry

1. What is the one thing you are gripping most tightly right now — the thing you would be most afraid to lose? What would it look like to begin loosening that grip *today*, while it is still your choice?

2. If you imagine yourself, at the moment of tasting death, looking back at this week — what would you wish you had spent more time on? What would you wish you had spent less time on? What stops you from rearranging your time *now*?

3. *Turjaʿūn* — you will be returned. The passive voice. In what areas of your life are you still pretending to be the driver? Where are you exhausted from steering a car that was never yours to steer?

4. The ayah calls death a *return* — a coming home. Does that match how you feel about your own death? If not, what does that tell you about how you have been living?

5. The Companions heard this ayah and walked out of Makkah. What is the *Makkah* in your own life — the comfortable familiar place you know Allah is asking you to step away from? And what would it cost you to take the first step?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

You are a soul on its way to a tasting and a return — so open your hand now, face the return now, and live as someone who has already given everything back to the One who gave it.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not let this ayah become information. Let it become the *texture* of your day.

The next time you feel your fist closing around something — a worry, a possession, a person, a position — let *kullu nafsin dhā'iqatu-l-mawt* rise in your chest. Not as a threat. As a reminder. *I was going to let this go anyway. Let me let it go for Allah, not for the worms.*

The next time you feel disoriented — not knowing what to do, where to turn, what matters — let *thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn* rise in your chest. *I am being returned. What is the version of this moment that fits a soul on its way home?*

And then act.

That is the whole work of this ayah. Seven words. A lifetime of opening hands and turning toward home.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah — You who named us *tasters of death* and *those who will be returned* — loosen our grip on what You will take from us anyway. Let us give it back to You before You ask for it. And when the tasting finally comes, let our soul find that it has already been walking toward You for so long that the last step feels like coming home.

Āmīn.
