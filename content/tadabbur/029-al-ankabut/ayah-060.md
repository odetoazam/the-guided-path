# Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 60 — The Creature That Carries Nothing

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَكَأَيِّن مِّن دَآبَّةٍ لَّا تَحْمِلُ رِزْقَهَا ٱللَّهُ يَرْزُقُهَا وَإِيَّاكُمْ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ

**Transliteration:**
Wa ka-ayyin min dābbatin lā taḥmilu rizqahā — Allāhu yarzuquhā wa iyyākum, wa huwa as-Samī'u al-'Alīm.

**Translation:**
And how many a creature there is that does not carry its own provision — Allah provides for it, and for you. And He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World This Ayah Walked Into

Imagine you are a Muslim in Mecca around the twelfth year of revelation. You have a small business in the marketplace. Maybe a tannery. Maybe you sell dates. Maybe you weave cloth. Your father did this work. Your grandfather did this work. The customers know you. The suppliers know you. The seasons of trade are written into your bones.

And one day, the word comes: leave.

Leave the city your family has lived in for generations. Leave the well where your children drink. Leave the customers who buy from no one else. Leave the network, the reputation, the language of place that has fed you since you were old enough to eat solid food.

Go to Yathrib. A city you have never seen. Where you know almost no one. Where you have no trade. Where you do not yet know which streets are safe and which are not. Where you will arrive carrying what you can fit on a camel — and almost nothing more.

The Prophet ﷺ asks this of his people. And the believers begin to leave. Some have already left for Abyssinia. Others are preparing for Madinah.

But for many — and the Quran knows their names without naming them — a single question keeps them frozen at the gate of the city. Not "Is Islam true?" That question is settled. Not "Do I love the Messenger?" That love is real.

The question is smaller and uglier and harder to admit. It is this:

**If I leave, how will I eat?**

[PAUSE]

### What This Surah Has Been Doing

Surah Al-Ankabut — "The Spider" — has been a sustained meditation on one question: what does faith cost, and is the cost survivable? It opens by warning that no one is left untested: "Do people think they will be left to say 'we believe' and they will not be tried?" It tells the stories of Nūḥ, Ibrāhīm, Lūṭ, Shu'ayb — every one of them facing exile, ridicule, or violence for their belief. It compares the false supports people lean on to a spider's house — visually elaborate, structurally nothing.

By the time we reach ayah 56, the surah has built up to a direct command: "O My servants who have believed, indeed My earth is spacious, so worship Me alone." In other words: the city you cannot leave is not the only city in My earth. Move.

Then 57: "Every soul will taste death." A line that is almost casual in its devastation. The fear of dying somewhere unfamiliar — Allah dismantles it in seven words.

Then 58-59: "Those who believed and did righteous deeds — We will surely settle them in chambers of Paradise... those who were patient and upon their Lord relied."

And then 60. The ayah we are about to walk into.

### The Hook

I want you to notice something before we begin. In this ayah, Allah will mention the smallest, most helpless creature you can think of. He will say it does not carry its provision. And He will say He feeds it. And then He will turn to you — the human, the planner, the saver, the worrier — and place you in the same sentence as that creature.

But He will not say what you expect Him to say next.

You would expect, after speaking about provision, the name "ar-Razzāq" — the Provider. That is what the verse is *about*. But that is not the name He uses.

He uses two other names instead. And the names He uses tell you something He knew about your fear that you did not know He knew.

Let's find out what it is.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: كَأَيِّن — "How many you'd be astonished to know"

Before we open this word, I want to teach you something about Arabic — and English — that will let you see what Allah did here.

In English, when we want to talk about a quantity, we have a flat, neutral word: *many*. "Many people came." It's a measurement. There is no awe in it.

But English also has *another* construction that does something different. We say: "You would not *believe* how many people came." That phrase isn't a measurement anymore. It's an invitation to wonder. The number itself is being held up and admired. The speaker is saying: stop counting and start being amazed.

Arabic has both kinds of word for quantity. The neutral one is كَمْ — "how many," like asking a question on a form. But Arabic also has a heavier, more astonishing word that means something closer to that English phrase about not believing.

That word is **كَأَيِّن** (ka-ayyin).

Allah does not begin this ayah with kam. He begins it with ka-ayyin. Listen to the sound — the kaf opening, the long vowel, the doubled ya, the final nasal. It almost has the shape of a sigh of wonder.

[PAUSE]

**Notice what He is doing with this word.**

He is not giving you a statistic. He is not saying "there exist many creatures." He is saying: *you would be astonished if you knew the number*. Stop and consider the multitude. The classical scholars connected this form to *takthīr* — the act of expressing vast, almost uncountable abundance. It is the word you use when language gives up and points instead.

Now — why does this matter for the fear we are talking about?

The person frozen at the gate of Mecca, terrified to leave because of their livelihood, has a very small world in their head. They see the few customers they have, the one well they drink from, the single supplier they trust. Their imagination of provision has shrunk to a list they can count on one hand.

Allah begins by destroying the smallness of that list.

**"You would not believe how many."**

Look up. Look at the desert. Look at the ants under the rock. Look at the spider — and notice that Allah has just named this surah after the spider. Look at the lizards skittering across the wadi floor. Look at the worms in the soil. Look at the birds. Look at the fish in the Red Sea you have never seen. Look at the foxes, the hares, the beetles, the bees, the gazelles. How many a creature.

And He has not yet said anything about provision. He has only said: *the field is vast*. Your imagination is too small. Before we even talk about who feeds whom, expand your sense of how much is being fed.

**Here's what's remarkable:** The fearful mind shrinks the world to one job, one customer, one source. The first thing Allah does — before any theology, before any command, before any reassurance — is widen the world back to its actual size.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: دَآبَّةٍ — The Creature That Crawls

The next word is small, indefinite, and chosen with a precision you have to slow down to see.

**دَآبَّةٍ** (dābbah).

The root د-ب-ب in classical Arabic carries the sense of something that moves slowly, that creeps, that crawls along the ground. The lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* — record that dābbah is the most general word in Arabic for "creature that moves" — but in its core image, it is the small thing close to the earth. The ant. The lizard. The thing you might step on without noticing.

**Now notice what Allah did not say.**

He did not say *ṭayr* — bird. He did not say *bahīmah* — cattle. He did not say *wuḥūsh* — wild beasts. He did not say *insān* — human. He chose the word that points to the smallest, most overlookable creature in the imagination of the seventh-century Arab listener.

And He left it indefinite. Not "the creatures." Not "those creatures." Just — *a creature*. Any creature. The ant on the wall. The beetle in the corner. The lizard you saw yesterday and forgot.

[PAUSE]

**Stay with this for a moment.**

The person standing at the gate of Mecca, paralyzed by the question of how they will eat in Yathrib, has been thinking of themselves as *important*. Not arrogantly — but practically. They have credentials. A trade. A name. A network. They are a *somebody* in this market, and that somebody-ness is what feels like it secures their bread.

Allah does not begin His argument by saying "You are the noble human, the inheritor of Adam, surely Allah will provide for you." That would have flattered the ego He is trying to free.

Instead, He begins with the *least* of His creatures. The thing without name, without trade, without credential, without network. The crawling thing under the rock.

The argument is built from below. *If I feed even this — what makes you think your bread depends on the prestige of your position?*

It's the opposite of how we usually argue. We argue from the top: surely the king will be cared for. Allah argues from the bottom: surely the ant is cared for — *and you are not lower than the ant*.

### Element Three: لَا تَحْمِلُ رِزْقَهَا — "Does Not Carry Its Provision"

Now the verb. And here we have to be very careful — because what Allah says, and what you hear at first, are not the same thing.

The phrase is **لَا تَحْمِلُ رِزْقَهَا** — "it does not carry its provision."

The verb *taḥmilu* is from ح-م-ل, the root that means to bear, to carry, to transport, to load. It is the same root used for the camel that carries the load and the womb that carries the child. It is the word for *bearing* — taking weight on yourself and moving it.

The negation is in present tense. *Lā taḥmilu* — it does not currently, does not habitually, does not as a pattern of its life, *carry* its provision.

**Now — what does this actually mean?**

It does not mean the creature has no provision. The creature is eating. Watch the ant for ten minutes and you will see it eat. The verse is not denying that the creature receives rizq.

It is denying something else. **It is denying that the creature is the one transporting the rizq into its own life.**

The dābbah does not stockpile a winter pantry. It does not have a freezer. It does not have savings. It does not run a business that gives it leverage over a supplier. It does not have a 401(k). It does not have a contract guaranteeing twelve months of bread. It steps out of the rock in the morning and finds today's food. And tomorrow it steps out again and finds tomorrow's food.

The rizq comes — but the creature is not the one *carrying it in*.

[PAUSE]

**Notice something.**

Allah does not then say, "And you also do not carry your provision." He doesn't extend this exact phrase to humans. There is an asymmetry in the verse, and it is intentional.

Because we *do* carry. We carry briefcases to work. We carry resumes. We carry deals. We carry the weight of plans. We carry, and we carry, and the carrying feels like the reason we eat.

Allah is not asking you to stop carrying. He is asking you to notice that the creature carrying *nothing* is still being fed.

If your carrying is the engine, what is the engine of the ant?

And — this is the question that does the work — *what if your carrying is not actually the engine of your provision either, but only the appearance of it?*

[PAUSE]

The dābbah's case is your case stripped of the illusion. The carrying you do is real labor. But it is not the *cause* of the bread reaching your mouth. The cause is the same cause that feeds the dābbah. You have just been allowed to participate in your provision in a way the dābbah is not. That participation is a mercy, not a guarantee.

### Element Four: اللَّهُ يَرْزُقُهَا وَإِيَّاكُمْ — The Order, and the Pronoun

Read the next phrase with me. **Allāhu yarzuquhā wa iyyākum.**

"Allah provides for it — and for you."

Two things here that the Arabic listener catches and the English listener can easily miss.

**First — the order.**

Allah mentions the dābbah's provision *before* the human's. "He provides for *it* — and *you*."

This is striking. In any normal address, the human listener would expect to be mentioned first. We are the addressee. We are the noble creature. The Quran usually centers us. Why does Allah put the ant before us here?

Because the entire psychological move of the ayah depends on it. If He had said "He provides for you and for it," you would have heard, "Yes, of course He provides for me — and also for the small creatures, as a kind of footnote." The reassurance about *you* would have been first, and the creature would have been the supporting evidence afterward.

But Allah reverses it. He says: *He provides for it.* Full stop. Sit with that. Take it in. The ant is fed. The beetle is fed. The spider — yes, the spider this very surah is named after — is fed. The blind worm in the soil is fed.

And *then* — almost like a wave breaking on the shore — He adds: **wa iyyākum.** *And you.*

You are not the headline. You are tucked into the sentence after the ant. And the moment you let yourself be placed there — after the ant, alongside the ant, sharing the same divine attention — the entire architecture of your anxiety collapses. Because if He has not forgotten the ant, He has not forgotten you.

[PAUSE]

**Second — the pronoun.**

Allah does not say *wa yarzuqukum* — "and He provides for you," using a simple suffix. He says **wa iyyākum** — using the separate, independent object pronoun.

In Arabic, *iyyākum* is a heavier, more deliberate form of "you." It is the pronoun used when you want to give the object its own weight in the sentence — to set it apart, to spotlight it, to make sure the listener registers it as a distinct thing being addressed. It is the same form used in *iyyāka na'budu* — "*You alone* we worship" — where the pronoun carries the emphasis of singling out.

Here, *wa iyyākum* does something tender. It lifts the human listener out of the grammatical machinery of suffixes and gives them their own moment of being addressed. Allah has just put you after the ant — and now He gives you back your dignity by using the most weighted form of "you" that Arabic possesses.

It is as if He says: *I have placed you after the ant to humble your fear. And I have given you the heavy pronoun to honor your humanity. Both at once.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Five: السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ — The Names That Do Not Belong Here

Now — this is the line that does the deepest work, and it works through what is *missing* more than through what is present.

The ayah ends: **wa huwa as-Samī'u al-'Alīm** — "And He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing."

**Pause and notice what your mind expected.**

The whole verse is about provision. About rizq. About who feeds whom. By every law of Arabic stylistic expectation — by the patterns the Quran itself has established elsewhere — the closing divine names should match the theme. We would expect:

- *wa huwa ar-Razzāq al-Karīm* — "And He is the Provider, the Generous."
- *wa huwa ar-Razzāq dhū al-quwwah al-matīn* — "And He is the Provider, Owner of strength, the Firm." (which is how 51:58 closes a similar theme)

But here — in *this* verse, the most famous verse in the Quran about provision — Allah does not name Himself ar-Razzāq.

He names Himself *as-Samī'* — the Hearing. And *al-'Alīm* — the Knowing.

[PAUSE]

**Why?**

Let's sit with this carefully, because it changes the entire weight of the verse.

The fear of rizq lives in two places in a human being.

**The first place is the mouth.** It is the worry you say out loud. To your spouse: "What if we run out?" To your friend: "I don't know how I'm going to make rent." To Allah in du'a, the prayer you whisper at night: "Ya Allah, I am afraid." The spoken fear. The complaint. The articulated dread.

For that fear — Allah is *as-Samī'*. The Hearing. He hears every word of it. The conversation in the kitchen. The phone call. The whispered du'a at fajr. The complaint to a friend. *He hears it.* And He is telling you, at the end of this verse, that the worry you have been speaking — He has been listening to it the entire time.

**The second place is deeper. It is the fear you do not speak.**

The fear too ugly to admit. The fear that you yourself only half-know is there — the silent dread in the chest at 3 a.m., the unnamed anxiety that makes you check your bank account for the third time today, the buried terror that the world will not catch you if you fall. The fear so private that even your closest friend has never heard it. The fear you have not put into words because words would make it more real than you can bear.

For *that* fear — Allah is *al-'Alīm*. The Knowing. The One who does not need your speech to know what you carry. He knows the fear you have not spoken. He knows the dread you would not admit even to yourself.

[PAUSE]

**Here's what's remarkable.**

Allah does not close this ayah with ar-Razzāq, because *ar-Razzāq* would only have answered the question on the surface. It would have said: *I am the One who provides — so do not worry.* That is a true and beautiful answer, and it is the answer the verse has already given in its main clause.

But Allah knows that the *real* problem of rizq is not actually about food. It is about being unseen. It is about the suspicion — somewhere in the dark of the human heart — that we are alone in the universe with our small, embarrassing, undignified anxieties about money and survival. That nobody is watching the trembling. That the trembling is invisible.

By closing with as-Samī' al-'Alīm, Allah answers the deeper question. He says: *I am not just the One who feeds. I am the One who hears the voice that asked the question. I am the One who knows the fear behind the voice. Your trembling is not invisible to Me. Your anxiety is not unheard. The conversation in your kitchen — I have been there. The thought at 3 a.m. — I have been with it.*

**You are not alone with your fear of rizq. You have never been alone with it.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have walked through five linguistic features of this ayah. The astonishing *ka-ayyin* that widens your shrunken world. The humble *dābbah* that argues from below. The carrying that the creature does not do but you do — and the question of whether your carrying is really the engine of your bread. The reversed order that places you after the ant. The two divine names that do not seem to belong to a verse about provision — until you realize they are the only names that fit.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of human life under Allah's care.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The City You Cannot Leave

Let me start somewhere far from rizq and slowly move back to it.

There is a kind of paralysis that almost everyone you know is living inside. Not the obvious kind, where someone is physically trapped. The subtler kind. The kind where someone *knows* they should make a change — and can articulate the change clearly — and yet they do not move.

The job they should leave because it is destroying their spirit, but the salary makes leaving feel impossible.

The city they should move from because their family is somewhere else, but the career is here, and "I can't just abandon what I've built."

The haram income they know they should cut off, but they have grown accustomed to it, and the income that would replace it is not visible to them yet.

The relationship that nourishes nothing in them, but they have built a life inside it, and the cost of leaving feels unsurvivable.

The neighborhood, the school, the social circle, the identity — the thing they should walk out of, that they pray about walking out of, that their *fitra* tells them at night to walk out of.

And they do not move.

Why?

[PAUSE]

If you ask them, they will give you a financial answer. *I can't afford to leave.* Or a logistical answer. *It would take years to rebuild.* Or a face-saving answer. *People depend on me here.*

But underneath all of those answers, there is one fear doing the actual paralyzing. It is the fear of *rizq*. Not just money — but everything in the wide sense of provision: the bread, yes, but also the dignity, the network, the recognition, the sense of being a somebody, the structure that holds the days together.

The Muslims in Mecca were living this. Hijrah was not theoretical. It meant walking out of the city that was the entire shape of their economic and social life. And when the Prophet ﷺ commanded it, many obeyed — and some, we know from the seerah, hesitated. Some delayed. Some made calculations.

The hesitation was not a failure of belief. It was a failure to trust that Allah's hands extend beyond Mecca.

And this is the precise place where ayah 60 lands.

It is the ayah for everyone standing at a threshold they cannot cross because they cannot see how they will eat on the other side.

[PAUSE]

Allah does not say to such a person, "Leap and you will be fine." That would be a lie. People do leap and get hurt. He says something stranger and truer.

He says: *Look at the ant.*

The ant does not have a plan for next year. The ant does not have a savings account. The ant does not have a backup city in case the rock it lives under gets overturned. The ant does not have a network, a credential, a contract.

And the ant is alive. The ant is *eating*. Right now, somewhere in your kitchen, an ant is being fed.

The argument is not that you should become an ant. The argument is that the *system* that feeds the ant is the same system that feeds you — and that system does not stop at the gate of Mecca. It does not stop at the edge of your current city. It does not stop at the boundary of your current job. The system is as wide as the earth Allah called "spacious" four ayahs earlier.

**The city you cannot leave is small. Allah's earth is not small.**

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes which decision feels possible. The hijrah — the move, the resignation, the cutting off, the leaving — that has been impossible for years because the question *how will I eat?* has been a wall. Ayah 60 does not knock down the wall by promising you specifics. It dissolves the wall by widening the field. The question *how will I eat* presumed that the source of bread is *here, in this exact spot*. The ayah says: the source of bread is everywhere a dābbah crawls. Including the next city. Including the next chapter. Including the place you have not yet seen.

You do not have to know *how*. You only have to know *Who*.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Loneliness Inside Financial Fear

Here is the part of money-fear that nobody talks about.

It is not, at its root, about the money.

If you ask someone who lies awake at 3 a.m. worried about their finances what is actually keeping them awake, they will tell you about numbers. About the mortgage. About the credit card. About the kids' tuition. About the freelancer income that didn't come in this month. They will give you the spreadsheet.

But if you sit with them long enough, and listen past the spreadsheet, you will hear something else underneath. You will hear: *I am alone with this. Nobody knows how bad it is. Nobody is going to catch me if it falls apart. I am the only line of defense between my family and disaster.*

The loneliness is the real wound. The money is the surface.

This is why financial fear is so corrosive — far more corrosive than any single financial setback would predict. People can lose jobs and recover their spirit. People can have lean months and survive emotionally. What people cannot survive is the persistent, low-frequency conviction that *they are facing this alone*.

[PAUSE]

Now I want to take you back to the end of the ayah, because this is where it does its deepest healing.

**wa huwa as-Samī'u al-'Alīm.**

Why those two names, and not ar-Razzāq?

Because Allah was answering the wound, not just the spreadsheet.

If He had said *ar-Razzāq*, He would have given you a fact: *I provide. I am the Provider.* And the fact is true and beautiful — but you can hear a fact and remain alone with your fear. The fact does not climb into the room with you at 3 a.m.

But *as-Samī'* does.

As-Samī' means: the conversation you had with your spouse last week, where you tried to keep your voice steady so the kids would not hear how scared you were — Allah was in that conversation. He heard every word.

The du'a you whispered after fajr, the one that did not have eloquent Arabic, the one that was just *Ya Allah, I am tired* — Allah heard that. The phone call to your sister where you finally said the thing out loud you had been hiding for three months — Allah was on that call. He has been listening to every word of your fear, the eloquent words and the broken ones, for as long as the fear has existed.

And *al-'Alīm* means: even the fear you have not put into words. Even the dread you have not let yourself notice. Even the suspicion you would never admit out loud — *what if Allah is not paying attention to my small, ugly, undignified money worries; what if my anxieties are too unspiritual for Him to bother with* — even *that* suspicion, He knows. He knew it before you noticed it. He has been with it the whole time.

[PAUSE]

**Here's what this means.**

You are not alone with your financial fear. You have never been alone with it. The loneliness was the lie. The trembling was always witnessed. The 3 a.m. dread always had Someone in the room.

And once you realize this — really realize it, not as a theological proposition but as a fact about the room you are actually sitting in — the fear loses its corrosive quality. It might not disappear. The bills might still be hard. The numbers might still be tight. But the *quality* of the fear changes. It is no longer the lonely fear that eats the spirit. It becomes a witnessed fear — and witnessed fear, somehow, is bearable.

This is what Allah gave you at the end of this ayah. Not just the promise of provision. The promise of presence inside the worry about provision.

**What does this change about how you live tomorrow?**

When the fear rises — at the bank, at the kitchen table, at 3 a.m. — name it out loud to Allah, knowing He hears. *Ya Samī'.* He hears me. And if I cannot find the words, *Ya 'Alīm.* He knows me. You are not building a case for Him to consider. You are stepping back into the room with the One who has been there the whole time.

This is what the verse is teaching, in the end. Provision is real, and Allah handles it. But the deeper gift the verse hands you is not the provision itself — it is the *companionship* inside the worry. The discovery that the room you thought was empty was never empty.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Let me walk you back through the ayah, now that we have done the work, and show you what it has been doing.

It begins with **ka-ayyin** — astonishment. The fearful mind sees a small list of provision sources. The verse opens by widening the field beyond what you can count.

It moves to **dābbah** — the smallest creature. The fearful mind thinks its prestige is its security. The verse undermines this by feeding the creature with no prestige at all.

It says **lā taḥmilu rizqahā** — does not carry its provision. The fearful mind believes the carrying is the engine. The verse exposes the carrying as the appearance, not the cause.

It says **Allāhu yarzuquhā wa iyyākum** — provides for it, and you. The fearful mind feels singular in its struggle. The verse places you in the company of every creature that has ever been fed.

And it closes with **as-Samī' al-'Alīm** — Hearing, Knowing. The fearful mind suspects it is alone with its worry. The verse names two divine attributes that exist specifically to ensure that you are not.

The verse is not a piece of theology dropped into the surah. It is a precisely engineered psychological intervention for a person paralyzed at the threshold of hijrah. Every word does work on the specific shape of the fear that keeps people from moving.

### The Linguistic and the Thematic Are the Same Insight

What did the linguistic journey show us? That Allah, in a verse about rizq, declined to use the divine name for rizq — and instead used names of hearing and knowing.

What did the thematic journey show us? That financial fear is not, at its root, about money — it is about loneliness.

These are the same insight from two directions. The Arabic chose Samī' and 'Alīm because the human heart's real wound under financial fear is not the absence of bread — it is the suspicion of being unwitnessed. The linguistic choice and the psychological reality are one and the same. Allah wrote the verse with knowledge of what is actually broken in us.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the "city" you cannot leave because you cannot see how you will eat on the other side? Name it specifically. Is the wall really the money, or is it something else wearing the costume of money?

2. When you trace your fear of provision to its deepest root, is the root really about food and shelter — or is it about a suspicion that you are facing the world alone? Sit with this honestly.

3. Allah placed you after the ant in this verse. What does it feel like, emotionally, to be sequenced after the smallest creature? Does it humiliate you, or does it dissolve a knot you have been carrying for years?

4. The names of Allah you reach for in your du'a — are they shaped by your actual wound, or by what you assume the wound *should* be? When did you last call on Him as *as-Samī'* — the One who hears your specific fear?

5. If you genuinely believed, at the level of the body and not just the mind, that every worried thought you have ever had about money was heard by Allah at the moment you had it — how would tomorrow look different?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The ant carries nothing and is fed, and the Hand that feeds the ant is the same Hand whose ear is bent over the worry you have not yet spoken — so move.

### The Closing Invitation

This week, try one experiment. The next time the financial worry rises — at the bank, at the kitchen table, at 3 a.m. — do not rush past it. Do not pretend it is not there. Stop. Notice it. And say, even silently: *Ya Samī'. Ya 'Alīm. You hear this. You know this. I am not alone in this room.*

And then — and this is the harder part — *believe* that something just happened. Because something did. You stepped out of the lie of solitude and into the company of the One who fed the ant before you woke up this morning.

The hijrah you have been postponing is not always geographic. Sometimes it is internal. Sometimes the threshold you cannot cross is the threshold between *I am alone with this* and *I have never been alone with this*. The ayah is offering to walk you across.

### Du'a

Ya Samī', You who heard the trembling I have never spoken — let me trust today that nothing in my chest is hidden from You. Ya 'Alīm, You who know the fear before it becomes a word — feed me as You feed the smallest creature that does not carry its own bread. And carry me across the threshold I have been standing at, until I stand finally and only in the spaciousness of Your earth.

Āmīn.
