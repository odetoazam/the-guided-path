# The Inheritance That Outlives the Inheritor

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 27

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
[ayah:29:27] وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُۥٓ إِسْحَـٰقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ وَجَعَلْنَا فِى ذُرِّيَّتِهِ ٱلنُّبُوَّةَ وَٱلْكِتَـٰبَ وَءَاتَيْنَـٰهُ أَجْرَهُۥ فِى ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ وَإِنَّهُۥ فِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ لَمِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ

**Transliteration:**
Wa wahabnā lahu Isḥāqa wa Yaʿqūba wa jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi an-nubuwwata wal-kitāba wa ātaynāhu ajrahu fid-dunyā, wa innahu fil-ākhirati la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn.

**Translation:**
"And We gifted to him Isaac and Jacob, and We placed within his descendants prophethood and the Book. And We gave him his reward in this world, and indeed, in the Hereafter, he will be among the righteous."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Before we step into this ayah, we have to know where we are standing.

Surah Al-Ankabut takes its name from a spider. Not a lion. Not an eagle. A spider. And specifically, from a single image the surah will give us a few ayahs later: the spider's house is the frailest of houses — *awhanu al-buyūt*. A structure that looks like shelter and isn't. A wall you can put your hand through. A roof that won't hold a single drop of rain.

That image is not decoration. It is the lens through which this entire surah wants you to see the world. Everything people lean on, everything they build their lives around, everything they would never let go of because they think *this is what holds me up* — the surah keeps asking: what if it's a web? What if the thing you mistake for shelter is actually the most fragile thing in the room?

And right in the middle of this surah, Allah tells the story of Ibrahim — peace be upon him.

Ibrahim is the man who lived this question. His people had a web they called religion. His father had a web he called livelihood. His city had a web it called civilization. And Ibrahim — alone — walked through every one of them, watched them tear like cobweb under the weight of a single honest question: *what are these statues that you worship?*

Then they tried to burn him.

The ayah right before ours is the verse of his migration. *Fa-āmana lahu Lūṭ, wa qāla innī muhājirun ilā rabbī.* "Lot believed him. And he said: I am migrating to my Lord." Picture that. An old man. Walking away. From his land. From his family. From his father. From his city. From the only world he has ever known. And he doesn't even say *I am migrating to a new land.* He says *I am migrating to my Lord.* The destination isn't geographic. The destination is a relationship.

Now — here is the moment we have to sit with before we open ayah 27. **What does Ibrahim have, on the road, the day after he leaves?**

Nothing visible. No house. No tribe. No congregation. No son yet. No book yet. No descendants to speak of. No nation that knows his name. He has a wife, a nephew, and a Lord. That is the inventory.

And then, into that emptiness, Allah speaks ayah 27.

### The Hook

Here's what's strange. If you were going to reward a man who walked away from everything — what would you give him? Land? A throne? Recognition? A long life?

Allah gives him something else. And the *something else* is so quietly placed in this verse that you can read past it a thousand times without seeing what just happened.

[PAUSE]

In one short verse, Allah tells us what He gave Ibrahim in this world, and what He is saving for him in the next. And the gift He gives in this world is not the thing you would expect a man to be paid in. It is something a man cannot live to fully receive.

Stay with that. Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wahabnā* — The Verb of Pure Gift

Before we touch the word Allah uses, let's talk about gifts in general.

There is a difference — in any language, in any culture — between something you *earn* and something you are *given*. You earn a paycheck. You are given a birthday present. The paycheck has a logic — hours in, dollars out. The present has no logic — someone simply wanted to delight you.

Now in Arabic, when something is transferred from one person to another, the language has many verbs to describe it. *Aʿṭā* — to give, generically. *Manaḥa* — to grant, often with a sense of awarding. *Razaqa* — to provide, often in the context of sustenance. *Ātā* — to bring, to hand over.

And then there is one verb that sits in its own corner: *wahaba*.

*Wahaba* is the verb of pure gift. It is the verb that has no transactional residue at all. When you *wahaba* something to someone, you are not paying them, not awarding them, not even providing for them. You are simply *gifting* — for no reason except that you wanted to.

This is why the noun *al-Wahhāb* — the Bestower — is one of Allah's names. Not *al-Muʿṭī* in this specific shade, but *al-Wahhāb*. The One whose giving has no contract behind it.

Now look at the ayah.

> **wa wahabnā lahu Isḥāqa wa Yaʿqūba**
> "And We *gifted* to him Isaac and Jacob."

Notice something. Notice what verb Allah did *not* use.

He did not say *We gave him Isaac and Jacob.* He did not say *We provided him.* He did not say *We awarded him.* He said: **We gifted him.**

Let's sit with this.

Ibrahim is, at this point in his life, an old man. The Quran will tell us elsewhere — by the time Ishaq is announced, Ibrahim is laughing in disbelief at his own age. *A-alidu wa anā ʿajūz?* "Shall I bear a child, and I am an old woman?" Sarah says. Ibrahim is past the season of children. The biology is closed.

So when a child arrives — at that age, in that season, after that migration — Allah is signaling something with His verb choice. He is saying: **this is not what you earned by leaving your people. This is not the payment for your sacrifice. This is a gift.**

And here is what is remarkable. We tend to read sacrifice stories transactionally. *Ibrahim gave up everything, so Allah gave him children.* As if children are the salary for leaving home. But the verb refuses that reading. *Wahaba* doesn't take payment language. It takes love language.

Allah is saying: I didn't owe you Ishaq. I gifted you Ishaq. Your migration didn't purchase your son. Your son was already a present I had been holding for you.

[PAUSE]

There's a deeper layer. When you receive something as *wages*, you can be proud. You earned it. When you receive something as a *gift*, the only response is gratitude — because you didn't earn it. By using *wahaba*, Allah is gently keeping Ibrahim in the posture of receiver, even at the height of his reward. The man who walked away from everything is not, in this verse, being congratulated. He is being *given to*.

That changes the whole emotional register of the ayah. This is not a victory lap. This is a love letter.

---

### Element Two: *Jaʿalnā fī Dhurriyyatihi* — "We Placed Within His Descendants"

Now look at the second clause. Allah does something here with grammar that is easy to miss because the English softens it.

> **wa jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi an-nubuwwata wal-kitāba**
> "And We placed *within his descendants* prophethood and the Book."

Two things to teach before we open this.

First, the verb *jaʿala*. *Jaʿala* in Arabic is broader than *to make*. It carries a sense of *appointing, instituting, establishing, placing*. When Allah *jaʿala* the sun a lamp, He didn't manufacture it — He *appointed* it to that role. When He *jaʿala* the day for living and the night for rest, He instituted that order. *Jaʿala* is the verb of *putting something in its proper place in a larger design*.

Second, the word *dhurriyyah*. The root *dh-r-r* carries a sense of *scattering*, like fine seed scattered across a field. *Dhurriyyah* is "descendants" — but it's specifically the image of descendants as *scattered seed*, going outward across time, across geography. Not a single line, but a fan. Not a thread, but a spreading.

Now hear the clause again:

> **wa jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi an-nubuwwata wal-kitāba**

Allah didn't say: *We gave him prophethood and the Book.* He said: *We placed prophethood and the Book within his scattering-seed.*

Stay with what just happened.

Ibrahim, the man on the road, the man who has nothing visible — Allah is telling him about gifts he will never live to see opened. The Book that Allah is referring to is not in his hand. The prophets He is describing are not yet born. Mūsā, ʿĪsā, Yūsuf, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, our Prophet ﷺ — none of them are walking the earth yet. They are all *scattered seed* that has not yet sprouted.

And Allah is saying: I have already placed them. *Jaʿalnā.* Past tense. Already done.

Here's what is remarkable. The verb *jaʿala* with the preposition *fī* — "in" — has a specific texture. It's not "We gave to your descendants prophethood." It's "We *placed inside* your descendants prophethood." Like a seed buried in soil. Like a code written into a line. Like a promise that has been *built into* something rather than merely *handed to* it.

Ibrahim's body, by Allah's decree, is now the carrier of every future prophet and every future Book.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. Allah names the gifts as *an-nubuwwah* and *al-kitāb* — both with the definite article *al-*. Not *prophethood and a book*, but **the prophethood and the Book**. The definite article in Arabic gives the noun a kind of finality, a totality. As if to say: *every* prophethood that will come, *every* Book that will descend — all of it runs through this man you are looking at.

Think of what this means for Ibrahim emotionally.

He left his father. His father, the idol-carver, was so painful a wound that the Quran returns to it again and again. And here is Allah saying: *the line that you walked away from has cost you a father — and gained you, through your sons, every prophet who will ever live.* Mūsā is yours. ʿĪsā is yours. Muḥammad ﷺ is yours.

The man who lost a father became the father of fathers.

And the verb *jaʿalnā* — "We placed" — quietly says: *I did this. Not your wisdom. Not your fertility. Not your timing. I appointed it. The seed scatters because I scattered it.*

---

### Element Three: The Asymmetry of *Dunyā* and *Ākhirah*

Now here is where you have to read absence as carefully as presence.

Look at the structure of the final two clauses:

> **wa ātaynāhu ajrahu fī ad-dunyā**
> "And We gave him his reward in this world"

> **wa innahu fī al-ākhirati la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn**
> "And indeed, in the Hereafter, he will be among the righteous."

Two halves. *Dunyā* and *ākhirah*. This life and the next. Allah balances them like the two pans of a scale.

But look more carefully. The two halves are not parallel.

In the *dunyā* half, Allah uses the verb *ātaynāhu* — "We *gave* him." Active. Concrete. Past tense. A thing handed over. "His reward."

In the *ākhirah* half, Allah does *not* use a verb of giving. He uses a verb of *being*. *Innahu... la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn* — "he is, indeed, among the righteous." Not "We will give him a reward in the Hereafter." But: **He is, in the Hereafter, among the righteous.**

This is the asymmetry. And it is everything.

Let me teach a little before we open this.

In Arabic, there is a category of grammatical construction called the *nominal sentence* — *jumlah ismiyyah*. When you want to say "Zayd stood up," you can say it two ways. *Qāma Zaydun* — verb-first, describing an action in time. Or *Zaydun qāʾimun* — noun-first, describing a *state*, a quality, an essential characteristic. The verbal sentence says *he did*. The nominal sentence says *he is*.

The second half of our ayah is a nominal sentence, and it is reinforced with the particle *inna* — "indeed, truly, certainly" — and the *lām* of emphasis on *la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn*. Three layers of certainty stacked on each other. *Inna* + *la-* + nominal sentence. As if Allah is saying: *let there be no doubt about this. This is fixed.*

Now ask the question: why this asymmetry?

[PAUSE]

Here's what is being said.

In the *dunyā*, what Allah gives Ibrahim is *ajr* — a reward, a wage, a recompense. A *thing*. A package. Something handed over. Sons. Lineage. Memory. The fact that every Abrahamic faith on earth — half the species — still says his name. *That* is the *ajr* in this world. A reward you can point at.

But in the *ākhirah*, Allah doesn't say He will give Ibrahim a reward. He says Ibrahim **is**, in the next life, **among the righteous**.

Notice — being among the righteous is not a *thing*. It is a *station*. It is a category of being. It is *who you are*, not *what you got*.

In other words: in this world, Allah gives Ibrahim things. In the next world, Allah gives Ibrahim *himself, purified and confirmed.* The reward in the *dunyā* is a possession. The reward in the *ākhirah* is an *identity*.

And there is one more layer to this absence.

Look at how Ibrahim originally asked for this. Go to surah 26 — *al-Shuʿarāʾ*. Ibrahim makes a duʿāʾ: *Rabbi hab lī ḥukman wa-alḥiqnī biṣ-ṣāliḥīn* — "My Lord, gift me wisdom, and *join me to the righteous*." He used the same verb — *hab*, the imperative of *wahaba*. *Gift me*. And the same word — *ṣāliḥīn*. *The righteous*.

He asked to be joined to the righteous. Years and years later, Allah, in our ayah, *confirms it*. *Innahu fī al-ākhirati la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn.* "He is — there is no doubt — among the righteous."

The duʿāʾ was answered. But notice the time signature. Ibrahim asked in the *dunyā*; Allah confirms in the *ākhirah*. Some answers to your duʿāʾ arrive in this life. Some answers to your duʿāʾ are sealed in the next. And both are equally answered.

[PAUSE]

So the asymmetry — *ātaynāhu* in dunyā, *innahu... la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn* in ākhirah — quietly teaches us how to read our own lives. What Allah hands you here is partial — a payment on account. What He establishes you as *there* is total — your final, settled name in the registry of the universe.

---

### Bridge

So we have walked through three linguistic moments. *Wahabnā* — the verb of pure gift, refusing to make Ibrahim's reward a transaction. *Jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi* — the placing, the planting, the burial of every future prophet inside one walking man. And the asymmetry of *dunyā* and *ākhirah* — *things* given in this life, *being* confirmed in the next.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what kind of universe we are living in, when we walk away from something we love for the sake of Allah.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Reward You Cannot Live to Receive

Let me start somewhere ordinary.

Anyone who has planted a tree knows a particular feeling. You dig the hole. You set the sapling. You water it. And you know — quietly, in the back of your mind — that the tree you are planting is not for you. By the time it gives real shade, you may be old. By the time it bears full fruit, you may be gone. Your hands are doing labor today whose harvest will arrive after you. You are working *past your own life*.

There is a saying — different cultures have versions of it — that a society grows great when old men plant trees in whose shade they will never sit.

Hold that image. Now come back to Ibrahim.

Ibrahim is being told, in our ayah, that his reward in the *dunyā* is not what people normally think of as a reward. He is not getting more lifespan. He is not getting wealth. He is not getting recognition from his contemporaries — most of them already hate him, and most of them will die hating him. What he is getting is a *lineage carrying prophethood and the Book*.

But here's the thing. **Ibrahim won't be alive for most of it.**

He will live to see Ishaq. He will live to know Yaʿqūb. But Yūsuf? Yūsuf is his great-grandson — Ibrahim's biological window mostly closes before Yūsuf's story even begins. Mūsā? Mūsā is centuries downstream. ʿĪsā? Two millennia downstream. Muḥammad ﷺ? Two and a half thousand years from Ibrahim's lifetime.

And Allah is calling all of this — every prophet, every book, every nation that will trace itself to this man — *ajrahu fī ad-dunyā*. **His reward, in this world.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. This is one of the deepest moves in the entire Quran's vocabulary of reward.

Allah is saying that what happens *in this world* after you are gone is still *in this world.* Your *dunyā* doesn't end when you die. Your *dunyā* extends through the consequences of your existence that keep rippling through the world after your body is in the ground. The tree you planted? When it gives shade in 2080 — that shade is still part of *your* reward in this *dunyā*. The child you raised who raised her children who raised their children to know Allah — that is *your dunyā wage*, still being paid out, long after your funeral.

We have a very narrow definition of "my life." We think *my life* equals *the years my heart beats.* But the Quran has a wider definition. **Your life is the wake your boat leaves on the water.** The boat sinks. The wake travels on.

This changes how to think about almost everything.

It changes how to think about parenting. The version of yourself your great-grandchildren meet — through the values you embed in your children, who embed them in theirs — is *you, still living in this world*. You will be paid the reward of that life. In *this* world.

It changes how to think about teaching. The student whose mind you opened, who opens another mind, who opens another — that chain is your earthly reward, long after you have stopped breathing.

It changes how to think about *daʿwah*, about quiet correction, about lending someone a book, about answering a question patiently when you didn't have to. *Ajrahu fī ad-dunyā* — your reward in the world. The world that keeps spinning after your name leaves the rolls.

And the practical question this ayah leaves us with is uncomfortable: **what am I planting that I won't live to harvest?**

If the answer is *nothing* — if everything I'm building is sized for my own lifetime — then the verse is gently reminding me that I am building smaller than Allah is willing to fund. Allah funds at *centuries*. Allah funds at *millennia*. He funded Ibrahim at twenty-five hundred years. He is willing to fund you at three generations, at five, at ten. But you have to plant something whose harvest doesn't fit in your calendar.

The practical takeaway: **One thing you do this week, do for the year 2120.** Not for now. Not for next quarter. Not for the version of you that wants to see it work. For a great-grandchild's great-grandchild. A line in a will. A book chosen carefully. A habit of speech with your child that they will pass to their child. Something whose *ajr* is paid in a generation you will not meet, and which is *still, the Quran tells us, your reward in this dunyā*.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Inheritance That Survives the Fire

The second theme begins with a question. **What can a fire not burn?**

Ibrahim, just before the migration, was thrown into a fire. The Quran tells us this directly. *Qulnā yā nāru kūnī bardan wa-salāman ʿalā Ibrāhīm* — "We said, O fire, be coolness and peace upon Ibrahim." The fire that his people built to consume him was rendered cold by a word. He walked out of it whole.

But here is the question that the fire-story sets up, even if we don't always notice. **The fire was supposed to end Ibrahim. What were they actually trying to burn?**

Not just his body. They were trying to burn his message. They were trying to burn the *line*. The line of pure tawḥīd that started with him and that, if extinguished, would have died with him. The fire was the world's attempt to *delete the future of monotheism* by deleting one man.

And the fire failed. Cold. Peace.

But here is what's stunning. The miraculous fire-extinguishing is not the answer the Quran gives to *what survives the fire*. The miracle in surah al-Anbiyāʾ is the *body*. Our ayah gives the deeper miracle: even if they had succeeded at the body, **they could not have burned what Allah had placed.**

> *wa jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi an-nubuwwata wal-kitāba*

Read this with fire in your mind.

Fire burns what it can touch. A man's skin. His house. His books. His city. Even the Kaʿbah, were it possible, could be reduced to ash. Fire is the great negator of physical things. Anything material, anything visible, anything you can put your hands on — fire can argue with all of it.

**But fire cannot burn what Allah has placed.**

The Book had not yet descended. The prophets had not yet been born. The prophethood was not yet a scroll or a recitation or a community. It was a *placement* — an embedded promise — in the lineage of a man on a road. Fire has no jurisdiction over *placement*. You cannot burn a decree.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this opens for us.

Every one of us has fires in our life. Not the literal kind. The kind that come for our work, our reputation, our plans, our families. Illnesses, layoffs, betrayals, accidents, slanders. The Surah Al-Ankabut name is telling us that everything that *looks* like shelter — career, finances, even health — is more web than wall. *Awhanu al-buyūt.* Fire goes through it.

So what's the response? Resignation?

No. The response is to ask: *what part of my life is in the category of placement, not possession?*

Possession is what you have. Placement is what Allah has put through you. Possession can be burned. Placement cannot be.

The job is possession. The integrity with which you did the job — placed in the people who watched you, who learned from you — is placement. The job can be lost. The integrity that scattered into other lives cannot.

The wealth is possession. The generosity that flowed through it — embedded in the people who received it, in the duʿāʾ they made for you — is placement. The wealth can vanish. The duʿāʾ cannot be retracted.

The body is possession. The act of worship the body offered — placed eternally in the record — is placement. The body will die. The sajdah cannot be unsajdaed.

This is the whole logic of *dhurriyyah*. The *seed* of the meaning you carry, scattered into other lives, into other moments, into Allah's record. Fire-proof. Time-proof. Loss-proof.

Now here is the demand of this theme.

If you knew — really knew — that placement survives and possession burns, **how would you spend the next week?**

Most of us, if we are honest, would shift our energies. We would invest less in possession and more in placement. We would tell our children the truth more often instead of just the convenient thing. We would do the obscure ʿibādah no one sees. We would teach the thing we know to someone, without payment, because *teaching is placement*, and *placement does not burn*. We would forgive the person we have been refusing to forgive, because *forgiveness is placement*, and *the resentment we are carrying is possession that will burn anyway, painfully, with us*.

The practical takeaway: **Identify one thing in your life that is pure possession — one thing you are protecting that the fire will eventually take regardless — and decide, today, to convert some of its energy into placement.** Take one hour you would spend defending possession, and spend it instead seeding something into another soul. Allah has a category for that. He calls it *ajr fid-dunyā*. And He calls the man who lived that way *min aṣ-ṣāliḥīn*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the verse as a whole. Notice the structure.

> *wa wahabnā lahu Isḥāqa wa Yaʿqūba*
> *wa jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi an-nubuwwata wal-kitāba*
> *wa ātaynāhu ajrahu fī ad-dunyā*
> *wa innahu fī al-ākhirati la-mina aṣ-ṣāliḥīn*

Four clauses. And they are arranged in widening concentric circles.

Circle one: *Ishaq and Yaʿqūb.* Two men. Two specific sons. The smallest circle — Ibrahim's immediate family.

Circle two: *His descendants, prophethood, the Book.* A larger circle — generations he will never meet, books that will descend on continents he will never see.

Circle three: *His reward in this world.* Larger still — the whole *dunyā*, the whole arc of human time on this earth.

Circle four: *Among the righteous in the ākhirah.* The largest circle — eternity itself, the final assembly of all the pure souls.

The ayah is a series of expanding rings. Each ring is bigger than the last. And Ibrahim — the man who walked away from his land with nothing — is at the center of all of them.

This is what migration *to Allah* does. The man walks alone, and the universe arranges itself around him in widening circles of meaning that culminate in eternity.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Wahabnā* told us this is a gift, not a wage — and the first theme showed us why: because *ajr fid-dunyā* extends past your lifetime, and you cannot be said to have *earned* something whose payment continues two thousand years after your funeral. Only a gift behaves that way.

*Jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihi* told us that prophethood is *placed*, not merely *handed* — and the second theme showed us why: because *placement* is the only category of reality that fire cannot touch. The whole surah is a teaching that the visible is web. This verse is the answer: the invisible, the *placed*, is wall.

The asymmetry of *ātaynāhu* in dunyā and *innahu* in ākhirah told us that this life pays in things and the next life pays in being — and both themes pointed at the same destination: that the reward of a life walked toward Allah is not *what you collect*. It is *what becomes of you*.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life is possession, and what is placement? Which one am I spending most of my energy on?

2. If *ajr fid-dunyā* extends past my lifetime, what am I willing to start now whose harvest I will never see?

3. Ibrahim was promised lineage, prophets, and a Book — none of which arrived in his lifetime as he was promised them. Am I willing to be paid in promises that won't be itemized until centuries after my death?

4. The verse uses *wahaba* — pure gift, no transaction. When something good comes into my life, do I receive it like a wage I deserved, or like a gift I did not earn?

5. Ibrahim left his father, his city, and was thrown into a fire — and Allah, in one verse, made him the father of every prophet to come. What am I being asked to leave, and what *placement* is Allah preparing on the other side of that leaving?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**When you walk away from a web for the sake of Allah, He plants in your line what fire cannot burn, and pays you in this world with a reward that outlives your lifetime and in the next with a name that does not change.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

You will not be Ibrahim. You don't have to be. But you will be asked, again and again, in small and large ways, to choose between possession and placement. Between a wage and a gift. Between a web and a wall.

The invitation of this ayah is not to leave a city. It is to start treating the consequences that ripple out of your life — your children, your teaching, your kindness, your patience under provocation — as the *real wages* Allah is paying you in this world. To stop measuring success by what you can see in your hands, and start measuring it by what is being *placed* — quietly, secretly, by Allah — through your life into other lives, into other centuries, into the unseen ledger that fire cannot reach.

If something shifts after this verse, let it be this: **plant one thing this week that you will not live to harvest.** And trust the verse — that *that* is the reward Allah names *fī ad-dunyā*.

---

### Du'ā

O Allah — You who gifted Ibrahim what he did not ask for at the age he could not expect it, place in our scattered seed what fire cannot burn. Let our reward in this world be the lives our lives shape after we are gone, and let our name in the next world be among Your righteous — fixed, settled, with no doubt and no asterisk. Āmīn.
