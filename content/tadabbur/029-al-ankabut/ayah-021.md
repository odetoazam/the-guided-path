# The Mercy That Chooses and the Punishment That Chooses

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 21

---

### The Verse

**Arabic:**

> [ayah:29:21] يُعَذِّبُ مَن يَشَآءُ وَيَرْحَمُ مَن يَشَآءُ ۖ وَإِلَيْهِ تُقْلَبُونَ

**Transliteration:**

> Yuʿadhdhibu man yashāʾu wa yarḥamu man yashāʾu wa ilayhi tuqlabūn.

**Translation:**

> He punishes whom He wills and shows mercy to whom He wills, and to Him you will be turned back.

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we step into these eight words, we have to walk back into the room where they were first spoken. Not the room where Muhammad ﷺ recited them in Makkah — that is the second room. The first room is older. The first room is the courtyard of an idol-shop in ancient Mesopotamia, and the speaker is a young man named Ibrahim.

You have to picture this. A community has built its life around statues. Statues their fathers carved. Statues their grandfathers carved. Statues that have become the load-bearing walls of their economy, their politics, their family identity. To question the statues is not to question religion. It is to question *everything* — your inheritance, your livelihood, your tribe, your father's honor.

And into this room walks a young man and he says: **these things you worship cannot help you and cannot harm you.**

The community does what communities always do when their foundation is touched. They reach for fire. They light a pyre. They throw him in.

And then — and this is the moment Surah Al-Ankabut wants you to feel — *nothing happens to him*. The fire becomes cool. The web that everyone trusted to hold them up has snapped. The fire that everyone trusted to silence dissent has refused.

And now Ibrahim stands in front of them again. Not in triumph. In *clarity*. And he begins to speak. He tells them how creation began. He tells them how it will be repeated. He tells them that the journey of every soul ends in one place. And then he says these eight words.

**Yuʿadhdhibu man yashāʾu wa yarḥamu man yashāʾu wa ilayhi tuqlabūn.**

He punishes whom He wills. He shows mercy to whom He wills. And to Him you will be *turned back*.

Now, the hook. We're going to spend an hour with this verse, and I want you to know what we are walking toward. There is a word at the end of this ayah that should not be there. Arabic has a perfectly good word for "return." It's used everywhere in the Quran. *Tarjiʿūn*. *You will be returned.* It would have fit here perfectly. And Allah didn't use it. He used a different word. A word that doesn't mean "return" at all. It means something stranger. Something that, once you see it, will change how you read every verse about the afterlife you've ever encountered.

And before we get to that word, there is the opening — a sentence built like a set of scales, perfectly balanced, mercy on one side, punishment on the other. And the question that scale forces on you is the most uncomfortable question a believing heart can ask.

Let's begin.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Doesn't Take an Object — *Yashāʾu*

Before we look at what's in this verse, I want to teach you to listen for what's *missing* from it. Because Arabic, like every language, has a grammar of expectation. When you hear a verb, your ear starts waiting for the thing the verb is *about*. If I say "He hit," your mind is already leaning forward — hit *what*? If I say "She decided," your mind is already asking — decided *to do what*?

This is how grammar holds you. It creates a forward pull, a small unfinished space, and then it fills the space.

Now look at this verse. *Yuʿadhdhibu man yashāʾu* — He punishes whom He *wills*. *Wa yarḥamu man yashāʾu* — and He shows mercy to whom He *wills*.

Twice in this verse, the word *yashāʾu* appears — "He wills." And both times, it stops there. Allah does not tell us *what* He wills. He does not say "whom He wills to punish." He does not say "whom He wills to forgive." He just says — **whom He wills**.

[PAUSE]

In Arabic, this is called a *muṭlaq* construction — an absolute, unrestricted statement. The verb *shāʾa* (to will) here is left without an object. Classical grammarians and exegetes — Ibn ʿAtiyyah, al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī — all notice this. They say: Allah deliberately suspended the object so that the will would not be domesticated.

Let me try to show you what that means.

Imagine a king who says: "I pardon whom I wish to pardon for stealing bread." You can map his will. You know its boundary. You know its rule. You walk away thinking: *if I steal bread, I might be pardoned.* His will has been made small enough to fit inside your reasoning.

Now imagine a king who says: "I pardon whom I wish."

That sentence does not fit inside your reasoning. It is wider than your mind. You cannot trade with it. You cannot earn it the way you'd earn a contract. You cannot calculate it. All you can do is **stand before it**.

This is what Allah is doing here. He is refusing to let His mercy be reduced to a transaction. He is refusing to let His punishment be reduced to a formula. The classical mufassirūn say: *yashāʾu* without an object means — His will is governed by His wisdom (*ḥikmah*) and His knowledge (*ʿilm*), not by anything you can leverage.

This is not arbitrariness. This is the opposite of arbitrariness. Arbitrariness would be a will tied to a coin flip. This is a will tied to a wisdom so deep that no rule you could write would be wide enough to express it.

Notice something. The believer's heart has two diseases that this construction cuts at the same time. The first disease is **despair** — the heart that says, "I've done too much; mercy cannot reach me." The second disease is **entitlement** — the heart that says, "I've done enough; punishment cannot touch me." Both of those diseases are built on the same false foundation: that we know the rules by which Allah's will operates. *Yashāʾu* — unrestricted — pulls the foundation out from under both diseases at once.

The heart that hears *yashāʾu* correctly does not know whether it will be punished. It does not know whether it will be shown mercy. So it does the only thing left to do. It *asks*. It begs. It returns.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. This verb appears twice in the same ayah, in the same form, with the same absent object. Allah did not say it once and let mercy and punishment share it. He said it twice. He gave mercy its own sovereign *yashāʾu*, and He gave punishment its own sovereign *yashāʾu*. As though to say: neither of these is a leftover. Neither of these is the absence of the other. Both are choices. Both are willed. Both are precise.

And there is one more thing. Order. *Yuʿadhdhibu* comes first; *yarḥamu* comes second. In Arabic rhetoric, when two things are paired, the final position often carries the weight — it is the note the ear is left holding. Allah did not end this clause on the threat. He ended it on the mercy. The last word in your ear before the conjunction is *yashāʾu* — He shows mercy to whom He wills.

What this single verbal construction reveals: **the believer's posture before God is not calculation, it is supplication — because His will is wider than any rule you could write to negotiate with it.**

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: *Man Yashāʾu* — The Unspecified Recipient

Now I want to stay inside the same phrase, because the grammar is doing a second piece of work that is easy to miss.

Look at the word *man* — "whom." In Arabic, *man* is a relative pronoun that refers to persons, and like its English cousin "whomever," it is deliberately vague. It does not name. It does not point. It does not specify a group, a tribe, a category, a class. It just opens a door.

Compare two ways Allah could have phrased this verse.

He could have said: "He punishes the disbelievers and shows mercy to the believers." That sentence would have been theologically correct, and it would have given every listener a category to step into. The believer would have heard it and exhaled. The disbeliever would have heard it and either dismissed it or panicked.

But that is not what He said.

He said: *yuʿadhdhibu **man** yashāʾu wa yarḥamu **man** yashāʾu*. **Whomever** He wills. The category is gone. The label is gone. The membership card you were going to flash at the door is gone.

Let's sit with this. Because at first glance it feels destabilizing. If Allah does not say "the believers will receive mercy," what is left for the believer to stand on?

Here is what classical exegetes draw out of this. The Quran teaches us elsewhere — explicitly and abundantly — that *mercy is the default and the dominant attribute of Allah's relationship with His creation*. "My mercy encompasses all things" (Al-Aʿrāf 7:156). "He has decreed mercy upon Himself" (Al-Anʿām 6:12). So when Allah says here, "He shows mercy to whom He wills," He is not retracting any of those promises. He is preserving His sovereignty over them.

The point of *man* in this verse is not to introduce doubt. The point of *man* is to **dismantle the membership-card mentality**.

Stay with me on this. There is a kind of religiosity — and every tradition has it — that treats salvation like a club. You get the card. You flash the card at the gate. You're in. And the moment you have the card, you stop *seeking*. You stop *begging*. You stop *fearing*. Because the card is doing the work for you.

Allah, in this verse, refuses to let your card do the work for you. He says: **whomever**. The category does not save. The label does not save. Only His will saves, and His will is tied to His wisdom and His knowledge of what is actually in your heart — not what's on your card.

This is what scholars mean when they say a believer must live between *khawf wa rajāʾ* — fear and hope — wings of a bird that can only fly if both are intact. *Man yashāʾu* is the verbal architecture of those two wings. Hope, because His mercy is wider than any category you fear you're outside of. Fear, because His punishment is more precise than any category you assume you're inside of.

And here's what's even more striking. The verse is being spoken by Ibrahim — to his own people. To people who, on every external label, are *not* recipients of mercy. They are idolaters. They just tried to burn him alive. And what is Ibrahim's last word to them before he turns away? Not "you will be punished." Not "you are the disbelievers." But *whomever He wills*. Even *you*, even now, are inside the *man*. The door is not closed. The will of God is not a wall you have already crashed into. It is wider than your last action.

[PAUSE]

What this second feature reveals: **the unnamed recipient is the open door. The category does not save and the category does not damn — only the will of the One Who sees what is actually inside you.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Tuqlabūn* — The Word That Doesn't Mean Return

Now we arrive at the word I promised you at the beginning.

The verse closes: *wa ilayhi tuqlabūn* — "and to Him you will be... " — and here we need to slow down, because almost every English translation gives you "returned." That translation is not wrong. It is incomplete. And the incompleteness costs you the verse.

Let me teach you the concept first.

In Arabic, "to return" is *rajaʿa*. Its passive — *turjaʿūn* — "you will be returned" — appears many times in the Quran. It is the standard verb for the journey back to Allah. *Wa ilā Allāhi turjaʿu al-umūr* — "and to Allah all matters are returned." Clean. Linear. Point A to Point B.

But Allah did not use that verb here. He used *qalaba*. And *qalaba* does not mean "return." It means **to flip, to overturn, to invert, to turn something inside out.** The root *q-l-b* is the same root that gives us *qalb* — the heart. And why is the heart called *qalb*? Because in classical Arabic morphology, the heart is the thing that **turns**. It flips between states. It does not stay still. It is named for its inconstancy.

Now look at the verse again. *Wa ilayhi **tuqlabūn**.* And to Him you will be **flipped**. Inverted. Turned over.

[PAUSE]

This is not the same image as *return*. Return is geographical — you came from somewhere, you go back to it. *Qalb* is structural — you were oriented one way, and now you will be *re-oriented*. Everything that was facing outward will face inward. Everything that was hidden will be exposed. Everything that was performed will be stripped. Everything that was buried in the chest will be brought to the surface.

The classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib al-Aṣfahānī in his *Mufradāt*, and the entry on q-l-b in *Lisān al-ʿArab* — explain that *qalaba* is used for the turning of a coin, for the flipping of a soil-plow that brings the bottom layer to the top, for the rotation of something from one face to the other. It is not the verb of a traveler going home. It is the verb of an *unveiling*.

Now hold that beside the earlier word — *qalb*, the heart, named because it *turns*. In this life, your heart turns toward things. It turns toward what you love, what you fear, what you crave, what you avoid. Every day, all day, your heart is in motion, and the things it turns toward are the truest map of who you are.

What this verse is saying — and the word *tuqlabūn* is the key that opens it — is that **on the Last Day, Allah will not just bring you back. He will turn you inside out**. The orientation of your heart — the thing you spent your whole life choosing to turn toward — will be made visible. The inside will become the outside. The hidden direction will become the public reality.

This is why Allah used *yuʿadhdhibu* and *yarḥamu* in the same breath as *tuqlabūn*. Mercy and punishment will not be assigned to an external label. They will be assigned to a *qalb that has been turned over and shown for what it actually was*.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable, and where it gets personal. The classical mufassirūn — Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī — note that *tuqlabūn* is in the passive voice. *You will be flipped.* Not *you will flip yourselves.* The flipping is not in your hands. You do not get to choose, on that day, which face is shown. The exposure is sovereign. The unveiling is not negotiable.

In this life, you can curate. You can choose which side of yourself you show to the world. You can post the good moments and hide the rest. You can perform piety and conceal cruelty, or — for those of us with the opposite problem — you can perform indifference and conceal a heart that is secretly tender. We all have a public face and a hidden one.

*Tuqlabūn* is the promise that the curation ends.

And notice the cosmic asymmetry. The opening of the verse names two destinies — punishment and mercy. The closing of the verse names *one* event — the flipping. Why? Because the flipping itself **is** the moment when the two destinies become visible. Mercy is not assigned to a category. Mercy is assigned to a *heart that, when turned over, was found to have been turning toward Allah all along*. Punishment is assigned to a *heart that, when turned over, was found to have been turning away*. The destiny was being chosen, drop by drop, in the small turnings of every ordinary day.

[PAUSE]

What this third feature reveals: **the Day of Judgment is not primarily a place you arrive at. It is a moment in which what you have already become is made visible. The judgment is the unveiling of a verdict you have been writing your whole life.**

[PAUSE]

---

### Bridge

Three linguistic features. *Yashāʾu* without an object — His will cannot be domesticated. *Man* without specification — His category cannot be claimed by a card. *Tuqlabūn* instead of *turjaʿūn* — the destination is not a place but an exposure.

Together, they make a single shape. They say: **the believer cannot be passive, cannot be entitled, and cannot perform. The believer can only become, in private and in small turnings, the kind of heart that, when flipped, faces the right direction.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of the Membership Card

I want to begin somewhere familiar.

You have, in your life, walked into a room holding a card. Maybe it was a literal card — a passport at a border, an ID at a club door, an invitation at a wedding. Or maybe it was metaphorical — a credential, a degree, a family name, a job title, a follower count. Whatever the card was, it did the same thing every card does: **it made you feel safe in advance.** You hadn't done anything yet. You hadn't proven anything in that room. But the card had already proven you. The card had already decided that you belonged.

Now I want you to notice something about the psychology of holding a card. The longer you hold it, the less alert you become. The card replaces vigilance. Why pay attention to whether you actually belong if the card has already settled the question? Why work on the qualities the card was supposed to certify if the card itself is the qualification?

This is the spiritual condition that Ibrahim is speaking into in this verse. Not just for his people — for *us*. Because every religious community develops cards. The card of being born into the faith. The card of having performed the public ritual. The card of having memorized the right texts. The card of being part of the right group, the right *madhhab*, the right mosque, the right circle, the right lineage of teachers.

And the cards are not necessarily false. The rituals matter. The community matters. The lineage matters. *But the card cannot do the work of the heart.*

When Allah says *yarḥamu man yashāʾu* — He shows mercy to whom He wills — He is not destabilizing your hope. He is destabilizing your **card**. He is saying: the card is not what I see. I see the *man* — the whomever — that lives underneath the card. I see who you are when no one is looking and no rule is being enforced.

Let me give you a specific picture. Imagine two people praying side by side. One has been Muslim his whole life, prays five times a day, can recite long surahs from memory. He is praying with his body but his mind is on a grievance he won't release, on a person he won't forgive, on a transaction he plans to make tomorrow that is just a little dishonest but he has rationalized it. The other is a new convert. She still stumbles over the words. Her *qiyām* is awkward. But in her chest, in this prayer, there is a tenderness so raw that she is on the verge of tears the entire time, and her one continuous thought, beneath the broken Arabic, is: *Ya Allah, I am Yours, please don't lose me*.

On every external metric — the card metric — the first person is more religious. He has more knowledge, more years, more fluency, more visible piety.

And what *tuqlabūn* tells you is: on the day of flipping, those two hearts will be turned over. And what will be revealed is what was actually inside. *Man yashāʾu* is the unspecified door through which the second heart can walk and through which the first heart can be left outside.

This is not a comfortable thought for those of us with cards. It is not supposed to be.

But notice the mercy hidden inside the discomfort. The same teaching that strips the entitled person of their false safety **gives the broken person their real hope**. If salvation were a card, the person without the card would have no chance. But salvation is not a card. Salvation is a *qalb that turned toward Allah*. And that is something that the most discredited, most disqualified, most marginal person can still possess. The door is not credentialed. The door is *whomever*.

The practical application is small and difficult. It is this: today, you check the orientation of your heart, not the status of your card. You ask, in the privacy where no one is watching: *what is my heart turning toward, right now, in this small choice?* Not in the big public choices. In the small invisible ones. Because the small invisible ones are what *tuqlabūn* will expose.

### Theme Two: The Day the Inside Becomes the Outside

I want to try an experiment with you.

Picture a soil-plow turning a field. You've seen this image, even if only in a documentary. The plow goes through, and the dirt that was on top — the dirt that was exposed to the sun, the rain, the wind, every passing animal — gets pushed down. And the dirt that was *underneath* — dark, unseen, untouched — gets brought up. The field is *inverted*. What was hidden is now in the light. What was on display is now buried.

This is the image inside *tuqlabūn*. The classical Arab listener, hearing this verb, would have seen the plow. They would have seen the soil flipped. They would have understood: the Day of Judgment is not a courtroom in the modern sense. It is a *field being plowed*. The hidden layer of every human heart is going to be brought up into the light.

Now ask yourself the most uncomfortable question that follows from that image. *What is in the hidden layer of my heart right now?*

Don't answer fast. The fast answer is curated. The fast answer is the one you'd give if someone asked you publicly. The fast answer is the *top* layer of the soil — the one already exposed to the wind and the sun, already shaped by what you want people to see.

The question *tuqlabūn* puts to you is about the *under-layer*. The thoughts you don't say. The resentments you don't name. The fantasies you don't admit. The small cruelties you've justified. The kindnesses you wouldn't dare claim publicly. The love for Allah that you keep hidden because you're embarrassed to be seen as too religious. The doubt you keep hidden because you're afraid to be seen as not religious enough.

All of it — every layer of soil under the visible one — will be turned up.

Now, here is the part of this that I want you to receive carefully, because it can go in two directions, and only one of them is the right one.

The wrong direction is *despair*. To hear *tuqlabūn* and to think: *if everything in me will be exposed, I am lost*. This is the response that the *Shayṭān* whispers, because despair is one of his most effective tools. He uses it to convince you that the under-layer of your heart is so bad that no plow should ever be allowed to turn it.

The right direction is *honesty in the present*. To hear *tuqlabūn* and to think: *if everything will be exposed then, let me be honest about it now, while there is still time to till my own soil*. To do the inner work that the Prophet ﷺ called *muḥāsabah* — accounting of the self. Not the public accounting of the card. The private accounting of the *qalb*. To go down into the layer that will be flipped, and to start, today, gently, to tend it.

Because here is the second, more beautiful thing the plow image teaches you. **The plow does not just expose. It prepares.** A field is plowed *so that something new can be planted*. The flipping is not the end of the story. The flipping is the moment when a heart that has been tilled, even imperfectly, even slowly, even painfully, is finally able to receive what Allah wants to plant in it.

For the heart that has been hardening itself for a lifetime — that has never been turned, never been broken open, never been honest with itself — the flipping is exposure without preparation. The under-layer is brought up, and there is nothing in it to plant.

But for the heart that has been turned, in private, in the dark, by its own hand, again and again, through *istighfār*, through *tawbah*, through honest prayer, through the small daily reorientations toward Allah — for that heart, the flipping on the Day of Judgment is not a shock. It is a recognition. The under-layer that gets brought up is a layer that has *already been tended*. The plow does not bring up rot. It brings up a soil that has been quietly, secretly, prepared to grow something.

And what grows there is mercy. *Yarḥamu man yashāʾu.* He shows mercy to the heart whose hidden layer was, all along, soft.

The practical application here is more specific than the first. It is this: build, somewhere in your day, a moment of *honest interior turning*. Not a long one. Not a performed one. A small one. A moment in which you say to Allah, quietly, what you would never say in front of another human being. The most unflattering version of yourself. The version you most want to hide. Bring it into the light *now*, when bringing it is still your choice, so that on the day it is brought without your choice, it has already been touched by His mercy.

This is what scholars call *tilling the qalb*. And it is, in the end, the only preparation for the day of *tuqlabūn* that the verse itself recommends.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what the three linguistic features and the two themes have done together.

The first feature — *yashāʾu* without an object — said: Allah's will is wider than any rule you can write. The first theme said the same thing from the human side: the card cannot do the work. These are the same insight from two directions.

The second feature — *man* without specification — said: the door is whomever. The second theme said the same thing from the experiential side: the under-layer of any heart can be tended. Same insight. Two directions.

And the third feature — *tuqlabūn* — gave the architecture for both. Because the reason the will cannot be domesticated and the category cannot be claimed is that the *judgment is not on the surface but on the flipped under-layer*. Allah does not judge the card. He judges what is exposed when the card is taken away and the soil is turned.

The whole verse is a single sentence that says: **stop performing, start preparing.**

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

1. What is on the *under-layer* of my heart that I have never honestly named, even to myself? What would be brought up if the soil of me were turned today?

2. Which card am I most tempted to flash at the gate of mercy — and what would be left of my relationship with Allah if that card were taken from me right now?

3. When I read *yashāʾu* — He wills — does my chest tighten with fear, or open with hope? And what does the tightening or the opening tell me about which of the two diseases is closer to my heart at the moment?

4. Where in my day is there a moment of *honest interior turning* — and if there isn't one, where could I build it, this week, without anyone knowing it exists?

5. Ibrahim spoke these eight words to people who had just tried to kill him. If they could still be inside the *man*, who in my life have I already declared to be outside it? And what does that declaration say about my own card?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The Day of Judgment is not the day you arrive somewhere — it is the day you are turned inside out before a will too wide to be domesticated and too precise to be performed; so the only preparation is to turn yourself, gently and honestly, toward Him now, in the layers no one else can see.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to walk away from this verse with a new project. You need to walk away with one slightly tilled corner of yourself. One small thing you have been hiding from yourself that you bring, today, into a moment of private speech with Allah. Not to fix it. Just to bring it. *Ya Allah, this is in me, and You see it, and I am turning it over to You before the day it is turned over for me.*

That is the whole invitation. That is the whole practice.

---

### Du'a

Ya Allah — You who punish whom You will and show mercy to whom You will — let us be among the *man* in Your mercy. Till the hidden soil of our hearts now, gently, by Your own hand, so that on the day of *tuqlabūn*, what is brought up in us is something You can plant Your *raḥmah* in. And let our last turning, on the day we are turned, be the turning we have been quietly practicing every day of our lives — toward You, and only toward You.

Āmīn.
