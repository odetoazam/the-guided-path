# The Forgery of False Gods and the Sustenance You Cannot Manufacture

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 17

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
[ayah:29:17] إِنَّمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ أَوْثَـٰنًا وَتَخْلُقُونَ إِفْكًا ۚ إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ تَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ لَا يَمْلِكُونَ لَكُمْ رِزْقًا فَٱبْتَغُوا۟ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرِّزْقَ وَٱعْبُدُوهُ وَٱشْكُرُوا۟ لَهُۥٓ ۖ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Innamā taʿbudūna min dūni'llāhi awthānan wa-takhluqūna ifkā. Inna alladhīna taʿbudūna min dūni'llāhi lā yamlikūna lakum rizqā. Fa-btaghū ʿinda'llāhi'r-rizqa wa-ʿbudūhu wa-shkurū lah. Ilayhi turjaʿūn.*

**Translation:**
"You only worship, besides Allah, mere idols — and you fabricate a lie. Indeed, those you worship besides Allah do not possess for you any provision. So seek provision from Allah, and worship Him, and be grateful to Him. To Him you will be returned."

### Rebuilding the World

Before we step into this ayah, let's stand inside the moment.

A young man is speaking. His name is Ibrahim. The whole town has stopped to listen — not because they want to, but because what he is saying is so direct, so unflinching, that they cannot look away. He is surrounded by the people who raised him, the elders who shaped him, the priests who taught him. And he is dismantling, in the space of one breath, the entire economic, religious, and emotional architecture of their lives.

Because here is what we have to understand: idols in Ibrahim's world were not just spiritual props. They were the **center of an economy**. People made them. Sold them. Sacrificed at them. Took oaths by them. Asked them for rain, for crops, for healthy children. The temples generated revenue. The priests held social power. To attack the idols was to attack a livelihood, a worldview, and a class structure all at once.

And Ibrahim — young, alone, raised inside that system — looks at the entire arrangement and says one word that flips it: **you are *making* the very thing you are *worshiping*.**

Now zoom out further. This ayah sits in Surah Al-Ankabut — the Surah of the Spider. The surah whose central image is a creature that spins a home so fragile that the slightest disturbance destroys it, yet feels safe inside it. The surah is, from first ayah to last, an X-ray of the things human beings lean on that **cannot hold them**.

Ibrahim's speech is the diagnostic moment of the whole surah. It is the place where the question becomes explicit: *What are you actually leaning on? And can it hold you?*

---

### The Hook

There is something genuinely strange in this ayah — something that sounds, on first reading, almost contradictory. Ibrahim accuses his people of two things in the same breath: that they **worship** idols, and that they **fabricate** them.

But look closer. He doesn't use the normal Arabic verb for *making* or *carving* or *crafting* an idol. He uses a verb that, in the Quran, is almost always reserved for one Being and one Being alone.

**He accuses them of *creating*.**

And then, having torn down what they thought was holding them up, he gives them an instruction about provision — about *rizq* — that contains a small preposition so precisely chosen that it changes the meaning of the entire economic life of a believer.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa-takhluqūna ifkā* — "And you create a lie"

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, when you want to describe someone **making** something, you have a whole toolkit of verbs. There are verbs for carving (*naḥata*), for shaping (*ṣawwara*), for fashioning (*ṣanaʿa*), for fabricating in the sense of building (*banā*). Each of them carries the idea of taking something that already exists — wood, stone, clay — and giving it a new form.

But there is one verb in Arabic that means something different. The verb **خَلَقَ** *(khalaqa)* — "to create." In its most precise classical usage, *khalq* is the bringing forth of something according to a measure, an originating act. The lexicographers note that when applied absolutely, with no further qualifier, *khalq* belongs to Allah. The Quran reserves it, again and again, for the One Who originates from nothing: **اللَّهُ خَالِقُ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ** — "Allah is the Creator of every thing."

So in everyday Arabic, if you wanted to say "you made an idol," the natural verb would be *ṣanaʿtum* or *naḥattum*. You would not say *khalaqtum* — because that verb sits, by default, on the shelf labeled *for Allah alone*.

#### Now look at what Ibrahim does

**وَتَخْلُقُونَ إِفْكًا** — *wa-takhluqūna ifkā* — "and you *create* a lie."

Ibrahim deliberately reaches up to that shelf, takes down the verb that belongs to Allah, and hands it to his people — not as a compliment, not as a metaphor, but as a **diagnosis**.

Let's sit with this. He is saying: you, who have refused to worship the only true *Khāliq*, have decided to play *khāliq* yourselves. The very thing you accused Allah of not doing — creating gods worth worshiping — *you* are now doing. You have become little manufacturers of divinity.

And what are you creating?

**ifkā.**

The root **أ-ف-ك** (*ʾ-f-k*) carries the meaning of turning a thing from its right direction, twisting reality, inverting truth. The classical lexicons describe *ifk* as a particularly egregious kind of falsehood — not a small lie, not a polite fiction, but a **lie that flips reality on its head**. The same root is used for the cities of the people of Lūṭ that were "overturned" (*al-muʾtafikāt*) — physically inverted, sky becoming ground.

So Ibrahim's accusation is not just "you tell lies." It is: **you are creating an *inverted reality*.** You are taking the truest thing — that Allah alone deserves worship — and you are flipping it upside-down with your own hands. The lie is not a small distortion. It is a complete inversion.

Here's what's remarkable. Notice the architecture of this single phrase. Ibrahim has put the **creator verb** next to the **inversion noun**. You think you are creating something? Yes — you are. You are creating *the upside-down*. You are not adding to reality; you are subtracting from it. You are not building a god; you are manufacturing your own confusion.

What this single half-ayah reveals: **every false object of worship is, in fact, a small act of forgery in which the human being plays creator and produces nothing but an inverted truth.**

### Element Two: *Awthān* — Not "gods," but *lumps*

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic there are several words used for idols. The most common is *aṣnām* (*ṣ-n-m*), which usually refers to a fashioned, sculpted figure — something that resembles a being, an animal, a person. The word *aṣnām* implies craft, recognizable form, intentional design.

But the word Ibrahim chooses in this ayah is different.

**أَوْثَانًا** — *awthān*, plural of *wathan*.

The root **و-ث-ن** (*w-th-n*) is connected, in the classical lexicons, to the idea of something **firm, fixed, settled in place** — but in the context of *wathan*, it refers to a stationary object of veneration that may be just a stone, a mound, a lump. Lisān al-ʿArab notes the distinction: a *ṣanam* has a recognizable form; a *wathan* may have no form at all. A *wathan* can be a rock, a tree, a heap. It is the **stationary thing** people have decided to bow to.

#### Now stay with that

Ibrahim, surrounded by carved, beautifully sculpted *aṣnām* in their temples, does not call them *aṣnām*. He calls them **awthān** — *lumps*. Stationary things. Inanimate fixed objects.

It is a downgrade. A deliberate linguistic deflation. He refuses to give them even the dignity of being called *sculptures*. He calls them what they actually are: **inert masses that happen to be sitting where you have decided to bow.**

Notice something else. *Wathan* implies *fixed in place*. It does not move. It does not act. It does not respond. The very name Ibrahim has chosen for these idols already encodes their fatal flaw: **they cannot come to you, cannot act for you, cannot do anything but sit there.**

And then he completes the diagnosis in the next clause:

**لَا يَمْلِكُونَ لَكُمْ رِزْقًا** — "they do not possess for you any provision."

The verb **يَمْلِكُونَ** (*yamlikūn*) comes from **م-ل-ك** (*m-l-k*), which means *to own, to have authority over, to possess in such a way that one can dispose of the thing*. It is not just "they don't have provision to give you." It is: **they don't even possess it. They are not in the chain of custody at all.** They are not late in providing; they are not unwilling to provide; they are simply *outside the system of provision entirely*.

Here's what's quietly devastating. Look at the geometry of this clause:

- *awthān* — lumps, stationary
- *lā yamlikūna* — they do not possess
- *lakum* — for you
- *rizqā* — any provision

These objects are not just powerless. They are not just unhelpful. They are **stationary lumps that do not even own the very thing you are coming to them to receive.** It is like going to a man who has no bank account, no wallet, no pocket, no coins on his person — and asking him for a loan. The transaction is not difficult; it is **structurally impossible**.

What this single description reveals: **the tragedy of false worship is not that the idol refuses to give; it is that the idol has nothing to give. Worship aimed at what does not possess is, by its own internal physics, a closed loop with no exit.**

### Element Three: *Fa-btaghū ʿinda'llāhi'r-rizqa* — The preposition that changes everything

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, when you ask someone to seek something from a source, there are several ways to phrase the source. You can say *min fulān* — "from so-and-so." You can say *ʿinda fulān* — "with so-and-so," or "at so-and-so." You can say *ladā fulān* — "in the presence of so-and-so."

These look similar in English, but in Arabic they encode subtly different relationships.

*Min* — *from* — suggests **direction of transfer**. The thing moves from a source toward you.

*ʿinda* — usually translated *with* or *at* — describes **where something is located, in whose possession or presence it sits**. It is the preposition of *the place where the thing is kept*.

A small example: if I say *al-māl ʿinda Zayd* — "the money is *ʿinda* Zayd" — I am telling you not just that Zayd has it, but that Zayd is the **custodian, the one with whom it rests**. It is the language of a treasury.

#### Now look at what Ibrahim says

After diagnosing the idols as stationary lumps that do not even possess provision, he gives the prescription:

**فَٱبْتَغُوا۟ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرِّزْقَ** — *fa-btaghū ʿinda'llāhi'r-rizqa* — "so seek, *at Allah*, the provision."

He could have said *min Allāh* — "from Allah." That would have been perfectly correct. It would have meant: provision comes *from* Allah toward you.

But he says *ʿinda Allāh*. **At Allah. With Allah. In the custody of Allah.**

Let's sit with this. The preposition *ʿinda* is the language of where the **treasury** is. It is telling Ibrahim's people — and us — that *rizq* is not a substance flying around the universe that you have to chase. *Rizq* is a deposit. It is **kept somewhere**. It has a location. It rests *ʿinda Allāh*. With Him. In His vault.

Now notice what this does to your understanding of seeking. If provision is *ʿinda Allāh* — kept with Him — then every door of provision you knock on in this world is not the *source*. It is, at best, a **window** through which what is *ʿinda Allāh* is dispensed. The job, the salary, the client, the harvest, the rain — none of these *possess* the provision. They are channels. The treasury is one. The channels are many.

This is why Ibrahim used the verb **ٱبْتَغُوا۟** (*ibtaghū*) — not *iṭlubū* (a generic "ask"), but *ibtaghū*, from the root **ب-غ-ي** (*b-gh-y*). The classical lexicons describe *ibtighāʾ* as a more intensive, deliberate seeking — a *seeking-out* that involves direction, intention, and effort. It is not passive receiving. It is active orientation.

So Ibrahim is saying: **seek actively, with intention, with effort — but seek *at* the right address.** Move, work, knock — but know whose vault you are knocking on.

Notice also the contrast he builds. The idols *lā yamlikūna lakum rizqā* — do not even possess provision. But Allah is the one *ʿinda*-Whom *rizq* sits. The asymmetry is total. On one side: stationary lumps with empty hands. On the other: the One in Whose presence all provision is kept. To direct your seeking to the first when the second exists is not just spiritually wrong — it is **practically irrational**.

And here is the quiet revolution embedded in this single preposition: *ʿinda* tells us that anxiety about provision is, fundamentally, **a question of address, not of amount**. The amount is settled. It is *ʿinda Allāh*. Already there. Already counted. Already assigned. The only real question is: are you knocking on the right door?

What this single preposition reveals: **rizq is not produced by your seeking; it is dispensed from a treasury that is already full. Your work is not to manufacture it but to align yourself with its custodian.**

### Bridge

So we have stood with three things.

We have seen that Ibrahim accused his people of **creating a lie** — of playing creator and producing nothing but inversion. We have seen that he refused to call their gods *sculptures* and named them *stationary lumps* with no possession to dispense. And we have seen that he redirected them not to ask *from* Allah but to seek *ʿinda* Allah — at His treasury, where provision already rests.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being actually lives. About what we lean on. About where our anxiety comes from. About what it means to be fed.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Quiet Manufacturing of Modern Idols

Begin where you actually live.

When you imagine an idol, you probably picture something obvious. A statue. A figure of stone. Something visibly worshipped in a temple somewhere far from you, far from your life. And so when you hear Ibrahim's accusation — *you create a lie* — it can feel like it belongs to another century. Other people. Other problems.

But stay with the verb. *Takhluqūna ifkā* — *you create a lie*. Ibrahim is describing a specific psychological motion. He is describing the moment a human being takes something that is **not the source of life**, decides it is, and then begins to *manufacture meaning around it* until the false centrality of that thing becomes self-evident.

Consider how this actually works in your day.

You take a metric — a follower count, a salary number, a body shape, a partner, a job title, the approval of one specific person. The metric, in itself, is not evil. It is *neutral*. It is, in Ibrahim's vocabulary, **a lump**. A stationary fact. Something that just *sits there* in your life.

Then you begin the manufacturing. You start telling yourself stories about what this metric means. "If I hit that number, I'll finally feel okay." "If they choose me, my life begins." "If I look like that, I'll be loved." Each story is a small act of *khalq* — a small act of creating. You are taking the lump and you are **breathing onto it a significance it never possessed**. And as you do this, day by day, sentence by sentence in your own head, the lump becomes a god. Not because it grew. Because *you grew it*.

This is what Ibrahim is naming. The idol is not, in the first instance, the object. The idol is the **manufactured meaning** wrapped around the object. The lump is just a lump. The god is what you have made the lump *be*.

And here is the cruelty of the system. Because you made it, you cannot rest in it. A god you manufactured has the same problem as the idols Ibrahim is describing: it does not *possess* what you are asking it to give. The follower count cannot dispense self-worth. The salary cannot dispense peace. The partner cannot dispense salvation. They were never in the chain of custody. You aimed your worship at a stationary lump, and the lump, true to its nature, **did not move**.

The Quran's diagnosis of idolatry is therefore not primarily a diagnosis of *theology*. It is a diagnosis of **misdirected dependency**. It is the question: *what is the thing in your life that you are treating as if it possessed what only Allah possesses?*

And the most painful version of this question is not "what idol do I bow to?" It is: **what idol am I currently manufacturing? What lie am I, this week, this month, this year, in the slow act of creating?**

Now move to the second layer.

There is a particular relief in Ibrahim's language. Because he says *takhluqūna* — *you create*. Which means: **what you have created, you can stop creating.** The idol is not an external prison. It is a daily construction. The factory is inside you. The hours of production are your own attention. The raw material is your own thoughts.

This means the dismantling of an idol is not, in the first instance, an act of destruction. It is an act of *no longer manufacturing*. You do not have to topple the statue. You have to stop feeding it the breath of your own meaning-making. The lump, left alone, becomes a lump again. The god dissolves into the rock it always was.

And then — only then — does the second half of the ayah become livable. Because as long as you are still producing the idol, the instruction *fa-btaghū ʿinda'llāhi'r-rizqa* cannot land. You cannot seek at Allah's treasury while you are still building a counterfeit vault in your own basement.

**The practical application:** Identify, today, one specific manufactured idol. Not a generic one — a specific one. The exact metric, the exact person's approval, the exact outcome you have decided your peace depends on. Then ask: *what story am I telling myself that gives this lump its size?* The story is the *khalq*. The story is what you can stop.

### Theme Two: The Address of Provision — Why Anxiety About *Rizq* Is, at Root, a Question of Geography

Begin where you actually live.

Most of us, if we are honest, carry a quiet, low-grade anxiety about provision. Not necessarily a catastrophic fear of starvation. Something subtler. A constant background tension about: *will the next paycheck come? what if this client leaves? what if the market turns? what if I lose my job? what if I cannot afford the thing my child needs? what if old age finds me without enough?*

This anxiety is not irrational. It is a normal response to living in a world where provision feels precarious — where the chain of cause and effect between *your effort* and *your sustenance* is visible, fragile, and easily disrupted.

But Ibrahim's instruction, *fa-btaghū ʿinda'llāhi'r-rizqa*, contains a quiet reframe that, if you let it settle, changes the very texture of that anxiety.

Notice what he does *not* say. He does not say: *and Allah will give you rizq, so don't worry, sit at home.* He does not abolish seeking. He says **ibtaghū** — *seek out*. Actively. With effort. With direction. The verb keeps the human being in motion.

But the preposition relocates the destination. **ʿinda Allāh.** *At Allah.* In His presence. In His custody.

So your hands are still working. Your feet are still walking to the office, to the field, to the shop, to the interview. The *motion* of seeking is preserved. But the **mental address** of that seeking has been moved.

Stay with this. Imagine two people doing the exact same job. Same desk. Same hours. Same salary. From the outside, identical. But on the inside, one of them is in a state Ibrahim is warning against: he believes his *rizq* is *ʿinda* his employer. His sustenance, in his own mental geography, lives in the company's bank account, in the client's good mood, in the market's whims. So every fluctuation in those external things sends a tremor through his nervous system, because he believes the *vault* is shaking.

The other person is doing the same job, but his mental geography is different. He knows the *rizq* is *ʿinda Allāh*. The job is a window, not the vault. The client is a channel, not the source. The market is weather, not the sky. So when the client leaves, his stomach does not drop — because the vault did not move. The window closed. Allah will open another.

This is what *ʿinda* does. It does not abolish work; it **abolishes the worship of the workplace**. It does not abolish gratitude to people who pay you; it abolishes the **manufactured belief that they possess what you need**. They are channels. The treasury is one.

Now here is where the ayah deepens.

After *fa-btaghū ʿinda'llāhi'r-rizqa* — "seek provision at Allah" — Ibrahim adds two instructions: **وَٱعْبُدُوهُ وَٱشْكُرُوا۟ لَهُۥ** — *wa-ʿbudūhu wa-shkurū lah* — "and worship Him, and be grateful to Him."

Notice the ordering. **Seek provision. Worship. Be grateful.** Why does *shukr* — gratitude — sit *here*, at the end of the sequence about provision?

Because *shukr* is the **acknowledgment of the address**. When you are grateful to Allah for what arrives in your hand, you are doing more than saying *thank you*. You are saying: *I know where this came from*. *I know whose vault it left*. *I know which channel was just the window and which Hand actually opened it*. Gratitude is the public confession of correct geography.

This is why ingratitude — *kufr al-niʿma* — is theologically dangerous. It is not just rudeness. It is the **relocation of the source**. When you receive *rizq* and attribute it entirely to your skill, your network, your luck, your employer — you have, in that moment, performed a small act of *takhluqūna ifkā*. You have created a lie about the address. You have moved the vault, in your own mind, from *ʿinda Allāh* to *ʿinda* yourself or *ʿinda* the channel.

And so the ayah closes with the line that puts every act of seeking and every act of receiving into final perspective:

**إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ** — *ilayhi turjaʿūn* — "to Him you will be returned."

The verb *turjaʿūn* is passive — *you will be returned*. Not *you will return* (active). You will be **brought back**. Which means: the same Hand that dispenses your *rizq* during your life is the same Hand to which you yourself are being delivered at the end of it. Every paycheck is a small rehearsal. The provision moves from the vault to you. And then, at the appointed time, **you yourself move from the world back to the One Who owns the vault**. The same arrow. The same direction. *Ilayhi*. To Him.

So the architecture of the second half of this ayah is one continuous motion:

1. **Seek at His treasury** — orient your work to the right address.
2. **Worship Him** — let your acts of devotion match where you have located the source.
3. **Be grateful to Him** — confess the address every time something arrives.
4. **To Him you will be returned** — and at the end, the seeker himself follows the same path the *rizq* traveled.

What this whole sequence reveals: **your relationship with provision is, in the end, a rehearsal for your relationship with the One Who gives it. The way you treat the gift is the way you are training your soul to meet the Giver.**

**The practical application:** The next time you receive something — a paycheck, an unexpected help, a meal, a kindness — pause for three seconds before doing anything else, and **name the address out loud or in your heart**: *This is from ʿinda Allāh, through this channel.* Say it slowly enough that your nervous system hears it. Over weeks, this small habit relocates the vault in your interior geography. Anxiety about provision begins to loosen — not because the world has become more reliable, but because you have stopped worshipping the wrong address.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now that we have walked through the ayah, look back at its shape. The whole verse is built on a perfect inversion-and-correction:

**First half — the diagnosis:**
- *You worship besides Allah — mere lumps.*
- *You create a lie.*
- *Those you worship do not possess for you any provision.*

**Second half — the prescription:**
- *Seek provision at Allah.*
- *Worship Him.*
- *Be grateful to Him.*
- *To Him you will be returned.*

Notice the symmetry. The first half is a closed loop with nothing in the middle — you worship lumps that have nothing, so you receive nothing, and the loop spirals. The second half is an **open arrow** — seek at His vault, worship Him, thank Him, and be returned to Him. It moves in one direction. It has a destination. It does not loop back on itself; it terminates *in Him*.

Ibrahim has not just made an argument. He has drawn, in the structure of his sentence, the difference between **closed-loop idolatry** and **open-arrow tawhīd**. One traps you in the lump. The other delivers you home.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The verb *takhluqūna* — *you create* — is the same insight as the theme of *manufactured idols*. You are the factory. The lie is the product.

The word *awthān* — *stationary lumps* — and the clause *lā yamlikūna lakum rizqā* — *they do not possess for you any provision* — are the same insight as the theme of *anxious dependency on the wrong address*. The lumps cannot dispense what they do not own.

And the preposition *ʿinda* is the geographical correction at the center of both themes. The vault is at Allah. The address has always been at Allah. Every act of worship, every act of seeking, every act of gratitude is a small motion of **re-aligning yourself with the only address that actually holds the thing you are looking for**.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the lump in my life right now that I am, by daily small acts of meaning-making, slowly turning into a god? What specific story am I telling myself to inflate it?

2. When my income, my work, or my safety feels threatened — where, in my actual interior, do I locate the vault? At my employer? At the market? At my own ability? Or *ʿinda Allāh*?

3. If I tracked one week of my speech and thought, how often do I name the *channel* (the boss, the client, the deal) versus how often I name the *address* (Allah)?

4. Where in my life am I doing the right action with the wrong mental geography — working hard, but seeking *from* a created thing instead of *at* the Creator?

5. If I knew, with certainty, that every *rizq* I am ever going to receive is already counted and resting *ʿinda Allāh*, what would I stop doing tomorrow? What would I start?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Every false object of worship is a stationary lump onto which the human being has breathed manufactured meaning, and the cure is to stop creating the lie, relocate the vault to *ʿinda Allāh*, and let gratitude rehearse, in this life, the return that is already coming.**

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is an invitation to **a single small relocation**: move the address of your seeking from *the channel* to *the treasury*. Not by stopping your work. Not by abandoning the world. But by adding, beneath every action, the quiet sentence: *the vault is ʿinda Allāh*. Let that sentence sit underneath your effort, your worry, your gratitude, your fear. Over time, it will dissolve the lumps that have been pretending to be gods.

You do not have to topple anything. You just have to stop manufacturing.

### Du'a

Allāhumma, You Whose treasury holds every provision before we knew we needed it — relocate the address in our hearts. Stop our hands from creating the lies we worship. Let us seek at Your door, work at Your door, and at the end be returned through Your door, knowing it was Yours all along.

آمين.
