# The Weight They Carry, And The Weight On Top Of It

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 13

### Arabic

> وَلَيَحْمِلُنَّ أَثْقَالَهُمْ وَأَثْقَالًا مَّعَ أَثْقَالِهِمْ ۖ وَلَيُسْأَلُنَّ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَمَّا كَانُوا يَفْتَرُونَ

### Transliteration

> Wa la-yaḥmilunna athqālahum wa athqālan maʿa athqālihim, wa la-yusʾalunna yawma-l-qiyāmati ʿammā kānū yaftarūn.

### Translation

> "And they will surely carry their own burdens, and other burdens along with their burdens. And they will surely be questioned on the Day of Resurrection about what they used to fabricate."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

To understand this ayah, you have to step inside a specific moment in early Makkah.

Picture it. A young man — maybe nineteen, maybe twenty-three — has started praying. His family noticed. Word got around the tribe. And now one of the elders — someone respected, someone whose voice carries weight in the assemblies — pulls him aside.

The elder doesn't threaten him at first. He uses a different tool. He says something like this:

*"Look. I understand. This new message is compelling. The man speaks beautifully. But come back to our way. Come back to what your fathers were on. And listen — if you're worried about sin, about consequence, about what happens after — don't be. **We'll carry it for you.** Whatever burden falls on you for leaving Muhammad's way, it falls on us. You'll be safe. We'll take the weight."*

That offer is documented. The ayah right before this one — verse 12 — quotes it almost verbatim: *"Follow our way, and we will carry your sins."*

It was psychologically brilliant. It targeted the exact pressure point of a new Muslim: the fear of getting it wrong. The fear that maybe — just maybe — leaving the religion of your forefathers really would land on you. And it offered a release valve. *Don't worry about that. We've got it. Our shoulders are wider than yours. Come back, and you walk light.*

Allah responds first in verse 12: He calls them liars. *They will not carry anything of his sins. They are indeed liars.*

But then comes verse 13. And verse 13 doesn't just say *they're lying*. Verse 13 says something far more terrifying.

### The Hook

Verse 13 says: they will carry. They will carry, in fact, more than they thought possible.

But not the way they promised.

[PAUSE]

What does it mean to carry someone else's weight on the Day when every soul is supposed to bear only what it earned? How can both be true — *no soul bears another's burden*, and *they will carry their own and burdens along with their burdens*?

And there is a word in this ayah — a small, sharp word at the end — that names the precise sin that doubles the weight. Not the sin of misguidance. Something more specific. Something the listener of that day would have heard and shuddered at.

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Emphatic Oath That Won't Let You Look Away — *la-yaḥmilunna*

Before we open the word, I want to teach you a feature of Arabic that English doesn't really have.

In English, if I want to make a statement more emphatic, I add words: *They really will carry it. They absolutely will carry it. They will definitely, certainly, without a doubt, carry it.*

Arabic does it differently. It compresses the emphasis **into the verb itself.** It puts a prefix on it — a small particle called *lām al-qasam* — and a suffix on it — a double *nūn* called *nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah*, "the heavy nūn of emphasis." Together, those two markers turn a normal verb into something closer to a sworn oath. The verb is no longer reporting. The verb is *promising under oath.*

Everyday example: if I say to you, "I'll come to your house," that's a statement. If I say, "I swear, I'm coming, no matter what" — that's a different speech act. It's a binding. Arabic does that grammatically, not by adding extra words.

Now look at the verb in this ayah:

> **وَلَيَحْمِلُنَّ** — *wa la-yaḥmilunna*

The verb *yaḥmilu* means "he carries." Add the *lām* in front — *la-yaḥmil*. Add the heavy *nūn* at the end — *la-yaḥmilunna.* You've now said: *they will most certainly, by oath, carry.*

And then — this is the part I want you to sit with — Allah does it **again** at the end of the same ayah:

> **وَلَيُسْأَلُنَّ** — *wa la-yusʾalunna*

*They will most certainly, by oath, be questioned.*

[PAUSE]

Two oaths in fourteen words. Two emphatic constructions, identically built, bookending the verse.

Let's sit with this. Why would Allah swear twice in one ayah?

Here's what's remarkable. Remember what the disbelievers had said. *Follow our way, and we will carry your sins.* That was **their** promise. *They* made an oath. *They* offered guarantees. Their whole rhetorical move was the move of someone who sounds confident, sounds binding, sounds like they have authority over outcomes.

Allah's response in verse 13 doesn't just contradict their claim. It overwrites it with a counter-oath that has actual binding force. **They** promised to carry. Allah swears: no, **they** will be the ones doing the carrying. **They** sounded sure. Allah is sure.

Notice something. The structure is almost taunting in its symmetry. They said *we will carry.* Allah says *they will carry, and they will be questioned.* The promise they offered as a service becomes the sentence they bear.

This is what the *lām al-qasam* and the heavy *nūn* are doing here. They are not decorative emphasis. They are a juridical signature. They are Allah signing a verdict that overrides the human contract.

And here's where the sonic texture starts to matter. Say the verbs out loud: *la-yaḥmilunna... la-yusʾalunna.* They land heavy. The double *nūn* is called *thaqīlah* — "the heavy one" — for a reason. Even the *name* of the grammatical feature is the same root as the word *athqāl* — "burdens" — that fills the middle of the ayah.

The *form* of the speech is heavy. The *content* of the speech is heavy. The two reinforce each other. You can't say this ayah lightly. Your mouth has to bear weight to speak it.

[PAUSE]

What this single grammatical feature reveals: *the verdict in this ayah is not a description. It is a binding oath sworn by Allah, in answer to a binding oath made by men — and the heaviness of the construction is itself part of the meaning.*

### Element Two: The Repetition That Multiplies — *athqālahum wa athqālan maʿa athqālihim*

Now look at the middle of the ayah. There is a word that appears three times in a row:

> **أَثْقَالَهُمْ وَأَثْقَالًا مَّعَ أَثْقَالِهِمْ**
> *athqālahum wa athqālan maʿa athqālihim*

Three times. Their burdens — and burdens — with their burdens.

Repetition in Arabic is never accidental. The language has many ways to avoid repetition — pronouns, synonyms, ellipsis. When Allah repeats a word three times in seven words, the repetition is the meaning.

Let me teach you the word first.

*Athqāl* is the plural of *thiql.* The root is **ث-ق-ل** — *thaqula* — "to be heavy." It is the same root as *thaqīl* (heavy) and *mithqāl* (a unit of weight), and as we just noticed, *thaqīlah* (the "heavy" nūn). When Allah says, in another place, *and the earth brings out her **athqāl*** (99:2), He means the dead — the heavy things buried inside her. *Thiql* is not just weight. It is **the weight that bears down on what carries it.** It is gravity exerting its claim.

Now look at the three instances. Each one is grammatically distinct.

1. *athqāla-**hum*** — *their* burdens. Definite. Possessed. Specifically theirs.
2. *athqālan* — burdens. Indefinite. **Bare.** No possessor. No specification.
3. *athqāli-**him*** — *their* burdens, again. Definite. Possessed.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that middle word. *Athqālan.* Indefinite. No owner attached.

In Arabic, when a noun is indefinite like this — especially in a context of warning — it carries a sense of *some unspecified quantity, and you don't know how much.* It is the grammatical equivalent of the phrase "and who knows how many more." The owners of the **first** set of burdens are named — *their* burdens, the ones they earned themselves. The owners of the **third** set are named again — *their* burdens, the ones they earned themselves. But the burdens in the middle? Anonymous. Unbounded. **Other.**

Notice the architecture. Allah doesn't say *they will carry their burdens twice.* He doesn't say *they will carry double burdens.* He builds a sandwich: *their burdens — [some other burdens] — their burdens.* The extra weight is placed **between** their own. It is **lodged inside** what they were already carrying. It is not added on top, like a heavier backpack. It is woven into the load itself, so that when they try to set down what they thought was theirs, they find the foreign weight has fused with their own.

And the word *maʿa* — "along with," "together with" — closes the trap. *Maʿa* means *companioned, accompanying, alongside.* The extra burdens don't replace their own. They don't substitute. They **travel with**. They accompany. They go where you go.

This is the load-bearing analogy. Picture a person who has been told they have a debt of, let's say, ten thousand. That's their debt. They've accepted it. They're carrying it. Heavy, but bearable. Then someone whispers to a friend of theirs: *don't worry, I'll cover your debt.* The friend believes it, relaxes, signs further contracts on the strength of that promise, takes on more obligations. Then the day comes. The collector arrives. And the person who made the promise is told: *every loan your friend took out trusting your word — those are yours now. Not instead of his. **In addition** to yours.* Their own ten thousand stays. The friend's new debts are layered into it. *Their burdens — [the friend's added burdens] — their burdens.*

Now hold that image and re-hear the ayah. *They will surely carry their burdens — and burdens, along with their burdens.* The misguider walks into Qiyāmah expecting his own account. He finds his own account intact. And he finds, **embedded within it**, the weight of every soul he sweet-talked off the path.

There's a hadith that makes this explicit — and it doesn't say the followers' weight is *transferred away* from them. It says the misguider bears *like* the weight, *without it being lessened* from the followers. Both pay. The follower for following. The misguider for the call. The same weight scored on two ledgers.

This is how the ayah reconciles with the principle in Surah Al-Anʿām and Al-Isrāʾ — *no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another* (6:164, 17:15). The misguider is **not** carrying *the follower's* burden. He is carrying **his own additional burden** — the burden he generated *by his call.* The act of calling someone off the path is itself a separate sin. The misguider's own ledger now has two columns: the disbelief he chose, and the misguidance he spread. Both are his. Earned. Owned. Indelibly his.

Notice something else, almost cruel in its precision: he offered to carry the follower's weight as a service. He thought he was giving. Allah's verdict reveals that the very *offer* — the call, the seduction, the "we've got you" — wasn't a transfer of weight away from the follower. It was the **manufacturing of new weight,** which now belongs to the offerer.

He didn't redistribute the load. He doubled it.

[PAUSE]

What this triple repetition reveals: *the burden of leading others away is not the same burden the followers carry — it is an entirely separate weight, generated by the act of calling, and it is woven into the misguider's own account so that he cannot set down one without the other.*

### Element Three: The Word That Names The Specific Sin — *yaftarūn*

We're going to end the linguistic journey with one word. The last word of the ayah.

> **يَفْتَرُونَ** — *yaftarūn*

"…about what they used to **fabricate**."

Before we open it, notice what Allah did *not* say. He didn't say *about what they used to disbelieve.* He didn't say *about what they used to do.* He didn't say *about what they used to associate.* He could have used any of those — and each appears elsewhere in the Quran in similar settings. He chose a different word. A specific word.

*Yaftarūn* is from the root **ف-ر-ي** — *farā.* The base meaning in classical Arabic is *to cut, to slit, to split open, to tear.* When a leather-worker would *farā* a hide, he would cut it open and stretch it — sometimes legitimately, to make a garment. Sometimes destructively, to ruin it. From this came the second meaning: **to fabricate something out of nothing**, to *manufacture* a falsehood the way a leather-worker fashions an object out of raw hide — to construct a lie that looks made, finished, real.

*Iftirāʾ*, the verbal noun, became one of the Quran's strongest words for lying. It is not the casual lie — *kadhib* — of someone who slips and says something untrue. *Iftirāʾ* is **manufactured lying.** It is *fabrication with intent.* It is the lie you build the way an artisan builds a chair — deliberately, with skill, knowing what you're making.

And here's the haunting part. In the Quran, *iftirāʾ* is almost always reserved for one specific type of lie: **a lie attributed to Allah.** *Wa man aẓlamu mim-mani-ftarā ʿalā-llāhi kadhibā* — "And who is more wrong than the one who **fabricates** against Allah a lie?" (6:21, 6:93, 7:37, 10:17, 11:18, 18:15, 29:68 — and many more). The pattern is consistent. *Iftirāʾ* is the linguistic signature of religious lying — of saying *Allah said* when Allah didn't, of dressing up your preference in His name, of manufacturing theology.

Now go back to verse 12. What did the misguiders offer? *Follow our way, and **we will carry your sins.*** They were not just disbelieving. They were *making a theological claim.* They were saying — with the confidence of religious authority — *our path absorbs consequence. Our way carries weight. Trust us with your soul.* They were **manufacturing a doctrine** they had no warrant for. They were doing *iftirāʾ.*

This is why Allah doesn't say at the end *they will be questioned about their disbelief.* He says they will be questioned about their **fabrication.** Because the specific weight that doubled the load — the *athqāl* between their *athqāl* — was not generic sin. It was the sin of **inventing a salvation they had no authority to offer.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because this is the joint. This is where the whole ayah hinges.

What they "fabricated" was the *promise of carrying.* The promise that gave the new Muslim license to come back. The promise that made apostasy feel safe. They cut that promise out of nothing — *farā,* tore it from raw material — and presented it as guarantee.

And so the questioning on the Day of Resurrection — *la-yusʾalunna yawma-l-qiyāmati ʿammā kānū yaftarūn* — is not a generic interrogation. It is specifically: *show me your warrant. Show me the ledger that authorized you to promise what you promised. Show me where it was written that you could carry souls.*

There is no warrant. There never was. *Iftirāʾ* by definition is fabrication without source. And so the questioning has no answer. The silence is the verdict.

Notice the tense: *kānū yaftarūn.* Not *iftarū* (perfect — they fabricated). *Kānū yaftarūn* — they **used to** fabricate. The continuous past. The *habit.* Allah is not asking about one slip of the tongue. He is asking about a **way of speaking.** A practice. A career of religious manufacture. Every time they said *our way is fine, our fathers were right, your sin is on us* — every utterance was another stitch in a garment they were sewing out of nothing.

And on the Day, the garment is held up. *Show me where this came from.* And they will have nothing to show.

[PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: *the doubled burden is specifically the weight of religious fabrication — of making theological promises one was not authorized to make — and the questioning on the Day is a demand for warrant that cannot be answered.*

### Bridge

Three things the language has shown us. The double oath that overwrites their human oath. The triple repetition that lodges the foreign weight inside their own. The final word that names the precise sin as **fabrication**, not generic disbelief.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words — but about reality itself. Because there are two truths buried here that the modern listener, in our particular moment, needs.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Weight Of Speaking For God Without Permission

Let me start where you live.

You have probably been in a room — maybe an online room, maybe a real one — where someone with a confident voice said something about Islam. Maybe a podcaster. Maybe an uncle. Maybe a sheikh on a livestream. Maybe a Twitter account with a green checkmark. And they said something like: *the Prophet would have done X. Allah loves Y. The deen is clear that Z.* And it sounded fluent. It sounded sourced. It sounded like religious authority.

But you weren't sure. Something in you flagged it. You couldn't place why.

Or maybe — and this is harder — you have *been* that voice. Maybe in a family group chat. Maybe with a younger sibling. Maybe with a friend who was struggling. You spoke confidently about what Allah wants, what the deen teaches, what the right path is — and you spoke a little past what you actually knew.

Verse 13 is about that.

Not about the obvious religious frauds. Not about the cult leaders. Those are easy to spot. Verse 13 is about the much more ordinary, much more widespread reality of *speaking for God with more confidence than your knowledge warrants.* The leather-worker's lie — *farā* — is rarely a coarse, obvious lie. It is a *finished* lie. A lie that has been *worked on,* shaped, made to look like a real garment. The terrifying thing about *iftirāʾ* is that it does not announce itself. It comes dressed.

Here is what the ayah is telling us. Every time we say *Allah wants this* when we are not sure, or *the deen teaches this* when we have not verified, or *the scholars agree on this* when they don't — we are not just being intellectually careless. We are doing something that has **weight in the unseen.** Weight that will be there on the Day. Weight that doubles when someone listens to us and changes their life on the strength of our voice.

The Prophet ﷺ said — and this is in Muslim — *whoever calls to guidance, his is the reward of those who follow him without their rewards being diminished; and whoever calls to misguidance, his is the burden of it like the burden of those who follow him without their burdens being diminished.* That hadith is *exactly* what verse 13 grammatically encodes. The triple *athqāl.* The weight that doesn't transfer — it duplicates.

[PAUSE]

What changes about how I live tomorrow?

Very specifically: I learn to say *I don't know* about religious questions. I learn to say *let me check that.* I learn to add *but Allah knows best* not as a verbal tic but as a real epistemic safety valve. I become slower to forward, to repeat, to "ackshually." I treat religious speech the way a careful pharmacist treats a prescription — knowing that what I dispense might land in someone else's body, and what I get wrong, *I will carry.*

If verse 13 lives in you, you become a more careful speaker. Not a silent one. A careful one. The deen still gets spoken — but only out of warrant. Only out of what you can show. Only out of what you would be willing to be questioned about *on the Day when nothing else will be on the table but the warrant.*

### Theme Two: The Lie That Looks Like Mercy

Now I want to take you somewhere harder. Because the first theme — be careful with religious speech — is recognizable. Most religious people will nod at it. The deeper layer of verse 13 is somewhere else.

Go back to what the disbelievers actually offered. *Follow our way, and we will carry your sins.*

Stop and notice what that sounds like.

It sounds like **mercy.** It sounds like **community.** It sounds like *we've got you.* It sounds like the kindest thing anyone has said to that struggling new Muslim in months — *don't worry, brother, the weight isn't all on you, we'll carry it together.*

The most chilling thing about the lie in verse 12 is that it imitates the language of love.

This is the deepest cut of verse 13. The misguidance that earns the doubled weight is **not** the obvious enmity. It is not the persecution, not the boycott, not the violence. It is the *soft offer.* The offer that says: *you don't have to carry this alone. Come back. We've got you.* The seduction wears the mask of solidarity.

Modern translation: think about every voice today that tells the struggling Muslim — *the deen is too hard, Allah is more lenient than that, just live your life, don't be so strict, we love you, come, we'll cover it.* Sometimes that voice is well-meaning. Sometimes it isn't. But notice what verse 13 says about the structure of that offer, regardless of intent: **the one making the offer is generating new weight on themselves.** They are not lifting weight off the listener. They are *manufacturing weight* that will land on them on the Day. They are doing leather-work on the unseen.

And here is the paradox we have to sit with. Because the same Quran that gives us verse 13 *also* commands us to be gentle with sinners, to give people hope, to not despair of Allah's mercy, to make the deen easy. So the line is not *don't ever say comforting things.* The line is much more precise.

The line is: **do not promise outcomes you cannot guarantee.** Comfort the brother. Sit with the sister. Tell them Allah is *al-Ghafūr*, *al-Raḥīm*, *at-Tawwāb* — that is *His* self-disclosure, His warrant, His word. But do not say *follow my way and I will carry your sins.* That is the line. The first kind of comfort points the soul *toward Allah.* The second kind points the soul *toward you* — and that pointing is the *iftirāʾ.* That pointing is the *farā*-cut of the leather-worker, fashioning yourself into a saviour you were never authorized to be.

[PAUSE]

The misguider in verse 13 is not a monster. The misguider is sometimes someone who started out wanting to help. Who saw a struggling person, felt for them, and overspoke. Who positioned themselves between the soul and Allah out of warmth — and didn't realize that the position itself, however warm, was a manufactured one.

This is why the questioning on the Day is about *iftirāʾ* and not about *kufr.* Disbelief is its own category — those who chose it carry it. The doubled weight is for those who **stood between** other souls and the truth, dressed in the language of safety, and absorbed people's trust into a promise that had no warrant behind it.

What changes about how I live tomorrow?

I stop positioning myself as the safe place. I redirect. When someone struggling comes to me — and they will — my job is to point them at *Allah's* mercy, not to substitute mine. My job is to say *the door is open, here is what Allah has said,* not *come to me, I've got you.* I am not the door. I am, at most, a finger pointing at it. The moment my finger curls toward myself — *I'll carry it, come to my way, trust me with your soul* — I have stepped over the line that verse 13 marks. And from that moment, every soul that listens to me is generating weight on me that will be *layered into my own.*

The ayah is not telling me to be cold. It is telling me to be **honest about where the carrying happens.** It happens at *Allah's* feet. Not at mine. Anyone who tells you otherwise — anyone, including yourself, in the mirror — is doing the leather-work of *farā.*

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the ayah whole.

> **وَلَيَحْمِلُنَّ** أَثْقَالَهُمْ وَأَثْقَالًا مَّعَ أَثْقَالِهِمْ ۖ **وَلَيُسْأَلُنَّ** يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ عَمَّا كَانُوا **يَفْتَرُونَ**

Two emphatic oaths frame the ayah — *la-yaḥmilunna...* at the opening, *la-yusʾalunna...* near the close. Between them, layered like a sandwich: *athqāla-hum — athqālan — athqāli-him.* Their own — anonymous — their own. The structure visually mirrors the meaning. Their own weight on the outside. The foreign weight, lodged in the middle, inseparable from their own. Pressed together by the closing *maʿa.*

And then the final word — *yaftarūn* — names the joint. The thing that earned the middle term. The fabrication. The leather-work. The manufactured doctrine that they passed off as warrant.

The ayah is, structurally, the very weight it describes. Two binding oaths bookending an interior packed with repeated heaviness, sealed by the word that names *why.*

You cannot move any part of this ayah without the whole thing collapsing. That is the architecture.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two directions.

From the language: a binding oath, a triple repetition, a precise word for manufactured falsehood. From the human: be careful what you say in Allah's name, and never position yourself as the safe place between a soul and its Lord.

Both directions arrive at the same point. **Speaking for God has weight. Inviting others to *yourself* in God's name has more weight. And the weight does not vanish on the Day. It is questioned. Specifically. By name.**

### Questions To Carry

1. When was the last time I spoke about Allah, the deen, or what is right with more confidence than my knowledge actually allowed?
2. Whose ledger am I currently on — am I pointing people *to Allah,* or am I, even gently, positioning myself as the door?
3. If I were questioned tomorrow about every religious claim I have made in the last year, how many would I have *warrant* for, and how many were *farā* — manufactured to sound finished?
4. Is there a voice in my life — a podcaster, a relative, an account — that comforts me by saying *the deen is lighter than that, don't worry, follow our way* — and have I confused that comfort for mercy?
5. Am I willing to say "I don't know" out loud, in a religious conversation, this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

*Speaking for Allah without warrant is not just intellectually careless — it is the generation of a weight that will be lodged inside your own, and questioned by name on the Day.*

### The Closing Invitation

Become a more careful speaker.

Not silent. Not afraid. *Careful.* The deen still needs voices. It needs you. But it needs you to speak only out of what you would be willing to carry — because by the verdict of this ayah, what you say *is* what you carry.

And when someone struggling comes to you, hand them off to Allah. Don't keep them. Point. Don't absorb. Let the carrying happen where it actually happens — at His feet, not at yours.

### Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let me speak in Your name without warrant. Do not let me become a door for any soul that should have walked through Yours. Make my words light enough that I can carry them on the Day, and keep me from the leather-work of farā — the lie that comes dressed as mercy. Āmīn.*
