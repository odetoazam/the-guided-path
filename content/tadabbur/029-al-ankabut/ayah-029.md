# The Four Indictments

## Surah Al-Ankabut, Ayah 29

### The Text

**Arabic:**

> أَئِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ ٱلرِّجَالَ وَتَقْطَعُونَ ٱلسَّبِيلَ وَتَأْتُونَ فِى نَادِيكُمُ ٱلْمُنكَرَ ۖ فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ أَن قَالُوا۟ ٱئْتِنَا بِعَذَابِ ٱللَّهِ إِن كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ

**Transliteration:**

> A-innakum la-taʾtūna r-rijāla wa-taqṭaʿūna s-sabīla wa-taʾtūna fī nādīkumu l-munkar. Fa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū iʾtinā biʿadhābi-llāhi in kunta mina ṣ-ṣādiqīn.

**Translation:**

> *"Do you really approach men, and cut off the way, and commit in your gatherings what is wrong?* — But the only answer of his people was that they said: *Bring us the punishment of Allah, if you are of the truthful.*"

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to remember where we are standing.

This is Surah Al-Ankabut — the Surah of the Spider. The surah that opens with one of the most disturbing questions in the entire Quran: *"Do people imagine they will be left to say 'we believe' and not be tested?"* The whole surah is a meditation on testing. On what happens when belief actually has to cost something. And as the surah unfolds, Allah walks us through a gallery of prophets — Nuh, Ibrahim, Lut — each standing before a people who refused the test.

When we arrive at ayah 29, we are inside the confrontation between Lut (peace be upon him) and his people. We are inside the moment a community is being told, with absolute clarity, exactly what is wrong with it. Not vaguely. Not by hint. By name. **Four names.** Four specific indictments that fall in rapid succession.

Now — what was the climate? Try to feel it. The people of Lut were not, as we sometimes flatten them in our minds, simply one people with one sin. They were a society. A civilization with gatherings, with travel routes, with public spaces, with a culture that had crystallized around a particular set of inversions. And Lut, alone among them, kept naming what they had become.

The original listeners of this surah — Muslims in late Makkah, surrounded by a society that was also rapidly crystallizing in its rejection — would have heard this passage and recognized something. Not the specific sins. The *pattern*. The way a people, when confronted by a prophet, eventually stop arguing and start daring.

And so here is the question that should already be pulling at you: when a prophet lists four things — four, not one — what is Allah teaching us about how corruption actually works? And what does it mean that the people, given a perfect mirror of themselves, did not weep, did not deny, did not even argue — but instead said: *bring on the punishment*?

Stay with that. We are going to spend real time here.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Architecture of "A-innakum la-taʾtūna"

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small grammatical fact about Arabic that you can carry into every ayah from here forward.

In Arabic, when you want to make a statement strongly emphatic — when you want to lock the listener's attention onto the unbelievability of what you are about to say — there are three tools you can stack. The first is the interrogative *hamza* (أ) — "is it really...?" The second is the particle *inna* (إنّ) — "indeed, truly." The third is the *lām* of emphasis (لـ) prefixed to the verb — "you really *are* doing this."

Each of these alone is strong. Two of them together is rare. **Three of them in a row is what you do when you are standing in front of something you cannot quite believe a human being could do.**

Now look at how Lut opens:

> **أَئِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ**
> *A-innakum la-taʾtūna...*

All three tools. Stacked. *"Is it really that you indeed truly approach...?"* — there is no English equivalent that does not sound clumsy, because English does not have these three intensifiers to stack. But Arabic has them, and Lut uses every single one before he even reaches the first indictment.

Notice something. He has not yet named what they are doing. He has only built the frame. And the frame itself — three layers of emphatic disbelief — is already a moral statement. It is saying: *what you are doing is so far outside what a human being should be doing that I need three grammatical tools just to ask the question.*

Let's sit with this.

Because we live, you and I, in a moment in history where moral confrontation has been replaced almost entirely by moral *softness*. We have been trained to approach evil sideways. To use euphemism. To say "this might not be the best choice" when what we mean is "this is destroying you." And we have come to mistake softness for kindness.

But Lut is not unkind here. Lut is, in fact, the most patient man in this story. Allah praises Lut. Allah calls Lut among the righteous. And yet **when Lut speaks to the corruption of his people, he does not soften the grammar. He stacks the emphasis. He builds the architecture of disbelief into the question itself.**

Here is what this invites us to consider: there are moments when the most loving thing a person can do is to refuse to phrase the evil softly. To use every tool the language gives you to make clear that what is being done is unbelievable. Not because shouting changes the listener — Lut's people did not change — but because **the prophet's job was never to be heard. It was to be clear.**

### Element Two: The Four Indictments — and What Allah Does With Them

Now we come to the heart. Lut lists four things. Look at them again, in the order they appear:

1. **لَتَأْتُونَ ٱلرِّجَالَ** — *la-taʾtūna r-rijāla* — "you approach the men"
2. **وَتَقْطَعُونَ ٱلسَّبِيلَ** — *wa-taqṭaʿūna s-sabīla* — "and you cut off the way"
3. **وَتَأْتُونَ فِى نَادِيكُمُ ٱلْمُنكَرَ** — *wa-taʾtūna fī nādīkumu l-munkar* — "and you commit in your gathering-place that which is wrong"

That is three actions — but the fourth is hidden in the architecture, and we will get to it.

First, notice the verb that opens the first and third indictments. **Taʾtūna** — from the root *atā* (أ-ت-ي), which means *to come, to arrive at, to approach.* It is the same verb that appears at the end of the ayah, when the people dare Lut: *iʾtinā biʿadhābi-llāh* — *bring* (or *come to us with*) the punishment of Allah.

Stay with that for a second. The verb is *circling*. Lut accuses them of two kinds of *coming* — coming to the men, coming into the gathering with what is wrong. And they respond by demanding Allah *come* with His punishment. The same root threads through accusation and response. Allah is showing us, through one verb, that this is a people who have made *coming-toward-evil* their pattern — and so, with breathtaking arrogance, they invite the final coming.

But let's go through the indictments themselves.

**The first** — *la-taʾtūna r-rijāl* — names the sexual inversion that the Quran elsewhere describes more explicitly. Notice: Lut does not describe the act in graphic detail. He uses the verb of *approach*. He names *who* and lets the listener understand *what*. There is a dignity in the indictment — the verbal restraint of a prophet who will not let even the description of evil become its own indulgence.

**The second** — *wa-taqṭaʿūna s-sabīla* — "and you cut off the way." Now: the classical commentators document multiple meanings for this phrase, and rather than pick one, let me show you how they form a single picture.

*Qaṭʿ as-sabīl* literally means cutting off the road. In the lexicons, this phrase carries the documented meaning of highway robbery — attacking travelers, making the roads unsafe. That is one layer. A second layer the classical commentators noted: cutting off *the way* by which travelers continued the human line — predating on travelers in the specific sexual sense related to the first indictment. A third layer: cutting off *the way of human procreation* itself, since the first indictment is by its nature sterile. A fourth layer: cutting off *the way to Allah* — making it impossible for any guidance, any wholesome relationship, to pass through their territory.

Each commentator chose one camera angle. But the phrase *qaṭʿ as-sabīl* is wide enough to hold all of them — and this is one of the wonders of Quranic compression. **One phrase. Four interlocking corruptions.** The people of Lut had built a society that was sterile at every level: sterile of safe passage, sterile of progeny, sterile of guests, sterile of guidance.

This is not a society with one sin. This is a society that has closed every road.

**The third** — *wa-taʾtūna fī nādīkumu l-munkar.* Here is where I want us to slow down completely.

The word **nādīkum** — "your gathering-place" — comes from the root *n-d-w* (ن-د-و), which in the Arabic lexicons is the root associated with calling out, with assembly, with the place where a people *gather together*. The *nādī* is the social space. The club. The forum. The meeting-house. It is not a private home. It is not a hidden corner. **It is the place where the community is on display to itself.**

Now look at what Allah did not specify.

Lut says they commit *al-munkar* in their gathering — *the* munkar, with the definite article, but he does not say which specific evil. The classical commentators offer a long list: that they catcalled travelers, that they engaged in their inversion publicly, that they gambled, that they mocked their guests, that they cross-dressed, that they spat at one another, that they snapped their fingers at each other for amusement. The list of suggested evils is long.

And here is the absence I want you to notice: **Allah did not narrow it.**

Why? Because to narrow it would be to miss the point. The point is not which specific evil they did in the *nādī*. The point is that the *nādī* — the public, social, shared space where a community presents itself to itself — had become the home of *al-munkar* itself.

The word **munkar** is from the root *n-k-r* (ن-ك-ر), which means *that which is rejected, denied, not-recognized.* The opposite of *maʿrūf* — that which is *known*, that which is *recognized by sound human nature as good*. **Munkar** is the evil that, in a healthy human community, makes everyone instinctively recoil. The thing the natural human conscience does not want to look at.

And Lut tells us that they had taken *that* — the thing every healthy soul rejects — and made it the centerpiece of the public square.

Let's sit with what that means.

Every society has private sins. Every society has hidden corruptions. The question that Allah is putting before us through this ayah is not whether there is evil in your city. The question is: **what has your city decided to celebrate publicly?** What does your *nādī* — your gathering-place, your forum, your feed, your timeline, your screen — make ordinary?

Because here is the terrifying mechanism that Lut is naming: the *nādī* is the place where *al-munkar* — the rejected, the unrecognizable — becomes *maʿrūf*, the recognized. **Public repetition converts the unrecognizable into the familiar.** The thing the heart used to recoil from becomes the thing the heart no longer notices. And when the *nādī* has fully converted *munkar* into *maʿrūf*, the society has lost the ability to even *see* what is destroying it.

This is why Lut's confrontation begins with three layers of grammatical disbelief. Because by the time the *munkar* has become a public spectacle, the society no longer believes it is *munkar* at all. Lut has to use every tool the language has just to reintroduce the people to their own evil.

### Element Three: The Response — *Iʾtinā biʿadhābi-llāh*

And now we come to one of the most chilling sentences in the Quran.

> فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ أَن قَالُوا۟ ٱئْتِنَا بِعَذَابِ ٱللَّهِ
>
> *Fa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū iʾtinā biʿadhābi-llāh.*
>
> *"And the answer of his people was nothing except that they said: Bring us the punishment of Allah."*

Let me show you what Arabic is doing here that English cannot quite carry.

The construction **mā kāna... illā** is the Arabic structure of *exclusive negation*. It means: *there was nothing... except.* It is a way of saying: of all the possible responses a human being could have given — argument, denial, justification, anger, tears, silence — *none of those existed*. Only one response existed. And that response was: bring on the punishment.

Notice something. They did not say: "You are wrong, Lut, we do not do these things." They did not deny the indictment. They did not say: "Yes we do these things and here is why we are right." They did not justify. **They simply skipped over the question of whether the indictment was true and went directly to daring Allah to act.**

This is the psychology of a soul that has crossed a particular line. The line where you are no longer interested in whether you are right. You are only interested in whether the consequence will actually arrive. *I do not care about the truth of what you are saying. I only care whether you can deliver on the threat.*

And look at the verb they use: **iʾtinā** — *bring* — from the same root *atā*. The same verb Lut used to accuse them: *la-taʾtūna r-rijāla*, *wa-taʾtūna fī nādīkumu l-munkar.* They have been *approaching* evil for so long that when they finally speak back to the prophet, the only verb that comes to their tongue is the same verb of *approach* — but now they aim it at Allah. *Let Your punishment approach us, the way we have approached every other evil.*

There is a terrible symmetry in it. The verb that named their corruption becomes the verb of their challenge. The language itself is showing us that a soul which has practiced approaching evil long enough will eventually approach the throne of Allah with that same posture.

And the final phrase: **in kunta mina ṣ-ṣādiqīn** — *if you are of the truthful.* Not even *if you are truthful* — but *if you are among the truthful ones.* They are not asking whether Lut himself is honest. They are asking whether he belongs to a class of beings whose threats actually materialize. They are running a cost-benefit calculation on the cosmos. **They have reduced the prophet to a betting line.**

This is what happens at the final stage of corruption. The prophet, who has been sent in mercy to save them, becomes for them a wager. Either his threat will land or it will not. Either way, they are not interested in changing.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So we have walked through three linguistic discoveries:

The architecture of stacked emphasis — Lut using every grammatical tool to reintroduce a people to the unbelievability of their own evil. The four indictments — sexual inversion, the cutting of every way, and the public installation of *munkar* in the *nādī*. The response — not denial, not argument, but the dare. The reduction of the prophet to a bet.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Conversion of the *Nādī*

Let me start somewhere you already know.

There is a feeling you have had — most of us have had it in the last ten years — of watching something that used to be unthinkable become ordinary. Maybe it was a piece of language that used to be a slur and is now in a magazine headline. Maybe it was a behavior that used to be hidden and is now on a billboard. Maybe it was a kind of consumption that used to be private and is now performed publicly for an audience.

You did not, in most cases, notice the moment it shifted. You noticed only that one day you saw it and shrugged, and you remembered that the year before you would have flinched. **The flinch had gone somewhere.**

That is the conversion of the *nādī*.

The mechanism by which Allah described the people of Lut is not a story locked in the past. It is a description of how every human society, including yours, including mine, processes evil over time. The gathering-place — whatever the gathering-place is in any given era — converts *munkar* into *maʿrūf* through repetition. The thing the heart used to reject becomes the thing the heart no longer registers.

And the most dangerous moment in that conversion is not when the evil is being celebrated. It is the moment **right before celebration, when the evil first becomes neutral.** When you stop hating it but have not yet started loving it. That is the moment the *nādī* has done its work. After that, celebration is a foregone conclusion.

Now look at where the *nādī* is for us. It is no longer the town square. It is the rectangle in your pocket. It is the algorithm that decides which behaviors get rewarded with attention and which behaviors get hidden. It is the feed that, by sheer repetition, is teaching your heart what to flinch at and what to scroll past.

Here is the question. What is *al-munkar* in your *nādī* — and have you noticed that you have stopped flinching at it?

This is not a question with a generic answer. It is a question you have to answer specifically. Because the ayah is not just naming the corruption of Lut's people. **It is teaching you the mechanism so you can recognize the mechanism running inside your own life.**

And here is the practical thing. The work is not to delete the *nādī*. The work is to *reintroduce yourself to the flinch*. To deliberately recall the thing you used to recoil from, and to ask: have I lost the recoil because the thing has gotten less serious, or because I have gotten more numb? The first is rarely true. The second is almost always true.

The flinch is a gift. It is the muscle of the *fiṭra* — the sound human nature Allah designed in you — telling you that *al-munkar* is still *al-munkar*, even when the *nādī* says otherwise.

Lut, peace be upon him, was the man whose flinch had not been killed. That is why he could name the four corruptions. That is why he could see what his entire society had stopped seeing.

**The first practical application:** make a list — written, private — of three things you used to flinch at and no longer do. Then ask, of each one, whether the thing changed or you changed. That is tonight's work.

### Theme Two: The Soul That Dares the Punishment

Now the second theme. This one is harder.

When the people of Lut said *iʾtinā biʿadhābi-llāh* — *bring us the punishment of Allah* — they were doing something specific. They were not just being defiant. They were performing a particular kind of spiritual move that I want you to recognize, because it is more common than you think.

Here is the move: when a person is confronted with a truth about themselves that they cannot refute, and they are not willing to change, **they will often respond by demanding proof that the consequence is real.** "If God really hated this, why am I not punished yet?" "If this were really wrong, surely something would have happened by now." "Show me the consequence, then I'll believe."

Notice what this move does. It transfers the burden of the conversation. The conversation was supposed to be about whether the indictment is true. The move turns it into a conversation about whether the punishment is enforceable. It changes the subject from *the soul* to *the cosmos.*

And it is one of the oldest moves in the human spiritual repertoire. It is the move of Pharaoh — *show me a sign*. It is the move of Quraysh — *bring down a piece of the sky*. It is the move of the people of Lut — *bring us the ʿadhāb*. **It is the move of the soul that has stopped asking whether it is right and started asking only whether it will be caught.**

Why is this move so dangerous? Because Allah, in His mercy, almost never delivers the consequence on demand. The whole structure of the *dunya* is built on delay. Allah gives time. Allah waits. Allah extends. The Quran is full of the language of *imhāl* — granting respite, leaving people to themselves for a while. **The soul that dares the punishment is, in effect, mistaking Allah's mercy for Allah's absence.**

This is the great trick the *nafs* plays on a person. It says: *look, the threat has not materialized. So the threat must not have been real.* But the threat is not unreal. The clock is simply long. The reckoning is not absent. It is delayed. And **the delay is itself the mercy** — the window in which the soul could have turned back, if it had not been so busy testing the timing.

For the people of Lut, the window closed. The ayāt that follow this one in the surah describe what happened — the angels arrived, the city was overturned, the rain of stones fell. They asked for the *ʿadhāb*. The *ʿadhāb* came.

But notice: it did not come the moment they asked. The text continues, the angels go first to Ibrahim, conversations happen, the structure of mercy holds open for one more breath. **Even in the moment they were daring Allah, Allah was still being merciful with the timing.**

What does this teach you?

It teaches you to read your own delays correctly. When you are in the middle of something you know is wrong, and the consequence has not arrived, the question is not "is the consequence real?" The question is: **"is the delay a window or a closing door?"** Allah's silence is almost never permission. It is almost always the open hand of mercy, held out one more time before it closes.

And the people of Lut teach us, by their counter-example, what to do with that delay. *Do not dare the punishment. Use the delay.* The delay is the most precious thing in your life right now, if you are honest about what you have been doing. Every breath that does not bring the consequence is a breath in which you could still turn.

**The second practical application:** identify one thing, right now, that you have been doing — and the consequence has not landed — and you have, somewhere in the back of your mind, been using the non-arrival of the consequence as quiet evidence that the thing is okay. Stop. Read the delay correctly. The delay is the window. The window is not the verdict.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look back at the ayah as a whole. There is a structure here that becomes visible only after we have done the linguistic work.

The ayah is built in **two halves connected by a hinge**.

The first half is Lut speaking. Four indictments — *taʾtūna r-rijāla*, *taqṭaʿūna s-sabīla*, *taʾtūna fī nādīkum*, with *al-munkar* as the climactic fourth. Three acts plus the name of all acts. A widening lens — from a specific corruption, to a corruption of every road, to a corruption of every public space, to the name of corruption itself.

The hinge is the silence Allah leaves between Lut's speech and the response. The phrase *fa-mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā* — *the answer of his people was nothing except* — is structurally the negation of every other possible response. In that negation, Allah is making us *feel* the absence of all the responses that should have been there. Tears. Argument. Even denial. None of them. Only the dare.

The second half is the response. And the response uses the verb of Lut's accusation — *iʾtinā* — to demand Allah's punishment. **The verb circles.** The accusation and the response are bound by the same word. The people of Lut, in their final speech to a prophet, become the grammatical mirror of their own corruption.

This is the architecture: an indictment in four parts, a hinge of silence, and a response that linguistically completes the indictment by aiming its own verb at Allah. The ayah is its own proof of what it is naming.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The three layers of emphatic disbelief that open the ayah — they are the *grammar of a prophet refusing to soften*. The four indictments climaxing in *al-munkar fī n-nādī* — they are the *map of how a society installs evil at its center*. The verb *atā* circling from accusation to dare — it is the *signature of a soul that has practiced approaching evil so long that even its response to Allah is shaped by that practice*.

And the two themes converge on one truth: **when the gathering-place converts the unrecognizable into the familiar, the soul eventually loses the ability to read its own delays.** The people who can no longer see *al-munkar* are the same people who will eventually dare the *ʿadhāb*. The two are not separate. The first produces the second.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is *al-munkar* in my *nādī* — in the feed, in the room, in the conversations I am present for — and when did I stop flinching at it?
2. What did I used to refuse to look at, that I now scroll past without registering? What does that tell me about which way I am moving?
3. Where in my life have I been treating Allah's delay as Allah's permission?
4. If a prophet stood in front of me and listed three indictments, what would the three be? Can I name them before he does?
5. What is one practice that would *restore the flinch* — that would put me back in contact with the part of my *fiṭra* the *nādī* has been wearing down?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When a people install in their public square the very thing the sound heart was made to reject, the prophet's grammar must become as emphatic as their numbness has become deep — and a soul that has practiced approaching evil long enough will, in the end, approach the throne of Allah with that same posture.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to be the people of Lut to be touched by this ayah. The ayah is not a museum piece. It is a diagnostic.

The invitation is to take one of the questions above — just one — and sit with it before sleep tonight. Not to fix anything yet. Just to *look*. To let Lut's emphatic question land in your own life: *a-innakum la-taʾtūna...* — *do you really...?* Whatever fills that blank for you is your work.

The work begins not with the act, but with the recovered flinch.
