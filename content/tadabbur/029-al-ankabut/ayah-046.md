# The Etiquette of Disagreement: How Allah Teaches Us to Argue

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> وَلَا تُجَٰدِلُوٓا۟ أَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ إِلَّا بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ ۖ وَقُولُوٓا۟ ءَامَنَّا بِٱلَّذِىٓ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْنَا وَأُنزِلَ إِلَيْكُمْ وَإِلَٰهُنَا وَإِلَٰهُكُمْ وَٰحِدٌ وَنَحْنُ لَهُۥ مُسْلِمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Wa lā tujādilū ahla-l-kitābi illā billatī hiya aḥsan, illā-lladhīna ẓalamū minhum, wa qūlū āmannā billadhī unzila ilaynā wa unzila ilaykum, wa ilāhunā wa ilāhukum wāḥid, wa naḥnu lahū muslimūn.

**Translation:**
> "And do not argue with the People of the Book except in the way that is best — except for those among them who commit wrong — and say: 'We believe in what was revealed to us and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is One, and to Him we have submitted.'"

---

### Rebuilding the World

Step into Makkah, near the closing of that thirteen-year storm. The believers are small in number, large in pressure. The Prophet ﷺ is meeting daily with people who not only reject his message but mock him, and somewhere in the gathering — perhaps at the edge of the crowd, perhaps as a traveler arriving at the Kaʿbah for trade — there is a different kind of listener. Not an idol-worshipper. A Christian monk. A Jewish merchant. A Najrani priest. **A person who already has a book.**

This is the new question facing the new community. How do you speak to someone who *already believes in revelation*? Who already prays. Who already fasts. Who already says they worship one God. Who carries scripture older than yours and feels — sometimes rightly, sometimes wrongly — that you are the late arrival making claims on their inheritance.

The Quraysh you can confront with the absurdity of stones. But the People of the Book — Allah's words, "ahl al-kitāb," literally *the family of the Book* — these are not strangers to revelation. They are kin to it. And the surah we're in, Al-Ankabut, has spent its breath teaching the believers that **faith is a trial, and trial does not always look like the sword. Sometimes trial looks like the conversation.**

The strand running through this surah is the spider's web — the image of attachments that look like shelter but cannot hold weight. The whole surah is asking: *what holds when you are pressed?* And right here, near the end, Allah turns the camera from the trial of being attacked to the trial of attacking back. From the trial of being wronged to the trial of how you respond.

### The Hook

Allah is about to give the believers a rule about argument — and then immediately within that rule, He gives them an exception, and then within the exception, a closing instruction that contains one of the most generous theological sentences in the entire Quran.

In thirty-one words, He will tell you who to talk to, how to talk to them, when to step back, and what to say when you cannot agree. And tucked inside those instructions is a sentence that — if Muslims took it seriously — would change the texture of every interfaith conversation that has ever happened. [PAUSE]

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to open three things in this ayah: a verb form that changes how you read the whole command, a word that should have been "good" but Allah made superlative, and an exception inside an exception that is so easily misread it has launched whole movements in the wrong direction.

---

### Element One: *Tujādilū* — The Mutual Verb

#### Teaching the Concept First

In Arabic, verbs are not flat. They come in forms — Form I, Form II, Form III, all the way to Form X — and the form is not decoration. The form is part of the meaning.

Form III in Arabic has a very specific flavor. It's called the *mufāʿalah* form, and what it indicates is **mutuality** — that the action goes back and forth between two parties. If Form I is "to know," Form III turns it into "to exchange knowledge with someone." If Form I is "to write," Form III becomes "to correspond." It's the form of *with*. The form of *between*.

You already feel this in English when you hear the difference between "I talked" and "I had a conversation." A conversation requires another person. It implies turn-taking. It implies a back-and-forth.

Hold that.

#### Opening the Word

The verb here is **تُجَٰدِلُوٓا۟** — *tujādilū*. The root is **j-d-l**, which in its base form means to twist, to braid, to weave together (the word *jadīlah* means a braid of hair). The image is two strands twisting against each other.

But Allah did not say *tajdulū* — Form I. He said *tujādilū* — Form III.

#### Sitting With It

This matters more than it seems. [PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah is not prohibiting *speaking* to the People of the Book. He is not prohibiting *teaching* them. He is not prohibiting *inviting* them. The word He chose specifically refers to the back-and-forth, the twisting of one argument against another, the conversation that has become a contest.

Stay with me. There is no Quranic prohibition here on conversation. There is no prohibition on disagreement. There is no prohibition on dialogue. **What Allah is regulating is specifically the moment the conversation becomes a contest of wills.** The moment the two strands start twisting against each other to see which one is stronger.

That moment — Allah does not forbid. He *qualifies*. "Do not engage in that twisting *except* in the way that is best."

Here is what's remarkable. The form of the verb already contains the warning. *Tujādilū* implies there is someone on the other side of you. Someone who feels what you say. Someone who can be wounded by how you say it. Someone whose response is part of the action. The Form III verb itself is reminding you: **this is not a monologue you are about to deliver. This is two strands twisting together, and the way you twist determines what kind of rope you make — or whether the rope breaks.**

[PAUSE]

So what one word reveals is this: Allah is not legislating silence. He is legislating *how* the encounter happens — because He chose a verb that already knew the encounter was mutual.

---

### Element Two: *Aḥsan* — Not "Good," but "Most Beautiful"

#### Teaching the Concept First

Arabic, like English, has degrees. Good. Better. Best. But Arabic does something English doesn't quite do. Its superlative form — *afʿal* — can mean both *better* and *the most beautiful*. It carries a sense of completion, of having reached the peak. When you say *aḥsan*, you are not just saying "more good" — you are reaching for the upper limit of a quality.

And the root itself, **ḥ-s-n**, is not just "good" in the way "good" is in English. *Ḥusn* in Arabic carries the meaning of **beauty**. Of aesthetic excellence. Of something being pleasing to behold. It's the word used for physical beauty, for moral beauty, for the beauty of a deed.

When the Prophet ﷺ defined *iḥsān* — the noun from the same root — he said it is "to worship Allah as though you see Him." *Iḥsān* is the peak. It is the third tier above *islām* and *īmān*. It is excellence so complete that you act as if the Beloved is watching.

Hold that. Because Allah is about to use a word from that same family to describe how you should argue.

#### Opening the Word

The phrase is **بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ** — *billatī hiya aḥsan*. "With that which is *aḥsan*."

Now ask: aḥsan than what?

This is one of those places where Arabic grammar leaves the comparison open on purpose. *Aḥsan* is comparative-superlative without a stated counterpart. Most beautiful — *compared to what?*

#### Sitting With It

Let's sit with this. [PAUSE]

Allah did not say "argue with them with what is *ḥasan*" — what is good. He said with what is **aḥsan** — the most beautiful, the most excellent. He did not set the floor. **He set the ceiling.**

Why does that matter?

Because in every disagreement you've ever had, there were many ways to make your point that would have been *good enough*. Many ways that would have been *true*. Many ways that would have been *defensible*. You could have argued and not lied. You could have argued and not insulted. You could have argued and been right.

Allah is not asking for *good enough*. He is asking for *the most beautiful version of the conversation you are capable of having*.

Notice something. This is the same word family — *ḥ-s-n* — that gives us *iḥsān*. The word the Prophet ﷺ used to mean "worship Allah as if you see Him." Allah is telling the believers that **the way you argue is itself a form of worship**. That the choice of word, the softness of tone, the patience with interruption, the willingness to grant the strongest version of the other person's case — these are not optional. They are not strategy. **They are devotion.**

Here's what's remarkable. The conversation with the People of the Book is being held to the highest aesthetic standard in the Quranic vocabulary. Not the standard of *truth* alone — though truth is assumed. Not the standard of *correctness* — though correctness matters. The standard of **beauty**. As if Allah is saying: *the truth I have given you is so beautiful that ugly delivery would betray it.* [PAUSE]

So what this single word reveals is this: in a disagreement about Allah, the *manner* of speech is not separate from the *content* of speech. Speak about beauty beautifully — or you have already misrepresented the Beauty you claim.

### Element Three: The Two *Illā*s — Exception Inside Exception

#### Teaching the Concept First

Arabic has a small word that does very large work: **إِلَّا** — *illā*, "except." It is the exceptive particle. In any rule of the form "do X except Y," the *except* clause carves out a piece that the main rule does not reach.

What is unusual in this ayah is that Allah uses *illā* **twice** — and the two exceptions are doing very different work. One restricts a *method*. The other restricts a *category of person*. And if you confuse them, you will read the whole verse wrong.

This is exactly the kind of place where careful reading matters most — because the looser reading has historically been used to flatten the careful one.

#### Opening the Construction

Here are the two clauses:

**First *illā***: "...do not argue with the People of the Book **except** in the way that is best..." (*illā billatī hiya aḥsan*)

**Second *illā***: "...**except** those among them who commit wrong (ẓulm)..." (*illā-lladhīna ẓalamū minhum*)

#### Sitting With It

The first exception is *about method*. It is the eye of the needle through which all conversation with the People of the Book must pass. You may argue, but only via the most beautiful manner. This *illā* narrows the *how*.

The second exception is *about persons*. Within the People of the Book, there is a sub-category — those who have committed *ẓulm*, who have *wronged* — and they are pulled out of the main rule.

Now here is where the reading must slow down. [PAUSE]

What is the second *illā* exempting? Is it exempting them *from being argued with at all*? Is it exempting them *from being argued with beautifully*?

The classical scholars sat with this. Some, like Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī, held that the exception means: *with those who have wronged, the constraint of beautiful argument no longer fully applies* — meaning the believer is permitted firmness, even confrontation, when meeting persistent injustice. Others, like Mujāhid and others among the early scholars, read the wronged ones as those who actively waged war or refused to even sit in conversation — these are no longer in the realm of *jidāl* at all, they are in a different category, the category of conflict.

But sit with what *all* the classical readings share. **The exception is narrow.** It is not "those who disagree with you." It is not "those who refuse Islam." It is not "those whose theology you find objectionable." The exception is for *ẓalamū* — those who have committed *ẓulm*. And *ẓulm* in the Quranic vocabulary is a heavy word. It means to put a thing where it does not belong. To transgress a boundary. To commit injustice, oppression, aggression.

Here is what's remarkable about this construction. Allah's rule is **the most beautiful manner**. Allah's exception is **active wrongdoing**. That means the *default* for engaging with every Jewish, Christian, Sabean, or scriptural-tradition person you encounter is *aḥsan*. Beauty. Excellence. The exception requires demonstration of *ẓulm* before it activates. **The burden of proof is on the exception, not on the rule.**

And notice something even sharper. The phrase is *illā-lladhīna ẓalamū **minhum*** — "except those *among them* who have wronged." That word *minhum*, "among them," is doing crucial work. It tells you that the wrongdoers are a subset, not a synonym for the group. Allah is refusing to let the believer collapse "the People of the Book" into "the wrongdoers among the People of the Book." **The category is preserved as honorable; only the violators are exempted.**

Composite sketch from the classical sources: this *illā* is a release valve for situations of *active aggression* — not a license for the believer to abandon *aḥsan* the moment they meet a difficult conversation partner. Some classical scholars even read it as so narrow it applies only to those who declared open war. The exception was meant to be rare.

[PAUSE]

So what this double-*illā* construction reveals is this: Allah builds the most beautiful manner as the *default*, then creates a narrow exit for genuine aggression, then signals — through *minhum* — that even the exception must be specifically located and named, not generalized. **The rule is wide; the exception is small; the exception inside the exception is smaller still.**

---

### The Bridge

Three things have appeared. A verb form (*tujādilū*) that reminded us this is mutual — the other side is alive, responding, can be wounded. A word (*aḥsan*) that demanded not the floor of acceptable speech but the ceiling of beautiful speech. And a double exception that protected the category while admitting only the narrowest exit for actual wrongdoing.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

We're going to open two themes here. The first is about the modern crisis of how we disagree. The second is the closing instruction of the ayah — that sentence I told you about. The one that could change every interfaith conversation if Muslims took it seriously.

---

### Theme One: The Crisis of How We Argue

#### Begin Where the Listener Lives

Open your phone right now. Scroll through any comment section. Watch a quote-tweet. Sit in any political conversation that has spilled past polite drinks into the territory where someone's voice is rising.

What is the dominant emotional texture of disagreement in our age?

It is *winning*. It is *clip-ability*. It is the dunk, the gotcha, the screenshot that humiliates. It is the ratio. It is the take-down. Whole careers are now built on the aesthetic of *demolishing* an opponent. Whole platforms are engineered to reward heat over light. We have an entire economic infrastructure built around the *opposite* of what Allah commanded in this verse.

And in this economy, the Muslim is not exempt. Look at the YouTube channels where Muslims debate Christians and atheists. Some of them are beautiful. Some of them are *useful*. But many of them — and the comment sections beneath them — are not *aḥsan*. They are *aghlab* — most dominant. *Akhzā* — most humiliating. They reward the speaker who reduces the other person to silence, regardless of the texture of how that silence was achieved.

We have unlearned something. And what Allah is teaching in this ayah is the thing we have unlearned.

#### Let the Ayah Illuminate

Allah did not say *win* the argument. Allah did not say *defeat* the People of the Book. Allah said: argue **in the manner that is most beautiful**.

This implies — and stay with this — that **the goal of the argument is not the conclusion of the argument**. If the goal were merely to land a point, *good enough* speech would do. Any speech that delivered the truth would suffice. But Allah is reaching past the conclusion. He is shaping the *manner* itself as a separate object of worship.

Why? Because the person on the other side of the conversation is also a creation of Allah. Because that person carries an *amānah*, a trust, of their own soul. Because the way you speak to them is *itself* a witness to the God you claim. **A Muslim who wins an argument by humiliating someone has not made the case for Islam — they have made the case against it.**

Notice something deeper. The verb *tujādilū* — that Form III mutual verb we opened in Part 1 — has already told us that the other person is *alive* in the conversation. They have a response. They have a heart. The Form III is not violent. It is *braiding*, not stabbing. Allah chose a verb whose root meaning is *interweaving*, then told us to do the interweaving in the most beautiful way.

So picture it. Two strands. One is yours. One is theirs. They are not the same — they twist in different directions, they hold different truths, they come from different histories. But *the braid* is what Allah is interested in. The *encounter*. The shape that emerges when two people meet around the most important questions.

#### Deepening Through Movement

There is a paradox here that we must not rush past.

The ayah is not asking the believer to *abandon* their position. It is not telling you to pretend you agree with what you do not agree with. It is not telling you that all paths lead to the same destination. The very next breath — which we are about to open — is one of the most direct affirmations of *tawḥīd* in the Quran. **The conviction is not softened.**

What softens is the *manner*. What sharpens is the *self-discipline*. Allah is not asking you to be less certain. He is asking you to be more beautiful.

This is hard. It is hard because the human ego *loves* the dunk. The ego loves the moment of being right, and being seen to be right, and seeing the other person realize they were wrong. The ego loves the rope twisting tighter. **The aḥsan command is a direct assault on the part of you that wants to win.**

And here is the spiritual texture that the modern reader might miss. The Prophet ﷺ never won an argument by humiliation. Not once. He won souls by *adab* — by the texture of his presence. When ʿIkrimah ibn Abī Jahl came to embrace Islam, the Prophet ﷺ rose, removed his cloak, spread it for him to sit on, and said, "Welcome, O migrant rider." This is the man whose father had spent his life trying to kill the Prophet. *And the Prophet ﷺ rose for his son.* That gesture made more cases for Islam than a thousand verbal victories.

#### Connect to Universal Struggle

So the question this theme leaves us with is not "how should I argue with non-Muslims." That is too small. The question is: **what is my relationship with the goal of winning?**

In your marriage. In your family. With your siblings. With colleagues. In the comment section. In the WhatsApp group. In the disagreement you are mid-stream in right now.

Is your conversation a *braid* you are weaving with another soul, or is it a *rope* you are tying around their throat?

[PAUSE]

The practical application here is small but immovable. Before any disagreement, ask one question: *am I trying to be right, or am I trying to be beautiful?* If the answer is the first, the ayah has already corrected you.

### Theme Two: The Sentence That Could Change Everything

#### Begin Where the Listener Lives

There is a sentence at the end of this ayah that, in a healthier history of Muslim engagement with the People of the Book, would be the most quoted sentence in the entire Quran in interfaith spaces. **It is almost never quoted.**

Most Muslims, when asked what the Quran says about Christians and Jews, will reach for the polemic verses. Most non-Muslims, when asked what they think the Quran says about them, will repeat verses out of context that someone showed them on YouTube. **And almost no one — Muslim or non-Muslim — will reach for this one.**

Listen to it again:

> "And say: **'We believe in what was revealed to us and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is One, and to Him we have submitted.'**"

Sit with what Allah just made the believer say. [PAUSE]

#### Let the Ayah Illuminate

Three claims are made in this single sentence, in a precise order, and the order is everything.

**First**: "We believe in what was revealed to us and what was revealed to you." Notice the symmetry. *Unzila ilaynā wa unzila ilaykum*. The verb is the same. The structure is the same. The Quranic claim is that the same *active reality* — divine revelation, *tanzīl* — is what gave the believer their book and what gave the People of the Book theirs. **The categories are parallel.** Allah does not say "we believe in what was revealed to us, and we acknowledge that you have something." He says, *we believe in what was revealed to you*. Belief. In their revelation. Not in its current state of preservation, not in every detail as they currently hold it, but in the foundational fact that **Allah spoke to them too**.

**Second**: "Our God and your God is One." *Wa ilāhunā wa ilāhukum wāḥid*. The pronouns repeat — *unā* (ours), *ukum* (yours). Allah is not saying "our God is also your God whether you like it or not." He is saying, *the God you worship and the God we worship is, in reality, one and the same*. The same Being. The same Reality. The God of Abraham. The God of Moses. The God of Jesus. The God of Muhammad ﷺ. **One.**

**Third**: "And to Him we have submitted." *Wa naḥnu lahū muslimūn*. This is where the believer's distinctive identity comes in. Notice it comes *last*. After the shared revelation. After the shared God. The Muslim identity is articulated as the *response* to a reality that is already shared, not as a *replacement* for it.

#### Deepening Through Movement

Why does the order matter so much?

Because most religious arguments begin by establishing *what makes us different*. Allah, in this verse, instructs the believer to begin by establishing *what we share*.

Stay with that. The Muslim, encountering the Christian, the Jew, the Sabean, is not instructed to lead with the disagreement. The Muslim is instructed to lead with the *foundation* — the shared God, the shared phenomenon of revelation, the shared reality of being addressed by the Divine. Only after that foundation is laid does the believer say *and we have submitted*. The submission — *islām* — is the Muslim's distinctive, but it is articulated against the backdrop of vast common ground.

Notice something even more remarkable. Allah does not even use the standard polemical tools that were available in the moment. He could have said: *and say to them, your book is corrupted*. He could have said: *say to them, you have associated partners with Allah*. These critiques exist elsewhere in the Quran, and they exist with specificity and force. But here, in this particular verse, in the moment of *jidāl* — when the believer is most tempted to lead with the critique — Allah pulls the believer back and instructs: **lead with what you share**.

Why? Because the goal is not to *defeat* the other. The goal is to *invite* them. And invitation does not begin at the door of difference. Invitation begins at the door of *kinship*.

The third movement here is theological. The sentence ends with *wa naḥnu lahū muslimūn* — "and to Him we have submitted." The word *muslimūn* is, of course, the word from which "Muslim" comes. But *islām* in its Quranic register is a verb before it is a noun. It is the act of *submitting* — of saying *lahū*, "to Him" — only Him, not to any partner, any associate, any rival claim. So the believer's identity, in the closing of this sentence, is articulated as a *posture* before God, not as a *team* against the other group.

This is shattering, if you let it land. The Muslim's distinctive — the act of submission to the One God whom you have already affirmed we share — is being offered to the other person not as a wall but as a door. *We share the God. We share the phenomenon of revelation. And the natural response to those two shared truths is the submission we have entered. We invite you into the same.*

#### The Paradox

Now, hold the paradox. This is not religious relativism. This is not "all paths are equal." Notice what Allah *did not* say. He did not say *and to Him you have submitted too*. He marked the submission as *ours*: *naḥnu*. *We*. The distinctive is preserved. The conviction is intact.

What is dissolved is not the conviction. What is dissolved is the *posture of hostility*. The conviction can be held — and is held — *while* leading with shared ground. **This is the genius of the verse. It refuses to make the believer choose between conviction and kindness, between truth and beauty, between firmness and warmth.** It says you can have all of them at once, in this exact order, in these exact words.

#### Connect to Universal Struggle

So what does this change?

It changes the texture of every conversation a Muslim might have with a Christian colleague, a Jewish neighbor, a religious-curious friend. It changes how a Muslim youth on campus responds when an atheism club asks about the Quran. It changes what a Muslim parent teaches a child when the child asks why the Sunday-school friend is "different."

The Quranic instruction is: do not lead with the difference. Lead with the shared God. Lead with the shared phenomenon of revelation. Lead with the kinship that is older than the disagreement. *Then* — and only then — articulate what is distinctive in your own posture before God.

[PAUSE]

The practical application is concrete. The next time you are in a religious conversation with someone of the People of the Book — or someone who comes from any monotheistic tradition — try, just once, leading with this sentence. *I believe in what was revealed to me. I believe in what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is one. And we have submitted to Him.* Watch what happens. Watch whether the rope twists tighter, or whether the braid begins to take shape.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look back at the whole ayah. Notice the shape:

| Movement | What Allah Does |
|---|---|
| **Command** | Do not engage in mutual argument (*tujādilū*)... |
| **Method** | ...except in the most beautiful manner (*aḥsan*)... |
| **Exception** | ...except those who have wronged (*ẓalamū*) among them... |
| **Instruction** | ...and say: |
| **Shared revelation** | We believe in what was revealed to us and to you |
| **Shared God** | Our God and your God is One |
| **Distinctive submission** | And to Him we have submitted |

See the structure? Allah builds *outward from constraint to invitation*. The verse begins as a prohibition, narrows into a manner, names a narrow exception, and then **opens** — *and say* — into one of the most generous theological sentences in the entire Quran.

The architecture itself is the lesson. Allah moves the believer **from restraint to relation, from manner to message, from how-not-to-argue to what-to-say-instead**. The verse refuses to be merely prohibitive. It hands the believer not just a rule but a *script*.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and thematic threads converge on a single point. The Form III verb told us the encounter is mutual — there is a person there, alive and responding. The superlative *aḥsan* told us the standard is beauty, not adequacy. The double *illā* told us the rule is wide and the exception is narrow. And the closing sentence told us the *content* the beauty is meant to carry: shared revelation, shared God, distinctive submission offered as invitation.

It is all the same insight, viewed from two directions. **The manner of speech is part of the message of speech. And the message Allah gave is so beautiful that ugly delivery is not just bad strategy — it is theological betrayal.**

### Questions to Carry

1. In your last serious disagreement, were you trying to be *ḥasan* — good — or were you trying to be *aḥsan* — most beautiful? What is the difference between those two, in your specific case?

2. When you encounter people of other Abrahamic faiths, do you instinctively lead with what you share, or what you dispute? What would change if you reversed the order?

3. The double *illā* makes the exception narrow and specific. Are there people in your life you have placed in the "exception" category — perhaps a sibling, a parent, a colleague — when they actually belong in the "rule" category?

4. *Iḥsān* — from the same root as *aḥsan* — is the highest tier of religious experience: to worship Allah as if you see Him. If you brought that same standard of *as if I see Him* into your next argument, what would change?

5. The sentence *"Our God and your God is One"* is in your scripture. When did you last say it out loud to someone who was not Muslim?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah forbids the believer from making the truth ugly through how it is delivered, and replaces the ugly delivery with a script of shared revelation, shared God, and distinctive submission offered as invitation.

### The Closing Invitation

Something should shift. Not a vow. An invitation. The next time you feel the rope twisting — in a comment section, in a conversation, in a private debate inside your own head with someone who is not there — pause. Ask: *am I being aḥsan, or just ḥasan? Am I being beautiful, or just defensible?*

And if you find yourself in conversation with someone of the Book — a Christian friend, a Jewish coworker, anyone whose tradition is woven from the same scriptural thread — try, just once, leading with what Allah told the believers to say.

*I believe in what was revealed to me and what was revealed to you. Our God and your God is One. And to Him I have submitted.*

See what door that opens.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who taught us that the truth is so beautiful that ugly delivery betrays it — make our speech *aḥsan*. Make our disagreement a braid and not a noose. Let the next conversation we have about You be one You would be pleased to overhear. And let us be among those who, when they meet a person of the Book, lead with the kinship before the difference, with the shared God before the distinctive submission, with the door of invitation before the wall of dispute. Āmīn.
