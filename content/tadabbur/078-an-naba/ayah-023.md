# The Ages That Keep Arriving

## Surah An-Naba, Ayah 23

> لَّٰبِثِينَ فِيهَآ أَحْقَابًا
>
> *Lābithīna fīhā aḥqābā.*
>
> **"Remaining therein for ages."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question Behind the Question

Let me begin not with this verse, but with the question this entire surah is built to answer.

Surah An-Naba opens with something strange. Allah does not begin by stating a fact. He begins by overhearing a conversation. **"About what are they asking one another? About the great news."** ʿAmma yatasāʾalūn. ʿAni-n-nabaʾi-l-ʿaẓīm.

There were people in Makkah standing in the marketplace, leaning against the walls of the Kaʿba, turning to one another and saying: *Do you really believe we'll be raised again? Bones brought back to life? After we've become dust?* It was the gossip of the city. It was the thing they couldn't stop circling back to — half-mocking, half-uneasy. **The great news.**

And the entire surah is Allah's response to that whispered conversation. He doesn't argue with them like a philosopher. He takes them by the hand and walks them through the world they already live in. *Did We not make the earth a resting place? And the mountains as pegs? And created you in pairs? And made your sleep a rest? And the night a covering? And the day for livelihood?* He builds the case from the ground they're standing on. The God who did all of this — you think He cannot do the other thing?

And then the surah turns. There is a Day of Decision. A trumpet. The sky torn open into gates. The mountains set moving until they're a mirage.

And then we arrive at the consequence. There is a place that **lies in wait** — Hell, described as *mirṣād*, an ambush, a checkpoint that nothing passes unnoticed. It is *maʾāb* — a homecoming — for those who transgressed every limit.

[PAUSE]

And here is where our verse lands. Allah has told us *where* they go. Now He tells us, in three words, *how long*, and *in what state*.

> **Lābithīna fīhā aḥqābā.**

Three words. And I want to tell you something before we begin. Most of us, when we read a verse about Hell, brace ourselves for fire, for chains, for boiling water. Those come — in the very next verses. But this verse does not name a single instrument of pain.

**This verse is about time.**

And I want to suggest to you, by the end of our hour together, that time itself — the bare passage of duration — is being presented here as part of the sentence. Not the heat. Not the thirst. The *clock*. The thing you and I are most desperate to kill on an ordinary afternoon, the thing we "pass" and "spend" and "waste" — here it is handed back to us in a form we were never built to carry.

Stay with me. Let's open these three words slowly.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Lābithīna* — The Word That Is a State, Not an Event

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In English, we mostly think in verbs. *He stayed. He is staying. He will stay.* The verb tells you *when* — past, present, future. It pins the action to a point on a timeline.

But Arabic has another kind of word, and it does something English struggles to do in a single stroke. It is called the *ism fāʿil* — the active participle. Instead of saying "he stays," it names the *doer of the staying*. It turns the action into a quality of the person. The closest English gets is words like "a sleeper," "a dweller," "a resident." Notice what those words do: a "sleeper" isn't someone who slept once. It's someone for whom sleeping has become a description, a category, an identity.

Now here is the thing about the participle: **it floats free of time.** It doesn't tell you when. It tells you *what kind*. It describes a settled, ongoing condition.

Allah did not say *labithū* — "they stayed." He did not say *yalbathūn* — "they will stay." He chose the participle: **lābithīn — the ones who are remainers.**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with that choice.

When you use a verb, you imply a beginning and a possible end. "He stayed" — and then, presumably, he left. The verb has edges. But the participle has no edges. It is not an event that happens to them. **It is what they have become.** The transgressor doesn't *do* staying. He *is* a stayer. The way you might say someone is a smoker, a believer, a king — it's not what they did this morning, it's what defines them.

Notice something else. The grammarians call this word here a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial accusative. It answers the question: *in what state?* When the verses before said Hell is a homecoming for the transgressors, the natural question is — *and they will be there how?* In what condition? And the answer comes: **lābithīn.** As remainers. The state in which they arrive is the state of one who is not leaving.

And here is a detail the great commentator al-Jalālayn catches, and it stopped me when I first read it. He calls this a *ḥāl muqaddara*. A *destined* circumstantial state. Not a state they happen to be in — a state that was *decreed* for them, written in advance, the predetermined shape of their being there.

Think about what that means. The "remaining" is not an accident of their situation, something that just turned out to last a long time. **It is the appointed design.** Before they ever arrived, the staying was already written as the very form of their arrival.

[PAUSE]

And one more layer, because the early reciters preserved something here. Al-Baghawī records that Ḥamza and Yaʿqūb read this word as *labithīn* — without the long *alif* — while the majority read *lābithīn*, with it. He tells us these are simply two dialectal forms of the same meaning. But listen to what the two forms do to your mouth. *Lābithīn* — the *alif* stretches, the word itself lingers, it draws out its own vowel the way the staying draws out the soul. **The word for "remaining" makes you remain on it.** Even the shorter reading, *labithīn*, sits heavy. The Arabs who first heard this did not just understand "staying." They *felt* a word that would not let go of the tongue.

So before we even reach the second word, Allah has told us: this is not something that happens to them. It is who they now are. The remainers. The dwellers. And it was written that way.

What this single word reveals: **the punishment is not first an action done to a person — it is a transformation of the person into the kind of being who stays.**

### Element Two: *Aḥqāban* — The Plural That Counts Forever

Now the second word. And this is the heart of the verse.

> **lābithīna fīhā aḥqābā** — remaining therein for *aḥqāb*.

Let me teach the structure first. The word *ḥuqb* (the singular) means a vast stretch of time — an age, an epoch, a long era. The classical sources give us numbers, and I'll come to those, but hold the bare meaning first: a *ḥuqb* is already an enormous span. Not a year. Not a generation. An *age*.

And Allah did not say *ḥuqban* — one age. He said *aḥqāban*. **The plural.** Ages. Age upon age upon age.

Now here is where the commentators do something I find breathtaking, and I want to walk you through their reasoning carefully, because there is a real theological question buried here, and the early scholars saw it clearly.

When you hear "for ages," a part of the mind quietly relaxes. *Ages* sounds enormous — but enormous things end. A mountain is enormous, and it erodes. An age, by definition, is a period — and periods conclude. So a careless reader might hear *aḥqāban* and think: *long, yes — but bounded. Eventually the ages run out.*

The mufassirūn refused to let that misreading stand. Listen to al-Qurṭubī. He says: *they remain in the Fire as long as the ages last — and the ages do not cease, for whenever one ḥaqib passes, another ḥaqib comes.* **Kullamā maḍā ḥuqbun jāʾa ḥuqbun.** Whenever one age is spent, another arrives.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what he just did? He took the word that sounded like it had an end — and showed you that the *plural* is precisely what removes the end. Al-Jalālayn says it directly: *aḥqāban — dahūran lā nihāyata lahā.* Ages with no end. Al-Muyassar: *dahūran mutaʿāqibatan lā tanqaṭiʿ* — successive ages that never break off.

So the structure is this: each age is finite. You could, in principle, count it. Eighty years, the reports say. Or seventy autumns of seven hundred years each — the numbers vary, and I'll come to them. **Every single age is a number you could write down.** But the moment that number is exhausted — the moment you reach the end of the counting — a new age begins. And it too is finite. And it too ends. And it too is replaced.

Why does this matter so much? Because of what it does to the *mind*.

An undifferentiated "forever" is almost merciful, in a way. The mind cannot grasp it, so it goes numb. It rounds it off to a blur. But Allah did not give the transgressor a blur. **He gave him a counter.** He gave him ages — discrete, countable, ending — so that there is always the false dawn of an ending in view, and always, the instant it arrives, the next age begins.

It is the cruelty — and I use that word carefully, as *ʿadl*, as exact justice — of a sentence measured in units that keep resetting. You count down to the end of the age. You reach it. And the clock starts over. And al-Muyassar will not let us mistake this for arbitrary torment: he glosses the whole passage as *jazāʾ ʿādil* — a recompense measured to *match* the very deeds they performed in the world, *muwāfiqan li-aʿmālihim*. The endlessness is not excess spilling past the crime. It answers something. The unending ages are calibrated to a life that was lived as though there would be no reckoning at all.

[PAUSE]

Now, about those numbers. Let me be honest with you about what the tradition holds, because the precision is itself part of the meaning. Al-Baghawī reports — and this is narrated *from* ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, may Allah be pleased with him, as an *athar*, a companion-report, not a saying traced to the Prophet ﷺ — that a single *ḥuqb* is eighty years, each year twelve months, each month thirty days, **and each day is a thousand years.** Mujāhid is reported to have given a different reckoning: forty-three *ḥuqub*, each *ḥuqub* seventy autumns, each autumn seven hundred years, each year three hundred and sixty days, each day a thousand years. Al-Ḥasan held yet another view.

Notice the *ikhtilāf* — the scholars differed on the exact arithmetic. That difference is not a weakness in the tradition; it is the tradition being honest. **No one was given the final number, because there is no final number.** The point of the staggering arithmetic is not to land on a sum. It is to start you counting and let you feel your mind exhaust itself before the count does. You multiply days into thousands of years, years into months, months into days, ages into ages — and at the end of all your multiplication, al-Qurṭubī is still standing there saying: *and then another age comes.*

And now — the sound of the word. Say it. *Aḥqāb.* Feel where it sits in your throat. The *ḥ* is a breath pushed from deep in the chest. The *q* is a hard stop at the very back, the deepest consonant in the language, the one that catches and blocks. **Aḥ-qāb.** The word itself catches in the throat. It does not flow. It stops. A word about ages that will not end is built from a sound that keeps getting blocked, that you cannot say smoothly, that lodges in the deepest part of you and refuses to release. The mouth performs the meaning.

What this word reveals: **eternity here is not described as a smooth forever, but as ages that keep arriving — so that the one who waits is never permitted the mercy of giving up hope all at once. He must lose it again, and again, and again, at the end of every age.**

### Element Three: *Fīhā* — The Small Word Pointing Back at the Ambush

I want to pause on the smallest word in the verse, because it is doing quiet work. *Fīhā.* "Therein." In *what*?

Go back two verses. **Inna jahannama kānat mirṣādā.** Hell was *mirṣād*. We reach too quickly for "ambush" and move on — but al-Ṭabarī slows us down. He renders it *dhāt raṣd*, *dhāt irtiqāb* — a thing that *possesses watching*, a sentinel that watches whoever crosses it and lies in wait for them. Hold that. Hell is not a passive pit one stumbles into; in al-Ṭabarī's reading it is *awake*. It watches the road. Al-Jalālayn records the two ways the very word can be heard — *rāṣida*, the one who watches, or *murṣada*, the place set up as a watch-post — and in truth it is both at once: the watcher and the watchtower. And al-Ṭabarī adds a detail that should make every one of us go still: it lies in wait not only for its own deniers but for *everyone who must pass that way*, the believer crossing over no less than the transgressor. *Lil-ṭāghīna maʾābā* — and for the transgressors, that watching-place is a homecoming, a place of return.

So when the verse says *fīhā* — "therein" — the *hā* reaches back and grabs that word. They remain *in the ambush*. They remain *in the watchtower*. They remain *in the homecoming*.

[PAUSE]

Hold all of those together. *Mirṣād* — the place that was watching for them. *Maʾāb* — the place they *return* to, as though it were home. And now *fīhā* — they stay *in* it, for ages.

There is something devastating in the choice of *maʾāb*, "homecoming," for a place of punishment. A homecoming is supposed to be the end of a journey, the place where the staying is sweet. The transgressor *does* get a homecoming. He *does* get a place to finally remain, to stop wandering, to settle. **The tragedy is that he settles into the ambush.** The rest he was running toward all his life — the place to finally stop — turns out to be this.

And *fīhā* — "therein" — seals him inside it. The participle made him a remainer. The plural made the remaining endless. And this little pronoun locates it: inside the very thing that was lying in wait for him the whole time.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have built.

*Lābithīn* — they have become remainers; it is no longer something they do but something they are, and it was decreed as the form of their being there.

*Aḥqāban* — and the remaining is measured in ages that end only to begin again, a clock that resets at the very instant of relief.

*Fīhā* — inside the ambush that called itself their home.

The linguistic construction has shown us the *architecture* of the sentence. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About time, and about us.
