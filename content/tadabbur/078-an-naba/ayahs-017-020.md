# The Appointment That Was Already on the Calendar

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with the sound of them before we touch the meaning.

> [ayah:78:17] إِنَّ يَوْمَ ٱلْفَصْلِ كَانَ مِيقَـٰتًا
> *Inna yawma-l-fasli kāna mīqātā.*
> **"Indeed, the Day of Decision is an appointed time."**

> [ayah:78:18] يَوْمَ يُنفَخُ فِى ٱلصُّورِ فَتَأْتُونَ أَفْوَاجًا
> *Yawma yunfakhu fi-s-sūri fa-ta'tūna afwājā.*
> **"The Day the Trumpet is blown, and you will come forth in throngs."**

> [ayah:78:19] وَفُتِحَتِ ٱلسَّمَآءُ فَكَانَتْ أَبْوَٰبًا
> *Wa futiḥati-s-samā'u fa-kānat abwābā.*
> **"And the sky is opened, and becomes gateways."**

> [ayah:78:20] وَسُيِّرَتِ ٱلْجِبَالُ فَكَانَتْ سَرَابًا
> *Wa suyyirati-l-jibālu fa-kānat sarābā.*
> **"And the mountains are set moving, and become a mirage."**

Now let's rebuild the room these words first landed in.

Picture Makkah, early. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling people to a single, scandalous idea: that they will be raised again. That the dust they will become will be reassembled. That there is a Day coming when the books are opened and the accounts are settled. And the response in the streets is not quiet disagreement. It is argument. The surah opens by capturing the very sound of that argument: *"About what are they asking one another? About the great announcement — the one over which they are in dispute."* (78:1–3)

They are talking about it at the wells, in the markets, around the fires. *Did you hear what he claims? That bones will live? That the sky will be torn?* It is the topic that will not die. And to that arguing, mocking, dismissive crowd, Allah does not begin with the Fire. He begins with the earth under their feet. *Did We not make the earth a cradle? And the mountains pegs? And create you in pairs? And make your sleep a rest? And the night a covering? And the day for livelihood? And build above you seven mighty heavens?* (78:6–12)

He walks them through the world they trust. The solid ground. The unmovable mountains. The reliable rhythm of sleeping and waking. The sky that has never once fallen.

And then — these four ayat. **The pivot.** Having shown them the world they think is permanent, He turns and tells them exactly what will become of it.

Here is the hook, and I want you to hold it for the next hour. In the very first sentence, Allah speaks of a Day that has not yet come — and He uses the past tense. He says the Day of Decision **"was"** an appointed time. *Kāna.* Was. Not "will be." And by the end of these four verses, the mountains those deniers were leaning on — the mountains He had just called *pegs* holding the earth steady — become a **mirage.**

A mirage. The emptiest thing the desert offers. The thing that looks like water to a dying man and is nothing at all.

How does the most solid thing you know become the least real thing you can name? And why does Allah speak of tomorrow as though it already happened? [PAUSE]

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Kāna* — When Allah Speaks of Tomorrow in the Past Tense

Let me teach you something about your own language before we open the Arabic.

When you are absolutely certain something will happen, watch how your speech shifts. A coach at halftime, down by twenty, does not say "we might win." He says "we've already won this — they just don't know it yet." A grandmother says of her newborn grandson, "he's a doctor, this one." He is three days old. She is not confused about the calendar. She is expressing a certainty so total that the future has, for her, already arrived. We collapse the future into the past **when doubt is gone.**

Hold that. Now look at the word.

> **كَانَ مِيقَٰتًا** — *kāna mīqātā* — "**was** an appointed time."

*Kāna* is the past tense. Root **k-w-n**, the verb of being, of having-been. In ordinary Arabic, *kāna* takes you backward in time: *kāna rajulun* — "there was a man." And yet here it is fastened to a Day that, for the original listeners, was entirely in the future. The Day of Decision **was** an appointment.

Notice something. Allah is not careless with tense. This is the same Speech that elsewhere uses the precise future. The choice of the perfect tense here is deliberate, and the classical commentators read it exactly as your grandmother's certainty reads. Al-Jalalayn glosses it plainly: *kāna* here means *waqtan* — it simply **is** a fixed time, established, settled. Al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi say the same: it is *waqtan wa mīʿādan* — a time and a promised meeting — for the reward and the punishment Allah has pledged.

Let's sit with this. Why would the past tense communicate certainty better than the future?

Because the future, in human experience, is the realm of *maybe*. The future is where our plans go to be cancelled. "I will call you" — and we don't. "We will travel next year" — and the year comes and goes. The future tense, for us, is soaked in contingency. But the past? **The past is the one thing in the universe that cannot be changed.** What happened, happened. It is locked. It is beyond negotiation, beyond hope, beyond regret's reach.

So when Allah wants to tell a mocking crowd that this Day is not a *maybe* — not a threat that might be empty, not a deadline that might slip — He does not reach for the future tense they could argue with. **He reaches for the one tense that is sealed.** He files the Day of Judgment under "already happened." [PAUSE]

And there is a second layer here, and the lexicon supports it. *Kāna* does not only mean "was" in the sense of a completed event. It also carries the sense of *what has always been the case* — a settled, standing reality. Read that way, the ayah says: this Day **has always been** an appointment. It was on the calendar before the calendar existed. Before you were born, before your grandparents were born, before the mountains were set as pegs — the appointment was already entered. You did not wander into a world that happens to have a Judgment at the end of it. **You were born into a world whose ending was scheduled before your beginning.**

Now watch what Allah does with this verb across the four ayat. He plants *kāna* in verse 17 — *kāna mīqātā* — and then he lets it grow:

- *kāna* mīqātā — the Day **was** an appointment (v.17)
- fa-**kānat** abwābā — the sky **became** gateways (v.19)
- fa-**kānat** sarābā — the mountains **became** a mirage (v.20)

The same verb of being threads through the passage like a heartbeat. Being, becoming, being. The Day *was* fixed; the sky *became* doors; the mountains *became* mirage. The whole of reality is being narrated in the language of what has settled and what has transformed. Nothing here is left in the trembling tense of "perhaps."

What this one word reveals: **the Day you find easiest to postpone is the one event Allah describes as already complete.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Al-Fasl* — The Day of the Great Un-Mixing

Before we open the next word, let me put an image in front of you.

You have seen a winnower work — or at least seen the principle. Grain comes off the threshing floor mixed: the wheat and the chaff, the edible and the worthless, tangled together so completely that no eye can sort them grain by grain. The worker tosses the whole mass into the air, and the wind does in a moment what hands could never do — it carries the light chaff one way and lets the heavy grain fall straight down. **Separated.** What was hopelessly mixed is now, suddenly, in two distinct piles.

Hold that image. Now the word.

> **يَوْمَ ٱلْفَصْلِ** — *yawma-l-fasl* — "the Day of *al-Fasl*."

We translate it "the Day of Decision" or "the Day of Judgment," and that is not wrong. But the root will not let us stay so abstract. **F-S-L.** Confirmed in the corpus. Its core, physical meaning is *to separate* — to part two things that were joined, to cut a join, to set apart what was together. From this one root the language grows a whole family: *fasl* is a chapter or section — the place a book is **divided.** *Mufassal* means detailed, spelled-out — meaning **separated into its distinct parts** so nothing stays blurred. And — this is the one that breaks the heart — *faṣīl* is the weaned camel-calf: the young one **separated** from its mother.

So before *al-Fasl* means a verdict handed down from a bench, it means **a separation.** A pulling-apart of what this life kept tangled together.

Let's sit with what was tangled.

In this world, the believer and the hypocrite pray in the same row. The honest merchant and the cheat sell in the same market. The one whose heart is full of God and the one whose heart is full of himself wear the same clothes, speak the same greetings, are buried under the same kind of stone. Good and evil are not sorted here. They are **mixed** — like grain and chaff on the threshing floor — and no human eye can do the winnowing. We guess. We misjudge. We honor the wrong people and overlook the right ones. The mixing is so thorough that injustice itself looks, from inside this life, like it might just get away with it.

Al-Qurtubi gives the reason for the name directly: it is called the Day of *al-Fasl* **because Allah separates between His creation on that Day** — *yafṣilu fīhi bayna khalqih.* The winnowing finally happens. The wind finally blows. And every grain that was hiding in the chaff, and every piece of chaff that was passing for grain, falls into the pile that was always, in truth, its own. [PAUSE]

Notice something the root insists on. *Fasl* is not the same as *destruction*. The winnower does not burn the floor; he sorts it. The Day of *al-Fasl* is not chaos — it is the end of chaos. It is the moment the universe stops being morally blurry. Everything that was ambiguous becomes exact. Everything that was *mufassal* — hidden in the tangle — is spelled out in full detail. This is why the same surah of the Quran that hammers this word — Surah Al-Mursalāt — repeats like a tolling bell: *"Woe that Day to the deniers... this is the Day of Decision (yawmu-l-fasl); We have gathered you and the former peoples."* (77:38) The deniers' real terror is not the Fire as raw pain. It is the **exposure.** It is the winnowing they spent a lifetime hoping would never come.

And here is the quiet mercy hiding inside the dread. If you have ever been wronged and watched the wrongdoer walk free — if you have ever told the truth and been called a liar — if you have ever done good in a room where only God was watching and no one ever knew — then *al-Fasl* is not only the Day you should fear. It is the Day you have been **waiting** for without knowing its name. The Day the grain is finally allowed to be grain.

What this word reveals: **everything this life mixed together, that Day pulls apart — and nothing will be left blurred.** [PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Mīqātan* — The Appointment You Did Not Schedule

One more word, and it changes how you will look at your own calendar.

Let me teach the shape of the word first, because the shape is the message. In Arabic there is a pattern — **mīfʿāl / mafʿal** — that turns a bare idea into a *fixed point* of that idea: a place where it happens, an instrument that does it, a set time for it. Take the root for *prostration*, *sajada*, and the pattern gives you *masjid* — not "prostrating" but **the place fixed for it.** Take the root for *rising*, and you get *maṭlaʿ* — the fixed point of rising. The pattern takes a flowing idea and **drives a stake into it.** It says: here. This spot. This moment. Fixed.

Now the word:

> **مِيقَٰتًا** — *mīqātan* — "an appointed time."

Root **w-q-t** — confirmed in the corpus — the root of *waqt*, time. But *mīqāt* is not loose time, not "sometime," not "eventually." The mīfʿāl pattern has driven the stake in. A *mīqāt* is **the fixed appointment** — the set place and set moment for a thing to occur. It is the word the Quran uses for the *mawāqīt* of Hajj: the precise boundaries where a pilgrim must enter the state of iḥrām — not "around there somewhere," but *exactly here.* It is the word used for Mūsā's appointment with his Lord: *"and We appointed for Mūsā thirty nights and completed them with ten, so the mīqāt of his Lord was forty nights."* (7:142) A specific count. A meeting on the books.

So when Allah says the Day of Decision *kāna mīqātā*, He is not saying "judgment will happen sometime." He is saying: **there is an appointment, and it has a fixed slot.** Al-Qurtubi's gloss draws this out beautifully — he says it is *waqtan wa majmaʿan wa mīʿādā*: a time, **and a meeting-place, and a promised rendezvous** — for the former peoples and the later peoples alike. One appointment. Every human being who has ever lived, summoned to the same fixed slot. [PAUSE]

Now let me bring it to your life, because this is where the word does its real work.

Think about the appointments you keep. You set them yourself. You chose the dentist on Tuesday, the meeting at three, the flight on Friday. And precisely because *you* set them, you feel you own them. You can move them. Cancel them. Show up late and apologize. Your whole relationship to your calendar is one of **control** — you are the one holding the pen.

But there is another kind of appointment. The kind you did not schedule and cannot move. The labor that starts when the child decides, not when the mother planned. The diagnosis that arrives on its own timeline. The phone call at 3 a.m. These appointments expose the lie the other ones tell — the lie that *you* are the author of your time. You are not. You schedule the small things to distract yourself from the one appointment that schedules **you.**

The Day of *al-Fasl* is a *mīqāt.* It is the appointment you did not set and cannot move. It was entered — remember verse 17's *kāna* — before you were born. It does not wait on your readiness. It does not slip when you are busy. It does not care that you meant to become a better person *next* Ramadan. The pilgrim does not negotiate with the mīqāt of Hajj; he either enters iḥrām at the boundary or he has violated it. And you will not negotiate with this one. **You will simply arrive.** [PAUSE]

What this word reveals: **the one appointment that matters most is the only one you had no hand in scheduling — and it is the only one you are guaranteed to keep.**

### Bridge

Three words. *Kāna* — the future spoken as the unchangeable past, so you would stop treating it as a *maybe*. *Al-Fasl* — the great un-mixing, the winnowing of everything this life left blurred. *Mīqāt* — the fixed appointment you did not schedule and cannot miss.

Together they have built something. A Day that is **certain** (kāna), **clarifying** (al-fasl), and **fixed** (mīqāt). Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because the last two verses stop describing the *Day* and start describing what happens to **the world** — and to **you** — when that Day arrives.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Mountains Become a Mirage — and What That Says About What You Thought Was Solid

Let me start where you live, not where the verse ends.

There are things in your life you treat as bedrock. Not consciously — you never decided to trust them; you just built on top of them as though they would always be there. Your health, quietly humming in the background until it isn't. The income that feels permanent until the email arrives. The person whose presence you stopped noticing precisely *because* you were so sure of it. We all walk around standing on a floor of assumptions we have never once tested, calling it "my life," calling it "solid ground."

And every so often, reality runs an experiment on one of these certainties. The bedrock turns out to be a stage set. The thing you leaned your whole weight against was — you realize, too late — **never load-bearing at all.** It only looked solid because you never pushed on it.

Now hold that, and look at what Allah does with the most solid thing the desert mind could imagine.

> **وَسُيِّرَتِ ٱلْجِبَالُ فَكَانَتْ سَرَابًۢا**
> "And the mountains are set moving, and become a mirage."

The mountains. Remember — eleven verses earlier, in this very surah, Allah called the mountains *awtād*, **pegs** (78:7). Tent-stakes driven into the earth to hold it steady. In the imagination of the people first hearing this, the mountain was the very *definition* of permanence. The thing that was there before your grandfather and would be there after your grandson. The unmovable. The synonym for "forever."

And Allah says: that — *that* — **becomes a mirage.**

Let's sit with the word He chose, because He could have said "dust" or "wool" or "scattered" — and elsewhere in the Quran He does. Here He chose **سَرَاب** — *sarāb* — the mirage. Why this word? Because a mirage is not just *gone.* A mirage is the specific cruelty of something that **looks completely real and is utterly empty.** Recall what a mirage *is*: the thirsty traveler sees, shimmering on the horizon, water — real-looking, certain, *right there* — and he drives himself toward it, and it recedes, and he arrives, and there is **nothing.** Sand. The Quran uses this exact image for the deeds of the deniers: *"the deeds of those who disbelieve are like a mirage in a desert — the thirsty one thinks it is water, until he comes to it and finds it to be nothing."* (24:39)

Do you feel the precision? Allah does not merely abolish the mountains. He converts the supreme symbol of solidity into the supreme symbol of *illusion.* The thing you would have bet your life was real is revealed to have been — at the level of ultimate reality — a shimmer on the horizon all along. [PAUSE]

Now follow this one more step, and let it land.

If the *mountain* — the most solid thing in your world — becomes a mirage on that Day, then what is the verse telling you about *now*? It is telling you that the solidity was never in the mountain. The mountain felt permanent only because the Day had not yet come to test it. **Its permanence was borrowed.** And so is everything you are leaning on. The health, the income, the relationships, the body, the youth — every one of them feels like bedrock for the exact same reason the mountain did: because the experiment that exposes it has not yet run. But the *mīqāt* is fixed. The experiment is scheduled. And on that Day, everything you mistook for water will be revealed as the sand it always was.

Here is the inversion at the center of this verse, and it is the whole point. We spend our lives treating the *seen* — mountains, money, flesh — as solid, and the *unseen* — the meeting with Allah, the reckoning, the Garden, the Fire — as the mirage, the "we'll-see," the maybe. The Day of *al-Fasl* **reverses it.** On that Day the seen world evaporates into mirage, and the unseen world stands up as the only solid thing there ever was. The chaff you chased blows away. The grain you ignored is all that remains. [PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow morning? This: the next time you feel the panic of a "solid" thing slipping — the health scare, the financial fear, the loss — you can meet it with a strange and steadying knowledge. *That thing was always a mirage. I was never standing on it. The slipping is not the catastrophe; it is the early, merciful preview of the un-mixing.* And you can begin — today, in small ways — to **transfer your weight.** To lean on the One thing the verse does *not* turn into a mirage. To build, even a little, on the appointment instead of on the sand. Because the only tragedy on the Day of *al-Fasl* is to arrive having spent your whole life standing on the mirage and calling it home.

### Theme Two: The Passive Voice of Power — Who Is Doing All of This, and Why You Are Not Told

Let me draw your attention to something you would walk right past, because it hides in the *grammar.* And it is, I think, the most quietly terrifying — and most quietly comforting — feature of this whole passage.

Look at the verbs across these four verses:

- *yunfakhu* fi-ṣ-ṣūr — the Trumpet **is blown** (v.18)
- *futiḥati*-s-samā' — the sky **is opened** (v.19)
- *suyyirati*-l-jibāl — the mountains **are set moving** (v.20)

Every single one is **passive.** Something *is blown.* The sky *is opened.* The mountains *are moved.* And in every case, the grammar does the one thing we are trained never to do in a sentence: it **omits the doer.** It does not say *who* blows the Trumpet. It does not say *who* tears open the sky. It does not say *whose* hand sets the mountains walking. The agent — the one performing these world-ending acts — is left grammatically *absent.*

Now, in a normal piece of writing, omitting the agent is evasion. "Mistakes were made." We use the passive to *hide* the actor. But this is the Speech of Allah, and the absence here is doing the opposite of hiding. Let me show you how.

Teach yourself the principle with an everyday scene. Imagine you walk into a room and the heavy oak table — the one it took four men to carry in — has been flipped onto its back. Nobody tells you who did it. What is your *first* feeling? Not curiosity. **Awe, edged with fear.** Because the absence of a named doer forces your mind to supply one, and the only thing that fits a flipped oak table is a *power great enough that naming it would almost be redundant.* The missing subject doesn't shrink the actor. It **magnifies** him. Your imagination rushes to fill a blank that no small force could fill.

That is precisely what the passive voice does here. *The Trumpet is blown* — and the unnamed Blower looms larger than if He had been named, because the grammar makes your soul reach for a Power vast enough to be left unspoken. And notice the second thing the passive accomplishes. By removing the agent, it puts the spotlight entirely on the *objects* — the Trumpet, the sky, the mountains — and on their utter **helplessness.** They do not act. They are *acted upon.* The sky does not *choose* to open; it **is opened.** The mountains do not *decide* to crumble; they **are set moving** like leaves before a wind they cannot resist. The grammar strips the entire created order of agency and lays it bare as pure object before an unnamed Subject. [PAUSE]

And now look at the one place in these four verses where Allah does *not* use the passive. Look at where the doer is suddenly, sharply named:

> **فَتَأْتُونَ أَفْوَاجًا** — *fa-ta'tūna afwājā* — "and **you** come forth in throngs." (v.18)

There it is. The Trumpet *is blown* — passive, agentless, cosmic. And then, like a camera snapping from the vast sky down to a single face in the crowd: **you.** *You* come. Active. Second person. Named. In the middle of a passage where the sky and the mountains have been reduced to helpless objects, the one creature given an active verb, the one summoned by name, is **you.**

Sit with that contrast. The sky — that mighty seven-fold structure He built above them (78:12) — is grammatically helpless. The mountains — the pegs of the earth — are grammatically helpless. And *you*, this small, soft thing, are the one who *comes.* Why? Because the sky and the mountains have no reckoning. They are scenery being struck. But you are the *point* of the whole Day. The cosmos is being dismantled as the *backdrop* to the one event that actually matters: **you, arriving, in a throng, to be sorted.** *Fa-ta'tūna.* You come. The whole universe is torn down around the appointment, and the appointment is with *you.* [PAUSE]

Here is where this turns from terror to a strange steadiness — and this is the practical heart of it.

Most of our daily anxiety comes from a single confusion: we keep trying to be the *active agent* over things that are, in truth, *passive objects in the hand of Allah.* We grip the outcome of the meeting, the test result, the relationship, as though the verb belonged to us — as though *we* open the sky and move the mountains of our circumstances. And we exhaust ourselves blowing on Trumpets that are not ours to blow. This passage corrects the grammar of the soul. The sky is *opened* — not by you. The mountains are *moved* — not by you. There is exactly one active verb assigned to you in the whole scene, and it is not *control.* It is *come.* **Your job was never to run the universe. Your job was to arrive — prepared.**

So what changes tomorrow? Stop trying to conjugate yourself as the agent of everything. Hand the passive verbs back to the One they belong to — *He* opens, *He* moves, *He* decrees — and pour your energy into the one verb that is actually yours: **the coming.** How will you arrive at the *mīqāt?* In which throng? Carrying what? That — and almost nothing else — is in your hands.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how these four verses are built, because the structure is itself an argument.

Verse 17 is the **thesis**: there is a Day, it is certain (*kāna*), it is a sorting (*al-fasl*), it is fixed (*mīqāt*). Then verses 18, 19, and 20 are three **camera angles** on that one Day, and watch the order:

- **v.18 — YOU:** the Trumpet sounds and *you* come forth. (the human being)
- **v.19 — ABOVE you:** the sky is opened into gateways. (the ceiling of the world)
- **v.20 — AROUND you:** the mountains are set moving into mirage. (the floor of the world)

The Day is named — then *you* are summoned — then the roof of reality is opened — then the foundations of reality dissolve. The entire frame you live inside, top and bottom, is dismantled, and standing in the middle of the wreckage, the only thing left with an active verb, the only thing the whole scene was built around, is **you, having arrived at the appointment.** The architecture *is* the message: when the seen world is stripped away, what remains is a soul and its meeting with its Lord.

### Bringing It Together

The three words and the two themes are the same truth seen from two directions.

The **words** said: this Day is *certain* (kāna, the sealed past tense), it is a *separation* (al-fasl, the winnowing), and it is a *fixed appointment* (mīqāt, the stake driven into time).

The **themes** said: when that fixed appointment arrives, everything you thought was solid is revealed as mirage (the mountains), and everything you thought was in your control is revealed as helpless object in an unnamed Hand (the passive voice) — leaving you with one verb that was ever truly yours: to *come.*

Put them together and you have the entire message of the passage in a single motion: **The most certain event in your existence is the one you most easily postpone; on it, everything you trusted will be sorted from everything that was real; and your only task — your only verb — was to prepare for an arrival you could not avoid.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life am I treating as *bedrock* that the verse is quietly calling a *mirage*? If it were stripped away tomorrow, would I discover I had been standing on it instead of on Allah?

2. I keep my own appointments carefully. How much of my energy goes to scheduling the small meetings I control — and how little to preparing for the one *mīqāt* I did not schedule and cannot move?

3. On the Day of *al-Fasl*, the great un-mixing, which pile do my private deeds — the ones no human eye has sorted — actually belong to?

4. How much of my daily exhaustion comes from trying to be the *active agent* over things that were always *passive in Allah's hand*? What would change if I handed those verbs back?

5. When the Trumpet sounds and the throngs come forth — *in which throng am I walking, and what am I carrying in my hands?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

The Day you find easiest to postpone is the only appointment Allah describes as already settled — the fixed hour when everything you mistook for solid ground evaporates into mirage, and the one thing left standing is you, arriving to be sorted.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to be afraid of this passage. You need to be *re-oriented* by it. The fear in it is real, but its deepest gift is **clarity** — the same clarity *al-Fasl* itself brings, only offered to you *now*, while there is still time to act on it. Most people will receive this clarity on the Day. You are being offered it today. Let it do, gently, what it will one day do completely: let it pull apart the mirage from the real in your own heart, so that you begin — in small, daily, unglamorous ways — to transfer your weight off the sand and onto the One who does not move. Not because you must. Because the appointment is already on the calendar, and you would rather arrive standing on something true.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who fixed the Day of Decision as an appointment before You shaped us — let us not be among those who chase the mirage and call it water. Steady our hearts on what is real before the mountains move, and on the Day You blow the Trumpet, let us come forth in the throng of those You are pleased with, arriving with hands that prepared for the meeting. Āmīn.*
