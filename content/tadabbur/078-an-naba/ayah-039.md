# The True Day, and the Will That Is Still Yours

## The Ayah

> ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْيَوْمُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۖ فَمَن شَآءَ ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِۦ مَـَٔابًا

*Dhālika al-yawmu al-ḥaqq. Fa-man shā'a-ttakhadha ilā rabbihi ma'ābā.*

**"That is the True Day. So whoever wills, let him take a return back to his Lord."**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

Imagine the very first people who heard this surah.

They are in Makkah. The community is small, mocked, pressured. And the single thing the Makkans cannot stop arguing about — the thing they bring up at the well, in the marketplace, around the fire at night — is one claim this small community keeps making:

*The dead will rise. There will be a Day.*

This is what they call **the great news** — *al-naba' al-'aẓīm* — the thing they are "in disagreement about" in the opening lines of the surah. To them it is absurd. *We become dust and scattered bones, and then we are reassembled? And questioned? And held to account?* It offends their sense of how the world works. The whole surah is Allah's answer to that scoffing.

And notice *how* He answers. He doesn't begin with a sermon. He takes them on a tour of things they can see with their own eyes. **Did We not make the earth a resting place? And the mountains as pegs? And create you in pairs? And make your sleep a rest? And the night a covering, and the day for livelihood?** He builds the bed they sleep in, the mountains that steady the ground under them, the rhythm of their own breathing in and out of sleep. The argument is: *the One who did all of that — reassembling you is the smaller task.*

Then the surah turns a corner. It stops describing the world you know and starts describing the world you don't. **The Day of Decision** — *yawm al-faṣl*. The trumpet. The sky torn into gates. The mountains turned to mirage. And then, just before our verse, the most awesome image in the whole surah:

> **The Day the Spirit and the angels stand in rows — none of them speaking except the one the Most Merciful permits, and who says what is right.**

Sit in that scene for a moment. The greatest of all created beings — Jibrīl, and ranks upon ranks of angels — standing silent. Not one of them dares open his mouth. The most powerful tongues in creation are sealed shut unless given leave.

Into that silence — that total, terrifying silence where even angels do not speak — comes verse 39.

And here is the hook. After describing a Day so overwhelming that angels stand mute, you would expect the next words to crush you further. You would expect: *and you have no power, no escape, no say in any of it.*

Instead, Allah says something that should stop you in your tracks. In the very breath after sealing the mouths of angels, **He hands something back to you.** He uses the word *shā'a* — "whoever wills." In the throne room where no one acts without permission, the human being is suddenly handed a will, a choice, a door.

Why? Why, of all places, *here*? Why hand a trembling human being their freedom precisely at the moment the universe is demonstrating how little control they have?

Let's find out. We'll go slowly. There are only nine words. And they will not run out before we do.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *al-Ḥaqq* — The Day That Is "The Truth," Not Just "True"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In English, when we say "the real day" or "the true day," *true* is an adjective hanging off the noun. It describes the day. The day is the thing; *true* is a quality we paint onto it.

Arabic can do that too. But Arabic can also do something English struggles to do: it can take a quality and turn it into *a noun with the definite article* — so that instead of saying the day is "true," it says the Day is **The Truth itself.**

Look at the phrase:

> *dhālika al-yawmu **al-ḥaqq***

The word *al-ḥaqq* — root **Ḥ-Q-Q** — does not merely mean "true" in the sense of "not a lie." Its root meaning is *that which is firmly established, fixed, real, that whose existence and occurrence cannot be doubted.* The classical commentators are unanimous and almost identical here. Al-Jalālayn glosses it as *al-thābit wuqūʿuhu* — "the One whose occurrence is firmly fixed." Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both say *al-kā'in al-wāqiʿ* — "the existing, the occurring." It is not "true" as a verbal opinion. It is **real as a wall is real.** Real the way the ground is real when you fall on it.

Now notice the definite article — *al-*. And notice the demonstrative that opens the verse: *dhālika* — "**that**."

Here is a small thing in Arabic that carries enormous weight. *Dhālika* is the **far** demonstrative — "that," pointing into the distance — as opposed to *hādhā*, "this," pointing to what's near. You'd expect, after describing the Day in such vivid detail two verses earlier, that Allah would say *hādhā* — "this Day, right here, that I've just shown you." But He says *dhālika* — "**that** Day," pointing far off.

Why point far away at something He's just placed right in front of us?

Because in Arabic the far demonstrative is the language of **majesty and exaltation.** You point at something far not because it's distant in time but because it's *elevated* — too lofty to gesture at casually. It's the same move as *dhālika al-kitāb* at the opening of Surah al-Baqarah — "**That** Book" — for a Book that is literally in your hands. You point far to say: *this thing is greater than the space between us; lift your eyes to it.*

So put the pieces together. *Dhālika al-yawm al-ḥaqq.* "**That** — the elevated, the lofty one — is **The Day, The Reality.**"

Stay with this. [PAUSE]

We live our whole lives among things we *call* real. Your job is real. Your reputation is real. The mortgage is real, the diagnosis is real, the argument you had this morning was very, very real. We pour the entire weight of our emotional lives into these realities. We lose sleep over them. We define ourselves by them.

And the Qur'an does not say they are illusions. It says something far more unsettling. It says: **there is one Day that is *al-ḥaqq* — The Reality — and in its light, everything you currently call real is revealed to be provisional.** Temporary. A rented reality. The job ends. The reputation is forgotten within a generation. The body that the diagnosis threatened was always going to return to dust anyway.

What the definite article does here is brutal and merciful at once. It removes the article from everything else. It says: there is *the* Truth — singular — and then there is everything you mistook for it.

Here's what's remarkable. When something is *al-ḥaqq* — fixed, established, certain — your *opinion* of it is irrelevant. The Makkans were "disputing" about it. They could dispute all they liked. The verse doesn't argue back. It simply names it: *That is The Day, The Real.* You can dispute the existence of the wall. The wall does not enter the argument. You meet it when you walk into it.

So the first word has already done something to us. It has quietly rearranged the furniture of what we think is solid. **The one thing we treat as far-off and unreal is the only thing that is fully real. And everything we treat as solid is the thing that's about to dissolve.**

### Element Two: *Fa-man Shā'a* — The Will Handed Back

Now the turn. The hinge of the whole verse.

> *fa-man **shā'a***

"So whoever **wills**." Root **Sh-Y-'**, the verb of *will, want, choosing.*

I told you to hold a question from the introduction: why, in the throne room where even angels stand silent and speechless, does Allah suddenly invoke the human *will*?

Let me first teach the grammar, because the grammar is the entire point.

That little particle *fa-* at the front — "so," "therefore" — is a particle of consequence. It means what follows is the *result* of what came before. So the verse is built as a logical sequence: *Because that is The Real Day… therefore, whoever wills…* The certainty of the Day is the *premise*. The handing-over of the will is the *conclusion drawn from it.*

Read that again, because it's the opposite of how we'd expect it to flow. We would expect: *The Day is real and overwhelming, therefore you are powerless.* But the verse says: *The Day is real, **therefore you had better use the one power you have.***

The certainty of the destination is not given to crush your agency. It is given to *activate* it.

Notice something else. The whole surah, up to this point, has been describing things done *to* you and *around* you, things in which you are utterly passive. Allah spread the earth. Allah set the mountains. Allah made your sleep. Allah will blow the trumpet, tear the sky, move the mountains. You are the object of every verb. Things happen *to* you, page after page.

And then — *fa-man shā'a.* The grammar shifts. **For the first time, the human being becomes the one who wills.** The subject of an active verb. In the middle of the most overwhelming display of divine power in the surah, the spotlight swings, and there *you* are, with a verb of your own.

This is the mercy hidden inside the terror. Allah shows you a Day so total that angels are silenced — and then says: *and yet, the choice of where you stand on that Day is still, right now, in your hands.*

Let's sit with what *shā'a* implies. It is wide open. The verse does not say "whoever is forced," or "whoever is good," or "whoever is chosen." It says *man shā'a* — **whoever wills.** The door is described as opening to *anyone who simply wants to walk through it.* No prior qualification is named. Not your past, not your record, not how far you've drifted. The single entry requirement named in the verse is **wanting to.**

Think about how radical that is given the context. We have just watched the transgressors sentenced earlier in the surah. We have just watched angels too awed to speak. And the door Allah describes is so low to the ground that the only thing required to enter is the willing of it.

[PAUSE]

But there's a sharpness here too, and honesty requires we feel it. If the only requirement is *willing*, then the only thing standing between a person and their Lord is **their own will.** No external barrier is named. Which means: whatever keeps us out, we built. The verse, in its gentleness, quietly removes every excuse. You cannot say *I wasn't allowed.* The door was described as open to *whoever wills.* The question the verse leaves ringing is not *will they let me in?* It is: **do I actually will it?**

### Element Three: *Ittakhadha… Ma'āban* — "Take For Yourself a Return"

Now the two words that complete the action — and they are the most beautiful in the verse.

> *ittakhadha ilā rabbihi **ma'āban***

"…**takes** towards his Lord **a return**."

Two roots to open here, and both are doing heavy lifting.

**First: *ittakhadha*.** This is the verb "to take," but it is in a specific shape the grammarians call **Form VIII** (*iftaʿala*). Form VIII characteristically turns an action *back toward the doer* — you don't just *take*, you **take for yourself, you acquire for your own benefit, you adopt something as your own.** It's the difference between *taking* a coat and *taking a coat to keep.* The form folds the benefit of the action back onto the one doing it.

So *ittakhadha* is not a cold, neutral "takes." It is *takes-and-makes-it-his-own.* Whatever this person reaches for, the grammar says **they are securing it for themselves, for their own keeping.** Hold that — it matters in a moment.

**Second: *ma'āban*.** Root **'-W-B** (*Awb*). And here is where the verse opens into something tender. The root *Awb* means **to return** — and specifically, to return to a place you *came from.* It is the root of home-coming. It is the very same root from which the Qur'an builds the word *awwāb* — the title Allah gives to His most beloved servants: *the oft-returning one,* the one who keeps coming back. David is called *awwāb.* Solomon is called *awwāb.* It is the root of the heart that keeps turning home.

So *ma'āb* is not just "a destination." It is **a place of return.** And a place of *return* carries a hidden claim: **you cannot return to somewhere you have never been.** The word itself whispers that *your Lord is your origin.* You are not being invited toward a stranger. You are being invited *back.* Home.

The commentators feel this and circle it from different angles, and each angle adds a layer. Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī read *ma'āb* as *marjiʿ* — "a returning, a coming-back to Allah through obedience." Al-Muyassar adds the means: "a return through righteous deeds." And Qatāda, as al-Qurṭubī reports, gives it as *sabīl* — **"a path."** Put them together as one picture: *ma'āb* is at once the **destination** (home, your Lord), the **road** that leads there (the path of obedience), and the **act of walking it** (righteous deeds). It is home, and the way home, and the walking — all in one word.

Now feel the whole phrase fire at once:

> *fa-man shā'a-ttakhadha ilā rabbihi ma'āban*

"So whoever wills — let him **take-and-make-his-own**, toward **his Lord**, **a way back home.**"

Notice what you are told to *take for yourself.* Not wealth. Not safety. Not status. The thing you are invited to acquire and clutch and keep — the one possession worth securing with the grasping force of Form VIII — is **a return route to your Lord.** That is the only treasure the verse names as worth taking.

And one more thing the grammar refuses to let us miss. Look at the name used for God here. Not *Allāh*. Not *al-Jabbār,* the Compeller — which is what you'd expect on a Day this overwhelming. The verse says *ilā **rabbihi*** — "toward **his Lord**." Root **R-B-B.** *Rabb* is the nurturer, the sustainer, the one who raised you from a drop to a person, who feeds and tends and grows what is His. And the possessive — *hi*, "**his** Lord" — makes it personal. Not "the Lord." *His* Lord. *Yours.*

So at the most fearsome moment in the surah, the road home is described as leading not to a Judge you've never met, but to **the One who has been nurturing you your entire life.** The terror of the Day and the tenderness of the name sit in the same breath. The Day is *al-ḥaqq* — terrifyingly real. And the One at the end of the road is *Rabb* — the One who raised you.

[PAUSE — major]

**The bridge.** Look at what these nine words have built. A Day named *The Reality* — fixed, unarguable, towering. A human will, handed back precisely when you'd expect it confiscated. A verb that says *grab it and keep it for yourself.* A destination that turns out to be *home.* And a name for God that turns out to be *the One who's been raising you all along.*

The grammar has told the whole story. Now let's ask what it reveals not just about words — but about how we are actually living.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Living Among Counterfeit Realities

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, right now, a list of things you would describe as "real problems." Real pressures. The mortgage. The performance review. The text you're dreading. The number on the scale or in the bank account. The opinion someone you respect holds of you. These things have *weight.* They sit on your chest at 3 a.m. They feel, in the dark, like the most real things in the universe.

Here's a test. Pick one of them — the heaviest one. Now ask: **will it exist on *dhālika al-yawm al-ḥaqq*?** Will the performance review survive into The Real Day? Will the number? Will the opinion of that person you can't stop trying to impress?

You already know the answer. None of them make the crossing. Not one.

And yet — be honest — they get nearly all of you. Your attention, your anxiety, your sleep, your *will.* The thing the verse just handed you — *shā'a,* your willing — you have been spending it, hour after hour, on realities that the verse has just quietly demoted to *provisional.*

This is the spiritual disease the word *al-ḥaqq* diagnoses with a single definite article. Not that we love false things — most of these things are *good* in their place. The disease is **mistaking the rented for the owned. Mistaking the provisional for The Real.** We have given the crown of *al-ḥaqq,* "The Reality," to things that were only ever on loan, and we have pushed the one thing that is actually *al-ḥaqq* out to the distance — *dhālika,* "that," far off, someday, not now.

Watch how the surah corrects the eyes. It spends most of its length on things you *can* see — earth, mountains, sleep, night, day, gardens — and then names the thing you *can't* see as *The Real One.* It is retraining your sense of solidity. It is saying: *the visible is the stage set; the unseen Day is the actual ground.*

Here is the movement that turns this from a scary thought into a *freeing* one. If the things crushing you at 3 a.m. are provisional — then their power over you is also provisional. The review cannot follow you onto The Real Day. So why does it get to own your will *today*? The verse doesn't only relativize your fears. It **liberates the will those fears were holding hostage.** It frees up the very *shā'a* it's about to ask you to spend.

What does this change tomorrow? Concretely: the next time something grips you with that 3 a.m. weight, run it through one question — *does this exist on al-yawm al-ḥaqq?* If yes, it deserves your will. If no, it does not get to own you. It can have your attention, your effort, even your concern. It does not get your soul. That single filter, applied honestly, reorders an entire life.

### Theme Two: The Return — You Are Trying to Go Home

Let me name a feeling you already know.

There is a specific ache that has no clear object. It shows up in good moments more than bad ones — a beautiful evening, a sky going gold, music that catches you off guard — and instead of pure joy it brings a strange tug, a homesickness *for you don't know where.* You have everything you wanted and still feel like an exile from somewhere you can't name. Every language has a word for it because every human feels it. We call it longing. Nostalgia. The Welsh call it *hiraeth* — homesickness for a home you can't return to, or that maybe never was.

The Qur'an just told you what it is. It's the root *Awb.* It's *ma'āb.*

The verse calls your final destination *a return* — and a return is only possible if you are *from* there. The ache you feel is not a malfunction. **It is the soul's memory of where it came from.** You feel like an exile because, in a sense the verse takes literally, you are one. You took the covenant in a gathering before this world; you came from your Lord; and the entire arc of a human life is the long road of *Awb* — the road back.

This reframes everything the surah has been doing. The Day is not, at root, a *trial* you are dragged to. It is a **homecoming** you are walking toward. The trumpet, the gathering, the standing — for the one who used their *shā'a* well, this is the door of the house opening. The transgressor experiences the same Day as a sentence; the *awwāb,* the one who kept turning back, experiences it as arrival.

Now bring back the grammar, because it makes this almost unbearably gentle. The road home leads *ilā rabbihi* — to *his Lord,* the Nurturer. Picture the most ordinary, powerful version of homecoming: a small child, who wandered too far, suddenly afraid, turning and running back — not to a magistrate, but to the parent who raised them. That is the picture *Rabb* paints under the terror of the Day. **The most fearsome Day in the surah ends with a child running home to the One who raised them.**

And here is the movement that should break something open in you. The verse says *fa-man shā'a* — *whoever wills* — let him take this return **now.** Present. Today. The road home does not open only on the Day. The commentators are explicit: *ma'āb* is taken *through obedience, through righteous deeds, through the path* — all of which are things you do *here.* The homecoming is not something that happens *to* you at the end. It is something you **begin** the moment you turn. Every prayer is a step on the road of *Awb.* Every turning-back after a slip is the *awwāb* in you, coming home again.

This is why the same root that gives us *ma'āb* gives us *awwāb* — the *oft-returner.* Notice: not *returner,* once. *Oft.* The beloved servants of Allah are not the ones who never left. They are the ones who **keep coming back.** Who fall and turn home, fall and turn home, until turning home becomes the shape of their soul. *Awb* is not a single dramatic homecoming. It is a *direction* you keep choosing.

What does this change tomorrow? It dismantles the lie that keeps the most exhausted hearts away — *I've drifted too far to go back.* The verse never mentions distance as a barrier. The only thing it names is *shā'a* — willing it. There is no point on the road of *Awb* where the road runs out. The door the verse describes is open to *whoever wills,* and it is open *now.* So the practice is brutally simple and available this instant: **turn.** One sincere turning of the will toward your Lord — one prayer prayed like it matters, one sin abandoned, one *istighfār* meant — and you are, by the verse's own definition, someone who *ittakhadha ilā rabbihi ma'āban.* Someone who took the road home.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Step back and see the shape of these nine words, because they are built with terrifying precision.

The verse has two halves, and they are mirror images of each other:

**Half one — what is fixed:** *Dhālika al-yawm al-ḥaqq.* "That is The Real Day." Here, everything is settled, certain, beyond your reach. The far demonstrative. The definite article. The unarguable noun. **You have no say.**

**Half two — what is yours:** *Fa-man shā'a-ttakhadha ilā rabbihi ma'āban.* "So whoever wills, takes a return to his Lord." Here, everything hinges on you. The open *whoever.* The verb of *will.* The self-directed *take.* **You have the only say that matters.**

And the hinge between them is that one small particle: *fa-* — "**therefore.**" The architecture is an argument. *Because* the Day is fixed and beyond you — *therefore* use the one thing that isn't. The certainty of what you cannot control is given as the reason to seize what you can.

The fixed half is the wall. The free half is the door in the wall. And the verse spends its first three words building an unscalable wall — only to spend its last six telling you there is a door, it is unlocked, and *you* are holding the handle.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes are the same insight, approached from different doors.

*Al-ḥaqq* told us: **only one thing is fully real, and everything else is on loan.** The first theme lived that out: stop spending your soul on counterfeit realities.

*Ma'āb* and *Rabb* told us: **the one real destination is a homecoming to the One who raised you.** The second theme lived that out: the ache you feel is the road calling, and the road is open now.

And *shā'a* — the will — is the thread tying both together. Because here is the whole verse in one breath: **In a universe where almost nothing is up to you, the single thing that is up to you is the only thing that finally matters — and it is still, right now, in your hand.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What am I currently treating as *al-ḥaqq* — utterly real, worthy of my sleep and my will — that will not survive *al-yawm al-ḥaqq*?
2. If the only barrier the verse names between me and my Lord is my own *willing* — then what, honestly, is my will set on right now?
3. The verse says *take* a return — *ittakhadha,* grab it and keep it. Is the return home something I am actively *taking,* or something I keep telling myself I'll get to later?
4. I feel the ache of *Awb* — the homesickness with no object. Have I ever named it for what it is? And what do I usually do to numb it instead of following it home?
5. Am I waiting for a single dramatic return — or am I willing to be an *awwāb,* one who simply keeps turning back, today and tomorrow and the day after?

### One-Sentence Distillation

After parading a Day so real that even angels fall silent, Allah hands you back the one thing that is yours — your will — and shows you the door home is unlocked from your side, opening to the One who raised you, and open *now.*

### The Closing Invitation

You were never asked to be powerful enough to face the Day. The angels themselves stand mute before it. You were asked for one thing only: *shā'a* — to *will* it. To want the way home enough to take a single step.

So let the invitation be small enough to actually accept. Not "transform your whole life by Thursday." Just: **take one return today.** One prayer prayed as if it is the road it actually is. One turning-back you've been postponing. One honest *yā Rabb* from the part of you that has been homesick this whole time. That is *ittakhadha ilā rabbihi ma'āban.* That is the verse, lived, in the next hour.

The Day is fixed. The door is open. The handle is in your hand.

### Du'ā

*Yā Rabb* — You who raised us from a drop and have nurtured us every day since — we confess we have crowned a hundred counterfeit realities while leaving the only Real Day in the distance. Make us of those who *will* the way home, and having willed it, *take* it and hold it fast. And when our feet slip, make us *awwābīn* — those who keep turning back to You — until the turning becomes the shape of our hearts, and the homecoming, when it comes, is only the door of Your house opening to a child who never stopped walking back. *Āmīn.*
