# The Roof That Never Falls

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Let me begin by reading these words to you, slowly, the way they were first heard.

> [ayah:78:12] وَبَنَيْنَا فَوْقَكُمْ سَبْعًا شِدَادًا
> [ayah:78:13] وَجَعَلْنَا سِرَاجًا وَهَّاجًا
> [ayah:78:14] وَأَنزَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلْمُعْصِرَٰتِ مَآءً ثَجَّاجًا
> [ayah:78:15] لِّنُخْرِجَ بِهِۦ حَبًّا وَنَبَاتًا
> [ayah:78:16] وَجَنَّـٰتٍ أَلْفَافًا

*Wa banaynā fawqakum sabʿan shidādā. Wa jaʿalnā sirājan wahhājā. Wa anzalnā mina-l-muʿṣirāti māʾan thajjājā. Linukhrija bihī ḥabban wa nabātā. Wa jannātin alfāfā.*

"And We built above you seven strong heavens, and made in them a blazing lamp, and We sent down from the rain-laden clouds water pouring forth in torrents, that We may bring forth thereby grain and vegetation — and gardens of densely entwined growth."

Now, before we open a single word, I want you to step into the room where these words first landed.

It is Mecca. Early revelation. And the city is arguing. That is how the surah opens — not with a sermon, but with an overheard quarrel: *ʿamma yatasāʾalūn?* — "About what are they asking one another?" The whole town is buzzing about something. A piece of news so enormous the surah calls it *an-Nabaʾ al-ʿAẓīm* — **the Great News**. And the people are split over it. Some believe it. Most scoff. And the news is this: that the dead will rise. That this life is not the end. That there is a Day coming when every buried thing will be called back out of the ground.

To the people of Mecca, this was the most absurd thing imaginable. *"When we are dead and turned to dust and bones — we will be raised again?"* They found it laughable. A man crumbles to powder, the wind scatters him across the valley — and you tell me he will stand again, whole, to be questioned?

[PAUSE]

Now watch what Allah does. He does not, in this surah, give them a philosophical lecture on the possibility of resurrection. He does something far more disarming. He simply says: *look around you.* He begins a list. Did We not make the earth a resting-place? And the mountains as pegs? Did We not create you in pairs, and make your sleep a rest, and the night a covering, and the day a time to seek your living?

And then — He points **up**.

He points at the one thing these men had walked beneath their entire lives and never once questioned. The ceiling. The sky. The thing that has been over their heads since the day they were born, that will be over their heads on the day they die, that holds — silently, faithfully, every single second — and that not one of them had ever thanked Him for.

Here is the hook, and I want you to feel it before we explain it:

These men are standing in the open desert, arguing that Allah *cannot* rebuild a human body. And the entire time, **a structure infinitely more vast and more intricate than a human body is hanging over their heads, perfectly built, never cracking.** The proof they demand is the roof above them. They just never looked up.

So let us look up with them. Let us go word by word through what Allah placed over our heads — and discover that the sky is not scenery. **The sky is an argument.**

# PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: شِدَادًا — *Shidād*, the Word That Means "It Will Not Fail"

### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, there is a family of adjectives that don't just describe a quality — they describe a quality at its *maximum density*. Think about the difference, in English, between saying someone is "strong" and saying someone is "unbreakable." Both are positive. But "unbreakable" carries a promise inside it: not just that the thing is strong now, but that it will *hold* — under load, under time, under stress.

Arabic has a word built exactly on that promise. The root is **shīn–dāl–dāl** (ش-د-د). Its core meaning is to be firm, tight, bound fast, compacted to the point that nothing can loosen it. From this same root comes *shidda* — hardship, the thing that presses on you and won't let go. And it gives us *shadīd* — firm, intense, severe — and its plural, the word in our verse: **شِدَادًا**, *shidād*.

Notice the sound of it. Two dāls pressing together — *shi-dād*. The tongue has to brace against the roof of the mouth and hold. The word is *physically* tight to say. The sound enacts the meaning. This is a word you cannot say loosely.

### Opening the word

Allah says: *wa banaynā fawqakum sabʿan* — "We built above you seven" — and then He chooses, of all the words available, this one:

> شِدَادًا

Not "seven beautiful," not "seven vast," not "seven high." **Seven *firm ones*. Seven that hold.**

Let's sit with this.

### What the scholars saw in it

When the classical commentators arrived at this single word, they did not reach for poetry. They reached for the language of *engineering*. Look at what they say, and notice they are all circling the same reality from slightly different angles.

Al-Jalālayn explains *shidād* as the plural of *shadīda* — meaning **qawiyya muḥkama**, "strong, soundly constructed" — and then adds the crucial detail: *lā yuʾaththir fīhā murūr az-zamān*, **"the passage of time has no effect on it."**

Stay with that phrase. *Time has no effect on it.* Every roof you have ever lived under is, at this very moment, losing. Aging. The beam is sagging by a millimeter a decade. The mortar is breathing in moisture and breathing it out, and each cycle weakens it. Everything built by human hands is in a slow argument with time, and human hands always lose. But this structure — *lā yuʾaththir fīhā murūr az-zamān*. Time presses on it and it does not yield.

Al-Muyassar adds another layer: *matīnat al-bināʾ, muḥkamat al-khalq* — "firm in construction, perfected in creation" — and then the unforgettable phrase: *lā ṣudūʿa lahā wa lā fuṭūr*. **"It has no cracks and no fissures."**

[PAUSE]

I want you to think about what it takes for a builder to say that about anything. Walk into the most beautiful cathedral, the most engineered skyscraper, and put your eye close to the stone. You will find a crack. You will always find a crack. Cracking is what built things *do*. It is the signature of created matter under the pull of gravity and time.

And Allah says of what He built: **no cracks. No fissures.** Al-Qurṭubī gathers it into two precise words: *muḥkamat al-khalq, wathīqat al-bunyān* — "perfected in formation, secure in structure." Al-Baghawī keeps it spare and certain: *seven heavens* — built, firm, done.

Put the four together and you do not get four opinions. You get four engineers standing under the same impossible ceiling, each describing one feature of the same fact: **it will not fail.** Not under time. Not under load. Not at the seams. There are no seams.

### Where the word is pointing

Now — and this is the heart of it — *shidād* is a physical word. Firmness. Soundness. No cracks. But remember our principle: the physical image is never the destination. It is the stepping stone. So ask: what unseen reality is this firmness pointing us toward?

Here it is. **You have never once, in your entire life, worried that the sky would fall on you.**

Think about how strange that is. You worry about your job. You worry about your health, your children, your reputation, your savings. You lie awake over things far less likely to harm you than a collapsing ceiling. But the one thing you have *never* spent a second of anxiety on is the single largest structure in your existence — the thing literally suspended over your head, all day, every day, the whole of your life.

Why? Because it is *shidād*. Because at some level below thought, you *trust* it. You were born trusting it. You will die trusting it.

And here is what should stop us cold: **that trust is a gift you never thanked anyone for.** The absence of your fear is itself a mercy. The fact that you can walk outside, look up, and feel nothing — no dread, no vigilance — that *peace* is something Allah engineered into the structure of your world and then quietly handed to you. Free. Unnoticed.

Elsewhere Allah names this gift directly: *wa jaʿalnā as-samāʾa saqfan maḥfūẓan* — "We made the sky a *protected roof*" (21:32). A roof. The most domestic word imaginable. He calls the heavens a *ceiling over your house*. And *shidād* is the word that tells you the ceiling will hold.

One clear statement, then we move on: **Shidād means the sky is not just vast — it is faithful. It holds without thanks, without fail, without crack. And the peace you feel beneath it is a mercy you were given before you knew to ask.**

## Element Two: وَهَّاجًا — *Wahhāj*, the Lamp That Does Two Things at Once

### Teaching the concept first

Arabic has a special gear it shifts into when something is happening *intensely, abundantly, again and again*. Grammarians call it *ṣīghat al-mubālagha* — the form of intensification. You take a simple description and you *double a letter* in the middle, and suddenly the word doesn't just say the thing happens — it says the thing *pours out* of the subject.

Here is the everyday version. In Arabic, *ghāfir* means "one who forgives." Fine. But *Ghaffār* — with the doubled fā — means "the One who forgives and forgives and forgives, whose very nature is to forgive without end." Same root. One doubled letter. An ocean of difference.

Hold onto that mechanism — *the doubled middle letter that turns a quality into a torrent* — because the next two words in our passage are both built on it, and once you hear it, you cannot unhear it.

### Opening the word

Allah says: *wa jaʿalnā sirājan* — "and We made a lamp." Already, *sirāj* — lamp — is a chosen word. He could have said *shams*, sun. He says *lamp*. A lamp is a thing placed in a room *for the people in the room*. You don't light a lamp for the lamp's sake. You light it so others can see, so others can be warm. By calling the sun a *lamp*, Allah has already told you who it is *for*.

And then He describes the lamp:

> وَهَّاجًا

*Wahhāj.* Root: **wāw–hāʾ–jīm** (و-ه-ج), which means to blaze, to glow with heat and light together. And look — the middle letter is doubled. *Wah-hāj.* This is the intensive form. Not a lamp that glows. **A lamp that *blazes without ceasing*.**

### Deepening

Let's notice something the classical lexicographers were careful about. The root *wahaj* doesn't mean only "bright." It means the *combination* of two things: blinding light **and** burning heat, fused in one glow. *Al-wahaj* is the flare that both illuminates and scorches.

Why does that matter? Because Allah is making a claim about the sun that is, when you think about it, an extraordinary economy of design. He hung a *single* object in the sky and made it do the two things every living thing on earth absolutely requires: **light to see by, and heat to live by.** Not two lamps — one for warmth and one for vision. One lamp. *Wahhāj.* Doing both, intensely, continuously, for the entire span of your life, without being refueled, without dimming, without ever once being relit.

Stay with the intensive form for a moment longer. *Wahhāj* — the doubled hāʾ — tells you this blazing is not an event but a *nature*. The sun does not "shine sometimes." Blazing is *what it is*. It has poured out light and heat every single day since before your grandparents' grandparents were born, and it will keep pouring tomorrow whether you are grateful or not.

### Where the word is pointing

So where does *wahhāj* take us? Back to the same place as *shidād*, by a different road.

The sun is the most *taken-for-granted* fact in human life. We build our entire existence on the unspoken assumption that tomorrow it will rise and blaze again. We schedule, we plant, we plan years into the future — all of it resting on a confidence we never examine: *the lamp will be lit.*

And Allah, by calling it *sirāj* — a lamp — is gently reminding the deniers of something they have forgotten. **A lamp is lit by someone. A lamp implies a hand that lit it.** You have spent your whole life warmed and illuminated by a flame you never questioned, never fed, never thanked. The sun is not a fact of physics you are owed. It is a lamp in the room of your life, and **the One who lit it is the same One you are arguing cannot raise you from the dead.**

A clear close: **Wahhāj is the word for a single, ceaseless blaze doing the work of two — light and heat, both, forever. And a lamp that constant is not an accident you inherited. It is a gift, lit on purpose, for you.**

## Element Three: ثَجَّاجًا — *Thajjāj*, the Word You Can Hear Falling

### The same gear, now in water

Now Allah moves from the heavens to what comes *down* from them, and watch — He reaches for the very same intensive form, the doubled middle letter, a third time. This passage is built on it. *Wah-hāj. Thaj-jāj.* The rhyme is not decoration. It is Allah putting three of His gifts into the same grammatical shape — the shape of *abundance that pours.*

The verse: *wa anzalnā mina-l-muʿṣirāti māʾan* — "and We sent down, from the rain-laden clouds, water" — and then:

> ثَجَّاجًا

*Thajjāj.* Root: **thāʾ–jīm–jīm** (ث-ج-ج), to pour out, to gush, to flow in torrents. Doubled jīm. Intensive form. **Water that pours and pours and pours.**

### The sound is the meaning

Say it out loud. *Thaj-jāj.* Feel where it lives in your mouth — the breathy *th* at the front, then the two hard *j*'s tumbling out after it. The word *gushes*. It sounds like a downpour hitting the ground. This is one of those words where, if you knew no Arabic at all and simply heard it, your body would guess it had something to do with rushing water.

This is not my fancy. The Prophet ﷺ used this very word for the most physical, pouring acts of worship. When asked which deed in Ḥajj is most beloved, he spoke of *al-ʿajj wa-th-thajj* — the *raising of voices* in talbiyah and the *pouring of blood* in sacrifice. *Thajj.* The gushing. The same root. It is the language of things that flow out without restraint.

### The hidden precision before the torrent

But look at the word right *before* the rain: *al-muʿṣirāt* — "the rain-laden clouds." The root here is **ʿayn–ṣād–rāʾ** (ع-ص-ر), and it is the same root from which we get *ʿaṣīr* — *juice that has been pressed out*. The same root as wringing out a wet cloth. The clouds are described as things that are being *squeezed*.

Do you feel the design in that pairing? First the squeezing — *muʿṣirāt*, the slow, patient pressing of clouds heavy with water. Then the release — *thajjāj*, the torrent. Allah has, in five words, given you the entire hydrological drama: the gathering and compressing of vapor until it can hold no more, and then the bursting forth. The restraint, and then the flood.

### Where it points

And here is the destination. Why does the torrent come?

Read the next line: *li-nukhrija bihī* — "**so that** We may bring forth by means of it." That little *li-* at the front — the *lām* of purpose — is one of the most important letters in the passage. The water does not just fall. It falls *for a reason*. It falls *on purpose*. *So that* something will live.

The torrent of *thajjāj* is not chaos. It is *aimed*. Every gushing drop is under orders. It pours down — wild, abundant, overwhelming — and yet it is in service of a single quiet intention: that grain should grow, that a seed should split open in the dark soil and reach upward.

A clear close: **Thajjāj is abundance under command — water poured out in torrents, yet every drop aimed at a purpose. The flood is wild; its mission is precise. Nothing Allah pours down is wasted, and nothing falls without a reason.**

## Bridge — From Words to Reality

Step back and see what these three words have shown us.

*Shidād* — a roof that holds, forever, without crack. *Wahhāj* — a lamp that blazes, ceaselessly, doing the work of two. *Thajjāj* — a torrent that pours, abundantly, yet entirely on purpose.

Three intensive words. Three gifts at maximum density. And notice the *sequence* they describe — it is not random. He builds the ceiling. He hangs the lamp inside it. He sends the water down through it. And then — *li-nukhrija* — out of the ground comes the food. **It is a complete system.** Roof, light, water, harvest. A house, lit and watered, with a garden growing in it.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because once you see that the sky is a *house built for you*, two truths come knocking that change how you will walk under it tomorrow.

# PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Architecture of Being Held

### Begin where you already live

Let me ask you something ordinary.

When was the last time you felt truly *safe*? Not entertained. Not distracted. *Safe* — that deep, bodily settling where some vigilant part of you finally stands down and lets go.

For most of us, that feeling is rare and fragile. We chase it constantly. We buy insurance, install locks, check the bank balance, refresh the news, scan the faces of the people we love for signs that something is wrong. There is a watchman inside us who almost never sleeps. Psychologists have a whole vocabulary for him now — hypervigilance, baseline anxiety, the nervous system stuck in a low hum of threat. Modern life has us braced. We are, most of us, quietly waiting for the ceiling to fall.

And here is the strange, beautiful irony hidden in this passage. While that inner watchman exhausts himself scanning for the next collapse — **an actual ceiling, the largest one in existence, has been holding over your head this entire time without a single failure, and he has never once given it a thought.**

### Let the ayah illuminate it

*Wa banaynā fawqakum sabʿan shidādā.* "We built **above you** seven strong heavens."

Don't pass over that word *fawqakum* — "above you." Allah did not say "We built the seven heavens." He said "We built, *above you*, seven strong ones." He locates *you* underneath the construction. You are not a spectator looking at the sky from outside. **You are inside the building.** It was raised *over your head.* The roof has an occupant, and it is you.

There is a tenderness in that preposition that we miss because it is so quiet. A builder who says "I built a roof" is describing his work. A builder who says "I built a roof *over you*" is describing his *care for you*. The first is architecture. The second is shelter.

### Building through the movements

Here is the first movement. You have spent your life seeking security in things that crack. Your savings can crack overnight. Your health can crack with one diagnosis. Your relationships, your reputation, your plans — all of them are built things, and built things, as we learned, *develop fissures*. You have been trying to feel held by structures that are, every one of them, slowly failing. *Lā ṣudūʿa lahā wa lā fuṭūr* was never said about your bank account.

Second movement. And yet — over all of that fragile, cracking life of yours — there has hung, the entire time, *one structure that does not crack.* A roof Allah built with His own attribute of *iḥkām*, perfection, the same word the scholars used: *muḥkamat al-khalq.* What if your sense of safety has been aimed at the wrong floor? What if real *sakīna* — that settling of the heart — was never going to come from the cracking things you guard, but from lifting your eyes to the One thing over your head that has never failed, and recognizing *Who* holds it?

Third movement. Because the roof is not the point. The roof is the *evidence*. The One who can suspend seven firmaments over your head for seventy years without a tremor — *that* is the One you are asked to trust with your provision, your children, your death. The unshaking sky is a daily, physical demonstration of a Caretaker whose grip does not loosen. The Prophet ﷺ taught us to say each morning, *Allāhumma anta rabbī* — "O Allah, You are my Lord" — and to entrust the day to the One who entrusts the sky to no one but Himself. **The held sky is teaching the unheld heart how to be held.**

Let that paradox sit, because it is a real one: the safest place in your life is the one place you never thought to look — straight up, at a thing so reliable it became invisible.

### What changes tomorrow

So here is the practical turn. Tomorrow, once — just once — step outside and *look up*. Deliberately. And instead of seeing weather, see the verse: *wa banaynā fawqakum sabʿan shidādā.* Say to yourself: *that has held over me my entire life and I never thanked Him.* Let the unfailing roof become a doorway into trusting the One who built it with the cracking parts of your life — the parts the watchman guards. The sky is a daily invitation to put the watchman to rest. Take it.

## Theme Two: Everything Is *For* You — The Economy of the *Lām*

### Begin where you already live

There is a particular ache that defines modern life, and it is the suspicion that **no one is really looking out for you.** That you are, fundamentally, on your own. That the universe is indifferent — vast, cold, mechanical — and you are a small accident clinging to a rock, responsible for extracting your own survival from a world that does not care whether you live or die.

This is not a fringe feeling. It is the water most people swim in now. And it produces a specific kind of exhaustion: the exhaustion of believing that if *you* don't make it happen, *nothing* will. That every good thing must be seized, earned, defended, because nothing is *given*.

Now hold that feeling against the verse.

### Let the ayah illuminate it

Go back to that little letter we noticed — the *lām* of purpose in *li-nukhrija*. "**So that** We may bring forth thereby grain and vegetation, and gardens of densely entwined growth."

I want you to feel the full weight of that *so that*. It means the entire chain we have just walked through was *aimed at an outcome*. The heavens were built — *so that*. The lamp was lit — *so that*. The clouds were squeezed and the water poured in torrents — *so that*. The whole magnificent machinery of sky and sun and rain is bending toward one purpose: *li-nukhrija bihī ḥabban wa nabātā* — that something would *grow*, that you would *eat*.

This universe, in the language of this verse, is not indifferent. **It is bent toward your provision.** The indifference you feel is contradicted by the grammar of the sky.

### Building through the movements

First movement. Notice the verbs across these five verses. *Banaynā* — We built. *Jaʿalnā* — We made. *Anzalnā* — We sent down. *Nukhrija* — We bring forth. Every single one is *Allah's* action, and every single one terminates in *your* benefit. You did not build the roof. You did not light the sun. You did not squeeze the clouds. You did not command the seed to split. **The entire supply chain that ends in the bread on your table begins in acts you had no part in.** You arrived at the very end of a process that was already running, set in motion *for you* before you existed.

Second movement. Look at the final image, the crescendo: *wa jannātin alfāfā* — "and gardens of *densely entwined* growth." The word *alfāf* comes from the root **lām–fāʾ–fāʾ** (ل-ف-ف), which means to wrap, to fold together, to intertwine — the same root from which you'd describe arms *embracing* or threads *woven* into each other. The gardens are not described as merely green or fruitful. They are *tangled together in abundance* — branches woven into branches, so lush they overlap and embrace.

[PAUSE]

This is the language of *more than enough*. Allah did not stop at *ḥabb* — grain, the bare minimum for survival. He went on to *nabāt* — vegetation, variety. And then He overflowed into *jannātin alfāfā* — gardens so thick with growth they tangle into one another. This is the signature of a Giver who does not provide grudgingly. **He does not give you survival and stop. He gives, and then He keeps giving until the garden is too full to walk through.** The very excess is a message.

Third movement. And now feel the argument close around the deniers — and around us. The surah opened with people scoffing at resurrection: *bringing the dead back to life? Impossible.* And here, in the middle of His answer, Allah uses the word *nukhrija* — "We **bring forth**" — to describe the seed pushing up out of the dead soil. It is the *same verb*, the same act of *bringing the living out of the lifeless.* Every spring, the earth that looked dead all winter erupts in *jannātin alfāfā.* You have witnessed resurrection with your own eyes, every year of your life, and called it *gardening.*

The One who brings the green shoot out of the buried seed — is He unable to bring *you* out of the buried grave? **The argument was never abstract. It was growing in your backyard.**

### What changes tomorrow

The practical turn here is about the heart's posture. If the universe is bent toward your provision — if the grammar of the sky is *li-nukhrija*, "so that you may have" — then the exhausting belief that *it all depends on you* is simply false. You are not the source. You are the recipient. Your striving matters, but it is striving *within* a system already tilted in your favor by a Caretaker who built the roof and lit the lamp before you drew breath.

So tomorrow, at one meal — just one — pause before you eat and trace the line backward. This grain came from a seed, the seed from rain, the rain from a squeezed cloud, the cloud lifted by a blazing sun, the sun hung in a roof that does not crack — and every link in that chain was forged by a Hand that meant it *for you.* Then say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* and mean it differently than you ever have. Not as a reflex. As the only honest response of someone who has just realized the whole sky was working on his behalf.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, look back at the shape of these five verses and see the building Allah constructed in language.

He gives you, in order: **a ceiling** (*shidād*), **a light inside it** (*wahhāj*), **water coming down through it** (*thajjāj*), **and a garden growing up from the ground** (*alfāf*). Read that sequence again. Roof. Lamp. Water. Garden. **It is a house.** A furnished, lit, watered home with a garden too lush to cross — and *fawqakum*, "above you," tells you who lives in it.

And three of those four gifts — *wahhāj, thajjāj,* and the firmness of *shidād* — are cast in the intensive form, the doubled-letter grammar of *abundance that pours.* This is not a minimal provision. The very architecture of the language is overflowing. Allah did not just say "I provided for you." He said it in the grammatical key of *extravagance.*

## Bringing It Together

See now that our two themes and our three words were never separate. They were one insight approached from two doors.

The **linguistic** door showed us *shidād, wahhāj, thajjāj* — firmness, blaze, torrent — each a gift at maximum density. The **thematic** door showed us a sky that *holds* us and a universe *bent toward* feeding us. They meet in a single realization: **the world over your head is not neutral.** It is a built, lit, watered house, raised *for you*, by One whose care does not crack and whose giving does not stop.

And all of it serves the surah's one great purpose. The deniers said the resurrection was impossible. Allah answered by pointing at the unfailing roof, the ceaseless lamp, and the seed He pulls living from the dead earth — and let the conclusion form silently in their chests: *the One who built all this, who does this every spring, can surely do it once more with you.* The sky was never scenery. **The sky was always the argument.**

## Questions to Carry

1. The roof has held over me for every day I have lived. When did I last thank the One who built it — or have I only ever noticed ceilings when they leaked?
2. I seek my security in things that crack. What would change if I sought it in the One thing over my head that never has?
3. I feel, somewhere deep, that I am on my own — that no one is providing for me. Is that feeling *true*, or is it contradicted by every meal I have ever eaten?
4. I have watched the dead earth turn green every spring of my life. Why is it so easy to believe in *that* resurrection and so hard to believe in my own?
5. Allah gave me not just grain, but *gardens too tangled to walk through.* Where in my life has He given me *more* than enough — and have I called it luck instead of mercy?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The sky over your head is a house Allah built, lit, and watered *for you* — a daily, unfailing argument that the One who holds it can raise you, and the One who feeds you was never indifferent.

## The Closing Invitation

Nothing about your day needs to change except one thing: where your eyes go, and what they see when they get there. Tomorrow, look up. Not at the weather — at the *roof*. Let *shidād* remind you it has never failed. Let *wahhāj* remind you the lamp was lit on purpose. And the next time you eat, trace the bread back up through the rain and the cloud and the sun to the Hand that meant it for you. You have been living inside a gift this whole time. The invitation is simply to *notice it* — and to let the noticing become gratitude, and the gratitude become trust.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You built the sky above me strong and unfailing, and I lived beneath it for years without once thanking You. You lit the lamp, You poured the rain, You raised the gardens too full to cross — all of it for me, before I knew to ask. Teach my heart to look up, to trust the Hand that holds the roof with the parts of my life I am afraid will fall, and let the green shoot You raise from the dead earth each spring be my certainty that You will raise me too. *Āmīn.*
