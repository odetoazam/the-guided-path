# Its Own Earthquake
### A Reflection on Surah Az-Zalzalah, Ayahs 1–3

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **إِذَا زُلْزِلَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ زِلْزَالَهَا**
> **وَأَخْرَجَتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ أَثْقَالَهَا**
> **وَقَالَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ مَا لَهَا**

*Idhā zulzilat-il-arḍu zilzālahā. Wa akhrajat-il-arḍu athqālahā. Wa qāl-al-insānu mā lahā.*

And in English, as close as English can come:

> *When the earth is shaken with its own final earthquake,*
> *and the earth discharges its burdens,*
> *and the human cries out, "What is happening to it?"*

I want you to slow down before we go anywhere with this.

Because there is a thing your body has trusted since before you had language. Before you had memory. Before you knew your own name, you knew this one thing without being taught it: **the ground holds still.**

Everything you have ever built — every house, every road, every plan, every assumption about tomorrow — rests on a single silent agreement you never signed and never thought about. The floor will be there. The earth will stay where it is. You put your weight down and it holds. You lie down at night and it holds. You bury your dead in it and it holds them, and you walk away, and you trust that it will keep holding them.

This is the deepest assumption of human life. It is so deep that we don't even experience it as an assumption. We experience it as *reality itself.*

[PAUSE]

Now. The people who first heard these words — in the streets of Makkah, or in the early community at Madinah — lived even closer to the ground than we do. They slept on it. They traveled across it for weeks. They knew what an earthquake was — that rare, terrifying moment when the one thing that is supposed to never move, **moves.** When the foundation of all foundations betrays you. There is no fear quite like it, because there is nowhere to run *to* — the running surface itself is what's failing.

And into that knowledge, Allah drops three short verses. They are so short, so compressed, that you could recite all three in a single breath. There is a narration — recorded by al-Tirmidhī, who himself called it *gharīb*, a singular report — that whoever recites *Idhā zulzilat* is given a reward weighed against half the Qur'an. Half the Qur'an, in eight short verses about the ground shaking.

Why? What is buried in here that weighs that much?

That's our question. Hold it. Because Allah is not, in the end, talking to you about geology. The earthquake is not the subject. The earthquake is the **doorway.** And what's on the other side of it is something about you, and about what you are carrying right now, that you have spent your whole life not looking at.

Let's open the door slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Shakes When You Say It — *Zulzilat / Zilzālahā*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse.

In most languages, the *sound* of a word and its *meaning* are unrelated. There is nothing about the noise "earthquake" that feels like the ground moving. It's an arbitrary label. The English word for the most terrifying event is no more frightening to pronounce than the word for "breakfast."

But Arabic has a category of words where the sound and the meaning are fused — where the mouth performs the meaning as it speaks. And there is a particular pattern for this: **reduplication.** When you want to express something that *repeats, trembles, rattles, vibrates* — something that happens again and again in quick succession — Arabic doubles the consonants. The structure of the word imitates the structure of the event.

You hear it in everyday Arabic. *Waswasa* — a whisper that comes again and again, the nagging repeated whisper of doubt. *Daghdagha* — to tickle, that repeated little touch. *Qalqala* — a rattling, an unsettledness. The doubled sound *is* the repeated motion.

Now say the verse's verb out loud. **Zul-zi-lat.** And the noun beside it: **Zil-zāl.** The root is *za-la-za-la* — z, l, z, l — the same two letters, twice. [PAUSE]

Feel what your mouth just did. Your tongue went to the same place twice in a row, fast — *zal-zal* — it **trembled.** The word does not describe shaking from the outside, like a scientist reporting it. The word *shakes.* The tremor is in your mouth before the meaning reaches your mind. Allah chose a word whose body is already convulsing.

This is the first thing to sit with: **the verse is not telling you about an earthquake. It is making your own tongue have one.** Before you have understood a single concept, your body has been made to tremble. That is not decoration. That is Allah teaching through the flesh — *istidlāl bil-maḥsūs* — using the felt, the sensed, the physical, to carry you toward something you cannot be told directly.

But there's more here, and it's the heart of this whole verse. Look at the structure again:

> *zulzilat-il-arḍu **zilzālahā***

The verb already said it. *Zulzilat* — "it was shaken." That's a complete statement. The sentence does not *need* another word. And yet Allah adds *zilzālahā* — "its shaking," the verbal noun, repeated from the very same root, placed right after the verb that already contains it.

In Arabic grammar this construction has a name: the **maf'ūl muṭlaq**, the cognate accusative — when you take a verb and place its own verbal noun beside it. Let me teach you what it *does* before we apply it.

When you say, in Arabic, "he was shaken" — that's a fact. But when you say "he was shaken *with a shaking*" — placing the noun-form of the very same word beside the verb — you are doing one of two things. Either you are **intensifying** it — *he was shaken with a real shaking, a total shaking, the full thing* — or you are **specifying** it — *he was shaken with a particular, definite shaking, the one that was meant.* It's the difference between "she cried" and "she cried *the cry*" — you instinctively know the second one means something final, something total, something that had been building.

Now here is what stops me every time. Allah does not just say "shaken with a shaking." He says *zilzāla**hā*** — "its shaking." **Its own.** The earthquake is given a possessive. It belongs to the earth.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that, because it's strange. An earthquake, to us, is an accident. A random event. Plates slipping. Bad luck of geography. It belongs to no one. But Allah speaks of an earthquake that the earth *owns* — *its* earthquake. The specific, appointed, full-measure convulsion that was always *hers.* As if, this entire time, the earth has been carrying within it a single shaking that had its name on it — a tremor that was destined, assigned, held in reserve.

Think of it this way. There is a difference between *a* breakdown and *the* breakdown. You can know someone who has held themselves together for thirty years — composed at every funeral, steady through every loss, never once letting it out. And you sense, watching them, that somewhere inside them is a grief with their name on it. *Their* collapse. Not a collapse — *the* one. The one that is coming, that was always coming, that they have been carrying folded up inside the chest the whole time. And when it finally comes, it will not be a little crying. It will be *theirs* — the full, total, owned release of everything that was held.

That is what Allah is saying about the earth. This patient, silent planet that has held our weight and our dead and our secrets for all of human history — it has, within it, **its own earthquake.** Not a tremor. *The* tremor. The one with its name on it. The one it has been carrying the whole time you've been walking on it, trusting it, taking it for granted.

And one word — *zilzālahā*, "its own" — tells you the most unsettling truth of this verse: **the stability you depend on was never permanence. It was patience.** The ground is not unshakeable. It is holding still *on purpose,* under an appointment, until the appointment comes.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Two: The Earth Stops Being a Victim — *Zulzilat* (passive) becomes *Akhrajat* (active)

Now I want you to watch something move between verse one and verse two. It is the kind of shift you would never notice in translation, and once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

Let me teach the tool first. In Arabic, as in English, a verb can be **passive** or **active.** Passive: "the door was opened" — something happened *to* the door; the door did nothing; we don't even know who opened it. Active: "the door opened the room to the light" — now the door is the *doer.* The same noun can be a victim in one sentence and an agent in the next, and that shift carries enormous meaning. It's the difference between *being acted upon* and *acting.*

Now look. Verse one:

> *zulzilat-il-arḍu* — "the earth **was shaken**."

That is **passive.** The earth is not the doer. Something is being done *to* it. And notice — Allah does not tell us by whom. The verb is built so that the actor is hidden, unspoken, removed from view. Why? Because in that first instant, the earth is purely a *recipient.* It is seized. Commanded. Moved by a power beyond itself that the verse deliberately leaves unnamed, because to name it would be almost too much — you simply feel the overwhelming force that has taken hold of the foundation of the world.

Now verse two:

> *wa **akhrajat**-il-arḍu athqālahā* — "and the earth **brought forth** its burdens."

[PAUSE]

The earth is now the **doer.** *Akhrajat* — "she brought out, she discharged, she expelled." This is an active verb, and the earth is its subject. In one verse it was seized; in the next it acts.

And this verb — *akhrajat* — is built on a specific form. The simple root *kharaja* just means "to go out," on its own, intransitively. But *akhraja* — the form Allah uses here — is **causative**: not "to go out" but "to *make* go out," "to bring forth," "to expel," "to deliver up." The earth doesn't merely let things drift out. It *actively delivers* what it was holding. It surrenders it. It hands it over.

Do you see the arc? Watch the earth's whole story compress into two verbs. First: *it is shaken* — passive, helpless, gripped by command. Then: *it brings forth* — active, participating, surrendering its contents with its own hand. **The earth goes from victim to witness in the space between two verses.**

Here is where it points. There is a reluctant testimony that happens in this world that you have seen. A person is dragged into the courtroom — they did not want to come, they were *summoned,* compelled, seized by a power outside themselves. Passive. They sit down on the stand against their will. And then something turns. Once they are there, under oath, the dam breaks — and they begin to *give it all up.* Everything they held back for years comes out, freely now, in full. The compelled witness becomes the willing one. The thing that was done *to* them becomes a thing they *do.*

The earth is that witness. First the Hand of Allah seizes it — *zulzilat,* it was shaken, it had no choice. Then it opens its own mouth — *akhrajat,* it brings forth — and it begins to hand over **everything it was holding.** And if you read a few verses further into this surah, you find out what it does next: it *speaks.* It narrates its news. The ground that held its silence for all of history finally testifies. This verse is the moment the witness is seized; the rest of the surah is the witness talking.

And the quiet terror underneath it: **the things you trusted to keep your secrets do not keep them forever.** The ground you buried it in. The walls that saw it. The places that held what you did when no one was watching. They are not loyal. They are under appointment, just like the earthquake. On a day Allah has named, they stop being your silent keepers and become His speaking witnesses.

### Element Three: The Burdens With No List — *Athqālahā*

Look at what the earth brings forth. *Athqālahā* — "its burdens, its weights, its heavy things."

I want you to notice an *absence* here, because this is one of those places where what Allah leaves out speaks louder than what He puts in.

When Allah says the earth discharges "its burdens" — He does not tell you what they are. There's no list. The word *athqāl* is the plural of *thiql*, and *thiql* simply means **a heavy thing, a weight, a load** — anything that presses down, anything carried with difficulty. It is left wide open. Unspecified. General.

Now, the scholars of tafsir, looking at the whole surah, fill it in — and notice that they don't disagree so much as add layers to the same picture. Al-Baghawī and the early commentators say: *the dead.* The earth heaves up the bodies it has been holding since the first burial — every person who was ever laid into it, brought back to the surface. Al-Muyassar gathers the tradition fully: *the dead and the treasures* — *mawtā wa kunūz* — both the bodies and the buried gold, the hoarded wealth, everything precious that was hidden in the ground. The earth gives up its corpses and its caches in the same convulsion.

And don't smooth past that pairing, because it is devastating. On the same day, from the same ground, by the same heave — the **bodies** and the **treasure** come up together. The two things human beings spend their lives on. We bury our dead in the earth and we bury our wealth in the earth — our savings, our security, the things we hoard, the things we are sure will protect us. And on that day the earth empties *both* out onto its surface, side by side, equally weightless now, equally surrendered. The corpse you grieved and the gold you guarded, lying in the same expelled heap.

[PAUSE]

But here's why I think Allah left the word *open.* Why He said *athqāl* — "heavy things" — and refused to narrow it. Because the moment you specify it, you contain it. If He had said "the dead," you would think: bodies. If He had said "the treasures," you would think: gold. But by saying only **"its heavy things,"** Allah lets the word hold everything the earth was ever made to carry — and lets your own conscience reach for what *you* have made it hold.

Because what is heavy? *Thiql* is the word for a burden. And the surah is about to tell you — just a few verses later — that what truly gets weighed on that day is **deeds.** Down to the atom. So when the earth brings forth its *athqāl,* its heavy things, the word is already reaching toward the heaviest things of all — not the bodies, not the gold, but the *record.* Everything that was done on the surface of this earth and pressed down into it. Every act that the ground witnessed and absorbed. The earth has been carrying the weight of human history — not just our corpses, but our *conduct* — and on that day it sets all of it down in front of us.

The earth has been the most patient archive in existence. Nothing dropped into it was ever actually lost. And the day comes when the archive opens.

### Bridge

So look at what these three short verses have done with their grammar alone.

A verb that *shakes in your mouth* — teaching you that stability was never permanence, only patience under appointment. A possessive — *its own* earthquake — telling you the collapse was assigned, named, carried in reserve the whole time. A shift from passive to active — the earth seized, then surrendering, victim becoming witness. And an open, unspecified word — *its burdens* — that quietly widens until it can hold everything you've ever set down on this earth, including your own deeds.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about **reality itself,** and about the person standing on that trembling ground.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Everything Is Being Held — Nothing Is Discarded

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a phone in your pocket, and it remembers things you have forgotten. Type a few letters and it completes a sentence you wrote two years ago. Somewhere on a server you will never see, there is a record of where you were on an ordinary Tuesday last spring, what you searched at 2 a.m., the message you typed and then deleted before sending. We have built, in our own time, an extraordinary thing: a world that *forgets nothing.* You sensed, when you were younger, that things faded — that a moment passed and was gone. And now you live with the low, constant unease of knowing they don't fade. They are held. Somewhere, everything is held.

We find this unsettling about technology. We call it surveillance. We worry about it.

And here is what these verses do: they tell you that the world has *always* been like this. That the ground itself — the dumb, silent dirt under your feet — was never a forgetter. It was a keeper. *Wa akhrajat-il-arḍu athqālahā.* The earth brings forth its burdens. It brings them forth because it *had* them. It was holding them the entire time.

Think of what we actually do with the earth. We use it precisely *because* we believe it forgets. We bury what we want gone. The dead, yes — but also the shameful, the discarded, the thing we did and never told anyone, the version of ourselves we wanted to leave behind. There is a deep human instinct that says: *put it in the ground, walk away, and it is finished. The earth will take it and the earth will keep quiet.*

The Qur'an says: the earth is not keeping quiet. **It is keeping records.** [PAUSE]

And sit with the difference between those two things, because it is the whole difference between how you think the universe works and how it actually works. You have been living, quietly, on an assumption of *erasure* — that the past dissolves, that what is unwitnessed is unrecorded, that what is buried is gone. And these verses reach under that assumption and pull it out. Nothing dissolved. Nothing was unwitnessed — the ground itself was the witness. Nothing buried is gone — it is *held,* under appointment, until the day of discharge.

Now — does this terrify you or does it free you? Because it is both, and which one it is depends entirely on what you have buried.

For the person who was wronged in the dark — who suffered something no one saw, who was told it never happened, who buried it because there was no one to tell and no one who would believe — this verse is **water in a desert.** *The earth saw.* The ground was a witness. What was done to you in a place where no human eye reached was not unwitnessed by creation. It was held. And it will be brought forth. The universe is not built to let injustice dissolve into the dirt. The dirt remembers.

And for the person who did something in the dark and walked away counting on the silence of the ground — the same verse is a summons. The thing you set down and left is not gone. It is *held.* It is *athqāl* — a weight — and weights get brought up, and weights, the surah will tell you, get weighed.

So here is what it changes about tomorrow. You begin to live as though the world is the kind of place that *holds,* not the kind that *forgets* — because it is. Which means the small good you did that no one thanked you for is not lost; it is held. And the small wrong you did that no one caught is not erased; it is held. You stop performing for the human eyes that forget and start living before the creation that remembers. **You let the earth's memory become your conscience.**

### Theme Two: When the Floor Moves — Losing the Last Thing You Trusted

Now the third verse. The human speaks.

> *wa qāl-al-insānu mā lahā* — "and the human cries out: *what is happening to it?*"

I want to spend real time here, because this is the most psychologically precise three-word sentence I know.

First, *who* speaks. Not "the believer." Not "the disbeliever." Not "the people." *Al-insān* — **the human being,** generic, all of us, the species. Whatever the differences between us, in this moment we are reduced to one reaction. And the word *insān* itself, the scholars note, carries within it the trace of *nisyān* — forgetfulness — and of *uns* — familiarity, being at ease. This is the creature who *forgets,* and who grows *comfortable.* And both of those are exactly what break in this verse.

And what does this human say? Not a sentence. Barely even a question. *Mā lahā.* Two words. "What — to it?" "What's *with* it?" "What's *wrong* with it?" It is the broken, breathless syntax of total shock. When a human being is genuinely overwhelmed — when the thing they cannot process is happening *right now* — they do not speak in full sentences. They sputter. *What — what is this — what's happening?* Allah captures the panic in the grammar itself. The sentence is too short to be calm. **It is the sound of the mind failing to keep up with reality.**

[PAUSE]

But here is what undoes me about *mā lahā.* Think about what the human is asking about. Not "what's happening to *me*?" Not "what's happening to the *sky*?" The human is asking about *the earth* — *mā lahā,* "what's wrong with *it.*" And to ask that question — in shock, in disbelief — is to confess something. **It is to confess that you never expected the earth to do anything at all.**

You only say "what's wrong with it?" about a thing you assumed would never change. You don't say it about the weather. You say it about the one thing that was supposed to be constant. The human's question is the sound of an assumption shattering in real time. The deepest, most unexamined trust a person has — *the ground will hold* — is breaking, and all the human can produce is this stunned, childlike *what's wrong with it?*

Let me bring this all the way into your life, because this is not only about the Last Day. This is about every floor you have ever stood on.

We do not, most of us, build our security on God. We build it on *stable ground* — on things we have quietly decided are permanent. The job that will always be there. The health that has always held. The person who will always pick up the phone. The body that has always done what you asked it to. The country, the savings, the routine, the roof. We walk on these the way we walk on the earth: without thinking, without thanking, *certain* they will hold. And we are at *uns* with them — at ease, familiar, comfortable. We have forgotten — *nisyān* — that they were ever gifts and not guarantees.

And then one of them moves.

The call comes. The scan comes back wrong. The person who was always there is suddenly not. And do you know what comes out of your mouth? *Mā lahā.* "What — what's happening? This wasn't supposed to —" The exact same broken syntax. The same shattered assumption. You discover, in one horrible instant, that the thing you had been standing on your whole life was never the bedrock you took it for. It was *the earth* — patient, appointed, holding still on purpose — and its purpose, for you, just ran out.

[PAUSE]

So why does Allah show us this? To frighten us? No. Read it again. Allah is showing you the human's reaction *so that you do not have to have it.*

The whole tragedy of *mā lahā* is that it is the cry of someone who **built on the wrong foundation** and only finds out at the moment of collapse. The human says "what's wrong with it?" precisely because the human spent a lifetime trusting the ground instead of the One who holds the ground still. The shock is the measure of the misplaced trust. The more total your reliance on the floor, the more total your devastation when it moves.

And here is the way out, and it's the entire point. There is One who does not shake. *Al-insān* trembles because *al-insān* built on the trembling thing. But the One who *commands* the earthquake — *zulzilat,* the hidden Actor of that passive verb — He is not in the earthquake. He is above it. He appointed it. And the heart that has put its weight down *on Him* instead of on the ground does not say *mā lahā* in panic on that day. It says, as the believers will say, *this is what the Most Merciful promised, and the messengers spoke truth.* Same event. Opposite cry. The difference is **what you were standing on the whole time.**

So what shifts tomorrow? You do a quiet audit of your floors. You ask, about each thing you have been standing on without thinking — *am I treating this as the bedrock, or as the earth?* The health, the job, the people, the plans — receive them, love them, use them — but **stand on Allah.** Put your actual weight, the weight you cannot afford to have dropped, on the only One who has never once been *zulzila* — never shaken, never moved, never appointed to collapse. So that when a floor moves — and a floor will move — you are not the human saying *what's wrong with it?* You are the one who never mistook the gift for the Giver, and whose footing was never on the ground to begin with.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Surah Bracketed by Weight

Now let me show you something that has been hiding in plain sight, and that you could only see *after* doing the work we just did.

In verse two, the earth brings forth its **athqāl** — its *burdens, its heavy things.* That word comes from the root *th-q-l* — weight, heaviness.

Now travel to the end of this same short surah, just a few verses on. There, Allah says that whoever does a **mithqāl** — *a weight* — of an atom of good will see it, and whoever does a **mithqāl** of an atom of evil will see it. *Mithqāl.* Same root. *Th-q-l.* Weight.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has built? The surah opens with the earth surrendering its **great weights** — *athqāl,* the massive burdens, the bodies and the treasures and the heaped record of all history. And the surah closes by telling you that what is actually measured, what actually counts, is the **weight of a single atom** — *mithqāl dharrah.* The surah is bracketed, beginning and end, by the same root: weight. It opens with the heaviest thing imaginable — a planet emptying its entire load — and it narrows, and narrows, until it lands on the lightest thing imaginable: one atom's worth of one deed.

And *that* is the message of the whole surah, hidden in its skeleton. The day the earth gives up its colossal weights is the day you are judged by your *tiny* ones. The cosmic upheaval is in service of an atomic accounting. All of that shaking, all of that discharge, the whole planet turned inside out — for *what?* So that an atom's weight of what you did would not be lost. The scale of the cataclysm is the measure of how seriously your smallest act is taken.

The earth would not have shaken loose its mountains to overlook the thing you did when you thought no one saw.

### Bringing It Together

So the grammar and the themes were always the same truth, approached from two sides.

The word *zilzālahā* — "*its own* earthquake" — taught us that stability is patience under appointment, not permanence. And the human's cry *mā lahā* — "what's *wrong* with it?" — is simply what it sounds like when a person who forgot that runs out of time. One is the truth; the other is the shock of meeting the truth unprepared.

The shift from *zulzilat* (the earth is shaken) to *akhrajat* (the earth brings forth) taught us that the silent ground becomes a speaking witness. And the open word *athqāl* — "its burdens" — taught us that what it brings forth is everything it was holding, including the record of what we set down on it. One is the witness taking the stand; the other is the testimony in its hands.

It all converges on a single point: **you are standing, right now, on a witness, carrying a weight, under an appointment.** And the only question is what you have built your footing on.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. I do not have your answers.

1. What have I buried in the ground — done in the dark, left unspoken — believing the earth would keep it quiet? And how would I live differently if I truly knew the earth was holding it, not hiding it?

2. Which of my "floors" am I standing on as though it were bedrock — a person, a job, a body, a plan — that is actually *the earth:* a gift, holding still on appointment, not a guarantee?

3. If a floor moved tomorrow, would my cry be *mā lahā* — "what's wrong with it?" — or would I find that my weight was never on the floor to begin with?

4. The surah says an atom's weight is seen. What is the smallest good I have been dismissing as *too small to matter* — and the smallest wrong I have been excusing as *too small to count?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

The ground you trust to hold still and keep quiet is a patient witness under appointment — holding everything you set down on it, until the day it surrenders its weight and you are measured by your own.

### The Closing Invitation

I am not going to tell you to be afraid. Fear is the human's reaction — *mā lahā* — and the whole point is that you don't have to have it.

I am going to invite you, instead, to do one small thing this week. The next time you put your foot down on the ground — getting out of bed, stepping out a door — let it remind you, just for a second, of two things. That the ground is holding still *for you,* on purpose, by His command. And that it is holding still *temporarily.* Let the most ordinary, ignored sensation in your life — the floor under your feet — become a tap on the shoulder. *Not forever. Not yours. Stand on the One who holds it.*

That is all. Let the earth, which never forgets, remind you not to forget.

### A Closing Du'a

O Allah, You who hold the earth still by Your command and shake it by Your command — we have been standing on Your gifts as though they were guarantees, and forgetting You beneath them. Steady our hearts on You alone, so that when every floor we trusted moves, we are not among those who cry *what is happening?* — but among those who knew, all along, where their weight truly rested. And make the earth bring forth, on our behalf, an atom's weight of good that we set down in it for Your sake. *Āmīn.*
