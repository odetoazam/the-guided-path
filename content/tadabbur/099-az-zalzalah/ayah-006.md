# The Scattering and the Showing

## Surah Az-Zalzalah, Ayah 6

> [ayah:99:6] يَوْمَئِذٍ يَصْدُرُ ٱلنَّاسُ أَشْتَاتًا لِّيُرَوْا۟ أَعْمَـٰلَهُمْ

*Yawma'idhin yaṣduru-n-nāsu ashtātan li-yuraw aʿmālahum*

**"That Day, mankind will proceed forth in scattered groups, to be shown their deeds."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Crowd That Becomes a Procession

Let me put you somewhere before we begin.

Imagine the largest crowd you have ever stood inside. Not seen on a screen — stood *inside*. A stadium emptying after a match. A pilgrimage. A city square on a night when everyone has come out for the same reason. Feel what that feels like. There is a strange comfort in it. You are anonymous. You are carried. You move when the crowd moves. Your individual choices dissolve into the choices of the mass. If you stumble, the crowd holds you up. If you go quiet, no one notices. You are *one of many*, and there is a deep, almost narcotic safety in being one of many.

Now hold that feeling, because the Quran is about to take it away from you.

This surah, Az-Zalzalah — "The Earthquake" — opens with the ground itself losing its composure. The earth is shaken with its final convulsion. It throws out its burdens — the dead it has held, the secrets it has swallowed. And then, astonishingly, **the earth speaks**. It testifies. It tells everything it witnessed, because its Lord inspired it to speak.

And here is the moment we are sitting with today. After the ground has emptied and spoken — what happens to the people?

The crowd forms. The greatest gathering in all of existence. Every human being who ever drew breath, assembled on one plain. And you would expect, after such a gathering, that the surah would describe a *crowd*. A sea of faces. A mass.

[PAUSE]

But that is not what happens. Watch the very next thing Allah says.

He does not describe a crowd. **He describes a scattering.** And He does not say the people *will see*. He says they will *be shown*. Two small words, and inside each of them is a reality so precise that once you see it, you will not be able to stand inside a crowd the same way again.

The question I want you to carry into this reflection is simple: **What is the difference between a crowd and a procession of individuals?** Because the Day of Judgment, in this ayah, is the moment the first becomes the second. And the engine that drives that transformation is sitting inside two verbs that most translations rush right past.

Let's slow down. Let's earn this.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Yaṣduru* — The Verb of the Herd Leaving the Water

Before we open this word, I want to teach you something about how Arabic thinks, because without it the verb will pass you by.

In the world of the Quran's first listeners — a desert people, a people of herds and wells — there were two great movements that organized the rhythm of life. There was **wurūd** (ورود): the coming *in*, the moving *toward* the water. You drive the thirsty herd down to the well, and they crowd in, pressing together, desperate, converging on the one thing that keeps them alive. And then there was **ṣudūr** (صدور): the going *out*, the moving *away* from the water once the thirst is answered. The herd, having drunk, turns and disperses back across the open land, each animal drifting toward its own grazing.

Wurūd is the gathering. Ṣudūr is the dispersal. One word for coming to the water, one word for leaving it.

Hold that image. Now look at the verb Allah chose.

> يَصْدُرُ — *yaṣduru*

It is from the root **ṣ-d-r** (ص د ر) — the very root of *ṣudūr*, the herd leaving the water. The same root, by the way, that gives us *ṣadr*, the chest — that which goes out in front, that which leads. The word is alive with the image of **issuing forth, of departing from a place you had been pressed into.**

Now here is why this is breathtaking.

Earlier in the Quran, when Allah describes humanity approaching the reckoning — approaching the Fire, the standing — He uses the language of *wurūd*. *"And there is none of you except he will come to it"* — *wa in minkum illā wāriduhā* (19:71). Coming *to*. Pressing *in*. The whole of humanity driven, like a vast thirsting herd, *toward* the great standing — the *mawqif al-ḥisāb*, the place of reckoning where every soul is brought to a halt and made to account.

And here, in our ayah, after the reckoning — they **ṣadara**. They issue forth. They leave the water.

The classical commentators saw exactly this. Al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi, the Jalalayn — they are unanimous in the picture: *yarjiʿūna ʿan mawqif al-ḥisāb* — they return, they depart, *from the place of reckoning*, having stood, having been accounted. Al-Baghawi adds the destination: *fa-ākhidhun dhāta-l-yamīni ila-l-jannah, wa-ākhidhun dhāta-l-shimāli ila-n-nār* — one taking the way of the right hand toward the Garden, one taking the way of the left hand toward the Fire.

[PAUSE]

So sit with the full motion now. All of humanity was driven *in* — pressed together at the watering place of the reckoning, the way a desperate herd crowds a well. The greatest convergence in history. And then, the reckoning done, **they turn and issue out** — *yaṣduru* — back across an open plain that has no horizon, each toward a destination that is now, finally, theirs alone.

Notice what the verb does that no abstract word could. It does not say "the people will go to their fates." It activates an ancient, bodily, *felt* image: the herd leaving the water. And every desert listener knew the one truth about that moment — **when the herd leaves the water, the herd comes apart.** They came in as a pressing mass. They leave as scattering individuals, each drifting toward its own ground.

The verb has already begun to break the crowd. And the next word finishes the job.

## Element Two: *Ashtātan* — The Word That Sounds Like What It Means

> أَشْتَاتًا — *ashtātan*

Listen to it before you understand it. *Ash-tā-tan.* Say it. There is a fracturing in the mouth — the *sh*, then the hard *t*, then the doubling, then the cut-off *tan*. The word itself feels scattered, broken into pieces, dispersed across the tongue. This is no accident. Arabic, more than almost any language, lets the *sound* of a word carry the *meaning* of a word, and here the phonetics are doing real work: **a word about dispersal that disperses as you pronounce it.**

The root is **sh-t-t** (ش ت ت). It means to be scattered, to be separated, to be diverse and disparate and pulled into pieces. *Ashtāt* is its plural — *scattered groups, separate parties, things that do not cohere.* From this same root comes the Arabic expression *shattā* — used for things that are utterly various, that have nothing holding them to one shape.

Now grammar matters here. The people — *an-nās* — are one word. Singular in form, collective in meaning. **One mass.** One noun: "mankind." And then immediately, jammed up against it, *ashtātan* — scattered, plural, fragmented. The collective noun and the scattering descriptor are placed side by side, and the second word detonates the first.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the asymmetry? They were gathered **as one** — *an-nās*, mankind, the single great noun. They depart **as fragments** — *ashtātan*, scattered, no two threads of the cloth going the same way.

And here is the deepest layer of this word — something easy to miss. *Ashtātan* does not only mean they split into two groups, the people of the right and the people of the left. The commentators say *firaqan firaqan* — group after group after group. The scattering is not a clean binary. It is a *shattering*. Within the people of the Garden there are ranks upon ranks. Within the people of the Fire there are depths below depths. The word refuses to let you imagine even a tidy division into two tribes. It insists on a spectrum so fine that, in the end, **the only honest number of groups is the number of human beings.**

Because what, finally, scatters them? Not their nation. Not their tribe. Not the language they spoke or the crowd they stood inside. Look at what the ayah is about to tell us drives the scattering. They scatter according to *aʿmālahum* — **their deeds.** And no two people's deeds are identical. Which means the scattering, followed to its end, does not stop at two groups, or seventy, or seven thousand. It keeps subdividing until **every soul stands in a group of one.**

This is the terror and the dignity of the word *ashtātan*. The crowd you hid inside your whole life — the nation, the movement, the consensus, the "everyone does this" — that crowd does not survive the watering place. You came in as *an-nās*. You leave as *you*.

## Element Three: *Li-Yuraw* — Not "To See," But "To Be Shown"

We come now to the two final words, and to the most precise thing in the entire ayah. If you take only one grammatical observation from this whole reflection, take this one.

> لِّيُرَوْا۟ — *li-yuraw*

Most translations render this "to see their deeds." That is not wrong, but it has quietly dropped the most important feature of the word, and I want to restore it to you.

First, the *li-* at the front. This is the **lām of purpose** — *li-yuraw*, "*in order that* they be shown." It tells us that everything before it — the issuing forth, the scattering — is not random motion. It has a goal. The whole vast procession out of the reckoning exists *for one reason*. The scattering is not the point. The scattering serves a point, and the point is what comes next.

Now the verb itself. The root is **r-ʾ-y** (ر أ ي), the root of seeing. But Allah did not use the simple form, *li-yaraw*, "that they may see." He used a heavier form — **Form IV, and in the passive**: *li-yuraw*, **"that they may be shown,"** "that they may be *made* to see."

Let me make sure the weight of that lands. There is a world of difference between *seeing* and *being shown*.

When you *see* something, you are the one in control. You direct your gaze. You can look, and you can look away. You choose the angle, the moment, the distance. Seeing is an act you perform upon the world.

When you are *shown* something, the control is gone. Another hand directs your gaze. Another will decides what is placed before you, and lifts it, and holds it there, and does not let you look away. **To be shown is to be made to see what you might never have chosen to look at.**

[PAUSE]

Feel the reversal. In this life, we are the seers. We look where we want. We curate what crosses our vision. We scroll past what unsettles us. We have spent a lifetime perfecting the art of *looking away* — from our own actions, from the thing we did and buried, from the deed we have not let ourselves examine since the night it happened.

On that Day, the seeing is taken out of our hands. *Li-yuraw* — **they will be shown.** A hand that is not theirs will lift the deed up before their eyes and hold it there. And the passive voice does something else, something the Arabic does with terrifying economy: **it leaves the One who shows them unnamed.** It does not say "so that *We* may show them" — it says "so that they may *be shown*." The agent is hidden behind the passive, and every listener knows exactly who the hidden agent is. It is the same Lord who, three verses earlier, inspired the *earth* to speak. The One who can make the ground testify can certainly make the soul *look*.

And notice the final word — *aʿmālahum*, "their deeds." Their *deeds*. Not a report of their deeds. Not a summary, not a verdict read aloud. The deeds *themselves*, made into something that can be placed before the eyes and *seen*. Think about what that means. A deed is the most invisible thing in the world. An act of kindness done in secret, a word of cruelty muttered under the breath, a prayer prayed in an empty room, a glance that should not have lingered — these are *events*, gone the instant they happen, leaving no body, no mass, no surface to reflect light. And on that Day they are made *visible*. The invisible is given a face. *Li-yuraw aʿmālahum* — the most unseeable things you ever did are turned into the most undeniable things you will ever see.

## Bridge: What the Words Have Shown Us

Look at what these three words have built, standing together.

*Yaṣduru* — they issue forth, the herd leaving the water, the great convergence beginning to come apart.

*Ashtātan* — they scatter, the single mass fragmenting down and down until every soul stands alone.

*Li-yuraw* — and the whole motion has one purpose: they are *made to see* the deeds they spent a lifetime not looking at.

Three words. A procession, a scattering, and an unveiling. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about language — but about the shape of reality itself, and about the life you are living right now.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Crowd Was Always a Loan

Let me begin where you actually live, not where the ayah ends.

You belong to crowds. We all do. It is not a flaw; it is how human beings are built. You belong to a family, a people, a generation. You belong to the group chat, the followers, the movement, the side. And much of the moral weight of your life feels *distributed* across these crowds. When everyone around you does a thing, the thing feels lighter. "This is just how people are now." "Everyone cuts this corner." "My whole generation thinks this way." The crowd absorbs the responsibility the way a stadium absorbs a single voice. You are carried. You are one of many.

There is a modern word for the engine underneath this. Psychologists call it *diffusion of responsibility*. The famous, terrible finding: a person collapses on a busy street, and the *more* people are present, the *less* likely any single one is to help — because each one feels the responsibility spread thin across the crowd. "Someone else will do it." The presence of the many dilutes the duty of the one. We are wired to let the crowd carry what we do not want to carry alone.

And we do not only do this with help we withhold. We do it with sins we commit. The crowd is the great anesthetic of the conscience. *Everyone is doing it. It is normal now. Who am I to be the strange one?* The crowd does not just give us company. **It gives us cover.**

Now bring the ayah down onto that.

*Yaṣduru-n-nāsu ashtātan.* The people issue forth — scattered.

What this verse declares, with the force of the herd leaving the water, is that **the crowd was never real in the way you trusted it to be.** It was a loan. It was the temporary pressing-together at the watering place of this world. And the instant the reckoning is done, the loan is recalled. The crowd does not walk you to your destination. The crowd *evaporates at the exit.* You issue forth *ashtātan* — and the word, remember, keeps subdividing until you are a party of one.

[PAUSE]

Here is the thing that should reach in and rearrange something. The "everyone" you appealed to your whole life — *that everyone is not standing next to you when you are shown your deeds.* The generation does not step forward to take its share of your glance. The movement does not absorb the act you did in its name. The group chat does not stand in the dock beside you. Every single one of them has *also* issued forth, scattered, each to be shown *their* own deeds. The diffusion of responsibility is revealed for what it always was: an illusion of this world's crowding, dissolved the moment the crowd disperses.

And notice — the Quran is not cruel about this. It is *clarifying*. It is doing you the mercy of telling you *now*, while you can still act, that the cover you have been hiding under is temporary. The accountability was always individual. The crowd merely hid that fact from you. *Ashtātan* tears off the disguise.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means you can no longer let any act be authored by "everyone." Tonight, examine the places in your life where the crowd is doing your moral accounting for you. The thing you permit yourself *because* others permit it. The duty you neglect *because* surely someone else will carry it. Name one. Just one. And ask: *when I issue forth ashtātan — when this exact crowd has scattered and I stand in a party of one — will I still want to have done this?* That single question, asked honestly, is the verse doing its work in you before the Day arrives.

## Theme Two: You Will Finally Have to Look

Now the second movement — and this one is gentler and more frightening at once.

There is a deed in your life you have not looked at.

I do not know which one it is. You do. There is something you did — perhaps long ago — that you have filed away in the one folder of the mind you never open. You have become an expert at *not looking*. When the memory rises, you change the channel of your own attention with a speed you have practiced for years. You have built, around this one deed, an entire architecture of *looking away.* And you call the silence "moving on."

We live in the golden age of looking away. We have engineered the world for it. The feed scrolls; the unpleasant thing slides off the top of the screen. The notification arrives; the uncomfortable thought is interrupted. We have built machines whose entire purpose is to make sure we never have to sit, undistracted, with a single image of our own life. We are seers who have forgotten that seeing is a choice — and we have chosen, ten thousand times a day, to look at *anything else.*

And into that life of practiced looking-away, the ayah speaks one word: *li-yuraw.*

**You will be shown.**

[PAUSE]

Remember what we uncovered in the grammar. Not *you will see* — that you could survive, because seeing you control. *You will be shown.* The folder you never open is opened by a hand that is not yours. The deed you filed away is lifted up and held before your eyes, and — here is the unbearable mercy of the passive voice — *you cannot look away,* because the looking is no longer yours to direct. The One who inspired the earth to speak now inspires your own gaze to land, and hold.

And what is shown is not a description. It is *aʿmālahum* — the deeds themselves. The most invisible events of your life given a face you must meet.

Now — I called this the *mercy* of the verse, and I meant it, so let me show you why.

There are two ways to first meet your hidden deed. You can be shown it on the Day, when nothing can be changed, when the showing is only verdict. Or you can be shown it *tonight* — by choosing, while choosing is still yours, to do the thing the whole world is built to prevent: **to look.** To open the folder yourself. To sit with the deed you buried, and see it, and bring it before the only One who can lift it off you.

This is what *tawbah* — turning back — actually *is*, seen through this ayah. Repentance is not primarily speaking words. **Repentance is electing to be shown your deed early.** It is volunteering for the *li-yuraw* now, in the privacy of the night, while the showing can still become a washing instead of a sentence. The believer is the one who runs *toward* the seeing — who says to the soul, *let us look at this together, now, before we are made to look at it then.*

[PAUSE]

And here the surah lifts its own weight, because look at where it is going. Our ayah ends "to be shown their deeds." And the very next words — the closing of the surah — tell you *what* will be shown:

> *"So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (99:7–8)

Do you feel the design? In our verse, the verb was *passive* and *collective*: *li-yuraw*, "**they** are shown." In the next two verses, the very same root of seeing turns *active* and *individual*: *yarah* — "**he** will see it," "**he** will see it." First the great procession is made to see; then it narrows, in two strokes, to one human being seeing one atom's weight. The scattering reaches its destination. *Ashtātan* — group after group, down to the party of one — and that one, at last, *sees.* Not a deed lost. Not an atom unaccounted. The most invisible good you ever did, the kindness no one witnessed — *you will see it.* And the smallest evil you thought had vanished — *you will see that too.*

So the showing is not only terror. For the one who spent the kindness no one saw, it is the day the invisible is finally honored. The atom's weight of good you thought fell into the void — it was being *kept.* It was being made *visible* for this exact moment, *li-yuraw,* so that you could be shown that nothing — *nothing* — was ever wasted.

What does this change about tomorrow? Two things, and they are the same thing from two sides. First: stop running from the one deed you will not look at. Choose to be shown it now. Sit with it tonight and bring it to your Lord, so that the showing becomes a cleansing and not a sentence. And second: stop despising the atom's weight of good — the small kindness, the secret prayer, the patience no one praised. You are storing visible treasure. *Li-yuraw.* It is all being kept to be shown to you.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape, because the surah has been building it under our feet.

The earthquake empties the earth (verses 1–2). The earth, inspired, *testifies* (verses 3–5). And then humanity issues forth from the reckoning — scattered, to be shown their deeds (verse 6) — and what they are shown is weighed to the atom, good and evil, each one *seen* (verses 7–8).

Watch the single thread the root of *seeing* stitches through the ending. *Li-yuraw* — they are shown (passive, the whole crowd). *Yarah… yarah* — he sees it, he sees it (active, the single soul, twice). The surah moves from the **collective made to look** to the **individual who looks** — which is exactly the journey our one verse enacts in miniature: *an-nās*, the one mass, becoming *ashtātan*, the scattered, becoming, finally, the single human standing before a single atom of his own doing. **The grammar of the surah is the scattering itself.** The crowd of the verb narrows, word by word, to the one who sees.

## Bringing It Together

Our two discoveries are one discovery seen from two directions.

The *linguistic* journey showed us a procession: the herd leaving the water (*yaṣduru*), the mass fragmenting to a party of one (*ashtātan*), the soul made to see what it would not look at (*li-yuraw*).

The *thematic* journey showed us why this should reorder our living *now*: because the crowd we hide inside is a loan recalled at the exit, and the deed we refuse to look at will be shown to us by a hand that is not ours — unless we choose to be shown it early, while the showing can still save us.

They are the same truth. **You are more alone, and more seen, than the crowd has let you believe.** And both halves of that — the aloneness and the being-seen — are mercies, because they are telling you the truth *now*, while you can still act on it.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life is "everyone does it" doing my moral accounting for me — and would I still do it if I knew the crowd scatters at the exit?
2. What is the one deed I have built a lifetime of looking-away around? Am I willing to be shown it tonight, by my own choosing?
3. What atom's weight of good have I been despising as too small to matter — when it is being kept, visible, to be shown to me?
4. If I issued forth *ashtātan* tomorrow — a party of one — what would I most wish I had carried, and what would I most wish I had set down?
5. Am I a *seer* who looks away, or am I learning to be one who looks?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The crowd you hid inside dissolves at the gate of the reckoning, and you walk out alone to be made to see the deeds you spent a lifetime refusing to look at — so look now, while looking is still yours.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to wait to be shown. That is the whole gift hidden inside the dread of this verse. The *li-yuraw* of the Last Day can become a *li-yuraw* of tonight — a quiet, deliberate, voluntary sitting-with the truth of your own actions, brought before the One who shows. Let this verse not merely frighten you about a Day to come. Let it move you to open, this week, the one folder you have kept closed — and to bring it to your Lord before it is lifted before your eyes. And let it move you, too, to stop dismissing the small good. You are scattering toward a destination built atom by atom. Build it on purpose.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who inspired the earth to speak and who will show every soul its deeds — let me be shown my failings *now*, by my own turning, while the showing can still become a cleansing. Make me the one who looks rather than the one who looks away. And on the Day we issue forth, scattered and alone, let what You show me of my smallest good outweigh all that I feared to see. Āmīn.
