# Lord of the Rising and the Setting

### *Rabbu-l-mashriqi wa-l-maghribi lā ilāha illā huwa fa-ttakhidh-hu wakīlā*

> **"Lord of the East and the West — there is no god but Him — so take Him as your Disposer of affairs."**
> — Surah Al-Muzzammil, 73:9

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind These Words

Before we touch a single word of this ayah, I want you to feel where it falls.

Imagine the earliest days. Revelation has begun, but the world has not yet been told. A man comes home, shaken to his core, and asks to be wrapped in a cloak. He is not yet "the Prophet" in the eyes of his city — he is a man carrying something so vast that his body trembles under it. And into that trembling, the voice comes: *Yā ayyuhā-l-muzzammil* — "O you wrapped up one."

**Stand in the night. Recite slowly. Remember the Name of your Lord. Cut yourself off to Him — completely.**

That is the build-up across the first eight verses. Notice — these are not gentle suggestions for a man at ease. This is *training*. Verse five tells us why: *innā sanulqī ʿalayka qawlan thaqīlā* — "We are about to lay upon you a heavy word." A weight is coming. A mission that will cost him his city, his safety, his sleep, his people. And before the weight lands, he is being strengthened in the dark, alone, on his feet, before his Lord.

[PAUSE]

And then comes our verse. After all the commands — stand, recite, remember, devote — there is a turn. Because a soul being asked to carry something impossibly heavy will eventually ask the only honest question:

*But who is going to carry* **me***?*

You can tell someone to stand all night. You can tell them to cut themselves off from every comfort. But unless you tell them **who they are leaning on**, you have only given them more weight, not less.

So Allah does something here. He stops issuing commands — and instead He *introduces Himself*. He tells the trembling one exactly who is on the other end of all this devotion. And in nine short words, He moves from the widest thing the eye can see, to the deepest thing the heart can do.

Here is what I want you to wonder about as we begin. This ayah names Allah as **Lord of the East and the West** — the One who owns the horizons, who commands the sunrise and the sunset, who governs the entire visible sweep of creation. And then, in the very same breath, it asks the human being to do something almost shockingly small and personal: *fa-ttakhidh-hu wakīlā* — "so take Him as the one who handles your affairs."

Why would the Owner of the cosmos describe Himself in such cosmic terms — only to then offer Himself for the most domestic, intimate job imaginable: *managing your day*?

[PAUSE]

Stay with that question. Everything that follows is the unfolding of it.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Al-Mashriq wa-l-Maghrib* — Not "East and West," but "The Place of Rising and the Place of Setting"

Let me teach you something about how Arabic builds a word, because if you do not see this, you will read straight past the most visual image in the ayah.

In Arabic, there is a particular mould — a *shape* — that words can be poured into, called the **ism makān**: the noun of *place*. You take a verb of action and reshape it so that it no longer means the action, but **the location where the action happens**. In English we do something similar, but quietly. "To bake" is an action; a "bakery" is the place. "To play" is an action; a "playground" is the place.

Now hold onto that, and look at our two words.

The root here is **sh-r-q** — to rise, to shine, the radiance of the sun breaking over the edge of the world. Allah does **not** simply say *sharq*, "east," as a flat compass direction. He says **al-mashriq** — poured into the mould of *place*. It means: **the place where the rising happens.** The very spot on the horizon where, every single morning, the light tears open the dark.

And its partner, the root **gh-r-b** — to set, to depart, to grow distant and disappear. Again, not *gharb*, a direction, but **al-maghrib** — **the place where the setting happens.** The edge of the world where the light slips away and is swallowed.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference? "East and West" is a map. **"The place of rising and the place of setting" is a daily event.** It is not coordinates. It is *the most reliable drama in all of human experience* — the thing every human being who has ever lived has watched: light arriving, light departing. Dawn and dusk. The opening and closing of every single day since the first day.

And Allah says: **I am the Lord of that.**

Now — here is where it deepens. Why these two? Why name the rising and the setting specifically?

There is a device in Arabic and in the deepest layers of human language called **merism**. You name the two extremes in order to capture *everything in between*. When we say someone "searched high and low," we do not mean only the high and the low — we mean **everywhere**. When the Quran says Allah created "the heavens and the earth," the two poles stand for *the entire created order*. You name the two ends to seize the whole.

So when Allah says **"Lord of the place of rising and the place of setting,"** He is not claiming two spots on a horizon. He is claiming **the entire arc of existence the sun travels** — and everything beneath it. From where light begins to where light ends. **The whole sweep of the world, named by its two edges.**

[PAUSE]

Let me show you that this is deliberate, by reading an absence.

Elsewhere in the Quran, Allah uses the *plural*: *rabbu-l-mashāriqi wa-l-maghārib* — "Lord of the many risings and the many settings" (Surah 70:40), because the sun rises at a slightly different point on the horizon every day of the year. And elsewhere still, the *dual*: *rabbu-l-mashriqayn wa rabbu-l-maghribayn* — "Lord of the two easts and the two wests" (Surah 55:17).

But **here**, in the verse meant to steady a trembling heart, He uses the **singular**: *al-mashriq, al-maghrib*. One rising. One setting. **One unbroken sweep.**

Why the singular here? Notice *who* this verse is for. It is for a man being handed an impossible burden, who needs not a lesson in astronomy but a *sense of total enclosure* — the feeling that wherever he turns, from the first light of the day to the last, **he is inside the domain of his Lord.** The singular gives you the whole sky as *one continuous roof*. There is no gap in it. There is no seam where you could fall out of His dominion.

[PAUSE]

So sit with what this first element has revealed. The Owner of the horizons is not far away in His cosmic majesty. The very same sun that will rise on your hardest morning, and set on your most exhausting night — **that rising and that setting belong to Him.** Your whole day, from its first light to its last, is already inside His hand before you wake.

That is the first thing. Hold it. We will need it.

## Element Two: *Lā ilāha illā Huw* — The Hinge of the Entire Verse

Now we come to the center of the ayah — and I mean that structurally. There are three movements here, and this is the middle one. It is the hinge on which the whole thing turns.

*Lā ilāha illā huwa.* "There is no god but Him."

We say these words so often that they have gone smooth in our mouths, like a stone worn down by a river. So let me slow it down and show you the architecture, because the *grammar* of this phrase is doing something the translation cannot carry.

In Arabic there is a specific construction called **lā al-nāfiyah li-l-jins** — "the *lā* that negates the entire category." It is not a weak "no." It is the strongest, most total negation the language possesses. When you put this *lā* in front of a noun, you are not saying "there is no *particular* god here." You are saying **"there does not exist, anywhere in all of reality, a single member of this entire category."** It wipes the whole field to zero.

So *lā ilāha* — before we even reach the exception — is a *complete erasure*. **No god. None. Not one. Nowhere. Of any kind.** The slate is wiped utterly blank. Everything that a heart could possibly turn to, lean on, fear, or beg from — gone. Cancelled. Reduced to nothing.

[PAUSE]

And the human heart, hearing only that first half, would *panic*. Because the heart cannot live in a vacuum. It was built to lean. Take away everything it leans on and you have not freed it — you have left it falling.

And **then** — only then, into that total emptiness — comes the exception: *illā huwa.* "Except Him."

Feel the sequence. First everything you ever leaned on is pulled away. Then, in the silence where they used to be, **one** remains. Not added to the others — He is what is left **when every other thing has been proven to be nothing.** Tawhid is not "Allah is the best of the gods." It is: *the others were never there at all, and He is the only One who ever was.*

[PAUSE]

Now — why is this here? Why does this phrase sit *between* "Lord of the horizons" and "take Him as your Disposer"?

Because it is the bridge. Look at the logic of the three movements:

First: *He owns the entire arc of the world* — the place of rising to the place of setting.
Second: *and there is nothing else in existence to turn to* — no god but Him.
Third: *therefore* — take Him as the one who handles your affairs.

Do you see it? The first movement tells you **He is enough** — His dominion covers everything. The second movement tells you **there is no alternative** — there is no one else to even consider. And only a heart that has absorbed *both* of those can do the third thing freely. You cannot wholeheartedly hand your life to Allah while you are still keeping a backup option in your other hand. The *lā ilāha* is what empties that other hand. It does not leave you with nothing. **It leaves you with nothing-but-Him — which is the same as leaving you with everything.**

[PAUSE]

This is what the early commentators saw in the structure. Al-Qurṭubī notes that "whoever truly *knows* that He is Lord of the risings and the settings" — whoever lets that knowledge land — **"severs his striving and his hope toward Him alone."** The knowing produces the cutting-off. The *lā ilāha illā huwa* is where the knowing becomes a severing.

So this central phrase is not a doctrinal footnote dropped into the middle. It is the **pivot**. It takes the cosmic fact of the first movement and turns it into the personal surrender of the third. It is the hinge.

Hold that. One more word remains — and it is the one the whole verse has been building toward.

---

## Element Three: *Fa-ttakhidh-hu Wakīlā* — "So Take Him, For Yourself, as the One Who Carries Your Affairs"

Now we arrive at the destination of the ayah. And there are two pieces of this little phrase I need to open for you, because both are doing enormous work.

### First: the verb — *ittakhidh*, and the secret of its shape

Let me teach you a feature of Arabic verbs before we use it.

Arabic verbs come in *forms* — patterns that take a basic root and bend its meaning in a specific direction. The plain root here is **ʾ-kh-dh**, *akhadha*: simply "to take." But the verb in our ayah is not *khudh-hu*, "take it." It is **ittakhidh-hu** — the same root bent into what grammarians call **Form VIII**.

And Form VIII, very often, carries a particular flavor: it is **reflexive** — the action loops back onto the doer, *for the doer's own benefit, by the doer's own deliberate choice.* The plain form "to take" becomes, in Form VIII, **"to take *for oneself*, to adopt, to acquire deliberately as one's own."** *Ittikhādh* is not a casual grab. It is the act of *making something yours* — choosing it, claiming it, installing it in your life on purpose.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what that does to the command. Allah does not say "rely on Me" as a vague disposition. He uses the verb of **deliberate, self-implicating acquisition.** *Fa-ttakhidh-hu* — "**take Him for yourself.** Make Him — by your own choice, for your own sake — the One you have appointed." It is the language you would use for adopting something into the very center of your life and naming it yours.

This matters enormously, and here is why. Tawakkul — reliance on Allah — is sometimes imagined as a passive collapse. "I'll just let go and let things happen." But the verb here is the opposite of passive. It is an **active appointment.** Form VIII makes it a decision *you* reach for, *you* claim, *you* install. You do not drift into trusting Allah. You **take** Him — deliberately, the way you would deliberately appoint the most capable person you know to handle the thing you cannot handle yourself.

And there is a tenderness hidden in this being a command. He does not say "I am your wakīl, like it or not." He says **"take** Me as your wakīl" — leaving the reaching-out to you. He makes Himself *available* for the role, and asks you to be the one who claims Him for it. The relationship is offered; you accept it.

### Second: the role — *wakīl*

What is a *wakīl*?

The root is **w-k-l**, and from it comes the whole world of **tawkīl** — delegation, appointment, entrusting. A *wakīl* is the one to whom you **hand over your affairs** so that *he* manages them on your behalf. In the language of daily life, across the centuries, a *wakīl* is your **appointed agent** — the one who acts in your place, conducts your business, represents your interests, carries the matter you have entrusted to him.

Listen now to how the early commentators gloss this single word — and notice they speak almost with one voice. Al-Jalālayn: *muwakkalan lahu umūruk* — "One to whom your affairs are committed." Al-Baghawī: *qayyiman bi-umūrik, fa-fawwiḍhā ilayh* — "One who **stands over your affairs** — so **hand them over** to Him." Al-Qurṭubī: *qāʾiman bi-umūrik* — "One who **takes charge** of your affairs" — and then he adds a second shade reported among the scholars: *kafīlan bi-mā waʿadak* — "a **guarantor** of what He has promised you."

[PAUSE]

Let those layer one on top of the other, because together they paint a complete picture — not competing definitions, but one figure seen from several angles:

He is the **One who stands over your affairs** — they do not stand on their own; *He* holds them up.
He is the **One who takes charge** — the management is His, not yours.
He is the **guarantor of what He promised** — so the outcome is underwritten, secured, by Him.

And al-Muyassar gathers the heart of it into one instruction: *fa-ʿtamid ʿalayhi wa fawwiḍ umūraka ilayh* — **"so lean your whole weight on Him, and consign your affairs into His hands."**

### Now — bring the two pieces together with the rest of the verse

Here is what I have been waiting to show you.

The verse opened by calling Him **Rabb** — *rabbu-l-mashriqi wa-l-maghrib.* And *Rabb* is not merely "Lord" in the sense of owner. The root **r-b-b** means the One who **nurtures, sustains, raises, and brings to completion** — the Master who *governs and provides for* what is under Him. He is the *Rabb* — the Sustaining Master — of the entire arc from sunrise to sunset.

And the verse closes by asking you to take Him as **wakīl** — the One who **manages your affairs.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see the staggering symmetry? **The One who governs and sustains the rising and setting of the sun — that is the very One you are being asked to appoint as the manager of your small, breakable, anxious life.**

The logic is unanswerable. If He is already conducting the affairs of the *cosmos* — the orbits, the dawns, the dusks, the whole sweep of light across the world, flawlessly, without your help and without His effort — then handing Him the affairs of *your one day* is not asking Him to take on something heavy. **It is asking the ocean to hold one more drop.** The Manager of the horizons can carry your Monday morning without strain.

That is why the verse moves the way it does. It shows you the size of His dominion *first* — the place of rising to the place of setting — precisely so that when it finally asks you to entrust your affairs to Him, you already know, in your bones, that they are *nothing* to a Lord of that magnitude.

And the **fa** — that little prefix *fa-ttakhidh*, "**so** take Him" — is the *fa* of consequence. It means: *given all of the above — because He owns the horizons, because there is no god but Him — it simply follows. Take Him as your Disposer.* The surrender is not a leap in the dark. **It is the only conclusion the premises allow.**

---

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words have built. *Al-mashriq wa-l-maghrib* — the whole world enclosed, sunrise to sunset, inside one dominion. *Lā ilāha illā huw* — every other refuge erased, leaving Him alone. *Fa-ttakhidh-hu wakīlā* — and so, deliberately, you appoint that Lord as the One who carries your affairs.

But these are not just words about words. Let us ask now what this reveals not merely about a verse — but about **how a human being is meant to live inside a world too heavy to carry alone.**

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Exhaustion of Being Your Own *Wakīl*

Let me begin where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with the body. You can sleep eight hours and wake up carrying it. It is the tiredness of being **the one in charge of everything.** The one who has to anticipate every problem, manage every outcome, hold every thread so that nothing unravels. You lie down at night and your mind will not stop running the simulations — *what if this, then what, and then I'll have to handle that.* You are the manager of your life, on call twenty-four hours a day, with no one to delegate to.

Psychologists have a name for the state underneath this: **hypervigilance.** The nervous system stuck in the "on" position, scanning for threats, never standing down — because somewhere deep down you believe that if *you* stop holding it all together, it will all fall apart. You are, in the language of our verse, trying to be **your own wakīl.** Your own guarantor. The one who stands over your own affairs and makes sure they turn out right.

[PAUSE]

And here is the quiet tragedy of it: **you are catastrophically unqualified for the job.** Not because you are weak — but because the job is impossible. You cannot see tomorrow. You cannot control other people's choices. You cannot guarantee the outcome of a single thing you set in motion. You are trying to be the guarantor of results you have no power to secure. No wonder you are exhausted. You have appointed yourself to a position that *no created being can hold.*

Now hear the verse again, into that exhaustion: *fa-ttakhidh-hu wakīlā.* **"Take Him as your Disposer of affairs."**

This is not Allah adding a religious duty to your already overflowing list. **It is Allah offering to take the list.** Remember the word — *ittakhidh*, the deliberate appointment. He is saying: *you have been doing a job you were never built to do. There is One who is actually qualified — who sees tomorrow, who governs the horizons, who guarantees what He promises. Appoint Him. Hand it over.* Al-Baghawī's gloss was a single imperative: *fa-fawwiḍhā ilayh* — **"hand them over to Him."**

[PAUSE]

But let me be careful, because there is a misunderstanding that can ruin this.

Taking Allah as your *wakīl* does not mean you stop *acting*. Remember — this whole surah is one long command to *act*: stand the night, recite, strive, devote. The Prophet ﷺ was given this verse not so he could lie down and do nothing, but so he could *stand up and do the impossible* — carry revelation to a hostile world — **without being crushed by the weight of guaranteeing the outcome.**

That is the precise gift here. *Tawakkul* does not remove your effort. It removes your *burden of the result.* You tie your camel — you do every honest thing within your power — and then you release your grip on the outcome, because the outcome was **never yours to hold.** You appoint a *wakīl* for exactly the part you cannot control. You keep the *doing*. You hand over the *guaranteeing.*

And the moment you genuinely do that — the moment you let Him be the guarantor of what He promised — the hypervigilance has nowhere left to stand. The nervous system can finally come down off the wall, because the thing it was straining to hold up is now being held by the One who holds the sunrise.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tonight, name one thing you have been trying to be the *guarantor* of — one outcome you have been gripping with white knuckles, replaying, managing, losing sleep over. And do the two-part act this verse teaches. First, the *doing*: what is the next honest, within-your-power step? Take it. Then, the *handing over*: say the words and mean them — *Anta-l-wakīl. You are the One who carries this. I have appointed You.* Keep your hands on the work. Take your hands off the result.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: Why He Showed You the Horizons Before He Asked for Your Trust

Now I want to return to the question I planted at the very beginning — because we are finally ready to answer it.

Why does Allah describe Himself in *cosmic* terms — Lord of the East and the West — only to offer Himself for the *intimate* job of managing your affairs?

Here is the human problem this answers. When we are afraid, our world *shrinks.* Anxiety has a strange gravity — it pulls everything in close. The thing you are worried about fills the entire screen of your mind until it feels enormous, total, all there is. The unpaid bill, the difficult diagnosis, the relationship fracture, the looming deadline — under fear, that one thing swells until it blocks out the sky.

And what does Allah do, for a heart in exactly that state — a Prophet about to be handed a city's worth of hostility? **He does not shrink Himself down to the size of your problem. He expands your vision back out to the size of His dominion.**

[PAUSE]

*Rabbu-l-mashriqi wa-l-maghrib.* Look up. Look at the place where the sun rises. Look at the place where it sets. Look at the whole arc of light that crosses the world while you sleep and while you wake. **I am the Lord of all of that.** And the problem that is filling your entire screen right now — set it against *that* horizon. How large is it now?

This is the medicine of the verse. It does not deny that your problem is real. It **re-scales** it. It places your crisis back inside a cosmos governed flawlessly by the One you are about to lean on — and in that context, the crisis returns to its true size. Still real. No longer infinite.

And notice the *order* once more, because the order is the mercy. He shows you the **bigness of the Lord** *before* He asks for the **bigness of your trust.** He earns the surrender. He does not say "trust Me" and leave you to manufacture the feeling from nothing. He first lets you *see* who He is — the Owner of the horizons, beside whom there is no other god, the Guarantor of His own promises — and *then*, when the seeing has done its work, the trust is not a strain. It is the **natural exhale** of a heart that has finally seen the truth.

[PAUSE]

This is the deep mechanism of all sincere reliance. *Tawakkul* is not willpower. You cannot force yourself to trust by clenching harder. **Trust is the byproduct of accurate vision.** You trust a surgeon you *know* is the finest in the world — the trust flows effortlessly from the knowledge. The reason our reliance on Allah is so weak is not that our willpower is weak. It is that our *vision* of Him is small. We have shrunk Him to a vague comfort somewhere in the background of a life we are still, frantically, trying to run ourselves.

The cure is not to try harder to trust. The cure is to *see Him more truly* — and let the trust follow on its own. That is what this verse does. It does not command the feeling. **It restores the vision that produces the feeling.** Look at the horizons. Then the *lā ilāha illā huwa* — there is no one else anyway. Then, and only then, *fa-ttakhidh-hu wakīlā* — the handing-over that now feels not like a leap, but like coming home.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time fear shrinks your world down to a single suffocating thing — physically *look up.* Step outside at dawn or at sunset and watch the very horizon this verse names. Let the sky be larger than the problem. And in front of that horizon, say it: *He is the Lord of all of this. He is the Lord of mine too.* Let the vision come first. The trust will follow it home.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole thing — because this verse is built with a precision you could not have appreciated at the start.

Three movements, in a single arc:

**One — the dominion:** *Rabbu-l-mashriqi wa-l-maghrib.* He governs the entire visible world, sunrise to sunset, named at its two extremes to mean *everything.*

**Two — the exclusion:** *Lā ilāha illā huw.* And there is no other anywhere in existence to turn to — every alternative erased, leaving Him alone.

**Three — the response:** *Fa-ttakhidh-hu wakīlā.* So, deliberately, appoint *that* Lord as the One who carries your affairs.

See how the *fa* — the "so" — sits on top of the first two like a roof on two pillars. The surrender is not asked of you in a vacuum. It is the **conclusion of an argument.** *Because* He owns the horizons, *because* there is no other, **therefore** the only rational thing a human being can do is hand over the management. The verse does not demand blind trust. It builds the case — and then lets you draw the only conclusion the case allows.

## The Two Roads Meeting

And now watch our linguistic discoveries and our themes turn out to be **the same insight from two directions.**

From the **words**, we found that *Rabb* — the Sustaining Master of the cosmos — is bookended against *wakīl* — the manager of your private affairs. The grammar itself was telling you: *the One large enough to govern the sky is the One you appoint for your small life.*

From the **themes**, we found that the cure for the exhaustion of self-reliance, and the cure for the shrinking vision of fear, are the **same cure**: stop being your own guarantor, expand your vision to the size of His dominion, and let trust follow seeing.

They are one truth. **The bigness of the Lord is precisely what makes the handing-over safe.** You can release your grip *because* the One catching it holds the horizons. The grammar and the heart arrive at the same place: *Anta-l-wakīl.*

## Questions to Carry

- What is the one outcome in my life I have been trying to *guarantee* — a job I was never qualified to hold? What would it feel like to genuinely appoint Allah to it instead?
- When fear shrinks my world to a single thing, do I reach for control — or do I reach for the *vision* of who my Lord actually is?
- The verb was *ittakhidh* — a deliberate taking. Have I ever *actually* appointed Allah as my wakīl, by choice, in words — or have I only assumed it vaguely while still running everything myself?
- Where am I keeping a "backup god" — a second thing my heart secretly leans on — that the *lā ilāha illā huwa* is asking me to release?
- Do I trust Allah weakly because my *will* is weak, or because my *vision* of Him is small?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Because the One who governs the rising and setting of the sun is the only God there is, the only sane response of a human heart is to stop trying to carry its own life and deliberately hand it over to Him.**

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to keep being your own wakīl. You were never meant to be. The position is taken — held already by the One who needs no rest to govern the horizons and no permission to keep His promises. This verse is not laying another weight on you. It is offering to take the weight you were never built to carry. The invitation is simply this: *see Him truly — and then let go into the hands that hold the sunrise.*

## A Brief Du'a

*O Lord of the place of rising and the place of setting — there is no god but You. We confess we have worn ourselves out trying to be the guarantors of lives we cannot even see tomorrow's edge of. So we take You — deliberately, gratefully — as our Wakīl. Stand over our affairs, carry what we cannot, and let our hearts finally come down off the wall and rest in the truth that You alone are enough. Āmīn.*
