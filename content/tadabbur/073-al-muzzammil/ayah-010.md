# The Beautiful Goodbye: When Walking Away Becomes Worship

## Surah Al-Muzzammil, Ayah 10

> [ayah:73:10] وَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَٱهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا
>
> *Waṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wahjurhum hajran jamīlā*
>
> **"And be patient over what they say, and part from them with a gracious parting."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Man in the Blanket

Let me take you back to a house in Mecca, in the dark.

There is a man wrapped in a cloak. The surah is named after him — *Al-Muzzammil*, "the one enwrapped," the one who has pulled the blanket over himself. And he has reason to. The words have started coming. The revelation has begun to descend, and it is heavy — Allah Himself calls it, a few verses earlier, *qawlan thaqīlā*, **a heavy word**. The weight of being chosen has settled on his shoulders, and out in the streets, the city he loves has begun to turn on him.

So he wraps himself up. And in that wrapping, Allah comes to him — not to let him hide, but to prepare him.

Look at what comes *before* our verse. *Stand the night, except a little.* Recite the Qur'an slowly, deliberately, *tartīlā*. Remember the name of your Lord. Devote yourself to Him with complete devotion. The first nine verses of this surah are almost entirely about **the night** — the private hours, the long standing, the slow recitation in the dark when no one is watching.

[PAUSE]

And then comes verse 10. The night is over. The sun is up. And now he has to walk out the door.

Out there are the people. The ones saying things. The relatives who now cross the street. The leaders calling him a poet, a madman, a sorcerer. The mockery in the marketplace. The friends who became strangers overnight.

And Allah gives him two instructions for the daylight — just two. **Be patient over what they say. And part from them — beautifully.**

Here is what I want you to feel before we go a single step further. Allah does not say *"and part from them."* He could have. The sentence would have been complete. Instead He adds a word — *jamīl*, **beautiful** — and attaches it to the one act in human life that is almost never beautiful: **the act of walking away from people who have hurt you.**

We know how that usually looks. The slammed door. The blocked number. The cold silence engineered to wound. The story we tell everyone else about why we cut them off. The leaving is real — but it is *ugly*. It drips with the very bitterness we claim to be escaping.

Allah looks at the most justified resentment a human being could possibly hold — His own Messenger, rejected by his own people, for telling them the pure truth — and He says: when you withdraw, withdraw *beautifully*.

How? **How do you make a goodbye beautiful?** And why would the God of the universe care about the *aesthetics* of a separation, when the people deserve so much worse?

Stay with me. Because the answer is hiding in a single grammatical move — and it will change how you leave every room you ever have to leave.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Grammar of Beauty — *Hajran Jamīlā*

### First, a small lesson in how Arabic intensifies

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word, because without it you will read straight past the most beautiful thing in this verse.

In English, when we want to emphasize a verb, we reach for an adverb. *He ran.* Not enough? *He ran quickly.* Still not enough? *He ran incredibly quickly.* We stack words *in front of* the action.

Arabic does something else, something more elegant. To intensify a verb, Arabic takes the verb, **turns it back into a noun, and lays that noun down right beside the verb it came from.** Grammarians call this the *maf'ūl muṭlaq* — the "absolute object," or the cognate accusative.

Here is an everyday example. In Arabic you don't just say "he was happy." You can say *fariḥa faraḥan* — literally, *"he rejoiced a rejoicing."* You take the act and you double it back on itself. And the magic is this: **once you've laid that noun down, you can hang an adjective on it.** *Fariḥa faraḥan ʿaẓīmā* — "he rejoiced a great rejoicing." Now you're not just saying he was happy. You're saying: there was a thing called his joy, and that thing was enormous.

Hold onto that mechanism. The verb becomes a *thing*. And a thing can be described.

### Now open the word

Allah does not say *wahjurhum* — "and avoid them" — and stop.

He says:

> **وَٱهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًۭا جَمِيلًۭا**
>
> *wahjurhum **hajran jamīlā***

"And part from them — *a parting* — *a beautiful one*."

The verb *uhjur* (part, withdraw, distance yourself) is turned into the noun *hajr* (a parting) and laid down beside itself. And then — exactly as the grammar allows — Allah hangs an adjective on it.

*Jamīl.*

Beautiful.

[PAUSE]

### Sit with what just happened

Notice what this construction *does*. By turning "part from them" into "a parting," Allah lifts the separation up off the ground and makes it into an **object you can hold and examine.** It is no longer just something you do in a fit of emotion and forget. It is now a *thing* with a quality, a shape, a character. And the character Allah assigns to it is *jamīl* — beautiful, graceful, comely.

The root is *j-m-l*. It is the root of *jamāl*, beauty in its fullest sense — not just pretty, but *fitting, dignified, whole, free of ugliness.* It is the same root from which the Arabs named the camel, *jamal* — the animal that carries enormous weight across the desert with a slow, unbothered grace.

So *hajr jamīl* is not merely "leaving nicely." It is a withdrawal carried out with such dignity that **the leaving itself becomes a thing of beauty.**

Now — *what is the ugliness it is excluding?*

The classical commentators answer this with surgical precision. Imam al-Jalalayn defines *hajran jamīlā* as a parting **"with no *jazaʿ* in it"** — *jazaʿ* being the root of panic, of anguished thrashing, of the emotional collapse that makes you lash out. A beautiful parting is one stripped of panic.

Imam al-Qurtubi goes further and tells us what the beauty positively requires: *"do not confront them, do not busy yourself with paying them back* — *because that would mean abandoning the call to Allah."* Watch this. For al-Qurtubi, the beauty of the parting is measured by **what you do not stop doing.** You withdraw from their harm, but you do *not* withdraw your good toward them. You stop the retaliation — you never stop the *daʿwah*, the invitation, the wishing-good. The door you close is the door of revenge. The door you leave open is the door back to guidance.

And Imam al-Muyassar completes the portrait: *"oppose them in their false deeds, while turning away from them, and leaving off revenge."* A separation in *conduct* — but never a separation poisoned by the appetite for their pain.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the full image now? A *hajr jamīl* is a withdrawal with:
- **no panic** (al-Jalalayn — *no jazaʿ*),
- **no retaliation** (al-Muyassar — *no revenge*),
- **and no closed heart** (al-Qurtubi — *the call to Allah stays open*).

You step back. But you step back *clean*.

### And this is not the only time Allah crowns a hard act with beauty

Here is what's remarkable. This exact move — taking the hardest human action and hanging *jamīl* on it — is a signature of the Qur'an. Allah does it again and again, always over the things that hurt:

- To the Prophet ﷺ over the deniers, in another surah: *fa-ṣbir ṣabran **jamīlā*** — "so be patient with a **beautiful** patience" (70:5). Even the *enduring* is to be beautiful.
- To a man releasing a wife: *sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan **jamīlā*** — "release them with a **beautiful** release" (33:49). Even the *divorce* — the breaking of a marriage — is commanded to be beautiful.
- To the Prophet ﷺ over those who mock: *fa-ṣfaḥi al-ṣafḥa al-**jamīl*** — "so overlook with a **gracious** overlooking" (15:85).

Patience. Separation. Divorce. Overlooking. Every one of them is one of the *ugliest* moments in a human life — and over every one of them, Allah writes the same word: *jamīl*.

[PAUSE]

What is Allah teaching us across all of these? **It is not enough to do the right thing. He is concerned with the *manner* in which you do it.** Two men can both walk away. Two women can both endure. One does it with a face full of poison, and one does it with grace. To Allah, these are not the same act. The grammar of the cognate accusative exists precisely so that the *quality* of the act can be named — and Allah uses it to name the quality He wants: beauty.

**What this single word reveals: leaving is not the test. *How you leave* is the test.**

## Element Two: The Weight That Presses Down — *Ṣbir ʿalā*

### A small lesson about a small word

Now let me draw your eye to a word so small you would never stop on it. The preposition *ʿalā*.

In English we translate it "over" or "upon," and we move on. But prepositions are not decorations in Arabic; they are *positions in space*, and the position tells you the relationship.

The core meaning of *ʿalā* is **superposition** — something resting *on top of* something else. The book is *ʿalā* the table — on it, weighing on it. When Allah says the pilgrimage is *ʿalā al-nās* — "upon the people" — He means it as a weight that rests on their shoulders, an obligation pressing down. *ʿalā* is the preposition of **the thing that sits on top of you.**

### Now hear the command again

Allah does not say *iṣbir* — "be patient" — and leave it bare. He says:

> **وَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ**
>
> *waṣbir **ʿalā** mā yaqūlūn*

"And be patient **ʿalā** — *upon, over, on top of* — what they say."

Feel the geometry of that. Their words are not coming at you from the front, where you could see them and brace. The preposition places them **above you** — *ʿalā* — pressing down. A weight on the shoulders. The image the grammar paints is of a man standing under a load.

And what does *ṣabr* mean, at its root? This is important. *Ṣabr* — root *ṣ-b-r* — does not mean "to feel calm." It does not mean the absence of pain. The Arabs used this root for **binding something fast so it cannot move** — they spoke of *ṣabr* as restraining, holding firm, refusing to break under pressure. *Ṣabr* is what a load-bearing wall does. It is not numb. It feels every kilogram. It simply **does not give way.**

[PAUSE]

So put the two together — *iṣbir* and *ʿalā* — and the verse is not asking the Prophet ﷺ to *not feel* their words. The weight is real; the grammar puts it right on top of him. He is being asked to **stand under that weight without collapsing.** To be the wall, not the reed.

### And notice the asymmetry

Here is something worth pausing on. *ʿalā* gives us a weight from above. But the word it governs — *mā yaqūlūn*, "what they say" — is just *speech*. Just words. Just sound vibrating in the air, gone in a second.

And yet Allah frames *words* as a weight heavy enough to need *ṣabr* — the bracing of a load-bearing wall.

**Allah is taking the human pain of being talked about with total seriousness.** He does not say "they're only words, ignore them." He does not minimize it. He names it as a genuine weight — *ʿalā*, on top of you — and *then* commands the strength to bear it. He validates the heaviness before He commands the endurance. The God who made you knows exactly how much a sentence can crush a heart.

**What this small preposition reveals: Allah never asks you to pretend the weight is light. He asks you to become strong enough to stand under it.**

## Element Three: The Words He Refused to Quote — *Mā Yaqūlūn*

### Read the silence

Now I want to do something we are taught to do in deep reflection: read an *absence* as carefully as a presence.

Allah says, *"be patient over **what they say**."* *Mā yaqūlūn* — "what they say."

And then He stops.

He does not tell us *what* they said.

Think about how strange that is. The Qur'an is not shy about quoting the enemies of the Prophet ﷺ. Elsewhere it records their exact taunts: *"a poet,"* *"a madman,"* *"a sorcerer,"* *"tales of the ancients,"* *"why was this Qur'an not sent down to a great man?"* Allah preserves their slanders verbatim in other places, often to dismantle them.

But here — in the tender moment, in the night-surah, when He is preparing His Messenger to walk out into the abuse — He does **not repeat the insults.** He compresses every one of them into two anonymous words: *mā yaqūlūn.* "Whatever it is they say."

[PAUSE]

### Sit with what the silence is doing

Why withhold the content?

Consider what quoting it would have done. To name the specific insult is to **give it a second life.** It would have made the Prophet ﷺ — and every reader after him until the end of time — *re-hear* the exact wound. The slander would have been enshrined, repeated, dignified by inclusion.

Instead, Allah *refuses to dignify it.* He waves the entire category away — *mā yaqūlūn*, "what they say" — the way you might say of a cruel person, *"whatever he said, it doesn't matter."* The vagueness is not laziness. **The vagueness is contempt for the slander and tenderness for the wounded.**

And there is a second gift hidden in the silence. Because Allah did not specify *which* words, this command now stretches to cover **every word ever said against a believer**, in every century, in every language. *Mā yaqūlūn.* Whatever they say. The taunt of Mecca, yes — and also the comment under your post, the whisper in the family group, the thing your coworker said about your prayer, the thing you were never even meant to hear. By leaving the words blank, Allah wrote your name into the verse.

**What this absence reveals: Allah would not repeat their words — and in refusing to, He both stripped the insult of its power and made the comfort large enough to hold yours.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Bridge: From Words to Reality

So look at what the grammar has shown us. Three small moves:

- A verb turned into a *thing* and crowned with **beauty** — *hajran jamīlā* — so that the *manner* of leaving could be named and commanded.
- A single preposition, *ʿalā*, that places their words as a **weight on top of you** — taking your pain seriously, then commanding you to bear it like a wall.
- A deliberate **silence** where the insults should be — *mā yaqūlūn* — refusing to repeat them, and in that refusal, covering all of us.

Three grammatical decisions. Now let's ask what they reveal not just about words — but about how a human being is actually supposed to *live* among people who hurt them.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Beautiful Goodbye

### Begin where you already live

You have left someone.

Maybe you haven't done it yet, but you've thought about it. The friend who kept taking. The relative whose every comment was a small cut. The group chat you finally muted. The person whose number you blocked at 2 a.m. with your heart pounding. We have a whole modern vocabulary for it now — *going no-contact, cutting them off, blocking, ghosting, estrangement.* It has become, in many ways, the defining relational move of our age. The therapist says protect your peace. The internet says cut off anyone who doesn't "serve your growth." And there is real wisdom buried in some of that — there *are* people you must step back from.

But be honest with me about *how* we do it.

We don't just leave. We **leave with a flourish.** We make sure they see the blocking. We tell the mutual friends our version first. We rehearse the cold thing we'll say if we ever run into them. We leave a door slammed so hard it still rattles years later. The separation becomes a *performance of our hurt* — and we mistake the performance for strength.

And here is the thing nobody tells you: **the bitterness doesn't leave when the person does.** You can put a thousand miles and a blocked number between you and someone, and still carry them in your chest every single day, re-litigating the argument in the shower, flinching at their name. You *left* — but you're not *free.* The hajr was real. But it wasn't *jamīl*. It was *qabīḥ* — ugly — and the ugliness stayed with *you*, because it was always yours.

[PAUSE]

### Now let the verse illuminate it

This is the genius of what Allah does in this verse. He does not forbid the leaving. He, the Most Wise, knows there are people you must withdraw from — He is *commanding* His own beloved Messenger to do exactly that. So the question of the verse is never *"should I leave?"* The question is *"what kind of leaving frees me, and what kind chains me?"*

And His answer is one word: *jamīl.*

Remember what the scholars told us *jamīl* means here. Al-Jalalayn: **no *jazaʿ*** — no panic, no thrashing, no emotional collapse. Al-Muyassar: **no revenge.** Al-Qurtubi: **the door of good stays open** — you stop the retaliation, never the wishing-well.

Now read those three back as a description of *your* heart, and you'll see something staggering. **Every condition of the *jamīl* parting is a condition that frees the person doing the leaving — not the one being left.**

- *No jazaʿ* — the panic was never hurting them; it was eating *you*.
- *No revenge* — the appetite for their pain was a hook *in your own mouth.*
- *The open door* — refusing to seal your heart against them keeps *your* heart soft, capable of feeling anything good ever again.

The *hajr jamīl* is not a gift you give the person you're leaving. **It is the only kind of leaving that actually lets *you* walk away whole.** The ugly goodbye keeps you tethered by the rope of your own resentment. The beautiful goodbye cuts the rope on *both* ends.

### Build it one more layer

Think about Yaqub — Jacob — peace be upon him. When his sons did the unthinkable to Yusuf and came home with the lie and the bloodied shirt, what did he say? *Fa-ṣabrun jamīl* — "so, beautiful patience" (12:18). The same word. His sons had just destroyed his family. He had every right on earth to rage. And he reached for *jamīl.* Not because they deserved his grace — but because his grief, left ugly, would have consumed the rest of his life. He chose the beautiful response to protect the one heart he was responsible for: **his own, before his Lord.**

[PAUSE]

### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical weight of it. The next time you have to step back from someone — and you will — do not ask only *"am I right to leave?"* Ask the harder question: **"Is my leaving *jamīl*?"**

Run the three tests. Is there *jazaʿ* — am I leaving in a panic, a thrash, a need to make a scene? Is there revenge — am I quietly hoping they hurt? Is the door sealed — have I made myself incapable of ever wishing them well again?

If any answer is yes, the separation may still be *necessary* — but it is not yet *beautiful*, and the ugliness will follow you out the door like a shadow. The work is to withdraw your *harm-exposure* without withdrawing your *humanity*. To close the door of retaliation and leave open, even just a crack, the door of *"may Allah guide them, may Allah heal this."*

That crack of open door is not weakness. According to al-Qurtubi, it is the *entire point.* Because a sealed heart can never become the doorway through which someone finds their way back.

## Theme Two: Why Their Words Couldn't Reach Him — The Reservoir of the Night

### Begin where you already live

Let me ask you something uncomfortable.

Why do words wreck us?

You can have a wonderful day, and one comment — one *sentence* from one person — can hollow it out completely. A single line under a photo. A single tone in a text. We are, all of us, astonishingly *destabilizable* by speech. And we live now in an ocean of it: notifications, comments, the permanent record of everything anyone ever said about us, available to re-read at 3 a.m. We are more exposed to *mā yaqūlūn* — "what they say" — than any humans who have ever lived.

And here is the pattern, if you watch yourself honestly: **we are wrecked by words in exact proportion to how *empty* we are.** When you are running on nothing — no sleep, no prayer, no inner fullness, no connection to anything larger than the moment — a small insult lands like a boulder. But there are other days, rarer days, when you are *full* — when you have prayed, when your heart is anchored, when you woke up *with* God — and the same insult bounces off. Same words. Completely different impact. The variable was never the words. **The variable was how full you were when they arrived.**

[PAUSE]

### Now let the verse illuminate it

This is why the *order* of Surah Al-Muzzammil is not an accident, and it is the single most important thing in this entire reflection.

Allah does **not** open the surah with verse 10. He does not start with *"be patient over what they say."* If He had, it would be a demand with no resource — *just cope, just endure*, with empty hands.

Instead, look at what He puts *first*:

- *Stand the night, except a little* (73:2).
- *Recite the Qur'an slowly, with tartīl* (73:4).
- *Remember the name of your Lord and devote yourself to Him with total devotion* (73:8).

**Nine verses of filling the reservoir.** The long night standing. The slow, deliberate recitation in the dark. The remembrance. The total turning-toward Allah when no one is watching.

*And only then* — verse 10 — *"be patient over what they say, and part from them beautifully."*

Do you see the architecture? [PAUSE] **The night is the cause. The patience is the effect.** Allah does not ask the Prophet ﷺ to endure the daylight on an empty heart. He spends nine verses *filling* him in the dark — and only then sends him out to be spoken about. The *ṣabr ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* of verse 10 is not generated by gritting your teeth. **It is the overflow of a soul that spent the night with its Lord.**

Remember Element Two — *ʿalā*, the weight that presses down from above. You cannot stand under a weight on an empty frame; you'll buckle. Verses 1–9 are Allah building the frame. Verse 10 is Allah placing the weight. **He builds the wall before He leans the load against it.**

### Build it one more layer

This reframes everything about *why* we collapse under criticism. We treat resilience as a *response* — something we summon in the moment the insult arrives. *Stay calm, don't react, take the high road.* And it almost never works, because by the time the words land, it is too late to build what you needed.

The Qur'an locates resilience somewhere else entirely: **not in the moment of the wound, but in the hours before it, in the dark, in private, with God.** The man who can take *mā yaqūlūn* in the daylight is the man who took *qiyām al-layl* in the night. The patience is *withdrawn from an account that was filled when no one was looking.*

And notice — this is exactly why Allah, in the *very next verse*, takes the burden off the Prophet's hands entirely: *"And leave Me with the deniers"* (73:11). First He fills him in the night (1–9). Then He commands the beautiful patience and parting (10). Then He says, *now hand the rest to Me* (11). The human being's job is to get full in the dark and walk out clean. **The reckoning is Allah's job, not yours.** That's the whole sequence: be filled, be patient, be beautiful in withdrawal — and then *let Me handle them.*

[PAUSE]

### What this changes tomorrow

So stop trying to win the battle at the moment of the insult. You'll lose it there almost every time, because resilience cannot be *manufactured on the spot.* It can only be *spent* — and you can only spend what you stored.

The practical instruction is breathtakingly concrete, and it is the literal instruction of this surah: **store it in the night.** Or in whatever your night can be — the few minutes before dawn, the prayer you don't rush, the slow recitation where you actually *hear* the words instead of racing to finish, the dhikr that fills the empty tank. The person who is unshakeable by *mā yaqūlūn* in public is, without exception, a person with a private life with Allah that no one sees.

Tomorrow, when someone's words land like a weight on top of you — *ʿalā* — do not first ask *"how do I respond?"* Ask *"how full am I, and when did I last fill up?"* And then build the habit upstream, in the dark, so that the next time the weight comes down, there is a wall already standing to meet it.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape of it.

Surah Al-Muzzammil is built like a single breath. **Breathe in, in the night** — verses 1 through 9: the standing, the slow recitation, the remembrance, the devotion. The soul fills in the dark. **Breathe out, in the day** — verse 10: *be patient over what they say, and part from them beautifully.* The soul spends what it stored. **And then release** — verse 11: *leave Me with the deniers.* Hand the outcome back to God.

In. Out. Release.

Verse 10 is the *exhale* — and an exhale is only as strong as the breath that came before it. That is the proof hidden in the surah's own order: Allah will not ask you to be patient and beautiful in the daylight until He has first filled you in the dark.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes are the same truth approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a verb turned into a *thing* and crowned with *beauty* — *hajran jamīlā* — and a preposition, *ʿalā*, that laid the weight of words on top of a human heart. The themes showed us *why*: because the beautiful goodbye is the only goodbye that sets *you* free, and because the strength to bear the weight is not summoned in the moment — it is drawn from the reservoir of the night.

Put it in one image. **A wall built in darkness, standing serene in the daylight under a weight it was made to carry — and on its far side, a door left quietly open.** That is *ṣabr ʿalā mā yaqūlūn wahjurhum hajran jamīlā.* Patience under the weight. A parting without poison. A door not sealed.

*(One honest note for the seekers among us: many of the classical scholars — al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi citing Qatadah — held that the legal ruling of this verse, the command of mere withdrawal, was later abrogated by the verses of fighting, since the early Meccan situation was not the later Medinan one. That is a recognized position in the science of naskh. But even those same scholars — al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubi himself — preserve the verse's enduring instruction for the heart: the patience, the freedom from panic, the refusal of private vengeance. The legal context shifted with the community's circumstances; the discipline of the soul it teaches did not.)*

1. Think of the last person you walked away from. Was your leaving *jamīl* — free of panic, free of the wish for their pain, with the door of good left open? Or are you still tethered to them by a rope you tied yourself?
2. When words wreck you, what is usually true about your *inner fullness* in that moment? When did you last fill the reservoir?
3. Allah refused to repeat the insults — *mā yaqūlūn.* What words are you giving a second, third, hundredth life by replaying them? What would it mean to let them stay unquoted?
4. Is there a relationship where you have closed not just the door of harm, but the door of *du'ā* — where you can no longer even bring yourself to ask Allah to guide them?
5. What is *your* night? And what would change in your days if you actually built it?

## The One-Sentence Distillation

When you must withdraw from those who wound you, Allah does not ask you to feel no weight — He asks you to be filled in the dark so that you can bear their words like a standing wall, and walk away with a parting so clean of panic and vengeance that the leaving itself becomes beautiful.

## The Closing Invitation

You are going to have to leave rooms. You are going to be talked about. This is not a possibility; it is the texture of a human life, and it was the texture of the most beloved life that ever walked the earth. The invitation of this verse is not to avoid the wound — the Prophet ﷺ was not spared it — but to change two things: *where* you draw your strength from, and *how* you carry yourself on the way out. Fill up in the night. And when you go, go beautifully.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, fill us in the dark so we can stand in the daylight. When their words press down upon us, make us walls and not reeds — feeling every weight, breaking under none. And when we must part from anyone, teach us the parting You called beautiful: no panic, no revenge, no door sealed shut — only patience, and dignity, and a heart still soft enough to pray for them. Then take from our hands what is too heavy for us, and leave the reckoning to You. Āmīn.*
