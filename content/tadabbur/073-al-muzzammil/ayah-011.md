# Leave Me With Them
### A Tadabbur of Surah Al-Muzzammil, Ayah 11

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with them before we do anything else.

> [ayah:73:11] وَذَرْنِى وَٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ أُو۟لِى ٱلنَّعْمَةِ وَمَهِّلْهُمْ قَلِيلًا
>
> *Wa dharnī wal-mukadhdhibīna ūlin-naʿmati wa mahhilhum qalīlā*
>
> "And leave Me with the deniers — those possessors of ease and luxury — and grant them respite, a little."

Six words. One of the shortest verses you will ever reflect on for the better part of an hour. And yet inside these six words there is a transaction taking place — a weight being lifted off one set of shoulders and set down somewhere else. If you can see that transaction by the end of tonight, something in the way you carry your own grievances may never sit the same way again.

### Let us rebuild the world this verse landed in

To feel this verse, you have to feel where the Prophet ﷺ is standing when he hears it.

This is Mecca. Early. Before the migration, before Badr, before any of it. The community of believers is small, mocked, and surrounded. And the surah that carries this verse — Al-Muzzammil, *the one wrapped up* — opens with the Prophet ﷺ wrapped in his cloak, and Allah calling to him: *rise in the night. Stand. Pray through the dark. Recite slowly, deliberately, and brace yourself, because We are about to lay upon you a heavy word.*

So picture the first half of this surah as a kind of training. Night after night, the Prophet ﷺ is told to rise when the world is asleep, to stand in the cold and the silence, and to lean the entire weight of his soul on his Lord. That's verses one through nine. The vigil. The leaning. The building of a strength that has nothing to do with muscle.

And then comes the daytime. The mockery. The chiefs of Quraysh — the men with the money, the men with the comfortable houses and the soft lives — laughing at him, calling him a liar, turning their people against him. Imagine carrying that. Imagine the human pull to answer back, to defend yourself, to make them *see*, to wish on them what they are wishing on you.

So Allah speaks. Verse ten: *be patient over what they say, and withdraw from them with a beautiful withdrawal.* And then verse eleven — our verse — He says something that I want you to hear as if for the first time:

**"Leave Me with them."**

### The hook

Notice who is talking, and notice what He is asking.

The Lord of all that exists — who needs nothing, who is threatened by no one, who could end the deniers in less than the time it takes you to blink — turns to His Messenger and says, in effect: *step back. Take your hands off this. Leave them... to Me.*

Why would Allah, who needs no help, frame it as though the Prophet ﷺ had been *holding* something He now wants released?

And then there is the stranger thing still. Of all the ways He could have described these enemies, He chooses to call them the people of *naʿmah* — comfort, luxury, the soft life. Why, at the exact moment of describing those who deny Him, does He pause to mention their *furniture*? Their *ease*?

Stay with me. Both of these — the *"leave Me,"* and the *"luxury"* — are doors. And behind both of them is the same room.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Dharnī* — "Leave Me"

Before we open the word, let me teach you the idea underneath it, using something from your own life.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that has nothing to do with the body. It is the exhaustion of *carrying a case*. You have been wronged — by a relative, a colleague, someone who lied about you — and now you are the prosecutor, the judge, and the prison guard all at once. You rehearse the argument in the shower. You draft the text you'll never send. You lie awake assembling the evidence of how right you are and how wrong they were. Nobody assigned you this job. You took it on. And it is *crushing* you.

Now imagine someone with real authority — someone who can actually act, who actually has jurisdiction — puts a hand on your shoulder and says: **"Let go of it. Leave it with me. I've got it from here."**

The relief in that sentence. That's the territory we're walking into.

#### Now the word

> **وَذَرْنِي** — *wa dharnī*

It is an imperative — a command — built on the root **و-ذ-ر** *(wa-dha-ra)*, which means *to leave, to let go of, to forsake, to set aside*. And attached to its end is that small, enormous suffix: **ـنِي** — *me*.

So literally: **"And leave Me."** *Leave Me with the deniers.* Hand the matter over. The grammar of the phrase — *leave Me and the deniers* — is the Arabic way of saying: *let the affair fall between Me and them. Get yourself out from between us. This is no longer your jurisdiction; it is Mine.*

Let's sit with this, because there's something here the classical commentators caught.

Al-Qurtubī glosses *dharnī* not coldly but warmly: he reads it as **"be content with Me for their reckoning"** — *irḍa bī li-ʿiqābihim*. In other words, *you don't have to be the one to settle this account. Be at peace; the account is Mine, and I am enough.* Al-Jalālayn says the meaning is precisely: **"I am sufficient for you against them"** — *anā kāfīkahum*. The whole verse, on this reading, is one long exhale. It is Allah relieving His Prophet of a weight.

#### Notice something about the verb itself

Here is a detail the Arabic carries quietly, and it deepens everything.

The root *wa-dha-ra* — "to leave, to let go" — is, in classical Arabic usage, what the grammarians call a **defective verb**: it lives almost entirely in the *present and the command*. Its past tense fell out of use; the Arabs simply didn't say it. You use it to tell someone to let go *now*, going forward — *dhar*, *leave it* — but the form that would reach back and re-handle the past barely exists.

Sit with how perfect that is. Allah hands the Prophet ﷺ a verb that, by its very nature, **does not reach backward**. *Dharnī* is not "re-litigate what they did to you yesterday." It is "release it, from here, forward." The word for letting go is itself a word that cannot dwell in the past. [PAUSE]

#### And notice the object

The thing the Prophet ﷺ is told to *leave with* — is **Allah Himself.** *Leave Me.*

Think about what that quietly assumes. You can only "hand something over" to someone who is *standing there, ready to take it.* The verse does not say "leave them alone" or "ignore them." It says *leave them with Me* — which means the burden is not being dropped into a void. It is being placed into Hands. The deniers are not being released into chaos; they are being transferred into the custody of the One who sees every one of them, every word they said, every night they slept soundly after a day of mockery.

That is the difference between *abandoning* a grievance and *entrusting* it. The first leaves you empty. The second leaves you light — because you know exactly Whose desk it now sits on.

**What this single word reveals:** The command to the wronged servant is not "fight harder" and it is not "pretend it didn't happen." It is *transfer custody.* Take your hands off the case and place it, deliberately, into the only Hands with the authority — and the full knowledge — to judge it rightly.

### Element Two: *Al-Mukadhdhibīn* — the *active* deniers

Let me teach the form before the word, because Arabic does something here that English cannot do in a single word.

In Arabic, you can take a simple verb and *intensify* it — put it through a kind of grammatical pressure that makes it repeated, deliberate, energetic, done-with-effort. Grammarians call this the second form, **Form II**, and you build it by doubling the middle letter of the root. The simple verb *kadhaba* means *to lie*. Push it through Form II and you get *kadhdhaba* — and now it does not mean "to tell a lie." It means **to actively, repeatedly, insistently declare something false. To brand it a lie. To make a campaign of calling it false.**

There's an everyday version of this. There is a difference between a person who privately doubts you, and a person who stands in the marketplace pointing at you and shouting *"liar."* The first is a state of the heart. The second is a *project.*

#### The word

> **وَٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ** — *wal-mukadhdhibīn*

This is the active participle of that intensive form — *al-mukadhdhibīn*, **"the ones who actively, persistently brand [the truth] a lie."** Not *al-kāfirīn*, the disbelievers in general. Not the doubters. Not the confused. The ones who have made *denial* their work.

This matters, because Allah is being precise about *who* is being handed over. The verse is not a license to give up on every person who hasn't yet believed. The Prophet ﷺ spent his life patiently, achingly, hoping for the very people who rejected him. Allah elsewhere has to *console* him for grieving so hard over those who turn away. So this is not "leave Me with everyone who disagrees with you."

It is narrower and sharper than that: leave Me with **the deniers** — the ones whose rejection is not a stage of searching but a settled, active, deliberate campaign against the truth.

#### Hear the word, too

Say it slowly. *Mu-kadh-dhi-bīn.* Feel where it catches in the mouth — that doubled, throated *dh*, the consonant that has to be forced. There is a roughness to it, a kind of grinding. Compare it to the soft exhale of *dharnī* just before it. The verse moves from the gentle release — *dhar-nī* — straight into the harsh machinery of *mukadhdhibīn*. The sound itself enacts the meaning: the soft letting-go of the believer set right beside the grinding insistence of the deniers.

**What this word reveals:** What is being entrusted to Allah is not *people Allah might yet guide* — it is a specific, hardened, active rejection. The Prophet's patience and hope remain wide open for the seeking. But the deliberate, campaigning denial — *that* is what gets handed across the desk.

### Element Three: *Ūlin-Naʿmah* — "the possessors of ease"

Here is the door I promised you in the introduction — the one that seems, at first, to make no sense.

Of every possible thing Allah could have said about these deniers, He chooses to describe their *comfort*. Let me teach the word, and then let me show you why it is placed exactly here.

> **أُو۟لِي ٱلنَّعْمَةِ** — *ūlin-naʿmah*

*Ūlī* means *possessors of, owners of, the people characterized by.* And *naʿmah* — from the root **ن-ع-م**, softness and ease — refers to **the soft life: luxury, affluence, comfort, the pampered existence.**

I want you to catch a fine distinction the Arabic makes, because it is doing real work. There are two close words from this same root. *Niʿmah*, with a kasra, is the broad word for *blessing, favor* — every gift from Allah, breath and sight and faith itself. But the word *here* is **naʿmah**, with a fatḥa — and the lexicographers single this one out to mean specifically *the ease of luxury, the softness of the affluent life, comfort-as-indulgence.* Al-Jalālayn glosses it directly: *al-tanaʿʿum* — pampered living. Al-Qurtubī: *ūlin-naʿmah* means **the people of wealth, luxury, and the pleasures of this world** — *ahl al-ghinā wat-tarafu wal-ladhdhati fid-dunyā.*

So Allah is not naming a *blessing* they were given. He is naming a *softness* they sank into.

#### Now — why mention it at all?

Here is where the verse opens. Stay with me, because this is the heart of the linguistic journey.

Remember where this surah began. It began with the command to *rise in the night.* To leave the warm bed. To stand in the cold dark and pray. The believer's posture in this surah is **vertical** — standing, in the hardest hours, before his Lord.

And now, describing the deniers, Allah gives us the opposite posture in a single word: *ūlin-naʿmah.* The people of the *soft* life. The ones reclining in ease while the Prophet ﷺ stands through the night.

Do you see what just happened? **The verse has drawn a contrast between two ways a human being can spend the night.** One is wrapped in a cloak, rising to stand before Allah. The other is wrapped in luxury, sunk into a comfort so total that it has anesthetized the very need for God.

This is not a throwaway description. Allah is naming *the soil that the denial grew in.* These men did not deny because they had examined the evidence and found it wanting. They denied because they were *comfortable* — and comfort, when it becomes total, quietly removes the felt need for anything beyond itself. Why would a man who lacks nothing reach for the One who provides everything?

The luxury is not incidental to the denial. The luxury *is the engine* of the denial.

#### And the classical reports sharpen the picture

The commentators tell us — and here the early reports differ, so let me name the difference honestly rather than flatten it. Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī report that this verse came down concerning **the chiefs of Quraysh, the mockers** — *ṣanādīd Quraysh al-mustahziʾīn.* But Qurtubī then carefully preserves the disagreement: Muqātil said it referred specifically to *the ten who fed the army at Badr;* Yaḥyā ibn Sallām said it was *Banū al-Mughīra;* Saʿīd ibn Jubayr was told *it was twelve men.* Among the narrations, then, there is a range — but every one of them points at the same kind of person: the *elite.* The affluent. The men at the top of Mecca's comfort, who had the most to lose and the softest beds to lose it from.

**What this word reveals:** Allah handed us, in one word, the diagnosis of a specific spiritual disease — the denial that is not born of confusion but of *comfort.* The people most insulated from need are often the people least able to feel their need for God. And the verse names it without anger, almost clinically: *the possessors of ease.*

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have built, sitting side by side.

*Dharnī* — let go, hand it over. *Al-mukadhdhibīn* — the hardened, active deniers. *Ūlin-naʿmah* — sunk in luxury. And we still have the last phrase waiting: *and grant them respite, a little.*

The grammar has shown us a transaction: a burden lifted off the Prophet ﷺ and placed into the Hands of his Lord, with a precise note attached about *who* these people are and *why* they became what they became.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way a human heart is supposed to carry the people who wrong it, and the way God measures the comfortable man's time.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The burden you were never meant to carry

Let me start where you actually live.

Somewhere in your life there is a person — or several — who wronged you and never paid for it. Maybe they slandered you. Maybe they cut you off, took what was yours, humiliated you in front of people who mattered. And here is the part that eats at you: *they're fine.* They moved on. They're comfortable. They sleep well. And you, the one who was actually wronged, are the one lying awake — running the tape, rehearsing the confrontation, building the case.

There is a specific cruelty in that arithmetic. The wronged person ends up doing all the *suffering* and all the *labor.* You become the unpaid prosecutor in a case the defendant doesn't even know is ongoing.

Now hear the verse land into exactly that exhaustion.

**"Leave Me with them."**

Think about what the Prophet ﷺ is being released from. He is the most wronged man in Mecca. Mocked daily, called a liar, his message spat on by men who had everything. If anyone had the *right* to carry a case, it was him. And Allah does not say to him, "Win the argument." He does not say, "Get even." He says: *take your hands off it. The case is Mine now.*

Let that reframe the whole thing. The compulsion to make your enemy *see* — to extract the apology, to force the reckoning, to be the one who balances the scale — that compulsion is not justice. It is a *burden you assigned yourself.* And the verse is Allah gently informing you that **you applied for a job that was never open.** The position of Judge over the people who wronged you is *filled.* It has always been filled. By the One who misses nothing.

Here is the movement I want you to feel. When you insist on settling the score yourself, you are — without realizing it — telling Allah that you don't quite trust Him to do it. That He might let it slide. That if you don't keep the case file warm, it'll be forgotten. *Dharnī* — "leave Me with them" — is Allah saying: *do you really think it could be forgotten with Me?*

And notice the strange mercy underneath it. The reason you can let go is *not* that the wrong didn't matter. The verse doesn't minimize the denial — it names it in the harshest terms, *al-mukadhdhibīn.* You are not asked to pretend the wound is small. You are asked to believe that it is *seen* — fully, precisely, by Someone with the authority and the perfect knowledge to settle it in a way you, with your partial view and your hot blood, never could.

[PAUSE]

The freedom here is enormous, and it's practical. You can stop being the prison guard. You can put down the case file. Not because justice doesn't matter — because justice matters *too much* to be left in your trembling, biased hands instead of His perfect ones.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time the memory of that person rises up and your chest tightens and the old argument starts replaying — try the verse as an actual instruction. Say it: *I leave them with You.* Not as a dismissal of the wrong. As a *deliberate transfer of custody.* Put the file on His desk and walk out of the office. He was always going to handle it. You were only ever exhausting yourself by refusing to let go.

### Theme Two: The comfort that quietly costs you God — and the shortness of "a little"

Now let me take you to the second door, and this one points the mirror back at us.

We read *ūlin-naʿmah* — the people of luxury — and it is easy, far too easy, to picture the Quraysh chiefs and feel safe. *That's them. The arrogant rich pagans. Not me.* But the verse is not a museum piece. It is a diagnosis, and the disease it names is one of the most common in the comfortable modern world — perhaps the most common.

Here is the pattern. Comfort, when it becomes total, does something almost imperceptible: **it removes the felt need for God.** Not your belief, necessarily. Your *urgency.* The man whose every need is met — climate-controlled, well-fed, entertained, financially cushioned, never genuinely afraid — slowly loses the *posture of need.* And the posture of need is the posture of prayer.

Think about your own life. When do you turn to Allah with your whole heart? Not usually in the comfortable hours. It's in the hospital waiting room. The night before the result. The moment the money runs out, the diagnosis comes, the relationship cracks. *Need* is what bends the spine into sajdah. And comfort is the slow anesthetic that tells you, *you don't need to bend tonight. Everything's fine.*

That is why Allah names their *luxury* in the same breath as their *denial.* The surah opened by commanding the believer to *rise in the night* — to manufacture, on purpose, a posture of need and dependence when the body would rather sleep. And the deniers are described as the exact opposite: reclining in an ease so complete that the very muscle of need has atrophied.

So the verse is asking each of us, quietly: **which posture is mine?** Am I the one who rises in the dark to stand in need before my Lord — or have I become so comfortable that I have, without ever deciding to, stopped feeling that I need Him at all?

This is not a call to hate comfort. It is a warning about what *total, unexamined* comfort does to the soul. The night prayer in this surah is, among other things, the cure — a deliberate, chosen return to the posture of need, so that *naʿmah* never gets the chance to anesthetize you completely.

#### And now the last word — *qalīlan*

> **وَمَهِّلْهُمْ قَلِيلًا** — *and grant them respite, a little.*

Here is where the verse turns, and where its mercy and its terror sit in the same phrase.

*Mahhilhum* — *grant them respite, give them time.* It's a gentle word; there is no thunderbolt in it. Allah is not asking the Prophet ﷺ to wish destruction on them. He is saying: *let them be, for now. Give them their stretch of time.*

But then comes the measuring word: **qalīlan.** *A little.*

Sit with the contrast. To the men sunk in *naʿmah*, their comfort feels permanent. The wealth feels like it will last. The mockery feels like it carries no cost. The good life feels *endless.* And Allah, in a single word, prices the whole thing: *qalīlan.* A little. A short while. Whatever it looks like from inside the luxury, the actual measure of it, from above, is *small.*

The early commentators tell us how literally this landed. Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī note that many of these very men were killed not long after — at Badr. *Wa mahhilhum qalīlan* — "give them a little time" — and the little time ran out on a battlefield within a few years. The respite was real. It was also *short,* exactly as the word said.

[PAUSE]

Now bring it home, because *qalīlan* is not only about them. It is the true measure of every worldly comfort, including yours and mine. The Qur'an elsewhere calls the entire span of this life *matāʿun qalīl* — a brief enjoyment. Whatever ease you are resting in tonight — and thank Allah for it — its real measurement is *qalīl.* Little. The danger of *naʿmah* was never the comfort itself. It was forgetting that the comfort was *short* — mistaking *qalīlan* for *forever.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Two things. First — audit your comfort. Not to feel guilty for it, but to ask honestly: *has my ease quietly eroded my urgency? When did I last stand before Allah from a posture of real need, rather than out of routine?* And if the answer stings, you already know the surah's prescription: rise, even briefly, in the night, and put yourself back in the posture of need on purpose. Second — when you look at the comfortable people who deny and seem to pay no price, hold the word *qalīlan* in your mind. Their respite is not a contradiction of justice. It is a *measured* thing, and the measure is *small.* You are watching the short version of a story whose ending Allah has already written.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture of the verse

Now that we've done the work, step back and see how the six words are built. They are not a random list. They are a single, complete motion.

> **وَذَرْنِي** — *let go* (the hand opens)
> **وَٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ** — *of the hardened deniers* (here is what you're holding)
> **أُو۟لِي ٱلنَّعْمَةِ** — *the ones comfort blinded* (here is why they became this)
> **وَمَهِّلْهُمْ قَلِيلًا** — *give them their short time* (and here is what becomes of it)

It opens with a hand releasing and closes with a clock running out. In between, it names exactly *who* is being released and *why* they are the way they are. The verse is a complete transaction: the believer lets go of the case, and Allah quietly notes, on His way to taking it, that the defendants' apparent advantage — their luxury, their unpunished comfort — is both the *cause* of their ruin and a thing already measured at *small.*

### Bringing the two paths together

Look at how the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two sides.

From the **words**, we learned: the verb of letting-go cannot reach into the past; the deniers are an *active campaign*, not the merely lost; their *naʿmah* is the soft soil their denial grew in; their respite is measured *qalīlan*.

From the **themes**, we learned the lived version: you were never meant to carry the case against the people who wronged you, and the comfort that seems to protect the deniers is the very thing dissolving their need for God — on a clock that reads *short.*

They meet in one place. **The wronged believer and the comfortable denier are spending the same night in opposite postures** — one standing in chosen need before his Lord, light because he has handed his burden across; the other reclining in an ease he thinks is permanent, which Allah has already priced at *a little.* The whole verse is the hinge between those two nights.

### One-sentence distillation

*The night-trained heart does not carry the case against its enemies — it hands them across the desk to the One who said "leave them to Me," and discovers that their comfort was the soil of their denial and their respite was only ever measured a little.*

### Questions to carry

1. What case file am I still keeping warm — whose wrong against me am I still privately prosecuting, refusing to hand across the desk? What would it feel like to actually say *I leave them with You* and mean it?
2. Has my comfort quietly become an anesthetic? When did I last stand before Allah from real need, rather than routine?
3. I am quick to notice the *naʿmah* of those who deny and seem to escape — am I as quick to remember that my own ease is measured *qalīlan* too?
4. Do I let go forward, the way the verb *dharnī* lets go — or do I keep dragging the past back into a present that was supposed to be released?
5. If I truly believed the case was already in His Hands — fully seen, perfectly recorded — what would I stop carrying tonight?

### The closing invitation

You don't have to walk out of this carrying a new burden of guilt about your comfort, and you don't have to manufacture forgiveness you don't yet feel for the people who wronged you. The invitation is smaller and truer than that. It is simply this: *put the file down.* Open the hand. Let *dharnī* be a real instruction tonight — transfer the custody of your grievance to the only One with the authority to judge it, and the only One who will not let it slide. And in the same motion, glance at your own ease and remember the word *qalīlan*, so that comfort never gets to do to you what it did to them.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah — You who said "leave Me with them," teach my hands to open. Take from me the cases I was never meant to carry, and let me trust that nothing is forgotten with You. And do not let my comfort blind me to my need for You; keep me among those who rise in the night with open hearts, not among those whose ease made them forget — for I know that all of this is qalīl, a little, and that only what is with You remains.*
