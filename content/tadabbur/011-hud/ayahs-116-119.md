# The Remnant That Resists Corruption, the Reform That Delays Ruin, and the Mercy That Saves from Difference

## Surah Hud (11:116-119)

[ayah:11:116] فَلَوْلَا كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْقُرُونِ مِن قَبْلِكُمْ أُو۟لُوا۟ بَقِيَّةٍ يَنْهَوْنَ عَنِ ٱلْفَسَادِ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا مِّمَّنْ أَنجَيْنَا مِنْهُمْ ۗ وَٱتَّبَعَ ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟ مَآ أُتْرِفُوا۟ فِيهِ وَكَانُوا۟ مُجْرِمِينَ

## Introduction

After Surah Hud commands us to stand straight, not lean toward zulm, keep the prayer, and remain patient, it asks a devastating historical question.

Why were there not, in those earlier civilizations, people of moral remainder who kept standing up against corruption before it swallowed the land?

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most important passages in the surah for understanding why societies collapse. It is not only that corruption exists. It is that too few people retain enough inward residue of truth to confront it. Too few people remain with enough moral remainder to say no before fasad becomes normal.

Then the surah states a principle that protects the heart from cynical readings of history: Allah does not destroy towns unjustly while their people are **muslihun** - actively reforming. After that, the horizon broadens even further. Human beings will continue to differ, except those whom Allah shows mercy. And the passage ends by leaving us between two immensities: divine mercy and the fulfilled word that Jahannam will be filled by those who refuse it.

The hook of the passage is this: **Hud teaches that civilizations are preserved by people of moral remainder who resist corruption, that real reform matters before Allah, and that in a world where difference persists, mercy is the only true exit from collective ruin.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Ulu baqiyyah**: The People Who Still Have Something Left

The passage asks:

**fa-lawla kana mina al-quruni min qablikum ulu baqiyyatin yanhawna 'ani al-fasadi fi al-ard**.

Why were there not among the generations before you people of **baqiyyah** who forbade corruption in the earth?

[PAUSE]

That word is extraordinary. **Baqiyyah** comes from remaining, being left, enduring after loss. al-Tabari reads **ulu baqiyyah** with precision: those possessed of a remnant *of understanding and intellect* (baqiyyah min al-fahm wa al-'aql) — people who still take heed of Allah's admonitions and ponder His proofs, and so recognize what is owed to Him. Ibn Kathir frames them as a group of wise people who called to good and forbade the corruption spreading in the land, and al-Muyassar as a surviving core of the people of good and righteousness who forbade the disbelievers their disbelief and their corruption on earth. Building on that classical register — which is first epistemic and rational before it becomes anything else — one careful way to sit with the phrase is: people who still have something sound left in them, in whom both clear sight and moral substance remain after a society has begun to decay.

The verse is not merely asking for loud activists or socially visible critics. It is asking for a remnant of inward durability — of preserved understanding that has not been dulled. The kind of people who do not get completely dissolved by the atmosphere around them.

And what do these people do? **Yanhawna 'ani al-fasad**. They forbid corruption. Their remaining goodness does not stay private. It takes the form of resistance.

Then comes the grief:

**illa qalilan mimman anjayna minhum**.

Except a few of those We saved.

The remnant existed, but it was small. Small enough to become the exception named after the disaster. Ibn Kathir notes that it was precisely these few whom Allah rescued at the sudden striking of His vengeance, when His anger descended on the rest.

### 2. **Ma utrifu fihi**: Luxury as a Path of Moral Capture

Then the ayah says:

**wa-ittaba'a alladhina zalamu ma utrifu fihi**.

Those who wronged followed what they had been given in luxury.

[PAUSE]

This is not a casual mention of comfort. al-Muyassar reads it plainly: the mass of the wrongdoers followed the pleasures and enjoyments of the world they had been indulged in, and were criminals, wrongdoers, *by that very following* — so the punishment fell justly upon them. The verse is naming one of the engines of civilizational ruin: people become morally loyal to the very state of ease that has softened their resistance to truth.

They followed what they had been indulged in. They did not merely enjoy it. They were led by it. Their pleasures became directional. One way to feel the weight of the phrase is that their luxury was no longer background; it quietly taught them what not to challenge, what not to lose, and therefore what truths had to be suppressed.

Then the ayah lands the moral verdict:

**wa kanu mujrimin**.

They were criminals.

The Qur'an does not let luxury remain morally neutral once it becomes an accomplice to fasad.

### 3. **Muslihun** and the Mercy Hidden in the Principle of Non-Destruction

Then comes the principle:

**wa ma kana rabbuka li-yuhlika al-qura bi-zulmin wa ahluha muslihun**.

Your Lord would not destroy towns unjustly while their people are reforming.

[PAUSE]

Notice the choice of word. The verse does not only say **salihun** - personally righteous. It says **muslihun** - reforming, setting things right, actively repairing. al-Muyassar glosses it as those who *shun corruption and injustice* (mujtanibun lil-fasad wal-zulm) — the accent is on active avoidance and repair, not mere private piety.

That is immense. The surah is teaching that Allah's way with societies is not detached from whether there are people still pushing back against disorder, still working repair into public life, still refusing to let corruption become the final language of the land.

Here the classical commentators open a genuine ikhtilaf on **bi-zulm** — "unjustly." al-Tabari records the majority sense: Allah would not destroy the towns *wrongfully* while their people were setting right their dealings and obeying Him, for to ruin a reforming people would itself be injustice, and He is never unjust; rather He destroyed them for their disbelief, their persistence in error, and their rejection of the messengers. A second reported reading takes **bi-zulm** to mean *bi-shirk* — that He would not destroy a community merely for idolatry so long as they were otherwise **muslihun** in their conduct toward one another. Either way, the verse ties a community's survival to whether the work of reform is still alive within it.

This does not mean a society must be sinless. It means the line of islah must still be alive.

### 4. Difference, Mercy, and the Final Sorting

Then the passage widens beyond one civilization:

**wa law sha'a rabbuka la-ja'ala al-nasa ummatan wahidah wa la yazaluna mukhtalifin**.

Had your Lord willed, He could have made humanity one community. But they do not cease differing.

[PAUSE]

This gives perspective. Human difference is not happening outside Allah's sovereignty. He could have erased it. He did not. The world we inhabit is one in which divergence, fracture, and opposing paths persist by His wisdom.

Then comes the exception that matters more than the rule:

**illa man rahima rabbuk**.

Except the one your Lord has shown mercy.

The exception is not a footnote. It names the only ones lifted out of that endless divergence — those whom the mercy of the Lord reaches and holds.

Then the verse says:

**wa li-dhalika khalaqahum**.

And for that He created them.

The referent of *dhalika* — "that" — is the single most debated question in this passage among the classical commentators, and it is worth knowing that the disagreement is real and old rather than pretending the verse yields one settled sense. On what does "for that" turn? One camp reads it back to the nearest noun, **mercy**: He created them *for mercy* — humanity was made so that the people of mercy might be gathered into it (a reading reported from Ibn Abbas and others). Another prominent camp reads it back to **ikhtilaf**, difference itself: He created them *for* this differing — the divergence is part of what they were made for, the arena in which they are tried and sorted into the saved and the lost. A third way widens *dhalika* to the whole matter — mercy and difference together, the trial and its outcome. The Qur'an leaves the pronoun open, and none of these readings licenses us to resolve it with false confidence. What they share is that the verse humbles us before a wisdom we did not design.

And then the passage closes with terrifying finality:

**la-amla'anna jahannama mina al-jinnati wa al-nasi ajma'in**.

I will surely fill Jahannam with jinn and human beings altogether.

Mercy is real. Rescue is real. But so is the completed word against those who persist in rebellion.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Civilizations Often Die Because Too Few People Keep Enough Truth Left in Them

There is a kind of social failure deeper than corruption itself. It is the disappearance of the people who still feel responsible to confront it.

[PAUSE]

When a culture becomes saturated with ease, image, appetite, and collective self-protection, moral people are tempted into one of two retreats. Some go inward and keep their goodness private. Others remain publicly present but are slowly trained not to disturb the atmosphere. Both retreats create the same result: fasad keeps spreading because too few **ulu baqiyyah** remain — too few who still keep both the understanding to see the decay and the will to name it.

Hud asks whether we are becoming the kind of people who still have enough left in us to interrupt decay. Not merely to observe it. Not merely to criticize it from a safe distance. But to actually stand in its way.

### Theme 2: What Protects a Society Is Not Innocence but Islah

The verse about destruction is profoundly hopeful. Allah does not say He only preserves a people who have no wrong at all. He says He does not destroy while they are **muslihun**.

[PAUSE]

That means a society may still be wounded, uneven, sinful, and struggling, yet remain under a different horizon if the line of repair is alive. If there are still people reconciling, forbidding harm, restoring justice, resisting corruption, and calling one another back to Allah, the surah tells us that such work is not marginal. It is one of the things that stands between a people and ruin.

This should change how we look at ordinary acts of repair. Honest correction, community care, teaching truth, resisting exploitation, returning people to prayer, refusing to flatter injustice - all of this belongs to **islah**. And Hud gives that work civilizational weight.

## Closing Synthesis

The earlier peoples were not only ruined by their sins; they were also ruined by the scarcity of people with enough understanding and moral remainder to forbid corruption before it took over. Those who wronged were carried by the luxury that had softened and captured them. Allah, however, does not destroy towns unjustly while their people are actively reforming. Human beings will continue to differ, except those whom Allah gathers under mercy, and the whole matter still ends under a fulfilled divine word: Jahannam will be filled by those who reject that mercy.

[PAUSE]

This passage leaves us with both burden and relief. The burden is that moral remainder must become moral action. The relief is that islah matters, mercy matters, and collapse is not narrated as random.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with these ayahs is to ask: in the environments I live in, what forms of corruption have become so normalized that people no longer expect resistance? What comforts have quietly taught me what not to challenge? Am I preserving faith privately while withdrawing from islah publicly? And when I look at human difference — over which the commentators themselves differed as to why we were made for it — do I treat it as a puzzle to master, or as a condition in which I beg Allah for the mercy that keeps me on truth?

[PAUSE]

Hud leaves us with a hard, necessary hope: **a society is not preserved by prosperity alone, but by the remnant that still resists corruption and by the mercy of Allah that gathers people out of ruin and onto truth.**
