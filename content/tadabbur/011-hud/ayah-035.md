# If I Fabricated It, Then My Crime Is on Me; I Am Clear of Yours

## Surah Hud (11:35)

[ayah:11:35] أَمْ يَقُولُونَ ٱفْتَرَىٰهُ ۖ قُلْ إِنِ ٱفْتَرَيْتُهُۥ فَعَلَىَّ إِجْرَامِى وَأَنَا۠ بَرِىٓءٌ مِّمَّا تُجْرِمُونَ

*Am yaquluna iftarahu. Qul ini iftaraytuhu fa-'alayya ijrami wa ana bari'un mimma tujrimun.*

**Or do they say, "He fabricated it"? Say: if I have fabricated it, then upon me is my crime, and I am free of what you commit.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

False accusation often expects panic.

It wants the truthful person to become flustered, over-explain themselves, and get dragged into a fog where the original clarity of the message disappears under defensive motion. One of the quiet miracles of this ayah is that it teaches a prophetic answer that is morally sharp and emotionally unpanicked.

[PAUSE]

The accusation is blunt: **iftarahu** - he made it up. He fabricated it. He forged revelation and attached it to Allah. But the answer does not spiral. It does not become theatrical self-vindication. It says something far more powerful: if I did fabricate it, then I bear that crime myself. And as for the crimes you are committing, I am clear of them.

But before any of that, notice where this verse is standing.

We are in the middle of a story. Nuh has been arguing with his people for a very long time. They have just told him to stop debating and bring on the punishment. He has answered them. And then, mid-scene, a completely different voice breaks in: *or do they say, he fabricated it?*

Ibn Kathir treats this as a deliberate interruption. He describes it as placed in the middle of the story in order to affirm the story itself. That is worth sitting with. The pause is not a distraction from the narrative; it is a defense of it. The charge being answered is not only "you invented a teaching." It is "you invented this account." Al-Tabari makes that explicit: the objection he hears behind the verse is, did Muhammad make up this Qur'an, and this report about Nuh?

So the ayah does two things at once. It answers a live accusation, and by answering it right here — inside the story, without fully stepping out of it — it quietly insists that the story is true enough to be interrupted for.

[PAUSE]

There is a second reading, and it is worth knowing. Al-Muyassar keeps the whole verse inside the narrative: the objectors are Nuh's own people, the accusation is against Nuh, and the reply is Nuh's own reply. The wording allows this, because the verse names nobody. It says only "do *they* say," and then simply "say." Both readings are held by serious commentators, and neither has to be forced onto the words.

What is striking is that the answer works identically in either mouth. That may itself be the lesson. This is not a one-off rebuttal tailored to one man's moment. It is the standing prophetic reply to a standing human accusation, and it fits Nuh in his generation as exactly as it fits the Prophet ﷺ in his.

The hook of the ayah is this: **Hud teaches that truth does not need frantic self-defense; it can calmly place liability where it belongs and refuse moral entanglement with the collective crimes of denial.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Iftarahu ... iftaraytuhu**: The Accusation Is Answered in Its Own Vocabulary

The ayah opens:

**am yaquluna iftarahu**.

Or do they say: he fabricated it?

[PAUSE]

Even the first word is doing work. **Am** is usually flattened in translation to "or," but both al-Tabari and al-Muyassar hear it as a turn: *rather* — do they say this? It is the sound of one subject being set down and another picked up. The verse is pivoting, and the pivot is audible in the opening syllable.

The verb **iftara** is not casual mistake. It is invention with false ascription, especially in matters attributed to Allah. In the Qur'an this is among the gravest forms of speech-corruption: to manufacture something and then pin it onto divine authority.

Al-Tabari explains the word with two others: **takharrus** and **ikhtilaq**. The first is speaking from guesswork — saying a thing without knowing whether it is so. The second is manufacture — assembling something that was not there. Together they describe one continuous offense: guess, then build, then sign Allah's name at the bottom. The accusation is not "you were mistaken." It is "you constructed this, deliberately, and attributed it upward."

What is striking is that the response repeats the same root:

**qul ini iftaraytuhu**.

Say: if I fabricated it...

He is taught not to flee the word. The accusation is brought into the open and answered directly. There is dignity in that. Sometimes falsehood gains strength because the truthful become afraid even to name the lie being told about them. The Qur'an teaches a steadier posture. Name it. Then place responsibility precisely.

### 2. **Fa-'alayya ijrami**: If I Lied, the Burden Is Mine

Then comes the phrase:

**fa-'alayya ijrami**.

Then upon me is my crime.

[PAUSE]

The word **ijram** comes from the field of **j-r-m**, جرم, the root of incurring offense, criminality, culpable violation. The response is astonishingly direct. If I am guilty, then I carry that guilt. If I have done what you claim, then I do not ask you to absorb it for me, excuse it for me, or blur it for me.

There is a moral confidence here that only truth can sustain. Liars usually want diffusion. They want fog, shared blame, procedural distraction, emotional smoke. Truth can afford clarity.

Notice also the order of the words. The verse does not say "my crime is upon me." It says **fa-'alayya** first — *upon me* — and only then names the crime. Fronting a phrase like that in Arabic tends to narrow it, to fence it off. Both Ibn Kathir and al-Muyassar read that narrowing, and both render the clause with a word the verse never spells out: *mine alone.* If there is a sin here, it has one address and no other.

And notice the singular:

**ijrami** - my crime.

It is individualized. Account will not be collectivized. The burden of speech before Allah sits where it belongs: on the speaker.

Al-Tabari draws that out in both directions. His paraphrase runs roughly like this: my sin, in whatever I fabricated against my Lord, is upon me and not upon you — you will not be held to account for my wrong, and I will not be held to account for yours. That is the entire architecture of moral responsibility in one breath. Nobody inherits anybody's record. And it cuts both ways, which is why it is a comfort and a warning at once: no one else's guilt will be piled onto you, and no one else will be standing there to absorb yours.

There is also something the verse deliberately does not say. It gives us one branch of the condition — *if I fabricated it, then...* — and then stops. The other branch is left hanging in the air. Al-Muyassar is the one who says it out loud: and if I am telling the truth, then you are the criminals. The verse leaves that sentence for the listener to finish inside his own head.

That is not a gap. It is a method. An accusation said aloud can be argued with. A conclusion the accuser arrives at by himself is much harder to shake off.

### 3. **Wa ana bari'un mimma tujrimun**: Innocence Is Not Merely "I Didn't Do It"; It Is Moral Separation

Then the ayah completes the answer:

**wa ana bari'un mimma tujrimun**.

And I am clear of what you commit.

[PAUSE]

The word **bari'** carries the meaning of being free, absolved, disentangled, clear of association. This is more than denying one charge. It is a refusal to be woven into the criminal fabric of the accusers.

And now notice the shift from the singular to the plural. First:

**ijrami** - my crime, if I did it.

Then:

**mimma tujrimun** - from what you all are committing.

This is powerful. The ayah refuses false symmetry. It does not say, "If I lied and you lied, then we are all flawed." No. One hypothetical personal liability is set against the ongoing, multiple crimes of the deniers. Their problem is not merely one mistaken sentence. It is a whole pattern of جرم.

The Messenger is being taught to separate himself from that pattern with complete moral clarity.

Al-Muyassar spells out what "what you commit" actually contains. He unpacks it into three things: your disbelief, your calling the truth a lie, and your crime. So it is not a vague cloud of wrongdoing. It is a sequence — you refuse, then you accuse, then you act — and the answer declares itself clear of all three stages, not only the last one.

Then comes the sharpest turn in the verse, and it is easy to walk past. What exactly is the crime they are committing? Al-Tabari names it plainly: their own fabricating against Allah. The word comes back around. They accused him of **iftira'** — inventing something and attaching it to Allah — and it turns out that inventing things about Allah is precisely what they have been doing the entire time, in every claim they have made about Him.

So the accusation boomerangs, and it does so without a raised voice. He never says "you are the liars." He says only: I am clear of what you commit. It is left to the listener to notice what they commit — and to notice that it is the very thing they charged him with.

That is a devastating reply delivered as a calm sentence.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Integrity Does Not Need Performative Panic

Many of us think that innocence must always sound frantic.

If someone misrepresents us, slanders us, questions our intention, or accuses us of hidden motives, we feel an almost immediate urge to over-defend. We want to explain every angle, narrate every detail, recruit witnesses, and somehow force everyone to feel our innocence at the same speed we feel their injustice.

[PAUSE]

This ayah teaches another posture. State the matter clearly. If I did what you say, I bear it before Allah. If not, then I do not need to drown in your theater. There is a serenity available to the truthful because they know that moral reality is not ultimately decided by suspicion campaigns.

That does not mean we never clarify. The ayah itself is a clarification. But it is not a clarification driven by panic. It is driven by accountability before Allah.

### Theme 2: One of the Hardest Forms of Taqwa Is Refusing Shared Criminal Atmosphere

The second half of the ayah reaches into another part of our lives. Sometimes we are not being accused personally. Sometimes we are being invited into a shared moral atmosphere.

Everyone around us is mocking something sacred. Normalizing a corruption. Repeating a slander. Smearing sincerity. Treating revelation as human invention. And the pressure is not always, "Say it too." Often it is subtler: laugh with us, nod with us, don't distance yourself from us, don't make our wrongdoing feel like wrongdoing.

[PAUSE]

The word **bari'** becomes medicine here. A believer must sometimes say with the heart, the tongue, or at least the posture of refusal: I am not with this. I will not lend myself to this جرم. I will not let belonging become partnership in offense.

This can be socially expensive. It may make you feel awkward, exposed, severe, or out of step. But every age has its forms of collective جرم, and one of the marks of faith is learning how not to dissolve into them.

And there is one more inward layer. Sometimes the collective atmosphere we need to separate from is not "out there." It is the conversation inside our own chest. The cynical inner voice. The reflex that treats divine command as human invention. The part of the self that wants to reduce revelation to psychology, habit, or culture so that surrender can be postponed.

This ayah can be read there too. I am not with that crime. I will not help that inner slander against Allah's truth.

### Theme 3: Sometimes the Strongest Defense of a Truth Is to Keep Telling It

Look at the placement again, because the placement teaches something on its own.

Someone has said: you made this up. And the response is not to put the story down and mount a defense. The response is one verse of clarity — and then the story continues. The very next verse returns to Nuh and picks the account back up as if nothing had happened. The narrative was never surrendered.

Ibn Kathir's reading is that the interruption exists in order to confirm the story. That is worth noticing. The confirmation is not a separate argument bolted on from outside the account. It is a brief, sober pause placed inside the telling, after which the telling simply resumes at full confidence.

[PAUSE]

Most of us do the opposite. When our sincerity is questioned, the work stops. The project gets set down while we go and handle the accusation. Weeks disappear into managing other people's suspicion. And very often the accusation was never really about the claim at all; it was a way of getting the work to stop.

This verse models something steadier. Answer once. Answer honestly. Put the liability exactly where it belongs. Then go back to what you were doing. Over time, the most persuasive evidence that something was not invented tends to be that the one accused of inventing it is still calmly at work on it afterward.

## Closing Synthesis

The ayah opens with the accusation of fabrication and answers it with remarkable steadiness. If I fabricated it, then my crime is on me. I will answer for what I said. But I am clear of what you are committing.

[PAUSE]

The response is brief because the truth does not need sprawling theatrics to remain true. It places liability where it belongs, names innocence as moral separation, and refuses to let the accusation create a false partnership between messenger and denier.

And it is brief for another reason. It never finishes its own sentence. Only one half of the condition is spoken — *if I fabricated it* — and the other half, the half that would convict them, is left for them to complete. The verse trusts silence to do work that argument cannot.

[PAUSE]

Its position matters as much as its words. Dropped into the middle of Nuh's story, answered in a few syllables, and then the story resumes — the whole shape of the passage says that a truth worth telling does not stop being told just because someone doubts it.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this ayah is to ask: do I need endless human vindication before I can stay steady in the truth? Where am I being pressured into a shared atmosphere of جرم simply so I do not feel isolated? And when falsehood becomes socially normal around me, do I know how to remain **bari'** of it before Allah?

[PAUSE]

Hud leaves us with this calm moral discipline: **truthful speech can bear personal accountability without fear, and real innocence includes refusing any inward or outward alliance with the crimes that are being committed against Allah's truth.**
