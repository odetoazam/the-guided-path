# The Man Who Came Running

## Introduction

Listen to how Allah tells it.

---

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand inside the town.

Surah Ya-Sin is telling a parable, and the Prophet ﷺ has just been given the most crushing news a caller to Allah can receive: your own people will not listen. The surah opens by swearing an oath — *by the wise Qur'an, you are among the messengers* — and then, almost immediately, it tells him the shape of the road ahead. *You only warn one who follows the Reminder.* Most will not. Allah has, in the words of the surah, placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them.

Imagine what that does to a heart that loves people. The Prophet ﷺ was not a man who wanted to be right. He was a man who wanted them saved. And the surah is telling him: they will turn away, and it will feel like screaming into a sealed room.

So Allah gives him a story. Not a law. Not an argument. A **story** — because a heart in that particular kind of pain does not need to be corrected, it needs to be accompanied. He tells the Prophet ﷺ: this has happened before. There was a town. We sent it messengers — first two, then reinforced by a third. And the town did exactly what your town is doing. They said *you are only men like us*. They said *we see you as a bad omen*. They threatened to stone the messengers to death.

And then — from the far edge of that town — someone came running.

Not a prophet. Not a scholar. The tradition remembers him as Ḥabīb the carpenter, but the Qur'an does not even give him a name. Just *a man*. One man, who heard that the messengers were about to be killed, and who could not stay where he was.

Here is the moment I want to hang a question on, and then leave it hanging.

This man says a few sentences. Then he dies — the tradition says his own people fell on him and killed him on the spot. And Allah, who could describe that death in agonizing detail, who describes the destruction of entire nations elsewhere with thunder and shrieks — **says nothing about it at all.** One moment the man is speaking to a mob. The next word out of Allah's mouth is: *Enter the Garden.*

What happened in the gap? And why did Allah refuse to let us see it?

Stay with that. We are going to earn the answer.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### One: *A man — running*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the text, because if I don't, you'll walk right past the first miracle.

In English, when we say "a man came," the word "a" is doing quiet work. It's telling you: you don't know this person. He's not *the* man, some figure we've already met — he's *a* man, one of the crowd, unremarkable, anonymous. Arabic does the same thing, but it does it without a separate word. It does it with a sound at the *end* of the word — a little *-un* hum called *tanwīn*. When a noun carries that hum, it's indefinite. Anonymous. One-of-many.

Now — Arabic normally likes its sentences in a certain order: verb, then who did it. "Came the man." But Allah does something here. He says: *wa jāʾa min aqṣā-l-madīnati* — "and there came, from the farthest end of the city..." — and He holds the man back. The whole phrase about *distance* comes first. Only after you've traveled all the way across the town does the subject finally arrive: *rajulun.* **A man.** With that anonymous hum on the end.

Sit with the order Allah chose. First the running. First the distance — *aqṣā*, from a root meaning the utmost, the farthest limit, the edge beyond which there is no more city. This man was as far from the messengers as a person could be and still be inside the walls. Then, last of all, almost as an afterthought: *a* man. Not *the* leader. Not *the* elder. Nobody you'd know.

Notice what the indefiniteness does. Allah could have named him. He didn't. And that withholding is a gift — because the moment you name him, he becomes *someone else*. He becomes Ḥabīb the carpenter, a specific saint, a person you are not. But leave him anonymous, and the door stays open. *A* man. Any man. **This** man could be you.

Now listen to the verb Allah hangs on him: *yasʿā.* From the root *saʿā* — to run, to strive, to move with urgent purpose. It is not *mashā*, to walk. It is not *jāʾa* by itself, to merely arrive. Ya-Sin has already used *jāʾa* — "there came" — so why add *yasʿā* on top of it? Because *jāʾa* tells you he arrived, and *yasʿā* tells you *how his body was arriving.* He was running. He was out of breath. Feel the phonetics of it — *yas-ʿā* — that *ʿayn* in the middle is a sound made from the very bottom of the throat, a sound with effort in it, a sound you cannot make lazily. The word itself has exertion inside it.

Here's what I want you to see. Allah does not describe this man's face. We don't know his age, his wealth, his eloquence. Allah gives us exactly two facts: he was *far*, and he was *running.* And those two facts, placed side by side, are the entire portrait of a certain kind of soul.

Let me bring it close. You know the feeling of being at the back of a crowd when something is going wrong at the front. There's a car accident. Someone has collapsed. And there's a strange social physics in a crowd — the people closest do the least, each one assuming someone else will act, and the whole mass just... watches. Psychologists have a cold name for it: the bystander effect. The more people present, the *less* likely any single one is to help, because responsibility gets divided until each person's slice is nearly zero.

Now picture the one person who breaks it. Where do they usually come from? Very often — from the *edge*. From the back. From the farthest end. The person with the least standing, the least to protect, sometimes sees most clearly that *someone has to move,* and moves. The center is paralyzed by everything it has to lose. The edge is free.

That is *min aqṣā-l-madīnati rajulun yasʿā.* The center of the town — the elders, the powerful, the ones with reputations — sat still and let messengers be threatened with stoning. And from the farthest edge, an anonymous man ran. **The distance he had to travel is the measure of everyone in between who didn't move.** Every person he passed was closer than he was. Not one of them ran.

So the first word of this story is not really about one man. It is a question laid at the feet of everyone who heard the commotion and stayed comfortable. And the last thing Allah lets you do is hide behind his name — because He never gave it. *A* man. The seat is still open.

### Two: The Name He Reached For

Now the man begins to reason out loud, and I need you to watch one word — one Name — the way you'd watch a chess player's hand hover over a single piece.

He is arguing against idolatry. He says: why would I take, besides Allah, gods who can do nothing for me? And he builds the argument around a scenario of *harm*. He says — *in yuridni... bi-ḍurrin* — "if He should intend me harm." *Ḍurr,* from a root — *ḍarra* — that means injury, affliction, the harm that presses down on you. And listen to the sound of it: *ḍurr.* A heavy, blocked, doubled consonant. The tongue jams against the roof of the mouth and the sound can't get out cleanly. It is a phonetically *difficult* word, and it is naming a difficult thing. The sound is doing the meaning's work.

Here's the question. When you are about to speak of *harm* coming from Allah — of affliction, of the possibility that the Divine will could press down on you — which Name of Allah would you reach for? Most of us, honestly, would reach for a Name of power. *Al-Jabbār*, the Compeller. *Al-Qahhār*, the Dominant. A Name with teeth in it, to match the harm.

The man does not. Watch his hand settle on the piece. *In yuridni-r-Raḥmānu bi-ḍurr.* "If **the Most Merciful** should intend me harm."

*Ar-Raḥmān.* From the root *raḥima* — the same root as *raḥm*, the womb. The Name that means mercy so vast it is almost a physical enclosure, mercy like the womb that surrounds and sustains a life that has done nothing to earn it. And now hear the two words back to back in his mouth: *ar-Raḥmān... bi-ḍurr.* The Most Merciful... with harm. He has placed the softest, most enveloping Name in the language directly against the hardest, most blocked word for injury.

Why would he do that?

Let me teach the tool, because there's a principle of eloquence buried here. In Arabic rhetoric, when you attach a description to a subject, that description is understood to be *relevant to the action* — it colors it. To say "the doctor cut me" and "the healer cut me" are two different sentences even if it's the same person and the same cut. The description tells you how to read the act.

So when the man says "if *the Most Merciful* should intend me harm," he is not making a grammatical accident. He is telling you how to read the harm. He is saying: **even if affliction comes to me, it comes from the One whose very Name is mercy — so it, too, will somehow be mercy.** The harm does not come from a random cruelty in the universe. It does not come from *Al-Qahhār* crushing an enemy. If it comes at all, it comes from *ar-Raḥmān*, and a womb does not injure the child it carries except to bring it, finally, out into life.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what he just did? He is *arguing for tawhīd* — for pure monotheism — and even mid-argument, even while imagining his own suffering, his tongue cannot speak of Allah except through mercy. His theology has soaked so deep that it colors his instinctive word-choice. This is a man who, at the thought of being harmed by God, calls Him *the Merciful.*

And then he closes the point. Those false gods, he says — their intercession *lā tughni ʿannī shayʾan,* would not avail me a thing, *wa lā yunqidhūn,* nor could they rescue me. Notice the asymmetry he sets up, because it's the whole argument in miniature. On one side: *ar-Raḥmān,* one Being, whose harm is still mercy. On the other side: a *plurality* of gods — he uses the plural — who cannot deliver even one atom of benefit. **He is not weighing many gods against one God. He is weighing a crowd of nothings against the single Everything.** And the tie-breaker isn't power. It's mercy. He'd rather be in the hands of the Merciful, even for harm, than in the hands of a hundred idols for rescue.

One way to sit with this: the man has understood something we spend our whole lives trying to feel. That safety is not the absence of harm. Safety is *whose hands you are in when the harm comes.*

### Three: The Silence Where His Death Should Be

We come now to the gap I opened at the very start — and I've made you wait for it on purpose, because you cannot feel an absence until you've felt the fullness around it.

Look at how much the man says. Eight lines of speech. He calls his people. He argues. He reasons through tawhīd with the care of a scholar. He builds to a climax — *innī āmantu bi-rabbikum fa-smaʿūn* — "Indeed, I have believed in your Lord, so hear me." That word *fa-smaʿūn,* "so listen to me" — he is turning to the mob, the very people about to kill him, and asking them to *witness his declaration.* His last recorded act among the living is to make sure they heard him say it.

And then.

*Qīla-dkhuli-l-jannah.* "It was said: Enter the Garden."

Stop. Read what is *not* there. Between "so hear me" and "Enter the Garden," a man died. The tradition tells us they fell on him and killed him where he stood. That is a violent, agonizing death — a lone man torn apart by his own townspeople for the crime of defending strangers. Allah describes deaths elsewhere in the Qur'an. He describes the drowning of Pharaoh, the shriek that seized nations, the earth swallowing Qārūn. He is not shy of death.

Here — nothing. Not one word. The mob, the blows, the pain, the last breath — Allah **draws a veil over all of it** and cuts, in a single instant, from the man standing on the ground begging his people to listen, to a voice from the unseen saying *Enter.*

Now — the grammar of that voice. *Qīla.* "It was said." Passive. Allah does not tell us who spoke. Was it the angels? Was it Allah Himself? The passive leaves it unnamed, and that unnaming is not vagueness — it's *awe.* It is the same reverent hush you'd feel if I told you "and then a voice said." You don't ask whose voice. You just know it came from beyond the veil, and that it was the only voice that mattered.

Let me teach you why the silence is the point, with something from ordinary life.

When you tell the story of the worst night of your life — a loss, a betrayal, a hospital — and you've healed from it, watch what happens to how you tell it. In the raw years, the story is *all* about the wound. Every detail of the pain. But once you've truly moved through it, the telling changes. You'll say something like: "and then I lost him, and — well. Anyway. That's when I met the people who became my whole life." You *skip* the wound. Not because it didn't happen. Because from where you now stand, it is no longer the important part of the sentence. The pain has become a comma. The healing is the clause.

That is what Allah's silence is doing. He is telling this story *from the man's own vantage point now* — from inside the Garden. And from inside the Garden, the man's murder is a comma. It is the least important thing that happened to him that day. So Allah tells it the way the man himself would tell it: *"I told them to listen... and then I was in Paradise."* The death doesn't even get a word, because from the far side, **the death was nothing but the doorway, and who narrates the doorway when they're describing the house?**

[PAUSE]

Read the absence and read it carefully: the thing his killers thought they were doing — *ending* him — was so utterly weightless that the Qur'an will not even spend a syllable acknowledging it happened. They thought they wrote the last line of his story. Allah didn't even let their act into the paragraph.

---

**So here is what the three words have shown us together.** *A man, running* — that anyone, from the edge, unnamed, might be the one to move. *Ar-Raḥmān against ḍurr* — that a heart soaked in tawhīd speaks mercy even into its own harm. And *the silence where his death should be* — that from the far side of the veil, the worst thing they can do to you doesn't merit a sentence.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not about grammar, but about the shape of a human life, and how it can be built so that even death cannot interrupt it.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: You Are Being Asked to Prove the Wrong Thing

Let me start with something you already do, probably several times a day.

Someone on the internet says something you find outrageous — a belief, a lifestyle, a claim about God — and you feel the automatic response rise up: *prove it.* Where's your evidence? The burden is on the person making the claim. We live in a culture that has made "the burden of proof is on you" into a kind of intellectual religion. And it has a specific unspoken assumption baked into it: that the *default* is not-believing, and belief is the strange thing that needs defending. Belief is the claim. Non-belief is just... the neutral ground you stand on until someone drags you off it with enough evidence.

So the modern believer walks around feeling like the defendant. Perpetually on the back foot. *Justify your faith. Why do you believe in God? Explain yourself.* And a whole industry of apologetics has grown up to help us do exactly that — to build the case for the defense, to prove belief to a jury that has already been told it's the neutral one and we're the ones with something to answer for.

Now listen to the man from the edge of the city, because he does something that should reorganize your entire posture.

He does not say "here is why I believe." He says — *wa mā liya lā aʿbudu-lladhī faṭaranī.* And the way to feel this is almost impossible to render in polite English, but it's something like: **"And what is *wrong with me* that I would *not* worship the One who originated me?"**

Feel the flip. He does not treat worship as the claim that needs defending. He treats *not*-worshipping as the absurdity that needs explaining. He puts the burden of proof on disbelief. *What would have to be broken in me,* he's saying, *what would have to have gone wrong, for me to look at the One who made me from nothing and decide not to turn toward Him? That's the position that needs a justification. Not mine.*

And watch the word he chose for "created." He does not say *khalaqa*, the ordinary word for made. He says *faṭaranī* — from the root *faṭara*, which carries the sense of originating something by splitting it open into existence, bringing it forth brand-new, the way a seed splits the soil or a spring breaks open from rock. It is the same root as *fiṭra* — the primordial nature Allah placed in every soul, the inner compass that points home before anyone teaches it to.

So when he says *the One who faṭara-nī* — the One who originated me — he is invoking the very faculty that recognizes God. He's saying: the same Hand that split me into existence set a needle in me that points to Him. To *not* worship Him isn't neutrality. It's holding the compass and deliberately walking against the needle. **That** is what needs explaining.

Let me bring this all the way home, into your Tuesday.

The next time you feel that familiar pressure — that you owe the world an explanation for your faith, that you're the one with the burden — I want you to hear the carpenter's voice. He's not asking you to win a debate. He's asking you to notice which way the needle in your own chest already points. You did not install your longing for meaning. You did not manufacture your ache in the face of beauty, your instinct that cruelty is *wrong* in some way that goes deeper than opinion, your sense when you're alone under a sky full of stars that you are small and held and *seen.* You didn't build any of that. It was split into you. It was *faṭara.*

And the honest question — the one the man puts to his people and Allah preserves for you — was never "prove that God is there." It's the quieter, harder one: **"What is going on with me, that I would look at all of this — and turn away?"**

That reframe won't win you an argument on the internet. But it will change how you pray tomorrow morning. Because you'll stop coming to the prayer mat as a defendant assembling evidence, and start coming as someone finally, simply, following the needle home.

### Theme Two: What You Carry Into the Garden

I want to tell you about the strangest thing a heart can do, and I'll start with the opposite of it, because the opposite is where most of us live.

Think of the last time someone truly wronged you. Not a small thing — a real one. Betrayal. Public humiliation. Someone who took something from you that you can't get back. And notice what your mind does with them, especially late at night. It rehearses. It builds the case. It imagines the day they finally understand what they did — the apology, the regret on their face, the moment they realize you were right all along. Psychologists will tell you this is one of the most persistent loops the human mind runs: the fantasy of vindication. We want to be *proven right* to the people who hurt us. We rehearse our own funeral sometimes, just to imagine them standing there, sorry.

There's a bitterness in it that we don't even notice because it's so normal. We don't want them saved. We want them *sorry.* We want to arrive in glory and have them see us in it, and burn a little.

Now. The man from the edge of the city has just been murdered by his own people. Not strangers — *his people, qawmī,* the ones he called out to. They fell on him and killed him for the crime of telling them to listen to the truth. If any human being ever earned the right to the vindication fantasy, it is this man. He gave them everything and they killed him for it.

And Allah opens the door of Paradise, and the man walks in, and out of everything he could feel in the first instant of eternal bliss — relief, vindication, the pure ecstasy of being right, of being *safe now, finally, away from them* — out of all of it, here is the first sentence that comes out of him:

*Yā layta qawmī yaʿlamūn.* **"Oh, if only my people knew."**

Layta. It's a particle of yearning — of a wish reaching for something almost out of reach. *Would that. If only.* And it's the emotional tell of the whole verse. He is not wishing for more. He is not wishing they suffer. In the first breath of Paradise, the deepest longing in this man's heart is a wish *for the people who killed him.* And look at what he wishes they knew — *bimā ghafara lī rabbī wa jaʿalanī mina-l-mukramīn* — "how my Lord has forgiven me, and placed me among the honored."

He doesn't wish they could see his *reward*, so they'd feel small. He wishes they could see his *forgiveness.* Because — and this is the thing that will take your breath — if they could just see how good God was to *him,* maybe they would run toward that same God too. **His murder did not stop his daʿwah. He is still calling them. From inside Paradise, with his killers still on the earth, he is still trying to get them in.**

The mob thought they killed a preacher. They didn't even slow him down.

[PAUSE]

Now hold this against the vindication loop we all run. The natural human ending to this story is a man in Paradise looking down with grim satisfaction: *see? I told you.* Instead, the Qur'an gives us a man in Paradise whose heart is still *turned toward the ones who wronged him* — not with a wish for their regret, but with a wish for their rescue. He wants them in the room with him. He is homesick, in Heaven, for the people who hated him.

Here is the architectural detail that seals it, and it took me a long time to see it. His speech begins — line one, the moment he arrives running — with the words *yā qawmi.* **"O my people."** And his speech ends — the very last thing he says, after death, from inside the Garden — with the words *yā layta qawmī.* **"If only my people."** His people are the first word out of his mouth and the last. Living and dead, on earth and in Paradise, the frame never changes. *Qawmī. My people. My people.* Death fell right in the middle of that sentence and did not break it. He was mid-thought about their salvation when they killed him, and he was still mid-thought about their salvation on the other side.

Let me land this where you actually live.

You are carrying someone right now. You know who it is. Someone who wronged you, and whom your heart has quietly sentenced. You've decided, somewhere below the level of words, that they don't get your good wishes anymore — that the account is closed, that when things finally go your way you'd like them to be around to notice. That loop feels like justice. The carpenter is showing you it's actually a weight, and you're the one carrying it into your own future.

Because here is what his story asks: **when you finally arrive — at peace, at success, at the Garden — who do you want to be, standing there?** The one whose first thought is *I hope they saw that*? Or the one whose first thought is *I wish they were here too*? One of those hearts is free. The other one dragged its enemies all the way to Paradise just to resent them at the door.

The practical thing — and I mean something you can do this week — is smaller than forgiveness and it opens the door to it: make one duʿāʾ, tonight, for the person you least want to. Not for their regret. For their guidance. For their rescue. You will feel your whole chest resist it, and that resistance is the exact weight the carpenter set down. Say it anyway. That single sentence is you beginning to travel light.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Design Underneath

Step back now and look at the whole shape, because there is an architecture here that proves this was built, not merely told.

Watch how the man's speech moves. He begins pointed *outward* — *follow the messengers, follow those who ask no reward.* He is talking about *them,* the guides. Then his speech turns *upward* — *the One who created me... the Most Merciful... should I take gods besides Him?* Now he's talking about *God.* Then it turns *inward* and becomes a confession — *indeed, I have believed.* Out, up, in. And then the veil falls, the voice says *Enter,* and on the far side his speech turns one final direction: *back.* Back toward his people. *If only my people knew.*

Outward to the guides. Upward to God. Inward to belief. And then — after death — back to the people he started with. It is a complete circle. He ends exactly where he began: with *qawmī,* his people. The first two words of his life's last speech and the last two words of it are the same word. And Allah placed his murder in the dead center of that circle and made it *disappear* — so that the circle closes cleanly, as if death were never there.

That is the proof that these eight lines are not a report. They are a composition. Allah built a man whose care for his people was so total that it formed a ring around his entire death, sealing it out.

### The Two Roads Were One Road

And now see that the linguistic journey and the two themes were never separate — they were the same insight approached from different sides.

The anonymous man *running from the edge* is the same truth as *"what is wrong with me that I would not worship?"* — because both are about a soul that stopped waiting for permission. He didn't wait to be qualified to defend the truth, and he didn't wait for the burden of proof to shift; he simply *moved*, because the needle in him pointed and he followed it. And the *silence where his death should be* is the same truth as *"if only my people knew"* — because a man whose life is one unbroken sentence of concern for others does not experience his own death as an interruption. There's nothing for death to interrupt. The sentence just continues on the other side.

We began standing in a sealed town, next to a Prophet ﷺ being told his people wouldn't listen. We end inside the Garden, next to a man who was killed by people who wouldn't listen — and who is *still calling them in.* That is the comfort Allah was giving His Prophet, and gives you: the caller's work is never wasted, even when the called refuse, even when they kill you. Especially then.

### Questions to Carry

- When you feel the pressure to justify your faith, whose burden have you accepted — and what would change if you asked instead, *what is wrong with me that I would turn away?*
- There is someone your heart has sentenced. When you picture your own arrival — your success, your peace, your Paradise — is your first thought *I hope they saw that,* or *I wish they were here*?
- The carpenter ran because everyone closer stayed still. In the situation you're avoiding right now, are you the center that has too much to lose — or are you free enough, from the edge, to move?
- What in your life have you been treating as the important part of the sentence — a wound, a loss, an ending — that from the far side of the veil might turn out to be only a comma?

### The One Sentence

**A life built entirely around calling others to God is a sentence that death lands in the middle of but cannot end.**

### The Invitation

You will not likely be asked to die in a town square. But you are asked, constantly, to move from the edge while the center stays comfortable — to say the true thing, to defend the one being maligned, to follow the needle instead of the crowd. The carpenter is not a saint you admire from a distance. He is deliberately left unnamed so that the seat stays open. Allah never told you who he was, so that you could not be sure it wasn't you.

### A Closing Duʿā

O Allah, You who split us into existence and set in us a compass that points to You — let us follow the needle before we are asked to defend it. Make our hearts so full of care for others that even our hardships become commas and not endings. And when we finally arrive, let our first wish be not that our enemies saw us honored, but that they were standing beside us — forgiven, and among the honored, too. *Āmīn.*
