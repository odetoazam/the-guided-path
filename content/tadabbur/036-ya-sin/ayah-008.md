# The Collar That Locks the Head Upward

## Surah Ya-Sin, Ayah 8

> إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا فِىٓ أَعْنَٰقِهِمْ أَغْلَٰلًا فَهِىَ إِلَى ٱلْأَذْقَانِ فَهُم مُّقْمَحُونَ
>
> *Innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan fa-hiya ilā l-adhqāni fa-hum muqmaḥūn.*
>
> **"Indeed, We have placed iron collars upon their necks, reaching up to the chins, so that their heads are forced aloft."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word of this ayah, I want you to do something with your body.

Lower your head. Just slightly. Tuck your chin toward your chest, the way you do when you're about to walk down a dark staircase and you're watching your feet. The way you do when someone older than you walks into the room. The way your whole body folds when you go down into sujood.

Now hold that for a moment, and feel what it is. To lower the head is to *see the ground you walk on*. To lower the head is to *defer*. To lower the head is the oldest gesture of submission there is — older than language. A dog lowers its head. A child lowers its head. Every human being who has ever been overwhelmed by something greater than himself has lowered his head.

[PAUSE]

Now I want you to imagine that you *cannot*.

Imagine an iron collar fastened around your neck, and your wrists chained up beneath your jaw, so that your hands are pinned against your own throat and your chin is jammed backward and upward. Your face is forced toward the ceiling. You cannot look down. You cannot see your own feet. You cannot watch where you're walking. You cannot bow. And worst of all — you cannot put your forehead on the ground even if, suddenly, with your whole heart, you wanted to.

That is the image Allah gives us in this ayah. And here is what I need you to understand before we go one step further: **this is not a description of Hell. Not primarily.** The classical commentators are nearly unanimous that the first and governing meaning of this verse is *tamthīl* — a parable, a portrait. Allah is drawing a picture of what is happening, right now, in the chest of a person who has made the decision to never lower his head to the truth.

The surah has just told us, one verse earlier, that "the word has come true upon most of them, so they do not believe." Why won't they believe? You'd expect the next verse to talk about their hearts, their arguments, their stubbornness. Instead Allah reaches for the body. He reaches for *the neck*. He reaches for an iron collar and a head locked toward the sky.

And the question that should be burning in you right now is this: **Why would Allah describe a person's refusal to believe as a collar that He Himself placed on their neck? Whose choice is this — theirs, or His? And of all the images of bondage available — chains on the feet, a cage, a wall — why specifically the one that forces the head *up* and makes it impossible to *bow*?**

Stay with me. Because the answer is going to take apart something you think you understand about pride, about the closing of a mind, and about the single act of worship this verse is secretly, devastatingly, built around.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: جَعَلْنَا — "*We* Placed It"

Let me start with the very first thing Allah says about the collar, because it is the most uncomfortable.

He does not say "they put collars on their own necks." He does not say "collars fell upon their necks." He says: **إِنَّا جَعَلْنَا** — *Innā jaʿalnā* — "**Indeed, We placed.**"

The "We" is divine. The placing is divine. The collar, grammatically, comes from the hand of God.

Before we even feel the weight of that, let me teach you something about this verb, because Arabic gives us two different words for "to make," and the choice between them is never accidental.

There is **خَلَقَ** — *khalaqa* — which means to create something from nothing, to bring a thing into existence where there was no thing.

And there is **جَعَلَ** — *jaʿala* — the verb used here. *Jaʿala* does not usually mean to create out of nothing. It means to *make into*, to *assign a state*, to *render something as something*. When Allah says "*jaʿala* the night a covering" or "*jaʿala* for you the earth as a bed," He is not creating raw material — He is *appointing a function*, turning a thing toward a purpose. *Jaʿala* is the verb of arrangement, of consequence, of one thing being set into relation with another.

Notice what that does here. Allah does not say He *created* disbelievers with collars built in, manufactured for damnation. He says He *jaʿala* — He **rendered their state** as a state of collars. He set their condition into this shape. This is the language of *consequence following a cause*, not the language of arbitrary manufacture.

[PAUSE]

But that still leaves the hard question standing, doesn't it? Even if it's "rendered into" rather than "created as" — it is still *Allah* who renders it. *We* placed. So is their disbelief their fault, or His doing?

Here is where the commentators do something I want you to slow down for. Al-Jalālayn, having walked through the entire physical image, stops and says one word about all of it: **wa hādhā tamthīl** — "*and this is a parable*." And then he tells you what the parable is *for*: *wal-murādu annahum lā yudhʿinūna lil-īmān* — "the intended meaning is that **they do not yield to faith**, they do not lower their heads to it."

Do you see what just happened? The collar is not a literal shackle God welds onto an innocent neck. The collar **is** their own refusal — but described from the angle of its truth. And the truth is this: a refusal repeated long enough stops being a choice you are making and becomes a condition you are living inside.

Think about how this actually works in a human life. The first time you reject something true, it is an act of will. You feel the resistance. You know you're turning away. The hundredth time, you don't feel anything at all — the turning-away has become *who you are*. The muscle of refusal has set like concrete. By the end, you could not accept the truth even if you tried, because the very faculty that would have accepted it has been collared shut.

And from the *inside*, it does not feel like a collar you forged. It feels like *just how things are*. It feels like fate. It feels — and this is the terrifying precision of the verse — like something that was *placed on you*.

So when Allah says "**We** placed the collar," He is telling you the deepest truth about the human heart: **your sustained choices become your conditions, and your conditions are held in the hand of God.** You chose every link of the chain. And the chain, now complete, is in His grip. Both things are true. The verse refuses to let you escape into either pure free will or pure fate. It holds you in the place where they meet.

That is the first revelation, and we have not even reached the collar yet. One word — *jaʿalnā* — and the whole question of who is responsible for a hardened heart is laid open. [PAUSE]

## Element Two: فَهِىَ — The Hands Allah Does Not Name

Now I want to do something unusual. I want to show you a word that is *missing* — because in this ayah, what Allah leaves out is as deliberate as anything He puts in.

Read the verse again slowly: *Innā jaʿalnā fī aʿnāqihim aghlālan* — "We placed on their necks collars" — *fa-hiya ilā l-adhqān* — "**so they** [are/reach] up to the chins."

Stop on that word: **فَهِىَ** — *fa-hiya* — "so **it/she/they**." It is a feminine singular pronoun. *So it... up to the chins.*

So *what* is up to the chins?

Here is the puzzle the grammarians wrestled with, and it's a beautiful one. A collar is around the *neck*. But the verse says something is pushed up *to the chin* — *ilā l-adhqān*. A collar on the neck doesn't reach the chin. So what is being forced up against the jaw?

Al-Jalālayn answers it: *fa-hiya — ay al-aydī majmūʿatun ilā l-adhqān* — "so **the hands** — gathered up to the chins." Because, he explains, the *ghull*, the iron collar, was a device that **bound the hand to the neck**. It was a shackle that pinned the wrists up beneath the jaw. The pronoun *fa-hiya*, feminine, refers to **the hands** — *al-aydī* — which in Arabic is grammatically feminine.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the strangeness of this. **The word "hands" is not in the verse.**

Allah describes a contraption whose entire function is to pin the hands against the throat — and He *omits the hands*. He gives you the collar. He gives you the neck. He gives you the chin. He gives you a pronoun, *fa-hiya*, "so it" — and He makes you, the reader, supply the hands yourself. You have to reach into the image and find the wrists that aren't named, pinned where you can't see them.

Why would the verse do that?

Let me offer you what this invites us to consider. When a thing is so tightly bound that it has become *part of* the neck — when the hand is fused to the throat, immobilized, no longer a separate working limb — it stops being mentioned as a hand at all. It has lost its hand-ness. It can no longer *do* anything. A hand is the instrument of action, of reaching, of giving, of building. The collared hand is a hand that has ceased to function as a hand. So the verse does not honor it with its name. It dissolves into "it" — *fa-hiya* — a thing pressed against a chin.

There is a chilling logic to that absence. The disbeliever in this image has not only lost the ability to *bow*; he has lost the ability to *act*. His hands — the part of him that could have reached for the truth, that could have lifted itself in du'ā, that could have given charity, that could have built something — his hands are gone. Welded to his own throat. Unnamed. The verse buries them in a pronoun, the way his obstinacy has buried his own capacity to do good.

**Read the absence as carefully as the presence.** Allah did not forget to mention the hands. He withheld them, the way the collar withheld them from their owner. The grammar enacts the bondage. The missing word *is* the meaning. [PAUSE]

## Element Three: مُّقْمَحُونَ — The Camel That Will Not Drink

And now we arrive at the word the entire ayah has been building toward. The last word. The one that, once you understand it, you will never forget.

**فَهُم مُّقْمَحُونَ** — *fa-hum muqmaḥūn* — "so they are *muqmaḥūn*."

I have left this word in Arabic on purpose, because there is no English word for it. Translators write "with heads raised aloft," "forced up," "heads held high" — and all of them are reaching for something the English language simply does not have a single word to hold.

Let me first just let you *hear* it. *Muq-ma-ḥūn.* Say it. Feel where it lives in your mouth. The **qāf** — that deep, throated stop struck from the very back of the throat. The **ḥā** — that hard, breathy constriction, a sound made by squeezing the throat almost shut. This is not a soft word. It is a word you have to *force* out past a tightened throat. The word about a collared throat is *itself* phonetically choked. The sound of the word **is** the sensation of the thing. You cannot say *muqmaḥūn* with a relaxed neck.

[PAUSE]

Now — where does this word come from? Here is where it becomes unforgettable.

This word appears **nowhere else in the entire Quran.** Allah used it once, here, and never again. And to understand why, we have to go to the classical Arabic lexicons — to Lisān al-ʿArab, to al-Rāghib's Mufradāt — and ask: where did the Arabs use the root *q-m-ḥ*?

They used it for **a camel**.

Specifically, the *qāmiḥ* is the camel that comes to the water — thirsty, having walked the desert, the water right there in front of it — and then **lifts its head away from the water and refuses to drink.** It raises its muzzle to the sky and will not lower it to the trough. The water is there. The thirst is there. And the animal locks its head upward and turns away.

[PAUSE — let this land]

Do you feel what Allah has just done?

He could have described a man in chains. Instead He reached across the desert and pulled out the image of **the animal that dies of thirst beside the water it refuses to drink.** Not because the water is poisoned. Not because the water is far. Because something in the creature has set its head upward and *will not bring it down to drink*.

The truth is the water. The Quran is the water. Faith is the water. It is right there — *the word has come true upon most of them* — the message has reached them, the warning has landed, the proof is in front of their faces. And they are dying of a thirst they will not slake, beside a spring they will not bend to, because their heads are locked toward the sky.

**That is *muqmaḥūn*.** Not someone deprived of the truth. Someone *forced into the very posture of refusing it* — head up, gaze averted, neck rigid — by a refusal that has become a collar. Al-Muyassar puts it plainly: *fa-uḍṭurrū ilā rafʿi ru'ūsihim* — "they are *compelled* to raise their heads" — *fa-hum maghlūlūna ʿan kulli khayr, lā yubṣirūna l-ḥaqqa wa lā yahtadūna ilayh* — "shackled away from every good, they do not see the truth and are not guided to it."

[PAUSE]

And now let me show you the function of that strange verb form, because the grammar seals it. *Muqmaḥ* is a **passive participle of the fourth verb-form** — *aqmaḥa*. The fourth form here does not simply mean "to raise"; it carries the sense of a state *induced upon* the subject, a posture *brought about*. And the participle is **passive** — *muqmaḥ*, "one upon whom this has been done." So the word is not "they who raise their heads in defiance." It is **"they whose heads have been forced up and held there."**

Sit with that paradox. The arrogant man *thinks* he is holding his own head high. He thinks the upward tilt of his chin is his pride, his strength, his refusal to bow to anyone. The grammar tells you the opposite. *Muqmaḥūn* — passive — **he is not holding his head up; his head is being held up.** He is not the one doing the lifting. He is the one to whom the lifting is *being done*. His pride is not his crown. It is his collar. The very posture he mistakes for mastery is the posture of the bound. He believes he is too tall to bow. The truth is he is *clamped* so he cannot.

One word. *Muqmaḥūn.* The camel beside the water. The throat you cannot relax. The head you mistake for proud that is really just stuck. And it appears only once in the whole Book, because it had only one job, and it does it completely. [PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Jaʿalnā* — your sustained refusal becomes a condition, and the condition rests in the hand of God.

*Fa-hiya* — the hands that could have acted, reached, given, lifted — bound and unnamed, dissolved into a pronoun.

*Muqmaḥūn* — the head locked upward, mistaking its bondage for its pride, dying of thirst beside the water.

Three words. One movement: a human being slowly losing the ability to *lower himself*, until the inability becomes invisible to him and feels like dignity.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what is happening inside a mind that closes, and about the one posture this entire verse is secretly built to make impossible.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Prison You Build and Then Cannot See

I want to start somewhere very ordinary. Not in the desert. In a conversation you have probably had.

You're talking with someone — maybe about politics, maybe about religion, maybe about something that happened between you — and you realize, somewhere in the middle of it, that *nothing you say is going to change their mind.* Not because they've out-argued you. Not because they have better evidence. But because you can feel that there is no longer a door. Every piece of evidence you offer gets reinterpreted on its way in. Every fact that should trouble them gets absorbed and neutralized. They are not processing what you say; they are *defending against* it. And the strange thing is — they feel like the reasonable one. From inside their head, *you* are the one being stubborn.

Psychologists have names for this. *Confirmation bias* — the mind's tendency to accept what confirms what it already believes and to scrutinize-to-death anything that doesn't. *Motivated reasoning* — when the conclusion comes first and the "reasons" are assembled afterward, like a lawyer building a case for a verdict already decided. *Belief perseverance* — the finding that beliefs often *survive* the total destruction of the evidence that created them.

Here is the part that matters for us. None of these mental states feel, from the inside, like bias. They feel like *clarity*. They feel like *I have simply thought about this carefully and arrived at the truth.* The closed mind does not experience itself as closed. It experiences itself as **finally settled.**

[PAUSE]

That is the collar. That is *exactly* the collar.

Because watch the sequence the verse gives you. First, the choice — repeated, deliberate, the turning-away that you can feel in your body the first few times. Then, *jaʿalnā* — the turning-away hardens into a condition, set like concrete, no longer felt. And finally, *muqmaḥūn* — you are now locked in the posture of refusal, and **you experience that posture as your own freedom.** The head held high. The certainty. The settledness. The dying man, beside the water, who is *sure* he isn't thirsty.

The most frightening thing about this verse is not that the collar exists. It is that **the collared person cannot see the collar.** It has become part of his neck. *Fa-hiya ilā l-adhqān* — fused to him, so close to his own jaw that he no longer perceives it as separate from himself. He thinks the rigidity of his neck is his backbone. He has confused his prison with his spine.

And now ask yourself the question this is really asking. Not "do I know someone like that?" — that question is itself a small collar, the one that lets you keep your own head up while you examine everyone else's neck. The real question is: **Where in my own life have I stopped being able to lower my head? What is the belief, the grudge, the version of myself, the conclusion about another person, that I have defended so many times that I can no longer feel myself defending it? Where have I mistaken a collar for a spine?**

Because the believer is not the person who has no collars. The believer is the person who is *terrified of them* — who keeps checking his own neck. There is a reason the righteous before us wept over their hearts and feared hypocrisy in themselves while assuming the best of others. They understood that the collar goes on link by link, unfelt, and that by the time it is heavy enough to notice, it may be welded shut.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the specific practice. The next time you feel that hot, fast certainty rise up — that *I already know, I don't need to hear this, this person is obviously wrong* — treat that feeling not as a signal of your clarity but as a **possible click of the collar.** Pause. Lower your head, literally if you have to. Ask: *what if the thing I am so sure I don't need to hear is exactly the water I am refusing to drink?* You will not be right every time. But you will have kept your neck free enough to bend. And a neck that can still bend is a neck without a collar. [PAUSE]

## Theme Two: The Anti-Sujood

Now I want to take you to the heart of this verse — to the thing I believe Allah built the entire image around, and that you cannot un-see once you've seen it.

Let me come at it from your own body again.

There is one act in your life where you take the highest, most honored, most *you* part of your body — your face, the seat of your identity, the thing you present to the world — and you put it on the ground. Lower than your heart. Lower than your feet were a moment ago. On the floor. In the dust. Five times a day, you collapse the distance between the crown of your dignity and the earth people walk on, and you hold it there, and in that exact position — the lowest a human body goes — you are, the Prophet ﷺ told us, *closest to your Lord.*

Sujood. The lowering of the head to the ground.

Now go back to the verse and look at what the collar *specifically* prevents. Not walking — the feet are free. Not speaking — the mouth is free. Not seeing the sky — the eyes are aimed right at it. The collar pins the hands to the throat and forces the chin **up**, locks the head **back**, and makes one thing, and essentially one thing, physically impossible:

**You cannot lower your head. You cannot bow. You cannot put your forehead on the ground.**

[PAUSE — let this fully land]

This verse is a portrait of a body that has been engineered for exactly one incapacity: **the incapacity to make sujood.** *Muqmaḥūn* is the precise anatomical opposite of *sājid*. The sājid has his head at the lowest point. The muqmaḥ has his head locked at the highest. The sājid's hands are spread open on the earth. The muqmaḥ's hands are bound shut against his throat. The sājid's neck is bent in surrender. The muqmaḥ's neck is collared rigid. Every single feature of the disbeliever's bondage in this ayah is the photographic negative of a person in prostration.

Allah is not just telling you these people won't believe. He is telling you what disbelief *is*, in its essence, in the body: **it is the anti-sujood.** It is the posture of the head that will not come down.

And now the lexical image we built earlier detonates. Remember *kibr* — arrogance — and how the scholars defined it through the Prophet's own words: *kibr* is *baṭaru l-ḥaqqi wa ghamṭu n-nās* — the rejection of truth and the contempt of people. Where does the word for pride and arrogance live in Arabic? In images of the **head and the neck**. To "stretch the neck" is to be haughty. Allah commands in another surah: *wa lā tuṣaʿʿir khaddaka lin-nās* — "do not turn your cheek away from people in arrogance." The proud man is, by definition, the man whose head will not come down. He has, in his very bearing, already put on the collar of *muqmaḥūn* — he just hasn't felt it lock yet.

[PAUSE]

So here is the whole architecture of the human heart in two postures.

There is the head that comes down — *sujood* — and Allah says of it that it is the place of nearness, *wasjud waqtarib*, "prostrate and draw near." The lowest body, the closest soul.

And there is the head that will not come down — *muqmaḥūn* — and Allah says of it that it is the place of the collar, the barrier, the thirst beside the water, the not-seeing, the not-being-guided.

The entire spiritual life of a human being turns on this one hinge: **can your head still come down?** Not your knees. Not your feet. Your *head* — the proud part, the part that says "I," the part that wants to be the one who knows. Can it still go to the floor?

Iblīs — the very first refuser — was commanded to lower himself, *usjud*, prostrate to Adam. And he locked his head and would not. He gave a *reason* — "I am better than him, You made me from fire" — the perfect motivated reasoning, the conclusion first and the argument after. And in that single locked neck, the entire posture of *muqmaḥūn* was born. The Quran's whole drama, from the first prostration refused to the last, runs along this one line: the head that bows and the head that won't.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow — not abstractly, but tonight?

It means that your sujood is not a ritual you perform. **It is the act of repeatedly, daily, deliberately doing the one thing the collar exists to prevent.** Every time you go down, you are testing your own neck. You are proving to yourself that the collar has not yet welded shut, that your head still comes down, that you are not *muqmaḥūn*. The disbeliever in this verse would give anything, on the Day it all becomes clear, to do what you can do for free, right now, on your own floor: lower his head to the ground.

So the practice is almost embarrassingly simple, and it is the most important thing in this entire reflection. **The next sujood you make — make it the un-collaring.** Go down slower than usual. Feel the head leaving its high place. Feel the neck bending that the proud cannot bend. And as your forehead touches, understand what you are saying with your body: *My head still comes down. I am not locked. I drink the water. Whatever collars are forming on me — I break them here, with this, every day.*

A head that can still touch the ground has no collar on it. That is the whole secret of the verse, read from the other side. [PAUSE — major pause]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: A Body Bound, Then a World Sealed

Now that we have done the work, let me show you something about where this verse sits, because Allah did not place it alone.

Read the three verses as one motion:

**36:7** — "The word has come true upon most of them, *so they do not believe.*"

**36:8** — "We placed collars on their necks, up to the chins, so their heads are forced aloft."

**36:9** — "And We placed before them a barrier and behind them a barrier and covered them over, *so they do not see.*"

**36:10** — "It is the same for them whether you warn them or not — *they will not believe.*"

Do you see the ring? Verse 7 and verse 10 are the same sentence — *they do not believe* — wrapped around the outside like two clasps. And inside them, two images that mirror each other perfectly. Verse 8 binds **the body**: the neck, the hands, the head locked up so it cannot bow. Verse 9 seals **the vision**: a wall in front, a wall behind, a covering over, so it cannot see. One verse takes away the power to *submit*. The next takes away the power to *perceive*. Together they describe a human being immobilized in every direction — cannot bow, cannot look down, cannot see forward, cannot see back, cannot see at all.

And both inner verses are introduced by the same divine hand: *innā jaʿalnā* / *wa jaʿalnā* — "We placed." The same hand that we wrestled with at the very beginning. The collar and the wall come from the same place. And both are the truth of a refusal that began as a choice.

This is not a verse about cruelty. It is a verse about *consequence so complete that it looks like architecture.* You build the collar link by link with your own refusals, and at the end you find yourself inside a structure so total — body bound, sight sealed, front and back walled in — that it could only be described as something *placed* around you by the Lord of all causes.

---

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same insight, approached from two sides.

The words showed us a state: *jaʿalnā*, a condition rendered by sustained choice and held in God's hand; *fa-hiya*, the hands of action dissolved into a pronoun; *muqmaḥūn*, the head locked upward, the camel refusing the water, the pride that is really paralysis.

The themes showed us a life: the mind that closes so slowly it mistakes its prison for its spine, and the body engineered for one incapacity — the incapacity to bow.

And they meet on a single hinge. **Can your head still come down?** The collar is whatever in you refuses to let it. The sujood is the proof, renewed five times a day, that it still can.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I defended a position so many times that I can no longer feel myself defending it — where have I mistaken a collar for a spine?

2. When I feel that fast, hot certainty that I *already know* and don't need to listen — is that the sound of my clarity, or the click of a collar?

3. Is there water in front of me right now — a truth about myself, a person I've written off, a change I've refused — that I am dying of thirst beside, with my head locked away from it?

4. When I make sujood, am I performing a ritual, or am I daily proving to myself that my head still comes down?

5. Who in my life feels, from the inside, exactly the way the people in this verse feel from the inside — *settled, certain, free* — and how does that warn me about my own certainties?

## One-Sentence Distillation

A refusal repeated long enough hardens into a collar that locks the head toward the sky — unable to bow, unable to drink, mistaking its own bondage for pride — and the single act this posture forbids, lowering the head to the ground, is the very act the believer is built to perform every day.

---

## The Closing Invitation

I am not going to ask you to fear this verse, though it is fearful. I am going to ask you to use it.

You have a neck that still bends. You have hands that are not yet bound to your throat. You have a head that can still go all the way to the floor. The people in this ayah would trade everything for what you can do tonight without paying anything at all.

So do not let your next sujood be ordinary. Let it be the thing it secretly always was: the breaking of every collar before it sets. Go down slowly. Feel the proud part of you — the *I*, the one who knows, the one who is right — leave its high place and come to rest on the ground. And know, in your body, that you are free.

A head that still touches the earth wears no collar. Keep it free.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who place collars and You who lift them — do not let our refusals harden into a bondage we can no longer feel. Keep our necks soft enough to bow, our hands free enough to reach for You, and our heads always able to find the ground. Let us drink the water You have set before us, and never be among those who die of thirst beside it with their heads locked toward the sky. Make our sujood the breaking of every collar, until the day we meet You with our foreheads, and not our pride.*

*Āmīn.*
