# The Piety That Withholds

## Surah Ya-Sin, Ayah 47

> وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ أَنفِقُوا۟ مِمَّا رَزَقَكُمُ ٱللَّهُ قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ أَنُطْعِمُ مَن لَّوْ يَشَآءُ ٱللَّهُ أَطْعَمَهُۥٓ إِنْ أَنتُمْ إِلَّا فِى ضَلَـٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ

*Wa idhā qīla lahum anfiqū mimmā razaqakumu-llāhu qāla-lladhīna kafarū lilladhīna āmanū anuṭʿimu man law yashā'u-llāhu aṭʿamahu, in antum illā fī ḍalālin mubīn.*

> "And when it is said to them, 'Spend from that which Allah has provided you,' those who disbelieve say to those who believe, 'Should we feed one whom, if Allah had willed, He would have fed? You are in nothing but clear error.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

I want you to begin not with the verse, but with a feeling you already know.

You are standing in a marketplace. Around you, the wealth of a city moves — caravans, coin, grain measured into sacks. And at the edges, where the noise thins out, there are people with nothing. A widow. An orphan. A man whose camel died and took his livelihood with it. Everyone can see them. Nobody can not see them.

Now imagine someone — a believer, someone whose heart has been softened by this new message coming out of the mouth of the Prophet ﷺ — turns to one of the wealthy men of the city and says four words: *Spend. From what God gave you.*

And the wealthy man does not say, "No, I'm keeping it."

He says something far more sophisticated than that.

He says: *Why would I feed someone that God Himself chose not to feed? If God wanted that man to have food, He'd have food. His poverty is God's decision. Who am I to overrule it? In fact —* and here he turns the knife — *you're the one who's confused. You're the one who doesn't understand how reality works.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with that man for a moment. Because here is what is so unsettling about him: **he is not arguing against God. He is arguing with God's name in his mouth.** He has taken the deepest truth in existence — that nothing happens except by the will of Allah — and he has turned it into a wall. A wall between himself and the hungry. A wall built, brick by brick, out of theology.

This is the moment Ya-Sin freezes for us in verse 47.

This surah has spent its middle section walking us through the signs of God's provision — the dead earth revived (36:33), the gardens of date palms and grapes (36:34), the fruit "their hands did not make" (36:35), the night from which the day is "stripped" (36:37), the sun running to its resting place (36:38). Sign after sign after sign, all saying the same thing: *Everything you have, you were given.* And then, right after this overwhelming demonstration that we are receivers and not originators, the surah turns to a person who has heard all of it — and who uses the Giver's own decree as his excuse to give nothing.

The classical commentators tell us this scene had a real address. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī report that the poor companions of the Prophet ﷺ would say to the wealthy of Makkah: *the wealth you yourselves claimed belonged to God — give some of it to us.* And the answer came back, dripping with mockery: *If God wanted to feed them, He would. We won't go against His will.* (Note the *ikhtilāf* here — al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, as al-Qurṭubī records, read the address as concerning a group among the People of the Book commanded to feed the poor; the dominant reading ties it to the mushrikūn of Makkah. The two readings illuminate the same disease from different angles.)

Here is the hook. Here is the thing I need you to be hungry for by the end of this hour:

**The most dangerous refusal is not the one that denies God. It is the one that uses Him.** And the verse is about to show us — in its grammar, in a single word it places before their excuse, in the very verbs it chooses — exactly how that trick is performed, and exactly why it collapses.

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Voice With No Name

Let me teach you something about how language assigns responsibility before we touch the verse.

In every language, there is a difference between *"It was said that the meeting is cancelled"* and *"Marcus said the meeting is cancelled."* In the first, the statement floats free — true or not, it belongs to no one in particular; it simply *is in the air.* In the second, the words have an owner. If they're wrong, Marcus is wrong. We attach a face to them. Arabic does this with extraordinary precision through what's called the **passive voice** — *al-fiʿl al-majhūl*, literally "the verb whose doer is unknown," or more accurately, *unnamed.*

Now look at how the verse opens.

> وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ
> *Wa idhā qīla lahum* — "And when it is **said** to them..."

The word is **قِيلَ — *qīla*.** It is the passive of *qāla*, "he said." It means *it is said.* And notice — Allah does not tell us who said it.

Who is the one urging them to spend? A believer? An angel of conscience? The voice of the poor themselves? The fitra, the innate moral sense that every human is born with, that knows the hungry should be fed? Allah leaves it open. The command to give has **no named speaker.**

Let's sit with this, because it is easy to rush past.

Now watch what happens in the very next breath:

> قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟
> *qāla-lladhīna kafarū* — "**said** those who disbelieved..."

Here the verb is active — **قَالَ, *qāla*** — and it has a subject, fully named, fully exposed: *alladhīna kafarū*, "those who disbelieved." The refusal is given a face. The refusal is given an owner.

Notice something. **The call to generosity is left anonymous; the refusal is signed.**

Here's what's remarkable about that asymmetry. When good is spoken, it doesn't need a celebrity to carry it. It rises from everywhere and nowhere — from any throat that happens to be near the truth. Its authority doesn't come from *who* said it; it comes from the fact that it is *right.* The verse mirrors this in its grammar: the good word needs no attribution because it belongs to the order of things. *Of course* you should feed the hungry. It is so obvious it can be said by an unnamed voice and lose nothing.

But the refusal — the refusal has to be claimed. Because a refusal of the obvious is not the order of things; it is a *deviation* from it. And a deviation has an author. Somebody chose this. The grammar will not let them hide. *Qāla-lladhīna kafarū.* This is yours. You said it. Your name is on it.

[PAUSE]

There is a deeper layer still. When the speaker of "spend" is hidden, the verse strips away the disbelievers' easiest escape route. They cannot say *"I refused because I didn't like the man who asked"* — there is no man to dislike. They cannot make it personal, tribal, a matter of who has standing to make demands. The instruction arrives unattributed, which means it arrives **as pure truth, unmixed with the messenger.** And they reject it anyway. They are not rejecting a person. They are rejecting the thing itself.

What this single passive verb reveals: **goodness is the default voice of reality — it speaks from everywhere — and evil is the interruption that has to put its name on the record.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Command That Already Contains the Answer

Now I want to show you something about the *content* of the unnamed call, because the verse does something almost merciful: it defeats the disbelievers' argument *before they even make it.*

The instruction is:

> أَنفِقُوا۟ مِمَّا رَزَقَكُمُ ٱللَّهُ
> *anfiqū mimmā razaqakumu-llāh* — "Spend from that which **Allah has provided you.**"

Two words to teach first.

**أَنفِقُوا۟ — *anfiqū*.** This is a command — an imperative — from the root *n-f-q*. Lexically, the root carries the sense of something *going out, being expended, a tunnel through which things pass* (the same root gives us *nafaq*, a tunnel, and *nifāq*, hypocrisy — a thing with two openings). *Infāq* is the broad word for spending in God's cause — any outflow of wealth toward good. It is wide. It is generous in its very scope. *Spend.* Let it flow out.

And then: **رَزَقَكُمُ ٱللَّهُ — *razaqakumu-llāh*** — "Allah provided you." The root *r-z-q* is provision: every good thing that reaches a creature from outside itself. Food, wealth, health, knowledge — *rizq* is what is *delivered* to you. By definition, *rizq* is something **you did not generate.** It came *to* you. From Someone.

Now hold those two together and feel the trap close.

The command does not say "spend your money." It says "spend **from what Allah provided you.**" Built into the instruction is a complete theology of wealth: *what you are holding was given to you by Allah.* You are not the source. You are a station along the way — a *nafaq*, a tunnel — through which His provision is meant to keep moving.

And here is the stunning thing. **The disbelievers are about to build their entire excuse on the premise that God is the one who distributes provision** — "if God wanted to feed him, He would have." They are about to say: *poverty and wealth are God's decision.*

But the verse already said that. *Razaqakumu-llāh.* God provided **you.**

So if their principle is true — if a person's having or not having is simply God's decree, not to be interfered with — then by their own logic, **their own wealth is not theirs to claim either.** It was decreed *to* them exactly as the poor man's poverty was decreed *to* him. They want to use "it's God's distribution" as a reason to keep. But "it's God's distribution" is precisely the reason they cannot keep — because what was distributed to them was distributed *as a trust to pass on,* "spend from what He gave you," not as a possession to defend.

Do you see it? **They reach for divine decree as a sword to cut off the poor, and the verse has already turned that same sword to point at their own chests.** The phrase *razaqakumu-llāh* is not decoration. It is the refutation, planted in the soil of the command before the weed of their excuse ever sprouts.

What this construction reveals: **the moment you admit your wealth is *rizq* — given, not generated — you have already lost the argument for hoarding it.** You cannot say "it's all God's will" and then act as though it's all yours.

### Element Three: The Verb That Shrinks, and the Conditional That Hides

This is the heart of the verse's machinery. Watch the disbelievers' two moves — one in their verb, one in their grammar — and you will see the entire anatomy of a pious-sounding refusal.

**Move one: they shrink the word.**

They were told *anfiqū* — *spend.* Wide, open, generous. Wealth flowing outward in any form.

Listen to what they say back:

> أَنُطْعِمُ ... أَطْعَمَهُۥٓ
> *anuṭʿimu... aṭʿamahu* — "Should **we feed**... He would have **fed** him."

The root has changed. It is now *ṭ-ʿ-m* — **feeding, the giving of food**, *ṭaʿām.* From the broad ocean of *infāq* — spend in any way, on any good — they have narrowed the demand down to its smallest, most literal, most begrudging shape: *putting food in a mouth.*

This is what a resentful heart does to a generous request. It shrinks it. You are asked to *be generous,* and you hear *give away everything.* You are asked to *spend in God's cause,* and you negotiate it down to *the absolute minimum morsel.* They take the wide command and squeeze it until it is just a question about feeding — and then they refuse even that. Notice the contempt folded into the shrinking: by reducing *infāq* to *iṭʿām*, they make the request sound smaller and more absurd, easier to sneer at. *You want me to literally spoon-feed this person?*

And listen to the **sound** of it — *anuṭʿimu man law yashā'u-llāhu aṭʿamah* — that repeated cluster, *ṭuʿ... ṭa...*, the hard *ṭ* punching against the guttural *ʿayn*, three times in a single breath. It has a sneer built into its very phonetics, a kind of chewing, dismissive texture. The verse lets us *hear* the contempt before we even finish parsing the meaning.

[PAUSE]

**Move two — and this is the masterstroke of self-deception — they hide inside a conditional.**

> مَن لَّوْ يَشَآءُ ٱللَّهُ أَطْعَمَهُۥٓ
> *man law yashā'u-llāhu aṭʿamah* — "one whom, **if Allah had willed, He would have fed.**"

Let me teach you this structure, because it is everything. **لَوْ — *law*** is the Arabic particle for a *counterfactual* condition — an "if" about something that did *not* happen. *Law* means: "if (this had been the case, which it wasn't)." When you say *law shā'a* — "if He had willed" — you are stating, by the very grammar, that **He did not will it.** The structure presupposes the non-occurrence.

So their sentence smuggles in a theological claim disguised as humility: *God did not will to feed this man. His hunger is the visible proof of God's choice. Therefore feeding him would be working against God.*

Do you feel how seductive this is? It *sounds* like submission. It *sounds* like *tawḥīd* — pure reliance on God's will. They are not saying "we don't care about the poor." They are saying "we care so much about God's sovereignty that we dare not interfere with His distribution." They have wrapped the ugliest selfishness in the most beautiful doctrine.

And here is the lie at the center of it — stay with me, because this is the whole thing:

**They have confused God's *cosmic* will with God's *moral* command.**

It is true — gloriously, absolutely true — that nothing exists except by Allah's will (*al-irāda al-kawniyya*, the will by which things *are*). The poor man's poverty is, in that sense, by God's decree. But it is *also* true that Allah has *commanded* the feeding of the poor (*al-irāda al-sharʿiyya*, the will by which we are *instructed*). God *decreed* the man's poverty **and** *commanded* you to relieve it — and there is no contradiction, because **the man's poverty is the very test through which your obedience is meant to happen.** His empty hand is not God saying "leave him empty." His empty hand is God saying *"here — I have placed in front of you the occasion of your own salvation. Fill it."*

The disbeliever looks at the hungry man and sees a verdict. The believer looks at the hungry man and sees an *invitation.* Same man. Same hunger. Same divine decree behind it. **One reads it as God's "no" to the poor; the other reads it as God's question to the rich.**

[PAUSE]

This exact move — using *law shā'a-llāh,* "if God had willed," to escape moral responsibility — is not unique to this verse. Allah catches it elsewhere in the mouths of the idolaters: *"Those who associated others with Allah will say, 'If Allah had willed, we would not have associated... '"* (6:148) — and Allah's response there is devastating: this is nothing but conjecture and lies they tell to excuse themselves. The pattern is ancient and it is alive today. **The will of God is the most honest truth in existence; in the mouth of a man avoiding his duty, it becomes the most dishonest excuse.**

What this construction reveals: **a counterfactual "if God had willed" can dress cruelty as theology — but the grammar that lets you say "He did not will to feed him" is the same grammar that confesses *you* are the one refusing to.** They said "if God willed, He would have fed him." They did not finish the sentence. The honest ending is: *"...and instead, He willed to test me with this man, and I failed."*

### Bridge

Look at what these three features have shown us, woven together.

The call to give arrives **unnamed** — because goodness is the baseline of reality and needs no sponsor. The call already contains the words **"from what Allah gave you"** — because the instant you admit your wealth is provision, hoarding loses its footing. And the refusal **shrinks the request** and then **hides inside a counterfactual about God's will** — because the most effective way to refuse a moral duty is to make the refusal sound like reverence.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the grammar of one ancient exchange — but about the architecture of the human heart, and about a temptation that sits inside *every* one of us when we are asked to let go of what we are holding.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Sentence That Ends "Everything Happens for a Reason"

Let me start where you actually live.

You are scrolling, or driving, or standing in a checkout line, and you pass someone who is clearly struggling. A man on a corner with a sign. A family you know is drowning in debt. A whole region on the news, flooded, starving. And somewhere in the back of your mind — quietly, almost reflexively — a sentence forms. You have heard it a thousand times. You may have said it yourself:

*"Everything happens for a reason."*

*"God has a plan."*

*"They must have made their choices."*

*"If they wanted it badly enough, they'd have it."*

Now — pay close attention — most of the time these sentences are *true,* or at least pointing at something true. Everything *does* happen by God's wisdom. But notice what they *do* to you in the moment you reach for them. Notice the direction they point your body. Psychologists have a name for the instinct underneath them: the **just-world fallacy** — the deep human need to believe that people get what they deserve, because the alternative (that suffering can be undeserved, random-seeming, *not the sufferer's fault*) is too threatening to sit with. If the poor deserve their poverty, then *I* deserve my comfort, and I can keep it with a clean conscience. The just-world belief is not really about *them.* It is a story I tell to protect *me.*

And this is precisely — *precisely* — the move in verse 47. *"Should we feed one whom, if God willed, He'd have fed?"* is the seventh-century, theologically-fluent ancestor of *"everything happens for a reason, so it's not my problem."* It is the just-world fallacy in the language of *tawḥīd.* The hunger of the poor man becomes, in the disbeliever's telling, evidence of a cosmic verdict he is too pious to overturn.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the verse exposes, and it should make every one of us go quiet.

**A true statement about God can become a sin in the way you deploy it.** "God is in control" is true. But the *use* you put it to — to close your hand, to silence your conscience, to convert another person's suffering into your permission to do nothing — that use is from the nafs, not from faith. The disbelievers did not say anything *false* about God's power. They said something true *for the wrong reason, to the wrong end.* And Allah recorded it not as theology but as the speech of *alladhīna kafarū.*

So the question the verse hands you is sharper than "do you believe in qadar?" Of course you do. The question is: **when you invoke God's will, which direction does it send you — toward the suffering, or away from it?**

There is a clean test, and it is this. God's decree, rightly understood, *always* increases your responsibility and *never* excuses it. When the will of God is true in your heart, you look at the poor man and think: *God could have made me him and him me. The only difference between us is a rizq I did not earn. He has been placed in front of me — by that same decree — as the door to my own mercy.* That is qadar making you *softer,* heavier with duty, quicker to move. **The moment "it's God's will" makes you lighter, more comfortable, slower to give — you have not understood the doctrine. You have weaponized it.** You have done exactly what verse 47 condemns, only in a more fluent dialect.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is concrete and it is small. The next time that reflexive sentence forms — *they must have done something, everything happens for a reason, God has a plan* — catch it. And ask it one question: *Is this thought moving my hand toward my wallet, or away from it?* Because the believer and the disbeliever in this verse looked at the **exact same hungry man** and saw the **exact same divine decree behind his hunger** — and one reached out and one pulled back. The decree did not decide which of them you are. **You do. Every time.**

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: You Are a Tunnel, Not a Tank

Now the deeper root underneath the whole exchange. Because beneath the question "will you feed him?" is a far older question: *what do you think your wealth actually is?*

Let me give you two pictures of a human being holding money.

The first: a **tank.** A reservoir. Things flow *in* — salary, profit, inheritance — and they *stay.* The whole purpose of the tank is accumulation; its success is measured by *level,* by how full it is, how much is held against how much escapes. Every outflow is, by definition, a loss. To give is to drain. A man who sees himself as a tank experiences charity as *bleeding.* No wonder he resents it. No wonder he negotiates the request down from *infāq* to a single *ṭaʿām* and then refuses even that. Of course he does. You are asking the tank to leak.

The second: a **tunnel.** Remember the root of *anfiqū* — *n-f-q,* the same root as *nafaq,* a tunnel, a passage with two openings. A tunnel's entire dignity is in what passes *through* it. It receives precisely so that it can deliver. A blocked tunnel is not a *rich* tunnel — it is a *broken* one, a dead end, a thing that has failed its only purpose. For a tunnel, holding is not wealth; holding is *malfunction.* And giving is not bleeding — giving is simply *being what it is for.*

[PAUSE]

The whole confrontation in verse 47 is, underneath everything, a collision between these two self-images.

The believer says *anfiqū* — and that word is built from the tunnel. *Let it pass through you.* He is not asking the wealthy man to wound himself. He is reminding him of his function. *You were filled so that you could pour. That's what the filling was for.*

And the word the believer uses for the source — *razaqakumu-llāh,* "Allah provided you" — settles the matter completely. *Rizq* is, by its very definition, **what arrives from outside.** You did not manufacture your provision; it was *delivered.* You are the destination of someone else's giving before you are ever the source of your own. The whole Quran will say this again and again: *"To Allah belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth"* (3:180); *"Give to them from the wealth of Allah which He has given you"* (24:33); *"Spend from that of which He has made you trustees / successors — mustakhlafīna fīh"* (57:7) — He has made you not *owners* of the wealth but *successors* over it, stewards who hold what was held by others before and will be held by others after. **You never owned it. You were the current custodian of something passing through.**

This is why the disbeliever's excuse is not just selfish — it is *metaphysically confused.* He treats his wealth as a tank he has the sovereign right to seal, while simultaneously claiming that all distribution belongs to God. He cannot have both. Either it's his — in which case stop hiding behind God's will — or it's God's — in which case open the tunnel, because the One whose wealth it is has *commanded* it onward.

[PAUSE]

And here is the mercy hidden inside the tunnel, the thing the tank-hearted man will never feel. **A tunnel cannot be impoverished by what flows through it.** It loses nothing; it was never the owner of the water. But a tank lives in terror — terror of the level dropping, terror of the leak, terror of the day it runs dry. The man who thinks he owns his rizq spends his life *defending* it, and that defense is its own punishment: anxiety dressed as security, a clenched fist that thinks it is strong. The Prophet ﷺ taught us that wealth does not decrease through charity — *mā naqaṣat ṣadaqatun min māl* — and now you can feel *why* that is not just a reward-mechanism but a *description of reality.* The tunnel was never going to be diminished by doing the one thing it exists to do.

What does this change about tomorrow? Before the next time you give — even something small — pause for the length of one breath and correct the picture in your chest. Not *"I am parting with mine,"* but *"I am passing on His."* Say it, internally: *mimmā razaqaniya-llāh* — from what Allah provided me. Watch how the entire emotional texture of the act changes when you stop being a tank that bleeds and remember you are a tunnel that *works.* The giving stops feeling like loss and starts feeling like *function* — like a held breath finally released.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back and see the whole verse as one motion now.

It opens with an **unnamed voice** — *qīla,* "it is said" — calling for generosity, because the call to give belongs to the baseline of reality and needs no sponsor; it is simply the truth, in the air. That call carries inside it the words **"from what Allah provided you,"** which quietly establishes the entire theology of stewardship — *you are a tunnel, not a tank; this was given, not generated* — and in doing so disarms the excuse before it is spoken.

Then comes the **named refusal** — *qāla-lladhīna kafarū,* signed, owned, exposed — and watch its two-step mechanism: first it **shrinks** the wide command *anfiqū* down to a grudging *anuṭʿimu,* and then it **hides** that refusal inside a counterfactual about God's will, *law yashā'u-llāhu aṭʿamah,* converting the poor man's hunger into a fake divine verdict.

And do you see now that the linguistic discoveries and the two themes are **the same insight arriving from two directions?**

The grammar showed us a refusal that disguises itself as reverence. The themes showed us *why* that disguise is so tempting — because "it's God's will" *flatters* the nafs (the just-world comfort) and because owning-rather-than-stewarding *feels* like security (the tank). The verse diagnoses, in twenty-four words, a single disease with a linguistic symptom and a spiritual cause: **the human heart's genius for using the highest truths to excuse the lowest behavior.** And it leaves them with their own verdict ringing — *"You are in nothing but clear error"* — the misguided accusing the guided of misguidance, the manifest error (*mubīn*) pointing, in the end, only at the one who spoke it.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I look at someone suffering and the thought *"everything happens for a reason"* arises — does that thought move my hand *toward* them or *away* from them? What does the direction tell me about the thought's true source?

2. Where in my life have I used a *true* statement about God — "it's His will," "He's in control," "He has a plan" — to quiet a conscience that was asking me to *act*?

3. Do I experience giving as a *tank bleeding* or a *tunnel working*? What would change in my chest if I genuinely believed I was the second?

4. The disbelievers narrowed *spend* down to *feed,* then refused even that. Where do I negotiate a generous call down to its smallest possible version before deciding it's still too much?

5. God decreed the poor man's poverty *and* commanded me to relieve it. Am I living as though His decree is a verdict I must not disturb — or an invitation He has placed in front of me?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you are asked to give from what God entrusted to you, the cruelest refusal is the one that dresses itself in piety — using God's will as a wall against the very poor whom God's will placed in front of you as your door.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not, today, deny the will of Allah. That is not your danger. Your danger — my danger — is subtler and far closer: that we will *honor* His will with our tongues while *betraying* His command with our hands, and feel righteous doing it. The invitation of this verse is not to believe in qadar more strongly. It is to let qadar do to you what it did to the believers in the verse and not what it did to the disbelievers — to let "this is from God" make you *heavier* with mercy, not lighter; to look at the next empty hand you pass and read it correctly: not as God's *no* to them, but as God's *question* to you. Let the truth that you own nothing become the reason you give freely, instead of the excuse you give nothing.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who provided us from where we did not earn and gave us what we did not make — do not let us turn the truth of Your will into a wall against Your servants. Make us tunnels for Your provision and not tanks that hoard it. And when the hungry are placed before us, let us see Your invitation where the heedless see only a verdict — and let us answer it before we are answered for it.*

*Āmīn.*
