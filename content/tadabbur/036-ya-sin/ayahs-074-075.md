# The Army of What Cannot Save You

**Ya-Sin 36:74–75**

> [ayah:36:74] وَٱتَّخَذُوا۟ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ ءَالِهَةً لَّعَلَّهُمْ يُنصَرُونَ

> [ayah:36:75] لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ نَصْرَهُمْ وَهُمْ لَهُمْ جُندٌ مُّحْضَرُونَ

*Wa-ttakhadhū min dūni-llāhi ālihatan la'allahum yunṣarūn.*
*Lā yastaṭī'ūna naṣrahum wa hum lahum jundun muḥḍarūn.*

> And they have taken, besides Allah, gods — hoping that they might be helped.
> They cannot help them, while they themselves are, for them, a host arraigned.

---

## INTRODUCTION

Before we touch a single word, sit for a moment inside where these two lines land.

Ya-Sin has been building a case. Go back three ayat and you'll find Allah walking the listener through an inventory of ordinary mercy. The dead earth — He revived it, brought out grain, and they ate from it (36:33). Gardens of date-palms and grapevines, springs made to gush (36:34). And then, closest of all, the animals: *"Do they not see that We created for them, from what Our hands have made, cattle — so that they are their owners?"* He tamed them, so some they ride and some they eat, and in them are benefits and drink (36:71–73). Notice the intimacy of that list. This is not thunder and mountains. This is the goat in the pen, the milk in the bowl, the camel that carries the family across the desert. This is the mercy you touch with your hands every single day and stop noticing by the age of six.

Now feel the world these words fell into. Seventh-century Makkah. A house — the Ka'bah — built by Ibrahim to declare that there is only One. And inside it and around it, by the time of the Prophet ﷺ, some three hundred and sixty idols. Stone and wood, some of them, and some just heaps that a traveller would mound up and worship and then abandon at the next town. These were not abstractions to the Makkan. They were his security system. Before a journey, you stroked the idol. Before a battle, you carried it out on a litter at the front of the army — Quraysh literally brought their gods to Badr and to Uhud, set up before the troops like a standard, so the men would fight harder knowing the god was watching, knowing the god was, they believed, *fighting with them.*

Hold that image. The god carried out to the battlefield. Because the whole logic of the idol was one word: **help.** *Nasr.* Victory. Rescue. "We keep these gods," the Makkan would tell you, "so that when we are cornered, we are not alone. So that someone answers."

And here is the thing you must feel before we go one step further. Allah does not, in these two lines, mock the idols first. He does something far more surgical. He first grants the Makkan his own stated reason — *"hoping that they might be helped"* — He lets the man speak his motive out loud. And then, in the very next breath, He turns that motive inside out until the man is standing in the exact opposite of where he thought he stood.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. These people reached for their gods **so that they would be helped.** By the end of two short lines, who is helping whom? Hold that. Because the answer is going to reverse everything — and it is going to reverse it not with a sermon, but with grammar.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Ittakhadhū*: the verb of self-manufacture

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we open the word, because once you have it, the first word of the ayah will never look flat again.

In Arabic, a verb can be built up in layers. The bare root gives you the raw action. But you can put the verb into what the grammarians call the *eighth form* — you double it inward, add a texture — and something specific happens: **the action folds back onto the doer.** The doer isn't just doing the thing; the doer is doing it *for himself, to himself, taking it into his own possession.* Think of the difference in English between "he chose a name" and "he adopted a name." *Adopted* has that reflexive weight — he took it *on*, made it *his*, wrapped it around himself.

Now watch the very first word.


**وَاتَّخَذُوا** — *wa-ttakhadhū.* "And they took."

The root is *akhadha* — to take, to seize. And Allah puts it in exactly that eighth form. Not *"gods were given to them."* Not *"gods appeared among them."* **They took for themselves.** They manufactured this relationship. They reached out with their own hands and adopted these things as gods.

Stay with this, because it is the load-bearing beam of the whole passage and everyone rushes past it. The idol did not come to them. The idol did not knock. No god descended and demanded worship. A human being looked at a stone, or a cause, or a fear, and *made it into a lord for himself.* The Arabic will not let them off the hook and will not let us off the hook. Whatever this is, **it was chosen.** It was an act. The hands that Allah mentions three ayat earlier — *"from what Our hands have made"* — are answered here by the hands of the human being, reaching out to take what Allah never handed them.

Feel where that lands. The whole tragedy about to unfold is a tragedy the person walked into on their own two feet. And that is not cruelty in the text. That is dignity. You cannot walk out of a trap you were shoved into. You *can* walk out of one you built. **The verb that dooms them is also the verb that means it was never inevitable.**

### Element Two — *La'allahum yunṣarūn*: the passive voice of a hope that never arrives

Now the reason. Allah gives us their motive in three words, and every one of them is doing work.

<!-- morphology:36:74:7 pos=N -->
**لَّعَلَّهُمْ** — *la'allahum.* "Hoping that they..." "In the hope that they..."

*La'alla* is the particle of hope and expectation. And here is the first quiet devastation: **whose voice is this?** Allah is narrating their inner life. He is reaching into the chest of the idol-worshipper and reading out, to the whole of creation, the private sentence he never says aloud: *maybe this will save me.* And notice — even in their own optimistic framing, the strongest word available to them is *maybe.* *La'alla.* Perhaps. There is no certainty even in the fantasy. The best the idol ever promised, even to its most devoted servant, was *perhaps.*

Now the word the hope hangs on:


**يُنصَرُونَ** — *yunṣarūn.* From the root *n-ṣ-r* — help, aid, victory, rescue.

Say it and listen to its shape. This is a *passive* verb. Not "so that the gods would help them." Not even "so that they would win." **"So that they would *be helped.*"** The worshippers have positioned themselves as pure recipients. They are lying back with open hands. The whole posture of the sentence is a person waiting for help to *arrive at them* from the outside — someone else does the lifting, someone else does the saving, and *I* am simply the one it happens *to.*

And the classical mufassirūn tell us precisely what help they were lying back waiting for. al-Jalalayn glosses *yunṣarūn* here as being *shielded from the punishment of Allah through the intercession of their gods — bi-za'mihim,* "by their own claim." al-Muyassar reads it the same way: they seized these gods coveting *rescue from Allah's punishment.* And Ibn Kathīr adds the other half of the fantasy — that the idols would *bring them nearer* to Allah, brokers who would usher a small man's case into a Court he dared not enter alone. So the *nasr* in this word is not merely winning a raid in the desert. It is rescue *on the Day* — an advocate standing at the bar. Keep that courtroom in view. Because the last word of the very next ayah is going to drag them into exactly that court — and the advocate will not be there.

Hold that passive verb in one hand. We are going to need it in a moment, because Allah is about to take that exact word — *nasr* — and hand it back to them in a shape they never expected.

### Element Three — the reversal: from *yunṣarūn* to *jund*

Here is where the two ayat become a single trap that closes.

They took gods *la'allahum yunṣarūn* — so that they, the worshippers, **would be helped.** That is the hope. Now the reality, ayah 75:


**لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ** — *lā yastaṭī'ūn.* "They are not able."

Another built-up verb — the *tenth form* this time, the form of *seeking* a capacity, *reaching* for a power. It is almost merciful in its precision: it doesn't just say the gods don't help. It says they cannot even *reach* for the power to help. The capacity isn't withheld; it was never there to summon.

al-Ṭabarī draws the line exactly: these gods *cannot help them against Allah, should He intend them harm, nor ward off from them any injury.* Against a human enemy the idol was already useless; against the one they most needed shelter *from,* it is not even in the conversation. And notice a small, devastating courtesy in the grammar, one al-Jalalayn points out: the verb *yastaṭī'ūn* takes the plural form reserved for **rational beings** — for one clause Allah speaks of these lumps of stone as though they were thinking agents who *might* have acted, only to announce that these dignified "persons" can do precisely nothing. The sentence lends them the costume of competence and strips it off in the same breath. al-Muyassar presses it one degree further still: they cannot help their worshippers — *nor can they even help themselves.*


Not able to do what? **نَصْرَهُمْ** — *naṣrahum.* Their *nasr.* Their help.

There it is again — the same root, *n-ṣ-r*, that carried their entire hope one line earlier. The gods *cannot* do the one thing they were adopted to do. The account is now closed on the help going *downward,* from god to worshipper. Zero. The column is empty.

And now watch the sentence do the thing that should stop your breath.



**وَهُمْ لَهُمْ جُندٌ مُّحْضَرُونَ** — *wa hum lahum jundun muḥḍarūn.* "While *they* are, for *them,* a host arraigned."

*Jund.* An army. Troops. A garrison. And al-Ṭabarī reads the pronouns the way the whole passage has been leaning — *wa hāʾulāʾi al-mushrikūna li-ālihatihim jundun muḥḍarūn,* **"these idolaters are, for their gods, a host brought forth."** The worshippers are the idol's standing troops. In this world, they defend it, they fight for it, they get furious for its honour, they carry it to Badr on a litter, they will raise their swords the instant someone insults it. All that energy, all that loyalty, all that readiness to bleed — flowing *from* the worshipper *to* the god.

Do you see what just happened? Feel the direction of the arrow.

They reached for the idol so that **help would flow to them.** *Yunṣarūn* — the passive hand held out. And by the end of two lines, the help is flowing the *other* way entirely. They are not the ones being helped. **They are the help.** They are the army. They are the ones showing up, standing guard, doing the lifting, spending themselves — for a thing that, the sentence has just told us, *cannot lift a finger for them.*

The recipient became the servant. The one who wanted to be rescued became the rescuer of what cannot be rescued. This is not a scholar's clever cross-reference; the two words are the same root, *nasr*, deliberately set at opposite ends of the passage so the reversal is unmissable. **You reached out to be carried, and you became the one carrying.**

### Element Four — *Muḥḍarūn*: the passive they did not choose

And there is one more word, the last one, and it answers the very first.

We began with **ٱتَّخَذُوا** — *they took.* Active. Chosen. Their own hands. Now the ayah ends on:

**مُّحْضَرُونَ** — *muḥḍarūn.* "Brought forth." "Made present." "Arraigned."

Listen to the word first, before the meaning. *Muḥ-ḍa-rūn.* Feel the weight of it in the mouth — the heavy *ḥā*, the heavier *ḍād*, two of the densest, most pharyngeal sounds in the language, dragging one after the other. Say *yunṣarūn* and then say *muḥḍarūn.* The first flows. The second *lands* like something being hauled. The sound is doing what the meaning does: this is not a light word, and it is not a light thing. It is the word for a prisoner brought before the judge, a defendant *produced* in court whether he wishes it or not.

And it is passive. **They are *brought.*** No one asks them. The verb has no room in it for their consent.

The mufassirūn part ways on the fine mechanics here, and the disagreement is worth knowing. al-Ṭabarī records an *ikhtilāf* over *where* this bringing happens, leaning toward the scene of the Reckoning — *jundun muḥḍarūn ʿinda al-ḥisāb,* a host produced at the Judgment. al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar assign the final pronouns the other way and place the scene in the Fire: the false gods themselves *muḥḍarūn fī al-nār,* dragged in *alongside* their servants — and al-Muyassar adds the bleakest detail of all, that worshipper and idol are herded into the punishment *together, each disowning the other.* But notice where every one of these readings lands. Whichever way you assign the pronouns, no *nasr* ever travels downward; the whole company is *brought,* and the thing that was meant to save arrives only to share the sentence — or to vanish from the dock entirely. The advocate the man was counting on is either in the cell beside him or nowhere at all.

Now put the two poles of the passage side by side, and see the whole thing at once:

- It opens on their agency: *ittakhadhū* — **they took.** Their choice, their hands, their reach.
- It closes on their helplessness: *muḥḍarūn* — **they are brought.** Someone else's grip, someone else's hands, and no reach left at all.

The one free act — *taking* gods — is precisely the act that strips every future act of its freedom. The hands that reached out are the hands that will be led away. **What you seize in the window of your agency can become the thing that seizes you when the window shuts.**

That is the linguistic architecture, and now let us ask what it means not for grammar — but for a life.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — You think you own it. It owns you.

Let me start nowhere near a stone idol. Let me start with a man I think you know.

He is thirty-eight. Twelve years ago he made a decision — a good one, an admirable one. He decided he would build something. A company, a career, a name. And the reason was clean: *so that my family would be secure. So that I'd never be at anyone's mercy. So that when things got hard, we would be held.* Do you hear that? *La'allahum yunṣarūn.* So that we would be helped. That was the whole motive, and it was not a stupid one.

Now watch him at thirty-eight. Watch him at 11 p.m. answering one more email because the thing needs him. Watch him miss the dinner, again, because the thing has a launch. Watch his mood on Sunday night be entirely decided by a number on a dashboard. Watch him get *angry* — genuinely, chest-tighteningly angry — when someone criticises the thing, defends its honour at a party the way a man defends a member of his family. Ask him honestly and he will tell you: *I don't feel like I own this anymore. I feel like I report to it.*

He built the thing so it would carry him. And somewhere in those twelve years, without a single day where you could point and say *there, that's when it flipped,* **he became its army.** He shows up for it. He guards it. He bleeds for it. And here is the sentence from Ya-Sin, laid over his life like a template that was written fourteen hundred years before he was born: *lā yastaṭī'ūna naṣrahum.* When the diagnosis comes, when the grief comes, when the grave comes — **it cannot help him.** Not because it's evil. Because it's a thing, and things cannot save. He gave his one life as a standing army to a garrison that will not, when the siege comes, be able to send him a single soldier back.

This is the anatomy of every false god, and the Qur'an has just given us the X-ray. It is not that the idol *lies* to you at the start. It is that the direction of help silently reverses while you're not looking. You take it up as a *source* of rescue; you end as its *supply line.* The addiction begins as the thing that soothes you and ends as the thing you organise your entire day around soothing. The image you cultivate begins as the thing that will make you safe among people and ends as the master you perform for every waking hour, terrified of the moment the mask slips. The ideology, the grievance, the relationship you cannot breathe without — each one adopted *la'alla,* in the hope of being held, and each one quietly enlisting you as *jund,* until you'd swear an oath and raise a sword for a thing that could not raise a hand for you. And recall what al-Muyassar noticed about the end of the story: on the Day the two are herded together, *each disowns the other.* The thing you bled for turns and denies it ever knew you. That is the final wage of the false god — not merely that it cannot help, but that it will not even *claim* you when the accounts are opened.

[PAUSE]

Notice what the Qur'an is *not* saying, because this is where it becomes merciful rather than merely diagnostic. It is not telling you to want nothing, to build nothing, to love nothing. Three ayat earlier Allah is *celebrating* the cattle, the provision, the milk and the rides. The goods are good. The company can be good. The work is worship when it's aimed right. **The entire disease is in one preposition** — *min dūni-llāh,* "besides Allah," "short of Allah." The thing was never the problem. The thing *in the place of God* is the problem. The moment your security genuinely rests on it rather than on Him, the moment your mood belongs to it, the moment you would defend it before you would obey Him — you have not acquired an asset. **You have enlisted.**

So the practical question is not "what do I need to throw away." It is quieter and harder. Tonight, name the one thing your peace is actually resting on. Not the thing you *say* — the thing that decides whether you sleep. And then ask it the only question that matters: *when they lower me into the ground, can you follow me in?* If the honest answer is no — then it is your *jund,* and you are its army, and it is time to change which direction you are facing. Not to destroy it. To dethrone it. To move it out of *dūni-llāh* and back under Him, where it can finally be a mercy again instead of a master.

### Theme Two — The window of the free hand

Here is something we all know in our bones and spend our lives denying.

Every trap that ever closed on a human being was, at the start, a door standing open.

Talk to anyone who has climbed out of anything — a bottle, a debt, a relationship that was slowly deleting them. Ask them when it began. And they will almost never point to the middle, to the worst of it. They point to a small, forgettable, *chosen* moment near the start. The first yes. The first swipe of the card. The first time they let it slide. In that moment there was a free hand. *Ittakhadhū.* **They took.** It was, genuinely, a choice — small, reversible, almost weightless. And then a thousand small yeses later, they will describe the end of the road with a completely different grammar. *"I don't even decide anymore. My feet just take me there. I get pulled."* Listen to that. That is a human being describing themselves in the passive voice. That is *muḥḍarūn* in a t-shirt. **Brought. Produced. Led.** The free hand, used enough times in one direction, becomes a hand that is led.

The Qur'an compresses that entire arc into the distance between the first word of ayah 74 and the last word of ayah 75. *Ittakhadhū* → *muḥḍarūn.* From *they took* to *they are brought.* From the open door to the closed cell. And the terrifying, liberating point is that **it is the same people, and it is the same hand.** No one else did this to them. The very faculty that made them free — the power to reach out and take — is the faculty that, aimed wrongly and repeated, quietly hands their freedom away.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the terror of that for one honest second, and then let me show you the mercy folded inside it, because it is enormous.

If the trap is built by a chosen hand, then *right now,* in whatever this moment is, **you still have the hand.** The window Allah keeps describing — this dunya, this breath, this un-arraigned present tense where you are not yet *muḥḍar,* not yet brought before the Judge — this entire life is the window in which the hand is still free. The whole reason ayah 76 turns immediately to comfort the Prophet ﷺ — *"so let not their speech grieve you"* — is that their fate is not yet sealed *while they breathe.* The passive verb *muḥḍarūn* lives in the future. It has not happened yet. And every moment it has not happened yet is a moment the active verb is still available to you: you can still *take* — but this time take the rope Allah is lowering instead of the stone you were reaching for.

Make it concrete, because this cannot stay a beautiful idea or it changes nothing by Tuesday. There is one place in your life where you can feel the pull already — where the choosing has started to feel less like choosing. You know the one. It named itself the moment I said it. That is exactly the place where the window is still open and closing. Not the dramatic sins — the quiet dependency, the thing you've started arranging your life around, the little god you've been feeding *la'alla* it will make you okay. The single most powerful thing you can do this week is one deliberate act of the free hand *against* the pull, while you still have it freely: one prayer you make on time specifically to remember Who actually holds you, one refusal, one turning of the face. Not to win the whole war on Tuesday. To prove to yourself, with your own hand, that the hand is still yours.

Because there is coming a day when it will not be. On that day the verb will be passive and it will not ask you. Today it still asks. **Today the hand is still yours. Use it while it is still a taking and not yet a being-taken.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: a grammar that enacts a fall

Now stand back and look at the two ayat as a single designed object, because the design is the proof.

Trace the verbs in order, and watch what happens to the human being's power across them:

1. **ٱتَّخَذُوا** — *they took.* Full agency. The active hand.
2. **لَعَلَّهُمْ** — *hoping.* Agency thins into mere wish. They no longer act; they hope.
3. **يُنصَرُونَ** — *that they be helped.* Passive. They are now waiting to be acted upon.
4. **لَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ** — *they cannot.* The power they were counting on is declared empty.
5. **جُندٌ** — *a host.* Their remaining energy is conscripted — spent outward, on what cannot repay it.
6. **مُّحْضَرُونَ** — *brought, arraigned.* Total passivity. The hand is now led.

That is a staircase, and it goes one direction: **down.** From *they took* to *they are brought.* The passage begins with a human being at the height of his agency — reaching, choosing, seizing — and ends with him a defendant produced in a court he did not schedule. And every rung of the descent is a single, verifiable feature of the grammar. Allah did not *describe* a fall from power to powerlessness. **He built the sentence so that the words themselves fall.** You cannot read the two ayat aloud without your own tongue walking the person down the stairs. That is not accident. That is the fingerprint of an Author who fits the shape of the speech to the shape of the truth.

### The two journeys were one journey

And now the linguistic discovery and the two themes turn out to be the same single insight, seen from three sides.

The grammar said: agency spent on a false god converts into helplessness. Theme one said: the thing you adopt to carry you enlists you to carry it. Theme two said: the free hand, used wrongly, becomes a led hand. **These are not three lessons. They are one law, stated in three languages** — the language of morphology, the language of a man at his desk at 11 p.m., and the language of the recovering addict pointing to his first small yes. *Nasr* reaching *down* to you was always a fantasy from anything *min dūni-llāh.* The only real direction, from every false god, is *nasr* flowing *out* of you until you are empty and then being *brought,* arraigned, with nothing left. The idol never held you up. You were holding it up the whole time.

We began this by asking one question: they reached for their gods *so that they would be helped* — by the end, who is helping whom? Here is the answer the ayat forced on us. **They are.** They are the ones helping. They are the army. And the god they enlisted for cannot send one soldier back when the real siege — the grave, the Reckoning — finally comes. It cannot even claim them: on that Day, the classical commentators tell us, the two are brought together only to disown one another.

### Questions to carry

Do not answer these quickly. Let them find you over the next few days.

1. Name the thing your peace actually rests on this week — not what you'd say out loud, but the thing that decides whether you sleep. **Can it follow you into the grave?**
2. Where in your life has a *choosing* quietly started to feel like a *being-pulled*? Where has *ittakhadhū* begun sliding toward *muḥḍarūn*?
3. Think of something good you adopted *la'alla* — in the hope it would hold you: the work, the person, the plan. Has the direction of help reversed without your noticing? **Are you still being carried by it, or have you become its army?**
4. If the free hand is still yours only for now — what is the one deliberate act you would make with it today, if you truly believed the window closes?

### One sentence to carry

**Whatever you take besides Allah so that it might save you, you will end up serving — and it cannot save you.**

### The invitation

You are not being asked tonight to burn down your life, to want nothing, to love nothing. You are being asked to check the direction of one arrow. Everything good in your life — keep it. Just move it out from the place marked *min dūni-llāh,* out from *in the place of God,* and set it back underneath Him, where the cattle and the gardens and the springs of Ya-Sin were always meant to sit: as gifts that point to the Giver, not gods that replace Him. Do that, and the very same things that were quietly conscripting you become mercies again. The hand is still yours. That is not a threat. **That is the open door, and it is still open.**

### Du'ā'

O Allah, You gave with Your own hands what our hands then reached past. Turn our faces back before we are brought and can turn no more. Let nothing besides You sit in the place where only You belong, and make us, while the hand is still free, take hold of You — the only One whose help arrives when every other help is arraigned and empty. *Āmīn.*
