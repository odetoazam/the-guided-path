# The Welded Scale: When Warning and Silence Weigh the Same

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first, slowly, the way it would have fallen on the ears of the one it was first spoken to.

> [ayah:36:10] وَسَوَآءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ ءَأَنذَرْتَهُمْ أَمْ لَمْ تُنذِرْهُمْ لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ
>
> *wa-sawāʾun ʿalayhim a-anḏartahum am lam tunḏirhum lā yuʾminūn*
>
> "And it is the same to them — whether you warn them, or do not warn them — they will not believe."

Eight words. That's the whole ayah. Eight words, and they contain one of the most frightening sentences in the Qur'an. Not frightening because of fire or chains or thunder. Frightening because of something much quieter than that.

**It is the same to them.**

### Let's rebuild the world this landed in

Picture the man this was spoken to. Picture Muhammad ﷺ in the early years of Makkah.

This is a man who is *carrying* something. He has been handed a message he believes — he *knows* — will save the people he loves. These are his cousins. His neighbors. The men he traded with, the families he grew up among. He walks among them every day knowing what is coming, knowing the cliff edge they are walking toward, and he is reaching out his hand to pull them back.

And they walk past him.

Not with swords. Not even, at first, with mockery. Something worse. They walk past him the way you walk past a billboard you've seen a thousand times. The way you walk past the safety card in the seat pocket on an airplane. He warns, and… nothing moves. He stays silent for a day, exhausted, and… nothing moves. Warning and silence produce the exact same result.

Now think about what that does to a heart like his. Because the Surah has been building to this. Listen to what comes just before. Ayah 8: *We have placed upon their necks shackles reaching to the chins, so their heads are forced up.* Ayah 9: *We have set a barrier before them and a barrier behind them, and covered them over — so they do not see.*

Shackles. Walls in front. Walls behind. A covering over the eyes. These are physical images — you can see them, you can almost feel the iron — but **the Qur'an is never really talking about iron.** It is showing you something it cannot point at directly. It is showing you what a *heart* looks like when it has locked itself shut. And then, after all that imagery of locks and walls and blindness, comes our ayah. The verdict. The lived symptom of all that locking:

*It is the same to them.*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to sit with for the next hour. We are going to discover that Allah chose, for this verdict, a word He elsewhere uses for some of the most beautiful things in existence — the word for **balance, for proportion, for perfect symmetry.** The same root from which He says He *proportioned* you and *balanced* you when He made you (*sawwāka*, 82:7). The instrument of perfect equilibrium.

And here, He uses that instrument of balance to describe a catastrophe.

How does the word for *perfect balance* become the word for *spiritual death*? And why — when this is the most discouraging sentence imaginable for a teacher, a parent, a caller to good — why does the very next ayah suggest this verdict is actually a *mercy* aimed at the messenger himself?

Stay with me. Let's open it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: سَوَاء — the scale that has been welded flat

Let me teach you something before we touch the word, because the word will not land until you have the concept in your hands.

Think about what a scale is. A balance — the old kind, two pans hanging from a beam. What makes it a *scale* is that it **responds**. You put a feather on one side, and it dips. You put a coin on the other, and it swings back. Its entire reason for existing is that it is *sensitive to difference*. A scale that does not move when you add weight is not a calibrated scale. It is a broken one. Or worse — it is a scale that someone has welded solid. The beam no longer tilts. You can pile gold on one side and feathers on the other, and the needle does not so much as twitch. It reads the same. It reads *equal*. Not because the two sides are truly equal — but because the instrument that was *supposed* to detect the difference has died.

Hold onto that image. The welded scale.

Now, the word.

> **وَسَوَآءٌ** — *wa-sawāʾun*

The root is **س-و-ي** (s-w-y). And this is one of the great roots of the Qur'an. Its core meaning is *to make level, to make even, to set in proportion, to bring into perfect balance.* From this root comes the word for a *level, straight path* (*sawiyy*). From this root comes the staggering statement in Surat al-Infiṭār about your own body: **فَسَوَّىٰكَ فَعَدَلَكَ** — *"He proportioned you and balanced you"* (82:7). The careful, loving leveling of your bones, your features, the symmetry of your two hands. *Taswiyah.* Perfect equilibrium. It is a word of *craftsmanship* and *harmony*.

So when Allah opens this verdict with *sawāʾ*, He is not reaching for an ugly word. He is reaching for the word that means **two things have come into perfect equivalence.**

Notice something. Notice what the two things are.

The two things that have been made *equal*, *level*, *weighed identical* — are **warning them** and **not warning them.** The most urgent act of love a human can perform — grabbing someone by the shoulders and crying *the cliff is right there* — and complete, indifferent silence. These two opposites. Allah says: on the scale of their hearts, **these two weigh exactly the same.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel it now? The horror is *inside* the beauty of the word.

A healthy heart is a scale. It is *supposed* to respond. Warn it, and it should dip toward fear. Stay silent, and it should feel the absence. A living conscience is, by its very nature, *sensitive to difference* — between truth and falsehood, between the call and its absence, between danger named and danger hidden. That sensitivity *is* the life of the heart.

And *sawāʾ* tells you: that sensitivity is gone. The needle does not move. You can pile the most urgent warning in all of creation onto one pan, and total silence onto the other, and the heart reads them as **equal.**

The scale has been welded flat.

Here is what is so devastating, and I want to say it carefully. The tragedy is not that they *heard the warning and rejected it.* Rejection is at least *motion*. To reject something, you have to first weigh it, feel its weight, and push it away. That is a heart still working. No — the tragedy of *sawāʾ* is deeper than rejection. It is that the warning and the silence **register identically.** There is no weighing left to do. The instrument that does the weighing has stopped.

[PAUSE]

And now I want you to feel the full weight of Allah choosing *this* root. The root of *taswiyah*, of the careful proportioning of your very body. The same divine act that *balanced* you into existence describes, here, what it looks like when that balance has been turned against itself — when the finely-tuned instrument of the heart has been flattened into a single dead reading.

That is the first word. *Sawāʾ.* The welded scale. Sit with it.

[PAUSE — let it settle]

### Element Two: أَأَنذَرْتَهُمْ أَمْ لَمْ تُنذِرْهُمْ — the two arms of the balance, and the collapse of all time

We have the scale. Now Allah does something extraordinary: He shows us the *two pans* He is placing on it. And the way He builds the sentence is itself the lesson.

Let me teach the structure first.

In Arabic, there is a construction for laying out two alternatives as a perfect pair — *this, or that.* It uses the questioning *hamza* at the front — the little **أَ** sound, *a-* — paired later with **أَمْ** (*am*), which means *or*. **أَ … أَمْ …** *Whether… or…* It is the grammatical shape of a balance with two pans. You hear it and your mind automatically sets up two sides and waits to see which way it tips.

Now hear the ayah deploy it:

> **ءَأَنذَرْتَهُمْ أَمْ لَمْ تُنذِرْهُمْ**
>
> *a-anḏartahum* — *whether you warned them*
> *am lam tunḏirhum* — *or did not warn them*

Two pans. Warning on one side. No-warning on the other. The grammar itself builds the scale we just talked about — and then *sawāʾ*, sitting at the front of the sentence, has already told you the verdict before you even reach the two sides: *equal.* The needle will not move whatever you load on.

But now look closer, because there is something hidden in the *verbs* that most people walk straight past.

The first verb — **أَنذَرْتَهُمْ** — *anḏarta* — is in the **past tense.** The perfect. *You warned them.* It is *done.* A completed act, finished, in the books.

The second verb — **تُنذِرْهُمْ** — *tunḏir* — is in the **present-future tense.** The imperfect. *You warn them / you will warn them.* It is *open-ended.* Ongoing. Not yet finished. The warning that is still to come.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah just did?

He did not say "whether you warned them or did not warn them" using the same tense on both sides. He deliberately set the **completed past** against the **open future.** And He declared *both* of them *sawāʾ* — equal, weighing nothing.

So this is not just "warning versus silence." This is: **every warning you have already delivered, stretching back through all the years — and every warning you could ever deliver, stretching forward through all the years to come — all of it, the entire past and the entire future of the prophetic effort, laid on the scale together, and the needle does not move.**

He collapsed *time itself* onto the scale. Not "today's sermon made no difference." Rather: the sum total of warning across all of time, weighed against the sum total of silence across all of time — *equal.* Zero on both sides.

This is why the word *sawāʾ* had to come *first* in the sentence, before the two pans were even named. In Arabic word order, fronting a word puts the spotlight on it. The verdict is announced before the evidence is laid out — because the verdict does not depend on the evidence. It is not "let's warn them and see." It is "the result is already known: *equal* — now here are the two things that are equal."

One more thing, and it is tender. Notice **who** is being addressed. *You.* *You* warned them — *anḏarta-hum*. The grammatical "you," singular, masculine — is the Prophet ﷺ himself. Allah is not describing this situation in the abstract, from a distance. He has turned, mid-Surah, to speak **directly to His Messenger's exhausted heart.** Hold that. We will come back to why that direct address changes everything.

That is the second element. The scale, fully assembled: two pans, all of past warning, all of future warning, and a verdict spoken before the weighing even begins.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ — they will not enter the safety, and the silence of *in what*

Now we reach the last two words. The reading on the welded scale. The result.

> **لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ** — *lā yuʾminūn* — "they will not believe."

Two observations, and both of them open downward into something deep.

**First — the root.** The verb here is from the root **أ-م-ن** (Amn). And here is what most translations cannot carry: this is the *same root* as the word **أَمْن** — *security, safety, peace.* The root of *amān* — sanctuary, the state of being unharmed and unafraid. To *believe*, in Arabic — *to do īmān* — is, at its root, **to enter safety. To accept security. To be brought into peace.**

Let that reframe the entire ayah.

We tend to hear "they will not believe" as "they refuse to *agree* with a proposition." As if faith were merely accepting a fact. But the root says something far more poignant. *Lā yuʾminūn* means: **they will not enter the safety.** They will not come in from the storm. The door of *amān* is standing open, the shelter is right there, the warner is calling them in out of the danger — and they will not cross the threshold.

Think about what this does to the relationship between the two halves of the ayah. The *warning* (anḏara) is, by its nature, *the naming of danger.* The *belief* (īmān) is, by its root, *the entering of safety.* So the whole verse is the geometry of rescue: a man stands at the door of the shelter, pointing at the approaching danger, calling — and the people he is calling treat the door and the storm as *sawāʾ.* Equal. They will not come in. Not because they cannot see the door. Because the instrument inside them that distinguishes *shelter* from *storm* has gone flat.

[PAUSE]

**Second — listen to what is *missing.*** This is the kind of absence the Qur'an wants you to notice.

*Lā yuʾminūn* — "they will not believe." But believe… **in what?** There is no object. No *bihi* ("in it"), no *bi-Llāhi* ("in Allah"), no specified thing they refuse to believe. The verb is left standing alone, open.

Why would Allah leave the object empty?

Because the problem is not located in any *particular* truth they reject. It is not that they have examined this specific claim and found it wanting. The emptiness after the verb tells you: **the refusal has become unconditioned. Object-less. Total.** It is no longer "they will not believe *this*." It is simply — *they will not believe.* Full stop. Belief itself, as a capacity, as a movement of the heart, has shut down. You do not need to name what they reject, because the answer is: anything. Everything. The door itself, whatever lies beyond it.

An empty space after a verb where Arabic *expects* an object — and in that empty space, Allah has placed the totality of the tragedy. The refusal is so complete that it no longer even needs a thing to refuse.

[PAUSE]

### Bridging from the words to the reality

Look at what these three words have built, standing together.

*Sawāʾ* — the scale welded flat. *A-anḏarta… am lam tunḏir* — every warning of all time, weighed against every silence of all time, on the two dead pans. *Lā yuʾminūn* — the needle's final reading: they will not come into safety, and the refusal has grown so total it no longer needs an object.

That is the architecture of the sentence. But the Qur'an did not give us this sentence so that we would admire its architecture. The physical image of the welded scale is a stepping stone. Now let us ask what it is pointing at — what it reveals not just about *words*, but about the human heart, and about the strange mercy hidden inside this most discouraging of verdicts.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Flat Heart — when the most dangerous thing is *not* anger

Let me start where you live, not where the ayah is.

Think about the opposite of love. Quick — what is it? Most people say *hate.* But spend any time around human relationships and you learn something more painful: the opposite of love is not hate. Hate still *cares.* Hate still has the person's name in its mouth all day long. Hate is *energy* pointed at someone, and energy can always be turned around. The true opposite of love — the thing from which there is no easy return — is **indifference.** When someone stops being angry at you, stops fighting with you, stops anything-ing you, and simply… does not register you. When you walk into the room and the needle does not move. *Sawāʾ.* Whether you are there or not — equal.

Anyone who has watched a relationship die knows this. The shouting phase is frightening, but the shouting phase is *alive.* It is the day the shouting *stops* — the day it all becomes "fine," flat, *equal* — that the thing is truly over.

Now bring that back to the ayah, because this is exactly what Allah is diagnosing, and it is so much more terrifying than we usually let it be.

We read about the deniers in Makkah and we imagine *active hostility.* Fists raised, insults flying. And that was there. But *this* ayah is not describing the fist. The fist is a *response.* The fist means the warning *landed* — it provoked something. What this ayah describes is the stage *past* the fist. The stage where the warning lands on the heart and produces… nothing. Not rage. Not even a flinch. *Sawāʾ.* The same.

Because here is the thing about a living heart, even a sinful one: **a living heart can still be reached.** The man who hears the warning and rages against it is in agony — and agony means there is still a nerve. The terrifying condition is not the heart that fights the truth. It is the heart for which truth and falsehood, warning and silence, the door and the storm, have all flattened into the same gray reading.

[PAUSE]

How does a heart get there? The Surah told us, two ayahs earlier, and it is worth understanding because it is *not* a sudden curse. Ayah 9 spoke of barriers placed before them and behind them. The classical commentators understand this imagery in conversation with a principle stated all over the Qur'an: the heart is sealed *after* and *because* it repeatedly chose to turn away. The sealing is not arbitrary. It is the hardening that *habit* produces. Every time a warning is heard and pushed aside, the heart grows a fraction less sensitive to the next one. The needle moves a little less. And a little less. Until one day — *sawāʾ.*

You know this mechanism. It is the smoke alarm in the apartment with the low battery — *chirp… chirp…* — and for the first day it drives you insane, and by the third day **you genuinely no longer hear it.** Not "you ignore it." You *do not hear it.* Your mind has filed it under *irrelevant* and stopped delivering it to your attention. That is *taswiyah* in its dark form — the leveling of a signal down to silence through sheer repetition of ignoring it.

The first cigarette makes you cough. The hundredth does not. The first time you wrong someone your conscience screams. The hundredth time it whispers. The thousandth time — *sawāʾ.* The deed and its absence weigh the same.

[PAUSE]

So what does this ask of *us* — not of the Makkan deniers, but of you and me, sitting here? It asks a single, searching question, and I want you to take it seriously: **Where in my own life has the needle stopped moving?**

Because the deadening is never announced. No one wakes up and says "today my heart went flat toward the prayer." It happens by degrees, under the radar, precisely *because* the loss of sensitivity is also the loss of the ability to *notice* the loss. The welded scale cannot weigh its own deadness. So you have to go looking, deliberately, for the places where a warning that should move you produces *sawāʾ* — a reminder of death that once shook you and now slides off; a verse that used to bring tears and now is just words; a sin you once feared and now perform without a tremor.

The practical work this ayah hands you is almost frightening in its simplicity: **treat any growing indifference toward the truth as a five-alarm emergency** — *more* urgent than active struggle, not less. The day worship becomes *fine*, neither sweet nor bitter, neither present nor missed — that flatness is not peace. It may be the welding beginning. And the response to the welded scale is not more information — information is exactly what *sawāʾ* renders weightless. The response is to do whatever still moves the needle even slightly: the company that softens you, the place that humbles you, the act of service that cracks you open — and to run toward it before the reading goes fully flat. While the scale still trembles, even a little, the heart is still alive.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Messenger's Peace — you carry the warning, never the result

Now I want to turn the ayah around completely, because so far we have read it as a verdict on the deniers. But remember who Allah is *speaking to.* He turned, mid-sentence, and addressed *you* — *anḏarta-hum*, **you** warned them. This ayah is not, at its heart, aimed at the deniers at all. **It is spoken to comfort the one who has been pouring himself out for them.** And in that, there is a liberation so vast it can reorder your entire life.

Let me start, again, where you live.

You have, somewhere in your life, a person you cannot save. A parent drifting from the deen and every conversation goes nowhere. A child you raised with everything you had, who has walked a road you would give your life to pull them back from. A friend, a sibling, a spouse. You have warned, pleaded, modeled, prayed, stayed up at night composing the words that might *finally* land — and the needle does not move. *Sawāʾ.*

And here is what that does to a sincere person. It does not make them give up. It makes them **bleed.** Because the sincere caller secretly believes a lie — a beautiful, exhausting, crushing lie. The lie is this: *if they do not change, I have failed. Their belief is my responsibility. Their outcome is on my account.*

This is the weight that breaks da'wah workers, breaks teachers, breaks parents. Not the *effort* — the effort they would gladly give forever. What breaks them is carrying the *result.* Carrying something that was never theirs to carry.

Now hear how gently, and how firmly, Allah lifts that weight off the shoulders of the greatest caller who ever lived.

The Surah will say it almost explicitly a few ayahs later: *"You can only warn one who follows the Reminder…"* (36:11). And elsewhere, again and again, with what reads almost like a divine refrain of mercy: *"Upon you is only the delivery — and upon Us is the reckoning"* (13:40). *"You are not over them a controller"* (88:22). And — most piercingly — in a verse revealed to console this very Prophet over this very grief: *"Perhaps you would kill yourself with grief over them, if they will not believe in this message"* (18:6).

Do you hear Allah's tenderness in that? *Perhaps you would destroy yourself over them.* He sees His Messenger's heart breaking over the welded scales, and He intervenes — not to change the deniers, but to **release His beloved from a burden he was never assigned.**

[PAUSE]

This is the great divide the ayah carves, and you must see it clearly, because your peace depends on it. There are two separate domains:

The **warning** — *anḏara.* The delivery. The effort. The sincerity. The words, the prayers, the patient years.

And the **belief** — *yuʾminūn.* The entering of safety. The change of heart. The result.

The first domain is **entirely yours.** You are commanded into it, you are accountable for it, and you will be rewarded for every ounce of it regardless of what happens next.

The second domain is **entirely not yours.** It was never on your scale. It belongs to the One who alone holds hearts between two of His fingers and turns them as He wills.

And this ayah — *sawāʾ ʿalayhim* — is Allah Himself drawing the line between the two domains in front of you. He is saying to the warner: *Look. I am telling you in advance — for this particular people, the result will be the same whether you warn or not.* **And He commanded the warning anyway.** Ya-Sin is built on the command to warn (36:6). The result is declared null — *and the warning is still obligatory.*

Sit with the staggering implication. **If the warning were only valuable for its result, a warning declared in advance to produce no result would be pointless.** But it is not pointless — it is commanded. Which means the warning has worth *independent of whether anyone believes.* The delivery is itself the worship. The effort is itself the deed. The reward is in the *pouring out*, not in the *outcome of the pour.*

[PAUSE]

Think of the farmer. His entire job is to prepare the soil, to plant, to water. That is the farmer's whole religion. And then? He cannot reach into the seed and command it to split open. He cannot pull the shoot up with his fingers. Germination is simply not in his department. A farmer who lay awake at night in anguish, blaming himself for seeds that did not sprout, would be carrying a weight God never placed on farmers. His job is the planting. The growing belongs to the One who made things grow.

You are the farmer. The warning is your planting. *Lā yuʾminūn* — whether it grows — was never your field.

[PAUSE]

So what changes about *tomorrow*?

It does not mean stop warning. Notice that carefully — the ayah does not release the Prophet from *warning*; the very next breath has him still warning the receptive (36:11). The release is not from the *effort.* It is from the *grief over the result.* And those are completely different things. You keep planting. You plant with everything you have. But you plant *as worship to Allah*, not as a transaction with the listener. You make the call because the call is owed to your Lord — and then you lay the outcome down at His door and walk home with your hands empty and your heart light.

Here is the practical form of it, and you can begin tonight. The next time you make the effort for that person you cannot save — the conversation, the prayer, the example — change the address of your hope. Do not address your hope to *them* and their response. Address it to *Allah* and your own sincerity. Say, in your heart: *Yā Allah, I delivered what was mine to deliver. The result I place in Your hands, where it always belonged.* And then *rest.* Because the One who told the best of creation that the outcome was *sawāʾ* — and loved him too much to let him die of grief over it — is telling you the same thing. **Your job is the warning. Never the believing.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### A word of care before we gather it up

I have to pause on something, because this ayah has been argued over, and you deserve honesty about it. On its surface, *"it is the same whether you warn them — they will not believe"* can sound like fatalism: as if these people were simply *programmed* not to believe and never had a chance. Al-Qurṭubī notes that this very ayah was used in the early debates *against* the Qadariyya — those who tried to wall off human choice from divine knowledge entirely.

So let us be precise, the way the tradition is precise. Al-Muyassar's rendering is the key: the ones described here are *al-kuffār al-muʿānidūn* — **the obstinate, the entrenched.** Not "people who never heard." Not "anyone who happens to disbelieve." This is a verdict on a *specific* posture — hearts that have, by their own repeated turning away (the shackles and barriers of 36:8–9 are read as the *consequence* of their choosing), welded their own scales flat. Allah's knowledge that they *will not* believe is not a chain He locked on them; it is His perfect knowledge of the choice they themselves keep making. And the proof that the door of choice stays open is sitting right there in the *very next ayah*: *"You only warn the one who follows the Reminder…"* (36:11). Some hearts the warning *does* reach. The same rain; different ground. Indeed, al-Qurṭubī preserves the exchange in which the human is shown to walk his own road — *"either grateful or ungrateful"* (76:3). The welded scale is a description of where obstinacy *leads*, never a denial that the person did the welding.

Hold both truths, then, the way the believers always have: nothing escapes Allah's knowledge — *and* no one is wronged. *"There is no compulsion in religion"* (2:256) and *"it is the same to them"* (36:10) live in the same Book, in the same chests, without contradiction.

### Bringing it together

Now watch the two halves of our reflection become one thing.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a welded scale: *sawāʾ*, the instrument of balance gone flat; every warning of all time weighed against every silence of all time and reading *equal*; a refusal so total it no longer needs an object.

The **thematic journey** showed us, first, the terror that we ourselves could become that scale — that the most dangerous spiritual state is not anger at the truth but *indifference* to it, the needle that no longer moves. And second, it showed us the mercy folded inside the verdict: that Allah declared the result null *to His Messenger* — not to make him despair, but to **lift the weight of the result off his shoulders** and leave in his hands only the warning, which was the only thing that was ever his.

These are the same insight from two directions. *Sawāʾ* describes a heart that stopped weighing — and frees a messenger from weighing himself by the wrong scale. The disease of the deniers (treating warning and silence as equal) is the very thing that liberates the warner (his reward is equal whether they believe or not). The flattened scale that *condemns* the obstinate is the same flattened scale that *consoles* the sincere. One sentence, two recipients, two opposite effects — death to the one who refuses, peace to the one who delivers.

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life has the needle stopped moving — what warning, what reminder, what verse, once shook me and now reads *sawāʾ*?
2. Have I mistaken the *flatness* of indifference for the *calm* of peace? How would I tell the difference?
3. Who am I trying to save by carrying a result that was never on my scale — and what would it feel like to set that weight down and keep only the warning?
4. When I make an effort for someone's good, is my hope addressed to *their response*, or to *Allah and my own sincerity*?
5. If a warning declared in advance to change no one was still *commanded* — what does that tell me about where the worth of my efforts actually lives?

### One-sentence distillation

When a heart welds its own scale flat, warning and silence weigh the same — and the messenger's peace is in knowing that *delivering* the warning, never *commanding* the response, was ever his to carry.

### The closing invitation

Two movements, then, for after today. Inward: go hunting for the flat places — and treat any growing numbness toward the truth not as maturity or calm but as the emergency it is, and run toward whatever still moves you while it still moves. Outward: keep warning, keep planting, keep pouring yourself out for the ones you love — but lay the outcome down at Allah's door and walk home with empty hands and a light heart. Be the farmer. The planting is your worship. The growing was never your field.

### Du'a

*O Allah, who proportioned us and balanced us — do not let our scales go flat. Keep our hearts tender to Your warning, sensitive to Your call, alive to the difference between Your shelter and the storm. And for everyone we love and cannot save: let us deliver what is ours to deliver, and trust You with everything that was never ours to carry. You are the warner's peace and the turner of hearts. Āmīn.*
