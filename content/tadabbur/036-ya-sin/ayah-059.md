# Stand Apart: The Sin That Was Always a Severing

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the words in front of you, and then let us walk slowly toward what is inside them.

> وَٱمْتَٰزُوا۟ ٱلْيَوْمَ أَيُّهَا ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ
>
> *wa-mtāzū l-yawma ayyuhā l-mujrimūn*
>
> **"And stand apart this Day, O criminals!"**

Four words. One of the shortest sentences in this entire scene of Ya-Sin. And yet I want to suggest to you that almost everything the surah has been building toward is folded into it.

**Let us rebuild the world this verse drops into.**

You have to feel where you are standing when you hear this. Ya-Sin has been moving, verse after verse, toward the Day the dead are raised. And just before our verse, Allah has finished describing the people of the Garden. Picture them: they are not anxious. They are reclining, occupied with delight, shaded, given whatever they call for. And the scene reaches its summit — the very last thing said to them, in verse 58 — is the most tender word in the Arabic language:

> سَلَٰمٌۭ قَوْلًۭا مِّن رَّبٍّۢ رَّحِيمٍۢ
>
> **"Peace — a word from a Merciful Lord."**

Hold that. The final gift to the believers is not a fruit, not a river, not a palace. It is *a word*. A word spoken directly to them, *from* their Lord. Nearness. Intimacy. Allah Himself addressing them with *salām*.

And then — with no pause, no softening, the very next breath of the surah — the address turns. It turns to a different group of people standing in that same gathering. And the word that comes to them is not *salām*.

It is **imtāzū**. *Stand apart.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hunger I want to plant in you before we go a single step further. These two groups have, until this moment, been *standing together*. Mixed in the same crowd. Indistinguishable. And the entire drama of this verse is the moment they are pulled apart — and the strange, almost unbearable fact that, as we will see, **Allah does not say "I will separate you." He commands them to separate themselves.**

Why would He phrase it that way? Why does the worst moment imaginable arrive not as something done *to* the guilty, but as something He commands them to *do*?

Stay with that question. We are going to earn the answer.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *imtāzū* — the verb that turns back on the one who does it

Before we touch this word, I need to teach you something small about how Arabic verbs work, because it is the key that unlocks the whole verse.

In English, when I act, my verb usually points outward, away from me. *I cut the rope.* The cutting lands on the rope. But Arabic has a family of verb-forms — grammarians number them — and one of them, what they call **Form VIII**, has a peculiar character: the action very often *bends back* toward the one performing it. The doer is also, in some sense, the place where the action lands.

Think of the everyday difference between *"I separated the files"* and *"I separated myself off."* In the first, I'm acting on something outside me. In the second, the act curls back — I am both the one doing the separating and the thing being separated. That second flavor is what Form VIII so often carries: a reflexive, self-enacted motion.

Now look at the word.

> وَٱمْتَٰزُوا۟ — **imtāzū**

It comes from the root **م-ي-ز (m-y-z)**, which means to distinguish, to set apart, to sort one thing from another. And the classical scholars are careful here. Listen to how al-Qurtubī lays out the family of this root: he says *tamayyazū*, *amāzū*, and *imtāzū* all circle the same meaning — and then he gives the telling pair: *"miztuhu fa-nmāza wa-mtāza"* — **"I set it apart, and it set itself apart."**

Do you hear the grammar doing its quiet work? There is a form for *I separate something* — and there is **this** form, the one Allah chose, where the thing **separates itself off.**

Allah does not say *amayyizukum* — "I will sort you out." He says **imtāzū** — *you, sort yourselves out. You, stand apart. You do the separating, to yourselves, with your own motion.*

[PAUSE]

Let us sit with this, because it is heavier than it looks.

On the most terrifying morning of all existence, the command to the guilty is not "be dragged away." It is, grammatically, *make the separation yourselves*. And here is what that reveals: **the separation was never going to be imposed from outside. It was already there.** The Day does not invent the gap between the believer and the criminal. The Day simply commands the criminal to *step into the gap he himself had already dug* — quietly, choice by choice, in a life that looked, from the outside, blended in with everyone else's.

Al-Jalālayn catches exactly this. He notes that this command comes *"ʿinda ikhtilāṭihim bihim"* — **"at the moment of their being mixed in with them."** That is the whole picture. Until this instant, the guilty and the faithful were one crowd. Side by side. You could not tell them apart by looking. And then one word lands — *imtāzū* — and an invisible fault line that ran through that crowd the entire time suddenly becomes the only thing that is real.

Notice something. We spend our whole lives believing the masks hold. That blending in *is* belonging. The verb *imtāzū* is the moment the blending ends and every soul snaps to the place it actually occupied all along.

**What this one word reveals:** the separation of the Last Day is not an arbitrary punishment poured onto people from above. It is a *self-sorting* — a command to enact, in the open, the standing-apart you chose in secret.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *al-mujrimūn* — and the buried meaning of "crime"

Now look at *who* is addressed.

> أَيُّهَا ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ — **ayyuhā l-mujrimūn** — "O criminals!"

The word **mujrim** comes from the root **ج-ر-م (j-r-m)**. To us, in translation, it just means "criminal," "guilty one," "wrongdoer." A flat moral label. But the root underneath it is anything but flat — and once you see what is buried in it, this verse stops being a sequence of separate ideas and becomes a single, devastating image.

Let me teach the root before I apply it.

The lexicographers — al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt*, and the great *Lisān al-ʿArab* — trace **j-r-m** back to something concrete and physical: *jarama* originally means **to cut, to sever** — specifically, to cut the fruit or the dates *off* the tree. *Al-Rāghib says it plainly: aṣl al-jarm — qaṭʿ al-thamara ʿan al-shajar* — the root meaning of *jarm* is **the cutting of the fruit away from the tree.** Only later did the word travel from the orchard into the moral world, where *jurm* — a "crime" — came to mean any act that severs you from what is good.

Sit with that for a moment, because it is extraordinary.

A **mujrim** is, at the root, **a cutter. One who severs.** Every act of *jurm* is, in the deep grammar of the word, a small cutting — a snipping of one more thread between yourself and your Lord, yourself and the good, yourself and the people of the Garden.

And now — *now* — put the two words back together and read the verse as Allah built it:

> **imtāzū** ... **ayyuhā l-mujrimūn**
>
> "Stand apart ... O you who spent your lives cutting yourselves off."

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? **The command and the title are the same act.** The criminal is named by the very thing he is now commanded to do. *You severed — so now, sever yourselves off and stand there.* The punishment is not foreign to the sin. The punishment *is* the sin, finally allowed to finish its sentence. Every quiet cut in this life — the prayer abandoned, the truth refused, the warning shrugged off — was a strand severed. And the Day of Judgment is simply the moment all those cut strands fall away at once, and the soul that did the cutting stands alone, exactly as alone as it made itself.

This is *istidlāl* — Allah reasoning with us through something we can touch. A fruit cut from a branch withers; it had life only while it was attached. The *mujrim* is fruit that cut *itself* from the tree, and *imtāzū* is the word that finally lets it drop.

**What these two words reveal together:** the guilt of the criminal and the sentence of the criminal are not two things. The sin was a severing. The Day makes the severing visible. He is told to do, in one word, what he had been doing his whole life — only now there is no crowd left to hide the gap.

### Element Three: *al-yawm* — "THE Day," spoken in the present tense

There is a third word, and it is easy to read past. *Al-yawm.* "This Day." "Today."

> ٱلْيَوْمَ — **al-yawm**

Notice the definite article — *al-*. Not "a day." **The** Day. In Arabic, this definiteness points to a Day so singular, so universally known, that it needs no further description. There is only one Day that could be meant.

But here is the thing I want you to feel. The command is in the **imperative** — *imtāzū*, "stand apart" — and it is paired with *al-yawm*, "today." This is not a report about something that will happen. It is staged as something happening *right now*, in front of you, in the present.

The Qur'an does this deliberately. It takes the most distant future event and drags it into the grammatical *present* — *stand apart, today* — so that the listener, sitting here, centuries early, hears it as a live command. The Day is spoken as though the curtain has already gone up.

And that forces a question onto *us*, the living: **the verse is grammatically addressed to "the criminals" on the Day — but who is it really for?**

It is for the only people who can still do something about it. The ones being sorted on that Day cannot act anymore; their separating is finished. The address lands, in truth, on *us* — the ones still mixed in the crowd, still able to choose which side of the fault line we are quietly digging ourselves onto.

**What this word reveals:** the verse is a future event written in the present tense, aimed past its grammatical audience at the living listener. *Today* is not the Day of Judgment. *Today* is the day you still get to decide where you will be standing when that word is spoken.

### Bridge

So the construction has shown us three things that are really one thing. *Imtāzū* — *you* do the separating. *Al-mujrimūn* — you were always doing it; "criminal" means "one who severs." *Al-yawm* — and the verse speaks it as a live, present command aimed at whoever is still listening.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. What *is* this separation? And why should the mere word "stand apart" be among the most frightening things in the entire Qur'an?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The deepest punishment is not fire first — it is exclusion

Let me start where you actually live, far from any Day of Judgment.

Think of the worst pain you have felt that left no mark on your body. I would guess that if you go looking for it honestly, it has the shape of *exclusion*. The group chat you were quietly left out of. The table where the seats filled and none was yours. The moment you realized that the people you thought were *yours* had a closeness you were not part of. The child not picked for the team. The person standing at the edge of a circle that will not open to let them in.

Psychologists who study this have found something that should stop us: the brain registers social rejection in many of the same regions it registers physical pain. To be cast out genuinely *hurts* — not metaphorically. We are built, at the deepest level, as creatures who need to *belong*. And so the threat of being unbelonged — of being the one made to *stand apart* — is, for a human being, very close to the bottom of all fears.

Now hear the verse again with that in your chest:

> **"And stand apart this Day, O criminals!"**

Before the verse ever mentions fire — and Ya-Sin will mention the fire soon enough — the *first* word of punishment is **social.** It is exclusion. *Stand apart.* Get out of the crowd. You do not belong here, among these.

And the early commentators saw precisely this dimension. Qatāda, reflecting on *imtāzū*, says they were *"ʿuzilū ʿan kull khayr"* — **"set apart from every good."** Not from one good. From *every* good. The separating is total. Whatever warmth, whatever belonging, whatever nearness exists on that Day — the *mujrim* is told to step outside all of it and stand by himself.

[PAUSE]

But it goes deeper, and the commentators take us there. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk, as reported in both al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī, says the separating does not stop at "believers over here, criminals over there." He says **the criminals are separated even from one another** — this faction apart, that faction apart, each false allegiance pulled into its own corner. And in one narration he carries it to its end: that each one ends in a place sealed off, alone, *"lā yarā wa-lā yurā"* — **he neither sees nor is seen.**

Let that phrase land. *He neither sees nor is seen.* That is the terminus of *imtāzū*. Not a crowd of the damned suffering together — even *that* would be a kind of company. The separating keeps going until it reaches the absolute unit: one soul, unseen, seeing no one. The complete and final loneliness.

This is the thing dunya never lets us see clearly, because in dunya even the most isolated person is still surrounded by *something* — noise, strangers, the hum of a world. *Imtāzū* is the word that strips that away. It is the loneliness we have only ever tasted in small, terrifying sips, served at last as the whole cup.

**The core insight:** Hell, in its first and most human dimension, is *exclusion made permanent.* It is the eternal experience of being the one who does not belong, drawn out to the very edge and left there, unseen.

**And so, what does this change about tomorrow?** It reframes what *belonging* is for. Every time you choose the gathering of the faithful over the gathering that pulls you from your Lord — every time you sit in the circle of *dhikr*, of prayer, of good company, even when it is awkward, even when you feel like an outsider — you are *practicing belonging to the right crowd.* You are quietly sorting yourself toward *salām* and away from *imtāzū*. The masjid you drag yourself to on a cold morning is not just worship. It is you, deciding which crowd you want to be standing in when the word is finally spoken.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: No one is exiled on that Day — they only arrive where they already lived

Come back to the strangest feature of the verse, the one we flagged at the very start. Allah does not say *"I cast you out."* He says **imtāzū** — *you, separate yourselves.* The grammar refuses to make the criminal a victim of an external verdict. It hands him the verb.

Let me bring this into the world you know.

You have watched, perhaps, a friendship slowly end. Not in one explosion — those are rare. Usually it ends in a thousand tiny separations. A call not returned. A distance chosen, again and again, each time so small it hardly counts. And then one day you both look up and realize you are strangers — and *neither of you can name the moment it happened*, because it didn't happen in a moment. It happened in the accumulation of small, quiet self-removals.

That is *imtāzū* in this life.

We tell ourselves that we are *in* the crowd of the believers because we were born into it, because our name is Muslim, because we are physically standing among them. But belonging is not location. Belonging is *the direction of a thousand small choices.* And a person can stand in the front row of the prayer for forty years while every private choice quietly severs another strand — until, in the unseen reality of his heart, he is already standing apart. He just hasn't been *told* yet.

The Day of Judgment, then, does not *create* the separation. **It announces it.** *Imtāzū* is not a sentence handed down by a judge who could have ruled either way. It is a *revelation* — in the literal sense — an unveiling of a separation that was already complete. The masks that dunya allowed — the blending in, the going through the motions, standing in the crowd — are simply lifted. And every soul stands, exposed, exactly where it had been standing all along in the sight of Allah.

This is why the verb is reflexive. *You* did this. Not on that Day — over a lifetime. The Day merely says: *now stand where you put yourself.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the mercy hidden inside the terror — because there is always mercy hidden in the terror, if we look.

If the separation were imposed arbitrarily from outside, you could do nothing about it. You would be at the whim of a verdict you could not influence. But because the separation is something *you enact*, choice by choice — that means **it is still, today, in your hands.** The fault line is not yet fixed. Every prayer is a strand re-tied. Every turning back, every *tawba*, is a stitch back into the fabric you had been cutting yourself out of. *Al-yawm* — "today," in the verse's own present tense — is the window. The sorting is live, but it is not yet sealed.

The same Qur'an that says *imtāzū* to the severers says, elsewhere, that the One you are returning to is *Raḥīm* — and indeed that is the very word the believers in the verse just before were greeted with: *a word from a Merciful Lord.* The door to that crowd is open *now.* It is *imtāzū* that closes it — and *imtāzū* is spoken only when "today" has finally run out.

**The core insight:** You are not waiting to find out your verdict. You are *writing* it, quietly, in the direction of your daily choices. The Day does not decide where you belong. It only reads aloud what you have been deciding all along.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** it makes every ordinary, unwitnessed choice suddenly weighty — and that is a gift, not a burden. The small private act of integrity that no one sees, the sin refused in secret, the prayer prayed when you could have skipped it and no one would know — these are not minor. Each one is you, *re-stitching* yourself into the crowd of *salām*. Stop asking "am I a good enough Muslim?" as if it were a fixed score. Ask instead: *which way am I sorting myself, right now, in the thing no one sees?*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now I can show you the thing I asked you to hold at the very beginning — because now you are ready to see why it matters.

Go back to verse 58 and read it against verse 59, side by side, as Allah placed them:

> **36:58** — to the people of the Garden: *سَلَٰمٌ* — **"Peace** — a word from a Merciful Lord."
>
> **36:59** — to the others: *وَٱمْتَٰزُوا۟* — **"Stand apart,** O criminals!"

Look at what these two verses are. They are *two divine addresses, placed back to back, to two groups standing in one crowd.* And the entire difference between salvation and ruin is compressed into the *kind of word* each group receives.

The believers receive **salām** — a word that *closes distance.* It is greeting, nearness, the Lord Himself speaking *to* them. The motion of the word is *toward.*

The criminals receive **imtāzū** — a word that *opens distance.* Stand apart. Step back. The motion of the word is *away.*

[PAUSE]

And *that* is the architecture. That is what the whole sorting actually is. The Day of Judgment, stripped to its bones, is a **sorting by proximity to Allah.** One crowd is drawn near and addressed with intimacy; the other is told to recede into the dark. Paradise, at its heart, is nearness. Hell, at its heart, is *imtāzū* — distance, exclusion, the unseen and unseeing aloneness.

And the two roots we opened tell you *how* each group arrived. The believers, who in this life drew *near* — turned toward, gathered, attached themselves to the tree — are met with the word of nearness. The *mujrimūn*, whose very name means *those who cut, who sever, who detached* — are met with the word of detachment. **You came near, so come nearer. You cut yourself off, so stand off.** The end perfectly answers the means. The grammar of the Last Day is just the honest grammar of a life, read back.

### Bringing It Together

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes turn out to be a single insight approached from two sides.

The grammar told us: *imtāzū* is reflexive — you separate yourself; *mujrim* means one who severs; *al-yawm* speaks it as a live command to the living.

The themes told us: the worst punishment is exclusion, and no one is exiled on that Day — they only arrive, at last, where their own choices had already placed them.

These are the same truth. **The sin was a severing we performed quietly, strand by strand. The Day of Judgment is simply the moment we are commanded to stand in the separation we ourselves made — while the believers, who spent their lives drawing near, are met with a single word of peace.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When you feel like an outsider in a gathering of the faithful — awkward, not quite belonging — do you step *toward* it anyway, or do you quietly let the distance grow?
2. In the choices no one witnesses, which direction are you sorting yourself — toward the crowd of *salām*, or toward the edge?
3. If your private life were unveiled today, as it will be unveiled on *that* Day — which word would honestly fit you: *come near*, or *stand apart*?
4. You have tasted exclusion in small doses. Have you ever let that taste teach you to fear the *eternal* version of it — and to act before "today" runs out?
5. What is one strand, cut long ago, that you could begin re-tying this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The sin was a severing you performed quietly in life, and the Day of Judgment is simply the moment Allah commands you to stand in the separation you already chose — while those who drew near to Him are met with a single word: *Peace.*

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what I would invite to shift — gently, not as a weight laid on you, but as a door shown to you.

Stop imagining the Last Day as a verdict that will be *done to* you. Start hearing *imtāzū* as a verb you are conjugating right now, in this life, in the direction of your smallest unseen choices. The crowd of *salām* has an open door *today.* Every prayer, every turning back, every act of quiet integrity is one strand re-tied to the tree you were made to belong to.

You do not have to wait to find out which word you will hear. You are choosing it. Choose *nearness.*

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who greet Your beloved with *salām* — do not let us be among those told to stand apart. We have cut so many strands; tie us back to You. And when that Day comes and the crowd is sorted, place us in the gathering that hears the word of peace — *a word from a Merciful Lord.* Āmīn.
