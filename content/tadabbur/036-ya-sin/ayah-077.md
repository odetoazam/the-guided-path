# From a Drop to a Disputant

## The Silence That Learned to Argue Back

---

> [ayah:36:77] أَوَلَمْ يَرَ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَـٰهُ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ فَإِذَا هُوَ خَصِيمٌ مُّبِينٌ

*Awa lam yara l-insānu annā khalaqnāhu min nuṭfatin — fa-idhā huwa khaṣīmun mubīn.*

**"Does the human being not see that We created him from a mere drop — and then, behold, he is an open adversary, arguing out loud?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me put you in the scene. Not the comfortable scene of a person reading this on a screen, calm, fed, safe. The other scene. The original one.

A man is standing in the streets of Makkah. In his hand he holds a bone. Old. Weathered. The kind of bone you find in the desert that has lain out under the sun for years until it has gone chalky and grey, until it crumbles if you press it. And he is pressing it. He is crushing it between his fingers, letting the powder drift down into the wind, and he is laughing. He turns to the Prophet ﷺ and he says — and you can hear the contempt in it — *"Do you really claim that God will bring THIS back to life, after it has rotted away to nothing?"*

That is the man in this ayah. The classical commentators preserve more than one name for him — Ibn ʿAbbās names ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr names al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil, and al-Ḥasan and others name Ubayy ibn Khalaf, the one with the bone. We don't need to settle which. Because here is the thing the Qurʾān does, and I want you to feel the genius of it before we even open a single word.

Allah does not call him by his name.

He calls him *al-insān*. **The human being.** The species. You.

[PAUSE]

The man thought he was making a clever argument about biology — *how can rotted matter live again?* And Allah's reply, which begins here in ayah 77 and detonates in the ayah right after it, is not to argue chemistry with him. It is to turn him around to face a mirror and ask one quiet, devastating question.

*Where do you think YOU came from?*

This is the closing movement of Sūrat Yā-Sīn. The whole surah has been a gallery of signs — the dead earth that turns green again, the night that Allah peels off the day like skin off a fruit, the sun running along its course to a resting place it cannot miss. Sign after sign after sign of a power that gives life to what was lifeless. And now, at the end, Allah stops pointing at the horizon and points at the listener's own body. At the most intimate, most undignified, most easily-forgotten fact about him.

You started as a drop.

Here is the hook. Hold onto it for the next hour. **The same human being who cannot remember that he began as a single drop of fluid — that same being has become the most articulate, most confident, loudest arguer in all of creation.** And the ayah is going to show us, word by word, that this is not two unrelated facts. It is one tragedy. The silence and the shouting are connected. And by the time we see how, the bone crumbling in that man's hand is going to look very, very different.

Let's begin.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: نُّطْفَةٍ — The Word That Drips

### Teaching the concept first

Before we touch the ayah, I want to tell you something about how Arabic builds certain words. In Arabic — far more than in English — the *sound* of a word can carry its meaning. The mouth performs the thing the word describes. There is a word for a whip cracking, *sawṭ*, and you can almost hear the snap in it. There are words for whispering that you have to whisper to say properly.

So when we come to a word, one of the questions a careful reader asks is: *what is my mouth doing when I say this? And is that motion telling me something?*

Hold that.

### Opening the word

Allah says He created him **مِن نُّطْفَةٍ** — *min nuṭfah.*

Almost every translation gives you "from a sperm-drop" or "from a drop of fluid," and that is correct. But it is the dead, clinical version. Let's bring the word back to life.

The root is **ن-ط-ف** (n-ṭ-f). And the core meaning of this root, in the classical lexicons, has nothing to do with biology at all. *Naṭafa* means **to trickle. To drip. To ooze.** A *nuṭfah* in the old Arabic was the small amount of water left at the bottom of a bucket or a waterskin — the residue, the few drops clinging there that you would tip out and that would be gone before they hit the sand. The leftover. The trace amount.

Say it slowly. *Nuṭ-fah.* Feel where it happens. The *ṭ* taps once against the roof of the mouth — a single tap, like a single drop falling — and then the *fah* releases and is gone. The word itself drips and vanishes. **The sound performs the smallness.**

[PAUSE]

### Sitting with it

Now notice something the grammar is doing that the English completely hides. In Arabic, *nuṭfah* here is **indefinite** — *min nuṭfatin*, "from *a* drop," not "the drop." This is not laziness. Indefiniteness in this position carries the sense of *taqlīl* — belittling, diminishing. It is "from some drop." From a nothing. From a trickle so insignificant it doesn't even earn a definite article.

So Allah has chosen, out of every possible way to describe human origin, the word for the most negligible, most easily-spilled, most quickly-forgotten quantity of liquid in the desert imagination. He could have said "We created you" and stopped. He could have named the womb, the months, the forming. Instead He goes all the way back, past all of that, to the single trickling drop. The residue at the bottom of the bucket.

Why? Because the man with the bone was standing tall. He was full of himself, full of his own permanence, looking down at a crumbling bone and saying *this is nothing, this can never live.* And Allah's answer is to reach back into that man's own history and hold up the trickle he came from. *You want to talk about nothing that becomes something? Look in the mirror, you were a trickle.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because here is where the physical image opens into the unseen reality. The *nuṭfah* is not really about reproductive biology. It is a stepping stone. What it is pointing at is a spiritual fact that the human cannot bear to look at directly: **your existence is borrowed, recent, and small.** You did not author yourself. There was a "before-you" so total that you were not even a person — you were a few drops of fluid that a person could have wiped away without noticing. That is the *real* subject. The *nuṭfah* is just the place Allah makes you stand so you can finally see it.

What this single word reveals: **the arrogance of the human is built on amnesia, and the cure Allah prescribes is memory of the drop.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Two: فَإِذَا — The Hinge of Sudden Shock

### Teaching the concept first

Now I need to teach you one small piece of Arabic grammar, because if you don't have it, the most dramatic moment of this ayah will pass you by completely. If you do have it, you will never read this verse the same way again.

In Arabic there are two different words that both get translated into English as "when" or "then": *idhā* and *idh*. But there is a special, electric use of *idhā* that grammarians give its own name: **إذا الفجائية** — *idhā al-fujāʾiyyah*, "the *idhā* of suddenness," the *idhā* of surprise.

Here is how it works in ordinary speech. Imagine you say: *I opened the door — and there, suddenly, was a lion.* That little jolt, that "and there, suddenly" — that ambush of the unexpected — Arabic packs into one word: *fa-idhā.* It is the grammar of the plot twist. It always comes with a shock. You expect one thing; *fa-idhā* — and behold, the opposite.

We even gesture it. *fa-idhā!* — hands thrown up.

### Opening the word

Now look at the spine of our ayah:

> *khalaqnāhu min nuṭfatin* — **فَإِذَا** — *huwa khaṣīmun mubīn.*
> "We created him from a drop — **fa-idhā** — behold, he is an open adversary."

That *fa-idhā* is the *idhā al-fujāʾiyyah*. It is the hinge of the entire verse. And I want you to feel what it is doing.

### Sitting with it

Notice what Allah has placed on either side of this hinge.

On the left side: *nuṭfah.* A silent drop. The most passive, most helpless, most voiceless thing imaginable. A drop cannot speak. A drop cannot argue. A drop cannot object to anything; it cannot even hold its own shape.

On the right side: *khaṣīmun mubīn.* An open, declared, articulate adversary. Loud. Confident. Arguing.

And between them — not a long sentence, not a description of the decades it actually takes, not the years of nursing and growing and learning to speak — between them sits one word: *fa-idhā.* "And **suddenly** — behold."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the grammar is doing to time? Biologically, the journey from drop to grown arguing man takes twenty, thirty, forty years. It is the slowest, most gradual process there is. But Allah collapses all of it into the grammar of *instant shock.* *fa-idhā.* As if you blink — drop — and open your eyes — and there he is, full-grown, hands on his hips, **arguing with the One who made him from the drop.**

Why collapse it? Because that is exactly how it feels from Allah's vantage. The ingratitude is so total, the leap from what you were to how you behave is so obscene, that the grammar refuses to dignify it with a slow, sympathetic timeline. It just throws up its hands. *He was a drop and — behold! — he's an adversary.* The suddenness IS the rebuke. The shock in the grammar is the shock that *anyone who remembered the drop could ever stand and argue.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the unseen reality the little word is pointing toward. We think our development was gradual, earned, ours. We feel continuous with ourselves — *I have always basically been me.* The *fa-idhā* shatters that illusion. From the standpoint of the One who was there before the drop, your entire becoming was a single sudden gift, handed to you whole. **You experience your life as a long earning. Allah is showing you it was an instant giving.** And the one who forgets it was a gift is exactly the one who turns around and argues.

What this word reveals: **the distance between what you were and what you became is so vast that only a miracle could have crossed it — and you crossed it without once noticing the Miracle-Worker.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: خَصِيمٌ مُّبِينٌ — When the Gift Becomes the Weapon

### Teaching the concept first

One more piece, and this one is about the *shape* of words. In Arabic, the same root can be poured into different patterns, and the pattern changes the intensity. There is a difference between *one who disputes* and *one who is by his very nature a disputer.* The grammarians call the intense, characteristic, built-in version *mubālaghah* — exaggeration, the trait baked in.

There is a pattern, *faʿīl*, that often does exactly this: it makes the quality not an occasional action but a defining identity. *ʿalīm* is not "one who knows something" — it is "deeply, characteristically knowing." *raḥīm* is not "one who showed mercy once" — it is mercy as nature.

Hold that pattern in mind.

### Opening the words

Allah does not say the human became *khāṣim* — merely "a disputant." He says **خَصِيمٌ** — *khaṣīm*, on that intense *faʿīl* pattern. Not someone who happens to argue. Someone whose **arguing has become his nature.** A born adversary. The lexicons even note *khaṣīm* describes one *skilled* in dispute — not just hostile, but *good at it.* Effective. Persuasive in his opposition.

And then one more word: **مُّبِينٌ** — *mubīn.*

### Sitting with it

Now, *mubīn* is the active participle of the Form IV verb *abāna*, from the root **ب-ي-ن** (b-y-n) — the root of *bayān*, clarity, articulate speech, the power to make meaning plain. And here the classical commentators give us two faces of one reality, and we should hold both, the way you hold a coin and know it is a single coin.

Al-Qurṭubī reads *mubīn* as **مبين للحجة** — he *makes his argument clear.* The articulacy points outward: he constructs, he explains, he makes a case.

Al-Baghawī reads it as **بيِّن الخصومة** — his *hostility itself* is plain, manifest, undisguised. The clarity points at the man himself: there is no hiding it, his opposition is right out in the open.

Put the two faces together and you get the full portrait: **a being who argues with skill, and whose argument is unmistakably, openly aimed against his own Maker.** Articulate *and* exposed. Eloquent *and* caught in the act.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where I need you to slow all the way down, because this is the deepest thing in the verse, and it is hidden in plain sight.

Where did this man get *bayān*? Where did he get the power of clear speech that he is now using to argue against resurrection?

He got it from the same One who made him from the drop.

[PAUSE]

Allah says elsewhere, *ar-Raḥmān. ʿallama l-Qurʾān. khalaqa l-insān. ʿallamahu l-bayān* — "The Most Merciful taught the Qurʾān, created the human, taught him *al-bayān*, articulate speech" (55:1-4). Speech is a *mercy.* It is listed among the first gifts of *ar-Raḥmān.* The drop could not speak. The trickle at the bottom of the bucket had no voice, no argument, no clarity. And Allah, out of *raḥmah*, lifted that voiceless drop and gave it *bayān* — the most extraordinary faculty in creation, the power to form thoughts into sounds into meanings into worlds.

And what does *mubīn* tell us the human did with that gift?

**He aimed it back at the Giver.**

The very faculty of clear speech that Allah gifted to a silent drop — the human took that gift, turned around, and made it the weapon of his denial. *Mubīn* — "clearly, articulately" — is not praising his eloquence. It is indicting it. It is saying: *look how well I taught him to speak. Look how clearly he can now make a point. And look what point he chose to make.*

[PAUSE — long]

This is the unseen reality the words have been climbing toward all along. The tragedy of the human is not that he is weak. It is that he takes every single thing that was *given* to him — existence, growth, voice, intellect, clarity — and uses the gifts to deny the Giver. The drop was given a mouth, and the first thing the mouth did was argue that there is no resurrection, no return, no accounting. *Khaṣīmun mubīn.* The clearest possible speaker, making the clearest possible case, against the One who taught him to speak at all.

What these two words reveal: **ingratitude is not the absence of gifts. It is the turning of the gifts into weapons against the One who gave them.**

[PAUSE — major]

## The Bridge

Look at what these three words have built, standing in a row.

*Nuṭfah* — you began as a negligible drop.
*Fa-idhā* — and suddenly, as if by a miracle you never noticed, you were transformed.
*Khaṣīmun mubīn* — into the loudest, most articulate arguer against the One who transformed you.

That is the entire arc of a human life compressed into ten words. The grammar is not just describing a person. It is performing his biography. It begins small and silent and ends loud and defiant, and the whole thing is held together by a hinge of forgotten miracle.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About you. About the quiet ways this exact arc is living inside every one of us right now.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Amnesia of Origins

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of person we have all met, and if we are honest, have all *been.* The person who "made themselves." The self-made one. They will tell you about their success, their intelligence, their independence — and somewhere in the telling, the people who carried them disappear. The mother who went without so they could eat. The teacher who saw something in them at age nine. The thousand strangers whose labor laid the road they drove to success on. None of them appear in the story. In the story, there is only *I.* "I built this. I earned this. I am where I am because of me."

We have a name for this in modern psychology. We call it the **self-serving bias** — the deeply human tendency to attribute every success to our own agency and every failure to outside forces. We are, all of us, wired to write ourselves as the author of our own good fortune and the victim of our own bad.

Now hold that ordinary, recognizable human pattern up against this ayah. Because Allah is naming the *root* of it. The self-made myth is not a small social flaw. It is a theological condition. It is what happens when *al-insān* forgets the *nuṭfah.*

[PAUSE]

Think about it concretely. You did not choose to exist. You did not assemble yourself in the womb. You did not decide on your heartbeat, design your eyes, or string together the four billion letters of your own genetic code. You were a drop. And then, through a process you neither understood nor controlled nor even witnessed, you became a person. Every single thing that makes you *you* — your face, your voice, your capacity to read these words and be moved by them — was *given.* You were a passive recipient of the most extraordinary gift in the universe: yourself.

And yet how do we walk through the world? As owners. As authors. As people who are owed. We are offended when things go wrong, as though existence were a contract we signed and the terms are being violated. We forget, every single day, that we are walking, talking, arguing *trickles of water* that were lifted into personhood by a mercy we did nothing to deserve.

[PAUSE]

The Qurʾān returns to this wound again and again, because it is *the* wound. In Sūrat al-Qiyāmah: *"Does the human think he will be left without purpose? Was he not a drop of emitted fluid?"* (75:36-37). Same word — *nuṭfah.* Same argument. The forgetting of the drop is the beginning of the delusion that you are unaccountable, that you answer to no one, that this is all just *yours* to do with as you please.

Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing to fix it. The more capable we become, the *harder* it gets to remember the drop. The infant cannot forget its helplessness — it lives it every moment. But the competent adult, the successful one, the articulate one — he is the one most at risk. His very gifts become the veil. The stronger Allah makes him, the easier it is for him to forget that the strength was a gift. **The blessing becomes the blindfold.**

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practice, and it is small, and it is ancient, and it works. The believer is taught to begin things with *Bismillāh* — "in the name of Allah" — and to end them with *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — "all praise belongs to Allah." We say these so often they go numb. But hear them now in the light of the *nuṭfah.* *Bismillāh* before you eat is the drop remembering it did not grow the wheat. *Al-ḥamdu lillāh* after you succeed is the drop remembering it did not author its own success. These little phrases are not religious decoration. They are *spiritual antibodies against the amnesia of origins.* Every one of them is the human being saying: *I remember I was a drop. I remember this was given.*

Tomorrow, say one of them and actually mean it. Just once. Pick one moment of competence — closing the deal, acing the exam, holding your child — and at that exact moment of *I did this*, whisper *al-ḥamdu lillāh* and let it correct the record. That is the drop remembering itself.

[PAUSE — major]

## Theme Two: The Voice That Was Given, and What We Aim It At

Now the second theme, and it is the most personal thing in this verse.

Let me ask you to consider the single greatest power you possess. Not your strength — animals are stronger. Not your speed. Your **voice.** Your capacity to take the invisible weather inside your skull — thoughts, feelings, arguments, intentions — and convert it into sound, and have that sound reconstruct itself as meaning inside another human mind. That is *bayān.* It is, genuinely, a miracle we have completely stopped noticing. There is nothing else like it in the known universe. A drop of water learned to *make a case.*

And here is the modern doorway in. Think about the last argument you had. A real one. With a spouse, a parent, a colleague, a stranger on the internet. Now ask yourself honestly: *in that argument, what was I trying to do?* Were you trying to find the truth? Or were you trying to *win?* Were you using your words to *uncover* what was real — or to *defeat* the person across from you, to be *mubīn,* clearly and articulately right, regardless of whether you actually were?

[PAUSE]

We are all, by nature, *khaṣīmun mubīn.* Skilled, articulate adversaries. We are *good* at arguing. We can construct a justification for almost anything we want to do. We can make the weaker case sound like the stronger one. We can talk ourselves out of every obligation and into every desire. The faculty of *bayān* — that gift to a silent drop — has become, in most of us, most of the time, primarily a tool for *self-justification* and *winning.*

And the man in this ayah is just the extreme case. He took the highest possible use of speech — speaking *about ultimate reality, about life and death and God* — and used it to crumble a bone and sneer. He aimed the telescope of his eloquence at the deepest question a creature can ask, and used it to argue *against his own return to his Maker.*

[PAUSE]

Now sit inside the asymmetry, because this is where it becomes unbearable in the best way.

Allah gave the drop a voice **freely.** There was no obligation. He, *ar-Raḥmān,* simply *taught him al-bayān* — gave it as mercy, the way you give a gift to someone you love. And the verse shows us what the gift was used for: *khaṣīmun mubīn,* arguing, openly, against the Giver.

Imagine giving someone the most precious thing you own — and watching them turn it into the instrument they use to deny you. That is not just disobedience. It is a specific, intimate kind of betrayal. And the staggering thing — the thing that should break us open — is that *He keeps giving the voice anyway.* The man crumbling the bone is still breathing with lungs Allah is inflating. Still speaking with a tongue Allah is moving. Still alive, in the very act of denying the One who keeps him alive. **The voice arguing against the Giver is, in that very instant, on loan from the Giver.**

[PAUSE — long]

That is *ḥilm.* Forbearance. Allah does not snatch the voice back mid-blasphemy. He lets the argument finish. He gives us the rope and the time and the breath. Not because the argument is winning — the very next ayah obliterates it — but because His mercy outlasts our ingratitude, and He is giving the *khaṣīm* every possible moment to remember the drop and fall silent in gratitude before the silence is enforced.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practice. Before you speak in your next disagreement — and you will have one — ask one question: *Am I about to use this voice to uncover the truth, or to win?* Because the same faculty does both. *Bayān* can be the instrument of *dhikr* — remembering Allah, speaking truth, defending the wronged, saying the kind thing. Or it can be the instrument of *khuṣūmah* — winning, justifying, defeating, denying. It is the identical gift. The only question is which direction you aim it.

The drop was given a mouth. Tomorrow, in one conversation, point it back toward the Giver instead of against Him. Say the grateful word. Concede the point you know is true even though it costs you the win. Use the voice the way it was meant to be used. That is the *khaṣīm* learning, at last, what his clarity was *for.*

[PAUSE — major]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Now let me show you what was hidden in the verse's own shape the whole time.

Read it again and watch the *altitude* of each word:

**أَوَلَمْ يَرَ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ** — *does the human not SEE* (the faculty of perception, given)
**أَنَّا خَلَقْنَـٰهُ** — *that WE created him* (the act of giving)
**مِن نُّطْفَةٍ** — *from a DROP* — [the floor. The lowest point. Silent, small, indefinite, negligible.]
**فَإِذَا** — *and suddenly* — [the hinge. The unwitnessed miracle.]
**هُوَ خَصِيمٌ مُّبِينٌ** — *behold, an OPEN, ARTICULATE ADVERSARY* — [the ceiling. Loud, skilled, definite, defiant.]

[PAUSE]

The verse *climbs.* It begins at the absolute floor of existence — a drop — and it ends at the loud ceiling of human self-assertion — an open arguer. And it makes that entire ascent in ten words, hinged on a single particle of sudden shock.

That climb is not just the structure of the sentence. **It is the structure of a human life that has forgotten its Maker.** We all start at the floor — helpless, voiceless, given everything. And too many of us end at that ceiling — loud, capable, arguing, certain, owing no one anything. The verse is a staircase, and Allah built it so you could see, in one glance, the entire distance a soul travels from the drop it was to the disputant it became — and feel how that whole vertiginous climb was *His* doing, gift stacked upon gift, right up to the voice now being used against Him.

The architecture *is* the argument. The verse makes you ascend the very staircase it is describing, so that when you reach the top and find the *khaṣīm mubīn* standing there shouting, you realize with a jolt — *that's me. I climbed here on His gifts. And look what I'm doing with the view.*

## Bringing It Together

The three words and the two themes are one insight seen from two sides.

The *nuṭfah* taught us we are recent, borrowed, and small — and the theme of **amnesia** showed us that forgetting this is the root of all our self-made delusion.

The *khaṣīmun mubīn* taught us our voice was a gift — and the theme of **the weaponized gift** showed us that ingratitude is not lacking blessings but turning them against the Blesser.

And the *fa-idhā* — the hinge — is where they meet. The suddenness of the miracle we never witnessed is *exactly why* we forget it was a gift, and the forgetting is *exactly what* frees us to aim the gift back at the Giver. Amnesia and ingratitude are not two sins. They are one sin, breathing in and breathing out.

## Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. When was the last time I genuinely remembered — *felt* — that I began as a drop I did not author?
2. In my arguments, am I a *khaṣīm* seeking to win, or a servant seeking the truth? Which one am I more skilled at?
3. What gift has Allah given me that I am currently, perhaps unknowingly, using *against* its purpose?
4. The voice in my head that justifies what I want to do anyway — whose voice is that, and what am I aiming it at?
5. If my entire becoming was a single sudden gift, what would change about how I carry myself through one ordinary day?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The being who cannot remember he was a silent drop has become the loudest arguer in creation — and the very voice he argues with was the Giver's first mercy to the drop.**

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need to do anything large. The verse is not asking you to become someone else. It is asking you to *remember* who you already were — a drop — and to let that memory quietly correct the posture of your soul. Not into shame. Into *gratitude.* The opposite of the *khaṣīm* is not the silent one. It is the *grateful* one — the one who still has the voice, still has the clarity, still has the skill in speech, and now aims all of it back toward the One who gave it. The drop does not have to stay silent. It just has to remember to say *thank You.*

## A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, You made me from a drop when I was nothing, and You taught me to speak when I had no voice. Forgive me for every word I have aimed against You instead of toward You. Let me never forget the drop, and let the voice You gave me become an instrument of Your remembrance and not Your denial — until the day I return to You, grateful and silent, the way I began.*

*Āmīn.*
