# The Sleepers Who Hasten Out

## Introduction: The Moment the Argument Stops

Let me set the Arabic before you first, and then we will walk into it slowly together.

> وَنُفِخَ فِى ٱلصُّورِ فَإِذَا هُم مِّنَ ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَنسِلُونَ
>
> *wa-nufikha fī al-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina-l-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūn*
>
> **"And the trumpet will be blown, and at once — from the graves — to their Lord they hasten out."**

> قَالُوا۟ يَٰوَيْلَنَا مَنۢ بَعَثَنَا مِن مَّرْقَدِنَا ۜ هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ وَصَدَقَ ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ
>
> *qālū yā-waylanā man baʿathanā min marqadinā — hādhā mā waʿada-r-raḥmānu wa-ṣadaqa-l-mursalūn*
>
> **"They will say: 'O woe to us! Who has raised us from our place of sleep?' — This is what the Most Merciful promised, and the messengers told the truth."**

I want you to feel where these words land in the surah, because the placement is everything.

For most of Ya-Sin, Allah has been *arguing*. He has been making a case. Look at the dead earth — We sent water and it came alive; will you not see the resurrection in that? Look at the night, how We strip the day off it like skin off a fruit and you are left in darkness. Look at the sun running, the moon worn thin until it returns like an old curved branch. The whole surah is a courtroom of signs. And the people on the other side of the argument have one move, one sneer, which the surah quotes a little later: *"Who will give life to bones when they have rotted away?"* They are not asking a question. They are closing a door.

[PAUSE]

And then — these two ayahs.

Notice what Allah does. He does not give them a better argument. He does not say, *let Me explain the mechanism more carefully.* He does something far more devastating. He **stops arguing and simply shows you the thing happening.** The trumpet sounds. The ground opens. And suddenly you are no longer reading a debate about resurrection — you are *standing inside it*, watching dust-people pour out of the earth, confused, hurrying, blinking, asking the most human question imaginable: *what just happened to us?*

Here is the hook I want to plant in you, and I am not going to answer it yet. These people wake up terrified. They cry out *yā waylanā* — "oh, our ruin." It is the cry of someone who has just realized something catastrophic is true. And the answer that comes back to them — the single most surprising word in these two ayahs — is a name. Not a name you would expect at the gates of Judgment. Not *the Avenger*. Not *the Almighty*. Not even, simply, *Allah*.

A different name entirely.

Hold onto that. We have to earn our way to it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Passive That Hides the Blower, and the Particle That Skips the Wait

Let me teach you something about language before we touch the ayah — something you already know but have never named.

When you want to make an event feel **enormous and beyond control**, you take the doer out of the sentence. Compare two ways of saying the same thing. "The general gave the order, the cannons fired, and the city fell." Versus: "The order was given. The cannons fired. The city fell." The second one is colder, bigger, more like fate. Why? Because no human hand is visible in it. Things simply *happen*. In Arabic this is the passive voice — *al-majhūl*, literally "the unknown one," because the doer is left unknown.

Now open the first word.

> **وَنُفِخَ** — *wa-nufikha* — "and it was blown."

This is a passive verb, root *n-f-kh*, the act of blowing air. And Allah does not tell us **who** blows. We know from other passages it is the angel at the trumpet. But here, at this exact instant, the blower is erased from the grammar. [PAUSE]

Sit with this. Why hide the angel?

Because the moment is not about *an actor performing a task*. It is about an event so total that it falls on the entire creation at once, like a verdict no one can appeal. If Allah had said "and the angel blew the trumpet," your mind would picture a being, a figure, a someone you might reason with or run from. By removing him, the blowing becomes **weather**. It becomes climate. It becomes the unarguable fact of the sky itself. The sound is just *there*, and there is no one in the frame to negotiate with.

And then comes a tiny word that does something extraordinary with time.

> **فَإِذَا** — *fa-idhā*.

Arabic has a special particle the grammarians call *idhā al-fujā'iyya* — "the *idhā* of suddenness," the *idhā* of "and behold!" It is the linguistic equivalent of a hard cut in a film. No dissolve, no fade. One frame the screen is black; the next frame, full daylight, crowds everywhere. *Fa-idhā* means: **and all at once, with no gap, there they are.**

Now feel what these two features do *together*. The trumpet is blown — agent hidden, vast, weather-like. And *fa-idhā* — instantly, with no interval, no rising-from-sleep montage, no slow groan of the earth — **there they are**, already out, already hurrying. The grammar refuses to let any time pass.

Here is what is remarkable, and it connects to the second ayah in a way you will feel like a click. To the One blowing, and to us watching, the resurrection is *instant* — a hard cut. And to the people inside it, as we are about to see, it will *also* feel instant, but from the inside: it will feel like they shut their eyes one second ago. **The grammar of suddenness on the outside matches the experience of sleep on the inside.** Hold that thread.

What this single particle reveals: between death and standing before God, there is — experientially — no duration at all. The whole vast graveyard of human history is collapsed into the space between two heartbeats.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: يَنسِلُونَ — The Verb That Means "To Be Born"

Now the word that, for me, breaks the whole scene open.

> **يَنسِلُونَ** — *yansilūn* — "they hasten out."

Root: *n-s-l*. Let me teach the concept before I let the word do its work.

In Arabic, a single root is a *family*. Every word grown from it shares a common genetic core of meaning, even when the words seem to drift far apart. So before we decide what *yansilūn* "means" in translation, we ask: **what else grows from n-s-l?**

And here is the answer that should make you sit up. From this exact root comes the word **نَسْل** — *nasl* — which means **offspring. Progeny. Children. Descendants.** The continuation of life through birth.

The classical lexicographers tie these together directly. Al-Baghawī, commenting on this very ayah, notes that a child is called *nasl* — *li-khurūjihi min baṭni ummih* — "because of its *coming out* from the belly of its mother." The core of the root is **the quick emergence of new life from an enclosure.**

Now read the ayah again with that in your bones:

*mina-l-ajdāthi... yansilūn* — "from the graves... they come hastening out."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done? He did not say "they climb out" or "they crawl out" or even "they are dragged out." He chose the one verb whose deepest family-resonance is **birth**. The graves are not tombs in this image. They are **wombs**. The earth has been pregnant with the dead this whole time, and at the trumpet's sound it goes into labor, and out they come — fast, helpless, blinking at a light they have never seen, exactly as a newborn does.

And the verb is in the **imperfect tense** — *yansilūn*, the ongoing, unfolding, happening-right-now form, not the completed past. Arabic could have used a finished past tense: "they came out." Instead it uses the form that puts the camera *inside the event as it streams*. You are not reading a report of something that finished. You are watching bodies pour from the soil in real time, continuously, a river of them — *and it is still happening as you watch.*

There is one more layer. *Yansilūn* carries the sense of **moving quickly, hastening, with the eager hurry of something that cannot do otherwise** — the way water rushes, the way a foal stumbles up onto its legs within minutes of being born. These dead do not shuffle reluctantly toward judgment. The verb gives them **velocity.** They are pulled — toward what?

> **إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ** — *ilā rabbihim* — "**to their Lord.**"

Look at where the hastening points. Not "to the gathering-place." Not "to the reckoning." *Ilā rabbihim* — to their **Lord**, their *Rabb* — and *Rabb* is the One who nurtured, sustained, owned, and raised them like a master raises a household or a gardener raises a plant. The newborn rushes, by a gravity it does not choose, **toward the One who made it.** Even the deniers. Even those who spent a lifetime facing the other way. At the trumpet, every face turns the same direction, and every body runs the same way: home.

What this one verb reveals: **resurrection is not the end of life — it is a second birth.** The first time you were born, you came out of darkness, hastening, terrified, into a world your makers had prepared. So will it be the second time. You have done this before. You simply do not remember.

[PAUSE — let it land]

### Element Three: مَرْقَدِنَا — When the Sleepers Call Death "Our Nap"

Now we cross into the second ayah, and into the word that I think is the most psychologically tender in the entire passage.

The people wake. They cry out. And they ask:

> مَن بَعَثَنَا مِن **مَّرْقَدِنَا** — *man baʿathanā min marqadinā* — "Who has raised us from our **marqad**?"

Let me teach the form of the word, because the *shape* carries the meaning.

The root is *r-q-d*, which means **to sleep, to lie down in slumber.** Now Arabic has a noun-pattern called *ism al-makān* — the "noun of place" — built on the mold *maf'al* (*ma-...-a-...*). When you pour a verb's root into this mold, you get **the place where that action happens.** From the root for "sitting," you get *majlis* — the place of sitting. From "prostrating," *masjid* — the place of prostration, the mosque. And from *r-q-d*, "to sleep," you get **مَرْقَد — *marqad* — the place of sleeping. The bed. The resting-spot. The bedroom.**

So read what the dead actually say as they stumble out. They do not say, "Who raised us from death?" They do not say, "Who raised us from the grave?" They say:

**"Who woke us from our *bed*?"**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it is almost unbearably gentle and terrible at the same time.

From the inside, **death felt like a nap.** That is the disclosure. The grammar of suddenness we found in *fa-idhā* — the hard cut, the no-gap — is now confirmed from inside the experience of the dead themselves. To the corpse in the ground, the centuries did not pass. There was the moment of dying — like the moment your head hits the pillow and the light goes out — and then, *immediately*, this. The trumpet. The waking. To them, no time elapsed at all. Death was the briefest sleep. *We just lay down. Who woke us so soon?*

The Companions and early commentators noticed this is the cry of people genuinely disoriented — the confusion of someone shaken awake from deep sleep, who for a few seconds does not know where they are, what year it is, what is happening. Multiply that disorientation by the scale of eternity, and you have *yā waylanā* — the cry torn out of a soul that is realizing, even as it asks the question, that the answer is catastrophic for it.

And here is the sonic detail. Listen to the word: **mar-qad.** It is soft. It has none of the harshness of a word for *tomb* or *pit*. It is a word you could whisper to a child at bedtime. Allah lets the dead use the *softest possible word* for the place they have been — and that softness is exactly what makes the next words crack like thunder.

Because someone answers them.

### The Bridge: From the Word to the Reality

So far the language has shown us three things, each grounded in the shape of the words themselves. The **passive blowing and the particle of suddenness** told us this event falls like weather, with no gap in time. The verb **yansilūn** told us resurrection is a *birth* — the earth in labor, the dead hastening out like newborns toward their Lord. And **marqad** told us that, from the inside, all of death felt like a single night's sleep.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About *your* death, which is coming, and about who will be standing there when you open your eyes.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Your Death Is a Nap You Will Not Notice

Let me begin where you already live.

You have, at some point, fallen asleep exhausted and woken with a jolt, certain only a few minutes had passed — and looked at the clock to find it was morning. Eight hours. Gone. Not experienced. You were not *bored* for eight hours. You did not *wait*. The time simply did not exist for you. One moment, darkness; the next, light through the curtains, and the disorienting question: *is it already morning?*

Now stretch that.

People who have been under general anesthesia describe something even more uncanny. The surgeon says "count back from ten," you get to seven — and then you are in the recovery room and it is three hours later and a major operation has happened *to your own body* and you have no memory of any of it. No darkness. No dreams. No passage. The three hours were simply subtracted from your life with no remainder. People wake from it and say, *wait — did you do it yet?* It is already done.

[PAUSE]

That, the ayah tells us, is death. *Min marqadinā* — "from our bed." The grave is not a long dark wait. It is not centuries of lying conscious in the dark, counting. The dead experience it as **the anesthesia of God.** You close your eyes in this world — and *fa-idhā*, hard cut, no gap — you open them at the trumpet. To you, your great-grandmother who died ninety years ago and the man who will die a thousand years from now wake at the *same instant*, into the *same morning*. The graveyard is a dormitory, and the trumpet is the single alarm that wakes every sleeper at once, regardless of when they lay down.

Why does Allah want you to know this? Two reasons, and they pull in opposite directions, and you need both.

The first is **comfort**. The thing you fear most about death is often not the dying but the *imagined eternity of the grave* — the claustrophobia of it, the endless dark. Allah lifts that fear off you. There is no endless dark to endure. From your side, there is no *duration* at all. You will not feel the long wait, because for you there will be no wait. Lay your head down; wake at the trumpet. For the believer, this is a mercy: the distance between your deathbed and your Lord is shorter than a single night's sleep.

But the second reason is **terror**, and the ayah does not let you keep only the comfort. Because if death is a nap, then **the accounting is much closer than you think.** You imagine you have death, *and then* a long grey buffer, *and then* eventually, far off, judgment. The ayah deletes the buffer. There is your last breath — and then, *immediately*, your Lord. The Day you keep filing under "someday, distant, abstract" is, from the angle of your own experience, **the very next thing that happens to you after you die.** No commute. No waiting room. You shut your eyes here and open them *there*.

What does this change about tomorrow? It collapses the false distance you have built between your ordinary Tuesday and your Reckoning. You have been living as though the Day of Judgment is at the far end of a long corridor. The ayah says: it is on the other side of a single nap. **Live as someone who is about to be woken.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Name That Answers the Scream

Now we return to the question I planted at the beginning, and we are finally ready for it.

The dead wake. They are disoriented, terrified, crying *yā waylanā* — "oh, our ruin!" They ask, into the chaos: *man baʿathanā min marqadinā* — "**who** woke us from our bed?"

It is a real question. And it gets a real answer. Watch the grammar shift — the panicked human voice stops, and a calm, declarative voice replies:

> هَٰذَا مَا وَعَدَ **ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ** وَصَدَقَ ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ
>
> "**This is what *al-Raḥmān* promised, and the messengers told the truth.**"

There it is. The name I told you to hold onto.

They asked *who*. And the answer names Him: **al-Raḥmān. The Most Merciful. The One whose mercy is so vast and so essential to His being that it is almost His proper name.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel how strange this is. This is the gate of the most frightening Day in existence. This is the moment of dread, the *yā waylanā*, the scream of beings who suddenly understand they are about to be held to account. And of all the ninety-nine names Allah could have placed here — *al-Jabbār*, the Compeller; *al-Muntaqim*, the Avenger; *al-Qahhār*, the Subduer; *al-ʿAdl*, the Just — the name that answers the scream is **the Most Merciful.**

Why? Let us sit inside this, because it is the heart of the whole passage.

Begin with the most obvious reading. Even *this* — the resurrection, the gathering, the terror of the deniers — is an act of **mercy**. We flinch at that. How is Judgment mercy? But consider: a universe where the murdered are never avenged, where the oppressor and the oppressed both simply rot into nothing and the ledger is never balanced, where the child who suffered and the tyrant who caused it meet the identical fate of dust — *that* universe would be the cruelest one imaginable. The resurrection is the proof that nothing was meaningless, no tear unrecorded, no injustice final. To raise everyone and set the scales right is an act of *al-Raḥmān*, because mercy without justice is just indifference wearing a kind mask.

Go deeper. There is a tenderness in the *naming itself*, aimed at the terrified. To a soul screaming "who has done this to me?" the answer is not a threat. It is the gentlest of His names, offered like a hand. *It was the Merciful. The One who fed you, who carried you in your first birth, who let you sleep softly in your marqad — it is the same Merciful who wakes you now.* Even at the edge of the abyss, He introduces Himself by His mercy, as if to say: *whatever happens next, know that the One running it is not your enemy. He is the Most Merciful, and He always was.*

And now the deepest layer, the one that should make you tremble — because *al-Raḥmān* here is also the most piercing reproach the deniers will ever feel. They are about to face consequences. And the name that brings them to account is **Mercy**. Meaning: the very attribute that should have saved them is now the attribute that condemns them — because they were *warned, gently, by the Merciful, for an entire lifetime,* and they laughed. *This is what al-Raḥmān promised.* He did not ambush you. He *promised*. He sent word, again and again, through every messenger, out of mercy — and you called it a fairy tale. To be undone by the Most Merciful is to have no one to blame, because Mercy itself bent down to warn you and you waved it away.

And then the final clause snaps shut like a verdict:

> **وَصَدَقَ ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ** — *wa-ṣadaqa-l-mursalūn* — "**and the messengers told the truth.**"

Every prophet who was mocked. Every warner who was called a madman, a poet, a liar. Every man who stood in a marketplace and said *there is a Day coming* and was laughed out of town. In this single instant — *they were right.* All of them. The thing they staked their lives on, the thing the world called delusion, turns out to have been the plainest fact in existence. *Ṣadaqa* — they were truthful — root *ṣ-d-q*, the very opposite of the lie the world accused them of. The waking dead pronounce, with their own mouths, the verdict the world refused to give: **the messengers were telling the truth the whole time.**

What does this change about tomorrow? Two things, very concretely.

First: it tells you *how to think about the warnings you are receiving right now.* The Qur'an open in front of you, the reminder that pricked you in the khutbah, the verse that made you uncomfortable this week — these are not threats from an enemy. They are *al-Raḥmān*, promising you, gently, in advance, so that you will not be among the screaming. The warning **is** the mercy. To receive a warning before the Day is itself the kindness of the Most Merciful. Do not wave it away.

Second: it tells you which side of the truth you want to be standing on when the messengers are vindicated. Because they *will* be. *Ṣadaqa al-mursalūn* is not a hope; in the ayah it is already spoken, already done. The only open question is whether, on that morning, you will say it as someone who believed them — or as someone who finally, too late, admits they were right.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the same insight seen from two directions.

The grammar of **suddenness** — the passive blowing, the *fa-idhā* hard cut — and the word **marqad**, the bed, are telling you one unified thing: *there is no gap.* From the inside, death is a nap; from the outside, resurrection is instantaneous; the two confirm each other perfectly. Your reckoning is not far. It is one sleep away.

The verb **yansilūn** — the second birth, the hastening newborn rushing *ilā rabbihim*, toward its Lord — and the name **al-Raḥmān** are also telling you one unified thing: *the One at the other end is the One who made you and nurtured you.* You are not being born into the hands of a stranger or an executioner. You are hastening, as you once hastened from your mother's womb, toward your *Rabb*, who introduces Himself, even there, even then, as **the Most Merciful.**

The whole passage is the architecture of a single mercy: *He gave you a soft bed, He wakes you gently though the Day is hard, He calls you home by the name of His mercy, and He warned you in advance through truthful men so you would not be afraid.*

### Questions to Carry

1. If the Day of Judgment is, from the angle of my own experience, *the very next thing that happens after I die* — am I living as though it is that close, or as though it is at the far end of a long corridor?
2. The deniers cry *yā waylanā*, "our ruin," the instant they wake. What word will *I* cry when I open my eyes — and what am I doing today to choose it?
3. The warnings I receive — the verse, the reminder, the unease — the ayah calls these *the promise of al-Raḥmān*. Do I treat them as the mercy they are, or as an irritation to be waved away?
4. If *ṣadaqa al-mursalūn* — "the messengers told the truth" — is already a settled fact, what in my life would change if I lived as though it were *already* true, rather than something to be confirmed later?
5. I have been born once already — hastening, blinking, into a world prepared for me. What have I prepared for the second birth that is coming?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the trumpet wakes you from what felt like a single night's sleep and you hasten out of the earth like a newborn toward your Lord, the One who roused you will not introduce Himself as the Avenger but as the Most Merciful — who promised you this exact morning, in advance, through messengers who were telling the truth the whole time.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing about this ayah asks you to be afraid in a way that paralyzes you. It asks you to *re-locate* the Day — to move it from the distant, abstract future into its true position: one sleep away, on the other side of your pillow. And it asks you to notice that the Hand that will shake you awake is the same Hand that has been gently warning you all along, by the name of Mercy. The invitation is simply this: let the warning *be* the mercy it is. Receive it now, while it is still a promise and not yet a morning.

### Du'ā

O *Raḥmān*, You who will wake us from our beds of dust by the gentlest of Your names — let us be among those who heard Your promise as mercy and not as a threat, who believed the messengers before they were vindicated, and who hastened toward You in this life before the trumpet makes us hasten in the next. Make our waking on that Day a waking into Your mercy, and let the word on our lips be not *yā waylanā*, but the peace of those who knew You were telling the truth. Āmīn.
