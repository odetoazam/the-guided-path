# One Scream, and They Are All Present

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic, then in sound, then in a meaning you can carry.

> [ayah:36:53] إِن كَانَتْ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً فَإِذَا هُمْ جَمِيعٌ لَّدَيْنَا مُحْضَرُونَ
>
> *In kānat illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-idhā hum jamīʿun ladaynā muḥḍarūn.*
>
> **"It will be no more than a single shout — and at once they are all brought present before Us."**

And then, immediately:

> [ayah:36:54] فَٱلْيَوْمَ لَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْـًٔا وَلَا تُجْزَوْنَ إِلَّا مَا كُنتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ
>
> *Fa-l-yawma lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾan wa-lā tujzawna illā mā kuntum taʿmalūn.*
>
> **"So today no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will you be recompensed except for what you used to do."**

### Let us rebuild the world these verses fell into

You have to understand where we are in this surah, because Ya-Sin has been building toward this moment with enormous patience.

This is the surah they call **the heart of the Quran** — *qalb al-Qurʾān.* And a great deal of that heart is occupied with one argument, the oldest argument between the human being and his Lord: *Will the dead really live again?* The deniers in Makkah did not find God's existence absurd. What they found absurd was **resurrection.** That a body could rot into the soil, scatter into dust, mix with the roots and the rain, lose every boundary that made it a "self" — and then be reassembled, named, and called to account. To them, that was the joke. "Who could possibly do that?"

And just a verse before ours, Allah lets them speak. He records their objection in their own voice. When the trumpet sounds and they are pulled from their graves, they cry out:

> يَا وَيْلَنَا مَن بَعَثَنَا مِن مَّرْقَدِنَا
>
> *"O woe to us! Who has raised us from our sleeping place?"* (36:52)

Notice the word they reach for. *Marqad.* Their **bed.** Their place of sleep. They experienced death the way you experience a deep, dreamless night — and now something has woken them, and they are disoriented, blinking, asking *who did this.*

Our verse is the answer. [PAUSE]

But here is the strange thing — and this is the hook, so stay with me. When the human being imagines resurrection, he imagines a **project.** Something enormous. Billions of bodies, every atom tracked across every continent and ocean and era, an unimaginable logistics of reassembly. We picture it as the hardest thing that could ever be done. We picture *labor.*

Allah is about to answer the "who" and the "how" of resurrection. And His answer is going to be **shorter than the question.** He is going to describe the single most overwhelming event in the history of creation — the raising of every human being who ever lived — and He is going to describe it in a way that makes it sound like **almost nothing at all.**

One word is going to do that work. And then a second verse is going to take you from the terror of being raised to something even more searching: the question of whether, when you finally stand there, you will be treated *fairly.*

Let's go in slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "It was nothing but…" — the grammar of subtraction

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you a structure, because Arabic does something here that English can only limp after.

In everyday speech, when you want to say *"there is only one thing,"* you have two ways to do it. You can build **up** — "There is one thing." Or you can build by **subtraction** — "There is nothing… except one thing." The second way is heavier. It first wipes the whole table clean — *nothing, nothing at all* — and then, into that cleared emptiness, it sets down a single object. By the time the object arrives, you've already felt the vastness of everything it is *not.*

Arabic has a dedicated machine for this. It's called *qaṣr*, restriction — and one of its most common forms is the pairing of a negation with an exception: **مَا … إِلَّا** *(mā … illā),* "nothing… except." Here Allah uses its twin: **إِن … إِلَّا** *(in … illā).* That little **إِن** at the start is not the "if" you may have learned. The classical commentators are unanimous on this — al-Jalālayn glosses it directly: «إن» means *mā*, "not." It is pure negation.

Now look at what Allah actually says:

> إِن كَانَتْ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً
>
> *"It was nothing — except a single shout."*

Feel the motion of it. First: **negation.** *In kānat* — "it was not." The sentence opens by erasing. For one beat, the entire event of resurrection is held in the negative — *it was nothing.* Whatever colossal machinery you were imagining, whatever cosmic effort — **no.** None of that. The table is wiped clean.

And then, **إِلَّا** — except. Into that cleared space, Allah sets down one object. *Ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan.* A single shout.

Do you see what the grammar just did to your imagination? You came expecting the largest thing that has ever happened. The sentence agreed to talk about it — and then refused you everything except **one sound.** [PAUSE]

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing at an unseen reality through something your senses can grasp. You cannot picture "the omnipotence required to resurrect the dead." It has no shape. So Allah does not hand you the omnipotence. He hands you its **opposite measure** — the smallness, the effortlessness — and lets you arrive at the power by subtraction. The less it costs Him, the more you understand who He is.

Here is what this single feature reveals: **for the One who made you, raising you is not the hard version of anything. It is so far beneath His strain that the only honest way to describe its cost is to first say it cost nothing.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: "A scream — a single one" — why say "one" twice

Now stay inside that object Allah set down. Look at it closely, because there is something here that should stop you.

> صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً
>
> *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* — "a single shout."

Two words. A noun, and then an adjective meaning "one." And on the surface, fine — "a single shout." But Arabic readers across the centuries have paused here, and I want you to pause too, because of the *shape* of the first word.

Let me teach you the concept first. In Arabic, the very **pattern** a word is poured into can carry meaning before the letters do. There is a noun-pattern called *ism al-marra* — the "noun of a single occurrence." Its mold is *faʿla* — فَعْلَة. When you cast an action into this mold, the word comes to mean **one single instance** of that action. From *ḍarb* (striking) you get *ḍarba* — **one** strike. From *akl* (eating) you get *akla* — **one** sitting of food. The oneness is baked into the shape.

Now — *ṣayḥa.* صَيْحَة. From the root *ṣ-y-ḥ*, to cry out, to scream. And it sits in exactly that mold: فَعْلَة. The form itself already leans toward *one* cry, a single discrete burst of sound.

So here is the question that should unsettle you a little. If the word's own shape already whispers "a single scream"… **why does Allah then add وَٰحِدَةً — "one" — out loud?**

Arabic does not waste words, and the Quran least of all. When a meaning is already present in the form, and Allah states it again anyway in plain vocabulary, the repetition is not decoration. It is **emphasis with intent.** He is nailing the door shut. He is saying: do not soften this, do not let your mind smuggle in a second sound, a follow-up, a process, a sequence of events. **One.** Not one-then-another. Not a building wave. A single, indivisible cry, and the matter is finished.

Think about what we instinctively do when something enormous is described to us. We *pluralize* it in our minds. We assume stages. We assume time. We hear "the resurrection of all mankind" and we cannot help but stretch it across some duration. Allah reaches into that instinct and closes it off twice — once in the shape of the word, once in the word *wāḥida* itself. **Stop multiplying it. It is one.** [PAUSE]

And the commentators help us feel the *sound* of that one cry. Al-Qurṭubī, reaching for what this scream is, gives it a voice. He says it is the call — at the final blast — to the scattered remains: *"O decayed bones, severed joints, and shredded hair! God commands you to gather for the verdict."* One command. And from every grain of dust across every age, the response is total.

What this word reveals: **the gap between the size of the cause and the size of the effect is the entire point.** A single scream on one side. The whole of humanity, reassembled and standing, on the other. When the cause is that small and the effect is that vast, you are no longer looking at the scream. You are looking at the One behind it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: "And suddenly — they are *brought*" — the collapse of time and the loss of the will

Now watch what happens the instant the scream sounds. This is the most cinematic moment in the verse, and it turns on two small features that most translations rush past.

> فَإِذَا هُمْ جَمِيعٌ لَّدَيْنَا مُحْضَرُونَ
>
> *fa-idhā hum jamīʿun ladaynā muḥḍarūn*
>
> "and suddenly — they, all of them, before Us, brought present."

First, that **فَإِذَا** — *fa-idhā.* Let me teach you this particle, because it is a jewel. Arabic has a special *idhā* called *idhā al-fujāʾiyya* — the **"idhā of suddenness."** It does not mean "when." It means something closer to *"and behold!"* — the grammatical equivalent of a curtain yanked open. It is the word you use when you turn a corner expecting an empty street **and the street is full.** It carries surprise, immediacy, and the total absence of any gap.

So place the two halves of the verse side by side and feel the seam between them. On the left: *a single shout.* On the right, with no transition, no "and then," no passage of time: *fa-idhā* — **and there they all are.** [PAUSE]

There is no interval. The grammar refuses to give you one. Between the cause and the consequence Allah has placed not a process but a *seam so thin it is not even time.* The scream and the standing are almost the **same instant.**

Sit with what that does to death itself. We think of the time in the grave as a long, long wait — centuries, for some; mere days for others; for the last person to die, almost no time at all. But the verse just told you something the deniers already sensed when they called the grave their *marqad,* their **bed.** From the inside, it will feel like **no time passed at all.** A scream — and you are up. Like a sleeper who is certain he just closed his eyes, and is told the night is over. The years you fear, the long darkness of the grave — to the one inside it, the whole of it collapses into the space between *ṣayḥa* and *idhā.* The longest wait in creation will feel like a single blink.

Now the second feature — and this one is quieter, but it may be the heaviest word in the verse.

**مُحْضَرُونَ** — *muḥḍarūn.* From the root *ḥ-ḍ-r*, "to be present." But Allah does not use the simple form that would mean *"they were present"* or *"they came."* He uses the form *aḥḍara* — to **bring** someone, to **make** them present, to **produce** them — and then He casts it into the **passive.** *Muḥḍarūn.* Not "they who came." **"They who were brought."** "Those who were made to be present."

Hold onto that passive, because theology lives in it.

Through the whole of your life, you came and went by your own will. You decided where to stand. You chose which rooms to enter and which doors to walk out of. Your presence anywhere was a thing *you* granted. The defining experience of being a self is *agency* — the felt certainty that **you** are the one moving.

And in the final word of the gathering, Allah strips it away. Not "they came before Us." **"They were brought."** The grammar removes the human being from the driver's seat entirely. You will not *attend* the reckoning the way you attend a meeting. You will be **produced** there, the way evidence is produced, the way the accused is *brought* into the courtroom whether he wishes it or not. The single most important appointment of your existence is the one appointment you do not make, do not schedule, and cannot decline. [PAUSE]

And one more thread before we leave this verse. **لَّدَيْنَا** — *ladaynā,* "before Us." Arabic could have said *ʿindanā,* a more general "with Us." *Ladā* is tighter — it means *right here, in immediate presence, directly in front.* Not gathered somewhere in the vicinity of God. Gathered **directly before Him,** in the closest sense the language has. There is no back row on that Day. There is no distance to hide in.

What these features reveal together: **on that Day, the two things you trusted most — that time would protect you, and that you were the one in control — both dissolve in a single word. Time collapses to a blink. Your will is set aside. And you find yourself produced, present, directly before Him.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### A bridge

Look at what these three features have built. *In … illā* wiped the table clean and set down one small object. The doubled "one" sealed it shut: a single, indivisible cry. And *fa-idhā … muḥḍarūn* showed you the result arriving with no time and no will — every human being suddenly produced, directly before God.

So far we have been reading the *grammar* of resurrection — how Allah constructs the sentence. But these constructions are not really about words. They are about **reality.** Now let's ask what this opens up not just in the language, but in how we are meant to live, and die, and stand.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The thing you dread most may cost Him the least

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of dread that is built into being human. It is the dread of *the impossible task.* You have felt it. The project so large you cannot see its edges. The debt so deep you cannot imagine the last payment. The grief so total you cannot picture a version of yourself that has carried it and survived. The mind, faced with something that exceeds it, does a specific thing: it inflates. It makes the obstacle *bigger.* It says, *this would take everything, and even everything would not be enough.*

We do this with God, too — without noticing. When we doubt the resurrection, or doubt that our specific, scattered, forgotten self could really be raised and known and called by name, what is actually happening is that we have **measured God's power by the size of the task as we feel it.** We feel the task is enormous. So we quietly assume it is enormous *for Him.* We project our own straining onto the One who does not strain.

And into exactly that assumption, the verse drops one word. **Ṣayḥa.** A scream. *One.*

Do you see how the grammar we studied was actually a therapy for the human mind? The structure *in … illā* — "it was nothing but" — is precisely calibrated to reach the part of you that inflates. It meets your inflation and reverses it. You came in having made the task infinite. The verse says: *no — first, it was nothing. And then, one sound.*

This is a recurring divine signature, and once you see it you'll see it everywhere. Elsewhere Allah says the matter of the Hour is *"as the twinkling of an eye, or even nearer"* (16:77). And in Sūrat al-Qamar, He describes the raising of all creation with the same compression: *"Our command is but **one** — like the blink of an eye"* (54:50). The same word keeps appearing — *wāḥida,* **one.** One command. One blink. One scream. Allah is teaching you, across surahs, the same lesson with the same vocabulary: **the things that would shatter you to attempt, He accomplishes with a single, effortless word.**

Now turn this from cosmology back to your own chest, because here is where it has to land. [PAUSE]

If raising the dead — *all* of them, across *all* of time — costs Him no more than one cry, then **what is the size of your problem in His sight?** The reconciliation you think is impossible. The heart you think is too hardened — your own, or someone you love. The forgiveness you think you've forfeited. The change you've tried and failed at so many times you've stopped believing in it. You have inflated these. You have measured them against your own exhausted strength and concluded *it cannot be done.*

But you were never the one who was going to do it.

The God who gathers humanity with one sound is not straining over your single, specific life. **The smallness of the cause is the mercy.** It means the thing you've been carrying as a boulder is, to Him, the matter of a word — *kun,* and it is. Your despair was never a measurement of the task. It was only a measurement of *you.*

**What this changes tomorrow:** When you next catch your mind inflating an obstacle into an impossibility — pause, and notice that you have just done your private version of doubting the resurrection. You have measured God by the size of *your* fatigue. And then ask Him, plainly, for the thing you had written off as too big. Because the One you are asking gathers the dead with a scream. Your impossible thing is not even an effort.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: "No soul wronged in the least" — the justice you have been aching for your whole life

Now we cross into verse 54, and the scene changes completely. The scream has sounded. The collapse of time has happened. You have been *brought* — present, directly before Him. And the very first thing Allah declares, before any sentence is passed, is **a guarantee about how you will be treated.**

Let me start, again, where you live.

There is a hunger in every human heart that almost never gets fed in this world. It is the hunger to be **judged accurately.** Think about how rare that is. You have been misjudged your whole life — by people who saw your worst moment and decided that was you; by people who never saw the effort behind your failures; by people who took credit for what you did and assigned you blame for what you didn't. You have sat in rooms where the verdict was decided before you spoke. You have watched the guilty walk free and the innocent pay. Some of the deepest wounds you carry are not from being *hurt* — they are from being *misread.* From injustice. From the universe failing to see you as you actually were.

This is so deep in us that even small children, before they can read, scream the same two words: **"That's not fair!"** Where does that come from? Why is a sense of justice installed in a creature before it can even reason? Because it is a fingerprint. It is a longing placed in you *by* the Just One, pointing back toward Him — a thirst that proves the existence of water.

And here, in verse 54, the water arrives. Listen to the precision of it:

> فَٱلْيَوْمَ لَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْـًٔا
>
> *"So today, no soul will be wronged in the least."*

Look at how carefully this is built. **Lā tuẓlamu** — "will not be wronged" — and Allah uses the **passive** again. He does not name *who* might do the wronging, because the answer is *no one* — not God, not an angel, not a record, not a circumstance. The door to injustice is simply not there. And then **nafsun** — "a soul" — left deliberately **indefinite.** In Arabic, an indefinite noun under a negation becomes a universal: not "a soul," but **no soul whatsoever** — not one, not the smallest, not the most forgotten. And then **shayʾan** — "in *anything.*" Not wronged in the great matters and slightly shortchanged in the small ones. Not in *anything.* Not by an atom. Not by a hair.

Three layers of precision, stacked, to seal off injustice so completely that there is no crack left for it to enter through. The God who can erase injustice from existence with the same ease that He gathers the dead — **does.** [PAUSE]

Now watch the most intimate move in these two verses. It is a feature of Arabic eloquence called *iltifāt* — the sudden, deliberate **shift of address.** Look at the pronouns across these lines. In verse 53: *they.* "**They** are all brought present." Distant. Third person. A crowd seen from above. And the first half of verse 54 keeps that distance: "no **soul**" — *a* soul, *any* soul, someone, anyone.

And then, mid-sentence, without warning:

> وَلَا تُجْزَوْنَ إِلَّا مَا كُنتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ
>
> *"…nor will **you** be recompensed except for what **you** used to do."*

**You.** *Tujzawna.* The camera that was hovering over the whole of humanity suddenly drops, swings around, and points **at you.** The crowd dissolves. The "they" becomes "you." [PAUSE]

This is not an accident of style. This is the verse doing to *you* what the Day will do to *you.* On that Day, you will not experience the reckoning as a statistic in a crowd of billions. The crowd is real — *jamīʿun,* all of them, brought together. But the judgment is not collective. It swings around and lands on the single, specific self. *You.* What *you* did. Allah builds that experience into the grammar: He lets you stand in the anonymous crowd for two and a half lines — *they, a soul, anyone* — and then He turns and says **you,** so that you feel the floor of anonymity drop out from under you exactly the way it will on that Day.

And then the second restriction — the one that mirrors verse 53. Remember *in … illā,* "nothing but one scream"? Here it returns: **illā mā kuntum taʿmalūn** — "**except** what you used to do." The same machine of restriction, now turned onto your account. You will be recompensed for **nothing but** your own deeds. Not for what you intended and never did. Not for what was done to you. Not for what you were assumed to be. **Only** — *illā* — what you actually, habitually did. *Kuntum taʿmalūn* — "you *used to* do," the form for the ongoing, habitual, the pattern of a life rather than a single slip.

Now feel the strange doubleness of this promise, and do not let me resolve it too quickly. [PAUSE]

For the one who was wronged in this life — this is the sweetest water imaginable. *Every* injustice you ever swallowed in silence, every time you were misread and could not defend yourself, every credit stolen and every blame misassigned — on that Day, *none of it survives.* No soul wronged in anything. The record is exact. You will finally, *finally,* be seen as you actually were.

But turn the same sentence around, and it becomes the most sobering promise in the Book. Because "recompensed for *nothing but* what you did" cuts both ways. It means there will be no *favoritism for you either.* No lineage to hide behind. No reputation, no image, no carefully curated self. Just the deeds. The actual, habitual deeds — *what you used to do.* The same precision that vindicates the wronged also exposes the pretender. The exactness is a mercy and a warning in the *same words.*

**What this changes tomorrow:** Two things, depending on which person you are today. If you are carrying an injustice — something done to you that was never set right, that no court and no apology will ever fix — you can set it down. Not because it didn't matter, but because it is *recorded,* exactly, and it *will* be answered, exactly. You don't have to be your own judge anymore; that job is taken, by Someone who wrongs no soul in anything. And if you are the other person — if part of you has been coasting on image, on reputation, on being *seen* as righteous more than *being* it — then let *illā mā kuntum taʿmalūn* sober you tonight. Build the deeds. Quietly. Habitually. Because on the Day the camera swings around to **you,** the only thing in your hands will be what you actually did.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: two screams that frame the end of the world

I held something back, and now is the time to show it to you, because it is the proof that all of this was *designed.*

The phrase in our verse — **ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan,** "a single shout" — is not the first time it appears in this passage. Go back just four verses, to 36:49. The deniers are mocking, demanding to know when the promise will come. And Allah says:

> مَا يَنظُرُونَ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً تَأْخُذُهُمْ وَهُمْ يَخِصِّمُونَ
>
> *"They await nothing but a **single shout** — it will seize them while they are still arguing."* (36:49)

There it is — the *exact same words.* **One single shout.** But that one is different. That first scream **ends** the world. It catches them mid-sentence, mid-dispute, mid-life — and everything stops. The markets, the arguments, the plans for tomorrow — *seized,* in the middle of a word.

And then, in our verse, 36:53 — **the same phrase returns.** *Ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan.* One single shout. But this one does the opposite. This one **restarts** everything. It pulls them out of the *marqad,* the bed of death, and stands them up before God.

Do you see the architecture? Allah has placed **two identical screams** around the death and rebirth of the entire world. [PAUSE] One scream and the world ends — caught in the middle of an argument. One scream and the world is raised — standing before the Judge. The whole of human history, from the last breath ever taken to the first moment of the reckoning, is **bracketed by two single cries.** And between those two screams — which to Allah are the matter of two effortless words — sits everything you were ever afraid of, compressed into a blink.

That symmetry is not in the translation. It is not even obvious in the Arabic until you go looking. It was *built.* And its existence is the proof of the whole reflection: the One who composed these verses with two matching screams at the seams of existence is not a God who might lose track of your scattered dust. He is a God of such total command that He frames the death and resurrection of all creation with two perfectly matched words, **four verses apart,** and trusts the careful reader to find it.

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of these verses turn out to be **one insight from two directions.**

The *power* in verse 53 and the *justice* in verse 54 are joined by the same grammatical machine — **restriction,** *illā.* "Nothing but one scream." "Nothing but what you did." The *same exactitude* that makes His power effortless makes His justice flawless. A God precise enough to gather all of humanity with one indivisible cry is precise enough to weigh each soul without wronging it by an atom. **The economy of His power is the guarantee of His justice.** You could not have one without the other. The One who does not strain to raise you does not slip when He weighs you.

And the passive voice ties them together too. In 53: you are *brought* — *muḥḍarūn* — your will set aside. In 54: you are *not wronged* — *lā tuẓlamu* — no agent of injustice anywhere in sight. The same grammar that removes *your* control removes *any* possibility of unfairness. You lose the steering wheel, yes — but you lose it into the hands of the only One who has never once been unjust to any soul in anything.

### Questions to carry

Let these stay open. Don't answer them quickly.

1. What is the obstacle in your life that you have quietly decided is *impossible* — and have you ever once handed it to the God who raises the dead with a single scream?

2. When you imagine standing before Him, do you imagine it as *being brought* — produced, present, your will set aside — or do you still secretly imagine *managing* it?

3. Which person are you tonight: the one who needs the promise *"no soul wronged in anything,"* or the one who needs the warning *"recompensed for nothing but what you did"* — and what would change if you admitted it's both?

4. There is an injustice you are still carrying as a judge — replaying it, sentencing it yourself. What would it feel like to set it down because the verdict is *already recorded, exactly?*

5. The grave will feel like a blink. If the time between your death and your standing is *no time at all* — then the next breath you take is, in a real sense, almost the last thing before the scream. How does that change what you do with it?

### The one sentence

**The God who raises all of creation with a single scream — costing Him nothing — is the same God who will weigh your single soul without wronging it by an atom: His effortless power is the very thing that guarantees His flawless justice.**

### The closing invitation

You came in imagining resurrection as the heaviest thing that could happen. Leave knowing it is, to Him, the lightest — a scream, and a blink, and you are present. Let that do two things at once. Let it *shrink* your impossible problems, because the One you'd be asking does not strain. And let it *sharpen* your days, because the appointment you cannot decline is closer than the length of a night's sleep, and the only thing you'll carry into it is *what you used to do.*

Don't take this as a weight. Take it as an invitation — to hand Him the boulder you've been carrying alone, and to begin, quietly, building the deeds you'll actually want in your hands when the camera turns and says **you.**

### A brief du'a

*O You who gather all of creation with a single cry — the One for whom nothing is heavy — the thing I have been carrying as impossible is not even an effort to You; so I place it before You and ask. And when the scream sounds and I am brought, present, directly before You, do not let me stand on image or assumption, but let what I actually did be enough — for You wrong no soul in anything, and I have only ever needed Your mercy. Amin.*
