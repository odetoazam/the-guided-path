# The Body That Folds Backward and the Word That Wakes the Living

## Surah Ya-Sin, Ayahs 68–70

> [ayah:36:68] وَمَن نُّعَمِّرْهُ نُنَكِّسْهُ فِى ٱلْخَلْقِ ۖ أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ
>

> [ayah:36:70] لِّيُنذِرَ مَن كَانَ حَيًّا وَيَحِقَّ ٱلْقَوْلُ عَلَى ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ

*Wa man nuʿammir-hu nunakkis-hu fī l-khalq, afalā yaʿqilūn.*

## INTRODUCTION: The World You Have to Stand In

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand somewhere.

Picture the marketplace of Makkah in those years when this surah came down. The most prized thing in that culture was not gold, and it was not the sword. It was *the word*. The Arabs were a people drunk on language. A single tribe could be lifted or shamed by one poet. There were fairs where men travelled for days to hear a master string syllables together, and when a great line was composed it was sometimes hung — they said — on the walls of the sacred precinct itself. To call a man a poet was to call him a kind of magician. Poetry was the height of the human mind showing off what it could do.

And into that world walks a man, unlettered, reciting something. And the first thing his enemies reach for — because it is the highest category they have — is: *he's a poet.* A clever one. A man playing with words the way their champions played with words.

[PAUSE]

So hold that. Hold a civilization that worshipped eloquence the way ours worships intelligence and information. Now notice where Allah places His answer to them.

He doesn't begin by defending the Qur'an. He begins by talking about an old man.

He talks about the body that is given a long life — and then quietly, year by year, *folded back* on itself. The strong arm that once carried a sword now trembling to lift a cup. The mind that once memorized a thousand lines of verse now forgetting the name of its own grandchild. The same human frame, reversed.

And then — only then — does He turn to the accusation about poetry. And then He tells you what this Qur'an is actually *for*: not to decorate a wall, but to wake the living.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. Why does Allah answer a charge about *language* by first showing us a charge about *aging*? What does the slow reversal of an old man's body have to do with whether the Qur'an is poetry?

[PAUSE]

Because there is a single thread running through all three of these ayahs, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. It is the difference between things that decay and the thing that does not. It is the difference between a body that is folded backward toward death — and a Word that is sent to fold the dead heart *forward* toward life.

Let's earn that, slowly.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: نُنَكِّسْهُ — The Verb That Folds You Backward

### First, the everyday picture

Let me teach you something about a certain shape of Arabic verb before we ever touch the ayah.

In Arabic, you can take a simple action and put it into a heavier, more intense mold — what the grammarians call the second form, *taʿwīl* by doubling the middle letter. The plain verb becomes pressurized. *ʿalima* — he knew. Push it into the heavier form: *ʿallama* — he *taught*, he caused knowing to happen, deliberately, thoroughly. The doubling is not decoration. It is force. It is intention applied with weight.

Hold that idea: **the doubled-letter form is a verb under pressure — deliberate, intensive, done to something repeatedly or thoroughly.**

Now think about a piece of paper. There is a difference between a paper that *falls* and a paper that someone *folds*. Falling is gravity. Folding is a hand, deciding, pressing the crease in. One is an accident. The other is an act.

Keep both pictures: the verb under pressure, and the deliberate fold.

### Now the word

Allah says of the human being He grants long life: **نُنَكِّسْهُ** — *nunakkisuhu* — "We reverse him."

The root here is *n-k-s* (نكس). In the language it means to turn a thing upside down, to invert it, to set it on its head. When you take an object and flip it so that what was up is now down, the Arabs said *nakastuhu* — I inverted it, I turned it over onto its head so it became *mankūs*, upended.

And notice the form Allah chose. The Companion-reciters preserved this very point for us. ʿĀṣim and Ḥamza recited it as **نُنَكِّسْهُ** — with the doubled, pressurized form, *tankīs*. The intensive mold. The deliberate fold, pressed in with weight. (Others among the reciters read the lighter *nankusuhu* — the plainer flip. Both readings are authentic; both say *reversal*. The heavier reading simply leans on the crease harder.)

So this is not a body that merely *falls* with age, the way a leaf falls. This is a body that is *folded* — by a Hand, deliberately, thoroughly — back the way it came.

[PAUSE]

### Let's sit with this

Listen to what the classical scholars heard in it, because they did not hear "the man gets old and weak" and stop there. They heard something far more unsettling.

Qatāda said the meaning is that the human being is returned **to a state resembling childhood** — *ṣabā*, infancy. Al-Baghawī reports the same: he is returned to *arḏal al-ʿumur*, the most diminished part of life, "like a child at the very beginning of creation." Sufyān noted that when a man reaches eighty, his body changes and his strength deserts him.

Al-Ṭabarī goes one layer deeper still. The reversal in the *creation*, he explains, means the aged person "is returned to a state resembling childhood in his senility and old age — and he comes to know nothing after the knowledge he once had." The fold is not only in the muscle. It reaches into the mind. The library of a lifetime — names of loved ones, arguments constructed over decades, verses memorized by heart — the reversal does not spare any of it. Al-Muyassar makes the pairing explicit: he is returned to "weakness of the mind and weakness of the body together." Both diminish, hand in hand, along the same crease.

Now feel the precision of the word. The Qur'an did not say "We *weaken* him." Weakening would be a straight line down. The Qur'an said "We *reverse* him in the creation" — *fī l-khalq*. The arc of a human life does not simply descend. **It curves back on itself.**

Watch the symmetry the word is quietly drawing for you. The infant cannot hold his head up — the elder's head sinks. The infant has no teeth — the elder loses his. The infant cannot walk — the elder cannot walk. The infant babbles without sense — and the most aged forget words, forget names, forget faces. The infant must be carried, washed, fed, cleaned by another — and so, at the far end, must the elder.

You did not get weaker in a new way. **You were folded back into where you began.** The paper returns along its own crease.

### Deeper still — why this, of all the proofs?

Now ask the question Ya-Sin has been building toward this whole surah: *will Allah really raise the dead?* The deniers stand there saying, "Who will give life to bones when they have crumbled?"

And Allah's answer is not an argument from the sky. It is an argument from your own face in the mirror over fifty years.

You have *already watched* a complete human being be carried from helplessness, to strength, and then *returned, deliberately, by a Hand, all the way back to helplessness.* You have already seen a creation reversed. The One who folds the strong back into the weak — is He somehow unable to unfold the dead back into the living?

This is why the ayah ends where it ends. The body's reversal is not a sad fact about mortality. **It is a live demonstration, running in every household, that the Creator reverses states at will.** Resurrection is just one more reversal — and you have been watching reversals your whole life. Elsewhere in the Qur'an, this same three-stage arc is named directly: "Allah is the One who created you from weakness, then after weakness gave you strength, then after strength gave you weakness and grey hair" (30:54). The trajectory is not an accident. It is authored. The surah of Ya-Sin is simply asking you to let that authorship land.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing in the *sound* of the word I don't want you to miss. *Nu-nak-ki-suh.* Say it. The doubled *kāf* gives it a percussive catch in the throat — a little *snap*, like something folding shut. Even the mouth, making this word, performs a small reversal. The language is doing what it means.

So here is what this single word reveals, before we move on: **the long life you pray for is not a straight road forward. It is a Hand, gently and deliberately, folding you back toward where you started — so that you, of all creatures, have no excuse to doubt that you can be remade.**

[PAUSE — let it sit]

## Element Two: أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ — The Strange Place to Ask for Reason

### First, the concept

We need to be careful about an Arabic word we think we already know: *ʿaql* (عقل). We translate it "intellect" or "reason," and that's fine, but the root picture is sharper than that.

The root *ʿ-q-l* originally describes the rope that ties a camel's leg so it cannot wander off and destroy itself. *ʿIqāl* is that tether. So *ʿaql*, in the Arab imagination, is not raw cleverness. It is the *faculty that restrains you* — the inner tether that stops you running off the cliff your own desires are pulling you toward. A man of *ʿaql* is not merely smart. He is *bound* to reality. He is held to the truth.

Hold that: **ʿaql is not horsepower. It is the tether that keeps you connected to what is real.**

### Now the word — and the strangeness of its placement

After describing the body folded backward, Allah says: **أَفَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ** — "Will they not, then, use their reason? Will they not be *tethered* to the truth this shows them?"

Now here is something I want you to notice that is almost too quiet to catch.

He places the demand for *reason* immediately after a description of the faculty of reason *being taken away.*

Think about it. The very reversal He just described — the folding back into childhood — *that is precisely the season of life when the mind itself begins to fail.* The eighty-year-old whose body has been reversed is often the one whose memory has thinned, whose grip on names and faces has loosened. The faculty He is appealing to is the very faculty His own decree is quietly dismantling.

[PAUSE]

Why would He do that? Why ask "will you not reason" while pointing at a man whose reason is leaving him?

### Stay with me

Because that is the entire warning, folded inside one rhetorical question.

Allah is saying, in effect: *the window is open now.* You have your *ʿaql* now — the tether is in your hand now. There will come a day, if you are "granted long life," when you will not be able to assemble this thought, when your mind will be as soft as the infant's, when the argument that is so clear today will be beyond your reach. **The faculty He is appealing to is itself on the clock He just described.**

Al-Baghawī catches the logical core: "Will they not reflect and come to know that the One who was able to direct the changing states of the human being is able to raise him after death?" The reasoning is almost embarrassingly simple. *The One who turns your states is the One who can turn death into life.* It does not take genius. It takes only an *ʿaql* still tethered to what the eyes already see.

And al-Jalālayn sharpens what makes the argument so hard to evade: the One capable of this reversal is capable of resurrection, he says — and this reversal is *already known to them*. Not hidden in the heavens. Not requiring a scholar to decode. The deniers had watched it happen — to their own parents, their own companions, perhaps to their own bodies already beginning to slow. The evidence was not abstract. It was domestic. Personal. The proof was not in the sky; it was at the next table at the meal. If even that could not tether them to the truth — then the failure was not a failure of information. It was a failure of the tether itself. The camel had slipped its rope and was wandering toward the cliff.

So what does this word reveal? **Allah does not ask the deniers to be brilliant. He asks them only to stay tied to what their own decline is screaming at them — and He asks it now, while the rope is still in their hands.**

## Element Three: مَن كَانَ حَيًّا — The Word That Defines "Alive"

### First, the concept

We come to the third ayah, and the word I want is hidden inside the *purpose* of the whole Qur'an. Allah says this Recitation came down — **لِّيُنذِرَ مَن كَانَ حَيًّا** — "that it may warn *whoever is alive*."

Now, a plain reading says: it warns living people, as opposed to corpses. But stop and feel how strange that is. *Of course* it warns living people. You don't recite warnings to a graveyard. Why would Allah specify that the warning is for "whoever is alive"? The information seems empty.

This is one of those moments where you must **read the limitation as a clue, not as filler.** When the Qur'an qualifies something that seems obvious, the obvious reading is usually not the intended one.

### The reversal of the word "alive"

Here is what the qualification is doing. Allah is *redefining* the word *ḥayy* — alive — right under your feet.

Look at the architecture across these three ayahs. In the first, He shows you a man who is *biologically alive* — breathing, eighty years old, granted a long life — and yet folded backward, his faculties failing, drifting toward death. Fully alive, by the doctor's measure. And in the last ayah, He says the Qur'an warns "whoever is *alive*" — using a different scale entirely.

Because some who are breathing are not, by this second scale, *alive at all.* And the verse that follows the warning — *that the word may be proven true against the disbelievers* — names the alternative to being alive. The disbelievers are not addressed as recipients of guidance. They are not warned. The word is simply proven against them. There is no address, no reach. The warning that would find the living cannot find its way in.

This is the Qur'an's consistent use of *ḥayy* as a spiritual category. Elsewhere it says: "Is one who was dead and We gave him life — and We made for him a light by which he walks among people — like one who is in the darknesses?" (6:122). And: "Respond to Allah and to the Messenger when He calls you to that which gives you *life*" (8:24). In both cases, the living and the dead are people who are *breathing.* The distinction is whether the heart is still open to be reached.

The warning (*indhār*) reaches whoever still has a heart capable of receiving it. Al-Muyassar captures this precisely: the Qur'an is "a reminder by which those of deep understanding take heed" — *ūlū l-albāb*, those whose cores have not been sealed. They are the same people as the "living" of this ayah. They have a heart that is reachable. The *dhikr* — the reminder — can land in them and do what a reminder does.

Now notice what has happened structurally across the three ayahs. The man whose body was "reversed in creation" — al-Ṭabarī's observation was that "he comes to know nothing after the knowledge he once had." That is a *forced* diminishment of the interior life. God's decree, not the man's choice. But the kāfirūn against whom the word is proven? Their interior closure is something else entirely. It is not imposed from outside. It is built, brick by brick, by repeated rejection — until the heart is sealed from within, and the warning that would reach the living cannot find a way in.

[PAUSE]

The body's reversal is sorrow. The heart's reversal is the real calamity.

## Element Four: ذِكْرٌ وَقُرْءَانٌ مُّبِينٌ — What It Actually Is

Before we can fully feel the contrast of ayah 70, we have to stop at the middle ayah and hear what the Qur'an calls *itself*.

### The accusation and the flat denial

**وَمَا عَلَّمْنَٰهُ ٱلشِّعْرَ** — "And We did not teach him poetry."

The *mā* here is emphatic. Not *"he chose not to write poetry"* but *"We did not teach him."* This is Allah taking ownership of a category. He is saying: that training did not happen. The one who spoke these words did not pass through the school where the Arab poets were schooled — the years of listening, imitating, competing, refining the aesthetic ear. He was not given that formation.

And then the second phrase: **وَمَا يَنۢبَغِى لَهُۥٓ** — "nor would it befit him." This is the dignity clause, and classical commentators were precise about it. Al-Muyassar: "it would not befit him to be a poet." Not circumstantially, but categorically. The Prophet's office — bearing a message that is verifiably true or false, that stands as warning and proof, that makes theological and legal claims — is *constitutionally incompatible* with the license of the poet.

Think about what poetry is permitted to do. A poet may exaggerate without being a liar. He may imagine what did not happen without being a deceiver. He may praise a tribe for virtues it does not have, or curse an enemy with afflictions it has not suffered, and the audience will admire him for it, not censure him. That is the game, and it is an honored game. The Arabs knew the rules.

But a *warning* is different in kind. A warning either points to something real or it does not. A prophet either receives genuine revelation or he does not. These are true-or-false claims, not aesthetic achievements. The entire *function* of prophethood requires that the word being spoken be categorically distinct from the crafted beauty of a poet playing with images. If the Qur'an were poetry — even very good poetry — it would have *permission* to be untrue. And if it had permission to be untrue, the warning would mean nothing.

This is why Allah does not merely say "He didn't write poetry." He says it would not *befit* him. The unfittingness is not biographical. It is ontological.

### What it is instead

**إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا ذِكْرٌ وَقُرْءَانٌ مُّبِينٌ** — "It is only a remembrance and a clear Recitation."

*In huwa illā* — "it is nothing but." A restricting formula. Not *also* a reminder — *only* a reminder. The Qur'an declines the title of artwork. It declines the title of performance. It claims a single, more austere purpose.

**ذِكْر** (*dhikr*): remembrance, reminder. The root connects to memory, to *bringing back* what was forgotten, to restoring attention to something that is real and was always real but whose presence the mind had drifted away from. A reminder does not *create* truth. It returns you to it. It says: you knew this once. You have forgotten. Here it is again.

**قُرْءَانٌ مُّبِينٌ** (*qurʾānun mubīn*): a clear Recitation. *Mubīn* comes from *b-y-n*, to be clear, to distinguish, to make manifest. This Recitation makes things visible that were obscured. It does not traffic in the suggestive vagueness of great poetry; it *clarifies*. Al-Muyassar describes it as "clear in its guidance on truth and falsehood, clear in its rulings, its wisdom, and its admonitions." The *mubīn* is a claim to transparency, not a claim to aesthetic complexity.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the contrast with the aging body of ayah 68. The body is being reversed — knowledge draining, memory fading, everything built over a lifetime returning to nothing. The great poets of the Arab fairs were not exempt from this either. The most celebrated lines, given enough time, are forgotten. Even the poets themselves are forgotten. The word that men praised and hung on the Kaaba is today dust.

But *dhikr* — a reminder that points to what is real — does not decay along with the human vessel who recites it. The truth it points to was there before the recitation and will be there after. This is the contrast the surah has been building: the human frame returns to weakness; the divine word remains *mubīn*, clear, pointing outward toward what does not fold.

## Element Five: وَيَحِقَّ ٱلْقَوْلُ — The Word That Is Proven True

The final clause of this trio is among the most quietly devastating phrases in the surah.

**وَيَحِقَّ ٱلْقَوْلُ عَلَى ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ** — "and that the word may be proven true against the disbelievers."

### The root

*Yaḥiqqa* comes from *ḥ-q-q* (حقّ). The core of this root is truth that is *actualized* — not merely declared but confirmed, established, brought to pass. *Al-ḥaqq* is not just "the truth" in the abstract. It is truth that has been verified, truth that has arrived and cannot be disputed. When the root appears as a verb — *ḥaqqa ʿalayhi* — it means the thing was *established upon him*, it fell into place, the verdict was upheld.

When Allah says *yaḥiqqa l-qawl* — "the word is proven true" — He is speaking about the pre-ordained word, the divine statement that had already been made and is now being confirmed by events. The word of consequence that rests against those who disbelieve.

### Two purposes, not one

Notice that the two clauses of ayah 70 are parallel in form, joined beneath a single purpose particle *li-* at the opening:

*li-yundhira man kāna ḥayyan* — to warn whoever is alive  
*wa yaḥiqqa l-qawlu ʿalā l-kāfirīn* — and that the word be proven against the disbelievers

Both of these are the *purpose* of the Qur'an being sent. The same act — this Recitation arriving in the world — does both things simultaneously. To those who are spiritually alive: it warns, it reaches them, they can be gripped by it, and al-Muyassar's *ūlū l-albāb* take heed from it. To those whose hearts are sealed: the warning does not reach them — but its very arrival proves the word that was always true about their state.

This is not the Qur'an *making* anyone a disbeliever. It is the Qur'an *witnessing* the choice that was already theirs. The warning that arrives and is not received becomes, in the very fact of its non-reception, a proof. The light reaches the room. What each person does with it — that has already been written in the word that is here being confirmed.

### The weight of the preposition

The word "proven *against*" them uses the preposition *ʿalā*, which carries the sense of weight, of something resting *upon* a person. The proven word does not merely exist somewhere — it *bears down upon* those against whom it has been established. Al-Jalālayn's framing completes the logic of the whole trio: the One capable of reversing human creation — "which is already known to them" — is by that same capacity able to raise the dead. They should believe. *Fa-yuʾminūn.* The word "should" here is not a gentle suggestion. It is the conclusion that follows necessarily from the premises already in their experience. The only thing standing between them and that conclusion is a tether they chose to slip.

[PAUSE]

The body folded backward by the hand of the Creator: a mercy, a sign, a living proof of resurrection available to anyone who will look. The word folded across the recitation: a reminder, a warning, a clarity. For the living — a path forward. For those who refuse to live — a testimony, patient and exact, resting upon them until the Day when it is finally actualized in full.

# PART TWO: THE THREAD

Now we have enough to return to the question the introduction asked you to carry.

*Why does Allah answer a charge about language by first showing us a charge about aging?*

### The hidden kinship

Poetry ages. That is the thing no Arab champion of the word wanted to admit. The most celebrated poems of the fair circuits — the ones listeners wept over, the ones that inspired envy and admiration — were already in competition the next season. And the poets who made them? Mortal. Subject to the same *tankīs*, the same folding backward, that made the old warrior tremble and forget.

Al-Ṭabarī's phrase stays with you here: "he comes to know nothing after the knowledge he once had." The most formidable poetic mind of its generation, given enough time, arrives at the same destination as every other mind. The library is returned. The craft dissolves.

The word that emerges from the human imagination is subject to the same law as the human body that produces it: it rises, it peaks, it reverses, it fades. This is what *shiʿr* — poetry — ultimately is: the human creative faculty in its highest expression. Beautiful. Perishable.

And here is what the Qur'an is claiming by refusing that title: **it is not the product of the human creative faculty.** It did not emerge from a mind that will be reversed. It was not taught by the school that produces things which fade. It came from somewhere that does not fold, from a Source that is not subject to the *tankīs* that governs everything that comes from human hands and human mouths.

That is why the aged body is the answer to the poetry accusation. The deniers were saying: this is human creative output, very impressive human creative output, but human all the same — and therefore subject to all the laws that govern human things, including the law that says brilliant things eventually become dust.

And Allah says: look at an old man.

Look at a man whose body was created in one state and is being deliberately returned to that state. That returning — that fold, pressed in with weight — is a demonstration, running live in your experience, that the Creator is not subject to the same rules as the creation. He can begin things, run them forward to strength, and then fold them back. He is the Author of the arc, not a subject within it.

The word that comes from such a Source does not have to decay like the body. The body decays because it is creation. The *dhikr* does not decay because it points *beyond* creation, to the One who made it.

[PAUSE]

### Two arcs, opposite directions

Here is the final image I want to give you for all three ayahs held together.

There are two arcs at work in this passage.

The first arc bends backward. It is the arc of the body — from weakness, to strength, to weakness again. It is also the arc of every human word — from originality, to fame, to obscurity. Everything that comes from the human hand describes this arc: created, flourishing, reversed. The poem fades. The poet is forgotten. The body folds. The knowledge drains. Al-Muyassar's pairing is precise: weakness of the mind and weakness of the body, together, along the same crease. Even the greatest accumulation of human learning and eloquence is subject to this arc.

The second arc bends forward. It is the arc of the warning that reaches the living — the person who receives the *dhikr* and is woken up by it, who has that frightening experience of reading the Qur'an and feeling it read *them* back, who hears the word about the body folded backward and suddenly sees their own aging hands differently. That person's interior life, which was drifting in one direction, begins to move the other way. The one who was spiritually inert and receives the reminder and responds — they are folded *forward*, not backward. Revived.

That is the structure of these three ayahs held in the hand:

**A body folded backward** — the sign that the Creator authors reversals. Proof for resurrection. Proof that states are not fixed. Proof that the long life you called a gift contains, within it, a gentle and continuous demonstration that you are in the hands of One who moves things where He will.

**A Recitation that is not poetry** — the defense of the Word as something that does not belong to the arc of decay. Not a human product. Not subject to the laws of human creative output. A reminder that returns you to truth, clear as light, making manifest what the mind's drift had hidden.

**A warning for whoever is alive** — the purpose. This Word was sent to do something. Not to be admired. Not to be analyzed. To wake whoever can still be woken. And to stand as patient testimony against those who, given the warning, chose to remain folded shut.

## Closing: The Question That Stays With You

There is a question these three ayahs leave open, and I want to name it because I think it is the question they are designed to leave.

What kind of *ḥayy* are you?

Not: are you breathing? Not: are you functioning, intelligent, informed, even devout by outward measure? But: is there something in you that the *dhikr* can still reach? Is there a part of your interior life that, when it hears a word pointing to something real, is still capable of being gripped?

The Qur'an does not warn everyone. It warns whoever is *alive* — and the classical commentators understood this not as a limitation on who *deserves* to hear it but as a description of who can *actually receive* it. The warning is not withheld from the sealed heart out of cruelty. It simply cannot land there. The soil is stone.

But here is the mercy hidden in the architecture of the passage.

The fact that you are sitting with this text, that you are attending to these words, that you felt something when the image of the folded body came to you — these are signs that the door is still open. The sealed heart does not seek out the *dhikr*. It does not wonder about these ayahs. It does not feel the weight of the word that might be proven against it. The very experience of being moved by this passage is evidence that something in you is still alive to be moved.

The body will fold. That is written. The arc bends backward for all of us, and it bends with the deliberateness of a Hand that knows exactly where the crease is. Al-Ṭabarī's precise phrase waits at the end of that arc: "he comes to know nothing after the knowledge he once had." Al-Muyassar names what accompanies it: weakness of the mind and weakness of the body together, the initial state restored. There is nothing to be done about that arc. It is the sign built into the creation.

But the interior fold — the one that goes in the other direction — that is still yours. Not because you control it by will but because you are still, as you sit here, among the living by the scale that matters. The *ūlū l-albāb*, those of deep understanding, are not people of greater intelligence. They are people of greater *attentiveness*. They hear the *dhikr* and let it do what a reminder does: restore. Return. Fold them forward into what was always true.

[PAUSE]

You have watched people you love begin to fold backward. Maybe you have watched the knowledge drain from them slowly. The name forgotten. The face unrecognized. The words slipping. If that sight still moves you — if it still places in your chest that specific kind of awe that is very close to fear — then you are still alive to it. The *ʿaql* is still in your hand. The tether is there.

Use it. Now. While the rope is in your hand.

*Li-yundhira man kāna ḥayyan.*

This warning was sent for you.
