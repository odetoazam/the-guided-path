# The Two Walls and the Veil
### A Reflection on Surah Ya-Sin, Ayah 9

> [ayah:36:9] وَجَعَلْنَا مِنۢ بَيْنِ أَيْدِيهِمْ سَدًّا وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ سَدًّا فَأَغْشَيْنَـٰهُمْ فَهُمْ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ
>
> *Wa jaʿalnā min bayni aydīhim saddan wa min khalfihim saddan fa-aghshaynāhum fahum lā yubṣirūn.*
>
> **"And We have placed before them a barrier and behind them a barrier, and We covered them over, so they do not see."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let's set the scene before we touch a single word.

We are at the very opening of Surah Ya-Sin. The surah begins with two mysterious letters — Yā, Sīn — and then an oath: *by the wise Quran.* Allah swears that the Messenger ﷺ is among those sent, upon a straight path. And then the mission is named: he is sent to warn a people **whose fathers were not warned, so they are heedless** (36:6).

Heedless. *Ghāfilūn.* Hold that word in your mind, because everything that follows is a description of what heedlessness becomes if you let it grow.

Then comes a verse that should stop us cold. Allah says: **the word has already been justified against most of them, so they will not believe** (36:7). And then He gives us the image — the picture of what that state actually looks like from the inside:

**"Indeed, We have placed iron collars around their necks, reaching up to the chins, so their heads are forced up"** (36:8).

Picture it. A yoke. An iron collar so high and so rigid that the head is locked, the chin lifted, the gaze frozen — unable to look down, unable to look around. A person pinned in a single posture.

And then — *immediately* — comes our ayah. The image continues. The same prisoner. Allah adds: a wall rises up in front of him. A wall rises up behind him. And then, as if walls were not enough, a covering is drawn down over his very eyes.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what you must feel before we go further. The original listeners — the Quraysh of Makkah — were not strangers being described by a distant book. Some of them were *in the room.* Some of them were the very people who had stood before the Prophet ﷺ with stones in their hands. Surah Ya-Sin is reciting to them a diagnosis of their own hearts. And the most frightening thing about this ayah is not the punishment it describes.

It is the *mechanism* it describes.

Because this ayah is going to tell us something almost unbearable: that there is a point at which a person stops being unable to believe because of the walls around him — and the walls around him are there *because* of what he chose. Refusal, repeated long enough, stops being a decision. It becomes a condition.

**Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows:** How does a person who *can* see become a person who *cannot* — and at what moment did the door close behind him?

Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: سَدًّا — The Barrier That Comes Twice

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word.

In Arabic, when you want to name a thing precisely, you make it *definite* — you attach the definite article *al-*. "The wall." A specific wall, one you and I both know. But when you leave a noun *indefinite* — *saddan*, with that little *-an* ending we call **tanwīn** — you are doing something different. You are leaving it open. Sometimes that openness means "some barrier or other." But in the Quran's register, indefiniteness very often means *magnification* — a barrier of a kind you cannot fully specify, a barrier beyond your measuring.

Think of the difference between someone saying "there was a problem" and someone saying, with weight, "there was... *a problem.*" The vagueness itself becomes enormous.

Now look at the word.

**سَدًّا** — *saddan.* A barrier. A dam. A blockade.

And before we even ask what it means, *listen* to it. Say it out loud: *sadd.* That doubled *dāl* in the middle — in Arabic we mark it with a *shadda*, a sign that the consonant is pressed twice, held, stopped. The word does not flow. It *slams.* It is a sound that closes. The Arabs took this very root, *s-d-d*, and from it named the great dam that Dhul-Qarnayn built against Gog and Magog — *as-sadd* — a wall of iron and molten copper. This is not a curtain. This is masonry. This is a sound built like a wall.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the structure. Allah does not say "We placed a barrier around them." He says:

**مِنۢ بَيْنِ أَيْدِيهِمْ سَدًّا** — *before them, a barrier.*
**وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِمْ سَدًّا** — *and behind them, a barrier.*

The word *saddan* is spoken **twice.** Front and back. And the indefinite tanwīn sits on both — a barrier of unmeasured size in front, a barrier of unmeasured size behind.

Let's sit with that phrase, *min bayni aydīhim* — literally, "from between their hands." It is an Arabic idiom for what is *in front* of you, what lies *ahead.* And *min khalfihim* — "from behind them," what lies *behind*, what came *before* in time.

And here is where I want you to read what is *absent* as carefully as what is present.

Allah seals the front. Allah seals the back. He does **not** mention the left. He does **not** mention the right. He does **not** mention above or below. Only two directions. Why these two?

Because these are not merely the two directions of a body in space. *In front of you* is your future — what is coming, the consequences racing toward you, the warning of what lies ahead. *Behind you* is your past — history, the generations that came before, the ruins of the nations who denied and were destroyed, the lessons sedimented in everything that already happened.

Do you see what the two walls actually block?

**A human being learns in exactly two directions.** We learn by looking *back* — drawing lessons from the past, from history, from consequence already witnessed. And we are warned by looking *forward* — anticipating where a road leads, seeing the cliff before we reach it. Faith arrives through both doors: *remember what happened to those before you,* and *consider what is coming.*

Wall off the past, and history teaches you nothing — every ruin is just rubble, never a warning. Wall off the future, and consequence is invisible — you walk the same road that destroyed others and feel perfectly safe. **Seal both, and a person is locked in a permanent, sealed present** — unable to learn from what was, unable to fear what will be.

[PAUSE]

That is the architecture of the verse. Not a vague "they're trapped." A precise enclosure that severs the two channels through which a heart receives guidance.

One small but beautiful note the scholars preserve: this word was recited in two ways. Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, and Ḥafṣ read it *saddan*, with a *fatḥa* (an "a" sound); the others read *suddan*, with a *ḍamma* (a "u" sound). The classical commentators — including al-Baghawī, who records the difference — note that both readings sit comfortably on the same meaning of barrier and obstruction. The reading does not divide the meaning. It deepens our certainty of it.

So what does this single word, spoken twice, reveal?

**That the prison in this verse is not a cell with bars you can see through. It is a sealing of the two horizons — past and future — through which a soul receives light. And it slams shut with the sound of its own name.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: فَأَغْشَيْنَـٰهُمْ — And Then, the Veil Over the Eyes

Now watch what Allah does next, because it changes everything.

He has already walled them in. Front and back. You would think the image is complete. A person sealed between two barriers can no longer move toward guidance, can no longer move back toward the lessons of the past. What more could be needed?

And then comes one word:

**فَأَغْشَيْنَـٰهُمْ** — *fa-aghshaynāhum.* "And so We covered them over."

Let me teach you two things hidden in this single word.

First, that little *fa-* at the front. In Arabic, *fa-* is the particle of *consequence and sequence.* It means "and so," "and then, as a result." It is not the neutral "and" (*wa-*) that simply joins two things in a list. *Fa-* tells you that what comes next *follows from* what came before — in time, in logic, in cause. The walls came first. *And then, as the next move,* the covering.

Second, the verb itself. The root is *gh-sh-w*, and the basic verb *ghashiya* means to cover, to envelop, to come over something the way night comes over the day, the way a wave comes over a swimmer. But Allah does not use the simple form here. He uses **Form IV** — *aghshā* — which adds a causative force: *to make a covering descend over,* to cause something to be overspread. Al-Baghawī glosses it directly: *fa-aʿmaynāhum* — "We blinded them" — from *taghshiya*, which he says simply means *at-taghṭiya*: covering, draping, drawing something down over a thing.

Now feel the layering. Stay with me here, because this is the heart of it.

They are **already** walled in — front and back. A person walled in front and back can still *see* the walls. He can still see his own hands, the ground, the light overhead. His eyes still work. He is trapped, but he is not blind.

And *then* — *fa-* — Allah draws a covering down over the eyes themselves.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the terrible completeness of it? It is two distinct acts of sealing. The first seals the *world* — the avenues out. The second seals the *organ of perception* — the very capacity to see what is right in front of you. It is not enough that the road to guidance is blocked; now the traveler cannot even *perceive* that there is a road, or a block, or anything at all.

This is the difference between locking a man in a room and locking a man in a room *and then* taking away his eyes. The first man knows he is imprisoned. The second man does not even know there is such a thing as outside.

And here is what the classical scholars want us to understand about *how* this happens — because the order matters, theologically. Look at the sequence the surah itself lays down. Verse 7: *the word has been justified against them* — because of what they did, through their own sustained refusal. Verse 8: the collars. Verse 9: the walls, *and then* the covering. The Tafsīr al-Muyassar states it plainly: *We blinded their sight* — and then names the cause — *because of their disbelief and their arrogance* (*bi-sababi kufrihim wa-stikbārihim*). And it ends with a principle that should make every one of us pause: *everyone who meets the call of Islam with turning-away and obstinacy is deserving of this very recompense.*

So this is not Allah reaching down and arbitrarily blinding an innocent. The *fa-* of consequence runs through the entire passage. The covering is the *settled state* of a perception that was, again and again and again, turned away by its owner — until the turning-away became the eyes' new shape.

What does this word reveal?

**That divine sealing is not the beginning of the story. It is the end of it. The veil over the eyes is the final form of a refusal that the heart practiced so many times it forgot how to do anything else.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: لَا يُبْصِرُونَ — They Do Not See... What?

Now the ayah closes, and it closes on a word that is doing more than it appears to.

**فَهُمْ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ** — *fahum lā yubṣirūn.* "So they do not see."

Three things here, and each one opens a door.

**First, the verb itself.** It comes from the root *b-ṣ-r.* And in Arabic, *baṣar* is not only the light hitting the eye. The root *b-ṣ-r* carries *both* physical sight *and* inward insight — perception, discernment, the seeing that *understands.* From this very root comes *baṣīra*: insight, the eye of the heart. When Yūsuf's father regains his sight in Surah Yūsuf, the word is from this root. When the Quran says of the Day of Judgment *"your sight today is iron-sharp"* (50:22), it is *baṣar.* So when Allah says *lā yubṣirūn,* He is not merely saying their retinas have failed. He is saying the faculty of *seeing-into-things,* of perceiving meaning, of discerning the truth standing in front of them — that faculty is dark.

**Second, the tense.** Notice Allah does not say "they did not see," a closed past event. He uses the *imperfect* — *yubṣirūn* — the form for ongoing, continuous, present-and-future action. *They do not — and go on not — seeing.* It is not a single failure. It is a permanent condition, unspooling into the future. The walls were built (past tense, *jaʿalnā*). The covering was drawn (past tense, *aghshaynā*). But the blindness is *ongoing* — *lā yubṣirūn* — because it is no longer an act. It is now simply who they are.

**Third — and read this absence carefully — what is the object?**

In Arabic, a verb of seeing usually wants an object. You see *something.* But Allah leaves it open. *Lā yubṣirūn* — "they do not see" — and then... nothing. No object. They do not see *what?*

The classical commentators reach for the missing word, and notice they do not all reach for the same one — and that this is a mercy, not a confusion. Al-Baghawī completes it: *they do not see the path of guidance — sabīl al-hudā.* The Muyassar completes it: *they do not see right direction, nor are they guided — lā yubṣirūna rushdan wa-lā yahtadūn.* Each is a true completion. But the *grammar leaves the blank open* — and an open blank includes them all. They do not see the path. They do not see the proof. They do not see the Messenger standing before them. They do not see the ruins of the nations behind them. They do not see what is coming. They do not see *anything that matters.* The unspecified object makes the blindness total — it spreads to fill every truth they have turned from.

[PAUSE]

And this connects to one of the most piercing verses in the whole Quran, which Allah places in Surah al-Aʿrāf: *"They have eyes with which they do not see"* (7:179). The eyes are intact. The seeing is gone. There is no greater image of loss than a working eye behind a sealed heart.

What does this closing word reveal?

**That the punishment is not a darkening of the eyes. It is a darkening of the seeing. The lights are on, the eyes are open — and the man stands in front of the truth and perceives nothing at all.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what the construction has shown us. Three movements, each tightening on the one before:

The **two walls** — *saddan, saddan* — sealing the past and the future, the two horizons through which guidance reaches a heart.

The **covering** — *fa-aghshaynāhum* — drawn down, *as a consequence,* over the very organ of sight, so that even the walls are no longer perceived.

The **ongoing blindness** — *lā yubṣirūn* — not an event but a settled condition, total because its object is left unnamed.

A wall before. A wall behind. A veil over the eyes. A blindness with no bottom.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because every one of us has stood, at some point, closer to that prison than we would like to admit.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How a Choice Becomes a Cage

Let me begin where you already live.

There is a thing your mind does, every single day, that scientists have studied for decades and given a name: *motivated reasoning.* Here is how it works. Once you have decided what you *want* to be true — about yourself, about someone you resent, about a belief you've staked your identity on — your mind quietly stops being a judge and becomes a defense lawyer. It no longer asks *"is this true?"* It asks *"how can I keep believing what I already believe?"*

And the most chilling part the researchers found is this: it is not that you weigh the evidence and reject it. It is that **you stop seeing it.** Information that threatens what you've decided becomes, to your attention, nearly invisible. You scroll past it. It doesn't register. Two people read the same article and one of them genuinely does not perceive the paragraph that demolishes his position. He's not lying. He simply did not *see* it.

Now hold that beside our ayah.

Because the Quran described this fourteen centuries before there was a laboratory. *Fa-aghshaynāhum fahum lā yubṣirūn.* We covered them over, so they do not see. The Quran is telling you that there is a spiritual physics to refusal — that what you turn away from, you eventually lose the capacity to face.

But here is where the ayah goes deeper than any psychology paper, and I need you to follow this carefully.

The researcher says: *your bias shapes your perception.* True. But the Quran adds a dimension the laboratory cannot measure. It says: **"We" placed the wall. "We" drew the covering.** *Jaʿalnā. Aghshaynā.* The first person. The divine hand.

This is the staggering thing about the verse. It refuses to let the blindness be *only* psychological — a mere quirk of the brain. And it refuses to let it be *only* divine — an arbitrary sentence from above. It holds both at once. Read the surrounding verses and you cannot escape it: the word was justified against them *because of what they did*; the Muyassar names the cause — *their disbelief and their arrogance.* And yet the act of sealing is attributed to Allah. *We* did it.

How do both stand together? Let me give you the image that the early scholars themselves leaned on. Think of a man who steps out of the sunlight, walks into a cave, and keeps walking deeper, by his own legs, choosing each step, deeper and deeper away from the mouth of the cave. At some point he is in total darkness. Now — *who* darkened him? Did he turn off the sun? No. The sun is exactly as bright as it always was. The light is the same; the law of the cave is the same; *the one who set up the world so that walking-away-from-light-ends-in-darkness is Allah.* The man walked. Allah is the one who made walking-into-the-dark *mean* darkness. **Both are completely true. The steps were his. The law was God's.**

[PAUSE]

That is *fa-aghshaynāhum.* The covering is God's. The walking toward it was theirs.

And now the part that should reach inside your chest. The verse is not really a description of *them* — those distant deniers. It is a *warning to you.* Because the cave has a slope, and every soul is standing somewhere on it. Every time you encounter a truth about yourself that you'd rather not face — a sin you've normalized, a relationship you're wronging, a reckoning you keep postponing — and you turn from it, *a little of the seeing goes.* Not all at once. The walls of Surah Ya-Sin were not built in a day. They were built one refusal at a time, until *min bayni aydīhim saddan* — the barrier rose, and the man who built it could no longer remember laying the first stone.

**The terror of this verse is not that God blinds people. It is that He has made a world in which refusing to see is a thing you can practice until you're good at it.**

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practical edge, and it is sharp. The single most important spiritual habit this ayah demands is the willingness to *look at the thing you do not want to look at.* When you feel that flinch — that instinct to scroll past, to change the subject in your own head, to say "I'll deal with that later" about the very thing your conscience keeps raising — recognize it. That flinch is the trowel reaching for the first stone of the wall. The protection against the blindness of 36:9 is the daily, deliberate, uncomfortable practice of turning *toward* the truth about yourself while you still can. While the eyes still see.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Mercy Hidden Inside the Threat

Now let me show you something that may reframe this entire ayah — because at first hearing it sounds like nothing but terror, and I don't want you to leave with only terror. The mercy is here too, and it is precisely *because* of how the verse is built.

Begin, again, where you live. Think of a doctor delivering a hard diagnosis. There are two kinds. One says, "It's over. There's nothing to be done." The other says, "Here is exactly what is happening inside you, here is the mechanism, here is how far it has gone." The second doctor is frightening — but notice: he is frightening *because he still thinks you can act.* You do not explain the mechanism to a corpse. You explain it to someone you are trying to save.

So ask: *to whom is Surah Ya-Sin being recited?*

[PAUSE]

It is being recited to the very people of Makkah — including some who *were* the deniers, who *did* raise their hands against the Prophet ﷺ. The classical commentators preserve a narration — al-Qurṭubī reports it on the authority of Muqātil — about Abū Jahl and a stone: that he came to harm the Prophet ﷺ and found, when he drew near, that Allah had veiled his sight so that he could not see him at all, and he returned to his companions unable even to find them until they called out to him. Now — the scholars treat the ayah primarily as a *tamthīl*, a representation; al-Jalālayn says explicitly it is *tamthīl li-saddi ṭuruqi-l-īmāni ʿalayhim* — a likeness drawn for the blocking of the paths of faith upon them. The narration is one reported occasion; the verse's meaning is larger than any single incident. But sit with the staggering fact underneath it: **this devastating description of sealed hearts was being recited out loud, in the hearing of the very people it described — some of whom later believed.**

Do you understand what that means?

It means the wall in this verse, in this life, **is not yet the final wall.** It is a description of a *direction,* a warning of a *destination* — recited precisely so that the one walking toward it might stop. The man Abū Jahl, who could not see the Prophet ﷺ that day — the door was, in that moment, *described* as shut. But the recitation itself was God still calling. The Quran does not waste words on the truly finished. *The very existence of this ayah in your ears is proof that you are not the person it describes* — because the sealed do not hear warnings as warnings. They scroll past. And you... you are still here. Still reading. Still flinching, which means still feeling, which means the eyes still see.

[PAUSE]

This is the mercy folded inside the threat. The most frightening verse about blindness is itself an act of opening. Look at the order of the whole surah: the warning comes in verse 9 — but the surah does not end there. It goes on, for eighty more verses, telling the story of the man from the far end of the city who *ran* to his people calling them to the Messengers (36:20); it tells of the sun and moon, each swimming in its orbit (36:40); it tells of bones brought back to life (36:78–79). The surah that contains the harshest image of the sealed eye is the same surah that the Prophet ﷺ called *the heart of the Quran* — a surah saturated, from end to end, with the call to *see* again.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the way you receive your own fear. When a verse like this frightens you — when you read about the covered eyes and something in you goes cold — do not run from that fear, and do not drown in it either. **Receive it as the doctor's voice, not the coroner's.** The fact that it stings is the proof your sight is intact. Let the sting move you to do the one thing the blind cannot do: turn around. Make the istighfār. Repair the thing you've been postponing. Open the Quran you've been avoiding. The trembling you feel at this ayah is not the sound of the door closing. **It is the sound of someone, mercifully, knocking — while it is still open.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see how the ayah is built, because the structure is the proof.

Look at the order of the three movements, and notice it runs from *outside* to *inside*:

First, **the walls** — *out there,* in the world, sealing the horizons of past and future.

Then, drawn closer, **the covering** — *fa-aghshaynāhum* — descending onto the *body,* over the eyes.

Then, deepest of all, **the blindness** — *lā yubṣirūn* — *inside,* in the faculty of seeing itself.

The verse moves like a hand closing: from the far walls, to the face, to the eye, to the seeing behind the eye. Outside, to surface, to depth. And every step is bound to the last by the logic of *fa-* — consequence following consequence. It is not a list of three punishments. It is *one* sealing, tracing its way from the world all the way down into the soul.

And here is the architectural proof of everything we've said: the blindness is named *last.* It is the destination, not the starting point. The verse itself is built so that the inner darkness arrives only at the end of a sequence — exactly as it arrives in a human life: only at the end of a long sequence of refusals.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The **words** showed us a sealing that moves from the world inward and hardens into a permanent, ongoing condition — *jaʿalnā* and *aghshaynā* in the closed past, *lā yubṣirūn* in the open, unending present.

The **themes** showed us *how* a soul arrives there — by practicing refusal until refusal becomes its nature — and *why* the very verse that warns of it is itself the mercy that can still pull a soul back.

One sentence, two roads to it: **what you will not look at, you slowly lose the power to see — and the God who built that law into the world is, by telling you about it, still holding the door.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the truth about myself that I have been *scrolling past* — the one my conscience keeps raising and I keep changing the subject? How long have I been laying that wall?
2. When a hard reminder frightens me, do I receive it as a coroner's verdict or a doctor's diagnosis — and what does my reaction reveal about whether I still believe I can change?
3. Are there ruins *behind* me — past consequences, my own or others' — that I have walled off so I learn nothing from them? Are there warnings *ahead* of me I refuse to look toward?
4. The deniers in this verse had working eyes. Where in my life are my eyes open and my *seeing* dark?
5. If the existence of this warning in my ears is proof the door is still open — what is the one thing I will turn toward today, before I lose the wanting to?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Refusal, practiced long enough, builds its own prison — but the warning that names the prison is itself the door, still open, for everyone who can still tremble at the sound of it.**

### The Closing Invitation

You are not meant to leave this verse afraid. You are meant to leave it *awake.* The whole purpose of describing the sealed eye to people who can still see is to make them *use the sight while they have it.* So let this be the shift: the next time you feel that small flinch away from a truth about yourself, do not obey it. Turn toward the thing. Look at it. That single act of turning — repeated, made into a habit — is the opposite of the wall. It is how a soul stays open. Not because you are strong, but because the door is still open, and you were honored with eyes that still see.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who draw coverings and lift them — do not let our refusals harden into walls, nor our turning-away into blindness. Keep our eyes open to the truth we would rather not face, and let every warning that reaches us be a door You are holding, not a door closing behind us. Make us, by Your mercy, of those who see — and who, seeing, turn back to You while the turning is still ours to make.* Āmīn.
