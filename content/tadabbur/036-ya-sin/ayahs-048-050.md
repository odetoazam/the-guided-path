# The Question That Couldn't Wait for Its Answer

## Surah Ya-Sin, Ayahs 48–50

> **وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هَٰذَا ٱلْوَعْدُ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ ۝ مَا يَنظُرُونَ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً تَأْخُذُهُمْ وَهُمْ يَخِصِّمُونَ ۝ فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ تَوْصِيَةً وَلَآ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِمْ يَرْجِعُونَ**

*Wa yaqūlūna matā hādhā-l-waʿd, in kuntum ṣādiqīn. Mā yanẓurūna illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan ta'khudhuhum wa hum yakhiṣṣimūn. Fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna tawṣiyatan wa lā ilā ahlihim yarjiʿūn.*

> **"And they say, 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?' They await only a single Blast that will seize them while they are still quarrelling. Then they will not be able to make a bequest, nor will they return to their own people."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

Imagine the streets of Makkah in the early years. A man stands among his people and tells them something that, frankly, sounds impossible. He tells them that the dead — the ones already turned to dust, the ones whose bones have crumbled and scattered — will be brought back. He tells them there is a Day coming. A reckoning. A promise.

And how do they respond?

Not with a syllogism. Not with a philosopher's careful objection. They respond with the oldest weapon of the comfortable: **the smirk.** They say, *matā hādhā-l-waʿd* — "So… when is it? When's this big promise of yours? Go on, give us a date. If you're so truthful, name the day."

Now, notice what has just happened. They have not asked an honest question. An honest question wants an answer. **Their question is built to avoid one.** It is a question shaped like a question but functioning as a dismissal — the way someone today might roll their eyes and say, *"Oh sure, and when exactly is the world going to end? Tuesday?"*

This is the world of these ayahs. A world where the gravest reality a human can face has been turned into a punchline. Where the demand for a timestamp is a way of saying *I don't have to take this seriously until you prove it on my terms.*

And here is the hook. Here is what should make us lean in.

They asked, *"When?"*

[PAUSE]

The next two ayahs are going to answer that question. But the answer is not a date. The answer is not a year, or a sign, or a countdown. The answer is something far more unsettling than any date could ever be.

The answer is going to arrive **before they finish the sentence.**

Stay with me. Let's go and see how the Quran answers a mocking "when?" — and why its answer should change the way you and I live this very afternoon.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Matā* — The Question Designed Not to Be Answered

Let me begin with the smallest word in the passage, because it carries the whole weight of their hearts.

The word is **مَتَىٰ — *matā*.** It simply means *"when."* It is the interrogative of time. You and I use its equivalent a hundred times a day. *When's dinner? When does the train come? When will you be home?*

Here is something worth teaching first, before we go to the ayah. There are two completely different ways a human being can ask "when."

The first is the *when* of someone who already believes, and simply wants to prepare. A pregnant woman asks her doctor, *"When is the baby due?"* — she is not doubting the baby. She is **getting ready.** Her "when" is the question of a person already arranging the nursery.

The second is the *when* of someone who has already decided the thing isn't real, and is using the question as a trap. A teenager who has no intention of cleaning his room says, *"When? Like… when exactly?"* — and you know, you can hear it, that this "when" is not a request for information. It is a way of pushing the whole thing away. It is **deferral wearing the costume of curiosity.**

Now open the ayah: *wa yaqūlūna matā hādhā-l-waʿd.*

Notice the verb attached to it — *yaqūlūna,* "they keep saying." It is in the ongoing, repeating tense. This is not a single sincere inquiry asked once. This is a refrain. A habit. Something they *say* — over and over — whenever the subject comes up.

And the classical commentators heard exactly this tone. Al-Qurṭubī, may Allah have mercy on him, ties this question directly to what came just before it: they had been warned, *"Beware of what is before you and what is behind you, that you may receive mercy."* And **their entire reply to that warning was this sneer.** Al-Qurṭubī says plainly: *kāna hādhā istihzā'an minhum* — "this was an act of mockery on their part," meaning, *there is no reality to this threat at all.*

Al-Muyassar adds the second layer: they said it *ʿalā wajhi-t-takdhīb wa-l-istiʿjāl* — "by way of denial **and** of demanding it be hastened." Look at the contradiction inside their own hearts. They don't believe it's coming — *and yet* they're impatient for it to come. They mock its existence while taunting it to hurry up. That is not the posture of a mind weighing evidence. That is the posture of a heart that has already slammed the door and is now kicking it for sport.

[PAUSE]

So here is what this one small word reveals. *Matā* — "when" — is the question of a soul that wants the future on its own terms. It wants a date so it can decide whether to bother. It wants proof delivered to its doorstep before it will rise from its chair. And the deepest danger of this question is not that it's rude.

It's that **it assumes there will be time.** It assumes the asker controls the schedule. It assumes the gap between "now" and "then" is wide, and comfortable, and his to spend as he likes.

Hold onto that assumption. The very next ayah is going to take it apart.

---

## Element Two: *Ṣayḥatan Wāḥidatan* — One Blast, and the Withheld Plural

They asked *when.* Watch how Allah answers — not with a calendar, but with a sound.

*Mā yanẓurūna illā **ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan.*** "They await nothing but a single blast."

Let me first teach the grammatical shape, because the meaning hides inside the grammar.

The sentence is built on a frame the Arabs called *qaṣr* — restriction. *Mā… illā* — "nothing… except." It is the most exclusive frame in the language. It draws a circle and says: *everything is outside this circle except the one thing inside it.* In English we'd feel it as: *"All they are waiting for — the only thing left on their horizon — is this one thing."*

And what is the one thing? **ṣayḥah.** From the root that means a shout, a scream, a cry so loud it tears the air. The *ṣayḥah* is a sound. And here we touch the heart of how the Quran teaches: it gives us something we can *hear* — a blast, a cry — in order to point us toward something we cannot imagine: the instant the entire created order is seized at once. The physical sound is the stepping-stone. The destination is a reality with no image we could hold.

But now — *notice something.* Notice the word right beside it: **wāḥidah. One.**

Why specify *one*?

Stay with this, because the answer is in what Allah did **not** say.

In our world, catastrophe usually announces itself. There are tremors before the earthquake. There is a horizon that darkens before the storm. There are warnings, sirens, a first shock and then a second. We are a species that lives in the gap between the first sign and the real thing — and we have built our whole psychology of procrastination in that gap. *I'll deal with it when it gets close. I'll change when I see the signs.*

And Allah says: **wāḥidah. One.**

Not a first blast and then a second to confirm it. Not a warning shot. Not a rumble that gives you the afternoon to get your affairs in order. **One.** The first blast *is* the only blast. The warning *is* the event. There is no gap to live in.

The classical commentators name this blast with precision. Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both identify it as *nafkhat al-fazaʿ* — the blast of alarm and terror — at the rising of the Hour. Al-Jalalayn is more specific still: it is *nafkhat Isrāfīl al-ūlā*, the **first** blast of the angel Israfil, not the second. This distinction carries weight. The second blast, the one that raises the dead for judgment, is the very event the mockers were deriding when they said *matā*. But they will never reach it on their own terms. The one sound that is all they are waiting for is not the sound of their resurrection — it is the sound that kills and seizes them before the resurrection arrives. The thing they treated as a punchline is still ahead of them; they simply will not be awake for the approach.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened to their question? They asked *matā* — "when, give me time, give me a runway." And the answer carries within it the demolition of the very runway they were standing on. The thing they were counting on — the *interval*, the *advance notice*, the *space to get ready later* — that thing does not exist. There is only **one** sound, and by the time you hear it, it has already taken you.

And then comes the verb: *ta'khudhuhum* — "it **seizes** them." Not "it reaches them," not "it informs them." It *takes* them — the way a hand closes around something. The grammar makes the *ṣayḥah* the active one and the people the passive object. They spent their lives being the ones who demanded, who questioned, who set the terms. In a single instant the roles invert completely. **Now they are the ones being taken.**

This is what the missing plural was protecting. *Wāḥidah* — "one" — is not a detail. It is the whole theology of the verse. **You do not get a second sound. The first one is for keeps.**

---

## Element Three: *Yakhiṣṣimūn* — The Word That Tangles Your Own Tongue

Now we come to what I think is the most astonishing thing in this entire passage. And to feel it, you have to hear it more than understand it.

The ayah ends: *ta'khudhuhum **wa hum yakhiṣṣimūn*** — "it seizes them while they are *yakhiṣṣimūn*."

The word means *while they are quarrelling, disputing, squabbling with one another.* It comes from the root for adversary, opponent, the two sides of a dispute. So the picture is this: the blast catches them mid-argument. Not in prayer. Not in repentance. Not in some grand sin even. **In a quarrel.** Haggling over a price in the market. Arguing about a debt. Bickering over a boundary line. The small, sour, forgettable friction that fills so much of a human day.

The classical commentators do not let this picture stay small. Al-Jalalayn opens it wider: the state the blast finds them in is described as *fī ghafla ʿanhā* — "in complete heedlessness of it" — and *yakhiṣṣimūn* is shorthand for the entire range of that heedlessness: not only quarrelling, but buying and selling, eating and drinking. The word for "disputing" is a synecdoche. It is a window onto the full texture of a life absorbed in itself — a market day, a meal, a transaction, an argument — all of it equally deaf to the reality approaching from outside the frame of ordinary hours. The quarrel is just the particular sound that was playing when the music stopped.

But now — *say the word.* Try it. *Yakhiṣṣimūn.*

Feel what your mouth has to do. The tongue presses, doubles back on itself, gets caught on that heavy doubled *ṣ*, stumbles, and has to push through. The word is **difficult to say.** It tangles your own tongue in the act of pronouncing it.

This is not an accident. Let me show you the morphology, because it is exquisite.

The original form of this verb is *yakhtaṣimūn* — a Form VIII verb, the form Arabic uses for two parties doing something *to each other,* the reciprocal form, perfect for "disputing with one another." But in this word, the *t* of that form has been swallowed — assimilated — into the *ṣ* that follows it, collapsing two sounds into one heavy, doubled, straining consonant: *yakhi**ṣṣ**imūn.* Al-Jalalayn describes the mechanics precisely: the vowel of the *tā'* was first transferred to the *khā'*, and then the *tā'* itself was assimilated into the *ṣād* — two sounds colliding until one absorbs the other entirely. (The early reciters preserved this in several documented readings — *yakhiṣṣimūn, yakhaṣṣimūn, yikhiṣṣimūn* — and the differences are themselves a record of how hard this cluster is to land. Each reading is a different camera angle on the same struggling sound.)

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done?

He has chosen a word for *squabbling* that makes the listener's own tongue *squabble.* The two sounds inside the word are fighting each other for space — colliding, jostling, refusing to sit side by side — exactly like the two people the word describes. **The form of the word performs its meaning.** When you say *yakhiṣṣimūn,* your mouth is doing in miniature what they are doing with their lives: tangled up in friction, caught in a knot of dispute.

And the *ṣayḥah* arrives **right there.** Mid-tangle. Mid-quarrel. The most trivial possible posture for a human soul to be frozen in forever.

This is the answer to *"when?"* They wanted a majestic timetable. They got this: *you will be taken in the middle of an argument so petty you'll have forgotten what it was about — and you won't even get to finish your sentence.*

A whole reality, delivered in the texture of a single difficult word. This is what it means that Allah's speech is not like our speech.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have built for us. *Matā* — the mocker's demand for time, resting on the assumption that time is his to spend. *Ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* — the answer that there is no second chance, no warning shot, no gap to defer into. And *yakhiṣṣimūn* — the word that catches them, and us, mid-quarrel, the tongue itself stumbling.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the way you and I are actually living right now.

---

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Runway That Isn't There

Let me start where you live. Not in seventh-century Makkah. In your own week.

There is a sentence almost every one of us says, in some form, almost every day. It goes: *"Later."*

*I'll start praying properly later — once things calm down. I'll deal with that relationship later. I'll get serious about my faith later — after the exams, after the move, after the kids are older, after this hard season passes.* We are masters of the deferred self. We carry around an imaginary future version of ourselves who is more disciplined, more devoted, more whole — and we keep handing that future self the bill for everything we don't want to face today.

And here is the thing we never say out loud, but always assume: **that the runway is long.** That there will be a *later* to defer into. That the distance between this moment and the moment of reckoning is wide and soft and ours.

This is *exactly* the assumption buried inside the mockers' *matā.* "When?" is the question of a person who is sure there is a *between* — a comfortable corridor of time stretching out ahead, in which he is the one holding the schedule.

*Wāḥidah.* One. That is the Quranic answer to the whole architecture of deferral. Not "the day is close." Not "you should hurry." But something structurally more devastating than either: **there is no gap.** There is no runway of second chances separating now from then — just a single sound arriving in the space you thought you owned.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir notes that the disbelievers erred not only in denying the Hour but in their assumption that denial granted them safety. The question *"When?"* is not just mockery; it is a strategy. If you never accept a timetable, you never have to prepare. If you never prepare, you can live as though it isn't coming. And if you live as though it isn't coming...

You get caught mid-sentence.

Here is the question this theme leaves us with. Not "when will it come" — we have already been told that this is not a question we get to have answered. The question is: *what am I treating as the runway?* What am I parking in the future, in the comfortable space of "later," that belongs to right now?

The Quran does not tell us the Hour is near in order to cause panic. It tells us the Hour is near in order to end procrastination — to collapse the false distance between who I am today and who I keep promising to become. The mockers built their whole lives on that distance. *Wāḥidah* — one sound — removed it entirely.

---

## Theme Two: What You're Doing When It Comes

Now let me ask you something that might be uncomfortable.

If the blast came right now — in this exact moment — what would it catch you in?

Not in some theoretical future. **Right now.** Mid-sentence of this very thought.

The ayah gives us the picture: *wa hum yakhiṣṣimūn.* They were in a dispute. Not in prostration. Not in some dramatic final sin. In a dispute — probably about something small. The kind of argument where, an hour later, you can't even remember who started it.

And when the classical commentators open this image, they do not let it stay confined to a quarrel. They are *fī ghafla ʿanhā* — "in complete heedlessness of it." They are buying and selling. They are eating and drinking. They are at the market, at the table, mid-transaction, mid-meal. *Yakhiṣṣimūn* is not just the sound of two men arguing. It is the sound of a whole civilization completely absorbed in itself — the ordinary machinery of a day in which the remembrance of Allah has no room to enter.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing that should make us sit still for a moment.

They were not caught *despite* being busy. They were caught *because* they were busy. Their business was their heedlessness. Their transactions were their distance from the truth. Their quarrel was proof that the blast had not entered their calculations at all — because if it had, you do not argue about a price with someone you know you might never see again after this afternoon. You do not stay in the argument. You step out of it.

The posture of the caught is the posture of the absorbed.

This is not a scene about bad people doing bad things. It is a scene about ordinary people doing ordinary things — who simply never stopped to ask whether the ordinary was all there was. The market is not sinful. The meal is not sinful. The dispute over a boundary line is not sinful. But **all of them become traps** when they are everything, when they fill the whole frame of a life and leave no window for what is larger.

[PAUSE]

*Yakhiṣṣimūn.* The word tangles the tongue because the state it describes tangles the soul. You cannot say it cleanly. You cannot live it cleanly either. One sound — *wāḥidah* — arrives in the middle of all that tangle, and the posture you were frozen in is the posture you carry into what comes next.

This is the answer to their mockery. Not a punishment. Not a divine rebuke. Just: the moment arriving, finding them exactly as they had chosen to be found.

---

## Theme Three: The Two Impossibilities

The third ayah is the quietest. And perhaps the heaviest.

*Fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna tawṣiyatan wa-lā ilā ahlihim yarjiʿūn.*

"Then they will not be able to make a bequest, nor will they return to their own people."

Two things. Two impossibilities. Two things that, in any ordinary human death — even a sudden one — a person would reach for with their last strength.

The first: **tawṣiyah.** A dying bequest. A last instruction. The word comes from *waṣṣā* — to charge someone, to commission them with something before departure, to leave your intentions with the living because you can no longer act on them yourself. It is the word for what a person does when they know they are going: *"Take care of your mother. Pay what I owe to so-and-so. Don't let the children forget this."* It is the act of trying to carry some responsibility across the border of your own life — of sending your intentions forward into a world you can no longer shape.

This is the last act of human agency. The final gesture of someone who still believes their choices matter.

And the ayah says: *lā yastaṭīʿūna.* They **cannot** do it. Not that they choose not to. That they literally lack the capacity. *Yastaṭīʿūna* — from the root for power, ability, reach — they are simply no longer capable of it. The single blast has already removed them from the world in which tawṣiyah is possible. The debt is unsettled. The apology is unsaid. The will is unwritten. The instruction they were going to give their son when the moment felt right — that moment will not come.

[PAUSE]

The second: **yarjiʿūn** — they will not *return* to their people. To their families. Their homes. The people waiting for them. Maybe a wife who expected her husband back before sunset. Maybe a child who was told, *"Abī will be home soon."*

There is something that cuts to the bone in this detail. Death usually has a shape. It has a journey home — or at least a message, a body returned, a burial in a familiar place with familiar hands. The community of loss has rituals precisely because they help us across the grief of the unfinished. We find ways to close the gaps.

But *lā ilā ahlihim yarjiʿūn.* They do not return. The family waits. The table is set. The door stays open.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the poetry of it? They asked: *"When?"* — assuming they had all the time in the world to decide when to take things seriously. And the Quran's answer is not a date. The Quran's answer is a portrait of the two things they assumed they would always have more time for: their last words, and their last road home.

Both gone. Not in some distant, abstract reckoning. Gone in the middle of a Tuesday quarrel, before they finished the sentence.

This is what *matā* — "when?" — costs. It costs you tawṣiyah. It costs you the return. It leaves the people you love standing at a door you are never coming back through, waiting for a message you did not have time to send.

The question they sneered with was, *"Give us a date and we'll think about it then."* The ayah answers: the only date that matters is the one you did not know to prepare for. And the two things you were saving for later — the word you owed someone, the road back to the people who needed you — those are now, permanently, unfinished.

---

## The Answer They Never Got to Hear

We started here: *matā hādhā-l-waʿd, in kuntum ṣādiqīn.* "When is this promise — if you are truthful?"

They expected to outlast the question. They thought the gap between the asking and the answering was long enough to live a whole life inside. They built their dismissal on that gap. They banked their comfort on it.

And the answer — *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* — arrived before they were ready to receive it.

There is something I want to name before we close.

This passage is not primarily about them. It is about the question *matā* itself — the posture of deferral — which is not unique to seventh-century Makkah. It is the most durable posture a human heart can take. Every generation builds its version of it. Every individual soul finds its own way of saying, *"Yes, but not yet. Not today. Give me a date and I'll decide then."*

And the Quran's answer — across fourteen centuries and to every generation that has opened its pages — is the same. Not a date. Not a threat. But a picture:

A single blast. One. No second warning. The whole ordinary machinery of a life — the quarrels, the transactions, the meals, the half-finished arguments — seized mid-motion. And two things no longer possible: the final word, and the road back home.

[PAUSE]

This is the answer to "when?"

Not *then*. Not *later.*

**Now** is what you have. Not because the Hour is necessarily this afternoon. But because the assumption that you can decide *later* — the assumption that the runway is long and yours to spend — is itself the trap. It is the trap that caught them. It is the trap that, if we are honest, catches many of us every day.

The opposite of *matā* — the opposite of the mocker's "when?" — is not fear. It is not paralysis or obsession with death. It is something much simpler and much harder.

It is the posture of someone who already believes, and is therefore already arranging the nursery. The person who needs no date because they have already decided. The person who, if the blast came this afternoon, would not be frozen in a quarrel about nothing.

Not because they are perfect. But because they remembered that they might not get to finish the sentence.

The question they asked — *"When?"* — was built on the belief that they were holding the schedule.

The ayah is the reminder that they were not.

Neither are we.
