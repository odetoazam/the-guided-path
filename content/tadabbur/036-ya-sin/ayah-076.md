# Let Not Their Speech Grieve You

### Surah Ya-Sin · Ayah 76

> **فَلَا يَحْزُنكَ قَوْلُهُمْ ۘ إِنَّا نَعْلَمُ مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ**
>
> *Falā yaḥzunka qawluhum — innā naʿlamu mā yusirrūna wa mā yuʿlinūn.*
>
> **So let not their speech grieve you. Indeed, We know what they conceal and what they declare.**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Words

Let me set you down inside a particular kind of pain.

It is not the pain of being struck. It is not the pain of losing something. It is the pain of being *spoken about*. Of knowing that somewhere — in a gathering you are not part of, in a conversation that stops when you walk in, in the private chambers of people's opinions — your name is being handled. Turned over. Reduced to something small and ugly. And you cannot reach it. You cannot follow your name into every room it travels to and correct the record.

That is a uniquely human wound. And it is the wound this verse reaches into.

Picture Makkah in the years of the early message. A man — the most truthful man his people have ever known, the one they themselves nicknamed *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy — stands before them with a message from the sky. And what comes back at him is a torrent of words. *Poet. Magician. Madman. Liar. A man possessed.* The very people who would have trusted him with their gold now stand in their circles and dismantle his character syllable by syllable. And he hears it. Some of it reaches his ears directly. Much of it he can only imagine — the things said in the private councils of the Quraysh, the contempt nursed in chests he cannot see into.

[PAUSE]

And here is what I want you to feel before we go a single word further. The Prophet ﷺ was not made of stone. The Qur'an itself testifies to this again and again — that his heart constricted at their words, that Allah had to keep steadying him. He *grieved*. The most beloved of creation to Allah felt the sting of being slandered. So whatever comfort this verse holds, it is not the comfort of "be too strong to feel it." It is something far deeper. Something built for people who *do* feel it.

Now — here is the strand running through this part of the surah. Just before this verse, in 36:69, Allah defends His Messenger against the specific charge that he is a poet: *"We did not teach him poetry, nor is it befitting for him."* Allah Himself takes up the defense. And then, right after that public clearing of his name, the verse turns inward — from defending him *to them*, to consoling him *in himself*. And right after *this* verse comes the surah's mightiest argument: the man who holds up a decayed bone and sneers, *"Who will give life to these?"* — and Allah's thunderous answer about the One who made it the first time.

So this single verse sits at a hinge. It is the moment Allah settles His Messenger's heart so that the final proof can land with full force. Before the cannon fires, the hand must be steady.

**Here is the hook.** When you are wounded by what people say about you, there are two obvious cures the world offers. *Prove them wrong.* Or *stop caring.* Watch — across these nine words — Allah offer neither. He will not tell the Prophet to win the argument. He will not tell him to grow numb. He will hand him a third thing entirely. A thing so quiet you could read this verse a hundred times and walk past it. And it is the only cure that actually holds.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Who Is the Verb Pointing At?

Let me teach you something small about how language assigns blame — or assigns *agency* — before we open the verse.

In English, imagine I want to comfort a friend who has been criticized. I could say two very different things:

> *"Don't be sad."*

Or:

> *"Don't let their words get to you."*

Listen to the difference. In the first sentence — *don't be sad* — the sadness is **your** action. You are the one doing the being-sad. The command lands on you, and quietly, it implies a kind of failure: if you are still sad, you have disobeyed. You have not managed your own heart well enough.

But in the second sentence — *don't let their words get to you* — something has shifted. Now **the words** are the actor. The words are the thing reaching out to grab you. And you are simply being told to deny them the power to land. The burden is not on you to manufacture a feeling. The burden is taken off you, and placed onto the words — *they* are forbidden their grip.

Hold that distinction. Because Arabic makes it with surgical precision.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the verse. The phrase is:

> **فَلَا يَحْزُنكَ قَوْلُهُمْ**
> *Falā yaḥzunka qawluhum*

We translate it, naturally, as "Let not their speech grieve you." But notice what is the **grammatical subject** of that sentence. What is doing the action of grieving?

It is not the Prophet ﷺ. He is not the one grieving here, grammatically. He is the *object* — the little *-ka*, "you," tucked onto the end of the verb *yaḥzun-ka*. The one *doing* the verb, the subject sitting right there at the end of the phrase, is **قَوْلُهُمْ — *qawluhum*, "their speech."**

Let that land. Allah did not say to His Messenger, *"Do not grieve."* He did not place the command on the Prophet's heart and make him responsible for policing his own sorrow. **He addressed the speech itself.** He turned to *their words* — the words doing the wounding — and forbade *them*: you shall not grieve him.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the tenderness in that? This is the gentleness of a Lord who knows His servant's heart cannot be commanded like a muscle. You cannot order grief to leave the way you order a hand to open. So Allah does not lay that impossible command on him. Instead He reaches *between* His beloved and the words that are reaching for him — and He intervenes on the side of the words. *Let them not have him.*

The grief is real. Allah does not deny it exists. He simply forbids the words their *power* over the one He loves.

And there is one more thing the grammarians noticed here — al-Qurtubī mentions it. There are two recited tongues for this word, *yaḥzunuka* and *yuḥzinuka*, two valid Arabic dialectal forms of the same verb. The point both carry is identical: the *cause* of the grief is external. Something is being done *to* him by their speech. And it is precisely *that* — the external cause — that Allah disarms.

So the first discovery, before we have even reached the second half of the verse: **Allah does not command you out of your sadness. He stands between you and the thing causing it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Insult He Refused to Repeat

Here is a principle of reading the Qur'an that, once you see it, you can never unsee. **Read the silences as carefully as you read the words.** When Arabic *expects* a detail and the speaker withholds it, that withholding is doing work.

Let me show you the expectation first.

The verse says **قَوْلُهُمْ — *qawluhum*, "their speech."** Their *saying.* And immediately your mind wants to ask: *saying what?* What did they say? Because everywhere else in the Qur'an, when the disbelievers leveled their accusations, Allah frequently *quotes them.* He records the exact words. *They said, "a poet."* *They said, "you are bewitched."* *They said, "this is nothing but a lie he invented."* The Qur'an is not shy about putting their slanders on the page.

And the tafsīr tradition rushes in to fill the blank here, too. Al-Jalalayn supplies it: their saying that *"you are not a messenger"* and the like. Al-Qurtubī fills it in: *poet, magician.* Al-Baghawī: the lies of the disbelievers of Makkah in their denial of you. Every scholar reaches in to name the content — because the grammar leaves a space *begging* to be filled.

But here is what I want you to notice. **Allah Himself left it empty.** He did not say "let not their saying *that you are a poet* grieve you." He did not specify. He wrote, simply, *their speech.* One word, sealed shut over its contents.

[PAUSE]

Why? Sit with this. Why would Allah, who elsewhere quotes the insult in full, here refuse to repeat it?

Consider what the silence accomplishes. **First** — by leaving it unspecified, the word becomes a vessel large enough to hold *everything* they say. Not this insult or that one. *All of it.* Every name they called him, every accusation they ever leveled, every word that would ever be said against him until the end of time — all of it gathered into one undefined *qawluhum* and sealed under a single prohibition. A specific insult, named, would have shrunk the comfort to fit that one wound. The unnamed *speech* makes the comfort total.

**Second** — and feel the dignity in this — by refusing to repeat the slander, Allah refuses to *honor* it with a place in His eternal speech *at this moment of consoling His Messenger.* He will not dignify their words by recording them here, in the verse meant to lift His beloved above them. To quote the insult would be to let it sit, immortalized, beside the man it was hurled at. Instead Allah waves it away — *their speech* — as a thing too unworthy to even transcribe in the breath where He comforts His Prophet.

[PAUSE]

There is a mercy in *not being made to hear it again.*

Think of what it is, when someone wounds you, and a "friend" comes to comfort you by *repeating exactly what was said* — "do you know what they actually called you? They said—" and recites it back, and the wound opens fresh. That is not comfort. Allah does not do that to His Messenger. He gathers the whole ugly chorus into two syllables — *qawluhum* — and turns His Prophet's face away from it, toward Himself.

So the second discovery: **the insult He would not repeat is itself a mercy.** What Allah leaves unsaid is shielding you from having to hear it twice.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Two Verbs That Close Every Door

Now we arrive at the second half of the verse — the reason. And I want to teach you one device before we open it, because everything hangs on it.

There is a way of speaking that captures *totality* by naming its two opposite ends. We do it constantly. *"I searched high and low."* I do not mean I searched only the high places and the low places — I mean I searched *everywhere*, and I expressed "everywhere" by naming its two poles. *"For better or for worse."* *"They came young and old."* You name the two extremes, and the whole spectrum between them is swept up automatically. The rhetoricians have a name for this; let us simply call it *capturing the whole by naming its two ends.*

Now hold that, and look at how Allah grounds His comfort:

> **إِنَّا نَعْلَمُ مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ**
> *Innā naʿlamu mā yusirrūna wa mā yuʿlinūn.*
> **"Indeed, We know what they conceal and what they declare."**

Two verbs. **يُسِرُّونَ — *yusirrūna*, "they conceal,"** and **يُعْلِنُونَ — *yuʿlinūna*, "they declare."** The hidden and the open. The whispered and the shouted. The secret kept in the chest and the slander spoken in the street.

And these are the two ends. Between *what they conceal* and *what they declare* there is no third category. There is nothing a human being can do that is neither hidden nor open. So when Allah says *I know what they conceal and what they declare*, He has not listed two things He happens to know — **He has closed every door.** There is no hidden chamber His knowledge has not entered. There is no public square outside His view. The pairing is total. Nothing escapes.

[PAUSE]

Now look closer at the two words themselves.

Both are built on the same intensive pattern — the form the grammarians call Form IV. **أَسَرَّ — *asarra*** is to take a thing and *place it into secrecy*, to make it into a *sirr*, a kept secret, buried in the chest. **أَعْلَنَ — *aʿlana*** is to take a thing and *bring it out into the open*, to make it *ʿalan*, public, proclaimed. One verb buries; the other broadcasts. And the root of the first, *s-r-r*, is the very root of the word *sirr* — the secret. The most private thing a person owns. The thought they would die before letting anyone read.

And notice — both verbs are in the present-ongoing tense. Not "what they concealed once." *What they keep concealing. What they keep declaring.* It is their continuous condition. They are, right now and always, two-faced in this way: a public performance of contempt and a private reservoir of it. Both flowing at once.

[PAUSE]

But here is the discovery I have been holding for you. Here is where this verse opens completely.

Go back to the first half. What grieved the Prophet ﷺ? **قَوْلُهُمْ — *their speech.*** Their *saying.* And what is saying? Saying is *what is declared.* Saying is the *yuʿlinūn* — the part that comes out into the open, the part that reaches his ears. **The Prophet ﷺ is wounded only by the half he can hear.**

But look at what Allah knows. Allah knows the *yuʿlinūn* — yes, the declared part, the part the Prophet also hears. **But Allah also knows the *yusirrūn* — the concealed part. The contempt nursed in chests. The schemes laid in private councils. The mockery the Prophet never even heard.** Al-Baghawī catches it exactly: *what they conceal in their consciences of denial.* The part of their hostility that never reached the Prophet at all.

Do you see the architecture of the comfort now?

[PAUSE]

It is as if Allah says: *My beloved, you are grieved by their speech — by the fraction of their malice that escapes their mouths and reaches you. But understand who is taking up your cause. I am not consoling you with partial knowledge. I do not know only the half that wounded you. I know the half you never saw — the contempt they hid, the words they whispered when you were not in the room, the malice they were too cowardly to say to your face. You are grieved by a fragment. And the One who will requite them holds the whole.*

Your knowledge of their hostility is one word — *qawluhum.* His knowledge is the complete, doubled, exhaustive accounting — *what they conceal and what they declare.* And the tafsīr finishes the sentence that the *innā naʿlamu* leaves hanging in the air: al-Qurtubī and al-Muyassar both supply it — *fa-nujāzīhim*, **"and so We will requite them for it."** The knowing is not idle. The knowing is the prelude to the reckoning.

[PAUSE]

So hear the third discovery whole: **The Prophet is grieved by the part he can hear. Allah is consoling him with the part He alone can see. The reckoning is complete even though your knowledge of the wound is only partial — because the One settling the account knows what you could never know.**

### The Bridge

Look at what these nine words have built.

The grammar told us Allah does not command the heart out of its grief — He stands between His servant and the words. The silence told us He will not even repeat the insult — He shields His beloved from hearing it twice. And the two verbs told us that the One taking up the cause holds a knowledge so complete it makes the servant's wound look like a single drop drawn from an ocean Allah has already measured to its floor.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about words on a page, but about the shape of *your* life. About the particular ache of being a person who is spoken about. And about what it means to be *known.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Wound You Cannot Reach

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a thing your mind does that no one taught it to do. Someone says something cruel about you — or you merely *learn* that they did — and your mind, against your will, begins to *replay* it. You are washing the dishes and it surfaces. You are trying to sleep and it loops. You compose, in the theater of your own head, the perfect reply you will never send. You argue your case before a jury that has already gone home. The psychologists have a name for this loop. They call it *rumination*. And they have measured something you already know in your bones: a single cruel sentence will occupy more of your mind than a hundred kind ones. We are built with a tilt toward the wound.

And here is the cruelest part of it. **You cannot reach the words.** They have left their speaker's mouth and entered rooms you will never walk into, lodged in minds you will never correct. The slander travels and you cannot follow it. You are left holding a wound whose source is permanently out of your reach.

[PAUSE]

Now — what does the world tell you to do with that?

The world offers you two medicines. The first is: **fight back. Prove them wrong.** Win. Build the case, marshal the evidence, defend your name until the record is corrected. And the second, when the first exhausts you, is: **stop caring. Detach. They don't matter. Become the kind of person their words bounce off.**

Watch closely, because this is the heart of it: **Allah offers His Messenger neither.**

He does not say, "Refute them." He does not hand the Prophet a counter-argument to wield. And He does not say, "Be above it, they are beneath your notice." He does not ask him to manufacture indifference toward the very people he was sent, in mercy, to save.

Instead He does something the two worldly medicines never touch. **He relocates the Prophet's security.** He moves the ground the Prophet is standing on — from *their assessment of him* to *Allah's complete knowledge of them.*

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference. Both worldly cures leave your peace *tethered to the verdict of the crowd.* "Prove them wrong" still makes their opinion the thing that must be changed — you are still dancing to their judgment, just resisting it. "Stop caring" still makes their opinion the thing that must be *resisted* — you are still defined by what you are pushing away. In both, the crowd holds the center of the room. You are orbiting their verdict either way.

But Allah moves the center of the room. *Let not their speech grieve you — because I know what they conceal and what they declare.* He does not ask the Prophet to change their verdict or to deaden himself against it. He asks him to *step off the ground of their judgment entirely* — and stand instead on the ground of being fully, completely, exhaustively known by the One whose verdict is the only one that will be read aloud on the Day it matters.

This is the third cure. The one the world cannot offer because the world does not have an All-Knowing Lord to offer it. **The wound of others' words is not healed by winning the argument and not healed by going numb. It is healed by resting in the knowledge that Someone sees the whole — and has therefore already taken the matter into His own hands.**

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

When the loop starts — when the cruel sentence surfaces over the dishes, when the reply you will never send begins composing itself — you have a place to *put* it now. Not "I must prove them wrong by morning." Not "I must become a person who doesn't feel this." But: *Allah knows what they concealed and what they declared. He saw the part I never even heard. The account is already in better hands than mine, held by Someone with more complete knowledge of the wrong than I will ever have.* You are not asked to stop feeling the wound. You are asked to stop *carrying the verdict.* Set it down. It was never yours to settle.

### Theme Two: To Be Fully Known

Now let me take you to something deeper, hidden inside those same two verbs — *what they conceal and what they declare* — because they describe not only *them.* They describe *every human being who has ever lived.* Including you.

Here is a truth about the human condition. **We all live divided into a *yusirrūn* and a *yuʿlinūn.*** A concealed self and a declared self. There is the version of you that you *broadcast* — the public face, the proclaimed life, what you let people see. And there is the version you *keep* — the private chamber, the thoughts you would not speak aloud, the secret griefs, the secret sins, the secret hopes too fragile to expose. Every one of us is curating. Every one of us is performing an *iʿlān*, a public declaration, while guarding a *sirr*, a kept secret.

And our age has built an entire civilization on this divide. What is social media but a vast theater of *iʿlān* — the relentless declaring of a curated self? And what grows in exact proportion to it but the *sirr* — the gap between the life you announce and the life you actually live, widening until you barely recognize yourself in your own broadcast?

We are a people exhausted by the distance between our concealed and our declared.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the verse again, but turn it from *them* to *you.*

> *Indeed, We know what they conceal and what they declare.*

Here is the terror, and here is the comfort, and they live in the *same words.*

For the one who hides his contempt behind a smiling face — for the mocker in this very verse — *Innā naʿlamu* is a **warning.** Your *sirr* is not secret. The malice you were too careful to ever say aloud, the contempt you kept off your tongue and stored in your chest — *it is fully known.* You did not get away with the hidden part. There is no concealed self that escapes the reckoning. The performance does not fool the Audience who matters.

[PAUSE]

But turn it now toward the wounded believer. Toward you, on the night you cried where no one could see. Toward the grief you carried that you never let anyone witness. Toward the kindness you did that no human eye recorded, the prayer you whispered into an empty room, the *sirr* of your devotion and your pain that you declared to no one.

*Innā naʿlamu mā yusirrūn.* **We know what they conceal.**

Your hidden self is not lost in the dark either. The same knowledge that exposes the concealer's malice *witnesses the concealing believer's tears.* The part of your pain that no one saw — Allah saw it. The part of your worship that you performed for no audience — He was the Audience. You who feel *unseen*, who carry sorrows and devotions in a chamber you have shown to no one: **you are not unseen. You are fully, completely known by the One whose knowing is mercy.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this paradox, because it is the whole of the matter. The thing we most *fear* — being fully known, having the concealed chamber thrown open — is, for the one who turns to Allah, the thing we most *need.* To the mocker, "We know what you conceal" is the sword of justice. To the broken-hearted believer, "We know what you conceal" is the hand that finally, gently, says: *I saw. All of it. The part you showed no one. I was there.*

To be fully known is terror to the one running from Allah, and it is the deepest comfort to the one running toward Him. The same nine words. The same All-Knowing Lord. The difference is only which direction your face is turned.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

Two things, depending on which self you are tending. If there is a *sirr* in you — a concealed malice, a hidden contempt, a secret thing you would be ashamed to have declared — let this verse find it before the Day does. *Innā naʿlamu.* It is already seen; bring it into the open before Him in repentance now, while opening it costs you only humility and not disgrace. And if you are the one who feels *unseen* — who carries unwitnessed grief, unrewarded kindness, a devotion no one applauds — then stop performing for the crowd that cannot see your chamber anyway. Live for the Audience who already sees it. *He knows what you conceal.* Let that be the eye you live in front of, and you will be free of every other.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see how the whole verse is built, because the structure *is* the consolation.

There are two halves, and they are deliberately unequal.

**Your side** of the verse is one word: **قَوْلُهُمْ — *their speech.*** A single term. The fraction you can hear. The part that reaches your ears and grieves you.

**His side** of the verse is the full, doubled, exhaustive accounting: **مَا يُسِرُّونَ وَمَا يُعْلِنُونَ — *what they conceal and what they declare.*** Both poles. The whole spectrum. Nothing left out.

And listen — the two verbs even *rhyme: yusirrūn, yuʿlinūn.* The hidden and the open *sound alike* in the verse, and that is no accident. To Allah, the concealed and the declared are equally accessible, equally near, equally heard. The rhyme enacts the meaning: before Him, the whispered and the shouted are the same volume.

So the architecture itself preaches. **You are given one word for the wound. He claims the entire knowledge of it — including the half you could never reach.** The asymmetry on the page *is* the comfort in your heart. You hold a fragment; He holds the whole; therefore set the fragment down.

### Bringing It Together

Watch how the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be a single truth seen from two sides.

The grammar showed Allah stepping *between* His servant and the wounding words rather than commanding the servant's heart. The themes showed that the cure for being-spoken-about is not winning and not numbness but *relocating your ground* onto the One who knows. **These are the same act.** When Allah forbade the words their grip rather than ordering the Prophet not to grieve, He was *already* moving him off the ground of their verdict and onto the ground of being known. The grammar and the theme are one motion.

And the silence — the insult He refused to repeat — turns out to be the same mercy as the comfort of being-known. Allah will not let His Prophet hear the slander twice; and Allah *will* let His Prophet rest in the knowledge that the whole of the malice, including the unheard part, is already in His hands. The thing Allah *withholds* (the repeated insult) and the thing Allah *gives* (His total knowledge) are the same tenderness, pointing the same direction: *away* from their words, *toward* His knowing.

### Questions to Carry

1. When someone's words wound me, which of the two false cures do I reach for — proving them wrong, or going numb? And what would it actually feel like to set down their verdict instead of fighting it or fleeing it?
2. The Prophet ﷺ was grieved and Allah did not shame him for it. Where have I been treating my own grief as a failure of faith, when Allah treats it as a wound to be consoled?
3. What is in my *sirr* — my concealed chamber — that I would not want declared? And am I more troubled that *people* might see it, or that Allah already does?
4. Is there a hidden good in me — a private devotion, an unwitnessed kindness — that I have been quietly resenting goes unseen? What changes if I truly believe *innā naʿlamu* covers that too?
5. Whose verdict is currently the center of the room in my life — the crowd's, or my Lord's?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the words of others wound you, your refuge is not in silencing them and not in deadening yourself, but in resting before the One who knows the whole of what they hide and what they show — and who has therefore already taken the matter into His own hands.

### The Closing Invitation

So let something shift. Not a resolution to feel less — Allah never asked His beloved for that. A resolution to *carry* less. The next time your name travels into a room you cannot reach, and the loop begins, and your mind starts building the reply you will never send — let this verse arrive: *He knows what they conceal and what they declare.* The account is already held by the only One whose knowledge is complete and whose verdict is final. You were never the judge in this case. You can step down. You were only ever meant to be the one He defends.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who know what is concealed and what is declared — You see the words said about me that I cannot reach, and You see the chamber of my own heart that I have shown to no one. Let not the speech of people grieve me into forgetting that I am fully known by You. Be the Audience I live in front of, settle the accounts I cannot settle, and let the knowledge that You see the whole be enough for me. Āmīn.*
