# The Mercy of an Eye Not Erased

## Introduction

Let me give you the two verses first — in Arabic, then in sound, then in a plain English you can carry.

> وَلَوْ نَشَآءُ لَطَمَسْنَا عَلَىٰٓ أَعْيُنِهِمْ فَٱسْتَبَقُوا۟ ٱلصِّرَٰطَ فَأَنَّىٰ يُبْصِرُونَ
>
> *Wa law nashāʾu la-ṭamasnā ʿalā aʿyunihim fa-stabaqū ṣ-ṣirāṭa fa-annā yubṣirūn.*
>
> **And if We willed, We could have wiped out their eyes, so they would race for the path — but how could they see?**

> وَلَوْ نَشَآءُ لَمَسَخْنَـٰهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَكَانَتِهِمْ فَمَا ٱسْتَطَـٰعُوا۟ مُضِيًّا وَلَا يَرْجِعُونَ
>
> *Wa law nashāʾu la-masakhnāhum ʿalā makānatihim fa-mā-staṭāʿū muḍiyyan wa lā yarjiʿūn.*
>
> **And if We willed, We could have transformed them where they stand, so they could neither go forward nor turn back.**

### Rebuild the world

To feel where these verses land, you have to feel the pressure of the passage that built up to them.

For most of Ya-Sin, Allah has been laying out a case against the people who turned away. He has shown them the dead earth brought to life, the night peeled back from the day, the sun running its course, the ship riding the sea. Sign after sign after sign — every one of them seen with the eye, perceived through the senses. And the deniers looked at all of it and stayed shut.

Then, just before our verses, comes one of the most arresting images in the whole surah: a Day when **the mouths are sealed** — *al-yawma nakhtimu ʿalā afwāhihim* — and instead the hands speak, the feet bear witness. The faculties of the body, which a person spent a lifetime commanding, suddenly testify against their owner.

Now hold that image — the sealed mouth — because our verses do something parallel and even more intimate. The sealing of the mouth was set in the future, on the Day of Judgment. But here Allah brings the same logic into **right now**, into this very moment as you breathe. He turns from the mouth to **the eye**. And He says something that should stop us cold: *the sight you are using to read these words — I could erase it. If I willed.*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we begin, and then I want you to forget it until we earn it: this is one of the only places in the Qur'an where Allah describes a punishment **in order to tell you He is not sending it.** [PAUSE]

Most threats in scripture describe what *will* happen. This one describes what *could* happen — and pointedly does not. The entire verse is built on a door He is holding shut. Why would Allah spend a whole verse describing the catastrophe He is *withholding*?

Stay with that question. Everything opens from it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *law*: the grammar of the closed door

Let me teach you a small piece of Arabic before we touch the verse, because the whole emotional weight of these two verses hangs on a single two-letter word, and if you don't feel the word, the verses stay flat.

Arabic has more than one way to say "if."

There is *in* (إن) — the "if" of real possibility. *If it rains, I'll bring an umbrella.* It might rain. The door is open.

And then there is **law (لو)** — and *law* is a different animal entirely. *Law* is the "if" of the thing that did **not** happen. Grammarians call it *ḥarf imtināʿ li-imtināʿ* — roughly, "the particle of a withholding because of a withholding." It signals a condition that was **not** fulfilled, and therefore a result that did **not** occur.

When an Arab says *law*, the listener already knows, before the sentence even finishes, that we are in the world of the un-happened. *Law* means: *here is something that is not the case — let me show you what it would have looked like if it were.*

Everyday example. If I say to you, "Had I known you were hungry, I would have cooked" — the moment you hear "had I known," you already know two things: I *didn't* know, and I *didn't* cook. The whole sentence is a window into a reality that never came to be.

Now open the verse:

> **وَلَوْ نَشَآءُ** — *Wa **law** nashāʾu* — "And **if** We willed…"

[PAUSE]

Feel what that *law* just did. Before Allah names a single detail of the punishment, the word *law* has already told the listener: **this is not happening.** The eyes are not being wiped. The transformation is not coming.

So the verse is not a threat hanging over your head. It is a window He is opening onto a world that does not exist — *the world in which your sight was taken* — and He opens that window for exactly one reason: so that you would turn around and look at the world that *does* exist, the one where your eyes still work, and finally **see it for the gift it is.**

Notice something. We never feel our faculties as gifts while we have them. The eye becomes precious only in the story of its loss. And Allah, who knows the human heart better than the human does, does not wait for you to actually lose your sight to teach you its worth. He builds the loss *in language* — *law nashāʾu* — and lets you stand at the edge of that ruined world for the length of one verse, so that you can step back into your own life and see your open eyes for the first time.

That is what the closed door of *law* is doing. It is mercy disguised as a threat.

And there is one more thing in that opening phrase. He did not say "if We *had* willed," locking it in the past. The verb is **نَشَآءُ — *nashāʾu*** — present tense. *If We will.* Ongoing. Continuous. Not a decision made once long ago, but a willing that is live **right now, this second.** Your sight is not a gift He gave you at birth and walked away from. It is a gift He is **choosing not to withdraw** in this very breath. And the next. And the next.

This is what the linguists would call the present of renewal — the sustaining is happening continuously. Your eyesight is not a possession you own. It is a loan being re-extended, moment by moment, by a will that says *nashāʾu* — and chooses mercy each time.

### Element Two — *ṭamasnā*: not blindness, but erasure

Now the punishment itself. And here the precise word matters enormously, because Allah did not use the ordinary word for "blind."

> لَطَمَسْنَا عَلَىٰٓ أَعْيُنِهِمْ — *la-**ṭamasnā** ʿalā aʿyunihim*

The root is **ṭ-m-s (طمس)**. And *ṭams* does not mean "to darken" or "to close." Listen to where else this root lives in the language. You use *ṭams* for **wiping out a written line** until no trace of the letters remains. You use it for a **road effaced by the desert** until you cannot tell there was ever a path. You use it for **a star whose light is extinguished** — the Qur'an itself uses this root for the stars on the Last Day: *fa-idhā n-nujūmu ṭumisat*, "when the stars are wiped out."

*Ṭams* is **effacement.** Erasure. The smoothing-over of something until the feature is **gone.**

And the classical lexicographers make this almost unbearably vivid. Al-Qurṭubī reports that *al-maṭmūs* and *aṭ-ṭamīs* — the one whose eyes have been *ṭamasa'd* — refers to **the blind one in whose eye there is not even a slit.** Not an eye that has gone dark. An eye that has been **smoothed flat like the skin of the cheek** — no lid, no socket, no opening, as though a hand had passed over the face and wiped the eyes away entirely, the way you wipe chalk from a board.

Sit with the horror and the precision of that for a moment.

Allah is not describing a man who goes blind. He is describing a face from which the *very organ* of sight has been **erased** — wiped smooth, as if it had never been written there.

And now hear the sound of the word. **Ṭa-ma-sa.** The heavy, hollow *ṭā*, the closing *mīm*, and then that long hiss of the *sīn* — *sss* — the exact sound of something being **wiped away**, smeared flat, hushed into nothing. The word *sounds like the erasure it names.* Arabic does this when the meaning is heavy enough to demand it: the mouth performs the verb. To say *ṭamasnā* is to feel a wiping motion in the very making of the sound.

Here is why this word, and not "blind." Because **blindness is a loss.** *Ṭams* is an **un-making.** A blind person still has eyes — there is a faculty there that was damaged. But the verse is reaching past damage to something more total: the erasure of the instrument itself, as if it were never granted.

And that is the unseen reality this physical image is pointing toward. [PAUSE] Because al-Baghawī, commenting on exactly this verse, draws the line for us. He says: just as We have **already** blinded their hearts — *aʿmaynā qulūbahum* — so, if We willed, We would blind their outer eyes. The wiping of the physical eye is a **mirror** held up to a wiping that has *already happened on the inside.* These are people whose inner sight has already been effaced — smoothed flat, no slit, no opening to let the light in. The verse is saying: *the blindness you fear for your face is the blindness they already carry in their hearts — and do not even know it.*

The physical eye is the stepping stone. The effaced heart is the destination.

### Element Three — *fa-stabaqū ṣ-ṣirāṭa*: racing toward a road they cannot find

Now watch what Allah says they would *do* once their eyes were erased. This is the detail that turns the verse from frightening into devastating.

> فَٱسْتَبَقُوا۟ ٱلصِّرَٰطَ — *fa-**stabaqū** ṣ-ṣirāṭa* — "so they would race for the path"

Let me teach the verb. The root is **s-b-q (سبق)**, "to get ahead, to precede, to outstrip." But the form here is not the simple verb. It is **Form VIII — istabaqa** — and that *ta* woven into the middle of the word adds a flavor of **mutual, eager, competitive striving.** *Istibāq* is the word for a **race**, a contest, people vying to get there first, lunging forward. It is the same family of meaning the Qur'an uses for *fa-stabiqū l-khayrāt* — "so **race one another** to good deeds." It is energetic. It is forward-thrown. It is full of effort and urgency.

Now place that beside what just happened to them.

Their eyes have been **erased.** And the very next thing they do is **lunge forward in an eager race** toward *aṣ-ṣirāṭ* — the path, the road, the way.

[PAUSE]

Al-Jalalayn adds one detail that makes the scene more devastating still: *dhāhibīna ka-ʿādatihim* — "going forward **as usual.**" By sheer habit. The body doing what the body has always done, because the body does not know yet that the instrument is gone. The morning routine of a person continuing even though the morning has been erased from their face. There is something in this that is almost tender in its horror — the human organism still trying, still striving, still racing forward *ka-ʿādatihim*, as it always has. And getting nowhere.

Do you see the unbearable picture? They have lost the one faculty that makes finding a path possible — and they go *harder*. They don't freeze. They don't stop to grope. They **race.** All the energy is still there. All the striving, all the urgency, all the competitive drive to get ahead and find the way — completely intact. And completely **useless**, because the instrument that would tell them *which way is forward* has been wiped from their faces.

This is a portrait of motion without orientation. Of effort divorced from sight. Of a person sprinting with everything they have toward a road they can no longer locate.

Now a word about which *ṣirāṭ* the verse means — because classical commentators actually disagreed. Al-Ṭabarī records two camps: the first read *ṣirāṭ* here as **the path of guidance** — *mahajjat al-ḥaqq*, the clear way of truth — arguing that the verse describes spiritual blinding: *We would have blinded them from guidance and led them astray from the evident way.* The second camp read it as a literal road — people lunging through terrain they can no longer navigate. The two readings nest more than they conflict. Whether the road is outer or inner, the catastrophe is the same: **striving with full urgency toward the path, and being constitutionally unable to find it.** And both camps agree on the deeper indictment — that the energy of seeking is present, and the capacity to arrive is gone. The lostness is not for lack of effort. It is for lack of the light by which effort finds its way.

And then the verse lands its final blow, which is not a statement but a **question:**

> فَأَنَّىٰ يُبْصِرُونَ — *fa-**annā** yubṣirūn* — "so **how/from where** could they see?"

One last word to teach. **Annā (أنّى).** We translate it "how," but *annā* is sharper than "how." It carries the sense of **"from where? by what route? from what source?"** It is a question about *origin*. So the verse is not merely asking "how would they manage to see?" It is asking: **"From where would vision even come to them?"**

And that question contains the entire theology of the verse. Because it exposes an assumption every one of us walks around with and never examines: that sight comes *from us.* That seeing is something *I* do, with *my* eyes, by *my* power.

*Annā yubṣirūn* — "from where would they see?" — quietly demolishes that. Vision is not self-generated. It **arrives**. It comes *from somewhere.* And the moment the Giver wills *law*, it does not come at all. The question "from where would they see?" has exactly one true answer, and the verse leaves it unspoken so that you will say it yourself: **they would see from nowhere — because sight was never theirs to begin with. It was always being given.**

### Element Four — *masakhnāhum ʿalā makānatihim*: transformation that does not move you

The second verse opens with the same *law nashāʾu* — the same window opened onto a world that does not exist. But what it shows through that window is different in kind from the first.

> وَلَوْ نَشَآءُ لَمَسَخْنَـٰهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَكَانَتِهِمْ — *wa law nashāʾu la-**masakhnāhum** ʿalā **makānatihim***

The root of *masakh* is **m-s-kh (مسخ)** — and it means to **transform a thing into another form**, specifically a lesser or degraded form. It is not translation from one equivalent to another. It is the collapse of what something *is* into something beneath it. This word carries weight in the Qur'an: it is the verb used for the transformation of the Sabbath-breakers (7:166), the most terrible punishment recorded for a community in the Mosaic tradition.

And now listen to what the classical commentators understood this *masakh* to mean concretely. Al-Jalalayn specifies it directly: *qirada wa khanāzīra aw ḥijāra* — **apes and pigs, or stones.** A spectrum of diminishment. Apes: still animate, still living, but stripped of the dignity that defines human agency. Pigs: degraded further into what the tradition marks as a symbol of base existence. And then — stones. Where even *life* is taken, and what remains is matter without motion, without interiority, without a cry. The Qur'an has already shown us in 2:65 and 7:166 that this kind of punishment is in the record of divine history — the *law* here is not inventing a new threat. It is pointing to a precedent and saying: *this capacity is still present. You are not beyond its range.*

And then two words that do something strange and specific. **ʿAlā makānatihim** — "where they stand," "in their very places." Al-Muyassar unpacks this as *fī amākinihim* — in their own locations. Not relocated. Not scattered. **Transformed while remaining exactly where they are.**

This is the detail that makes the second verse even colder than the first.

The erasure of the eye was terrible because something was *taken from you.* But in the second verse, *you stay.* Your position is unchanged. The surroundings are unchanged. But **you** have become something else — something that cannot go forward and cannot go back.

> فَمَا ٱسْتَطَـٰعُوا۟ مُضِيًّا وَلَا يَرْجِعُونَ — *fa-mā staṭāʿū **muḍiyyan** wa lā **yarjiʿūn***

*Muḍiyyan* — going forward, proceeding. *Yarjiʿūn* — returning, turning back. Two directions. And both are gone. Al-Muyassar makes the directionality explicit: *lā yastaṭīʿūna an yamḍū amāmahum wa lā yarjiʿū warāʾahum* — they could not proceed **ahead of them**, and could not return **behind them**. The very grammar of movement — forward and backward, ahead and behind — is shut down in both axes at once.

There is a grammatical observation scholars have made here that repays attention. The first clause uses the perfect of inability — *mā staṭāʿū*, they were not able — a completed, bounded event. But the second clause shifts to the ongoing imperfect: *lā yarjiʿūn*, they do not return. Not "they could not return." They keep not returning. The inability to return is not a historical fact; it is a **permanent present tense.** The door behind them has shut with the finality of a verb that will not leave the continuous.

### Bridge

So look at what these two verses together have built. *Law* — the particle of the closed door, opening a window onto a world that does not exist, precisely so that you would turn back to the world that does and finally see it. *Ṭamasnā* — erasure, not mere blindness; the smoothing of a face to nothing; a mirror held against an interior erasure already underway. *Istabaqa ka-ʿādatihim* — the terrible picture of racing forward *as usual*, by the sheer force of habit, toward a road that can no longer be found. *Annā yubṣirūn* — the question that dismantles the ownership of vision. And then *masakh ʿalā makāna* — transformation in place, the self undone while the station holds, both directions sealed, the inability to return locked in the ongoing present.

Al-Muyassar makes the structural design visible: *just as We sealed their mouths* — kama khatamnā ʿalā afwāhihim — so the eye-effacement of 36:66 is built as a deliberate echo of the mouth-sealing in 36:65. Allah moves through the instruments of the human being one by one: the mouth that speaks, the eye that sees, the body that moves. Each faculty is held up as a gift that could, in divine will, be returned or transformed. The passage is a slow anatomy of mercy — the mercy hidden inside the fact that your mouth opens, your eye sees, your foot carries you forward. Every ordinary function disclosed as an act of ongoing divine sustaining.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The mercy you are standing inside and cannot feel

Let me start somewhere ordinary.

There is a strange feature of being human: we are almost completely blind to the goods we already possess. You do not feel grateful for your kidneys this morning. You did not wake up moved that your spine held you upright, that your eyelids closed and opened, that the light hit your retina and became *a world.* These are not in your awareness at all. They are the **floor** you stand on, and you cannot see the floor — you can only see the furniture sitting on top of it.

Psychologists have a name for this: *adaptation level.* The goods that are stable and continuous drop out of conscious awareness entirely. We only notice what *changes* — what appears and disappears, what arrives and is taken. The constant is invisible. And so the most fundamental gifts in your life — the ones that have been there every day without interruption — are precisely the ones you are most likely to never truly *feel.*

This is the psychology of an ungrateful heart, and it is not simply a moral failing. We are built to track change, not continuity. And so we are built, by our very architecture, to miss the most important things.

Now watch what Allah does with this.

He does not lecture about gratitude. He does not list what you should be grateful for. He does not say "remember your blessings." Instead, He opens a window — just for a moment — onto the world in which the stable good was removed. *Law nashāʾu* — if We had willed — and suddenly your eye, which you have not consciously noticed in years, is gone. The floor falls away. And for the duration of one verse, you are standing in the world without it.

And then the window closes. *Fa-annā yubṣirūn* — how would they see? And you turn back from the window, and the floor is there again. Your eyes still open. The light still arrives. Your feet still carry you.

And for a moment — if the verse has done its work — the floor is **not invisible anymore.**

This is the Quranic intervention into the human psychology of adaptation. Not argument, not instruction, but *simulation* — the controlled experience of loss, built entirely in language, designed to produce the felt awareness that no list of blessings could ever generate. The verse does not tell you to be grateful. It makes the not-having felt, so that the having can finally land.

### Theme Two — The faculty that does not know it is borrowed

Here is something hidden in *annā yubṣirūn* — from where would they see — that I want to sit with, because it cuts at something most of us carry unchallenged.

We speak of *my* vision. *My* sight. *My* perception. These are deeply natural ways to talk, and no one thinks to question them. But the question *annā* — from where? by what source? — exposes the grammar underneath the possessive. *My* vision implies that I am the origin, the source, the owner. But the verse is asking: *from where does the seeing actually come?*

And the answer the Qur'an gives, structurally, is: from the divine will that sustains it, moment by moment, in the present tense of *nashāʾu.* You are not the source of your own sight. You are the *location* through which sight is being extended, continuously, as an ongoing act of divine choosing.

The classical commentators observed that the deniers' inner sight had already been effaced — their hearts already *maṭmūsa*, smoothed flat. And one of the signs of that interior effacement is exactly this: the inability to recognize borrowed gifts as borrowed. The eye reads these words and experiences them as *mine*; the heart already smoothed cannot read through the eye to the will sustaining it. The opened heart — the one that lets the question *annā* land — can.

There is a concept in classical spiritual vocabulary — *murāqaba*, watchfulness, the awareness of being in God's sight — that this verse feeds from the opposite direction. *Murāqaba* is usually described as your awareness of *Him.* But these verses are about *His* awareness of you — the awareness so complete that it includes the real-time sustaining of each faculty, the breath-by-breath willed extension of your capacity to see, to move, to be. The loan is being renewed in the very moment you use it. The gift is arriving as you receive it.

### Theme Three — The body and the Day: a coherent argument

These two verses do not stand alone. They are the present-world face of an argument that unfolds across three modes of time.

In 36:65, the mouth is **sealed in the future** — *al-yawma nakhtimu ʿalā afwāhihim* — and the hands and feet testify. This is the eschatological reversal: the faculties of the body, turned against their owner on the Day of Judgment.

In 36:66–67, the eye is **counterfactually erased in the present** — *law nashāʾu* — and the body is **counterfactually frozen in the present.** This is the mercy-side of the argument: not what *will* happen but what *could* happen and *does not*, right now, in this life, while the window of return is open.

Read together, the argument is coherent and complete. The Day will come when the body testifies without your control — the mouth sealed, the hands speaking. But before that Day, Allah has given you something the Day will not offer: a present in which the faculties still work, the window is still open, and the mercy of a willed non-punishment is still being extended. The sealed mouth of the Last Day is the consequence; the open eye of this day is the mercy.

And there is one more thing the two-verse structure reveals. The first withheld punishment targets perception — the eye, the capacity to see and find the way. The second targets motion — the capacity to move forward or return. Together they are the two axes of a responsive human life: *seeing* what is true, and *moving* in response to it. These are the very faculties that were used, during the lifetime of the deniers, to look at the signs and walk away. The logic is exact. The faculties employed in the refusal are the faculties whose continued sustaining is the mercy. And the question the passage leaves hanging is not a theological abstraction — it is intensely personal: *what are you doing with the open eye and the moving foot, while they are still yours to use?*

### Theme Four — What it means to be frozen in place

The second verse has an image I want to return to before we close, because it carries something the first does not.

The erasure of the eye is the loss of a faculty. But the *masakh* — the transformation in place — is a different kind of undoing. It is the **dissolution of the self while the location holds.**

You are still there. You are still *ʿalā makānatika* — at your station, in your place. But what you *are* has changed past recognition. And because al-Jalalayn's endpoint is *ḥijāra* — stones — the deepest reading of the verse reaches past animal degradation into total paralysis. A stone does not suffer the inability to go forward and back because a stone has no forward or backward at all. The coordinates of movement have been abolished. There is no striving, no *istibāq*, no forward-thrown urgency. Just fixity.

This is the portrait of what a person arrives at when they spend a lifetime choosing, day after day, neither to advance toward the truth nor to return from their wrong direction. The *masakh* in place is not arbitrary. It is the *fulfilment* of the direction they were always moving toward — the condition of pure fixity, where movement was possible and was refused until the refusing became the self.

And the grammar insists on the permanence. *Wa lā yarjiʿūn* — not "could not return" (a past event, possibly revisable). But *yarjiʿūn* — the ongoing imperfect — they do not return, they keep not returning. The door behind them has closed with the finality of a verb that will not leave the present tense.

This is the verse's quiet warning about what spiritual stasis accumulates into. The person who is neither going forward nor going back in this life — who is not advancing toward what is true and not returning from what is wrong — is living in a rehearsal of that permanent fixity. The *masakh* does not arrive suddenly. It is the crystallization of a direction that was chosen incrementally, over many ordinary days, until the choosing became the shape of the self.

## Conclusion: The Unstated Gift

Let me bring us home.

These two verses are, on the surface, two descriptions of punishment. Erasure of sight. Transformation in place. Two images of divine power applied against the human form.

But the *law* at the beginning of each verse is doing the opposite of what a threat does. A threat announces what *will* come. *Law* announces what *is not coming*. Every word of horror in these two verses is wrapped in the grammar of the withheld, the restrained, the mercy-in-practice.

And so the two verses are, at their core, a disclosure of **what is happening to you right now** — the good you cannot see because it has been given without interruption.

Your eyes open this morning. That was not inevitable. *Law nashāʾu* — if He had willed — and the opening of your eye would be an event that no longer happened. But He did not will it. The mercy of *nashāʾu* — present tense, renewed, ongoing — extended the gift again. And you got out of bed and did not notice.

Your feet carried you to where you needed to go. *Law nashāʾu* — and you would still be in your place, frozen, neither able to proceed nor return. But He did not will it. The mercy again. Unnoticed again.

The Qur'an does not scold you for not noticing. It opens the window. It shows you the un-happened world, where the eyes are gone and the body is stone. It lets you stand there for two verses. And then it brings you back.

You are back. The eyes still open. The feet still move. The morning light still hits the retina and becomes a world.

*Fa-annā yubṣirūn* — from where would they see?

From the same place you see, right now. Which is: from nowhere that belongs to you. From the mercy of a will that said *nashāʾu*, and chose to give the gift again, in this moment, in this breath, in this opening of the eye that you did not earn and did not cause and have — until this very second — never quite fully felt.

Feel it now.
