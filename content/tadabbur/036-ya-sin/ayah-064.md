# Burn Today, Because You Used To

### Surah Ya-Sin, Ayah 64

> [ayah:36:64] ٱصْلَوْهَا ٱلْيَوْمَ بِمَا كُنتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ

*Iṣlawhā l-yawma bimā kuntum takfurūn*

**"Burn in it this Day because of what you used to disbelieve."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing at the Threshold

I want you to slow down before we even touch the words. Because to feel this verse, you have to know where you are standing when it is spoken.

You are not at the beginning of the story. You are at the end of it. Everything has already happened. Every morning you ever woke up, every choice you made and unmade, every time you meant to turn back and didn't — all of it is now behind you. The book is closed. The accounting is finished. And you are being told where to go.

Let me rebuild the room you are standing in.

A few verses earlier, in this same passage of Ya-Sin, a command goes out across the gathered creation: **"And stand apart this Day, O you criminals"** (36:59). Imagine that. A lifetime of blending in — being one face in the crowd, one more person who "didn't really practice but wasn't a bad person," one more soul who kept meaning to figure it out later — and now, suddenly, the crowd dissolves. You are made to stand alone. The camouflage is gone.

Then comes a reproach, almost tender in its disappointment: *Did I not make a covenant with you, O children of Adam, that you would not worship Shaytan?* (36:60). And: *This is the Hellfire you were promised* (36:63). Notice the word — *promised*. Not threatened. Promised. As in, it was told to you plainly, again and again, and you treated the telling as background noise.

And then, into that silence, the verse we are sitting with tonight:

**Iṣlawhā l-yawma bimā kuntum takfurūn.**

Five words. That is the entire verse. Five words to deliver a verdict that an entire life was building toward.

Here is what I need you to feel before we begin. This verse is not God losing His temper. There is no rage in it. Read it again and listen for anger — you will not find it. What you will find is something far more sobering: a perfect, calm, exact correspondence. A sentence that fits the crime the way a key fits a lock. And the most unsettling part — the part we are going to spend the next hour uncovering — is that **the punishment is shaped exactly like the life that earned it.** Not arbitrary. Not exaggerated. Shaped like the habit itself.

Stay with me. Because by the time we finish, I don't think you will hear the word *"used to"* the same way again. There is a small, ordinary phrase buried in this verse — *kuntum*, "you used to" — and it is going to turn out to be the most frightening word in the whole sentence. More frightening than the fire.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Iṣlawhā* — Not "Enter," but "Roast"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open this first word, because the whole verse pivots on it.

In English, when we talk about someone going to the Fire, we reach for the word *enter*. "They will enter the Fire." It is a doorway word. You enter a room, you enter a building — you cross a threshold and you are inside. The word says nothing about what happens after you cross.

Arabic has a word for "enter" — *dakhala*. And it would have fit here perfectly. The grammar would have been clean: *udkhulūhā*, "enter it." In fact, when the classical commentators explain this verse — al-Baghawi, al-Muyassar — they reach for exactly that word to gloss it: *udkhulūhā*, "enter it." They use the doorway word to explain it to us.

But Allah did not use the doorway word.

He used **ٱصْلَوْهَا** — *iṣlawhā*. From the root **ṣād-lām-yā (Ṣly)**.

Now let's open that root. In the classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — *ṣaliya an-nār* does not mean "to enter the fire." It means **to be roasted by it. To be exposed to its sustained heat. To suffer the fire, to endure its burning, to be cooked in it over time.** It is the word an Arab would use for meat placed over coals — *maṣliyy*, roasted. It is the word for someone who stands too close to the flame and feels his skin sear.

Feel the difference. *Enter* is a single moment — you cross the threshold, and the word is finished with you. *Iṣlawhā* has **no exit and no end.** It does not describe arriving at the Fire. It describes *being subjected to* the Fire. The doorway word ends at the door. This word follows you all the way in and never stops.

[PAUSE]

And listen to the sound of it. *Iṣlawhā.* That heavy, emphatic **ṣād** at the front — in Arabic this is one of the *mufakhkham* letters, the "thickened" sounds, pronounced with the back of the tongue raised, the mouth filled with weight. It is not a light letter. It lands like something heavy. And then the word trails off into that long, open **-hā** at the end — *iṣlawhaaa* — like a sound with no natural stopping point, a breath that keeps going. The very phonetics of the word enact what it means: a heavy beginning, and then a duration that does not close.

So already, in word one, we have learned something the translation could never carry. This is not a sentence about a destination. It is a sentence about an exposure that does not end.

Hold onto that. Because in a moment we are going to discover that the *exposure that does not end* is the precise mirror image of something the criminals themselves did.

---

### Element Two: *Kuntum Takfurūn* — The Grammar of a Habit

Now we come to the word I warned you about. The small one. The frightening one.

Let me teach you the grammar first, with something from your own life.

In Arabic there is a construction — *kāna* (the verb "to be," in the past) followed by a verb in the *present/ongoing* tense. **كُنتُمْ تَكْفُرُونَ** — *kuntum takfurūn.* Literally: "you *were* [continuously] disbelieving."

Here is why that construction matters. In English we have two very different ways of talking about the past:

*"You lied."* — One event. It happened once. It's over.

*"You used to lie."* — Something else entirely. Not one event. A pattern. A habit. A character. Something you did again and again until it became simply what you do.

Arabic's *kāna* + present tense is exactly that second meaning. It is the grammar of the habitual past. Of *"used to."* Of the thing you did so many times it stopped being an action and became a description of you.

So *kuntum takfurūn* does not mean "you disbelieved" — one bad decision, one dramatic rejection of God in a single moment of defiance. It means: **"you used to disbelieve."** It was your routine. Your default setting. The thing you returned to morning after morning, year after year, without ever quite deciding to. A practice. A habit. A way of being that you slid into so gradually you may never have noticed you were doing it at all.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how that changes everything?

We tend to imagine the people of the Fire as villains — as people who, at some defining moment, looked God in the face and said *no.* Dramatic. Deliberate. A clear, conscious rejection.

But the grammar tells a quieter and far more terrifying story. The verse does not say *you rejected* — one heroic act of defiance. It says *you used to disbelieve* — the slow, undramatic accumulation of a thousand ordinary turnings-away. The prayer you kept postponing. The reminder you kept scrolling past. The voice inside that you kept telling, *later, later, not now.* No single one of them felt like rejecting God. Each one felt like nothing at all.

And that is the point. **Kufr, in this verse, is not portrayed as an event. It is portrayed as a habit.** Not a door you walked through, but a path you wore smooth with repetition.

Now — let me open the root itself, because it deepens this even further.

The root of *takfurūn* is **kāf-fā-rā (kfr)**. And before it ever meant "to disbelieve," this root meant something very physical and very ordinary: **to cover. To conceal. To bury.**

This is documented and concrete — the Qur'an itself uses it this way. In Surah al-Ḥadīd, the farmer is called a *kāffār* (57:20) — not because the farmer is a disbeliever, but because the farmer **covers the seed with soil.** That is the literal root. The one who buries. The one who covers something over so it can't be seen.

So the disbeliever, in the deepest sense of the word, is **the one who covers.** The truth is there — plain, promised, repeated — and he takes it and buries it under the soil of his distractions. He doesn't necessarily argue with it. He doesn't shout it down. He just... covers it over. Day after day. Habitually. *Kuntum takfurūn* — *you used to keep covering it up.*

Sit with the picture: a lifetime spent gently shoveling soil over a truth you could not bring yourself to look at directly.

### Element Three: *Bimā* and *Al-Yawma* — The Cause and the Collapse of Time

Now watch how the verse assembles these pieces, because the architecture is exact.

Two small words hold the whole sentence together.

First: **بِمَا** — *bimā.* The little particle *bi-* attached to *mā* ("what"). Here it is what the grammarians call the *bāʾ of causation* — the "because" that links a recompense to its cause. We see this construction all over the Qur'an: *bimā kuntum taʿmalūn*, "because of what you used to do." It is the language of consequence. It tells you: what comes next is not random. It is *earned.* It flows directly from what came before. There is a causal cord running from the life to the verdict, and *bimā* is that cord.

And second — the word at the very center, the hinge of the whole sentence: **ٱلْيَوْمَ** — *al-yawma.* "**This Day.** Today."

I want you to notice the collision of time happening in this verse, because it is extraordinary.

The disbelief is in the **past, ongoing** tense — *kuntum takfurūn*, "you *used to* disbelieve." A habit stretched across an entire vanished lifetime.

But the burning is **al-yawma — TODAY.** Right now. Present. Immediate. This very moment.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what has happened? A whole life of *"later, later, not now"* — and it has all collapsed into a single, inescapable **now.** Every postponement they ever made, every *I'll deal with it eventually*, has arrived at the one day they cannot postpone. The habit was spread thin across decades. The reckoning is concentrated into a single point: *al-yawma.* Today.

And notice — the entire judgment scene of Ya-Sin pulses with this word. In the very next verse: *"al-yawma — Today We seal their mouths"* (36:65). The Day they spent a lifetime treating as far away, theoretical, somebody-else's-problem — that Day is now simply called *today.* The most ordinary word in any language. The word for the day you are actually living in. That is what the Last Day becomes when it arrives: not "the future." Just *today.*

The cruelest mercy of language: the Day they refused to believe in is announced to them in the present tense.

### The Bridge: From Words to Reality

So let's gather what these five words have shown us, because the construction is now complete and we can see its shape.

- **Iṣlawhā** — not "enter," but *be roasted,* a sustained exposure with no exit.
- **Kuntum takfurūn** — not a single rejection, but a *sustained habit,* a lifetime of quietly covering over the truth.
- **Bimā** — a cord of pure causation binding the second to the first.
- **Al-yawma** — and all of it collapsing into a single, present, inescapable *today.*

Now look at what is staring back at us. **A sustained, endless exposure to fire — *because of* — a sustained, lifelong habit of covering.** The grammar of the punishment is the same grammar as the crime. *Continuous* answers *continuous.* The form matches.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of "Used To"

Let me start where you actually live.

Think about anyone you know whose life quietly fell apart. Almost never does it happen in a single catastrophic decision. The person who wakes up one day fifty pounds overweight did not eat fifty pounds in an afternoon. The marriage that ends in silence did not collapse in one fight — it eroded across a thousand small evenings of two people choosing their phones over each other's eyes. The career that stalls, the friendship that dies, the talent that withers — almost none of it is the result of a dramatic choice. It is the result of a *habit.* Of "used to." Of something done so many small times that it became, invisibly, the shape of a life.

There is a phrase for this that you have probably heard: *the compound effect.* Tiny choices, repeated, compounding silently in the dark, until one day the total is presented to you and you cannot believe how large it grew. You never decided to become this. You just... kept doing the small thing. *You used to.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold that ordinary, human truth up against this verse — and watch it become eternal.

*Kuntum takfurūn.* "You used to disbelieve."

The most frightening thing about this verse is not that it describes monstrous people. It is that it describes **habitual** people. People exactly like us, who lived by accumulation, who never made a single grand decision against God — and were never asked to. Their whole case was built, brick by invisible brick, out of *used to.* Used to skip. Used to postpone. Used to scroll past. Used to mean to change tomorrow. Used to cover the seed of truth with one more handful of soil.

Here is the thing about a habit: it disguises itself as nothing. That is its entire power. Each individual instance is so small it carries no weight, sets off no alarm. *It's just today. It's just this once. I'll start next week.* And because no single instance feels like a decision, you never experience yourself as choosing. You feel like you are merely... drifting. Waiting. Getting around to it.

But Allah does not file your life under "intentions." He files it under *kuntum* — what you *actually, repeatedly did.* The grammar of the Day is not the grammar of what you meant to become. It is the grammar of what you kept doing.

And this should land on us as a warning that cuts in *both* directions — because the grammar of *"used to"* is exactly how the people of Paradise are described too. *"They used to sleep but little of the night"* (51:17). *"They used to race toward good deeds"* (21:90). Same construction. Same habitual past. The righteous are not people who made one heroic choice either. They are people who built a different habit. Who *used to* turn toward Him in the same small, undramatic, repeated way that the others *used to* turn away.

[PAUSE]

So here is the practical weight of this theme — and I want you to take it tonight, not someday. **Stop waiting for the dramatic turning point.** It is not coming. You are not going to have a single cinematic moment of transformation. You are going to have *today.* And then another today. The only question this verse really asks of you is: *what are you building a "used to" out of, right now, in the small repeated choices nobody sees?* Because that — not your intentions, not your someday — that is the file with your name on it.

Change one small repeated thing. Today. Make it a *used to.* That is the entire mechanism, working in your favor for once.

### Theme Two: The Mirror — When the Punishment Is Shaped Like the Sin

Now let me take you somewhere deeper, into the design of divine justice itself.

Let me begin again with something human.

There is a principle we all feel in our bones, even when we can't name it: that the most fitting consequence is the one that *fits.* When a liar is finally not believed even while telling the truth — we feel the rightness of it. When the one who abandoned everyone ends up alone — there is a terrible symmetry to it that feels less like cruelty and more like *recognition.* As though the consequence is not something added to the act from outside, but something that was *inside the act all along,* finally made visible. The act ripening into its own fruit.

The classical scholars have a name for this principle in divine justice: **al-jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal** — *the recompense is of the same kind as the deed.* The punishment is not arbitrary. It is not a random penalty assigned to a crime, the way a court might fine you a sum of money that has nothing to do with what you did. It is the deed itself, grown to full size and turned back toward you. The harvest of the seed you planted.

Now bring this back to our verse, and watch the mirror.

What was the sin? *Kuntum takfurūn* — a **sustained, continuous, lifelong** habit. Not one act, but an enduring state. Something that went on and on and on.

What is the recompense? *Iṣlawhā* — a **sustained, continuous** roasting. Not a single strike, but an enduring exposure. Something that goes on and on and on.

[PAUSE]

The *shape* of the punishment is the *shape* of the sin. *Continuous* is answered by *continuous.* They did not turn away once; they were not given a single instant of fire. They turned away *habitually, endlessly* — and so the fire is *habitual, endless.* The verb *iṣlawhā* — roasting, ongoing exposure, no exit — is the exact temporal mirror of *kuntum takfurūn* — disbelieving, ongoing, no end. Allah did not assign a punishment *to* their sin. He let their sin *become* their punishment, by giving it the same shape.

And now go deeper, into the root we opened earlier — because the mirror is even more precise than the time.

Remember what *kufr* literally is: **covering.** Concealing. Burying the truth under soil so you don't have to look at it. The disbeliever spent a lifetime *covering* — hiding the truth, hiding from it, keeping it out of sight.

And what does fire do? **Fire uncovers.** Fire is the one thing that cannot be hidden from and that hides nothing. It strips. It exposes. It removes every covering — clothing, comfort, distraction, the soil you piled over the seed. The one whose entire life was an act of *covering* is delivered into the one reality that *uncovers* absolutely. He spent his days burying the truth; now there is nothing left to bury it with, and nowhere left to hide.

And recall *al-yawma* — *today.* The whole life was spent treating the Day as covered, hidden behind a horizon, safely out of view. And now the covering is pulled off of *that* too: the Day they hid from is standing in front of them, uncovered, present, named simply *today.*

Everything they covered is now uncovered. That is the mirror, complete.

[PAUSE]

Now — I want to be careful here, and honest, because this theme can curdle into something it should not.

The point of *al-jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal* is **not** to make us gloat over the punishment of others. It is to make us tremble at the *design* — and then to turn the mirror around on ourselves while there is still time to change what it reflects. Because if it is true that the consequence is shaped like the deed, then this is not only a verse about the Fire. It is a diagnostic tool for your own life *right now.*

Ask yourself, tonight: *What am I covering?* What truth about myself, about my relationship with God, about the thing I know I need to fix — am I quietly shoveling soil over, day after day, so I don't have to look at it? Because covering is not free. The root teaches us that every act of concealment is building toward an exposure. The only mercy — and it is an immense one — is that the exposure has not happened yet. The soil can still be cleared away by your own hands today, in private, before it is cleared away for you in public on a Day called *today.*

The practical application is severe in its simplicity: **uncover it now, yourself.** Whatever you have been burying — name it, in your sujood, tonight. Drag it into the light voluntarily, while light is still a mercy and not yet a fire. The one who uncovers his own faults before Allah in this life is spared having them uncovered before all of creation in the next. *That* is the choice the verse is quietly offering, underneath the verdict.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we have done the work, let me show you the whole verse standing together, and you will see that it was a single, perfectly engineered sentence all along.

> **Iṣlawhā** — *roast in it, sustained, no exit*
> **al-yawma** — *today, the present that swallowed every "later"*
> **bimā** — *because of — the cord of pure consequence*
> **kuntum takfurūn** — *the habit of covering, sustained, no end*

Read it now from both directions and watch the mirror close.

From the front: an endless burning — *today* — because of — an endless covering.

From the back: an endless covering — was the cause of — *today's* — endless burning.

The first word and the last word are the *same shape.* *Iṣlawhā* (continuous, ongoing, no exit) is the exact reflection of *kuntum takfurūn* (continuous, ongoing, no end). And the act of *covering* (kufr) is answered by the one force that *uncovers* (fire). And the *Day* they treated as hidden is the *today* in which everything is revealed. The verse is not a list of words. It is a hall of mirrors, and every element reflects its own crime.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes were never two things. They were one truth approached from two doors.

The grammar showed us that *kufr* is a **habit, not an event** — *kuntum takfurūn,* "you used to." The first theme made that personal: your life is being filed under what you *repeatedly do,* not what you *intend* — so build your "used to" carefully, today.

The grammar showed us that *iṣlawhā* is a **sustained exposure** that mirrors a sustained sin, and that *kufr* literally means **covering** while fire **uncovers.** The second theme made that personal: the consequence is shaped like the deed, so uncover what you are burying — now, yourself, in mercy — before it is uncovered for you, in fire.

One insight, two directions: **the small, repeated, hidden things are the realest things about you — and they are building, silently, toward a Day when nothing repeated and nothing hidden will be hidden anymore.**

### Questions to Carry

1. If Allah filed my life under *kuntum* — "you used to" — what would the file actually say? Not what I intend. What I *repeatedly do.*
2. What truth am I quietly covering with soil, day after day, so that I never have to look at it directly?
3. The people of the Fire were not asked to make one dramatic choice. Neither are the people of the Garden. What small thing could I make into a *"used to"* — starting with today?
4. I keep telling God *later.* What in me is so certain that *later* will come?
5. Would I rather uncover my faults now, alone, in the dark of sujood — or have them uncovered then, before everyone, in a light that burns?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The Fire does not arrive as a stranger to punish you; it arrives wearing the exact shape of the life you quietly built — sustained for sustained, covering answered by uncovering — and it is called *today.*

### The Closing Invitation

I am not asking you to feel terror tonight. Terror fades by morning. I am asking you to feel the *design* — the calm, exact, unhurried fairness of a God who does not punish arbitrarily but lets every deed ripen into its own true fruit. And then I am asking you to do the one thing this verse, underneath its verdict, is quietly inviting: to uncover something. To clear away one handful of the soil you have been piling over a truth you already know. Not all of it. One handful, tonight. And then to begin, in the smallest repeatable way, building a different *used to.*

The verse was spoken to people for whom it was too late. You are reading it while it is still early. That gap — between their *today* and your *today* — is the entire space of mercy. Don't cover it over too.

### Brief Duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who let every deed grow into its own true harvest — do not let our habit be the covering of truth, but the uncovering of our own hearts before You. Pull back the soil we have piled over what we already know, while the pulling-back is still mercy and not yet fire. And make our *"used to"* a turning toward You, repeated until the Day we meet You — a Day we pray You make, for us, a *today* of light. *Āmīn.*
