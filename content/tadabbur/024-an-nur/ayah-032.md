# Marry Them Off: The Causative Command and the Promise That Answers Fear

## Surah An-Nur, Ayah 32

> وَأَنكِحُوا۟ ٱلْأَيَـٰمَىٰ مِنكُمْ وَٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ مِنْ عِبَادِكُمْ وَإِمَآئِكُمْ ۚ إِن يَكُونُوا۟ فُقَرَآءَ يُغْنِهِمُ ٱللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِۦ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ وَٰسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ

> *Wa-ankihū al-ayāmā minkum waṣ-ṣāliḥīna min ʿibādikum wa-imāʾikum. In yakūnū fuqarāʾa yughnihim Allāhu min faḍlihi. Wallāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīm.*

> **And marry off the unmarried among you, and the righteous among your male and female servants. If they are poor, Allah will enrich them from His bounty. And Allah is vast, all-knowing.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

Before we open this ayah, sit with where An-Nur has just taken us.

The surah began with thunder. The opening ayah is unusual in the Quran — Allah names the surah itself, calls it *farḍnāhā* (an obligation We have made obligatory), and tells us it contains *bayyināt* — explicit, clear signs. Then come the rulings: the punishment for unlawful intimacy, the punishment for the slander of chaste people. Then the story of the slander against ʿĀʾisha — long, painful, named for what it was.

Then the architecture of private life: don't enter homes without permission. Then the commands to lower the gaze — first to men, then to women, with that haunting parallel structure we have already explored.

By the time we arrive at ayah 32, the surah has built a complete world of moral expectation. Lower your gaze. Guard your chastity. Don't enter where you don't belong. Don't accuse what you cannot prove. The standard is high.

[PAUSE]

And here is where, if you were the listener in Madinah, you would feel a knot in your stomach.

Because the standard is high, but the body is not made of stone. The hormones are real. The longing is real. The desire to be known and held and loved is real. If the surah only legislated restraint without legislating the path to fulfillment — it would be a surah that asks the impossible.

So Allah does something here that we should not pass over quickly. Having raised the standard of chastity to its highest height, He immediately legislates the infrastructure that makes chastity possible.

He turns to the *community* and says: **marry them off.**

### The Hook

There is a hidden verb form in this ayah that changes who is being asked to do what. There is a word for "the unmarried" that includes people you may not have realized it includes. And there is a conditional sentence — "if they are poor" — that names the exact objection you used yesterday, last month, last year, to delay or discourage a marriage.

And then Allah answers it. Not with a suggestion. With a promise.

The question we need to carry into this ayah is this: **what is the relationship between fear of poverty and the will to marry — and which one does Allah want us to organize our lives around?**

[PAUSE]

Let's open it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Means "Cause It to Happen"

I want to teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the word in the ayah.

Arabic verbs come in patterns. The simplest pattern — what scholars call Form I — is the bare action. *Kataba* — he wrote. *Nazala* — he descended. *Dakhala* — he entered.

But Arabic has another pattern — Form IV. You take the same root and you add a hamza at the front, and you change the vowels. *Kataba* (he wrote) becomes *aktaba* (he caused [someone] to write — he dictated). *Nazala* (he descended) becomes *anzala* (he caused [it] to descend — he sent it down). *Dakhala* (he entered) becomes *adkhala* (he caused [him] to enter — he admitted him).

Form IV is causative. The actor in Form IV is not the one doing the action — the actor is the one *making the action happen* through someone else. You don't write — you make writing happen. You don't descend — you make descent happen.

This is a small grammatical fact. Hold onto it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the first verb of our ayah: **أَنكِحُوا — *ankiḥū*.**

The root is *n-k-ḥ* — the root of *nikāḥ*, marriage. Form I would be *nakaḥa* — he married, he entered into marriage. That is the personal verb. That is what one person does when they take a spouse.

But Allah does not say *inkiḥū* (Form I imperative — "you all marry"). He says *ankiḥū* — Form IV imperative. The causative.

Stay with me. This is doing something.

He is not saying to the unmarried people: *get married*. He is saying to the *community* — the parents, the elders, the friends, the masjid, the brothers and sisters who are already established — **cause marriage to happen for them**. Make it happen. Bring it about. Be the active agent who turns the unmarried into the married.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this dissolves.

In our culture, marriage is treated as a strictly individual project. Two people meet, they decide, they negotiate, they marry. The community is a spectator at the wedding. The community's job is to show up, eat the food, congratulate, and go home.

But Allah's grammar puts the community in a different role. The grammar makes marriage *the community's verb*. The unmarried are not the subject of "marry." The community is. The unmarried are the *object*. The community's job is to be the one doing the matchmaking, the encouraging, the funding, the housing, the protecting — until the marriage actually happens.

Here is what is remarkable. The verb is plural and addressed to *you* — *ankiḥū* — meaning every reasonable adult in earshot. Allah didn't address only fathers. He didn't address only guardians. He addressed *the believers*. Every one of us, in this grammatical sentence, has a job to do for someone else's marriage.

If marriage is one of the great containers Allah has made for chastity, then a community in which marriages are not happening is a community that has neglected a *farḍ* — and the surah's opening word *farḍnāhā* echoes here. The whole surah was an obligation Allah made obligatory. And the infrastructure of that obligation lives in this verb.

This single grammatical form rewires everything. Marriage is not your private problem. It is *our* obligation, made plural and causative by the very grammar of the command.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word That Refuses to Pick Sides

The next word is even more remarkable, and we will need to slow down to see what is in it.

**الْأَيَامَىٰ — *al-ayāmā*.**

Most translations render this as "the unmarried" or "the singles." That is correct, but it is not specific enough.

The root is *ʾ-y-m*, and the singular is *ayyim*. In classical Arabic lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib — *ayyim* is defined as a person who has no spouse. Crucially, the word does not distinguish:

- Whether they have never married
- Or whether they were married and the spouse died
- Or whether they were married and divorced

All three are *ayyim*. The widow, the divorcée, the never-married — Arabic gives them one word.

And it does not stop there. *Ayyim* — and its plural *ayāmā* — does not specify gender. Some lexicons note that it can lean toward describing women, but in usage and in the grammatical agreement here, *ayāmā* is treated as a gender-mixed plural that includes the unmarried man and the unmarried woman.

[PAUSE]

So here is what the word is doing. Allah is telling the community: facilitate marriage for **the unmarried among you** — and He chooses a word that is so wide it sweeps in:

- The brother in his late twenties who has never married
- The sister whose first marriage ended in divorce
- The elderly aunt whose husband died years ago
- The widower with three children
- The young man who is ready
- The woman who returned from a difficult marriage and has been told, quietly, that her chances are over

All of them. *Al-ayāmā minkum* — "the unmarried among you." Plural. Inclusive. Not segmented by age, not segmented by gender, not segmented by reason.

[PAUSE]

There is something here we should not pass over.

In many Muslim communities — be honest with yourself — there is a hidden hierarchy of *ayāmā*. The never-married young woman is treated as a project. The divorced sister is treated as a problem. The widow is treated as if her chapter has closed. The previously married brother carries less stigma; the previously married sister carries more.

Allah's vocabulary refuses this hierarchy.

He uses one word that covers them all. He does not say "marry off your never-married daughters." He does not say "marry off your young men." He says **al-ayāmā** — and every category is folded into one community of people who deserve, by virtue of being unmarried, the active causative effort of those around them.

When you next see a divorced sister in your community, the Arabic word in this ayah is calling her exactly what it is calling the seventeen-year-old waiting for her first proposal. *Ayyim*. Same category. Same claim on the community's effort.

[PAUSE]

That is what the grammar does. And the grammar is not optional. The grammar is the command.

### Element Three: The Conditional That Names Our Excuse

Now we come to the most stunning move in the ayah.

Allah has just commanded the community to facilitate marriage for the unmarried. And in classical rhetoric, when a command is given, the listener's mind immediately starts generating objections.

What does the human mind say in response to this command? Walk through it with me. The voice in your head says:

- *They can't afford it.*
- *He doesn't have a job.*
- *She has nothing to bring.*
- *They'll struggle. They'll be poor. They'll suffer.*
- *Better to wait until they're stable.*

These are the same objections a person in Madinah would have generated. They are the same objections a person in Toronto, Detroit, London, Karachi, or Cairo generates today.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah does next:

> **إِن يَكُونُوا فُقَرَاءَ — *in yakūnū fuqarāʾa* — "If they are poor..."**

Notice. He names it.

He does not pretend the objection doesn't exist. He does not say "and don't worry about money." He does not say "money isn't important." He picks up the exact word the objector is using — *fuqarāʾ*, poor — and He places it inside His own sentence.

This is one of the most respectful rhetorical moves in the Quran. Allah honors the worry. He does not dismiss it. He says: yes, you are right. They might be poor. That is a real possibility. I will not pretend otherwise.

[PAUSE]

And then He answers it.

> **يُغْنِهِمُ اللَّهُ مِن فَضْلِهِ — *yughnihim Allāhu min faḍlihi* — "Allah will enrich them from His bounty."**

Now look at the verb. *Yughni* — Form IV again, from the root *gh-n-y*. The root *gh-n-y* in Form I means to be self-sufficient, to be free of need, to have enough. Form IV — *aghnā / yughnī* — means *to make someone else self-sufficient. To cause them to be without need.*

Same grammatical pattern as *ankiḥū*. Allah commanded the community to cause marriage. Now Allah is saying *He Himself* will cause the enrichment.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this exchange.

You — the community — do the causative for the marriage.
He — Allah — does the causative for the provision.

You make the wedding happen.
He will make the wealth happen.

You take the visible step.
He attends to the invisible result.

This is the trade. This is the economy that this ayah is teaching us.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the source of the enrichment: **مِن فَضْلِهِ — *min faḍlihi* — "from His bounty."**

The root *f-ḍ-l* means surplus, excess, the part that overflows after a need is met. *Faḍl* is what is *more than* what was strictly required. Allah does not say *yughnihim Allāhu min māl* (from wealth) or *min rizq* (from sustenance) — He says *min faḍlihi*, from His overflow.

This is significant. The word names a category of provision that is not earned, not budgeted, not predicted. It is the surplus that exists *outside* the visible economy. It is the door that opens that you didn't know existed. It is the unexpected client, the unforeseen inheritance, the relative who calls, the contract that lands.

The provision Allah is promising is not from the column of "your salary." It is from the column of "His bounty" — a category that you cannot predict and you cannot calculate, because it does not live in the spreadsheet of your visible income.

[PAUSE]

And then the ayah closes with two of Allah's names placed beside each other:

> **وَاللَّهُ وَاسِعٌ عَلِيمٌ — *Wallāhu wāsiʿun ʿalīm* — "And Allah is vast, all-knowing."**

*Wāsiʿ* — from the root *w-s-ʿ* — means vast, expansive, having a capacity that exceeds whatever is needed. The root in everyday Arabic describes a wide road, an expansive room, a generous space. Used of Allah, it means: His resources do not run out. The well does not bottom. The bounty has no edge.

*ʿAlīm* — all-knowing. He knows every couple. He knows every fear. He knows every concern about rent and groceries and whether the heating bill can be paid in February.

Now hear them placed next to each other. *Wāsiʿun ʿalīm.* Vast in resource, perfect in knowledge.

This is the divine answer to the most basic human worry about provision. The worry says: *the resources are limited, and no one knows our specific situation.* Allah's two names answer in exact reverse: *the resources are limitless, and I know your specific situation.*

The fear is structurally inverted. The closing names of this ayah are the precise theological refutation of the scarcity mindset.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So the linguistic architecture of this ayah, when we put the pieces together, looks like this:

A causative verb makes marriage the community's job.
A gender-neutral, status-neutral noun ensures no unmarried person is excluded.
A conditional clause names the objection without dismissing it.
Another causative verb shifts the burden of provision from the couple to Allah Himself.
The source of provision is named as *faḍl* — Allah's surplus, not the couple's salary.
The closing names guarantee the source is vast and the application is precise.

Six grammatical and lexical moves, working in coordination, to dismantle the most universal objection to marriage and replace it with a different economy.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about how we are supposed to live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Economy of Fear and the Economy of Bounty

I want to start somewhere very ordinary. Somewhere you have probably been.

You are in your late twenties. You are sitting with your parents, or you are texting a friend, or you are talking yourself in circles in the car. The conversation is about marriage.

There is someone. The character is right. The deen is right. The compatibility is real. The proposal is on the table.

And then someone — them, you, your father, your mother, your own anxious internal voice — says it:

*Wait until you're more stable.*

Stable here is a code word. It means: until the savings account hits a certain number, until the job is more secure, until the apartment is bigger, until the parents-in-law are impressed, until the wedding can be the kind of wedding that doesn't embarrass anyone, until, until, until.

[PAUSE]

You probably know the cost of this *until*.

The cost is years. The cost is gazes that should have been lowered being lifted instead, because the body did not get the memo about the spreadsheet. The cost is relationships that exist in the gray space the surah was specifically built to make impossible. The cost is loneliness. The cost is depression. The cost is, sometimes, despair.

And the cost is theological. Because somewhere underneath the *until*, there is a quiet sentence that almost no one says out loud:

*I do not trust Allah to provide enough for me to take this step.*

[PAUSE]

That sentence is the one this ayah is addressing.

Notice that Allah did not tell us to be reckless. He did not tell us to marry before we are ready or to refuse to plan. He did not tell us that money does not matter. He told us something more precise.

He told us the *order of operations*.

The economy of fear says: *first the wealth, then the marriage.* Get the income, then get the spouse. Build the foundation, then build the home.

The economy of *faḍl* says: *first the marriage, then the wealth.* Take the step that pleases Me, and the provision will follow from a source you did not budget for.

[PAUSE]

This is not a denial of economics. It is a re-ordering of trust.

Because here is what the conditional sentence is actually doing — and you have to feel this. Allah is saying: I see your fear. I have anticipated it. I have written it into My own ayah, in My own words. I have not left you to wonder whether your worry is heard.

And then I am telling you: that worry is the wrong thing to organize your life around.

[PAUSE]

Think of it like this. Two friends both want to start the same business. Friend A waits until he has saved every dollar he might possibly need before he begins. Friend B starts when he has enough to begin, trusting that the work itself will draw what it needs as it grows.

Friend A may never start. The number is always higher tomorrow than it was yesterday. The price of waiting is invisible to him because he is counting in dollars and not in years.

Friend B starts. The work he does once he starts brings in clients, contacts, opportunities, relationships — almost none of which he could have predicted on the day he opened.

Allah is telling the community: don't be Friend A about marriage. The number you are waiting for will never arrive, because the number lives inside the economy of fear. The provision lives inside a different economy entirely — one that does not turn on until the step has been taken.

*Yughnihim Allāhu min faḍlihi.* He will enrich them — once they are married. Not before. Not as a precondition. As a consequence.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where I want to be careful. This is not a promise that every marriage will become wealthy. Allah did not say *yu'tīhim māl* — He will give them money. He said *yughnihim* — He will make them *not in need*. The promise is the absence of need, not the presence of luxury. The promise is enough, not abundance. The promise is the door, not the palace.

That distinction is the difference between a prosperity gospel and a Quranic theology of provision. Prosperity gospel says God will make you rich. Quran says Allah will free you from being in need. The first is a sales pitch. The second is a covenant.

[PAUSE]

**The application:**

If you are unmarried and waiting because of the *until* — examine the *until* carefully. Is it a real obstacle? Is the person you are considering actually unsuitable, or is it that you have absorbed a cultural standard of "ready" that this ayah was specifically revealed to dismantle?

If you are a parent and you are demanding a level of "established" before you accept a proposal for your son or daughter — examine that demand. Is it the deen speaking, or is it the economy of fear speaking through you?

If you have any role in someone else's marriage — and the grammar says you do — ask yourself: am I helping cause this marriage to happen, or am I one of the obstacles? Am I in the *ankiḥū* or am I in the *until*?

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Community That Causes Marriage

The second theme lives in the causative verb we opened with, and it speaks to something larger than any individual marriage.

Let me name what we are looking at honestly.

We live, most of us, in communities where the unmarried are alone in their unmarriedness. The thirty-two-year-old sister who has been looking for years carries the weight of that search by herself. The brother who has not been able to find someone scrolls apps in the dark. The widow whose chapter the community has quietly closed sits at the back of the masjid. The divorced sister learns to expect that no one will mention her name when proposals are being matched.

This is the community we have built. And whatever its name, it is not the community Allah's grammar describes.

[PAUSE]

The grammar of *ankiḥū* describes a community in which:

- The unmarried are *seen* — the community knows their names and circumstances.
- Their marital state is treated as the community's responsibility, not their individual failure.
- Active matchmaking is happening as a normal social function, not a desperate last resort.
- Money is pooled to make marriages possible — wedding costs lowered, mahr facilitated, housing arranged, dowries bridged — because the community understands that *we* are the verb.
- A divorced sister is folded back into the active *ayāmā* without stigma, because the word includes her on equal grammatical footing.
- A widow is approached for remarriage with the same energy the community uses for the never-married, because the word includes her on equal grammatical footing.

This is not an idealized fiction. This is the community the early Muslims actually built. Read the seerah. Read the lives of the companions. The Prophet ﷺ himself was actively involved in matchmaking. He facilitated the marriages of his daughters, of his companions, of his servants. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb famously offered his daughter Ḥafṣa to ʿUthmān and to Abū Bakr, openly and without embarrassment, because the grammar he had absorbed from the Quran did not let him treat his daughter's marriage as an exclusively private matter.

The companions understood that *ankiḥū* was a verb addressed to *them*.

[PAUSE]

Somewhere along the way, we lost this. We absorbed a different theology of marriage from a different civilization — one in which marriage is a private romantic project that the community attends, but does not author. One in which the unmarried sister's situation is her business, and the community's role is to gossip about it rather than to solve it.

When you read this ayah carefully, you realize that this drift is not a small drift. It is a structural failure that the surah is specifically diagnosing.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want to stay with.

The reason chastity is so difficult in our communities is *not* primarily because individual believers are weak. It is because the *infrastructure of marriage* is broken, and individual chastity was never meant to be carried on the back of a single person without communal support.

The surah legislated chastity in 30 and 31. The surah legislated the infrastructure of chastity in 32. They are one architecture.

If we keep enforcing the standard of 30 and 31 while neglecting the structure of 32, we are demanding the impossible. We are asking individuals to carry, alone, what Allah designed to be carried by a community.

[PAUSE]

**The application:**

Ask yourself, this week, who in your circle is *ayyim*. Make a list. Make it concrete. Names.

Then ask yourself: what is one specific thing I can do, this month, to make a marriage more likely to happen for one of those names? Not a vague intention. A specific action.

It might be a careful introduction. It might be a contribution toward someone's mahr. It might be opening your home for an iftar where two compatible people might meet without pressure. It might be calling a friend in another city about your sister. It might be talking honestly to your son about lowering his expectations about *until*.

You are the verb in this ayah. The grammar is not negotiable.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what this ayah has shown us, when the linguistic and the thematic are placed side by side.

The linguistic showed us a single ayah constructed with two causative verbs that mirror each other. The community causes marriage. Allah causes provision. The structure of the ayah is itself a covenant.

The thematic showed us that the obstacle to marriage in most of our lives is not actually money — it is fear of money, organized into a culture of *until*. And the cure is not more savings. The cure is a re-ordered trust and a re-built community.

These are two views of the same thing. The grammar *is* the theology. The theology *is* the grammar. The community that causes marriage is the community that has internalized that Allah is *Wāsiʿun ʿAlīm* — vast and knowing — and has therefore stopped letting fear of poverty be the boss of marriage.

### Architectural Reveal

Notice now what An-Nur is actually building, ayah by ayah.

The surah does not just legislate. It constructs a complete moral ecosystem. Ayahs 27-29 protect private space. Ayahs 30-31 protect the gaze. Ayah 32 builds the marriage infrastructure that makes 30-31 sustainable. Ayah 33 protects those who *can't yet* marry by commanding chastity until they can. Ayah 34 closes the legislative section with a return to *bayyināt* — clear signs.

The whole architecture is interlocking. You cannot legislate the gaze without legislating marriage. You cannot legislate marriage without legislating the trust in *faḍl* that makes marriage possible at any age, in any economy, for any *ayyim*.

The surah is whole. And ayah 32 is its load-bearing wall.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you imagine your own readiness for marriage — or the readiness of someone you know — what is the *until* you are using? Is it a real obstacle, or is it the economy of fear speaking through you?

2. Who in your community is *ayyim* whose name you have not seriously thought about? What part of you treats their marital state as their problem rather than yours?

3. If Allah named your specific worry inside His own ayah and answered it with His own name *Wāsiʿ* — what does it mean that you are still organizing your decisions around the worry rather than the answer?

4. Is there a marriage you could help cause this year — even by one specific action — that you have been spectating instead of authoring?

5. Where in your life are you waiting for visible provision before taking a step Allah has already commanded? What would it look like to take the step first?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah commanded the community to cause marriage, named the fear of poverty inside the same sentence, and promised that He Himself — vast and knowing — would cause the provision that the fear was waiting for.**

### The Closing Invitation

If something should shift after this, it is this: stop waiting for the fear to subside before acting. The fear is being addressed by the same Voice that made the command. The conditional clause is Allah's mercy on the worried mind. The promise is the answer to the worry. The grammar is the architecture.

You are part of a verb. Step into it.

### Du'ā

> O Allah, You are *Wāsiʿun ʿAlīm* — vast in resources, perfect in knowledge of our specific need. We confess that we have organized too many of our decisions around fear of poverty rather than trust in Your *faḍl*. Make us a community that causes marriage to happen — for the never-married, for the divorced, for the widowed, for everyone Your word *ayyim* names. Enrich those whose marriages are waiting on visible provision, from sources we cannot budget and could not predict. And let us be among those who took Your conditional clause seriously — who heard You name the fear, and trusted the One who answered it. Āmīn.
