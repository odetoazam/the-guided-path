# The Mouth That Believes and the Feet That Walk Away

## Surah An-Nur, Ayah 47

### Arabic

> وَيَقُولُونَ ءَامَنَّا بِٱللَّهِ وَبِٱلرَّسُولِ وَأَطَعْنَا ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّىٰ فَرِيقٌ مِّنْهُم مِّنۢ بَعْدِ ذَٰلِكَ ۚ وَمَآ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

### Transliteration

*Wa yaqūlūna āmannā billāhi wa bir-rasūli wa aṭaʿnā, thumma yatawallā farīqun minhum min baʿdi dhālik. Wa mā ulāʾika bil-muʾminīn.*

### Translation

> "And they say, 'We have believed in Allah and in the Messenger, and we obey.' Then a group of them turns away after that. And those — they are not believers."

## Introduction: The Surah of Exposure

Take a breath. Before we open this ayah, you need to know where we are standing.

Surah An-Nur is the surah of **light** — but light is not a gentle thing in this surah. Light is what shows you what was hidden. Light is what makes the veil meaningful — because there is something to veil. Light is what burns away pretense.

Up until this point in the surah, Allah has been legislating the **outward** — how you dress, how you enter homes, how you speak about other people, how you protect what is sacred and private. The laws of modesty. The laws of slander. The laws of household discipline. All of it has been about what is *seen* — what the eye catches, what the tongue releases, what the body does in the open.

And then — right when you have settled into the assumption that this surah is about the **outside** — Allah turns the light inward. He turns it on the gap between the **mouth** and the **feet**. Between what people *say* about themselves and what they *do* when no one is watching them say it.

This is the climate. The community in Madinah is being formed. People are arriving — sincere ones, half-hearted ones, opportunistic ones. The mosque is full. The hands are raised. The declarations are loud. And somewhere in this gathering, there is a group whose mouths are saying one thing while their feet are already turning toward the door.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel before we read another word: **everyone** in that gathering claimed iman. Everyone said *āmannā*. Everyone said *aṭaʿnā*. The declaration was identical. The Arabic was identical. The volume was identical.

So how does Allah tell them apart?

That is the question this ayah answers. And the answer is going to do something uncomfortable. It is going to come for *us*. Because we say these words too. We say them in salah, in dhikr, in conversation, in posts, in prayers. We say *āmannā*. We say *aṭaʿnā*.

What does Allah see when we say it?

That is the hunger I want you to walk into this ayah with. Not "what did the hypocrites do back then." But: **what is the test that distinguishes the mouth that means it from the mouth that does not — and how does my mouth fare against that test?**

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three things to unpack — and each one is doing something the English translation simply cannot carry.

### Element One: *Yaqūlūna* — The Tense That Catches Them in the Act

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs first, because this matters.

Arabic has two main verb forms. There is the **past tense** — *qālū*, "they said." Something that happened and is finished. Closed. Sealed. And there is the **present-continuous** — *yaqūlūna*, "they are saying," "they keep saying," "they say it again and again."

In English, "they say" sounds neutral. In Arabic, *yaqūlūna* is **a verb in motion**. It is not a recording of one moment. It is the camera left running. It is the saying that *keeps happening*. The repeated declaration. The habitual claim.

Now look at the ayah:

> **وَيَقُولُونَ** ءَامَنَّا بِٱللَّهِ وَبِٱلرَّسُولِ وَأَطَعْنَا

*And they keep saying — "we have believed in Allah and in the Messenger, and we obey."*

Notice something. The outer verb — "they say" — is in the **continuous, ongoing form**. But what they are saying — *āmannā*, *aṭaʿnā* — those are in the **past tense, completed form**. "We *have* believed. We *have* obeyed."

Sit with that asymmetry.

The *saying* is ongoing. The *belief* is reported as already finished. As if the work is done. As if the matter is settled. As if iman is an achievement they have completed and now merely *announce*.

Here is what is remarkable. Iman in the Quran is almost never a finished verb when it is real. Real iman is a thing that **grows** — *yazīdu īmānan*, "it increases them in faith." Real iman is a thing that **deepens**, that **trembles**, that **leans**, that **fears its own loss**. Real iman keeps moving forward.

But these speakers have **frozen** their iman in the past tense. They have placed it behind them. *We have already believed.* As if it were a transaction completed at the door of the mosque, rather than a living thing they must carry forward.

[PAUSE]

This is the first crack the ayah opens. Allah is not catching them in a single statement. He is catching them in a **pattern of statement** — a chronic, repeated declaration whose very repetition exposes its hollowness. The person who must keep announcing that they have already believed is performing the *announcement*, not living the *belief*.

Real iman does not need to keep declaring itself. It declares itself by what it does next.

What this invites us to consider: how often do *we* say the words to ourselves — "I believe, I trust Allah, I have submitted" — as a way of closing the matter, rather than as a confession that the matter is never closed? The ongoing *yaqūlūna* is a mirror. Some of us live inside it.

### Element Two: The Three-Part Claim — and What It Quietly Reveals

Look at exactly what they say:

> **ءَامَنَّا بِٱللَّهِ وَبِٱلرَّسُولِ وَأَطَعْنَا**

*"We have believed in Allah, and in the Messenger, and we obey."*

This is a three-part claim. Belief in Allah. Belief in the Messenger. Obedience.

In Arabic, the conjunction *wa* — "and" — is doing more than stringing items together. It is *binding* them. The structure is saying: *these three are a single package*. Belief in Allah, belief in the Messenger, and obedience — they come together or they do not come at all.

Now notice something the speakers themselves may not have noticed.

The first two — *āmannā billāhi wa bir-rasūl* — they connect belief to two **objects**: Allah and the Messenger. The third — *aṭaʿnā* — they leave **bare**. "We obey." No object. Obey *whom*? Obey *what*?

In Arabic grammar, when a verb that normally requires an object is left without one, it is doing one of two things. Either the object is so obvious it is implied — "we obey [Allah and the Messenger, of course]" — or the speaker is leaving a deliberate **gap**. A gap they can fill in later. A gap that lets them mean something different from what the listener hears.

Here is what the classical commentators sit with on this. When a person says *aṭaʿnā* without an object, the listener fills it in with the obvious — "they mean Allah and the Messenger, what else?" But the speaker, in their own head, may be filling it in with something else entirely. *We obey... when it suits us. We obey... what we already wanted to do. We obey... when our interest aligns with the command.*

The grammar leaves the door open.

[PAUSE]

This is the second crack. The verbal claim is structured to **sound complete** while leaving the most consequential word — the *object* of obedience — strategically unspecified. The mouth says "we obey" loudly enough that no one asks "obey what."

Stay with this. Because here is a question that should make you uncomfortable.

When *you* say "I obey Allah" — what is the object you are filling in silently? Is it *every command*, including the ones that cost you? Or is it *the commands I was already inclined to follow*? The grammar of the hypocrite is the grammar of the unspoken exception. *I obey... except this. I obey... unless. I obey... when.*

The believer, by contrast, says *samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā* — "we hear and we obey" — and the object is the entire revelation, with no gap left for the nafs to negotiate inside.

What this invites us to consider: every time you encounter a command of Allah and feel resistance, your nafs is reaching for the gap in *aṭaʿnā*. It is trying to fill in the unspoken object with something that excludes *this particular* command. The ayah is showing you the mechanism. Once you see it, you can refuse it.

### Element Three: *Yatawallā Farīqun Minhum* — The Word That Becomes a Body

Now we come to the hinge of the entire ayah. The word that turns everything.

> **ثُمَّ يَتَوَلَّىٰ فَرِيقٌ مِّنْهُم**

*"Then a group of them turns away."*

I need you to stay with the word *yatawallā*.

The root is *w-l-y*. It is one of the most active roots in the Quran. From this single root come words that mean **friend, ally, guardian, master, protector** (*walī*), and also **to take charge, to follow, to come next**, and — in this verb form — **to turn one's back, to walk off, to defect**.

The form *tawallā* (the fifth form, *tafaʿʿala*) carries an intensified, deliberate quality. This is not a passive drift. This is not "they happened to wander away." *Tawallā* is **a chosen turning**. A pivoting of the body. A deliberate act of **giving your back** to the thing you were just facing.

Think about what this means physically. To say *āmannā wa aṭaʿnā* — "we believe and we obey" — your body has to be **facing** the Messenger ﷺ. You are oriented toward him. Your face is toward him. Your declaration is toward him.

And then *yatawallā* — you **turn**. Your face goes elsewhere. Your back is now where your face was. The same body that just declared faith now presents its back to the source of faith.

[PAUSE]

This is not a metaphor that the Quran is using loosely. This is the precise physical action that the word describes. *Yatawallā* is a word that **becomes a body**. The mouth said one thing while facing forward. The feet did another thing by turning around.

Now look at what comes next. *Farīqun minhum* — "a group of them."

The word *farīq* comes from *f-r-q* — to split, to separate, to break apart. A *farīq* is not just "some people." It is a **fragment that has split off** from the larger body. It is a piece that has **separated itself**. The very word announces the rupture.

Allah does not say "and they all turn away." He says "**a group of them** turns away." And He says it after they had all said the same thing together. The declaration was unanimous — *āmannā, aṭaʿnā*. The turning is not unanimous. The turning is **selective**. The turning **reveals the splitting line that the declaration had hidden**.

This is the genius of the ayah. The mouth is a place where everyone sounds the same. The **turning** is where the difference shows. The crisis is what splits the *farīq* off from the rest. The command that costs something is what reveals who was facing forward in the first place.

[PAUSE — major pause]

And then the closing blow:

> **وَمَآ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ**

*"And those — they are not believers."*

Notice the construction. In Arabic, this is *mā... bi*-construction — *mā ulāʾika bi'l-muʾminīn*. The *bā* attached to *al-muʾminīn* is an emphatic particle. The negation is **absolute**. Not "they are not really believers." Not "they fall short of belief." It is: **they are not — at all — among the believers**.

And the demonstrative *ulāʾika* — "those" — is a distancing word. In Arabic, *ulāʾika* points to people who are **far**. Allah does not say *hāʾulāʾi* (these, near) — He says *ulāʾika* (those, far). Even in the grammar, He is **placing them at a distance**. The mouth had brought them near with *āmannā*. The feet had walked them far. Allah simply names where they actually are.

Now let me bring these three elements together before we go to the themes.

The ongoing *yaqūlūna* showed us that the declaration is performed habitually. The unspecified object of *aṭaʿnā* showed us the gap where the nafs negotiates. And *yatawallā* showed us the body that contradicts the mouth — followed by Allah's verdict that **the body, not the mouth, is the witness**.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Self You Announce vs. The Self Your Feet Reveal

Let me start somewhere you live.

Have you ever met someone who keeps telling you who they are? "I'm a generous person." "I'm honest." "I'm loyal." "I'm a family man." And the more they say it, the more you start to suspect — usually correctly — that the person they are *describing* and the person *standing in front of you* are not the same person.

Now flip it. Have you ever met someone who **never** says any of those things — and yet, when the moment comes, they show up. They give. They keep their word. They sit with you when no one else does. They never had to *announce* who they were because their **feet were already there**.

That contrast — between the **announced self** and the **enacted self** — is one of the oldest patterns in human life. And modern psychology has a name for the gap. It calls it the **disclosed self versus the lived self**. The story you tell about yourself, versus the story your behavior tells about you.

Here is what is uncomfortable. Research on self-perception shows that **the announcing makes the gap worse, not better**. The more we describe ourselves as something, the more we feel we have *already done the work* of being that thing. The declaration discharges the obligation. We feel pious because we said pious words. We feel generous because we posted about generosity. We feel obedient because we prayed *aṭaʿnā* in our salah. The *speech act* siphons off the energy that was supposed to fund the *real act*.

This is exactly what the ayah is showing.

*Yaqūlūna āmannā wa aṭaʿnā* — they keep announcing the finished iman. *Thumma yatawallā* — and then, *because* the announcement has done the emotional work for them, the feet feel free to turn. The declaration has paid the price. The body no longer needs to.

Now let the ayah illuminate this further. Allah does not say *they lied*. He does not say *they spoke falsely*. He simply juxtaposes the speech and the turning, and lets the contradiction speak. The verdict — *wa mā ulāʾika bi'l-muʾminīn* — is not that they failed at belief. It is that **belief was never what was happening**. Whatever was happening, it was something else wearing the costume of belief.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. Because here is the door this opens for us.

What if the most dangerous spiritual state is not unbelief — but the **performed belief that has so successfully convinced its own owner** that it no longer feels the need to be enacted? What if the worst kind of darkness is the one that has installed enough lights on its surface that no one, including the person inside, notices it is still dark?

The ayah is asking us a brutal question: **when was the last time my iman cost me something?** Not when did I *feel* it. Not when did I *say* it. When did it *cost* me — when did it pull my feet in a direction my nafs did not want to go?

If you cannot remember, the ayah is not just describing the hypocrites of Madinah. It is describing the architecture of a heart in danger.

The application is small and severe. **Tomorrow, when you encounter a command of Allah that you do not want to obey — a small one is enough — do not negotiate. Do not say "later." Do not say "I believe in this in principle." Just do it.** Because every micro-instance of *aṭaʿnā* that is not enacted *teaches your feet to turn*. And every micro-instance of obedience under resistance *teaches your feet to stay*.

The body is the vote. Not the mouth.

### Theme Two: The Hidden Mercy of Being Exposed

There is something about this ayah that, on first reading, feels harsh. Allah is calling out a group of people. He is saying their declaration is empty. He is saying *they are not believers*. The verdict is final.

But sit with it longer.

If you were one of those people in Madinah — a person whose mouth was saying *āmannā* while your feet were already calculating their exit — and you heard this ayah recited, what would happen inside you?

You would be **seen**. Maybe for the first time. Maybe in a way you had been hiding from yourself.

This is the strange mercy of the Quran. Allah does not let the hypocrite stay comfortable in the costume. He keeps **lifting the costume** — not to humiliate, but to give the person a chance to **see what they have become**, before death seals it permanently. The exposure in the ayah is not vindictive. It is **a final-call diagnostic**. It is the doctor saying: *the thing you have been calling health is not health. Look. While you can still do something about it.*

Think about how rare this is in life. We almost never get **honest mirrors**. Our friends flatter us. Our family makes excuses for us. Our own minds construct elaborate stories that protect our self-image. We can go decades without a single person telling us the actual truth about who we are. And we can mistake that absence of mirrors for goodness — *because nobody ever told me anything was wrong, nothing must be wrong*.

The Quran is the mirror that does not flatter.

When Allah says *wa mā ulāʾika bi'l-muʾminīn* — "those are not believers" — He is not closing a door. He is **opening a door** by closing a self-deception. He is saying: **the costume you have been wearing is not the body it pretends to be. Take it off, before you die in it.**

That is mercy with bite.

[PAUSE]

Now bring it to yourself. There is a part of you that does not want to look. There is a part of you that prefers the announced self over the enacted self because the announced self is easier to live with. There is a voice in you that, when you read this ayah, will whisper: *yes, this is about the hypocrites of Madinah, this is about other people, I am not like that.* That voice is doing exactly what *yaqūlūna* does. It is performing iman to itself in order to avoid the audit.

The mercy of this ayah is to interrupt that voice.

The application is interior. **Choose, this week, one moment to ask yourself the question the ayah is asking: where is the gap between what I say I am and what my feet do?** Not as a flogging. Not to spiral into self-hatred. But as a doctor's diagnostic. *Where, specifically, is the announced self running ahead of the enacted self?* Because that gap is the address where Shayṭān lives. Close it, even by an inch, and you have moved.

The ayah is not trying to make you despair. It is trying to make you **honest**. And honesty — even painful honesty — is the only soil that real iman grows in.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Step back and see what Allah has built in twenty-two words.

The ayah opens with a **mouth in motion** — *yaqūlūna* — endlessly declaring. Then a **claim in three parts** — belief in Allah, belief in the Messenger, obedience — with one strategic gap left in the third part. Then the pivot word — *thumma* — "*then*" — that breaks the unity of the gathering and reveals what the declaration was hiding. Then **a body turning** — *yatawallā*. Then **a fragment splitting off** — *farīqun minhum* — separating from a whole that had appeared seamless. And then the verdict — *wa mā ulāʾika bi'l-muʾminīn* — the demonstrative *ulāʾika* placing them, in the very grammar, at the **distance their feet had already traveled**.

The ayah's structure mirrors what it describes. It begins close — *yaqūlūna* names what you can hear. It ends far — *ulāʾika* names where they actually stand. The ayah itself **performs the gap** between the mouth and the feet.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions.

The **linguistics** showed us that the ayah is built around the gap between the ongoing claim and the completed claim, between the verb with an object and the verb with a strategic gap, between the body facing forward and the body turning.

The **themes** showed us that this gap is not historical. It is the architecture of every human heart that has learned to substitute announcement for enactment — and that the mercy of the ayah is to expose the gap while there is still time to close it.

The mouth is the easiest organ in the body. The feet are the hardest. The Quran knows the difference. Allah knows the difference. The question is whether we are willing to know it.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time my iman cost me something I did not want to give? If I cannot remember, what does that tell me?

2. When I say *aṭaʿnā* — "I obey" — what is the silent object my nafs fills in? Which commands am I unconsciously excluding?

3. Is there a place in my life where the *announced self* is running so far ahead of the *enacted self* that I have started to mistake one for the other?

4. When I encounter resistance to a command of Allah, do I treat it as a signal to push through — or as a signal to negotiate?

5. If Allah were to describe my last week using only my **feet** as evidence — not my words, not my intentions, not my self-image — what verdict would the body give?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Iman is not what you say after the call to faith — it is where your body is standing when the call has cost you something.**

### The Closing Invitation

This is not an ayah that asks you to feel guilty. It is an ayah that asks you to **become honest**. Not to despair at the gap between your speech and your action — but to *see* the gap, and then to *close* it, one small obedience at a time.

The next time you say *aṭaʿnā* — in salah, in dhikr, in the silence of your own heart — let the word be a **promise to your feet**, not a substitute for them. Let the mouth speak only what the body is willing to enact.

That is the iman the ayah is pointing toward. Not louder declarations. Not more sophisticated theology. Just the slow, costly, daily aligning of the feet with the words — until there is no longer any gap for *yatawallā* to live in.

### A Brief Du'a

> Yā Allāh, do not let our mouths run ahead of our feet. Make our *āmannā* a beginning, not an ending. Make our *aṭaʿnā* an open door with no hidden exception. And when the moment of turning comes — when the command costs us — keep our faces toward You, our backs toward what You forbid, and our feet planted in the direction we said we believed in. Āmīn.
