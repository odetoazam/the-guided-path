# Surah An-Nur, Ayah 22 — Don't You Love That Allah Should Forgive You

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَلَا يَأْتَلِ أُولُو الْفَضْلِ مِنكُمْ وَالسَّعَةِ أَن يُؤْتُوا أُولِي الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينَ وَالْمُهَاجِرِينَ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۖ وَلْيَعْفُوا وَلْيَصْفَحُوا ۗ أَلَا تُحِبُّونَ أَن يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ ۗ وَاللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa lā ya'tali ulul-faḍli minkum was-saʿati an yu'tū ulil-qurbā wal-masākīna wal-muhājirīna fī sabīlillāh; walyaʿfū walyaṣfaḥū; alā tuḥibbūna an yaghfira-llāhu lakum; wallāhu Ghafūrur-Raḥīm.*

**Translation:**
"And let not those of virtue and spaciousness among you swear off giving to relatives, the needy, and the emigrants in the path of Allah. Let them pardon and let them turn the page. **Do you not love that Allah should forgive you?** And Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."

## Introduction — The Wound Beneath the Ayah

Before we open this ayah, you have to feel where it landed. Not the verse — the silence right before it.

Madinah has just been through something it did not survive whole.

A rumour had moved through the city for nearly a month. The mother of the believers, Aisha (R), had been accused of something unthinkable. The Prophet ﷺ had not eaten properly. She herself had been bedridden, not knowing why everyone was suddenly cold to her. Her own father, Abu Bakr (R) — the steadiest man in the city, the one who left his entire fortune behind in Makkah for the sake of this faith — was sleeping with the weight of it on his chest.

Then the revelation came. She was exonerated. The slander was lies. The community exhaled.

But here is the part the Western imagination of this story usually misses: when the dust settled and the slanderers were named, one of them was a young man called **Mistah ibn Uthatha**.

Mistah was a Muslim. Mistah was poor. Mistah was Abu Bakr's **cousin**. And for years — quietly, without announcement — Abu Bakr had been the one paying his bills. Food. Clothes. The small dignity of being supported.

Now imagine Abu Bakr opening his eyes after that month. Imagine a man so generous that he had been sustaining the very mouth that helped slander his daughter.

He did what any of us would do. He said: *I swear by Allah, I will never spend another dirham on Mistah, ever again.*

This is not pettiness. This is the most natural human movement on earth — the soul drawing back from the thing that bit it. Anyone who has been betrayed by a relative, a friend, a person they fed and clothed and trusted — knows that silent oath. You don't even need to say it out loud. The heart simply closes a door.

[PAUSE]

Then this ayah came down.

It came down not as a general ethical principle. It came down with Abu Bakr's name written invisibly across it. The most beloved companion of the Prophet ﷺ. A man whom no one would have blamed for his oath. A man whose grievance was real, documented, and exonerated by Allah Himself.

And Allah said to him: *break the oath. Reopen the door.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook I want you to carry through this whole reflection. There are many ayahs in the Quran that command forgiveness. There are many that promise reward for it. **But this one does something different.** It does not command. It does not threaten. It asks a single, devastatingly soft question.

Five words in Arabic. *Don't you love that Allah should forgive you?*

We're going to spend the next hour understanding why Allah chose, in this moment of all moments, to put down the language of command and pick up the language of a question.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We're going to walk through three doors into this ayah. The first is the very first verb. The second is a pair of words you might think are synonyms but aren't. The third is that question itself — *alā tuḥibbūn*.

### Element One: *Lā Yaʾtali* — The Verb That Means Two Things at Once

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we even open the word.

In Arabic, when you take a basic three-letter root and put it into what's called **Form VIII** — the *iftaʿala* pattern — something happens. The verb becomes more *internal*. It becomes about something the soul **takes upon itself**. *Kasaba* means "to gain"; *iktasaba* (Form VIII) means "to earn through one's own effort, to internalize the gain." The form bends the action inward, towards the self.

Now the verb here is **يَأْتَلِ — *yaʾtali*** — Form VIII of the root أ-ل-و.

Open this slowly with me, because the classical commentators stopped here for a reason.

The root أ-ل-و in Arabic carries **two meanings simultaneously**, and both are documented in the lexicons. One meaning is **to swear an oath** — *ālā* — a binding spoken commitment. The other meaning is **to fall short, to be remiss, to hold back**. The same root.

So when Allah says *wa lā yaʾtali ulul-faḍl* — He has not picked an unambiguous word. He has picked the one word in Arabic that simultaneously means: *let them not swear an oath to withhold*, and: *let them not fall short, let them not be remiss.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Because this is not a coincidence of vocabulary. This is the Quran doing something with language that translation cannot carry.

Allah is saying to Abu Bakr — and through him to every one of us — two things in a single breath:

The oath you swore? It is itself a kind of falling short.

You think you are taking a stand. You think you are protecting yourself, drawing a moral line. But in the inner architecture of your soul, **the oath of withdrawal is identical to a moral failure.** They are the same gesture. The Form VIII makes the verb internal — this isn't about your tongue, this is about something your *self* has taken upon itself.

The classical mufassirūn — al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī — note this dual sense and let it stand. Both readings illuminate the same reality. Whether you read it as *don't swear off giving* or *don't fall short of giving*, Allah has chosen the one verb where the two are inseparable.

[PAUSE]

Here's what this single word reveals: **the moment your heart resolves to permanently withhold goodness from someone who wronged you, you have not completed your justice. You have begun your own remissness.**

Notice — Allah did not say *aqsama* (the ordinary word for swearing an oath). He could have. He chose *yaʾtali* instead. So the very first verb of this ayah is already telling Abu Bakr: *what you think is righteous resolve is also, secretly, a failing.*

That is one word. We are still on the first word.

---

### Element Two: *Al-Faḍl wa al-Saʿah* — Virtue and Spaciousness

Now look who Allah is addressing.

He does not address "those who give." He does not address "the wealthy." He addresses **أُولُو الْفَضْلِ مِنْكُمْ وَالسَّعَةِ — those of *faḍl* and *saʿah* among you.**

Let me teach you these two words, because the choice is surgical.

**Faḍl** (ف-ض-ل) does not mean "virtue" the way English uses the word. It means **excess, overflow, what spills over the rim.** The original physical sense is the surplus — what is left over after a need is met. So *al-faḍl* in a person is the **moral overflow** — the goodness that exceeds what was strictly required.

**Saʿah** (و-س-ع) does not mean "wealth." It means **spaciousness, breadth, room.** From the same root as *wāsiʿ* — Allah's name "the Spacious." It's the quality of having *room* — physical room, financial room, but most importantly, **inner room.** Spaciousness of soul.

[PAUSE]

Notice something extraordinary. Allah did not say *ulul-māl* (those of wealth) or *ulul-ghinā* (those of richness). He could have. Both words exist and both would have made grammatical sense.

He chose *faḍl* and *saʿah*. **Overflow and spaciousness.**

Sit with what this is doing.

If Allah had said "those of wealth," then the average person would think: *this verse isn't for me. I have nothing to give. I am not the wealthy class. I get to keep my oaths of withdrawal because I'm not Abu Bakr.*

But *faḍl* and *saʿah* are not categories of bank balance. They are categories of **the soul.** A person on minimum wage can have *saʿah* — spaciousness — that a billionaire lacks. A widow with one extra date can have *faḍl* — overflow — that an aristocrat does not. The qualification Allah uses targets your **interior weather**, not your tax bracket.

[PAUSE]

And here's what's remarkable. The very fact that Allah is addressing you with these words — *if you are still listening at this point in the ayah* — is Allah's quiet implication that **you have it.** You have *faḍl*. You have *saʿah*. The address itself is the diagnosis. Allah is not pleading with you to acquire spaciousness. He is reminding you that you already possess it — and asking why, then, you have suddenly become small.

This is what those of *faḍl* do when they are wronged: they shrink. They contract. They begin to behave as if they were *not* people of overflow. The oath of withdrawal is the soul of a spacious person trying to convince itself it is small.

And Allah, in this single epithet, refuses to let you tell yourself that lie.

[PAUSE]

Here's what this construction reveals: **generosity is not the function of how much you have. It is the function of how spacious your soul is. And if you have ever, in your life, given freely without calculation, you have already proven that you are *ulul-faḍli was-saʿah* — and Allah will hold you to what you already are.**

---

### Element Three: *Walyaʿfū walyaṣfaḥū* — Two Forgivenesses, Not One

Now we approach the third door, and this is where the language becomes most precise.

Allah commands two things, not one: **وَلْيَعْفُوا وَلْيَصْفَحُوا — *let them pardon, and let them turn the page.***

In English, both look like "forgive." In Arabic they are not the same word, and the classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib in *al-Mufradāt*, Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab* — distinguish them precisely.

**ʿAfw** (ع-ف-و) — the original sense in Arabic is **to erase, to efface.** The wind that blows over footprints in the sand and removes them is said to *ʿafā* the tracks. So *ʿafw* is the legal-moral act of **erasing the offense** — relinquishing your right to retaliate, removing the consequence.

**Ṣafḥ** (ص-ف-ح) — the original sense is **the broad face of a thing**, the page, the side. *Ṣafḥa* in modern Arabic means "page." To *ṣafaḥa* someone is to **turn the page**, to turn your face — *ṣafḥa* — toward something else, away from the offense.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the difference?

*ʿAfw* is what you do with **the offense itself**. You release the debt. You no longer hold the person legally or morally accountable. You erase the entry from the ledger.

*Ṣafḥ* is what you do with **your own heart afterwards**. You turn your face. You stop reading and re-reading the page. You don't keep returning to the wound to inspect how much it still hurts.

This is why the scholars say: a person can give *ʿafw* without giving *ṣafḥ*. You can technically forgive someone — you don't sue them, you don't curse them, you've "let it go" by every external measure — and yet every time you see them, the page is still open in your heart. You replay the conversation. You mention it to your spouse for the seventh time this year. You released the debt but you did not turn the page.

[PAUSE]

And Allah does not let you stop at *ʿafw*.

Notice the word order. He does not say *waṣfaḥū wa-aʿfū*. He says *waʿfū* first, *waṣfaḥū* second. **Erasing the offense comes first; turning the page comes after.** Because *ṣafḥ* without *ʿafw* is just suppression — you've "moved on" while the debt still sits in your accounting. But *ʿafw* without *ṣafḥ* is also incomplete — you released them, but you did not release **yourself**.

Allah wants both. And the order is: settle it externally, then settle it internally.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what this is asking of Abu Bakr.

The first command — *waʿfū* — is: take back the oath. Reopen the door. Let Mistah back into the support.

The second command — *waṣfaḥū* — is harder. It says: when Mistah walks through that door tomorrow, **do not look at him with the page still open.** Don't hand him the money with a cold face. Don't mention the slander. Don't even *think* about it when you see him. Turn the page so completely that the next chapter genuinely begins.

This is why every scholar of *tazkiyah* — the science of purifying the soul — has said that *ṣafḥ* is harder than *ʿafw*. Because *ʿafw* is an act of will. *Ṣafḥ* is a state of the heart.

[PAUSE]

Bridge: We have walked through three doors. The first verb told us that the oath of withdrawal is itself a falling-short. The address told us that we are spacious people pretending to be small. The double command told us that real forgiveness has an external act and an internal weather, and Allah wants both.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the divine economy itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Divine Question — *Alā Tuḥibbūn?*

Let me start somewhere that has nothing to do with the Quran, and you'll see why.

Imagine you have been wronged by someone — a sibling, a friend, a colleague who took credit for your work, a family member who said something unforgivable at a wedding. Imagine you have been carrying this for months. Now imagine three different people, all trying to convince you to forgive them.

The first person is your manager at work. They say: *Listen, you need to forgive them. It's the professional thing to do. The team needs harmony.* You hear it as an obligation. You comply, externally. Internally, nothing has shifted.

The second person is your imām giving a Friday khuṭbah. He says: *Allah has commanded forgiveness. The reward is great. Failure to forgive carries consequences.* You hear it as religion. You try harder. But the trying is mechanical, because the heart was not addressed.

The third person sits down beside you. Doesn't lecture. Doesn't command. Just looks at you and asks one question: *Don't you love it when someone forgives you?*

[PAUSE]

What just happened?

The third person did not give you a new piece of information. They did not threaten you. They did not even ask you to do anything. **They simply moved you from the position of judge to the position of defendant.** Suddenly you are not the one being asked to extend something. You are the one who has, a hundred times in your life, *needed* something extended.

This is what Allah does in five words.

He has just commanded — *waʿfū walyaṣfaḥū*. He could have stopped there. He could have added a threat (*and whoever fails to forgive will be punished*) or an inducement (*and whoever forgives will be rewarded*). Instead, He puts down the language of command and asks:

**أَلَا تُحِبُّونَ أَن يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَكُمْ — *Don't you love that Allah should forgive you?***

[PAUSE]

The Arabic interrogative *alā* is not a neutral question word. It is what the grammarians call an **istifhām taqrīrī** — an interrogative that is not seeking information but **affirming a known reality through the form of a question.** It's the rhetorical move of asking what you both already know the answer to, so that the listener has to **say it themselves.**

Allah is not wondering whether you love His forgiveness. Allah is putting you in the position where *you* have to face the fact that you do.

And the moment you face it, the architecture of the ayah locks into place. **Because if you love that Allah forgives *you*, then on what basis are you withholding forgiveness from another servant of Allah?** The ayah is structured so that you cannot answer "yes, I love Allah's forgiveness" and remain in your oath of withdrawal. The two positions become logically incompatible.

[PAUSE]

This is the divine economy. Not "forgive others **and** Allah will forgive you" — that's transactional, and the heart can do that mechanically. Rather: **the same hand that wants Allah's forgiveness must be the hand that gives forgiveness.** Because they are the same gesture, performed at different scales.

The hand that closes around its grievance against Mistah — *that* hand cannot then open to receive the mercy of Allah. Not because Allah is arbitrary. But because **a closed hand is closed.** It has only one shape. You cannot configure your soul to be ungenerous-toward-others and generous-toward-itself. The soul has one posture.

[PAUSE]

Abu Bakr (R), upon hearing this verse, immediately said: *Balā wa-Llāhi, innī uḥibbu an yaghfira-llāhu lī.* — **"Yes, by Allah, I do love that Allah should forgive me."** And he resumed supporting Mistah, and reportedly added more than what he had given before.

What did the ayah do to him? It moved him from the seat of the judge to the seat of the defendant. And from the seat of the defendant, the only sane verdict on Mistah was the verdict he himself was begging Allah to deliver on him.

**The practical translation:** The next time you find your heart closing around a grievance, do not argue with yourself about whether the person deserves your forgiveness. Argue, instead, about whether *you* deserve Allah's. If your answer is *I am still in need of His forgiveness* — and it is — then your hand is already shaped. Use it to forgive, in the same shape you want it to receive.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Withholding That Looks Like Justice

Here's the other thing this ayah quietly does, and it might be the most important thing of all.

Most people who withhold goodness do not think they are being unjust. They think they are being **just**.

Abu Bakr did not swear his oath out of cruelty. He swore it out of a perfectly defensible moral logic: *I supported him. He used what I gave him to harm my daughter. The relationship between us has earned its end.* This is not vindictiveness. This is **proportionate justice.** The relationship was a gift, and the gift was abused. Withdrawing the gift is not punishment — it is restoring the natural order.

Most of us live somewhere on this logic.

The relative who gossiped — you stop calling them. The friend who never showed up — you stop showing up for them. The cousin who took your help and turned around to undermine you — you stop helping. None of this *feels* like cruelty. It feels like **finally seeing clearly.** It feels like maturity. It feels like respecting yourself.

[PAUSE]

And the Quran, in a single ayah, refuses to let this logic stand uninspected.

Notice what the verse does not do. **It does not validate the grievance.** It does not say "Mistah was wrong, but..." It does not minimize what happened. It does not pretend Abu Bakr was being unreasonable. The injury is real. The verse does not deny it for a single syllable.

What the verse does is something subtler. It says: **the injury is real, *and* the injury does not earn you the right to permanently close the door.**

Why? Because of *who you are*. *Ulul-faḍli minkum was-saʿah.* You are a person of overflow and spaciousness. The injury was real, but it was not large enough to convert you into a different kind of soul. You do not get to be *ulul-faḍl* on the days that please you and *not ulul-faḍl* on the days you have been hurt. The category does not switch on and off.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this, because it cuts deep.

The withholding that looks like justice is, very often, **the soul's attempt to escape its own identity.** When someone hurts you, the easiest thing to do is to become temporarily smaller — to say *for this person, I am not generous. For this person, I am not patient. For this person, my faḍl does not apply.* You carve out an exception clause in your own soul. *Everyone else gets the spacious version of me. This person gets the small version.*

And the ayah says: no. **There is no small version of you that Allah will accept.** You are *ulul-faḍl* or you are not. And if you are — and the address itself testifies that you are — then the oath of exception is forbidden.

[PAUSE]

This is, by the way, why Islam asks something *more* than what most ethical systems ask. Most ethical systems ask you to refrain from harming the one who harmed you. The Quran asks you to **continue actively giving** to them. *An yu'tū* — *that they should give*. Not "stop hating." Not "don't curse them." **Give. Resume the support. Reopen the wallet.**

That's a different order of magnitude. That's not *non*-retaliation. That's **affirmative continuation of goodness through the wound.** Because anything less — anything that stops at "I don't hate them anymore" — leaves the door of grievance ajar. As long as the goodness is suspended, the wound is still in charge.

[PAUSE]

**The practical translation:** examine, this week, every relationship in your life where you have quietly stopped doing something good you used to do — for someone who hurt you. The phone call you no longer make. The duʿāʾ you no longer include them in. The invitation you no longer extend. Ask yourself, in front of Allah: am I the kind of person who has *faḍl* and *saʿah*? If yes — then on what authority did I create this exception? And do I love that Allah should forgive me?

If the answer is yes — and it is — then there is one phone call to make this week. One invitation to extend. One piece of goodness to resume. **Not because they deserve it.** Because *you* do — and the only soul that earns Allah's overflow is the soul that practices it.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now look at how the whole verse fits together. We did not see it at the start because we had not earned the seeing.

The ayah moves in three movements:

**First movement** — the verb of withholding (*lā yaʾtali*). The oath of withdrawal is named, and named with a verb that secretly accuses it of being a falling-short.

**Second movement** — the address and the command (*ulul-faḍli minkum was-saʿah... walyaʿfū walyaṣfaḥū*). You are reminded who you are, and given two precise commands: erase the offense, then turn the page.

**Third movement** — the divine question (*alā tuḥibbūn an yaghfira-llāhu lakum*). The whole structure is suddenly inverted. You thought you were the judge. Allah reminds you that you are the defendant.

And the ayah closes — *wa-Llāhu Ghafūrur-Raḥīm* — Allah is *al-Ghafūr* (the Forgiving) and *al-Raḥīm* (the Merciful). The closing names are not decoration. They are the **answer** to the question. *Don't you love that Allah should forgive you?* — yes, of course — *and Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.* The mercy you crave **is the very atmosphere you are being asked to create around someone else.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The verb *yaʾtali* told us that withholding is itself a moral failure. The theme of "the withholding that looks like justice" told us that this is the most disguised failure of all — because it wears the clothing of righteousness.

The pair *ʿafw / ṣafḥ* told us that there are two forgivenesses, external and internal. The theme of the divine question told us that both of them flow from the same source — your own ache for Allah's forgiveness, which only a hand of the right shape can receive.

The address *ulul-faḍli was-saʿah* told us that this address is itself the diagnosis — Allah only addresses you this way because you already are this. The theme of the spacious self refused to let us shrink for an exception.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life have you quietly sworn an oath of withdrawal — never spoken aloud, never named — against someone who hurt you?

2. If Allah were to address you tomorrow as *ulul-faḍli was-saʿah*, would you recognize yourself in the address? On what days do you live up to it? On what days do you secretly opt out?

3. Of the people you have technically forgiven — given *ʿafw* to — how many have you also given *ṣafḥ* to? Whose page is still open in your heart?

4. *Alā tuḥibbūna an yaghfira-llāhu lakum?* — When you sit with this question in front of Allah, what name comes up first? Whose forgiveness is the cost of your answering yes?

5. What is one specific act of goodness — phone call, duʿāʾ, financial support, simple kindness — that you stopped doing for someone who hurt you, and that this ayah is asking you to resume this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The hand that wants to receive Allah's forgiveness must be the same hand that gives it — because the soul has only one shape, and you cannot be small toward another and spacious toward yourself.**

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this is not a feeling. It is a single concrete reopening. One door. One piece of withheld goodness, resumed.

You do not have to feel ready. Abu Bakr (R) was not feeling ready. He was feeling the perfectly justified ache of a father whose daughter had been slandered by a man he had been feeding. The ayah did not wait for the ache to subside. It simply asked him a question, and the question rearranged his soul.

Let it rearrange yours.

### A Closing Duʿāʾ

*Yā Ghafūr, yā Raḥīm — we love that You should forgive us. Make our hands the shape of Yours. Loosen every silent oath we have sworn in the dark to withhold goodness from those who wronged us. Give us not only ʿafw but ṣafḥ — let the page truly turn. And when we stand before You ourselves, looking up with the same hope Mistah looked up with, find us already a people of faḍl and saʿah, because we became, by Your help, people who could not bear to be small.*

*Āmīn.*
