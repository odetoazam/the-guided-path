# The Burden That Stays With You

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

> [ayah:24:54] قُلْ أَطِيعُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا۟ ٱلرَّسُولَ ۖ فَإِن تَوَلَّوْا۟ فَإِنَّمَا عَلَيْهِ مَا حُمِّلَ وَعَلَيْكُم مَّا حُمِّلْتُمْ ۖ وَإِن تُطِيعُوهُ تَهْتَدُوا۟ ۚ وَمَا عَلَى ٱلرَّسُولِ إِلَّا ٱلْبَلَـٰغُ ٱلْمُبِينُ

*Qul aṭīʿū-llāha wa aṭīʿū-r-rasūl. Fa in tawallaw fa innamā ʿalayhi mā ḥummila wa ʿalaykum mā ḥummiltum. Wa in tuṭīʿūhu tahtadū. Wa mā ʿala-r-rasūli illā-l-balāghu-l-mubīn.*

**"Say: Obey Allah and obey the Messenger. But if you turn away, then upon him is only what he has been burdened with, and upon you is only what you have been burdened with. And if you obey him, you will be guided. And nothing is upon the Messenger except the clear conveyance."**

[PAUSE]

### The World This Ayah Walks Into

Picture, for a moment, the inside of Madinah in the year this surah is being revealed. This is not a city that has just embraced Islam in calm. This is a community recovering from the slander against ʿĀʾisha رضي الله عنها — the spiritual earthquake whose tremors run through every line of Surah An-Nur. The Prophet ﷺ has been wounded in a way no battle wounded him. Households have been torn open. Trust has been shaken at the most intimate level.

And so this surah does something unusual. It does not begin with cosmology or with the afterlife. It begins with rules — rules about marriage, rules about modesty, rules about how to enter a home, rules about who may look at whom, rules about what to do when you hear a rumor. This is the surah where Allah legislates the inside of the human household.

And then, just before the famous Light Verse and the famous Verse of Succession, comes this ayah. After all that legislation. After all those duties placed on the believer's shoulders. After the surah has asked so much of you.

Now imagine you are the Prophet ﷺ inside that moment. You have been the one delivering every one of those commands. You have watched some embrace them and some turn their faces away. You have been accused, doubted, mocked. And the temptation that visits every sincere caller — every parent, every teacher, every imam — is the temptation to carry their refusal as if it were your failure.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is what Allah says to His Messenger ﷺ — and through him to every soul who has ever loved someone and watched them walk away from what is good for them.

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah that appears twice in two different forms. Same root. Same meaning. But Allah uses it once for the Messenger ﷺ and once for those who turn away — and the word is not "duty." It is not "responsibility." It is something heavier than both.

It is the word for **a burden that has been placed on your back**. A load. Something hoisted onto you that you did not lift yourself.

Why would Allah call obedience a burden? And why would He, in the very same breath, give you a way to walk under that burden so light that it becomes guidance itself?

[PAUSE]

That is the door we are walking through tonight.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Ḥummila* — The Burden That Was Placed on You

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word.

In Arabic, every verb has a basic form — what grammarians call Form I — and then a series of derived forms, numbered II through X, each of which twists the meaning in a specific direction. Form II, formed by doubling the middle letter, almost always carries one of two meanings: it makes the action **intensive** (do something a lot, or do it thoroughly), or it makes it **causative** (cause someone else to do it).

Take a verb like *ʿallama*. The basic verb *ʿalima* means "he knew." Add the doubled middle letter — *ʿallama* — and now it means "he caused another to know." He taught.

Hold onto that idea: the doubled middle letter often means **someone did this to someone else**.

Now let's open the word.

The root is *ḥ-m-l* — to carry, to bear, to lift. The basic Form I is *ḥamala* — "he carried." A simple act. A man lifts a sack and walks.

But Allah does not say *ḥamala*. He doubles the middle letter. He says **ḥummila** — and on top of that, He puts it in the **passive voice**. Read it slowly: *mā ḥummila* — "what he was burdened with." And again: *mā ḥummiltum* — "what you all were burdened with."

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened grammatically. Allah has not said: "What he carries." Allah has not said: "What he chose to lift." Allah has said: **"What was placed upon him."** A weight that someone else loaded onto his shoulders. And the One doing the loading is unnamed — but in a passive construction in the Quran, when no agent is named, the assumed agent is always the One above all agents. **Allah Himself loaded this weight.**

Stay with this. The Prophet ﷺ did not pick up the mission of prophethood the way a volunteer picks up a task. The mission was placed on his back. *Ḥummila*. It is the same shape of verb you would use for a porter at a marketplace — someone walks up behind him and **places a sack on his shoulders**, and now he is carrying it not because he chose the sack but because the sack was given to him.

Here is what is remarkable: Allah uses the **exact same verb form, in the exact same passive voice**, to describe what is upon you and me.

*Wa ʿalaykum mā ḥummiltum.* "And upon you all is what you all were burdened with."

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah is telling us — every single one of us — that the obligations of our lives, the responsibilities we did not ask for, the duties of being a parent, a child, a Muslim, a human being accountable to his Creator — **none of these were chosen by us**. They were *ḥummiltum*. They were placed upon you. You did not lift this religion onto your own shoulders. It was given to you.

And that fact alone reframes everything.

Because when you think your obedience is something **you initiated**, you start to feel proud of it, or resentful of it, or proprietary about it. You start to feel that you are doing Allah a favor. You start to feel that the difficulty of obedience is a hardship being demanded of you by an external authority.

But when you understand that the obligation itself was *ḥummila* — was placed upon you, the way a mother places a child in your arms — then your relationship to it changes entirely. **You are not the source of this duty. You are the one entrusted with it.**

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing this word does. The doubled middle letter — that intensification — carries the sense of **weight**. It is not just carrying. It is carrying *something heavy enough to require effort*. The Quran is not telling the Prophet ﷺ "you have a job description." It is telling him: **you have been loaded.** And by the same word, it is telling us: **you have been loaded too.**

Each according to his capacity. Each according to his station. But each one of us is walking under a weight that was placed there from above.

What this single verb invites us to consider is staggering: the very things we resist as "obligations" are, in the Arabic of revelation, **trusts placed in our arms**. The weight is real. But the weight is also a sign that we have been chosen to carry something.

[PAUSE — sit with this]

### Element Two: The Asymmetry of *ʿAlayhi* and *ʿAlaykum* — Two Burdens That Will Never Touch

Now I want you to look at the structure of the middle of this ayah. Read it again with me, paying attention to the symmetry:

*Fa innamā ʿalayhi mā ḥummila — wa ʿalaykum mā ḥummiltum.*

"Then upon him is only what he was burdened with — and upon you is what you were burdened with."

Let me teach you a small Arabic word that is doing enormous work here. The word is *innamā*. It is a particle that grammarians call *adāt al-ḥaṣr* — the **particle of restriction**. When Allah says *innamā ʿalayhi*, He is not saying "upon him is what he was burdened with." He is saying: "**Upon him is only that, and nothing more.**" *Innamā* draws a line and says: this side, and only this side.

Now look at what the line separates.

On one side: the burden of the Messenger ﷺ — *mā ḥummila* — what he was burdened with.

On the other side: your burden — *mā ḥummiltum* — what you were burdened with.

And here is what the *innamā* is telling you with surgical precision: **these two burdens will never touch.** They are walled off from each other. The Messenger's burden does not extend onto your shoulders, and your burden — the burden of your own response, your own turning away, your own consequences — **does not slide back onto his.**

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter? Because every prophet, every reformer, every parent, every teacher in human history has been tempted by one specific delusion: the delusion that **if those you call do not respond, you have failed**. That their refusal is your defect. That their turning away is a verdict on your effort.

And what Allah is saying to His Messenger ﷺ — and through him to every caller who has ever loved someone enough to invite them — is: *innamā*. **Only.** What is on him is only what was placed on him. What is on you is only what was placed on you. **Their refusal is not on his ledger. Their refusal is on theirs.**

Look at the analogy from the world of medicine. A doctor with a patient who refuses treatment. The doctor has been entrusted — *ḥummila* — with the burden of diagnosing accurately, prescribing correctly, explaining clearly, and offering the medicine. The patient has been entrusted — *ḥummiltum* — with the burden of taking it. If the patient refuses, and the disease progresses, **the disease is not the doctor's failure.** Provided the doctor has fulfilled what was placed upon him, *innamā ʿalayhi mā ḥummila*. The doctor is finished. The patient now walks under his own load.

The horror of religious life — and of every kind of love that wants to save another — is that we keep trying to **carry burdens that were never placed on our shoulders**. We collapse under the weight of other people's choices. We confuse our obligation to *deliver* with an impossible obligation to *make them receive*.

[PAUSE]

And Allah, with one tiny particle — *innamā* — slices that confusion in half.

Notice also the order. Allah mentions the Messenger's burden first: *ʿalayhi mā ḥummila*. Then yours: *ʿalaykum mā ḥummiltum*. The Prophet ﷺ is being told about himself first — that **he is free**. Then the audience is told: but *you* are not free of *yours*. You cannot turn away and pretend the matter has nothing to do with you. The Messenger has done his part. **Now the weight is on your back.**

This is the most honest grammar of accountability I have ever seen. Two burdens. One particle that separates them. No overlap. No transfer. No escape.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Wa Mā ʿAla-r-Rasūli Illā-l-Balāghu-l-Mubīn* — The Closing Wall

Now we come to the final phrase of the ayah, and it is doing something extraordinary. Allah has already told us, in the middle of the verse, what is on the Messenger ﷺ. But He returns to it at the end — and this time He uses an even stronger structure.

Read it: *Wa mā ʿala-r-rasūli illā-l-balāghu-l-mubīn.* "And there is nothing upon the Messenger except the clear conveyance."

This is what grammarians call a *qaṣr* — a **construction of restriction** built out of two pieces: *mā* (a negation, "not") and *illā* (an exception, "except"). When Arabic puts these two together — *mā... illā* — it builds a wall and then carves out one single doorway in it. **Nothing is on him. Except this one thing.**

We just had *innamā*. Now we have *mā... illā*. Allah has used **two different restriction structures in a single ayah** to fence in the same idea: the Messenger's burden has a defined, finite shape, and outside that shape, **he owes nothing**.

[PAUSE]

And what is inside that single doorway? Two words: *al-balāghu al-mubīn*.

Let's open them.

*Balāgh* comes from the root *b-l-gh* — to reach, to arrive, to attain. The noun *balāgh* means "the act of conveying so that the message reaches its destination." It is not just speaking. It is not just announcing. It is **delivery to the point of arrival**. The same root gives us *bāligh*, "one who has reached maturity," and *bulūgh*, "the moment of arrival." When the Quran calls the Prophet's mission *balāgh*, it is saying: he must speak in such a way that **the words arrive in the listener's ear and arrive in the listener's understanding**.

Then: *mubīn*. From the root *b-y-n* — to be clear, to be distinct, to make a separation between things. *Mubīn* is the active participle of Form IV — **that which makes things clear**. Not just clear in itself, but clarifying for others. The Quran uses *mubīn* throughout to describe Allah's signs, His Book, His messengers — anything that **makes the line between truth and falsehood visible**.

So *al-balāghu al-mubīn* is: **the conveyance that arrives, and that, when it arrives, makes things distinct.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with what this means for the burden.

The Messenger's *ḥumūla* — what he was loaded with — is *al-balāgh al-mubīn*. He must speak. He must speak in a way that reaches. He must speak in a way that, when it reaches, **leaves no ambiguity** about what was being asked.

That is his burden. **And that is the entire scope of his burden.**

Once the words have reached, and once the words have been clear, *mā ʿala-r-rasūli illā* — **there is nothing else upon him**. Not your response. Not your refusal. Not your timing. Not your willingness. Not your slow heart or fast tongue. Not even, in the end, your guidance — because guidance, as the next part of the ayah quietly told us, is in another's hands entirely.

Here is what is breathtaking. Allah has constructed this verse as a kind of **legal instrument** — and the instrument has a single purpose: to free His Messenger ﷺ from a weight no human being could survive. The weight of being responsible for other people's salvation. The weight of feeling that every soul that turns away is a soul you have lost.

Allah says: *mā ʿalayka illā-l-balāgh*. **You owe them clarity. You do not owe them response.**

[PAUSE]

And by extension — by the same grammar that yoked your burden to his with the same word *ḥummila* — the same liberation comes to **you**. You owe your child clarity. You do not owe them response. You owe your spouse the truth. You do not owe them their reception of it. You owe your community the light you have. You do not owe them the obligation to walk by it.

The Messenger's restriction — *mā... illā* — is a model for every burdened heart that has ever loved someone too much to let them walk away in peace.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So what have these three words shown us?

*Ḥummila* — the burden was placed on you, you did not lift it yourself; it is therefore not your possession but your trust.

*Innamā* — your burden is bounded; it does not extend onto another person's responsibility, and theirs does not slide onto yours.

*Mā... illā-l-balāghu-l-mubīn* — the burden, once delivered clearly, is fulfilled; what comes after delivery is not on the deliverer's ledger.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we are to live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Difference Between Carrying a Message and Carrying a Person

Let me start somewhere very ordinary.

You have probably had this experience. You see someone you love walking toward something that will hurt them. A relationship. A decision. A drift away from something good. And so you speak. You speak gently the first time. They wave you off. You speak more carefully the second time. They get defensive. You speak with tears in your eyes the third time. They withdraw.

And now something happens to you. You start to lose sleep. You start to rehearse new arguments in your head at three in the morning. You start to feel the weight of their approaching disaster pressing on your chest. You start to feel, in some deep part of you, that **if you do not save them, no one will** — and that means it must be you, somehow, who finds the right words, the right moment, the right argument.

This is one of the most common and most exhausting forms of human suffering. And it lives inside every parent of a wandering child. Every spouse of a struggling partner. Every imam in a community where some refuse to hear. Every person who has carried a friend's potential addiction or self-destruction in their stomach for months.

[PAUSE]

What is happening in this state? You have **slid the other person's burden onto your own back**. You have started carrying not the message — which is *balāgh* — but the **outcome of the message**, which was never yours to carry.

And Allah, in this ayah, walks up to that exhausted heart and lifts something off of it.

He says: *innamā ʿalayka mā ḥummilta*. **Only what was placed on you is on you.**

What was placed on you was the obligation to *speak truthfully*, *clearly*, *with mercy*, *with persistence*. That is *al-balāgh al-mubīn*. To deliver. To make distinct. To bring the truth all the way to their ear.

What was **never** placed on you was the obligation to **make them accept it**. Their acceptance was *ḥummila* upon **them**. Allah loaded their soul with a freedom He intends to honor — even if it kills you to watch them use it badly.

[PAUSE]

This is so easy to say and so hard to live. Let me bring it down further.

The Prophet ﷺ — and we know this from the larger Quran — was **so grieved** by the rejection of his people that Allah had to address it directly more than once. In Surah Al-Kahf: *fa-laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka ʿalā āthārihim* — "Perhaps you will destroy yourself in grief over them" (18:6). In Surah Ash-Shuʿarāʾ: *laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka allā yakūnū muʾminīn* — "Perhaps you will destroy yourself that they do not become believers" (26:3).

Think about this. The greatest human being who ever walked the earth — the one whose mercy was so vast that Allah called him *raḥmatan li-l-ʿālamīn* — had to be *restrained* by his Lord from **bearing the weight of other people's refusal until it broke him.**

If the Prophet ﷺ needed this restraint, what makes you think you do not?

[PAUSE]

The first practical movement is this: when you have spoken truth to someone you love, and they have turned, **you must consciously place their response back where it belongs**. You must say to your own heart: *mā ḥummiltu illā al-balāgh.* I was loaded only with delivery. Their walking is not mine.

This is not coldness. It is not abandonment. You can still pray for them. You can still keep the door open. You can still grieve. **But you cannot carry their freedom for them.** Allah did not give it to you to carry.

There is a second movement, and it is harder. When the same person turns away again, and the temptation rises in you to argue, to coerce, to manipulate, to use guilt — that temptation is the temptation to **forcibly transfer their burden back onto your control**. To take their *ḥumūla* away from them. To strip them of their freedom because their freedom is hurting you.

And this — this is the violation of the ayah's architecture. Because if Allah Himself has placed the burden on their shoulders, **who are you to lift it off and replace it with your coercion?**

The mercy in this ayah is that it gives you back your sleep. The discipline in this ayah is that it forbids you from stealing another person's moral life.

[PAUSE]

The practical application is sharp: the next time someone you love refuses what you know is good for them, complete the *balāgh* — speak clearly, kindly, completely — and then, in your heart, perform a small ritual. Open your hands and say: *Yā Rabb, I have delivered. The rest of this is between You and them. Do not let me carry what You did not give me.*

Then close your hands. And let the person be free — even from your worry, which is its own kind of cage.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Obedience as the Lightening of the Load

Here is a paradox that the ayah is doing very quietly but very deliberately.

The whole ayah is built around the word *ḥummila* — the burden, the load, the weight. *Mā ḥummila. Mā ḥummiltum.* The Messenger carries a load. You carry a load. Even the act of refusal — *fa in tawallaw* — does not free you of your load. Turning away does not lift the burden; it just leaves you holding it without a path.

But then, in the middle of all this language of weight, comes one short clause that changes the temperature of the entire verse:

*Wa in tuṭīʿūhu tahtadū.* "**And if you obey him, you will be guided.**"

[PAUSE]

Notice the architecture. Allah does not say: "if you obey, you will be rewarded." He does not say: "if you obey, you will be saved." He does not say: "if you obey, you will succeed."

He says: **you will be guided.** *Tahtadū.*

The verb is from the root *h-d-y* — to guide, to show the path, to lead. And the form here — Form VIII — *ihtadā* — is reflexive. It means **to find the path for yourself, to come to be guided, to receive guidance into your own being**. It is not just being shown a road. It is becoming the kind of person whose feet know where to step.

Now here is the paradox. You would think that the answer to the question "how do I lighten my load?" is "do less." Less obligation, less duty, less obedience.

But the ayah is telling you the opposite. **The way to lighten the load is to obey.**

[PAUSE]

How can this be?

Think about a backpack on a hike. If you carry it on one shoulder, it is unbearable within a mile. If you sling it over your back with both straps and tighten the chest strap, the same weight becomes part of how your body moves. The weight has not changed. But your **alignment** has changed. The burden is now distributed along the architecture that was made to hold it.

This is what *ihtadā* does. Guidance is the body coming into alignment with the burden it was made to carry. Obedience is not adding weight. Obedience is **finding the carrying posture for the weight that was already on your back**.

The one who turns away — *tawallā* — is not lighter. He is heavier, because he is now carrying the same load **wrong**. Without alignment. Without rhythm. Without direction. Without anyone walking beside him.

The one who obeys is not weighed down by obedience. He is **carried** by it, because his life now has the shape of the road, and the road bears him.

[PAUSE]

This is why the same ayah that uses the language of burden ends with the language of guidance. The two are not in tension. They are the same fact from two angles. **Your burden is real. And the only way to make peace with your burden is to walk under it in the direction of the One who placed it there.**

There is something else hidden in this clause. Allah does not say *tuṭīʿū-llāha tahtadū* — "if you obey Allah, you will be guided." He says *tuṭīʿūhu* — "if you obey *him*" — referring back to the Messenger ﷺ.

This is not a demotion of Allah. It is a refusal to let you split obedience into a "spiritual" obedience to Allah that is somehow separable from a "practical" obedience to the Prophet's teaching. The two are one cloth. **The Messenger ﷺ is the path along which obedience to Allah actually walks.** You do not get to obey Allah in the abstract while ignoring the concrete shape of His Messenger's life.

The guidance — *tahtadū* — comes through walking *that* path, the one *he* walked, the one his words made *mubīn* for you.

[PAUSE]

The practical application: tomorrow morning, when an obligation rises in front of you — a prayer, a difficult conversation, an honest answer, the refusal of a small temptation — do not ask yourself "how heavy is this?" Ask yourself: **"If I lean into this with my whole weight, will I find that I am being carried?"**

Because the testimony of every soul who has obeyed deeply is that the burdens of obedience do not crush. They align. And what feels like submission from outside feels, from inside, like **finally walking right**.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now look at the whole verse standing together. There is a structure here that I deliberately did not show you at the start, because you needed to feel its parts first.

The ayah opens with a command: *Qul aṭīʿū-llāha wa aṭīʿū-r-rasūl* — "Say: obey Allah and obey the Messenger."

It closes with the explanation of what the Messenger does: *wa mā ʿala-r-rasūli illā-l-balāghu-l-mubīn* — "and there is nothing upon the Messenger except clear conveyance."

In between these two pillars, the ayah does a beautifully **symmetrical move**:

> **If you turn away** → his burden is bounded, your burden is bounded.
> **If you obey** → you will be guided.

Two scenarios. Two paths. Each one with its own consequence. And bracketing both — the same Messenger, with the same job description, with the same finite, fulfilled, completed *balāgh*.

[PAUSE]

What this architecture is showing you is something profound: **the Messenger's role does not change based on your response.** He is *al-balāgh al-mubīn* whether you turn or whether you obey. His station is fixed. **What changes is what happens to you.**

This is freedom for him. And it is total responsibility for you.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes we have explored are the same insight from two directions.

The word *ḥummila* — the burden placed upon you — and the theme of carrying a message versus carrying a person, are the same teaching. Both say: **the weight that was placed on you is yours, and the weight that was placed on another is theirs, and the moment you confuse them you will break.**

The phrase *al-balāghu al-mubīn* — clear conveyance — and the theme of obedience as alignment, are the same teaching. Both say: **your task has a defined shape. Find the shape. Walk inside it. Do not extend yourself beyond it. Do not collapse short of it. Either failure is exhausting. Only the fit is restful.**

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose response have I been carrying as if it were my own failure — and what would it mean, today, to set that response back down where Allah placed it?

2. When I deliver truth to someone I love, am I delivering it in a way that actually **arrives** — that is *balāgh*? Or am I delivering it in a way that protects me from the discomfort of being heard?

3. Where in my life am I treating obedience as **added weight** rather than as the **alignment** that lets me carry what is already on my back?

4. What is the *ḥumūla* — the burden — that Allah has loaded onto **my** shoulders, specifically? Not the generic Muslim burden. The one shaped to my life, my role, my season. Have I named it? Have I accepted that I did not choose it but was given it?

5. Is there someone whose freedom I am quietly trying to steal — through guilt, through pressure, through endless argument — because I cannot bear the way they are using it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Your job is to deliver clearly; their response is theirs to carry — and the moment you accept this is the moment obedience stops feeling like weight and starts feeling like the road that carries you.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, do this. Bring to mind the person whose drift, whose choice, whose refusal has been pressing on your chest. The one whose freedom has been costing you sleep.

Open your hands. Say to Allah: *Yā Rabb, You loaded me with delivery. You did not load me with their response. I have spoken what I could speak. I now place them back in Your hands, where they have always been.*

Then close your hands. And let your chest rise without that weight on it.

This is not abandonment. This is *innamā*. **This is the wall the ayah built between his burden and yours, finally letting you stand up straight under your own.**

### A Brief Du'a

اللَّهُمَّ، حَمِّلْنَا مَا تُحِبُّ أَنْ نَحْمِلَ، وَلاَ تُحَمِّلْنَا مَا لاَ طَاقَةَ لَنَا بِهِ.

*O Allah, burden us with what You love for us to carry, and do not burden us with what we have no strength to bear.*

اللَّهُمَّ، اجْعَلْ طَاعَتَكَ هُدًى لَنَا، وَلاَ تَجْعَلْنَا مِمَّنْ تَوَلَّى فَحَمَلَ وِزْرَهُ وَحْدَهُ.

*O Allah, make Your obedience a guidance for us, and do not let us be among those who turn away and end up carrying their own burden alone.*

اللَّهُمَّ بَلِّغْنَا، وَبَلِّغْ بِنَا، وَلاَ تَجْعَلْنَا نَحْمِلُ أَجْوِبَةَ الْقُلُوبِ الَّتِي لَمْ تَكِلْهَا إِلَيْنَا.

*O Allah, let us deliver, and let truth be delivered through us, and do not make us carry the responses of hearts You did not entrust to us.*

آمين.
