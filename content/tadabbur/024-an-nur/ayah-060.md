# The Dignity of the Setting Sun

## When Restraint Becomes Beauty

> وَٱلْقَوَٰعِدُ مِنَ ٱلنِّسَآءِ ٱلَّـٰتِى لَا يَرْجُونَ نِكَاحًۭا فَلَيْسَ عَلَيْهِنَّ جُنَاحٌ أَن يَضَعْنَ ثِيَابَهُنَّ غَيْرَ مُتَبَرِّجَـٰتٍۭ بِزِينَةٍۢ ۖ وَأَن يَسْتَعْفِفْنَ خَيْرٌۭ لَّهُنَّ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌۭ

*Wa'l-qawāʿidu min an-nisāʾi'llātī lā yarjūna nikāḥan fa-laysa ʿalayhinna junāḥun an yaḍaʿna thiyābahunna ghayra mutabarrijātin bi-zīnatin, wa-an yastaʿfifna khayrun lahunna, wa'llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm.*

**"And those women past childbearing who have no hope of marriage — there is no blame upon them if they set aside their outer garments, not displaying adornment. But that they refrain is better for them. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before This Ayah

To understand the weight of what Allah is doing in this single verse, we have to walk back into the surah and feel where we are.

Surah An-Nur is a surah of architecture. It builds — wall by wall, threshold by threshold — a society of dignity. It legislates how spouses speak about each other when accusations fly. It teaches how children should knock before entering a room. It tells believing men to lower their gaze. It tells believing women how to draw their *khimār* across their chests. It speaks of homes as sanctuaries. It places, in the very middle of all this law, the most luminous parable in the entire Quran — *Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth* — and surrounds that light with the rules that protect the lamp.

Notice what this means: Allah does not separate spirituality from clothing. He does not separate divine light from doorway etiquette. The same surah that contains *Āyat an-Nūr* contains the rules of who knocks and when. Because in the worldview of An-Nur, the home is the place where the light is kept.

By the time we arrive at ayah 60, Allah has spoken about almost every category of person. The young. The marriageable. The married. The unmarried seeking marriage. The slaves. The children before puberty. The children after. The travelers. The hosts.

And then — almost at the closing — Allah turns His attention to a group that the world has historically rendered invisible.

### The Invisible Ones

Across cultures, across centuries, there is a moment in a woman's life when the world stops looking at her. When marriage is no longer expected. When her children, if she had any, are grown. When she is no longer the center of the social calculus that marks her as desirable, eligible, watched.

In many human societies, this is treated as a kind of erasure. She becomes background. The young girl is celebrated. The bride is celebrated. The mother is celebrated — sometimes. But the elder woman, the one whose hair has whitened, whose step has slowed, whose station in the matrimonial market has closed — she is often spoken about, if at all, in a tone of quiet condescension.

[PAUSE]

Now hear what Allah does.

He does not skip her. He does not group her with "everyone else." He gives her *her own ayah*. He addresses her by a name no human language quite captures. He grants her an ease that no jurist could have invented. And then — in the same breath, with extraordinary tenderness — He invites her to something higher than ease.

### The Hook

There are five movements in this verse. Each one is a small miracle of restraint.

A name that elevates as it describes.
A negation that protects as it permits.
A verb of "putting down" that carries the weight of decades.
A word for "ostentation" with a meaning rooted in *towers*.
And a closing of two divine names that change everything.

What does Allah see when He looks at a woman the world has stopped looking at?

Stay with me. The answer is one of the most beautiful things in the Book.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Al-Qawāʿid* — The Ones Who Have Settled

Allah does not say "old women." He does not say "women past their prime." He does not use any of the Arabic vocabulary that would have been common, and that other languages reach for so easily.

He uses one word: **al-qawāʿid** — *those who sit*, *those who have settled*.

Before we open this word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning.

Arabic roots are like seeds. From a single three-letter root, a whole orchard grows. The root **ق-ع-د** *(q-ʿ-d)* is one of these generative seeds. It means, at its core, *to sit*, *to settle*, *to take one's place*.

But watch where it goes.

From the same root: *qāʿidah* — a base, a foundation. The thing on which a structure rests.
From the same root: *al-qawāʿid* in Surah Al-Baqarah — *the foundations* of the Kaʿbah that Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl raised.
From the same root: *qaʿada ʿan* — to refrain from action, to step back, to withdraw from a station one previously held.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what is happening here?

When Allah names this group of women, He does not name them by what they have *lost*. He does not name them by their distance from youth or their absence from the marriage market. He names them by the architecture they now occupy — *those who have taken their seat*. *Those who are foundational*.

Notice something. The Arabic word for the *foundations of the Kaʿbah* and the Arabic word for *these elder women* is the **same word**. *Al-qawāʿid.*

What does a foundation do? It does not move. It does not need to. The walls and the windows depend on it. The dome cannot rise without it.

The Quran is telling us — through nothing more than the choice of one word — that these women are not the dust that has settled at the bottom of the building. They are the bedrock the building stands on.

This is the difference between a tradition that worships youth and a Book that recognizes structure. Youth is the spire. Age is the foundation. Both are necessary. Only one of them can fail without the building falling.

### Element Two: *Allātī Lā Yarjūna Nikāḥan* — Those Who Have No Hope

Now Allah qualifies *al-qawāʿid* with a relative clause that, on the surface, sounds almost sad in English: *those who have no hope of marriage*.

But Arabic does something English cannot. The verb here is **yarjūna** — from the root **ر-ج-و** *(r-j-w)*.

Let me teach you the texture of *rajāʾ*.

In Arabic, there are several words for *hope*. There is *amal* — wishful expectation. There is *ṭamaʿ* — covetous longing, often with a negative tinge. And there is *rajāʾ* — the kind of hope a believer holds toward Allah for mercy. *Rajāʾ* is the word the scholars use for one of the wings of the heart in worship: *khawf wa rajāʾ*, fear and hope.

So when Allah says these women *lā yarjūna nikāḥan* — "do not *rajā* marriage" — He is not insulting them. He is being precise. He is saying: *they no longer orient their inward longing toward this station*. They have closed the chapter not in resignation, but in maturity.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The verse is not saying *no one wants to marry them*. The verse is saying *they no longer aim their hope there*. The agency belongs to them.

This is what classical commentators noticed — and it is why this ayah has been read with such tenderness. The condition is not biological alone; it is internal. It is about where the heart is pointed.

And here is what makes this remarkable: the same Quran that constantly commands believers to direct *rajāʾ* toward Allah is acknowledging that, for these women, the worldly stations of *rajāʾ* have closed — and what is left is the deepest *rajāʾ* of all. The hope of meeting their Lord.

The setting sun is no less the sun. It has simply turned its face from the world toward the horizon.

### Element Three: *An Yaḍaʿna Thiyābahunna* — The Verb of Putting Down

Now the legal core. Allah says: *there is no blame on them if they yaḍaʿna their thiyāb* — set down their garments.

The verb is **yaḍaʿna**, from the root **و-ض-ع** *(w-ḍ-ʿ)*.

This root is everywhere in the Quran. It is the verb used when:

- Maryam *gave birth* — she *set down* her child (3:36).
- The earth was *laid out* — Allah *set it down* (55:10).
- The scales of justice will be *set in place* on the Day of Judgment (21:47).
- The Mother of Cities had a Book *set down* in it (3:96).

Notice the pattern. *Waḍaʿa* is not a casual word. It is the verb of placing something down with intention, with weight, with the sense that the thing being placed is heavy and the placer is deliberate.

[PAUSE]

Allah did not say "if they take off" their garments. He did not use *khalaʿa* (to remove) or *naẓaʿa* (to strip away). He used *yaḍaʿna* — *if they set down*.

Do you feel the difference?

A garment that is *taken off* is discarded. A garment that is *set down* is honored. It is the verb you would use for laying down a burden you have carried with dignity for years. It is the verb Maryam uses when she becomes a mother. It is the verb Allah uses when He spreads the earth.

What this verb is whispering — and "whispering" is the right word because it is so subtle — is that the *thiyāb* these women have worn through decades of their lives are not a costume to be discarded the moment ease is permitted. They are a load borne with honor. And so the permission to set them down is given in a verb that *honors the load*.

This is what scholars call *adab al-Qurʾān* — the manners of the Quran. Even when granting an exemption, Allah preserves the dignity of the thing being exempted from.

### Element Four: *Ghayra Mutabarrijātin Bi-Zīnah* — The Tower Word

Here is where the verse turns. The ease is granted — *but* — *ghayra mutabarrijātin bi-zīnah*, "not displaying adornment."

The word **mutabarrijāt** is from the root **ب-ر-ج** *(b-r-j)*.

This is the same root as **burj** — *a tower*, a *fortress*, a *high prominent structure*. It is the same root in *al-burūj*, the constellations or fortified positions in the sky, after which an entire surah of the Quran is named.

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself — what is a tower? It is something that *rises above*. Something that *announces itself*. Something that *cannot be missed*.

So **tabarruj** — the verbal noun from this root in Form V (*tafaʿʿul*, the form of self-action and intentional process) — literally means *to make oneself into a tower*. To rise above. To put oneself on display in a way that *demands* the gaze.

Read this in light of the surah. Surah An-Nur has been teaching us — *guard the gaze, lower the gaze, the gaze is a covenant between Allah and the eye*. And then it closes the door from both sides. The viewer is told not to look. The viewed is told not to *make herself a tower*.

Now feel what Allah does in this ayah.

He grants ease — *set down your outer garments*. And then, immediately, He attaches the qualifier — *not making yourselves into towers of adornment*.

Why? Because the issue was never the cloth. The issue was always the *display*. The garment was a means. The dignity was the end. When the means becomes unnecessary because the underlying need has changed, the garment may be set down. But the *dignity* it was protecting — that never changes. That belongs to her at every age.

This is one of the most quietly profound things in the verse. Allah is teaching us that *modesty is not a function of cloth*. Modesty is a function of how the soul orients itself toward being seen. A woman of seventy who carries herself with that orientation has not lost an inch of her *ḥayāʾ* whether or not she wears the same outer garment she wore at twenty.

### Element Five: *Wa-An Yastaʿfifna Khayrun Lahunna* — The Form That Asks

After granting the ease, Allah says: **wa-an yastaʿfifna khayrun lahunna** — "but that they refrain is better for them."

The verb **yastaʿfifna** is from the root **ع-ف-ف** *(ʿ-f-f)* — the root of *ʿiffah*, *chastity*, *restraint*, *modesty of soul*.

But notice the form. This is not just *yaʿfifna* (they refrain). It is *yasta-ʿfifna*. It is **Form X**, the *istafʿala* form.

Let me teach you what Form X does in Arabic.

Form X is the form of *seeking*, *requesting*, *asking for*. *Aslama* (he submitted) becomes *istaslama* (he sought submission for himself). *ʿAlima* (he knew) becomes *istaʿlama* (he sought knowledge). *Ghafara* (He forgave) becomes *astaghfir* (I seek forgiveness).

So *yastaʿfifna* is not "they are chaste." It is "**they seek chastity for themselves.**" It is *they actively pursue restraint*. It is the verb of someone reaching for a higher station, not someone passively occupying a lower one.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what this means in the architecture of the verse.

Allah has just said: *there is no blame*. The legal floor is set. The permission is granted. He could have stopped there.

But He does not. He adds: *and that they* **seek restraint for themselves** *is better for them*.

Do you see the invitation?

Allah is distinguishing between two stations. The first is the station of *rukhṣa* — the dispensation, the ease, the permission. The second is the station of *ʿazīmah* — the higher resolve, the chosen excellence beyond what is required.

He is not commanding the higher station. He is *opening the door to it*. He is saying: *the floor is here. The ceiling is there. Walk wherever you can.*

And the word *khayr* — "better" — is not a comparative of moral burden. It is a comparative of *good*. *More good for them*. *Better for their hearts*. *Better for their standing*. The choice is theirs. But Allah, with the gentleness of a parent who has already given the permission, leans in and whispers: *if you can — the higher station is sweeter*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Five elements. *Al-qawāʿid* — the foundations. *Lā yarjūna* — the closed orientation of hope. *Yaḍaʿna* — the verb of dignified setting down. *Mutabarrijāt* — the tower of display, which is the real thing being guarded against. *Yastaʿfifna* — the active seeking of restraint as a higher station.

And we have not yet touched the closing. *Wa'llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm.*

What this linguistic construction has shown us is this: Allah, in twenty-eight words, has accomplished what entire treatises on dignity could not. He has named a stage of life that culture renders invisible. He has granted that stage an ease no one was bold enough to invent. He has done it with a verb that honors the burden being eased. He has guarded the underlying principle even as the outer expression changed. And He has invited the soul to a higher station without forcing the climb.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Dignity of the Setting Sun

There is a question modern psychology has been circling for decades: *what is the value of a person whose visible utility has ended?*

The young person produces. The parent raises. The professional builds. But what about the elder whose children are grown, whose career has closed, whose body has slowed, whose place in the visible economy of "doing" has diminished?

Our culture answers this question, mostly, with silence. We hide our elders in homes designed to keep them from interrupting the productive churn. We build entire industries around making the aging body *look* young again, as if aging itself were a defect to be edited. We treat the elder woman, especially, as someone who has *lost* her relevance — when youth turned its face away, the world's attention turned with it.

[PAUSE]

This ayah destroys that worldview from inside.

Look again at the word. *Al-qawāʿid*. The foundations.

In a building, the foundations are *the most important load-bearing element in the entire structure*. They are also the part you cannot see. You walk into a beautiful mosque and you marvel at the dome, the calligraphy, the chandelier. You do not marvel at the foundations. You do not even think about them. But the moment they fail, the dome means nothing.

Allah names these women with that word because, in the architecture of a believing community, they *are* that. They are the foundations. They are the grandmothers who carry stories, the elder aunts who hold the rooms, the women whose duʿāʾ are answered because their hearts have been polished by decades of standing in prayer. They are not invisible because they have lost relevance. They are invisible the way foundations are invisible — *because the building is standing on them*.

[PAUSE]

And then Allah does something extraordinary. Having named them with this word of structural permanence, He grants them ease. *Lay down the outer garment if you wish. The need is past. The season has changed.*

But then — and this is the sweetness — He does not say "and that's all." He says, *but if you can choose the higher station, it is better for you*.

Why?

Because Allah knows something about the elder woman that the world has forgotten: she still has a *self*. She still has a *heart that can climb*. She has not stopped being a soul on the path to her Lord just because the world has stopped watching. The visible economy of being-watched is gone, but the invisible economy of being-known-by-Allah has not even reached its peak.

[PAUSE]

Think of the setting sun. As it falls toward the horizon, the world looks away. The day is closing. The eyes turn elsewhere.

But the sun is not less the sun. In its setting, it produces some of the most extraordinary colors of the entire day — colors that the noon sun cannot make. The setting sun *paints*. It pours gold and rose across the entire sky. The viewer who turns away misses it. The viewer who looks sees something the noon sun never offered.

This is the elder woman in the eyes of Allah. The world has turned away. *Allah has not.* And the soul that was beautiful at twenty is, by this point, capable of producing colors no twenty-year-old soul could produce — colors of patience, colors of *dhikr*, colors of duʿāʾ at *tahajjud* in a body that aches but bows anyway.

#### What This Changes Tomorrow

Here is the practical edge. If you are reading this and your mother is alive — go *see her*. If your grandmother is alive — *call her*. The Quran has just told you that the woman society has classified as "past her time" is, in the architecture of revelation, *the foundation*. Treat her as such.

And if *you* are her — if the years have stacked and the world has turned its eyes away — hear what Allah just said about you. He gave you your own ayah. He named you with the word for *foundation*. He granted you ease and then invited you to climb higher because He knew you still could. The world's attention is not the measure of your worth. *Allah's attention is.* And He is, in this very ayah, watching you with extraordinary care.

### Theme Two: The Two Stations — When Allah Opens a Door and Then Whispers

There is a feature of Islamic law that secular ethics has never fully understood, and this ayah captures it as perfectly as any verse in the Quran.

The feature is this: **the floor and the ceiling are not the same**.

Most ethical systems work in binaries. Permitted or forbidden. Required or optional. Legal or illegal. They are flat. They tell you what you must do and what you must not do, and the space between is moral indifference.

Islam does not work this way.

Islam has *aḥkām* — categories. There is *farḍ* (obligatory). There is *ḥarām* (forbidden). And between them there is a vast inhabited space: *mustaḥabb* (recommended), *mubāḥ* (permitted), *makrūh* (disliked). And within this architecture there is also the distinction between *rukhṣa* — the dispensation, the easing — and *ʿazīmah* — the firm resolve, the higher path.

This ayah is a textbook example of *rukhṣa* paired with *ʿazīmah* in a single breath.

[PAUSE]

Watch it again.

*Fa-laysa ʿalayhinna junāḥun an yaḍaʿna thiyābahunna* — "there is no blame on them if they set down their garments."

That is the *rukhṣa*. The floor. The dispensation. Allah has examined the situation, the season of life, the changed circumstances, and He has *lowered the bar*. He has said: *what was required of others is no longer required of you. You may rest.*

Then, in the same verse, *wa-an yastaʿfifna khayrun lahunna* — "but that they seek restraint is better for them."

That is the *ʿazīmah*. The ceiling. The invitation. The whisper that says: *I have lowered the bar, but if you can keep climbing, the climb is sweeter than the rest.*

[PAUSE]

Think about how counterintuitive this is.

A human legislator, having just granted an ease, would never undermine it by saying "but actually the harder path is better." That would feel like guilt-tripping. It would feel like the legislator did not really mean the ease.

Allah does not work this way, and the reason is theological. Because Allah is not a legislator trying to manage compliance. Allah is the *Rabb* — the Lord, the Cherisher, the One whose laws are designed for the soul's *growth*. He grants the ease because He knows the body has tired. He whispers about the higher station because He knows the soul, at this very age, is precisely capable of reaching for it.

The ease is not the maximum. The ease is the safety net. *The sky is still open.*

[PAUSE]

There is a beautiful psychological insight buried here. When you tell a person who is exhausted, "you must do the hard thing," they often break. When you tell them, "rest — the hard thing is no longer required," they often collapse into a worse exhaustion, because they feel they have been let go.

But when you say — *rest if you need to. And if you choose to climb anyway, the climb is yours, and it is good for you* — something extraordinary happens. The person finds, often, that they *want* to climb. Not because they have been ordered to, but because they have been *trusted* to.

This is the pedagogy of the Quran. It does not order what cannot be ordered. It does not infantilize. It opens the door, names the easier path, and then gently — without pressure — describes the higher path. And the believing heart, hearing it described, often climbs of its own accord.

#### What This Changes Tomorrow

There are seasons in your life when something will become harder. Your body. Your circumstances. Your responsibilities. There will come a time — for some sooner than others — when an act of worship that was once easy becomes a struggle. Standing in prayer. Fasting. Performing wudū with cold water in winter.

Know that Allah, in this ayah, has shown you His pattern. *He grants the ease.* If standing is hard, sit. If fasting is harming you, do not fast. If wudū is causing illness, perform tayammum. He does not require what cannot be carried.

And in the same breath, He whispers: *if you can climb, the climb is good for you.* Not as an obligation. As an invitation. As a gentle reminder that the soul that reaches a little — even when it does not have to — finds a sweetness in the reach that the rest could not have given.

This is the Islam of two stations. The floor that catches you. The ceiling that calls to you. Both held open by the same merciful hand.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at the verse from above.

It opens with **a name** — *al-qawāʿid* — that elevates the named.
It moves to **a heart-state** — *lā yarjūna nikāḥan* — that locates them inwardly, not just biologically.
It grants **a permission** — *yaḍaʿna thiyābahunna* — with a verb that honors what is set down.
It guards **a principle** — *ghayra mutabarrijātin bi-zīnah* — that the underlying dignity is unchanged.
It opens **a higher station** — *wa-an yastaʿfifna khayrun lahunna* — without forcing the climb.

And then — and only then — Allah closes with the two names:

**Wa'llāhu samīʿun ʿalīm.** "And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."

[PAUSE]

Sit with this closing. Why these two names? Why here?

Because the entire ayah has been about *what is seen and unseen*. The elder woman whom the world does not see. The garment that may be set down or kept. The intention of *tabarruj* that may or may not animate the heart. The seeking of *ʿiffah* that may go entirely unwitnessed.

And Allah closes by saying — *I hear. I know.*

Whatever the world has stopped seeing about you, *I have not stopped seeing*. Whatever choice you make in the privacy of your own heart between the floor and the ceiling, *I am there*. The setting sun is not unwatched. The foundation is not unnoticed. The whispered intention to climb a little higher when no one would have asked you to — *I hear it*.

This is the seal of the verse. The whole legal and spiritual architecture rests on this one truth: *Allah is paying attention*. To the elder. To the unseen. To the small, quiet choice of restraint that no human eye will ever catalogue.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The word *al-qawāʿid* (foundations) and the theme of the dignity of the setting sun — these are the same insight. Allah names the elder woman by what she structurally *is*, not by what she has lost.

The pairing of *laysa ʿalayhinna junāḥ* and *khayrun lahunna* and the theme of two stations — these are the same insight. Allah's law is not flat; it is layered, and the layers are designed for souls to grow in.

And the closing names *as-Samīʿ al-ʿAlīm* are the integration of both. Because the dignity of the setting sun and the height of the higher station both depend on this: there is a witness who never looks away.

### Questions to Carry

1. Who in my life occupies the station of *al-qawāʿid* — and have I been treating her as foundation or as background?
2. Where in my own life has Allah granted me a *rukhṣa*, an ease, that I have mistaken for the ceiling rather than the floor?
3. When my season changes — and it will — will I know how to set things down with the dignity of *yaḍaʿna* rather than the despair of mere loss?
4. What is the higher station that Allah, in His patience, has been *whispering* to me about, without ever forcing the climb?
5. If I am old enough that the world has begun to look away — how does it change everything to know that Allah has not?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the world stops looking at her, Allah gives her a name that means *foundation*, an ease that honors what she has carried, and an invitation to climb higher than ever — and He seals it all with the truth that He is hearing every quiet choice she makes.

### The Closing Invitation

If there is an elder woman within reach — a mother, a grandmother, an aunt, a teacher — let this ayah carry you to her this week. Let her hear from you something that reflects what Allah has named her. *Foundation*. Not background. Not a remnant. The very thing this house is standing on.

And if you are her — receive this verse as the gift it is. The world's gaze is not the measure of your light. *Allah is hearing. Allah is knowing.* The setting sun, in His sight, is painting the most beautiful sky of the day.

### Du'a

*Yā Samīʿ, Yā ʿAlīm — You who hear what no human ear catches and know what no human eye sees: honor the foundations of our families, the elder women whose duʿāʾ have shaped us. Teach us to recognize them as the structure they are. And when our own seasons change, let us know how to set down our burdens with dignity, and let us hear, in the quiet of our hearts, Your whisper toward the higher station — and answer it. Āmīn.*
