# The Hand That Turns the Night Also Turns the Heart

## Surah An-Nur · Ayah 44

> يُقَلِّبُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّيْلَ وَٱلنَّهَارَ ۚ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً لِّأُو۟لِى ٱلْأَبْصَٰرِ
>
> *Yuqallibu-llāhu-l-layla wa-n-nahār. Inna fī dhālika la-ʿibratan li-ulī-l-absār.*
>
> **"Allah turns over the night and the day. Indeed, in that is a crossing-over for those who possess sights."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

Stop for a moment and feel where this ayah sits.

We are inside Surah An-Nur — *The Light*. A surah that opened by legislating consequences for the most painful kind of darkness a community can fall into: the darkness of slander against the innocent. A surah that taught believers how to walk into homes, how to lower the gaze, how to clothe the heart before clothing the body. A surah whose centerpiece — verse 35 — is the most luminous metaphor in the entire Qur'an: *Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth.* A niche. A glass. An olive tree. Light upon light.

And then, after all of that — after the laws, the parables, the masjids lit at dawn and dusk by men whom trade and selling do not distract — the surah does something that always catches me. It looks up.

The verse just before this one (24:43) walked us through the sky: clouds being driven, gathered, piled, until the rain comes from their hollows, and hail descends from mountains in the heavens, striking whom He wills, sparing whom He wills. Then comes our ayah. And then the verse just after (24:45) takes us down to the ground, to every creature that walks — some on their bellies, some on two legs, some on four.

So 24:44 is the middle panel of a three-part argument. **Sky. Time. Earth.** Three signs in a row, each one a different proof of the same Power. And the middle one — the one we are about to sit with — is the simplest. No clouds. No creatures. Just one sentence about the most basic fact of human experience.

The night turns over. The day turns over. And inside that turning, there is something for those who can see.

### What This Felt Like to the Original Listener

Imagine, for a second, what it meant to hear this in seventh-century Arabia. There were no streetlights. No clocks. No artificial illumination beyond the small flame of an oil lamp or a fire. The night was a real night — a black, vast, star-saturated thing that wrapped you up so completely you could not find your own hand. And the day was a real day — a sun that hammered the desert, a light that exposed every surface.

Night and day were not abstractions. They were *experienced.* They alternated over you. You felt the cold come and the heat come and the cold come again. You watched stars walk across the sky and disappear. You watched the world be erased and re-drawn every twelve hours.

To a listener like that, this ayah is not telling them a fact. It is asking them why they had stopped noticing.

### The Hook

I want you to notice something about this ayah before we open a single word of it.

Allah is in a surah called *Light.* The whole architecture of this surah is built around light — niches, lamps, glass, oil, "light upon light." If you were going to pick one cosmic sign to *prove* this Light, what would you pick?

You would probably pick the sun. Or the dawn. Or the way light scatters across water. You would pick something *bright.*

But Allah does not. In the middle of the surah of light, when He chooses one sign of His power from time itself, He chooses the **alternation.** Not the day. Not the night. The *turning between them.*

Why? [PAUSE] Stay with that question. Because the answer is going to take us somewhere we did not expect to go.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

There are three things in this ayah I want us to look at carefully. Just three. Each one is a small word, and each one is a doorway.

The verb: *yuqallibu.*
The noun: *ʿibrah.*
And the strange plural: *absār.*

### Element One — *Yuqallibu*: A Verb That Tips Over

Before we look at the word itself, I want to teach you a small piece of Arabic grammar — because without it, the whole ayah stays flat.

Arabic verbs come in patterns. Form I is the basic stem. *Qalaba* — to turn something over. Simple, single action. You flip a coin once. *Qalaba.*

But Arabic has a Form II — the same root, with the middle letter doubled. And Form II usually does one of two things to the meaning. It either makes the action **intensive** — done over and over, hammered in — or it makes it **causative** — making something turn rather than just turning it.

Hold that in your mind. Form I is one flip. Form II is many flips, or a *making-flip*.

Now look at our verb. **Yuqallibu.** That doubled middle letter — the *shadda* on the *lām* — that is Form II. The verb is intensive.

Allah is not saying *"Allah turns the night and the day."* That would be Form I — *yaqlibu.* He is saying something stronger. He is saying *Allah turns them over and over and over.* He is saying *Allah keeps tipping them.* The verb itself rocks back and forth. You can almost feel it in your mouth — *yu-qal-li-bu*, the doubled syllable like a hinge swinging.

[PAUSE]

But that is not the deepest thing. Stay with me.

The root of this verb is **qaf-lām-bāʾ** — *q-l-b.* And from this exact same root comes another word that you have heard a thousand times.

**Qalb.** The heart.

Why is the heart called *qalb* in Arabic? Because, the classical lexicographers tell us, *the heart is the thing that turns.* It does not stay still. It moves. It changes. It is named after its own instability. The Arabs called the heart *qalb* before Islam — because they noticed, even without revelation, that the chest contains something that will not sit still.

And the Prophet ﷺ used to say in his daily duʿā — recorded in the authentic collections — *Yā Muqalliba-l-qulūb, thabbit qalbī ʿalā dīnik.* "O **Turner of the hearts**, make my heart firm upon Your religion."

*Muqallib.* The active participle of our exact verb, Form II. *The One who keeps turning hearts over and over.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened.

The same verb that Allah uses for **the night and the day**, the Prophet ﷺ used to address Allah as the One who **does this to hearts.**

The cosmos does not have one verb. The heart does not have another. There is **one verb.** *Yuqallibu.*

The hand that turns the night until it becomes day — and then turns the day until it becomes night — and then turns the night until it becomes day again, over and over, never stopping, never tiring, never failing — *that same hand* turns the heart.

This is not metaphor stacked on metaphor. This is the same word, picked up from the cosmos and laid on the chest. Allah is telling you, with one verb, that what is happening above you is happening *inside* you.

The night you watched come last evening? The day you watched arrive this morning? That is not a piece of physics. That is a **demonstration** of the kind of Power that has its hand on your heart right now.

### What This Verb Actually Does to Us

Let's sit with the implication. Because this is going to matter for how we live our spiritual lives.

How many of us have asked: *Why does my heart not stay where I put it?* I went to a beautiful gathering last week. My heart was alive. Three days later it is dull again. I read Qur'an with tears in *Ramadan*. By Shawwāl I am cold. I made a sincere taubah on Friday. By Wednesday I am back inside the same sin.

We treat this as **failure.** We treat the cold heart as evidence that the warm heart was fake. We treat the doubt as evidence that the faith was naive. We assume that if I were truly close, I would *stay* close.

But look at what the cosmos is doing. The cosmos does not stay in light. **It cannot.** The very system Allah designed *requires* the day to be turned into night, and the night to be turned into day. If the day stayed forever, nothing would grow. If the night stayed forever, nothing would live. The alternation **is** the design. It is not the failure of the design.

And the verb that describes that alternation is the same verb that the Prophet ﷺ used to describe what Allah does to hearts.

[PAUSE]

This is not a license. We are still responsible for *what we do* in the night and *what we do* in the day. But it is a reframe so deep that I want you to feel its full weight before we move on.

The turning of your heart is not your enemy. The turning is the **architecture.** What looks like spiritual inconsistency is, often, the same Power that turns the night, applied to a smaller scale. The question is not *"Why does my heart turn?"* The heart turning is what hearts *do.* The question is *"Whose hand is on the hinge?"*

And the answer the Prophet ﷺ taught us is *yā Muqalliba-l-qulūb* — *O Turner of hearts.* Not *O Stopper of turning.* He did not ask Allah to freeze the heart in place. He asked the One who is turning it to **make it firm in the right direction** as it turns.

That is a completely different prayer than the one most of us are praying.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two — *ʿIbrah*: A Lesson That Has to Cross Over

Now look at the next word that matters. *La-ʿibratan.* "Indeed, in that is a *ʿibrah*."

Most translations say *"a sign,"* or *"a lesson,"* or *"an admonition."* All of those are correct. But all of those, in English, are flat. They make the word sound like *information.* Like a textbook handout.

The Arabic is doing something much more precise. And to feel it, we have to look at the root.

**ʿAyn-bāʾ-rāʾ** — *ʿ-b-r.* What does this root mean at its core? It means **to cross.** To go from one side of something to the other.

The same root gives us:
- *ʿAbara* — to cross a river, to traverse, to pass over.
- *ʿIbrī* — Hebrew. The Hebrews — *Banū Isrāʾīl* — were called *ʿIbrīyyūn* in Arabic because, in one classical etymology, they crossed over from one place to another. *The ones who cross.*
- *ʿAbrah* — a tear. Why is a tear called *ʿabrah*? Because *it is the thing that crosses over* — from inside the eye to the outside of the face. From hidden grief to visible grief. The tear is the crossing of pain into the world.

And from this same root: *ʿIbrah.* A lesson.

But not just any lesson. A lesson is called *ʿibrah* in Arabic because — to use it — *something has to cross from outside of you to inside of you.* Information that stays out there is not *ʿibrah.* It is data. It becomes *ʿibrah* only when it makes the crossing into your heart and changes you.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says, "in that is a *ʿibratan li-ulī-l-absār*" — He is not saying *"in that is information for those with eyes."* He is saying:

**"In that is a crossing-over, available to those who possess sights."**

The night-day alternation is not just an item to be observed and filed. It is *waiting* on the other side of a riverbank, *for someone to cross.* The lesson has to come over the water. It has to leave the cosmos and enter the chest.

Now feel how this loops back to *yuqallibu.*

The night turns into the day. The day turns into the night. The whole cosmos is doing one giant *crossing.* And every time that crossing happens — every dawn and every sunset — there is an *ʿibrah* available. A piece of meaning that wants to cross from the sky into your heart.

But — and here is the painful truth this word contains — *ʿibrah only happens to those who let it cross.*

If the lesson stays on the other side of the river, it is not *ʿibrah*. It is just sky. Just time. Just sunset. Just another day.

How many sunsets have we seen in our lives? Hundreds? Thousands? How many made the crossing? How many actually *moved* something inside us?

[PAUSE]

This is, I think, one of the most pointed words Allah uses about the spiritual life. Because it locates the failure not in the *signs* — the signs are there every twelve hours — but in **whether or not we let them cross over.**

A whole cosmos of *ʿibrah* is being performed for us, twice a day, every day, our entire lives. And most of it is sitting on the riverbank.

### Element Three — *Absār*: The Strange Plural

Now look at the last word in the ayah. *Li-ulī-l-absār.* "For those who possess **absār.**"

This is the word almost every translation renders as "those of sight," or "those of vision," or "those of insight." All correct. But again, English flattens something.

Notice that *absār* is **plural.**

In Arabic, the singular for sight is *baṣar.* You have *baṣar* — vision. Singular. One faculty. But Allah does not say *li-ulī-l-baṣar* — "those who possess sight." He says *li-ulī-l-absār* — "those who possess **sights**." The plural.

[PAUSE]

Think about that. *Sights.* What does that even mean?

In Arabic, plurals can do work that singulars cannot. They can mean **multiple instances** — many people seeing — but they can also mean **multiple kinds** — different modes of seeing. And the classical commentators on this verse and verses like it have noted that *absār* in the Qurʾān often points beyond just physical vision, into something layered.

Because here is the question this plural forces on us: **how many kinds of seeing are there?**

There is the seeing in your eyes — *baṣar.*

But there is also the seeing in the heart, which the Qurʾān names elsewhere — *baṣīrah.* Inner sight. Discernment.

There is the seeing of patterns — recognizing that the day after every night is not a coincidence but a system.

There is the seeing of meaning — recognizing that the system points beyond itself.

There is the seeing of the One who designed the system.

That is not one *baṣar.* That is *absār* — sights, layered, plural, working together. The one who only has the first kind sees the night and the day and feels nothing. The one who has the layered sights sees the night fold into the day and recognizes something turning underneath both of them.

And here is the gift inside this plural: *Allah is not asking for the inner sight without the outer sight.* He is not telling you to ignore the cosmos and just contemplate. He is telling you to use **all of the seeings you have** — physical, intellectual, spiritual — and let them stack on top of each other until the *ʿibrah* crosses.

The eye sees the dawn break.
The mind recognizes that this happens every single day, on a precise schedule, for billions of years.
The heart asks who is keeping the schedule.

That stack — that is *absār.* Plural. Working together.

The person of one sight sees a sunset and posts a photo.
The person of *absār* sees a sunset and trembles.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge to the Themes

So we have three words. Let me name what they have done together before we move on.

*Yuqallibu* told us: the same verb that turns the cosmos turns your heart. There is one Hand on both hinges.

*ʿIbrah* told us: the lesson is not automatic. The cosmos is performing it twice a day, but it has to *cross* into you. And it only crosses through certain channels.

*Absār* told us: those channels are plural. The crossing happens through stacked seeings — the eye, the mind, the heart, working as one instrument.

Now let us ask what this whole construction reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and how we live inside it.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Architecture of Turning

I want you to think about a person you know — maybe yourself — who is spiritually exhausted from their own inconsistency.

You know who I mean. The person who, every Ramaḍān, makes the same intentions. *This year is going to be different.* This year I will not slide back. This year the prayers will hold. This year I will not return to the same scrolling, the same anger, the same emptiness. And then Shawwāl arrives, and within two weeks they feel like a stranger to the person they were on Laylat al-Qadr.

Or the person who, after a beautiful gathering, after a lecture that cracked something open, after a night of *qiyām* where they finally felt close — wakes up three days later cold. Goes to work. Sits in traffic. Forgets. And by Friday they are wondering if any of it was real.

That person is, I think, almost all of us. Maybe right now.

And what we usually do, when we feel this, is we **moralize the turning.** We tell ourselves the warm heart was the real us, and the cold heart is a betrayal. Or we tell ourselves the cold heart is the real us, and the warm heart was a moment of self-deception. Either way, we treat the alternation itself as evidence of failure.

The cosmos in this ayah is telling us something completely different.

[PAUSE]

The cosmos is not failing when night arrives. The cosmos is not in *spiritual decline* between sunset and dawn. The cosmos is doing **exactly what it was designed to do.** Allah designed a system in which light has meaning *because* darkness exists, and darkness has meaning *because* light exists, and the *turning* between them is not a glitch — it is the engine.

And the verb He uses for that turning is the same verb the Prophet ﷺ used for what Allah does to *us.*

Sit with the implication.

What if the turning of your heart — the warmth that comes and goes, the closeness that arrives and recedes — is not a sign that your faith is broken? What if it is a sign that your heart is **alive**, and that *the same Hand* that maintains the cosmos is maintaining you?

[PAUSE]

This does not mean the lapses do not matter. It does not mean we shrug at the cold heart. The Prophet's ﷺ duʿā is not *yā Muqalliba-l-qulūb, leave my heart alone.* It is *yā Muqalliba-l-qulūb, **thabbit** qalbī* — *O Turner of hearts, **make my heart firm** upon Your religion.* He is asking, while the turning continues, that the *direction* be held.

The metaphor is exact. The night and the day still alternate. But underneath the alternation, there is a **direction** — the earth keeps spinning around the sun. The cycle is not random. The turning is *oriented.*

And this is the prayer of the believer: not that my heart will stop turning — it will not — but that as it turns, it stays oriented toward the One who turns it.

### What This Changes About Tomorrow

I want to bring this down to a specific thing you can carry.

When you next feel cold — and you will — try to do two things you may not have done before.

First, do not panic. Do not interpret the cold as proof that the warmth was fake. Cold is not a verdict. Cold is a phase of a turning that Allah Himself designed and Allah Himself is overseeing. The fact that you *notice* the cold and *grieve* the cold is itself a sign that your heart is still tracking — that the dial is still attached, even if it is reading low right now.

Second, when you feel cold, do not stop. *Yuqallibu* is intensive — it keeps going. The night does not turn into day because the night decided to. It turns because the verb is in Form II — repeated, intensive, constant, applied. Your job is not to *make* the heart warm again. Your job is to *stay in the path of the turning.* Keep showing up to the prayer. Keep opening the muṣḥaf. Keep the small habits that put your heart inside the orbit of His remembrance. The Hand that is turning the cosmos will turn you back. But it will turn you through the channels you keep open.

The Prophet's ﷺ duʿā was not *make me never need turning.* It was *I know You are the One turning. Hold me steady toward You as You turn me.*

That is the prayer of someone who has read this ayah.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two — Why Most of Us Miss It

Now let us turn to the second theme — because the first theme assumed something that the ayah does not assume.

The ayah does not say *"In that is a crossing-over for everyone."* It says *"for those who possess sights."*

That qualifier is doing painful work. Because it tells us that the *ʿibrah* is **available** but not **automatic.** The cosmos performs the crossing twice a day, every day, our entire lives. But most of the crossings sit on the riverbank, untouched.

Why? Because we did not bring the right kind of seeing.

Let me make this concrete. You have probably driven home from work at sunset. The sky was on fire. Pinks, oranges, a slice of gold along the horizon. Maybe you noticed it for two seconds. Maybe you took a photo. Maybe you didn't even look up because you were on a call.

Now, that same sunset — *literally that same sunset* — was, according to this ayah, an *ʿibrah*. A crossing. Allah was, in that moment, demonstrating with His own Hand the verb He uses for turning hearts. And He was offering the lesson, freely, to anyone with the *absār* to receive it.

We did not receive it. The lesson stayed on the other side of the river.

[PAUSE]

This is, I think, one of the quiet tragedies of modern spiritual life. We are not lacking signs. The signs are *everywhere.* What we are lacking is the *plural sight* — the stacked seeing — that lets the signs cross.

We have the first *baṣar.* We see the sunset.

But we do not have the second one — the recognition that this is not random, that it happens on a precise schedule, that the precision has a meaning.

We do not have the third — the recognition that the One who designed the schedule is offering us a piece of Himself in it.

We do not have the fourth — the willingness to let what we just saw rearrange us.

So the cosmos performs and we do not change. The cosmos performs again and we do not change. And by the end of a long life, we have witnessed perhaps thirty thousand turnings of the night into the day, and not one of them has crossed.

That is what *uli-l-absār* warns about. The sights are plural because the seeing is *layered.* And if even one layer is missing, the *ʿibrah* stays out there.

### How to Build the Sight

Now — and this is the hopeful part — *absār* can be **built.** It is not a fixed gift. The Qurʾān itself is, in many places, a training manual for stacked sight. It is constantly pointing at things — clouds, mountains, ships, wombs, stars, water, bees — and asking us to look. *A-fa-lā tubṣirūn?* "Will you not see?" *A-fa-lā tatafakkarūn?* "Will you not reflect?"

Why does Allah keep pointing at the same things? Because He is teaching us to *layer the seeing.* The Qurʾān is, among other things, a long pedagogical exercise in turning *baṣar* into *baṣīrah* — turning eye-sight into heart-sight — by making us look at the world *with the verses in our hand.*

So here is something practical. The next time you witness something the Qurʾān has named as a sign — and *the night-day alternation* is one of the most-named signs in the entire Qurʾān — try this. Stop. Just for a breath. And ask yourself: *what is Allah saying right now, with this sky?*

Not *what does this mean abstractly.* That is academic. Ask: *what is being demonstrated, here, for me?*

If you do this even once a day, for a year — not as a discipline, but as a habit of attention — your *absār* will multiply. You will notice that the same dawn that you have ignored for thirty years is suddenly carrying weight. The crossing will start to happen.

And once one crossing happens, others get easier. Because *ʿibrah* is contagious. The first lesson that crosses opens the path for the next one.

[PAUSE]

This is, ultimately, the gift inside this short ayah. Allah has not hidden Himself behind some impossible sign. He has placed His signature in the most visible thing in the world — the alternation of light and dark, which everyone sees, every day, for free. The signature is right there. We just have to bring all of our seeings.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Let me show you something I held back at the beginning, because it would have been too much then. Now you can see it.

This ayah has only twelve words. Look at how Allah has structured them.

*Yuqallibu* — *Allah* — *the night* — *the day.*

The verb comes first. Then the doer. Then the two things being turned.

In Arabic word order, putting the verb first is the unmarked, default form. But notice what that does *here.* The first thing your ear receives is **the turning.** Before you even know who is doing it. *Something is being flipped.* Then — *Allah.* Now you know who. Then — *layl, nahār.* Now you know what.

The sentence enacts the verb. It performs *yuqallibu* in its own grammar. The reader experiences a turning before they know the cosmology.

And the second half — *inna fī dhālika la-ʿibratan li-ulī-l-absār* — is built on emphasis. *Inna* (truly) plus *la-* (indeed) plus the indefinite *ʿibratan* (a great-and-uncountable ʿibrah). Three layers of emphasis. *In that — truly — there is, indeed, a crossing-over.* Allah is leaning on the table.

Why does He lean? Because He knows we will not believe Him. He knows that if He just said "in that is a lesson," we would nod and look away. So He stacks the emphasis to make us hesitate. *Truly. Indeed. A real ʿibrah.* Look again.

The whole ayah is, in its own grammar, a crossing being offered, with the volume turned up to make sure we hear.

### Bringing It All Together

What we found is this. The *verb* tied the cosmos to the heart with a single thread of grammar — *yuqallibu*, the same word the Prophet ﷺ used in his daily duʿā. The *noun ʿibrah* told us that the lesson is a crossing, not a transmission — it has to leave the sky and enter the chest. The *plural absār* told us that the crossing only happens through stacked seeing — eye, mind, heart, all at once.

And the two themes were the same insight from two directions. From the cosmos: the turning of the heart is not failure, it is design, and the same Hand is on both hinges. From the human: the design is wasted on us if we do not bring the layered sight that lets the lesson cross.

One Hand on the hinge. One sight, stacked, in us. And in between — a daily, free, repeated demonstration that, if we let it cross, will rearrange how we hold our own changing hearts.

### Questions to Carry

Five questions. Take any of them with you.

1. When my heart is cold, am I treating the cold as a verdict, or as a phase of a turning Allah Himself is overseeing?

2. The Prophet ﷺ asked Allah to *stabilize* him while being turned, not to *stop* the turning. What does it look like, this week, to ask for stabilization rather than for stillness?

3. How many sunsets have I witnessed and let pass without the *ʿibrah* crossing? What would it take to let the next one cross?

4. Where in my life is my *baṣar* — my outer eye — fine, but my *baṣīrah* — my inner sight — dim? What is one sign Allah keeps placing in front of me that I keep missing?

5. If the same Hand turns the cosmos and turns my heart, what does it change about how I experience my own inconsistency?

### One Sentence

The Hand that turns night into day, twice a day, every day of your life, is the same Hand on the hinge of your heart — and the demonstration is offered freely, twice a day, to anyone willing to let the lesson cross the river.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow, somewhere, the day will turn into the night, or the night into the day. You will watch it from a window, or a car, or a doorway. Most likely you will not look up. But if you do, even for a breath, remember:

That is not background. That is signature.

The same verb. The same Hand. Twice a day. Free.

All it asks is that you bring more than one sight.

### Du'ā

*O Turner of hearts, You who flip the night into the day with one hand and turn our hearts with the other — let the crossing happen in us. Stabilize our hearts in Your direction as You turn them. Do not let another sunset of our lives pass with the lesson sitting on the other side of the river. Make us, by Your mercy, **of those who possess sights.***

*Āmīn.*
