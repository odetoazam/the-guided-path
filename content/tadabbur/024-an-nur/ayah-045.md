# Every Creature From Water: The Threefold Walk and the Dignity of Minhum

## Surah An-Nur, Ayah 45

> وَٱللَّهُ خَلَقَ كُلَّ دَآبَّةٍ مِّن مَّآءٍ ۖ فَمِنْهُم مَّن يَمْشِى عَلَىٰ بَطْنِهِۦ وَمِنْهُم مَّن يَمْشِى عَلَىٰ رِجْلَيْنِ وَمِنْهُم مَّن يَمْشِى عَلَىٰٓ أَرْبَعٍ ۚ يَخْلُقُ ٱللَّهُ مَا يَشَآءُ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ
>
> *Wa-llāhu khalaqa kulla dābbatin min mā'in. Fa-minhum man yamshī ʿalā baṭnihi, wa-minhum man yamshī ʿalā rijlayn, wa-minhum man yamshī ʿalā arbaʿ. Yakhluqu llāhu mā yashā'u. Inna llāha ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr.*
>
> "And Allah created every walking creature from water. Among them is one that walks on its belly, and among them is one that walks on two legs, and among them is one that walks on four. Allah creates whatever He wills. Indeed, Allah is over all things All-Powerful."

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

Surah An-Nur has been doing something difficult. It has been legislating the most intimate corners of human life — accusations of unchastity, the etiquette of entering homes, the lowering of gaze, the pulling of veils, the marriage of the unmarried, the freedom of the enslaved. It has been working at the level of the body, the household, the street, the courtroom.

And then, around ayah 35, it lifts off. The parable of light. Niche, lamp, glass, blessed tree. The light of Allah upon the heart.

By the time we reach ayah 43, the surah has zoomed all the way out. We are no longer in the bedroom or the marketplace. We are looking at the sky.

Ayah 43: clouds gathering, joining, stacking — and rain falling from their fissures, hail striking whom He wills, lightning that almost takes the sight away.

Ayah 44: Allah turns night and day, and in this is a lesson for those who have eyes.

And now, ayah 45.

### The Hook

Allah has just shown us water from the sky. He has just shown us the alternation of darkness and light. And now He says: every walking creature was made from water.

The same water that fell from the clouds in 43 — He is now telling us this is the substance of every dābbah, every walking thing, every snake on its belly and every camel on four and every human on two.

But notice: He doesn't say "*and from this water We made them*." He doesn't connect 43 to 45 explicitly. He leaves the connection for the listener to make.

And listen carefully to what He does say. He says *fa-minhum*. *Among them*. Not *minhā* — the pronoun you'd use for things, for animals, for objects. *Minhum*. The pronoun you'd use for **people**.

Allah is referring to the snake crawling on its belly with the same pronoun He uses for human beings.

[PAUSE]

Why? What does it mean that the Creator of every walking creature — when He divides them into three — uses the pronoun reserved for persons?

That is the question we will sit with.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Min Mā'in* — From Water (Without "The")

Let me ask you a small question first.

If I tell you "I drank water" — that's one thing. If I tell you "I drank **the** water" — you immediately ask, *which water? what water?* The article "the" makes water specific. It points to a known instance. Without the article, water becomes generic — a kind of substance, a principle, a category.

Arabic has the same distinction. *Al-mā'* is "the water" — a particular body of water, the one we just talked about. *Mā'* alone — without the *al-* — is "water" as such. As a kind. As a substance.

Now look at what Allah says: *khalaqa kulla dābbatin **min mā'**in*. He created every walking creature **from water** — indefinite, with no article.

He doesn't say *min al-mā'*. He doesn't say "from **the** water" (the water we just mentioned in 43, falling from the clouds). He doesn't say "from **the** water of life" or "from **the** primordial water."

He says *min mā'in*. From water. As a principle.

Stay with this for a moment.

What does it mean to be made *from water* in the abstract? Water is the substance that has no shape of its own. Water takes the shape of whatever vessel holds it. Water is the medium of transformation — chemistry happens in water, life happens in water, every cell in every body is mostly water.

When Allah says *min mā'in*, He is naming the **fluid principle** — the part of creation that is shapeless until it is shaped. The substance that is most pliable, most receptive, most ready to become.

And He is saying: every dābbah — every walking creature — comes from that.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Modern science would not have surprised you with this. We now know every cell on earth is mostly water. We know the first single-celled organisms emerged in water. We know the embryo, regardless of species, develops in fluid. We know dehydration is death.

But the Quran is not making a biological claim that has to wait fourteen centuries for confirmation. It is making a **theological** claim that the biology happens to ratify.

The theological claim is this: **the substance of every walking thing is the most receptive substance there is.**

You — the listener — are not made from rock. You are not made from iron, although your blood carries iron. You are not made from fire, although there is heat in you. The Quran says the substance you trace back to is *mā'*: the substance that takes shape from outside itself.

Which means: at the level of your origin, you are pliable. At the level of your origin, you are something that received form rather than imposing it. The form you have — the eyes that see, the hands that grasp, the spine that stands — was not negotiated by the water you came from. It was given to the water you came from.

Notice what the indefinite *mā'* does. If Allah had said *al-mā'* — "the water" — He would have pointed to one specific reservoir. But He doesn't. He points to water as a kind. Which means: this isn't about a particular pond at the dawn of time. It is about every cell in your body right now being a vessel of fluid that was given its form.

You did not author your shape. You are not self-constructed. The water you are made of did not decide to become a person. Allah created every dābbah *min mā'in* — from water that He shaped.

[PAUSE]

This is the first humbling. Before we even reach the threefold structure, before we even reach the dignity of *minhum*, the very first phrase has placed us in our position. We are creatures whose substance was *received form*. The fluid in us did not negotiate its shape.

Now hold that. We are about to see what He shaped that water into.

### Element Two: *Fa-Minhum* — The Pronoun Reserved for Persons

Here is where Arabic does something that English struggles to translate.

In Arabic, when you refer back to a group of things, the pronoun you choose depends on **what kind of things they are**. For non-rational plurals — animals, objects, abstractions — the standard practice is to refer back with the singular feminine. So you would expect: *fa-minhā* — "among them" (literally, "among her," because the plural of non-rational things is grammatically feminine singular).

For rational beings — humans, angels, jinn — you use the masculine plural. *Fa-minhum* — "among them" (literally, "among the men-they").

The pronoun *minhum* is the pronoun of **personhood**.

Now read the ayah again. *Wa-llāhu khalaqa kulla dābbatin* — "Allah created every walking creature." A *dābbah* is, by linguistic root *d-b-b*, anything that moves on the earth — and the noun is grammatically feminine. The expected pronoun, when Allah comes to subdivide them, would be *minhā*.

But that's not what He says.

He says *fa-**minhum** man yamshī ʿalā baṭnih* — "and **among them** is one who walks on its belly." Then *wa-**minhum** man yamshī ʿalā rijlayn* — "and **among them** is one who walks on two legs." Then *wa-**minhum** man yamshī ʿalā arbaʿ* — "and **among them** is one who walks on four."

Three times: *minhum*. The pronoun of persons.

And one more thing. Look at the relative pronoun. He says *minhum **man*** yamshī. *Man* is the relative pronoun for **rational beings** — "the one who," "whoever," used of persons. The non-rational equivalent would be *mā* — "that which."

So Allah is doubling the rhetorical move. He uses the personal pronoun *hum* and then the personal relative *man*. Twice over, He grammatically dignifies every walking creature — the snake, the bird, the human, the camel — with the language reserved for persons.

[PAUSE]

The classical grammarians have a name for this. They call it *taghlīb al-ʿāqil* — "the preponderance of the rational." When a category includes both rational beings (like humans) and non-rational beings (like snakes), the Quran sometimes uses the rational form to cover the whole. The presence of the rational tips the grammar.

That is one way to read it. The category *dābbah* includes humans (we walk on the earth too, after all). And the human presence in the category causes Allah to use the human pronoun for the whole.

But notice what this means even on that minimal reading. **The presence of even one rational being in a category lifts the entire category into the language of persons.** The snake gets the pronoun of persons not because the snake is rational, but because the human is in the same sentence.

This is staggering enough on its own. The dignity of the human raises the grammar of every creature you share the category with.

But there is a deeper reading too.

Consider: Allah is describing every walking creature on earth as being known by Him, attended to by Him, individuated by Him. Each is *one who walks* — *man yamshī*. Each is named by what it does, by its mode of being, by its way of moving through creation. Allah is not looking at the creatures as a mass. He is looking at them as individual subjects, each one doing what it does.

And from the perspective of the One who created every dābbah from water — from the perspective of the Creator — there is no creature that is merely an *it*. Every creature is a *who*. Every walking thing is a *one who walks*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that.

The snake on its belly is not, in Allah's reference, a *what*. It is a *who*. The bird with its two legs is not a *what*. It is a *who*. The camel on its four is not a *what*. It is a *who*.

We — humans — generally only afford that grammar to our own kind. We say "she" of our pets sometimes, "he" of a horse we love, but we know we are stretching the language. We know that, by default, the snake is an *it* and the donkey is an *it* and the worm is an *it*.

Allah does not stretch the language. He just uses the language of persons for them all — without comment, without explanation, without softening.

Because to Him, they are.

### What This Reveals About How We Should See

A digression here, because it cannot wait.

When Allah refers to the creature on its belly as *minhum*, He is teaching us how Allah's eyes see. He is teaching us that the dignity we feel about *ourselves* — the felt sense that we are someone, not something — that dignity is not a privilege we earned. It is the standard view from above.

Every walking creature is someone to its Creator.

Now consider how the Prophet ﷺ taught us to relate to creatures. The hadith of the prostitute who was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog. The hadith of the woman who was punished for confining a cat until it died. The forbidding of using a living animal as a target. The command that one should sharpen the blade so the slaughtered creature does not suffer.

These are not arbitrary rulings. They are downstream of the grammar of *fa-minhum*. If the Creator refers to that animal as *one who*, then the human cannot relate to it as a *what*. The grammar of God informs the ethics of His servants.

[PAUSE]

This is the second humbling. The first was: you did not author your form. The second is: every other walking creature has the same dignity of "one who" before Allah that you have. You are not the only *who* in creation. You are *one* of many *whos*.

And the asymmetry — the truly trembling thing — is that you are the one whose grammar caused the elevation. *Taghlīb al-ʿāqil*. The rational tipped the language. The presence of the human in the sentence is part of why the snake gets called *minhum*.

The dignity rises *with you*, not above you.

### Element Three: *Yakhluqu Allāhu Mā Yashā'u* — The Tense That Doesn't Close

There is one more grammatical move I need you to notice.

The ayah opens with *khalaqa* — past tense, perfect aspect. "He created." A completed action. He created every walking creature from water. Done.

Then it gives the threefold structure: belly, two, four.

And then it says: *yakhluqu Allāhu mā yashā'u*. "Allah creates whatever He wills."

Notice the shift. *Khalaqa* (past, completed) → *yakhluqu* (present, ongoing). The verb has changed tense.

Why?

Here's the thing about verbs in Arabic. The perfect (*khalaqa*) describes an action as a closed, finished event. The imperfect (*yakhluqu*) describes an action that is in progress, repeated, or open-ended.

So Allah is making two distinct claims in two consecutive sentences.

**Claim one**: *I have already created every walking creature from water.* (Past, finished. The category as you know it is complete.)

**Claim two**: *Allah creates whatever He wills.* (Present, ongoing. Creation is not over.)

[PAUSE]

This is enormous and easy to miss.

Most people, reading 24:45, take it as a description of completed creation: "Look at all the creatures Allah has already made." A piece of natural theology. A celebration of what is.

But the second sentence doesn't say that. The second sentence is in the imperfect. *Yakhluqu* — "He creates" — present tense, ongoing.

Allah is not saying *I created*. He is saying *I create*. The past is past. The present is open.

And what is the object? *Mā yashā'u*. "Whatever He wills."

There is no specification. No category. No constraint. He creates **whatever** He wills. The clause is deliberately empty of content. It does not say "Allah creates more creatures" or "Allah creates new species." It says: whatever He wills, He creates.

This is the door left open.

[PAUSE]

What does it mean theologically that Allah's creative act is in the present tense?

It means that creation is not a one-time event in a deep past from which we have been falling forward in inertia ever since. It means that **right now**, while you are listening, Allah's creative will is active. He is creating whatever He wills, in this moment, without restriction.

Your own breath in this moment — is that a residue of an act completed long ago, or is it a fresh creation now? The Arabic verb *yakhluqu* says: now.

The moisture in the cells of your eyes as they move across these words — is that a leftover of the original *min mā'*, or is it being created in this instant? *Yakhluqu* says: in this instant.

[PAUSE]

This is the third grammatical move. Allah uses the perfect to describe the inventory of creatures (so we won't be surprised by what we find). Then He uses the imperfect to remind us that the *creating* is not finished — only this *creation* is finished. The act continues.

And He closes by sealing it: *inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*. Indeed, Allah is over **every thing** All-Powerful. *Qadīr* — the intensive form *faʿīl*, meaning thoroughly, completely able. Not just *qādir* (capable). *Qadīr* (decisively, intrinsically capable). And not over *some* things. Over *kulli shay'in* — every thing.

The seal is a reminder: do not read the threefold structure as an exhaustive list. Do not read the past tense as a closure. Do not read the diversity of creatures as the limit of what Allah can shape from water. He is *over every thing All-Powerful*.

The categories you see are not the categories He is bound by.

### The Bridge

So here is what the language has shown us, before we even arrive at the themes.

First: the substance you trace back to is fluid. You did not author your form.

Second: every walking creature, including the one on its belly, is a *who* to its Creator. The grammar of God treats every dābbah as a person.

Third: the act of creation is in the present tense. Allah is not finished. He creates whatever He wills.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Diversity Inside the Sameness

Have you ever stood at a family reunion and looked around the room?

If you have, you know a strange thing. You know that some people in the room look almost like you — the same nose, the same chin line, the same hair pattern. And other people in the room look almost nothing like you — different complexion, different build, different facial geometry. And yet, somehow, you are all from the same source. Some grandparent, some great-grandparent, was the well from which all of you came.

The diversity is shocking and the unity is shocking, and they are the same shock.

Now extend that. Step out of your family and into all of humanity. Tall, short, dark, light, broad, narrow — all from one pair, says the Quran. From Adam and Hawwa.

Now extend it again. Step out of humanity and into all walking creatures. Snake, sparrow, human, horse. All — Allah says — from water.

[PAUSE]

This is the move 24:45 wants you to make.

Look at how He builds it. *Khalaqa kulla dābbatin min mā'in.* One sentence. One unified origin. One substance.

Then: *fa-minhum... wa-minhum... wa-minhum.* Three lines. Three modes. Total divergence.

The structure of the ayah enacts the truth it describes. **Sameness at the source. Difference at the surface.** The water is one. The walking is many.

And here is what Allah is teaching by this structure.

There is a temptation, when you encounter difference, to assume separateness at the source. The serpent who slithers seems to come from a different reality than the camel who plods. The fish in the deep seems to come from a different cosmos than the bird in the high. The Hindu and the Muslim and the atheist and the worshiper of the sun all seem, at the surface, to be living different lives in different metaphysical zones.

But the ayah says: no. The diversity is real. Some walk on bellies. Some walk on two. Some walk on four. The differences are not illusory. They are not an embarrassment to be explained away.

But the source is one.

*Min mā'.* From water.

The differences in mode of motion do not contradict the unity at the substance.

[PAUSE]

What does this teach about how we hold the difference of others?

The temptation, when you encounter someone whose mode of life is alien to you, is to other them at the metaphysical level. To assume that they are a different *kind* of being. To treat the differences in walk as differences in essence.

The ayah refuses this. Even the snake on its belly — the creature in the Quran most associated with deception, with the cursed gait, with concealment — is *minhum*. Even the snake is one of them. From the same water. With the same dignity of "one who."

If Allah does this for the snake, what is your justification for refusing to do it for your sister whose path looks different from yours?

[PAUSE]

There is a second movement here.

Notice that the ayah lists three modes of walking but does not exhaust the modes of being. It does not mention birds (who fly more than walk). It does not mention fish (who swim, not walk). It does not mention insects (who walk on six). The threefold is not a taxonomy. It is a gesture toward the **range**.

The pattern *fa-minhum... wa-minhum... wa-minhum* in the Quran often signifies "the full spread" rather than "the complete enumeration." Three is the smallest number that can establish a pattern — one is solitary, two is a pair, three is a *kind of thing*. By giving us three, Allah is telling us: this is the **shape** of creaturely diversity. Some go low, some stand upright, some spread their weight. The shape of the diversity is real. The exhaustive list is not the point.

And what does this teach you?

That when you look at the room of human beings around you — your family, your community, your city, your species — you should expect a *spread*. You should not expect uniformity. You should not be alarmed when one walks on the belly and another on two and another on four. The spread is the design.

What you should attend to is not whether everyone walks the same. What you should attend to is whether they all came from the same water — whether they share a creator, whether they bear a soul, whether they are *minhum* before Allah.

[PAUSE]

Let me bring this to ground.

You are sitting somewhere right now. There is someone in your life whose mode of being you find difficult. Maybe a sibling whose choices baffle you. Maybe a coworker whose values feel alien. Maybe a relative whose religious practice looks different from yours, or even absent.

The ayah is not asking you to pretend the difference doesn't exist. The snake walks on its belly. The human walks on two. The difference is real.

But the ayah is asking you to refuse to other them at the substance.

They came from the same water. They are *minhum*. To Allah, they are *one who walks* — a *who*, not a *what*. The grammar of the Creator does not allow you to treat them as outside the category of dignity.

The application is concrete. The next time you find yourself slipping into thinking of someone as a *what* — as a category, as a problem, as a type — the ayah is asking you to reach for *who*. Reach for the personhood. Reach for the water you both come from.

This is not sentimental. This is grammatical.

### Theme Two: The Open Tense — Living Inside Ongoing Creation

Now I want to take you somewhere harder.

We talked about the verb shift — *khalaqa* (past) to *yakhluqu* (present). Past creation closed. Present creation open.

I want to spend the rest of our time on the implication of this for **how you understand your own life.**

[PAUSE]

There is a way of thinking about ourselves that is very common and very damaging. It goes like this.

I am the result of things that happened. My parents made me. My childhood shaped me. My traumas wired me. My genes coded me. My culture installed me. By the time I became aware of myself, I was already a finished product. The ingredients had been mixed. The dough had been baked. I am the result of a creation that ended a long time ago.

If this is your background assumption, then growth becomes negotiation with a fixed thing. You try to optimize the bread you have been given. You try to compensate for the ingredients you got. You try to redecorate the inside of a building whose walls were poured before you arrived.

This is exhausting. And — the ayah suggests — it is also wrong.

[PAUSE]

Listen again to the second sentence: *yakhluqu Allāhu mā yashā'u*. **Allah creates whatever He wills.** Present tense. Ongoing. Open.

The Creator is not in the past tense with respect to you. Yes, He created you in the past — past tense, *khalaqa* — at the level of your initial substance. But He continues to create — present tense, *yakhluqu* — at the level of what comes next.

Your tomorrow is not extracted from your yesterday. Your tomorrow is being **created** by the same Hand that created the snake on its belly and the bird on its two and the camel on its four.

And He creates *whatever He wills*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the analogy.

Imagine you have been told, your whole life, that you are a sculpture carved from a single block of stone. The block was quarried at your birth. The carving was done by the time you turned twenty. Now you are a finished sculpture, and your work — for the rest of your life — is to keep the dust off and prevent erosion.

That is the first picture of the self. The closed sculpture.

Now imagine, instead, that you are a piece of pottery being thrown on a wheel. The wheel is turning. The clay is wet. Something has been shaped already — there is a base, there is a curve, there is the beginning of a form. But the potter's hands are still on you. The wheel is still spinning. What you become next is not extracted from what you have been so far. What you become next is being **created** in the present tense.

That is the second picture. The open vessel.

The Quran's grammar — *yakhluqu mā yashā'u* — is the second picture.

[PAUSE]

This changes everything about the inner life.

If you are the closed sculpture, then your past is the cause of your present, and your present is the cause of your future, and the chain runs forward by inertia. You are responsible for staying on track within a system whose parameters were set in advance.

If you are the open vessel, then your past is not the cause of your future. The Creator is the cause of your future. And He creates whatever He wills.

This means: the parts of you that you thought were finished are not finished. The patterns you thought were locked are not locked. The capacities you thought you didn't have can still be given to you. Allah, in this moment, is creating — and what He creates next is not bound by what He created last.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here. I am not saying — the Quran is not saying — that effort doesn't matter, or that consequence doesn't follow from cause, or that you can magically become anything you wish. The world has lawful structure. *Sunan Allāh*. The patterns of cause and effect are real.

I am saying — and the Quran is saying — that the *source* of those patterns is in present tense. Allah is not bound by what He has done. He creates whatever He wills.

And this means that any prayer you make to Him for transformation is being addressed to a Creator whose creating is *now*. Not a Creator whose creating was *then* and who now manages a closed system. A Creator whose creating is *now*. A Creator whose hands are still on the wheel.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about your tomorrow?

It changes the address of your hope. You cannot hope for transformation from a system that was sealed shut at your birth. You can hope for transformation from the One whose verb is in the imperfect.

The water you came from — *min mā'in* — was given form. Past tense. *Khalaqa.*

But the form you will have a year from now, the heart you will have a decade from now, the soul you will meet your Lord with — that water is still being shaped. Present tense. *Yakhluqu.*

He creates whatever He wills.

[PAUSE — major pause]

The application here is concrete and demanding.

When you make duʿā tonight — when you raise your hands and ask Allah for something you have stopped believing is possible for you — make it knowing that the Hand you are addressing is currently shaping creation. Currently. The verb is in the present tense.

The thing you have given up on in yourself — the patience you can't seem to develop, the discipline you can't seem to maintain, the kindness that keeps slipping out of your grasp — these are not closed sculptures. These are wet clay on a turning wheel, and the Potter is still touching them.

The duʿā is not asking Him to break the seal on a finished work. The duʿā is asking Him to shape what He is already shaping.

This is the difference between begging and conversation. Begging assumes the door is locked. Conversation knows the door is open.

*Yakhluqu Allāhu mā yashā'u.* He creates whatever He wills.

You are inside the act.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Let me now show you something you couldn't see at the beginning.

The ayah is built in three movements, each of which mirrors the others.

**Movement One**: *Wa-llāhu khalaqa kulla dābbatin min mā'in.* The single source. Allah, water, every creature. **Unity at origin.**

**Movement Two**: *Fa-minhum... wa-minhum... wa-minhum.* The threefold spread. Belly, two, four. **Diversity in expression.**

**Movement Three**: *Yakhluqu Allāhu mā yashā'u. Inna llāha ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr.* The ongoing creating. Whatever He wills. Over everything All-Powerful. **The open future.**

Notice the architecture. Past unity → present diversity → ongoing creating. The verb tenses mirror the movement: *khalaqa* (past, closed), *yamshī* (present, current state), *yakhluqu* (present, ongoing). The ayah moves from a sealed origin to a manifest present to an open future, and each phase is anchored by a different aspect of Allah's relationship to the dābbah.

The frame is bookended by the divine name. *Wa-llāhu* at the start. *Inna llāha* at the end. The verse is held inside the name of God like the water of every creature is held inside the form He gave it.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar said: water without article (substance, principle), personal pronoun for animals (dignity of "who"), present tense for creating (open future).

The themes said: every walking thing came from one source (so honor the difference of those around you as variation within sameness), and the act of creation is now (so live as wet clay, not finished sculpture).

The grammar is the diagnosis. The themes are the prescription.

What ties them is the simple, transformative truth: **you are inside an ongoing act of creation, in a category of creatures who are all "who" before their Creator, and the substance you are made of is not yours to control — only to receive.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life have you been thinking of yourself as a finished sculpture rather than wet clay on the wheel? What part of you have you decided is locked, that the Quran's verb suggests is still open?

2. Who in your life — what relative, what coworker, what stranger — have you been treating as a *what* rather than a *who*? What would change if you used Allah's grammar for them?

3. The water in your body right now — is it residue of a past act, or is it part of an ongoing creating? Which way of feeling it is more honest, when you actually attend?

4. The threefold structure (belly, two, four) is not exhaustive. What in your life have you been treating as exhaustive — *these* are my options, *these* are my limits — that Allah's "*yakhluqu mā yashā'u*" might actually open?

5. If every walking creature is *minhum* — if the snake is "one who" before Allah — what does this say about how you should treat the animals in your immediate care, your environment, your food chain?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Every walking creature traces back to fluid given form by the same Creator who is, in this present moment, still creating whatever He wills — which means the dignity of every "who" is shared and the future of every form is open.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, do this one thing.

Place your hand on your chest. Feel the water in you — the moisture, the warmth, the pulse moving fluid through fluid.

Say, quietly: *min mā'.* From water.

Then say: *yakhluqu Allāhu mā yashā'u.* He creates whatever He wills.

Then say one thing — one duʿā, one ask — that you have stopped believing was possible for you.

You are speaking to a Creator whose verb is in the present tense, about a substance that is still being shaped, in a category of creatures every one of whom is *minhum* before Him.

Speak as such.

### Du'ā

Allāhumma, You who created every walking creature from water, who refer to the snake on its belly with the grammar of persons, who create whatever You will in the present tense — shape what is still wet in me. Let me see every creature You made as *one who*, not as *what*. And keep my hope addressed to the Hand that is, right now, still on the wheel.

Āmīn.
