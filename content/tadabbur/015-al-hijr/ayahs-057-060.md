# What Is Your Mission, O Messengers? Rescue, Exclusion, and the Measured Fate of Lot's Wife

## Surah Al-Hijr (15:57-60)

[ayah:15:57] قَالَ فَمَا خَطْبُكُمْ أَيُّهَا ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ

[ayah:15:58] قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّآ أُرْسِلْنَآ إِلَىٰ قَوْمٍ مُّجْرِمِينَ

[ayah:15:59] إِلَّآ ءَالَ لُوطٍ إِنَّا لَمُنَجُّوهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ

[ayah:15:60] إِلَّا ٱمْرَأَتَهُۥ قَدَّرْنَآ ۙ إِنَّهَا لَمِنَ ٱلْغَـٰبِرِينَ

*Qala fama khatbukum ayyuha al-mursalun.*

*Qalu inna ursilna ila qawmin mujrimin.*

*Illa ala Lut. Inna la-munajjuhum ajma'in.*

*Illa imra'atahu qaddarna innaha la-mina al-ghabirin.*

**He said: Then what is your mission, O messengers? They said: Indeed, we have been sent to a criminal people, except the family of Lut; indeed, we will surely save them all, except his wife. We decreed that she would surely be among those who remain behind.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

Not every visitor arrives for the same reason.

The same angelic company that brings a home glad tidings can also carry another home's judgment. The same arrival that opens one future can close another.

[PAUSE]

That is what gives this passage its weight. Ibrahim has just been told not to despair of Allah's mercy — as al-Muyassar frames the words just before, none despairs of his Lord's mercy except the erring who have turned away from the path of truth. And then, immediately, Ibrahim asks the obvious question: what is your mission? The answer reveals that the guests are moving through the earth with a double purpose. There will be rescue, but not without judgment. There will be salvation, but not without exclusion.

The hook of the passage is this: **Al-Hijr shows that divine intervention is never vague goodwill spread equally over moral reality; it arrives with mission, distinction, rescue for some, and a measured decree that leaves others behind despite outward nearness.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Fama Khatbukum**: Ibrahim Asks About the Matter Beneath the Visit

Ibrahim says:

**fama khatbukum ayyuha al-mursalun**

What is your **khatb**, O messengers?

[PAUSE]

The word **khatb** is more than a casual "what's up?" It points to a serious matter, a consequential affair, something weighty enough to deserve direct naming. The classical readings confirm this gravity: al-Tabari glosses the question as *ma sha'nukum, ma amrukum* — what is your affair, your matter — and al-Muyassar sharpens it further as *what grave matter (al-amr al-khatir) has brought you from Allah?* Ibrahim has understood that these are no ordinary guests and no ordinary tidings. There is a purpose still unfolding beneath the conversation.

This is a beautiful quality in Ibrahim's prophetic awareness. He does not stop at the gift that reached his house; he asks about the wider mission. One might read this as a refusal to reduce divine action to how it touches him personally — a quiet spiritual refinement — though it is worth being honest that the classical glosses render his question neutrally, as a simple inquiry into their errand, and do not name selflessness as its motive. The reading is ours to draw, gently, not theirs to assert.

Still, the shape of the question is instructive. Many people can notice Allah's mercy once it touches their own life, but they lose sight of the broader moral world in which that mercy is moving. Ibrahim's question keeps the lens wide.

### 2. **Qawmin Mujrimin**: The Passage Names a People by Their Crime

The answer comes:

**inna ursilna ila qawmin mujrimin**

We have been sent to a criminal people.

[PAUSE]

The key word is **mujrimin**, from the root **j-r-m**. The root carries the sense of cutting off, committing crime, becoming guilty through active offense. The ayah does not describe them merely as mistaken, confused, or unfortunate. They are named through moral criminality — and the tafsir makes plain what that criminality was. Al-Tabari says these are a people who had *acquired* disbelief in Allah (*qad iktasabu al-kufr billah*), and al-Muyassar names them as *mushrikin dallin*, associators gone astray. The verb al-Tabari chooses is striking: *iktasabu*, they earned it, they worked for it. Their guilt is not a misfortune that befell them; it is something the same active hand of the root **j-r-m** reached out and took.

That matters because the scene refuses sentimental fog. Divine judgment is not descending upon a morally blank population. It is being sent toward a people whose condition has been named and, in the classical reading, actively chosen.

And notice the passive dignity of the messengers' self-description: **ursilna**. We have been sent. Their movement is not freelance zeal. They are emissaries under command.

The earth, then, is not abandoned to improvisation. Mercy and judgment move through it by mission.

### 3. **Illa Aal Lut ... Qaddarna**: Nearness Does Not Save Without Alignment

Then comes the first exception:

**illa aal Lut. inna la-munajjuhum ajma'in**

Except the family of Lut. We will surely save them all.

[PAUSE]

The verb-form here intensifies assurance. **La-munajjuhum** carries emphatic rescue. Deliverance is not a possibility hanging in uncertainty. It is a determined act.

And here the classical readers add something quietly decisive about what "family" even means. Al-Tabari does not gloss **aal Lut** as merely his blood relatives; he glosses it as *those who followed Lut upon the religion he was on* — his people in faith. Al-Muyassar says the same in different words: Lut *and his household who believed in him*. In other words, the very word "family" is already being measured by faith, not by lineage. That reframing is the hinge of the whole passage.

Because then comes the second exception:

**illa imra'atahu**

Except his wife.

The shock of that exception never really softens. The household is named for rescue, and then one person from inside the nearest social circle is cut out of the promise. And the tafsir does not leave her exclusion vague: al-Muyassar names her plainly as *al-kafira*, the disbelieving one, and Ibn Kathir says she was among those already doomed. She shares the roof but not the religion — and it is the religion, not the roof, that the word **aal** was tracking all along.

The passage explains it with:

**qaddarna**

We decreed.

From the root **q-d-r**, to measure, determine, apportion, assign with exactness. Her fate is not accidental. Al-Muyassar unfolds the verb as *we decreed, by the command of Allah, her destruction along with those who remained behind in the punishment.* It is measured judgment, issued under divine command.

Then:

**innaha la-mina al-ghabirin**

She is surely among those who remain behind.

The **ghabirin**, in the tafsir, are precisely those who *stay behind in the punishment* — not the survivors who escape, but the remnant left in the ruin. The phrase is devastating because it reveals that physical nearness to a prophet does not itself produce salvation. You can stand close to truth geographically — even share a prophet's own home — and remain inwardly aligned with those under judgment.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Outward Proximity to the Righteous Is Not the Same as Inward Loyalty to Truth

This passage is one of the Quran's most sobering corrections to false security.

Lot's wife is not a stranger to revelation. She is not hearing of prophecy for the first time from a distant land. She lives inside the orbit of a prophet's house. She has proximity, exposure, contact, access.

[PAUSE]

And still, she is excluded.

Why? The tafsir has already told us. When al-Tabari defined **aal Lut** as those who *followed him upon his religion*, he quietly established the whole logic of this scene: belonging was never about the address. Salvation is not inherited from location alone. It is not guaranteed by being near good people, near good spaces, near good language, near good reputations, or near good projects. The heart must actually align. His wife had the nearest possible proximity a human being could have — and because she remained, in al-Muyassar's word, *al-kafira*, the closeness saved her from nothing.

This is painful but necessary. Many of us are tempted to build identity from adjacency. I come from this family. I sit with these people. I work in this environment. I know this vocabulary. I am seen around the right circles. And quietly the soul treats that nearness as if it were the same thing as obedience.

The ayah cuts through that illusion. A person may live near truth — even share its roof — and still remain among those left behind.

### Theme 2: Allah's Saving Mercy Is Precise, Not Vague

At the same time, the passage is full of mercy.

It does not merely say that some survive somehow. It names rescue clearly and emphatically.

**Inna la-munajjuhum ajma'in.**

We will surely save them all.

[PAUSE]

This is not a weak mercy, and it is not random. Allah knows exactly whom He is rescuing and exactly whom He is not. The same precision that can be frightening in judgment is deeply consoling in salvation. Nothing sincere is lost in the sweep of divine justice. No believer disappears into collateral damage. No faithful heart is accidentally counted with a criminal people. The word **ajma'in** — all of them — carries its own comfort: every last one who belonged by faith was gathered into the rescue, none overlooked.

That is especially important in scenes of upheaval. When judgment is descending on a society, it can seem as though everything is collapsing together. The ayah reassures the heart that Allah's knowledge does not blur under crisis. He distinguishes. He saves. He decrees with exactness.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask not only, "Am I near truth?" but, "Am I actually with it?" Not only, "Do I live in the orbit of righteousness?" but, "Have I inwardly joined it?" The difference between those two questions is the difference between being carried in the rescue and being left behind in the same town.

## Closing Synthesis

Ibrahim asks the messengers about the serious matter beneath their visit. They answer that they have been sent to a criminal people. Then the passage makes two cuts: first, an exception for the family of Lut — understood by the classical readers as those who followed him in faith — with emphatic promised rescue; second, an exception from that exception, because his wife, remaining in disbelief, has been decreed to stay behind.

[PAUSE]

The result is a passage of precision. Judgment is targeted. Rescue is targeted. Nearness is tested. And decree is shown not as abstraction but as exact moral distinction in history.

### Questions to Carry

- Where might you be mistaking nearness to good environments for actual loyalty to truth?
- Do you ask only how Allah's mercy touches your life, or do you stay awake to the wider mission of justice and rescue in the world?
- What forms of outward religious adjacency have become a false source of security for you?
- If truth were to sort the room today, what in you would actually be counted with it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**This passage teaches that Allah's rescue is exact and merciful, but also that outward nearness to a prophet, a household, or a righteous environment cannot save a heart that has remained inwardly with the wrong people.**

### Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let us be content with nearness to truth while our hearts remain elsewhere. Count us among those You actually save, not merely among those who lived beside the saved. Give us inward loyalty to what is right, and protect us from being left behind through hidden compromise or false security.*
