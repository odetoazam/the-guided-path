# Do Not Disgrace Me in Front of My Guests: Desire, Refusal, and Wandering in Intoxication

## Surah Al-Hijr (15:67-72)

[ayah:15:67] وَجَآءَ أَهْلُ ٱلْمَدِينَةِ يَسْتَبْشِرُونَ

[ayah:15:68] قَالَ إِنَّ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ ضَيْفِى فَلَا تَفْضَحُونِ

[ayah:15:69] وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَلَا تُخْزُونِ

## Introduction

There is a kind of public corruption that no longer hides.

It does not sneak. It gathers. It arrives excited. It feels entitled to another person's shame. It hears appeals to God, honor, and decency as obstacles rather than truths.

[PAUSE]

That is the atmosphere of this passage. The city — Sadum, the town of Lut, as al-Tabari names it — comes not grieving but celebrating, streaming toward Lut's house the moment word spreads that he has taken in handsome guests. Ibn Kathir preserves a detail that sharpens the whole scene: Lut is pleading here *before* he knows his guests are messengers of Allah. To him they are vulnerable travellers under his roof, and he stands between them and a mob with nothing but his voice. He pleads first from hospitality, then from taqwa, then from dignity. None of it penetrates. The crowd speaks as though the prophet himself is the one who has crossed a line. And then Allah gives the final diagnosis: they are moving inside an intoxication so deep that it has become blindness.

The hook of the passage is this: **Al-Hijr shows that desire becomes civilizationally catastrophic when it no longer feels shame, no longer hears taqwa, and begins to treat a prophet's defense of dignity as the problem rather than the cure.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Yastabshirun**: They Come Delighted by What Should Have Terrified Them

The first shock of the scene is the verb:

**yastabshirun**

They came rejoicing, receiving what they take as good news.

[PAUSE]

The root **b-sh-r** is the same root of glad tidings, the same root used earlier in the surah for the angels' joyful promise to Ibrahim. But here it is turned toward moral inversion. What delighted Ibrahim's house was mercy from Allah. What delights the city is the arrival of prey for corrupted desire. Both al-Tabari (on the authority of Qatadah) and al-Muyassar make the object of their "rejoicing" explicit and grim: they rejoiced at Lut's guests precisely because they meant to reach them with the *munkar*, the abomination — their delight was appetite dressed as good news.

That contrast is devastating. The same root of joyful reception can be filled with entirely opposite contents. One household receives bushra from heaven. Another crowd rushes toward degradation and experiences it as exciting news.

This is how moral perception collapses. Evil is not only done. It is welcomed. The heart no longer commits wrong reluctantly. It begins to celebrate the opportunity for it.

### 2. **Fa-la Tafdahun ... La Tukhzun**: Lut Appeals to Social Honor and Fear of Allah Together

Lut says:

**fa-la tafdahun**

Do not disgrace me.

And then:

**wa-ttaqu Allaha wa la tukhzun**

Be mindful of Allah, and do not shame me.

[PAUSE]

The pair of verbs matters. **Tafdahun** from **f-d-h** is exposure, scandal, public humiliation. **Tukhzun** from **kh-z-y** is disgrace, abasement, being brought low in humiliation.

He is not only asking them to leave his guests alone. He is naming what their act does to moral space itself. Al-Tabari reads his plea through the lens of an ancient duty: it is a *right* owed to a man that his guest be honored (*haqqun 'ala al-rajuli ikramu dayfihi*), so to violate the guest is to shred the host's honor along with him. Al-Muyassar adds the word that raises the stakes further — these guests are *fi himayati*, "under my protection." To assault them is to tear through the covenant of the one who sheltered them. It would violate hospitality, publicly dishonor him, and display shamelessness in the face of Allah.

And notice the order of his second appeal. He couples **wa-ttaqu Allah** — fear Allah — with **la tukhzun**. Al-Tabari unfolds the taqwa clause as a double warning: fear Allah *regarding me and regarding your own selves*, lest His punishment descend upon you. So the plea is not only "spare me the disgrace" but "spare yourselves the ruin." The Quran is teaching us that social honor and taqwa are not enemies. Proper fear of Allah should protect people from humiliating one another. A society that mocks both has already become deeply unsafe.

### 3. **Awa-lam Nanhaka ... Ha'ula'i Banati**: The Retort That Criminalizes Mercy, and a Plea Toward the Lawful

The crowd's answer is chilling in its logic:

**a-wa-lam nanhaka 'ani al-'alamin**

Have we not forbidden you from [taking in] the worlds — the people, anyone at all?

[PAUSE]

Sit with what this reveals. They had not merely normalized their crime; they had legislated against its opposite. Hospitality itself had been outlawed. To open your door to a stranger was the punishable offense. When a culture reaches the point of forbidding kindness and protecting predation, the moral order has been fully turned upside down — the good is criminalized and the crime is defended as civic order.

Lut answers with the most anguished line in the scene:

**ha'ula'i banati in kuntum fa'ilin**

These are my daughters, if you must act.

Here the classical commentators diverge, and the divergence is worth knowing. Some read **banati** literally — his own daughters, offered in the only lawful frame available, marriage. Others, noting that a prophet stands as a father to his whole community, read "my daughters" as the women of the town, whom Lut is steering toward the halal path of marriage rather than the abomination they crave. On either reading the thrust is identical: in his desperation Lut does not bargain over *whether* there will be restraint — he points, however painfully, toward the lawful and away from the forbidden. He is grasping for any channel that might redirect an appetite that has stopped listening.

### 4. **Fi Sakratihim Ya'mahun**: The Final Diagnosis Is Deeper Than Lust

Then the passage ends with one of the most piercing judgments in the surah — and it opens with an oath of extraordinary weight:

**la'amruka innahum la-fi sakratihim ya'mahun**

By your life, they are surely wandering blindly in their intoxication.

[PAUSE]

Do not pass over that opening word. **La'amruka** — "By your life." The classical mufassirun, Ibn Kathir and al-Tabari among them, hold that the one addressed is the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, and that it is *Allah Himself* who swears by his life. This is the only place in the entire Quran where Allah takes an oath by the life of a human being — a signal of the Prophet's honor so elevated that scholars single it out among the most exalted oaths in the Book. That the story of Lut's ruined city should be sealed with an oath by the beloved Prophet's very life presses the lesson forward across the centuries: what befell them is not a curiosity of the past but a warning held up before the last Messenger and his community.

The word **sakrah** is intoxication, stupefaction, the state in which perception is overwhelmed. Then the verse adds **ya'mahun** from the root **'-m-h**, wandering blindly, moving about without clear guidance, not merely unable to see but morally directionless.

This is crucial. The Quran does not reduce the scene to appetite alone. Appetite has become an altered state of perception. They are not simply wanting wrongly. They are seeing wrongly, moving wrongly, and losing their internal compass entirely.

That is why the verse comes as the final divine interpretation. From inside the city, the crowd probably feels bold, excited, maybe even socially supported. From above, sworn to by the life of the Prophet ﷺ himself, Allah names it intoxicated blindness.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: One Sign of Deep Corruption Is When Shame Changes Sides

One of the most frightening things that can happen to a person or a culture is not merely that wrong becomes common. It is that shame relocates.

The wrongdoer is no longer ashamed.

The one resisting the wrong is.

[PAUSE]

That is exactly what happens here. Lut pleads: do not disgrace me, do not shame me. Why should a prophet be the one talking about embarrassment while the aggressors speak with confidence — even citing their own ordinance against hospitality as if he were the lawbreaker? Because corruption has advanced far enough that the violator no longer feels exposed. Instead, decency feels cornered and made to plead for space.

This still happens in ordinary life. The honest person is made to feel awkward. The chaste person is treated as naive. The one holding a line of dignity feels socially embarrassed while the transgressor feels breezy, witty, and affirmed. When that inversion sets in, the problem is already much deeper than one act. It is a collapse in the moral distribution of shame.

### Theme 2: Not Every Intoxication Is Chemical

The Quran's closing diagnosis is profoundly relevant.

**Fi sakratihim ya'mahun.**

In their intoxication, they wander blindly.

[PAUSE]

Many people think intoxication only names what enters the body through drink or substance. But the ayah widens the meaning. A person can be intoxicated by desire, by crowd-energy, by public permission, by fantasy, by appetite intensified through repetition, by the feeling that everyone around them is validating the same blindness.

This kind of intoxication may even be more dangerous because it is often mistaken for normality. No slurred speech, no stumbling body, no obvious external sign. Yet inwardly the person is just as overrun, just as unable to perceive rightly, just as unable to stop.

And once that state deepens, exhortation becomes difficult to hear. Hospitality, taqwa, human dignity, prophetic pleading, all pass by the person like weak sounds outside a locked room. Recall that Lut had already warned them to fear Allah *for their own sakes* — al-Tabari's reading — and even that self-interested appeal, the plainest survival instinct, could not reach them. That is the terror of the state the oath describes: the intoxicated do not even hear the alarm that would save their own lives.

[PAUSE]

That is why guarding the heart early matters so much. Few people intend to arrive at full-blown blindness. They arrive by repeatedly treating lesser distortions as harmless. A little indulgence. A little shamelessness. A little group reinforcement. A little mockery of restraint. Over time the heart's sight dulls, and eventually it calls its own intoxication "clarity."

## Closing Synthesis

The people of the city come rejoicing over an opportunity for corruption. Lut pleads with them on behalf of his guests — not yet knowing they are angels sent by Allah — invokes taqwa for their sake and his, and begs not to be disgraced. They answer as though he is the problem, boasting that they had forbidden him the very act of welcoming strangers. He offers the lawful and honorable path if they must act at all. Then Allah Himself closes the scene with the real verdict, sworn by the life of the Prophet ﷺ: they are wandering blindly in the intoxication that has overtaken them.

[PAUSE]

So the passage gives us more than one historical scandal. It gives us a map of moral collapse. Wrong becomes exciting, dignity becomes embarrassing, mercy becomes a crime, prophetic counsel becomes irritating, and the crowd mistakes its own stupefaction for life.

### Questions to Carry

- Where have you seen shame relocate onto the person trying to preserve dignity rather than the person violating it?
- What desires or crowd-energies in your own life begin to act like intoxicants, dulling moral sight without obvious outward signs?
- Are there places where you now welcome what should have frightened you?
- When taqwa speaks into your impulses, does it still feel authoritative, or has it started to sound like inconvenience?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**This passage shows that corruption becomes especially deadly when desire is no longer hidden but celebrated, when shame changes sides, and when intoxication becomes a whole way of seeing wrongly rather than a passing urge.**

### Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let our hearts celebrate what should have made us tremble. Protect us from the intoxicants of desire, crowds, and shamelessness that blind moral sight. Let taqwa remain weighty in us, and make us people who preserve dignity rather than people who feed on its collapse.*
