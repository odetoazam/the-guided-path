# The People of the Thicket Were Wrongdoers, and Both Ruins Lie on a Clear Route

## Surah Al-Hijr (15:78-79)

[ayah:15:78] وَإِن كَانَ أَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ لَظَـٰلِمِينَ

[ayah:15:79] فَٱنتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ وَإِنَّهُمَا لَبِإِمَامٍ مُّبِينٍ

*Wa in kana ashabu al-aykati la-zalimin.*

*Fa-intaqamna minhum wa innahuma la-bi-imamin mubin.*

**And indeed the people of the thicket were certainly wrongdoers. So We took vengeance upon them, and indeed both of them are upon a clear route.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

The Quran does not let one ruined people stand alone as though history offered only a single warning.

As soon as the remains of Lut's people have been named as readable signs, the surah widens again. Another people. Another settled pattern of wrongdoing. Another requital. Another visible reminder on the road.

[PAUSE]

The brevity of this passage is part of its force. It does not need a long retelling to make the point. Once the moral grammar has been established, a few words are enough: they were wrongdoers, We took vengeance on them, and the traces remain visible.

Two ayahs. No prophet named. No city named. No dialogue, no warning scene, no delegation of angels, no bargaining. Just a verdict, a consequence, and a road. Compare that with the twenty-odd ayahs the same surah has just spent on Lut's people, and you notice something about how the Quran allocates space. A story is told at length once. After that, the pattern can be invoked in a sentence — because the reader is now expected to be able to fill it in.

The early commentators do fill it in. Al-Tabari, al-Muyassar and Ibn Kathir all identify **ashab al-aykah**, the companions of the thicket, as the people of **Shu'ayb** — the prophet sent to a trading community and rejected by it. (Commentators differ over whether these are precisely the same community as Madyan or a neighbouring people who received the same messenger; the Quran itself does not settle it here, and nothing in the lesson depends on which way it is resolved.) What the surah gives us in two lines, other surahs give at length. Here the point is not the story. The point is that there is more than one story, and that they are all on the same road.

The hook of the passage is this: **Al-Hijr teaches that divine warnings in history are not isolated anomalies but repeated, visible patterns, and the road itself becomes a witness against anyone who keeps treating every destroyed people as someone else's story.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **La-Zalimin**: The Moral Name Comes Before the Historical Detail

The passage says:

**wa in kana ashabu al-aykati la-zalimin**

The people of the thicket were surely wrongdoers.

[PAUSE]

Before details of the event, the Quran gives the moral name: **zalimin**.

That matters because the core issue is not geography first. It is not ethnicity first. It is not narrative color first. It is moral orientation. They were people of **zulm**: misplacement, wrongdoing, violation, putting things where they do not belong and failing to give realities their due.

The briefness sharpens the sentence. There is no decorative expansion. The verdict is clear and sufficient. Sometimes the Quran tells a long story. Sometimes it gives the distilled moral diagnosis in one line.

[PAUSE]

There is a small grammatical detail worth slowing down for. The sentence opens with **in** — which in ordinary Arabic usually means "if," and sometimes "not." Here it is neither. It is the lightened form of **inna**, the particle of emphasis, and that is why the **la-** is attached to **zalimin**: the lam is there to keep you from misreading the **in** as a negation. So the sentence is not "if they were wrongdoers." It is the opposite of a hedge. It is a double underlining: *they most certainly were*.

That is worth noticing because of how the sentence would otherwise feel. A short verdict passed on a people long dead could read as an aside. The grammar refuses that. The Arabic is built so that the reader cannot soften it into a maybe.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the name they are given: **ashab al-aykah**. **Ashab** is from the root ṣ-ḥ-b — companionship, keeping company, being habitually attached to something. And **al-aykah**, the early lexicographers and commentators agree, is the tangled grove: densely interwoven trees. Ad-Dahhak and Qatadah both gloss it that way, and al-Tabari fixes the meaning by quoting a line of pre-Islamic verse from Umayyah about doves calling on the branches of the **ayk** — showing that this was simply the ordinary Arabic word for a thick, shaded stand of trees.

So the Quran does not name them after their prophet, or their city, or their tribe. It names them after the grove. They are *the people of the thicket* — the ones whose identity had fused with the lush thing that fed them. One report al-Tabari preserves, from Khasif, adds the homely detail: they ate fresh fruit in the summer and dried fruit in the winter. Year-round abundance. A place where nothing ran out.

That is the community about which Allah says: **they most certainly were wrongdoers**. The abundance and the wrongdoing sat in the same sentence, in the same people, at the same time. Comfort had not corrected them. It had housed them.

[PAUSE]

And what was the wrongdoing? The commentators are specific rather than vague. Ibn Kathir lists three things: associating partners with Allah, waylaying travelers on the road, and cheating in weights and measures.

Sit with the middle two for a moment.

**Cheating in weights and measures** is zulm in its most literal, physical form. Zulm means putting a thing somewhere other than where it belongs. A tampered scale is exactly that: a thumb where it does not belong, a weight not given its due, a quantity misplaced by a fraction so small that no single customer can prove it. It is wrongdoing at the level of the decimal point. And the Quran calls it what it calls the destruction of Lut's people. Not a lesser category. The same word.

**Waylaying travelers** is the other half, and it will matter enormously in two ayahs' time. These were people who made the road unsafe. They lived beside a trade route and treated the people passing along it as prey.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, glossing **zalimin**, says plainly: *they were disbelievers in Allah* — that is, the tampered scale and the ambushed caravan were not separate from the shirk; they were what the shirk looked like on a Tuesday morning. And al-Muyassar sharpens the direction of the harm: **zalimin li-anfusihim**, wronging *themselves*. The cheated customer lost a few grams. They lost everything they were.

That is the Quranic accounting, and it is the opposite of how wrongdoing usually feels from inside. From inside, the person who shaves the scale is gaining. The verse says he is the one being robbed, and he is doing the robbing.

### 2. **Fa-Intaqamna Minhum**: Requital as a Measured Answer to Wrong

Then:

**fa-intaqamna minhum**

So We took vengeance upon them.

[PAUSE]

The root **n-q-m** carries the sense of requital, exacting response, not chaotic rage but punishment answering established wrongdoing. The **fa** matters too. It links the requital to the wrongdoing just named. Zulm is not morally weightless. It meets response.

Al-Tabari's paraphrase catches how tight that link is. He does not simply write "We took requital from them." He writes: *We took requital from the wrongdoers among the people of the thicket* — pulling the moral name forward from the previous ayah and repeating it inside the sentence about punishment. The verdict and the consequence are not two facts placed side by side. The second is the shape of the first.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, what the verb does *not* say. It does not say "they were destroyed," passive and impersonal, as though a natural disaster happened to fall on a town. It says **intaqamna** — *We* took requital. First person. Plural of majesty. The One speaking claims the act.

That is uncomfortable in a way modern readers should not rush past. It would be easier if the verse said a fault line slipped. But the Quran insists that the moral order is not a metaphor laid over an indifferent universe. Someone is keeping the account.

[PAUSE]

The commentators name what the requital actually was, drawing on the fuller accounts elsewhere in the Quran: **al-rajfah**, the shaking or quake, and **'adhab yawm al-zullah**, the torment of the Day of the Shade — a canopy that gathered over them and then broke on them. Ibn Kathir adds **al-sayhah**, the cry.

And here the naming of the people repays attention. They were **ashab al-aykah** — the people of the shade of the tangled trees. What came for them, the commentators say, was **al-zullah** — a shade of another kind. It is not for us to insist the Quran intends the echo as a stated cause. But the two words sit close enough in the tradition's own telling that it is hard not to hear it: the canopy they were named for, answered by a canopy they had not planned for. The thing a people leans on is not always the thing that will hold.

And there is a second echo, harder still. They cut the road. Their ruins now sit on it.

### 3. **Wa Innahuma La-Bi-Imamin Mubin**: The Route Itself Becomes a Teacher

Then comes the line that ties the previous story to this one:

**wa innahuma la-bi-imamin mubin**

Indeed, both of them are on a clear route.

[PAUSE]

The pronoun points back to the two ruined sites now being held together in the surah's field of vision. Al-Tabari is explicit about the referent: the **huma**, the dual "both of them," is *the city of the people of the thicket and the city of the people of Lut*. Two settlements, one sentence, one road. The grammar of the dual is what stitches the two stories into a single lesson — you cannot say "both" until there is more than one, and the moment there is more than one, you are no longer looking at an accident.

And the word **imam** here carries the sense of a path, route, or course that is followed and visible. Not hidden. Not lost. Not buried from human passage.

[PAUSE]

That word deserves a moment. **Imam** comes from the root a-m-m, the same root that gives **umm** (mother, source), **ummah** (a community moving together), and **amam** (in front, ahead). Al-Tabari gives the derivation directly: the road is called an **imam** *because it is headed for and followed* — because people aim at it and travel by it. An imam, in the root's own logic, is simply whatever goes in front and gets followed.

So the sentence is doing something quietly startling. The word the tradition uses for the one who leads the prayer, the one you line up behind, is here applied to a stretch of road with two dead cities on it. Al-Tabari's gloss makes the road almost a guide in its own right: *a route they take as their lead in their travels, and by which they are guided*.

And **mubin** — from b-y-n, to be clear, to make plain, to separate one thing from another — is not a decorative adjective on the end. Al-Tabari reads it actively: the road *makes plain, to whoever follows it, its own straightness*. It is not merely a visible road. It is a road that shows you it is the right one. The clarity is the road's own doing.

[PAUSE]

This is remarkable. The road is not just a way to move past the ruins. It is part of the sign itself. The traveler who passes is not innocent of what he has seen. The route has been made clear enough that forgetfulness becomes less excusable.

Al-Muyassar completes the thought with the word the Quran uses again and again for this kind of looking: the dwellings of Lut's people and Shu'ayb's people lie on a plain road that people pass on their journeys — **fa-ya'tabirun**, *so that they may take the crossing-over*. That verb, from '-b-r, is the same family as the word for interpreting a dream and the word for crossing a river. To take a lesson, in Arabic, is to cross from the outward thing to the meaning on the other side of it. The ruin is the near bank. The lesson is the far one. Most travelers stop at the water.

The surah is teaching us that Allah not only acts in history. He also leaves historical pedagogy in the world.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Repeated Warnings Are a Mercy, Not Redundancy

Human beings often grow numb to repetition.

If one warning is ignored, the second may feel like an old story. The third may feel remote. The fourth may feel like religious overstatement.

[PAUSE]

But the Quran treats repeated warnings differently. It treats them as mercy. Not because repetition itself saves, but because Allah keeps placing signs in the path of human arrogance rather than destroying people without precedent, pattern, or reminder.

So when the surah moves from Lut's people to the people of the thicket in just two ayahs, it is not being repetitive in a weak sense. It is building a cumulative moral field. It is saying: look again. And again. And again. How many overturned stories must stand on the route before the traveler finally reads the road?

[PAUSE]

There is a detail in the commentaries that makes this sharper. Ibn Kathir notes that the people of the thicket lived near the people of Lut — but *later*. Same region. Different generation. The second community had the first community's ruins within reach of an ordinary journey, and it did not help them.

Think about what that means. These were not people who lacked evidence. They had a destroyed city more or less down the road. They could have walked to it. They very likely had, on trade runs, since they lived off the traffic of that road. And they went on shaving the scales anyway.

That is the most unsettling part of the passage, and it is the part most likely to be about us. The failure was not informational. Nobody in that grove was unaware that cities can fall. They knew the ruins the way we know statistics — as scenery on the way to somewhere else.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, that their sins were not exotic. Shirk, yes. But also short-weighting customers and being unsafe to strangers. The Quran does not reserve civilizational judgment for spectacular villainy. It attaches it to a rigged scale.

That should make the passage harder to read at a distance. It is easy to be certain you are not the people of Lut. It is less easy to be certain you have never quietly given someone less than they were owed and told yourself the amount was too small to count.

### Theme 2: The Road You Travel May Already Be Teaching You

One of the most piercing elements in this passage is that both sites are on a clear route.

This means guidance is not always delivered only in formal lessons, books, sermons, or private reflection. Sometimes Allah places instruction in the landscapes, institutions, broken cultures, public ruins, and inherited memories that a person keeps moving through.

[PAUSE]

The tragedy is that people can become highly mobile while remaining spiritually untraveled. They pass by sign after sign without inward movement. They know the route but not the lesson.

Hold that against what these particular people did for a living. They lived beside a trade road and, we are told, robbed the travelers on it. The road was their opportunity. It was where their money came from and where their wrongdoing was carried out. And when the account was finally settled, that same road became the thing that carries their story to everyone who comes after.

The place where a person does his wrong is not neutral ground. It keeps a record.

[PAUSE]

That remains true now. Many of us travel through worlds marked by visible consequences of greed, shamelessness, exploitation, arrogance, and moral inversion. The signs are not absent. Often the problem is that we have learned to treat them as normal scenery.

And this is precisely where **mubin** presses. If the road were ambiguous, misreading it would be understandable. But al-Tabari's reading was that the route makes its own straightness plain to whoever follows it. The clarity is not something the traveler has to manufacture by unusual insight. It is already supplied. Which means the failure to learn is not a failure of eyesight. It is a failure of willingness.

The believer asks for another kind of travel: not just movement across the road, but movement inside understanding — the crossing-over that **i'tibar** actually names, from the ruin in front of you to the meaning waiting on the other side of it.

## Closing Synthesis

The companions of the thicket are named as wrongdoers. Allah took vengeance upon them. And both their ruined place and the earlier ruined place remain set upon a clear route.

[PAUSE]

Everything in the two ayahs is compressed, and everything in them is load-bearing. A people named for the shade that fed them. A verdict the grammar refuses to let you soften. A requital claimed in the first person by the One who issued it. Two cities bound together by a single dual pronoun. And a road that the Arabic calls an **imam** — something that goes in front and is followed — running straight through both sets of ruins and out toward wherever you happen to be going today.

The passage is short, but it lands a weighty point: historical judgment repeats, visible warnings accumulate, and the road itself becomes part of Allah's address to later people. If we keep passing by without learning, the problem is no longer lack of signs.

### Questions to Carry

- What repeated warnings in history or in your surroundings have started to feel ordinary to you?
- Do you treat visible consequences of wrongdoing as lessons, or mostly as background scenery?
- The wrongdoing named here included short-weighting people in small, unprovable amounts — where in your own dealings does a fraction go missing that nobody could ever catch?
- These people had a destroyed city within traveling distance and it did not change them. What evidence is already within your reach that you have stopped letting count?
- In what ways are you moving through roads, systems, and stories that are already trying to teach you something?
- What would it look like to become inwardly teachable by the routes you travel every day?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**This brief passage teaches that divine warnings in history accumulate along the road, and that the person who keeps passing visible signs without learning can no longer blame a lack of reminders.**

### Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let repeated warnings become ordinary in our eyes. Make us people who learn from what You leave visible on the road of history, and do not let us move through clear signs with closed hearts.*
