# Why Do You Forbid Yourself?

## Surah At-Tahrim 66:1–5

---

> يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ مَآ أَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ لَكَ تَبْتَغِى مَرْضَاتَ أَزْوَٰجِكَ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuhā an-nabiyyu lima tuḥarrimu mā aḥalla-llāhu laka, tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik. Wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm.*

**"O Prophet, why do you forbid yourself what Allah has made lawful for you, seeking the approval of your wives? And Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Door That Opens Onto a Kitchen

I want you to begin somewhere very small.

Not a battlefield. Not a mountain. Not the splitting of a sea. A kitchen — or what passed for one — in a modest mud-brick home in Madinah. A jar of honey. A husband who lingered a little too long in one wife's room because she had given him something sweet to drink. And the other wives, noticing, feeling the small sharp sting of a heart that wants more of someone it loves.

That is where this surah begins. Not in the heavens. In a home.

The surah is called **At-Taḥrīm** — "The Prohibition" — and it is named after the very first verb Allah uses to address His Messenger ﷺ in it: *lima tuḥarrimu*, "why do you forbid?" Think about that for a moment. An entire chapter of the eternal speech of God carries the name of a gentle correction issued to the most beloved human being who ever lived. [PAUSE]

Now — before we go further, I have to be honest with you about something, because the people who carried this religion to us were honest about it. **There is more than one account of what actually happened.** The scholars preserved more than one tradition, and they did not pretend otherwise.

Among the narrations — and this is the one recorded in the two most rigorous collections, Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, on the authority of ʿĀʾisha رضي الله عنها — the Prophet ﷺ used to enjoy honey at the home of one of his wives. Two of the others, out of that very human ache of jealousy, quietly agreed that when he came to each of them they would say his breath carried the smell of *maghāfīr* — an unpleasant-smelling gum. He so disliked the thought of carrying any offensive scent that he resolved never to take the honey again — and, in some reports, swore an oath upon it.

In another tradition — the one al-Jalālayn records — the incident concerns the Prophet's privacy with his bondwoman Māriya, and his swearing to keep away from her to soothe the feelings of a wife in whose room it had occurred.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī preserve the honey account through the Ṣaḥīḥ chain; other commentators preserve the other. **I am not going to choose for you, and I am not going to pretend the tradition is unanimous where it is not.** Here is what every single one of these accounts shares — and it is the only thing the verse itself actually names: *the Prophet ﷺ made something forbidden upon himself that Allah had made lawful, in order to keep peace, in order to please those he loved.* That is the act. That is what Allah addresses. Everything else is the texture of how it happened.

So hold the small thing. A jar of honey, or a private affection. A husband trying to keep everyone happy. And then — into that domestic, almost ordinary moment — **the sky speaks.** The Lord of the worlds turns His attention to a man's attempt to manage the feelings of the people in his house, and He says: *lima* — *why?*

Here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows: **Why would Allah send down Qur'an — recited until the end of time — about a man being too eager to please the people he loves?** What is so dangerous, so worth correcting, in that softest and most relatable of human impulses?

Stay with that. We're going to walk into it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yā ayyuhā an-nabī* — Corrected by His Title

Let me teach you something about how address works before we touch the verse.

In any language, **what you call someone in the moment you correct them tells them everything about where they stand with you.** Imagine a father who, when pleased, calls his son by his first name — but when angry, uses his full legal name: first, middle, and last. The shift in naming carries the emotional weather. We all know this instinctively. The name is never just a label. It is a position. It is a temperature.

Now look at how Allah opens.

He is about to correct His Messenger. He is about to ask him *why* he did something. And in that exact moment — the moment of correction — what does He call him?

> يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ
>
> *"O Prophet."*

Not "O Muhammad." Not his personal name. **His title. His rank. The highest thing he is.**

Let's sit with this, because it is easy to read past. Across the Qur'an, Allah addresses many people by their given names when He speaks to them — *Yā Ādam, Yā Nūḥ, Yā Mūsā, Yā ʿĪsā, Yā Dāwūd*. But when He turns to the final Messenger ﷺ in direct address, again and again He reaches for the honorific: *Yā ayyuhā an-nabī*, *Yā ayyuhā ar-rasūl* — O Prophet, O Messenger. The honorific is a kind of veil of dignity draped over him.

Notice what that does *here* — at the one moment you might least expect it. Allah is issuing a rebuke. And He prefaces the rebuke by elevating the one He is rebuking. He says, in effect: *Before I ask you why — remember who you are to Me. You are the Prophet.*

Here's what's remarkable. In our world, correction and honor feel like opposites. When someone corrects us, we brace, because correction usually arrives stripped of warmth — "you messed up," cold and bare. We have learned to expect that the moment of being wrong is the moment of being diminished. So we hide our mistakes. We get defensive. We cannot bear to be corrected because in our experience correction and contempt come bundled together. [PAUSE]

And then comes a Lord who **opens the correction with the title of honor.** Who says *Prophet* in the very breath that says *why did you*. The dignity is not withdrawn to make room for the rebuke. The dignity *carries* the rebuke.

Stay with me, because there's one more layer. This is not Allah being soft because the Prophet ﷺ is fragile. This is Allah teaching all of us, through him, what correction is *supposed* to look like when it comes from love rather than from contempt. **Love corrects without un-naming you.** It never says "you are nothing" in order to say "you were wrong." It holds your name and your worth steady in one hand while it points with the other.

What this single opening reveals: **you can be told you were wrong without being told you are worthless — and the proof is that Allah Himself corrected His most beloved this way.**

### Element Two: *Tuḥarrimu* — Building a Wall Where God Built a Door

Now to the verb that named the whole surah.

First, the concept. In Arabic, verbs come in patterns — "forms" — and the form a verb takes changes its meaning in regular, predictable ways. There is a particular pattern, what scholars call **Form II**, built by doubling the middle root letter. One of its core functions is what grammarians call the *factitive* or *declarative* sense: to *treat* something as having a quality, to *render* it so, to *declare* it to be a certain way.

Take the root for "truth," *ṣ-d-q*. The simple verb means "to be truthful." But put it in Form II — *ṣaddaqa* — and it means "to *treat as* true," "to declare true," "to believe." You didn't make the thing true; you *rendered it true in your dealing with it.*

Now the verb in our verse:

> لِمَ تُحَرِّمُ
>
> *"Why do you treat as forbidden..."*

The root is *ḥ-r-m* — the root of *ḥarām*, the forbidden, the sacred-and-off-limits. And it sits here in exactly that Form II, factitive shape. *Tuḥarrimu* does not mean the Prophet ﷺ *made* something forbidden in reality — he could not; that is not a power any human holds. It means he **treated as forbidden, declared off-limits to himself,** something that in the eyes of Allah remained perfectly open.

Feel the precision of that. The verse does not say "why did you abandon" or "why did you avoid." It says *why do you render-forbidden.* The grammar itself diagnoses the exact error: he took a thing God had left open and he *built a wall around it in his own heart.*

And right beside it, Allah names the truth the wall denies:

> مَآ أَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ لَكَ
>
> *"...what Allah has made lawful for you."*

Here is the root *ḥ-l-l* — to loosen, to untie, to make permitted — and it is in **Form IV**, *aḥalla*, the form whose force here is to *make* lawful, to render open. So we have two acts placed face to face in a single line, and the contrast is the entire point:

- **Allah** *aḥalla* — opened, untied, made lawful. A door.
- **The servant** *tuḥarrimu* — closed, tied up, made forbidden. A wall.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit in that gap, because it is one of the most quietly astonishing things in the verse. **The human being made his religion *harder* than God made it.** Not easier — that's the sin we expect, the cutting of corners. Here it is the opposite. He took God's permission and refused it. He looked at an open door and bricked it up, and he did it sincerely, out of a desire to do right by the people he loved.

And Allah's response is not "well done, how pious." Allah's response is *lima* — **why?**

Notice something. We tend to assume that being *stricter* than God is always safer — that if Allah permits something, surely there's extra reward in denying it to yourself. And there are noble forms of restraint. But this verse marks a boundary. To stand before something Allah has *deliberately opened* and declare it closed — to treat His permission as if it were a leniency you are too good for — is not a higher piety. It is, gently, an error. Because **it implies God's law was incomplete and you improved it.** It quietly substitutes your judgment for His.

This is why the surah carries the name *At-Taḥrīm.* Of all the things Allah could have named the chapter — there is a whole household drama here, oaths, secrets, repentance, angels — He named it after this: *the act of forbidding what He made lawful.* As if to say: of everything that happened in that house, *this* is the disease worth naming. The wall where God built a door.

And we are not exempt. Listen to the echo from Sūrat al-Māʾida: *"O you who believe, do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress."* (5:87) The wall-building is a human habit, not a prophetic one. We do it with food, with joy, with rest, with beauty, with our own forgiveness — we treat as forbidden the mercies God left wide open, and we call it devotion.

What this verb reveals: **piety is not measured by how much of God's permission you refuse. Sometimes refusing His gift is itself the thing in need of correction.**

### Element Three: *Tabtaghī Marḍāta Azwājik* — The Endless Chase After Approval

We've seen *what* he did — built a wall. Now the verse tells us *why*, and the diagnosis is in the verb.

> تَبْتَغِى مَرْضَاتَ أَزْوَٰجِكَ
>
> *"...seeking the approval of your wives."*

Two words to open here. First, *tabtaghī.* The root is *b-gh-y* — to seek, to desire. But again the form matters. This is **Form VIII**, *ibtaghā* — and Form VIII characteristically adds a sense of *effort, of striving, of seeking-out for oneself.* It is not passive wanting. It is *active pursuit.* The Prophet ﷺ was not merely hoping his wives would be content — the grammar says he was *reaching for it, working toward it, chasing it.*

And what was he chasing? *Marḍāt.* From the root *r-ḍ-w* — *riḍā*, contentment, approval, the settling of a heart into pleasure. *Marḍāt* is the deep good-pleasure of another. He was striving to *secure the approval of his wives.*

Now — pause and feel how sympathetic this is. This is not a man chasing wealth or power or revenge. He is chasing *the contentment of the people he loves most.* He wants peace in his home. He wants the faces of his wives to soften. Is there a more relatable, more *decent* motive in all the world? [PAUSE]

And yet Allah names it as the thing that pulled him into the wall.

Here is what I want you to see, and it requires staying very still. **The object of the seeking is the problem.** Look at where his striving was aimed: *marḍāta azwājik* — the approval *of your wives.* Created approval. The good-pleasure of human beings.

Now hold this verb up against the same root used elsewhere in the Qur'an. Allah praises those who spend *ibtighāʾa marḍāti-llāh* — "seeking the approval *of Allah*" (2:207, 2:265). Same striving. Same Form VIII reaching. Same word *marḍāt.* The *act* of seeking is identical. **Only the direction changes.** And the direction is everything.

Because here is the brutal arithmetic of created approval, and your own life has already taught it to you: *you cannot secure it.* The approval of people is a destination that recedes as you approach. Please one and you displease another. Soothe one wife's jealousy and you wound a different one. Win the room's applause today and tomorrow the room wants something new. There is no final, resting *riḍā* at the end of pleasing people, because human hearts are many and they pull in different directions and they are never, all at once, satisfied. [PAUSE]

This is why the striving was misplaced — not because love is wrong, not because he should not have cared, but because **he aimed an infinite effort at a target that can never finally be hit.** He poured the energy of *ibtighāʾ* — that active, striving seeking — into *marḍāt* that no human can fully give. And in the chase, he walled himself off from a gift God had opened.

Now feel how the verse ends, and feel the mercy of *where* it lands:

> وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ
>
> *"And Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."*

*Ghafūr* — from *gh-f-r*, and built on the intensive pattern *faʿūl*, meaning not just "one who forgives" but "the One who forgives much, repeatedly, by His very nature." *Raḥīm* — overflowing with *raḥma*, tender mercy. [PAUSE]

Sit with the placement. The verse opened with *why did you* — a rebuke. And it closes, before a single punishment is mentioned, before the word "sin" ever appears, with *Forgiving, Merciful.* The correction is *wrapped* in mercy on both ends — honored by his title at the start, and reassured by God's forgiveness at the finish. The *why* is held inside the *raḥma* like a small hard seed inside soft fruit.

It is as if Allah says: *I am pointing out the wall you built — and in the same breath I am telling you the door is still open, and I am still the Forgiving, the Merciful, and you have nothing to fear in being corrected by Me.*

What these words reveal: **the same striving that exhausts you in chasing people would fulfill you if turned toward God — and the One who points out your misdirection is the very One whose approval you were made to seek.**

### Bridge

Look at what the language has shown us, laid side by side.

The **address** — *Yā ayyuhā an-nabī* — taught us that correction can honor. The **verb** *tuḥarrimu* — that wall where God built a door — taught us the strange error of making the faith harder than its Author made it. And the **seeking** — *tabtaghī marḍāt* — taught us that an infinite striving aimed at the approval of people is an arrow that never lands, while the same striving aimed at God finds its mark.

But these are not just observations about a man and a jar of honey. They are diagnoses of *us.* So now let's ask what this verse reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart, and the way out.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Quiet Tyranny of Pleasing People

Let me start where you already live.

You know the feeling of saying yes when everything in you meant no. The text you answer at midnight because you cannot bear someone thinking you cold. The opinion you swallow because the table would turn if you spoke it. The plan you agree to, the money you lend, the boundary you let dissolve — all so that a particular face will not fall, a particular voice will not cool toward you. You know the low-grade exhaustion of being whoever the person in front of you needs you to be. And you know the strange loneliness of it: that the more people you please, the less anyone actually knows *you*, because you have been busy being their reflection. [PAUSE]

Modern language has a clinical name for the deeper version of this. It calls it *people-pleasing*, sometimes traced to what therapists call the *fawn response* — the nervous system's habit of managing a perceived threat not by fighting or fleeing but by appeasing, by making oneself agreeable, by preemptively dissolving anyone's displeasure before it can form. It feels like kindness. It is often fear wearing the costume of kindness.

Now bring the verse back. Because the Qur'an reached this human disorder *centuries* before the clinic did, and it placed the diagnosis not on a broken or weak man — but on the *best* of men, in the most sympathetic possible form.

The Prophet ﷺ was not appeasing out of cowardice. He was the bravest man alive; he had stood firm before armies and before his own dying child. His was the *purest* version of the impulse: he simply could not bear to cause pain to the women he loved, and so he reached for the smallest self-denial — *I'll give up the honey, I'll forbid myself the lawful thing* — to keep their hearts at ease. The most innocent people-pleasing imaginable. [PAUSE]

And *that* is precisely why Allah corrects it here. Because if the impulse needed correcting *even in him* — even in its noblest, gentlest form — then we cannot wave away ours as harmless. The verse is saying: *this thing you call kindness, this endless managing of other people's feelings at the cost of what is good for you — look closely. It has a cost. And the cost is that you start forbidding yourself what God permitted, and remaking your life around the shifting moods of people who can never all be satisfied at once.*

Here is the movement that transforms it. Watch *who* the displeased party becomes when you stop chasing human approval. The verse doesn't say "stop caring about your wives." It re-points the caring. *Tabtaghī marḍāta azwājik* — you were seeking *their* pleasure — and the antidote, present in the same root all over the Qur'an, is *marḍāti-llāh*, **His** pleasure. [PAUSE]

And this is the liberation that people-pleasing can never give you: **the approval of God is the one approval that, once you truly seek it, *settles*.** Unlike the crowd, He is One — not many hearts pulling in many directions. Unlike the crowd, He sees the *whole* of you, so you never have to perform a partial self for Him. Unlike the crowd, His *riḍā*, once granted, is not withdrawn tomorrow because your usefulness changed. To live for the pleasure of the One is to be freed from the impossible court of the many.

There is a famous wisdom passed down among the early Muslims — often attributed in the tradition to scholars like al-Fuḍayl and echoed by others — that runs: *whoever seeks to please Allah even at the cost of people's displeasure, Allah will suffice him from the people; and whoever seeks to please people at the cost of Allah's displeasure, Allah will hand him over to the people.* Hand him over to the people. **That phrase is the whole tyranny in four words.** When you live for their approval, you are *handed over* to them — to their moods, their conditions, their endless and conflicting demands. You become their servant. The verse is the rescue from exactly that servitude.

What this changes about tomorrow: the next time you feel the pull to dissolve yourself to keep someone's face from falling, ask the verse's question back to yourself — *lima?* Why? *Whose* approval am I actually reaching for, and can it ever be finally won? Then redirect the *ibtighāʾ* — keep the love, keep the kindness, but anchor the striving in the One whose pleasure does not recede. **Say one honest no this week to a person, for the sake of a truer yes to God.** Notice that the sky does not fall. Notice the strange new room to breathe.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Fully Known, and Gently Held

Now I want to take you into the third verse, because it opens a second wound — and a second healing.

Picture the scene the verse paints. The Prophet ﷺ confides something privately to one of his wives — *asarra*, from the root of *sirr*, secret; he *entrusted a secret.* And then:

> فَلَمَّا نَبَّأَتْ بِهِۦ وَأَظْهَرَهُ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ
>
> *"...and when she disclosed it, and Allah made it known to him..."*

The secret travels. It is passed along where it was not meant to go. And then comes the confrontation — gentle, but exact. He lets her know that he knows. And she, startled, asks the question every one of us would ask:

> مَنْ أَنۢبَأَكَ هَـٰذَا
>
> *"Who told you this?"*

*Who could possibly have told you?* And the answer lands like quiet thunder:

> قَالَ نَبَّأَنِىَ ٱلْعَلِيمُ ٱلْخَبِيرُ
>
> *"He said: I was told by the All-Knowing, the All-Aware."*

[PAUSE]

Sit here. *Al-ʿAlīm* — the One whose knowledge encompasses everything that is. *Al-Khabīr* — and this name is more intimate still; *khibra* is knowledge of the *inner reality* of a thing, the hidden grain of it, what lies beneath the surface. Not just the One who knows *that* a thing happened — the One who knows its inward texture, its private weight, what it meant in the dark of a heart. **Nothing whispered in any room is outside His hearing. No secret passed between two people is unwitnessed. There is no private corner in all of creation.**

Now — for most of us, that thought first arrives as *fear*. And there's a place for that fear; it is the spine of accountability. But watch what the verse does with the same knowing. Look again at the Prophet's response when Allah showed him what had happened:

> عَرَّفَ بَعْضَهُۥ وَأَعْرَضَ عَنۢ بَعْضٍ
>
> *"He made known part of it, and turned away from part."*

Here's what's remarkable, and it's easy to miss in the rush. Allah informed him of the *whole* matter. But the Prophet ﷺ, in confronting his wife, **disclosed only part and let part go.** He did not unload everything he now knew. He did not weaponize his complete information to corner or shame her. He named what needed naming for the sake of truth — and he *graciously released the rest.* [PAUSE]

Feel the two knowings stacked here, because together they are one of the most healing things in the passage. There is *al-ʿAlīm al-Khabīr* — the One who knows everything, down to the inner grain. And there is His Prophet, taught by that very disclosure to **know fully and yet cover gently.** The divine knowledge does not produce a tyrant who exposes; it produces, in the one who receives it rightly, a mercy that *conceals.*

This is the second healing the surah offers, and it answers a fear most of us carry without naming it: the fear that if we were *fully known* — every private thought, every petty jealousy, every secret we passed along, every wall we built out of fear — we would be *cast out.* That to be seen completely is to be condemned completely. We build whole personalities around not being fully known, precisely because we believe exposure equals rejection. [PAUSE]

And the Qur'an stands that fear on its head. Because *who* is the One who knows you most completely — *al-ʿAlīm al-Khabīr*, down to the inner grain of you? It is the very same One who, two verses earlier, sealed His rebuke with *ghafūrun raḥīm* — Forgiving, Merciful. **The One who knows you most is the One most ready to forgive you.** Your fullest exposure and your surest mercy are addressed to the same Knower. He does not need you to hide in order to love you. He already saw, and the door is still open.

And then, lest anyone despair, verse four does not slam shut. To the two whose hearts had *inclined* — *ṣaghat qulūbukumā*, leaned away from where they should have been — Allah does not begin with threat. He begins:

> إِن تَتُوبَآ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ
>
> *"If you two turn back to Allah in repentance..."*

The All-Knowing, the All-Aware — who saw the secret travel, who saw the hearts lean — opens not with the punishment but with *taubah.* With *come back.* The full knowledge and the open door, once again, belong to the same God. [PAUSE]

What this changes about tomorrow: stop performing for a God who already sees behind the performance. The relief on offer here is enormous — you can *stop pretending with Him.* Bring Him the actual self, the jealous thought, the secret you shouldn't have passed, the wall you built out of fear — bring it *because* He already knows it, and bring it to the One named *Forgiving* in the same passage He's named *All-Aware*. And then — learn the Prophet's gift in return: when *you* come to know someone's fault completely, **disclose only what truth requires and cover the rest.** Be, to the people in your home, a small echo of the God who knows all and conceals much.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Watch how the two halves of this reflection turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a man building a wall where God built a door — *tuḥarrimu* against *aḥalla* — and doing it while *striving* (Form VIII *tabtaghī*) for an approval (*marḍāt*) that human beings can never finally give. The grammar diagnosed a heart pointed the wrong way.

The **thematic journey** showed us *why* that misdirection is a tyranny — that living for the pleasure of the many hands you over to the many — and then showed us the way out: that there is One whose pleasure *settles*, One who knows you to the inner grain and is named *Forgiving* in the same breath, One before whom you never have to perform and never have to hide.

They are the same truth. **The disease is seeking from creation what only the Creator can give** — final approval, secure acceptance, the rest of being fully known and still held. The cure is to take the very same striving, the very same love, and anchor it where it lands: in the *riḍā* of *al-ʿAlīm al-Khabīr*, who is also *al-Ghafūr ar-Raḥīm.*

And see the architecture of the opening verse itself, now that we've walked it. It is mercy on both ends with the correction held safe in the middle: *Yā ayyuhā an-nabī* — honor — then *lima tuḥarrimu* — the correction — then *wa-llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm* — mercy. The rebuke never touches open air. It is sealed in dignity at the front and forgiveness at the back. **That is how God corrects.** That is the shape we are meant to carry into every home, every correction we ever give.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I built a wall where God left a door — calling it discipline or piety, when it may be fear, or the need to please?
2. Whose *marḍāt* am I actually striving for, day to day? If I traced my anxieties to their root, whose displeasure am I most afraid of — and can that person's approval ever finally be won?
3. What part of myself do I keep hidden because I believe that to be fully known is to be rejected — and what would change if I truly believed the One who knows me most is the One most ready to forgive?
4. When I come to know someone's fault completely, do I disclose all of it, or do I — like the Prophet ﷺ — make known what truth requires and gently cover the rest?
5. What is one honest *no* to a person that would be a truer *yes* to God?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**You were made to seek the pleasure of the One who sees all of you and forgives much — so stop walling off His gifts and exhausting yourself chasing the approval of those who can never finally give it.**

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing here asks you to love people less. The Prophet ﷺ was not told to stop caring for his wives — he was told to stop *forbidding himself God's gifts* in order to manage their feelings. So this is not an invitation to coldness. It is an invitation to *re-aim* the warmth. Keep loving. Keep being kind. But let the deep current of your striving — the *ibtighāʾ*, the part of you that aches to be approved of — flow toward the One whose *riḍā* does not recede, whose knowledge does not condemn, and whose door, even at the moment He corrects you, stays open.

Let the next time you feel that familiar pull to dissolve yourself for someone's approval be the moment you hear, gently, *lima* — and remember Who is actually asking, and Whose pleasure was the point all along.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You are al-ʿAlīm al-Khabīr — You know me to the inner grain, and still You are al-Ghafūr ar-Raḥīm. Free me from the exhausting court of human approval, and let my striving find its rest in Your pleasure alone. Where I have walled off Your gifts out of fear or the wish to please, open my hands again; and as You know all of me and conceal much, make me one who knows others and covers them. Āmīn.*
