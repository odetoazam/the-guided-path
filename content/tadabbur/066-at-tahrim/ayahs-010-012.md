# Under, Not Inside: Why Proximity Is Not Salvation

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read you the words first. Sit with the sound before you reach for the meaning.

> **ضَرَبَ ٱللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِّلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ ٱمْرَأَتَ نُوحٍ وَٱمْرَأَتَ لُوطٍ ۖ كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا صَـٰلِحَيْنِ فَخَانَتَاهُمَا فَلَمْ يُغْنِيَا عَنْهُمَا مِنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا وَقِيلَ ٱدْخُلَا ٱلنَّارَ مَعَ ٱلدَّٰخِلِينَ**

*Ḍaraba Allāhu mathalan lilladhīna kafarū imra'ata Nūḥin wa-mra'ata Lūṭ. Kānatā taḥta ʿabdayni min ʿibādinā ṣāliḥayn, fa-khānatāhumā, fa-lam yughniyā ʿanhumā mina Allāhi shay'ā, wa-qīla -dkhulā al-nāra maʿa al-dākhilīn.*

"Allah presents an example for those who disbelieved: the wife of Nuh and the wife of Lut. They were under two of Our righteous servants, but they betrayed them, so those two availed them nothing against Allah, and it was said, 'Enter the Fire with those who enter.'"

And then immediately, two more:

> **وَضَرَبَ ٱللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِّلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱمْرَأَتَ فِرْعَوْنَ إِذْ قَالَتْ رَبِّ ٱبْنِ لِى عِندَكَ بَيْتًا فِى ٱلْجَنَّةِ ...**

"And Allah presents an example for those who believed: the wife of Pharaoh, when she said, 'My Lord, build for me near You a house in the Garden, and save me from Pharaoh and his deeds, and save me from the wrongdoing people.'"

> **وَمَرْيَمَ ٱبْنَتَ عِمْرَٰنَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا ...**

"And Mary, the daughter of Imran, who guarded her chastity..."

### Rebuild the world

To feel the force of these three verses, you have to know where they land. They land at the very end of a surah whose opening is almost shockingly intimate.

At-Tahrim begins inside a home. Not a battlefield, not a courtroom, not the cosmos — a household. There was a private matter among the wives of the Prophet ﷺ, something whispered, something disclosed, a moment of domestic friction so ordinary that any one of us would recognize it. And from that small, human, domestic seed, the surah grows outward — *guard yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones* — until, by the final three verses, the camera has pulled all the way back from one household to the whole of human history.

And here is what you must feel about the original audience. These were people for whom *family* was everything. Your tribe was your insurance policy. Your lineage was your standing. Your marriage was your alliance. To be *connected* to the right people was, quite literally, survival. A man without a clan was a man without protection. Belonging was not sentimental — it was structural. It was the air they breathed.

Now imagine the believing households of Madinah — many of them brand new to Islam, many of them with one foot still in the world of their disbelieving relatives, fathers, spouses, in-laws. Some of them are married to people who do not believe. Some of them *are* the only believer in the house. And some, quietly, are leaning on the opposite: *I'm fine. I'm close to the Prophet. I'm in the believing community. I married into faith. I'm covered.*

[PAUSE]

Into that world — a world that runs on connection — Allah drops four women. Two of them were as connected as a human being could possibly be: married to *prophets*. And two of them were connected to nothing good at all — one married to the single worst tyrant in the Qur'an, one with no husband to lean on whatsoever.

And the verdict completely inverts what that world assumed.

### The Hook

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. The two women married to prophets — Nuh and Lut, two of the greatest men who ever drew breath — go to the Fire. The woman married to Pharaoh is saved. The woman with no husband at all is honored above almost all of humanity.

So what, exactly, *is* the thread your soul is hanging by? Because the Qur'an is about to tell you it is not the one you've been gripping your whole life.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تَحْتَ — "under," not "inside"

I want to teach you something about Arabic prepositions before we touch the verse, because everything turns on a single small word and you'll walk right past it if I don't slow you down.

In every language, the small words — the *of*, the *under*, the *with* — do quiet, enormous work. Think about the difference in English between standing *with* someone and standing *for* someone. Same scene, totally different relationship. The little word carries the weight.

Arabic is exquisitely precise about this. And when the Qur'an describes the marriage of these two women to their prophet-husbands, it does not say they were *with* them. It does not say they were *of* them or *part of* them. Listen:

> **كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ**
>
> *Kānatā **taḥta** ʿabdayn* — "They were **under** two servants."

The word is **تَحْتَ — taḥta** — and its plain, root meaning is *underneath*, *below*. It is a word of position. A word of place. The very same word the surah will use, by the way, when it describes gardens *beneath* which rivers flow. It is spatial. It is locational.

Now, this is a known idiom in Arabic for the marriage relationship — to be "under" a man is to be married to him, in his household, under his roof and his name. So at one level this is simply how Arabic says *married to*. That is documented, that is plain, I'm not inventing a secret.

But stay with me. Of all the ways Allah *could* have expressed this marriage, He chose the word of **position**.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. *Taḥta* describes where two things sit relative to each other in space. A roof is over a house. A book is under a lamp. The relationship is real — but it is entirely *external*. The book does not become the lamp. The proximity is geometric, not essential.

These two women were *taḥta* their husbands. Under the same roof. Under the same name. Under the protection, the prestige, the daily presence of prophethood. For years. Sharing meals. Sharing a home. As close, physically and socially, as two human beings get.

And the word Allah chose tells you the exact nature of that closeness. It was **positional**. They were *near* the light. They were *under* the same sky as revelation. But "under" is not "inside." You can stand under a waterfall your whole life and stay bone dry if you never open your mouth. You can live *taḥta* a prophet and your heart can be a sealed jar.

Here is what's remarkable. The Qur'an could have built false hope into the grammar — could have used a word of belonging, of fusion, of *being part of*. It refused. It reached for the most external preposition available — a word about *where you are standing* — because that is the precise and terrible truth of these two lives. They had the *position* of the saved. They did not have the *substance* of it.

And every one of us knows people — maybe we *are* people — who have spent a lifetime *taḥta*. Under the right roof. Inside the right mosque. Beneath the right teacher. In the position of faith. The verse is asking, gently and without mercy: *under* is not *inside*. Which one are you?

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: عَبْدَيْنِ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا — "two servants from Our servants"

Now look at what these two husbands are *called*. This is the second small miracle, and it sits right next to the first.

Nuh and Lut. Two prophets. Two of the most monumental men in all of scripture — one who survived the Flood, one who confronted a civilization drowning in its own corruption. If you were going to name them in a verse about how *close* their wives were to greatness, what title would you reach for? *Prophets*. *Messengers*. *The chosen*. The grandest word you have.

Allah does not. He calls them:

> **عَبْدَيْنِ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا صَـٰلِحَيْنِ**
>
> *ʿAbdayni min ʿibādinā ṣāliḥayn* — "two **servants** from **Our servants**, righteous ones."

The root is **ʿayn-bā-dāl (ʿabd)** — *servant*, *slave*, the one who belongs entirely to a master. Twice in a single breath: *ʿabdayn* — "two servants" — and then *ʿibādinā* — "**Our** servants." The dual, then the plural with the divine possessive.

Let that land. At the precise moment the Qur'an wants to describe the *highest* human station these women were attached to — it strips the prophets of every grand title and calls them what they themselves were proudest to be called: **slaves of God.**

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter for our question? Because of what it does to the logic of the verse.

If the husbands were "prophets," you might think: *well, prophets are special, they're a category apart, of course there's a lesson here but it doesn't quite reach me.* But the verse says **ʿabd — servant.** And servanthood is the one station that is *open to every single human being.* You cannot become a prophet. Prophethood is sealed and chosen. But *ʿubūdiyyah*, servanthood — that is the very thing you were created for, the thing the verse is measuring everyone against.

So watch what the grammar has just done. It has taken the two women who were attached to the highest human reality — and named that reality not by something exclusive (prophethood) but by something **universal** (servanthood). The implicit sentence is devastating: *These men reached the summit of what you too are called to be. They were true ʿibād. And being married to a true ʿabd did not make these women ʿibād themselves.*

There is one more layer here, and it's the deepest. Notice the asymmetry. The husbands are **ʿibādinā** — "**Our** servants." They belong to Allah. Directly. The relationship runs vertically — servant to Master, with nothing in between. And that is *exactly* the relationship the wives did not have. The husbands were bonded *to God*. The wives were bonded *to the husbands*. One was a vertical rope, anchored above. The other was a horizontal rope, tied to a man.

And when the storm came, only the vertical rope held.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: فَخَانَتَاهُمَا — the betrayal you assumed, and the one that was real

Now we arrive at the word that, for centuries, has stopped readers in their tracks. Because most people read it wrong on the first pass, and the correction is the whole point.

> **فَخَانَتَاهُمَا**
>
> *Fa-khānatāhumā* — "so they both **betrayed** them."

The root is **khā-wāw-nūn (khāna)** — *to betray, to be treacherous, to break a trust.* And here is the trap. In our world, when we hear that a wife "betrayed" her husband, our minds go to one place immediately. Marital infidelity. The body. We assume the worst, the most obvious sin, the scandal.

And the classical scholars stop us cold.

Ibn ʿAbbas — and this is preserved for us in al-Baghawi's commentary — says plainly: *"Mā baghat imra'atu nabiyyin qaṭṭ"* — **no prophet's wife ever committed sexual immorality, ever.** Not one. That door is closed. So what was the betrayal?

Al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar — they are remarkably united on this. The betrayal was **in religion — fī al-dīn.** The wife of Nuh would go to his people and say: *he's a madman.* She would tell them he was insane. The wife of Lut would signal to her townspeople when guests — the angelic visitors — arrived in the house, betraying them to a predatory community: a fire lit at night, smoke by day, to alert the corrupt.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the correction? The betrayal was not of the *body*. It was of the *cause*. It was *ideological treason* — siding with the enemies of faith against the very mission of the man whose roof you live under.

And this reframes *everything* about the example. Because if the betrayal had been sexual, we could comfort ourselves: *well, that's a gross, obvious sin, I would never.* But the betrayal was a matter of *loyalty* and *belief.* It was the quiet, daily decision to align her heart with the crowd outside rather than the truth inside her own home.

Here is why this is so precise, and so frightening. These women had *every external advantage.* Front-row access to revelation. A husband who was a living miracle. And the betrayal that destroyed them was *invisible from the outside* — it was where the heart pointed. You could not have spotted it at the dinner table. It lived in the secret allegiance of the soul.

Which means the verse has just told you: the thing that decides your fate is not your scandals. It is your *allegiance.* Where, when no one is watching, does your heart actually stand?

Notice one more grammatical mercy. The verb is *dual* — *khānatā*, "they both betrayed" — these two women, paired across centuries, generations apart, mirror images of the same failure. Allah binds them together not by time but by *type.* They are one example wearing two faces.

[PAUSE]

### And then: فَلَمْ يُغْنِيَا — "they availed them nothing"

And now the verdict, in the most precise verb in the verse:

> **فَلَمْ يُغْنِيَا عَنْهُمَا مِنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا**
>
> *Fa-lam yughniyā ʿanhumā mina Allāhi shay'ā* — "so those two [husbands] **availed them nothing** against Allah."

The verb is **yughniyā**, from the root **ghayn-nūn-yā (ghinā)** — which at its core means *sufficiency, richness, having-enough.* This is Form IV of the verb, *aghnā*. I want to be careful and exact with you here, because the form matters. Form IV here is not "causative" in the crude sense; *aghnā ʿan* is the established idiom meaning **to avail, to be of use on behalf of someone, to suffice them against a threat.** It is the verb of *coming to someone's rescue with what you have.*

So the literal texture is something like: *the two prophets could not make their wives "rich enough" — could not supply them with anything sufficient — against Allah.* The wealth of prophethood, the spiritual capital of two of the greatest men alive, was a fortune the wives could not draw on. The account was in the husband's name. It did not transfer.

And look at the final word: **shay'ā** — "**a thing**," in the indefinite, after a negation. In Arabic that construction means *not the smallest thing, not anything at all, not one atom.* Not "they couldn't save them much." **Nothing. Zero.** Two prophets, standing right there, and the transfer of merit was exactly, mathematically, nothing.

[PAUSE — major]

### The bridge

So look at what these three small features have shown us, standing together. **Taḥta** — they had the *position*, not the substance. **ʿAbd** — the greatness they were near was the very thing they failed to become. **Khānatā** — and what undid them was not scandal but secret allegiance. And **yughniyā** — when the reckoning came, the nearness transferred *nothing.*

Three words. One message, sharpening with each: *you are standing on your own.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about grammar — but about reality itself. Because the verse does not stop at the women who fell. It immediately shows you two who stood. And the second half of this passage is going to take the lesson and turn it inside out.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Faith Is the One Thing You Cannot Inherit

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a deep, almost unconscious assumption most of us carry, and we rarely say it out loud because saying it out loud reveals how strange it is. The assumption is this: *I am covered by my environment.* I come from a religious family. I'm part of a good community. I married a practicing person. My parents were devout. I'm surrounded by faith. And somewhere in the back of the mind, a quiet voice concludes: *therefore I am fine.*

We do this with everything, by the way. We think proximity is possession. The person who hangs around successful people *feels* successful. The person with a gym membership *feels* fit. The person who follows brilliant thinkers online *feels* intelligent. We mistake being *near* a thing for *having* it. It is one of the most natural errors the human mind makes — to confuse the address with the house.

And nowhere is this error more dangerous than in faith. Because faith *looks* inheritable. It runs in families. It passes through communities. You can be raised in it, married into it, surrounded by it. So surely some of it sticks to you by osmosis?

[PAUSE]

The wife of Nuh lived with Nuh for — by the accounts — the better part of a thousand years of his mission. Think about that. Centuries of marriage to a prophet. Centuries of watching the most patient man in history call to God morning and night. If faith could be absorbed through proximity, through exposure, through sheer *time spent near greatness*, she would have been a saint a hundred times over.

She drowned.

And here is the thing the verse forces on us: it wasn't *despite* her closeness to Nuh. Her closeness to Nuh was completely *irrelevant.* It "availed her nothing" — *shay'ā.* The proximity simply did not enter the equation at all. When she stood before God, the marriage certificate was not in the file. Only her own heart was.

This is one of the loneliest truths in the Qur'an, and also one of the most dignifying. Listen to how the Qur'an says it elsewhere — *wa-lā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā*, "no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another" (35:18). *Wa-an laysa lil-insāni illā mā saʿā*, "man shall have nothing but what he strove for" (53:39). The accounts do not merge. Not even spouse with spouse. Not even prophet with wife.

[PAUSE]

Now — why do I call this *dignifying*, when it sounds so frightening? Because the same law that says *no one's faith can save you* also says *no one's failure can damn you.* The rope is vertical. It runs from *your* heart, directly, to your Lord, with no human being in the chain — which means no human being can cut it.

And that is precisely what the next example proves.

The wife of Pharaoh. Asiya. Living in the household of the man who declared himself God, who slaughtered infants, who is the Qur'an's very portrait of tyranny and corruption. If proximity worked in reverse — if you absorbed the spiritual state of those around you — she would have been the most lost soul on earth. She was *taḥta* Pharaoh. Under his roof. Under his name. Under the daily presence of absolute evil.

And she is held up, for all of time, as the example *for the believers.*

[PAUSE — major]

Do you see what has just happened? Allah took the two variables we assume are decisive — *who you're married to, what surrounds you* — and showed you they are noise. The wives of prophets: lost. The wife of Pharaoh: saved. The single variable that explains all of it is the one thing that lives entirely inside you and transfers to no one: *what your own heart chose.*

So here is what this changes about tomorrow. Stop checking the room. Stop taking attendance of who you're standing near and concluding you're safe. The question is never *whose house am I under.* The question is *what is actually inside me when I'm alone.* The Prophet ﷺ himself said to his own daughter, in essence: *Fatimah, daughter of Muhammad — work, for I cannot avail you anything against Allah.* If the daughter of the Messenger ﷺ is told to *work* — to act, to build her own faith and not lean on his — then the comfortable arithmetic of "I'm surrounded by good people" is over. Build your own house. Tonight.

### Theme Two: Asiya's Prayer — The House Before the Rescue

Now I want to take you inside the most beautiful prayer a trapped person ever made. Because most of us, at some point, have felt trapped — and Asiya's words rewrite what a trapped person should ask for.

First, the human reality. Picture being inside a toxic situation you did not choose and cannot easily escape. A cruel workplace. A poisoned family system. A marriage gone dark. A culture that mocks everything you hold sacred. And feel the way that environment presses on you, the way it whispers: *you are what surrounds you. You'll never be clean in here. This place defines you.* That is the lived experience of being *taḥta* something evil. It feels like a stain you can't wash off because you can't leave the room.

Now listen to what Asiya — the wife of *Pharaoh himself* — asks for. Her prayer has an order, and the order is the miracle:

> **رَبِّ ٱبْنِ لِى عِندَكَ بَيْتًا فِى ٱلْجَنَّةِ وَنَجِّنِى مِن فِرْعَوْنَ وَعَمَلِهِۦ وَنَجِّنِى مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ**
>
> "My Lord, build for me **near You** a house in the Garden, **and** save me from Pharaoh and his deeds, **and** save me from the wrongdoing people."

Look at the sequence. What does she ask for *first*? Not escape. Not relief. Not even survival. She asks first for **a house. Near You.**

[PAUSE]

The word order is everything. A drowning person asks to get *out of the water* first. Asiya, drowning in the household of a tyrant, asks first for *nearness to God* — **ʿindaka**, "with You, in Your presence" — and only *then* for rescue from Pharaoh. She prioritizes the *destination* over the *escape.* She wants the house with the Lord before she wants distance from the tyrant.

And notice the architecture of that little word **ʿindaka — "near You."** She does not ask for a house *in Paradise* and leave it at that. The Garden is unimaginable luxury — rivers, gardens, every delight. She mentions the location *with God* before she mentions the Garden it sits in. *Build me a house near YOU* — and, by the way, it happens to be in Paradise. The nearness is the point. The Garden is the address. Her heart was not asking for a reward; it was asking for *proximity to the only One worth being near.*

Do you feel the inversion of our whole first lesson? The wives of the prophets had *physical* proximity to greatness and no proximity of the *heart* — and it availed them nothing. Asiya had *zero* physical proximity to good — she was married to its opposite — and her heart reached for the only proximity that is real: *ʿindaka.* Nearness to God. And *that* nearness, the one nobody can give you and nobody can take, is the one that saves.

[PAUSE]

And then watch the second half of her prayer: *save me from Pharaoh **and his deeds.*** Not just from the man — from his *deeds.* She is not merely asking to escape a person; she is asking to be kept clean of his *actions*, his system, his corruption, so that none of it clings to her soul. She refuses to let the environment write itself onto her. She lives in the house of evil and prays to remain unstained by it — and Allah grants it, and lifts her up as the example for every believer until the end of time.

This is the answer to the trapped person's despair. You are not your surroundings. Asiya proves a human being can stand in the very center of corruption and have a heart oriented entirely toward God. The room does not define you. The *direction of your heart* defines you. *Taḥta* Pharaoh on the outside; *ʿindaka* God on the inside.

[PAUSE — major]

So what does this change tomorrow? When you feel trapped — by a job, a family, a culture, a marriage — stop asking only *how do I get out.* Ask first, like Asiya: *bring me near to You — and keep me clean of this while I'm still in it.* Make the house with God your first prayer, before the escape. Because the escape may not come on your timeline. But the nearness can come *tonight*, in the middle of the very room you're trying to leave.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now, after all of that, step back and look at the *shape* of what Allah built. Because the structure is the proof.

Four women. Two examples for the disbelievers, two for the believers. Watch how they mirror:

- **The wife of Nuh** and **the wife of Lut** — maximum proximity to good (married to prophets), heart turned away → **the Fire.**
- **The wife of Pharaoh** — maximum proximity to evil (married to the arch-tyrant), heart turned toward God → **a house near Him in the Garden.**
- **Maryam** — no husband at all, no human proximity in the equation whatsoever, heart wholly God's → honored above the women of the worlds.

Do you see the descending line? First: closeness to good, *wasted.* Then: closeness to evil, *transcended.* Then finally — Maryam — *closeness to no human being at all*, and the highest station of them all. The structure is walking you, step by step, away from the human variable entirely. By the time you reach Maryam, the lesson is complete: **the horizontal relationships have been removed from the equation, one by one, until only the vertical one remains.**

And let me show you the single most beautiful grammatical signature in the whole passage, saved for the very last word. Of Maryam, Allah says:

> **وَكَانَتْ مِنَ ٱلْقَـٰنِتِينَ**
>
> *Wa-kānat mina al-**qānitīn*** — "and she was among the devoutly obedient."

**Qānitīn.** That is a *masculine* plural. Arabic has a feminine plural ready and waiting — *qānitāt* — and it would have fit the meter, fit the grammar, fit the subject. Maryam is a woman. The expected word is *qānitāt.*

Allah chose the masculine plural.

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars read this as a soaring honor: Maryam is not being placed merely among devout *women.* She is enrolled in the ranks of the devout *as such* — the whole company of the obedient, men and women, prophets and saints, across all of history. The grammar lifts her out of any sub-category and places her in the *general assembly of the godly.* The very last word of the passage refuses to let her be filed under "women's examples." She stands in the front rank of *all* the servants of God.

And isn't that the perfect ending to a passage about proximity? The two who were *under* prophets are gone. And the one who guarded her heart and was *qānitah* — devout — is grammatically welded to the highest company there is. Not because of who she was *near.* Because of what she *was.*

### Bringing It Together

So the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight seen from two directions.

The grammar said it: **taḥta** is position, not substance. **ʿabd** is a station you must *become*, not one you can stand beside. **Khānatā** is about secret allegiance, not public scandal. **Yughniyā… shay'ā** — the transfer is exactly nothing. **ʿIndaka** — nearness to God is the only proximity that is real. **Qānitīn** — what you *are* enrolls you in a company that *where you stood* never could.

And the themes said the same thing in human terms: *Faith cannot be inherited, married into, or absorbed from the room. It is the one possession that is purely, terrifyingly, gloriously your own — and therefore the one that no tyrant, no failed marriage, no dark environment can ever take from you.*

The wife of Nuh had everything around her and nothing inside. Asiya had nothing around her and everything inside. The Qur'an built the whole structure so you could not miss which one counts.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. I genuinely don't know your answers, and neither, perhaps, do you yet.

1. When I feel spiritually safe, what am I actually leaning on — *my own relationship with Allah*, or my *proximity* to people who have one?
2. If everyone I'm "under" — my family, my community, my spouse — were removed tomorrow, what would be left of my faith, standing on its own?
3. Where is my heart's secret allegiance — the one no one at the dinner table can see? When the crowd outside pulls one way and the truth inside pulls another, which one do I quietly side with?
4. Like Asiya, can I ask for *nearness to God* before I ask for *escape* from my hardship — or have I made the relief the goal and forgotten the One?
5. Am I trying to *be* a servant — *ʿabd* — or only to stand *near* one?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Nearness to the righteous cannot save you and nearness to the wicked cannot doom you — before Allah, every soul hangs by the single vertical thread of its own faith, and that thread is the one no human hand can either tie for you or cut.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is the shift, if you're willing. Stop taking attendance of the room. Stop measuring your safety by who surrounds you. The most important relationship in your life is not the one you can point to — it is the invisible vertical line between your own heart and your Lord, and it is built in private, by you, one act of *qunūt* at a time.

You cannot inherit it. You cannot marry into it. You cannot absorb it from a community, however beautiful. But — and this is the mercy hidden inside the warning — *no one can take it from you either.* Asiya proved that in Pharaoh's own house. Maryam proved it with no house at all. The thread is yours. Tend it tonight. Not because you must — but because it is the one thing in all the world that is truly, only, gloriously *yours.*

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, do not let us be of those who stood **under** the light and never let it in. Make us **ʿibād** — Your servants in truth, not merely the neighbors of the righteous. Like Asiya, build for us a house **near You** before You grant us any escape, and keep our hearts clean of every corruption we are forced to stand inside. And let our final standing be among the **qānitīn** — the devoutly Yours — by what we truly are, and not by who we happened to be near.* Āmīn.
