# The Refuge That Burns: One Command, Two Enemies, One End

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single meaning.

> **يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ جَـٰهِدِ ٱلْكُفَّارَ وَٱلْمُنَـٰفِقِينَ وَٱغْلُظْ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَمَأْوَىٰهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ ۖ وَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ**

*Yā ayyuhā an-nabiyyu jāhid il-kuffāra wal-munāfiqīna waghluẓ ʿalayhim, wa maʾwāhum jahannamu wa biʾsa al-maṣīr.*

**"O Prophet! Strive against the disbelievers and the hypocrites, and be firm with them. Their abode is Hell — and wretched is the destination."**

Now — here is the strange thing about where this verse lives.

### Rebuild the world

Picture Madinah in these years. The community is no longer the tiny, hunted band of Makkah. It is a society now. It has weight. And weight attracts a new kind of person — not the open enemy who fights you from the outside, but the one who *joins* you on the surface while his heart stays elsewhere. The Qur'an gives this person a name that did not exist as a category until Madinah: **al-munāfiq**, the hypocrite. The one who comes through the front door, prays in the same row, and carries a hidden self that contradicts the shown self.

And notice what surah we are *in*. At-Tahrim — "The Prohibition" — opens with something almost shockingly intimate: a private matter inside the Prophet's ﷺ own household, a secret shared, a confidence broken, a domestic moment that Allah Himself addresses from above the seven heavens. The whole surah has been about **the inside** — the hidden, the private, the gap between the face shown and the heart held.

And then, just a few verses before the surah ends, comes this command. A command about confronting the people whose entire existence is that same gap — the hypocrites — the ones who have made a *lifestyle* out of the difference between the shown and the hidden.

And right *after* this verse? The surah will give us four women. Two who lived beside prophets and betrayed them on the inside. Two who lived beside tyranny and stayed sincere on the inside. [PAUSE]

So this verse is a hinge. On one side, a household and a secret. On the other, the clearest portraits of sincerity and pretense in the whole Qur'an. And right in the middle: *Strive against the disbelievers and the hypocrites.*

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin — three quiet shocks hidden in this short verse.

First: the Prophet ﷺ is given **one single verb** to use against **two completely different enemies** — and yet the scholars say that one verb is to be carried out in two opposite ways. How can one command split into two?

Second: Allah describes the final dwelling of these people with a word that, everywhere else, means **safety**. Shelter. The place you run *to* when you are afraid. He calls Hell their *refuge*.

And third: the Prophet of mercy — the one Allah elsewhere *praises* for not being hard-hearted — is here commanded with a word built from the root of **hardness**. The same root.

Three tensions. Let's open them slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: One verb, two enemies — *jāhid*

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we go near this word, because Arabic does something English simply cannot.

In English, a verb is mostly a verb. "I struggle." That's it. But in Arabic, verbs come in *patterns* — internal shapes, like gears — and the shape itself carries meaning before any root is poured into it. There are ten of these patterns. The third pattern — what grammarians call **Form III**, the *mufāʿala* pattern — has a very particular flavor. It is the pattern of **a thing done against, or toward, another party.** It implies that there is *another side*. It is directional. Confrontational. It is effort aimed *at* someone.

Think of it like the difference between "to run" and "to race." Running is something you do alone. Racing requires an opponent — the very word implies someone on the other side of you. Form III is the "racing" shape. It builds the opponent into the grammar itself.

Now look at the word: **جَاهِدِ** — *jāhid*.

The root is **j-h-d** (ج-ه-د), and its core meaning is *exertion to the point of difficulty* — to pour out effort, to spend everything you have against resistance. From this root we get *juhd*, "utmost effort," and the whole family of words around striving.

And it has been cast into Form III. So this is not merely "make effort." It is *exert your utmost effort against an opposing party.* The struggle and the opponent are fused into one word.

Now — here is the first quiet shock. Watch the objects of this single verb:

> *jāhid il-**kuffāra** wal-**munāfiqīn***
>
> Strive against **the disbelievers** *and* **the hypocrites.**

One verb. Two objects. And these two objects could not be more different from each other.

The **kuffār** — the root **k-f-r** means *to cover, to bury* (a farmer who buries the seed in the earth is called a *kāfir* in old Arabic, because he *covers* it). The disbeliever is the one who covers over the truth he has seen. But crucially — *he does it openly.* He stands outside the community. He fights you from the front. You know exactly where he is.

The **munāfiqīn** — and look at this root, **n-f-q** — it is the root of the *nafaq*, the **underground tunnel** that a desert animal like the jerboa digs, with a hidden second exit. The hypocrite is named after a creature that builds an escape tunnel. He never fully commits to being above ground. He keeps a hidden exit at all times. He is *inside* the community, in the same row of prayer, and you cannot see where his heart truly is. [PAUSE]

So one of these enemies is *outside and visible*. The other is *inside and invisible*. And Allah gives the Prophet ﷺ **the same verb** for both.

Let's sit with this. Because here is where the classical scholars do something beautiful.

They were troubled by exactly the question you should be feeling right now: *how do you "strive against" two enemies who are so different — when one is an open soldier and the other is a man who prays beside you?*

And here is their answer — and notice, this is not four scholars disagreeing. This is four scholars describing **one reality from four angles**:

Al-Jalālayn says: *jāhid* the disbelievers **with the sword**, and the hypocrites **with the tongue and with proof** (*bil-lisāni wal-ḥujja*).

Al-Muyassar expands the same point: strive against those who *openly declared* disbelief by confronting them, and strive against those who *concealed* it through argument, through establishing the truth, through upholding the limits and the rites of the religion — and use *firmness* with both.

Al-Qurṭubī adds the layer that takes your breath: strive against the hypocrites *by establishing the proof against them* — and **by making known to them their condition in the Hereafter**: that on the Day of Judgment they will have *no light* by which to cross the bridge alongside the believers.

Do you see what just happened? [PAUSE]

The *single verb* **jāhid** did not split because Allah was vague. It split because the verb was always about **meeting each enemy with what actually defeats him.** The open enemy is met with open resistance. But the hidden enemy — the one in the tunnel, the one whose weapon is *concealment* — cannot be defeated with a sword, because you cannot stab a hidden heart. The hidden enemy is defeated by **exposure.** By *ḥujja* — by proof, by light, by making the hidden visible.

You fight concealment with revelation.

So one verb, yes. But a verb intelligent enough to know that the way you strive against a lie that hides is to *drag it into the light* — not to swing at where you think it might be.

**What this single word reveals:** *jāhid* is not a blunt instrument. It is the command to bring your utmost effort against an enemy *in the form that enemy's nature requires* — steel against the open sword, and light against the hidden tunnel. [PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Prophet of mercy, commanded with hardness — *waghluẓ*

Now to the second word, and the second shock.

**وَٱغْلُظْ** — *waghluẓ.* "And be firm with them." Root **gh-l-ẓ** (غ-ل-ظ).

Let me teach the root first, plainly. *Ghilẓa* in Arabic is **thickness, coarseness, density.** It is the opposite of *riqqa* (delicacy) and *līn* (softness). A thick rope is *ghalīẓ*. Rough, coarse ground is *ghalīẓ*. It is a tactile, physical word — you can feel it between your fingers. It is the texture of something that does not yield, does not give, does not soften under pressure.

So Allah is telling the Prophet ﷺ: against these two enemies, *be dense. Be unyielding. Do not soften.*

Now — stay with me — because here is where this should stop you cold.

This is the same Prophet ﷺ whom Allah describes elsewhere as *bil-muʾminīna raʾūfun raḥīm* — "to the believers, deeply kind and merciful." This is the man whose gentleness was so total that Allah revealed, in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān (3:159):

> *"And had you been **faẓẓan ghalīẓa al-qalb** — harsh, **hard of heart** — they would have scattered away from around you."*

Look at the word in that verse: **ghalīẓa al-qalb.** *The same root.* **gh-l-ẓ.**

In Āl ʿImrān, Allah *praises* the Prophet ﷺ for **not** being *ghalīẓ* — for not being hard. Allah says: it was *by mercy from Me* that you were gentle, and *had you been hard*, they would have fled from you.

And here, in At-Tahrim, Allah *commands* him: **waghluẓ** — *be* hard.

So which is it? [PAUSE]

This is not a contradiction. This is one of the most precise lessons in the entire Qur'an about the nature of firmness — and you can only see it if you read the *two verses together*.

Look closely at Āl ʿImrān. The forbidden hardness is *ghalīẓa al-**qalb*** — hardness **of the heart.** The hardness is *located in the heart*. It is an interior condition. A *hard heart* is one that has lost the capacity to feel, to be moved, to have mercy.

Now look at At-Tahrim. The command is just *waghluẓ ʿalayhim* — be firm **upon them**, in your *stance*, in your *dealing*, in your *posture toward them.* The hardness here is **external** — it is a firmness of *position*, not a calcification of the *heart*.

Do you feel the distinction opening? [PAUSE]

A *hard heart* is a disease. It is the inability to be soft *where softness is owed.* That is what Allah praised the Prophet ﷺ for never having.

But a *firm stance* is a virtue — it is the refusal to be soft *where softness would be betrayal.* That is what Allah commands here.

The hypocrite *weaponizes* your softness. He counts on it. His entire tunnel-strategy depends on you being too gentle to confront the gap between his words and his heart. To the man who exploits mercy, **mercy itself becomes complicity.** And so the command comes: *waghluẓ.* Be the thick rope here. Do not yield. Not because your heart has hardened — but precisely so that your soft heart is not *used* against the truth.

**What this single word reveals:** True mercy is not the absence of firmness. It is *knowing where each belongs.* A heart that cannot be firm is not merciful — it is merely soft, and softness in the wrong place is a kind of cruelty to everyone the hypocrite will go on to harm. The Prophet ﷺ held both: a heart that never hardened, and a stance that never wavered. [PAUSE]

### Element Three: The refuge that burns — *maʾwāhum*

And now the third word — and to me this is the deepest one in the verse.

**وَمَأْوَىٰهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ** — *wa maʾwāhum jahannam.* "And their abode is Hell."

The word translated "abode" is **مَأْوَى** — *maʾwā* — and its root is **A-W-Y** (أ-و-ي).

Let me teach you this root, because the translation "abode" hides everything.

*Awā* — the verb — means **to take shelter, to seek refuge, to retreat to a place of safety.** A *maʾwā* is not just any dwelling. It is *the place you run to.* It is the den, the nest, the harbor, the sanctuary. It carries warmth in it — the sense of *coming in from the storm.*

You know this root, even if you didn't know you knew it. In Sūrat al-Kahf (18:10), the young believers fleeing persecution say: *fa-**ʾwū** ilā al-kahf* — "so **take refuge** in the cave." Same root. The cave was their *maʾwā* — their sanctuary from a king who would have killed them.

And Allah uses this same root tenderly for the Prophet ﷺ himself: *a lam yajidka yatīman fa-**āwā*** — "Did He not find you an orphan and **give you shelter**?" (93:6). The *āwā* there is the divine embrace — Allah Himself becoming the refuge of the orphaned child.

So this root — *A-W-Y* — is one of the most *comforting* roots in the entire Arabic language. It is the word for the place where fear ends. The place you finally exhale. The destination of every frightened, fleeing, homeless heart looking for somewhere safe. [PAUSE]

Now read the verse again.

> *wa **maʾwāhum** jahannam.*
>
> "And their **refuge** — their sanctuary, the place they run to for safety — **is Hell.**"

[PAUSE — let this sit]

Do you feel the horror folded inside the mercy of that word? Allah did not say *wa masjanuhum jahannam* — "their *prison* is Hell." He did not say "their *punishment*." He used the word for **the place of safety.** The place you flee *to.*

And here is what it unlocks — and this is the whole point.

Think about what a hypocrite spends his entire life doing. He is *always seeking refuge.* That is the nafaq — the tunnel — built into his very name. He keeps a hidden exit at all times. He runs to whichever side is safe. With the believers, he takes refuge in looking like a believer. With the disbelievers, he takes refuge in winking that he was never really one of them. His whole existence is a frantic, exhausting search for *somewhere safe to stand* — without ever committing to anywhere.

He made *not-committing* his refuge. He made *the hidden exit* his sanctuary. He thought safety was in never being pinned down.

And Allah says: *fine.* Here is your *maʾwā.* Here is the refuge that strategy actually leads to. **The fire.** [PAUSE]

The man who spends his life running *to* safety discovers, at the end, that the place he was running toward was burning the whole time.

And contrast — because the Qur'an always gives the believer the mirror image. The believers are told their *maʾwā* is *al-jannah* — the Garden. Same word. The believer also has a refuge he is running toward his whole life. But the believer *committed.* He stopped digging tunnels. He picked a direction and ran toward it with his whole, undivided heart. And his *maʾwā* — the place he finally exhales — is the Garden.

Two people. Both running toward a refuge their entire lives. **The only question that ever mattered was: which one?**

**What this single word reveals:** Every human being is running toward a *maʾwā* — a place where the fear finally stops. You cannot help it; it is built into you. The terror and the mercy of this verse is that *you* are choosing, right now, with every act of sincerity or pretense, *which refuge you are running toward.* The hypocrite's tragedy is not that he had no refuge. It is that he sprinted, his whole life, toward a fire he mistook for safety. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us.

*Jāhid* taught us that confronting falsehood means meeting each lie in the form its nature requires — and that the way to defeat *concealment* is *light.*

*Waghluẓ* taught us that firmness and mercy are not opposites — that a soft heart and an unyielding stance can, and must, live in the same chest.

And *maʾwā* taught us that every soul is running toward a refuge, and that the entire weight of a life comes down to *which one.*

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the two human beings hiding inside this verse: the one who pretends, and the one who is real.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The exhaustion of the hidden self

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You know what it feels like to perform. To walk into a room and become a slightly different person than you are. To laugh at something you don't find funny. To hold an opinion in public that you contradict in private. To manage the gap — constantly, exhaustingly — between the self you show and the self you are.

Modern people have a hundred words for this. We call it the "social mask." We call it "code-switching." We talk about "imposter syndrome" — the terror that someone will see the gap between the competent face you show and the frightened person behind it. We talk about the curated self online versus the real self at 2am. There is an entire epidemic of exhaustion in our time, and a huge part of it is simply this: **the metabolic cost of maintaining a self that isn't true.** Pretending is *work.* It burns fuel. It never stops burning fuel.

Now — the Qur'an has a word for the most extreme form of this. *Nifāq.* And the root, remember, is the **tunnel.** The animal that never lives fully above ground, that keeps a hidden exit, that is always ready to be somewhere other than where it appears to be.

The hypocrite is the person for whom the gap between shown and hidden *has become the whole personality.* He is not occasionally performing. He *is* the performance. There is a tunnel where a self should be. [PAUSE]

And here is what this verse does that is so merciful, even as it sounds so severe.

It tells the Prophet ﷺ to *strive against* the hypocrites with **ḥujja** — with *proof,* with *exposure,* with *light* — and al-Qurṭubī said: by *making known to them their condition.* Letting them *see* themselves. The cure for the tunnel is to bring it into the open.

Now, the verse is literally about the Prophet ﷺ confronting external hypocrites in Madinah — let me be precise; the classical commentators are united that this is the apparent meaning, and I do not want to dissolve it into pure metaphor. But notice *which surah this is in.* At-Tahrim. The surah of the inner household. The surah that will, in two verses, hold up four women as mirrors of sincerity and betrayal. This entire surah is teaching the community to look at **the inside.**

And so the believer who reads this verse cannot help but turn the lamp inward and ask: *is there a tunnel in me?* Where is the gap between my shown self and my hidden self? Where am I keeping a hidden exit — a part of my faith I'm not fully committing to, a sin I keep in reserve, a corner of my heart I haven't surrendered? Where do *I* perform belief that I haven't fully made real?

This is not the verse accusing you. This is the verse *handing you the lamp.* Because the same *ḥujja* — the same light — that the Prophet ﷺ was told to shine on the hypocrites *outside,* the sincere believer volunteers to shine on the hypocrite *inside.* He exposes his own tunnels before they harden into his whole self.

The hypocrite refuses to be exposed, and so the light comes for him from the outside, against his will, on the Day when — al-Qurṭubī reminds us — he will have *no light of his own* to cross the bridge.

The believer exposes himself, gently, daily, *now* — and so he builds light to carry across that same bridge.

**The core insight:** Sincerity — *ikhlāṣ* — is simply the collapsing of the gap. It is closing the tunnel. It is becoming *one self,* shown and hidden finally the same. And that is not just a spiritual ideal — it is the only true rest. The hypocrite is exhausted because he maintains two selves. The sincere person is at peace because he maintains one.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tonight, find *one* tunnel. One specific gap between your shown self and your hidden self — one performance, one reserved corner, one hidden exit. Don't fix all of them. Find *one,* name it honestly to yourself, and close it. Make the inside and the outside agree on one single thing. That is *jihād* against the hypocrite — the one who matters most, the one inside. [PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Which refuge are you running toward?

Now the second theme, and I want to begin again where you actually live.

Every single person is running toward a refuge. You are doing it right now. You do it every day. When the week is hard, your mind goes somewhere — there is a place, a person, a substance, a screen, a fantasy, a bank balance, a relationship — *something* that your soul treats as the place where the fear will finally stop. Where you can finally exhale.

That is your functional *maʾwā.* Not the one you'd write on a form. The real one. The one your feet actually run toward when you're afraid. [PAUSE]

For some people it's their wealth — the number that, if it just got high enough, would finally make them feel safe. For some it's another person — "if I just have *them,* I'll be okay." For some it's status, the good opinion of the room. For some it's an escape — the scroll, the screen, the thing that numbs. For some it's control — if I can just manage every variable, nothing can hurt me.

Now hold this verse against all of that.

> *wa maʾwāhum jahannam.*

Allah is making a claim about *where these strategies actually lead.* The hypocrites had a refuge-strategy too — the most sophisticated one of all. *Keep all exits open. Commit to nothing. Be safe everywhere by being real nowhere.* It is the ultimate hedge. And Allah follows it all the way to its destination and shows them: this road you thought led to safety — *it ends in the fire.*

And here's the thing about a *maʾwā* that the verse forces us to confront. A refuge has a *direction.* You move *toward* it. Which means **every false refuge is not just a mistake — it is a journey in the wrong direction.** Every time you run to wealth for the safety only Allah can give, you take a step *down the tunnel.* You're not standing still. You're traveling. Toward something.

This is why the verse pairs *maʾwā* with one final word: **al-maṣīr.**

*Wa biʾsa al-**maṣīr*** — "and wretched is the **destination**." Root **Ṣ-Y-R** (ص-ي-ر). And here is the last gift of the verse. *Maṣīr* comes from the verb *ṣāra* — which doesn't just mean "to arrive." It means **"to become."** *Ṣāra* is the verb of transformation. *He became.*

So *al-maṣīr* is not merely "the destination" as a place on a map. It is **what you become.** It is the final state you *turn into.* [PAUSE]

Do you see what the verse just did? It told us their *maʾwā* — the place they run to — and then it named that place their *maṣīr* — *what they turn into.* Because here is the deepest law hidden in this verse: **you become the refuge you run toward.** The thing you flee *to* for safety slowly *becomes* you. Run to wealth long enough and you don't just *have* it — you *become* it: anxious, hoarding, never enough. Run to others' approval long enough and you *become* it: hollow, shape-shifting, with no self underneath. The munāfiq ran toward the safety of the hidden exit so long that he *became* a tunnel — and the tunnel's destination was the fire.

And the believer? He ran his whole life toward Allah — toward sincerity, toward the one true refuge — and *biʾsa al-maṣīr* becomes its opposite: *niʿma al-maṣīr,* a *blessed* becoming. He doesn't just *arrive* at the Garden. He *becomes* someone fit for it. The running shaped him. The refuge he chose made him into the person who belongs there. [PAUSE]

**The core insight:** You are not a fixed thing watching life happen. You are *becoming* — every day, in the direction of whatever you treat as your refuge. The verse is not threatening you. It is showing you the machinery of the soul: *show me what you run to, and I will show you what you are turning into.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time you feel that pull — that flinch toward your functional refuge when life gets hard — pause for one breath and *name it.* "I am running to ___ right now." Just name it. And then, even once, redirect the run. Turn the same fleeing motion toward Allah — a single *"Yā Allah,"* a two-rakʿah refuge, a moment of *"I take shelter in You."* You will not rebuild your soul in one night. But you will have turned, once, toward the only *maʾwā* that does not burn. And you become what you turn toward — so turn, even once, and let the becoming begin. [PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse as one built structure, because its shape is the proof of its meaning.

> *Yā ayyuhā an-nabiyy* — **the one who is sent.**
> *jāhid il-kuffāra wal-munāfiqīn* — **two enemies: the open and the hidden.**
> *waghluẓ ʿalayhim* — **one firm stance over both.**
> *wa maʾwāhum jahannam* — **one refuge for both.**
> *wa biʾsa al-maṣīr* — **one becoming for both.**

Watch what the grammar does. It *opens* by splitting the enemy in two — the *kuffār* and the *munāfiqīn,* the visible and the invisible, two distinct words, two distinct natures. The Prophet ﷺ is even told to use two different methods: the open hand for the open enemy, the light of proof for the hidden one.

And then — the verse *fuses them back together.* Look: *ʿalay**him*** — "upon **them**," one pronoun. *Maʾwā**hum*** — "**their** refuge," one pronoun. One *jahannam.* One *maṣīr.* [PAUSE]

The verse separates the two enemies in **method** and unites them in **destiny.**

And *that* is the entire theology of this surah compressed into a sentence. The man who fights the truth openly and the man who undermines it secretly *look* completely different in this world — one is an honest enemy, the other wears your own face. But strip away the surface, and the hidden reality is identical: both *covered* the truth they knew. The *kāfir* covered it with denial; the *munāfiq* covered it with performance. Same act. Same sin. Same refuge. Same becoming.

The hidden truth was always the same beneath the two different faces. And that is *exactly* what this whole surah — the surah of the inside, the surah of the disclosed secret, the surah of the four women — has been teaching from its first verse: **Allah judges what is under the surface.** The grammar enacts it. Two faces above. One heart below. One *maṣīr* for both.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The *words* showed us: a verb intelligent enough to fight concealment with light; a firmness that is not the opposite of mercy; and a *refuge* that is secretly a *becoming.*

The *themes* showed us: that the hypocrite is exhausted by maintaining two selves, and that every soul is running toward a refuge that is slowly turning it into something.

Put them together and you get the single beating heart of this verse: **Collapse the gap. Become one self. Run, with that one undivided self, toward the only refuge that does not burn.** The cure for the hypocrite outside and the hypocrite inside is the same cure — *light, sincerity, and a chosen direction.*

### Questions to carry

1. Where, specifically, is the gap between the self I show and the self I am? Can I name *one* tunnel — and would I dare to close it?
2. When life gets hard this week, where do my feet *actually* run? What is my functional refuge — not the one I'd claim, but the real one?
3. If *al-maṣīr* means what I am *becoming* — then in the direction I am currently running, *what am I turning into?*
4. Is my softness ever in the wrong place — am I "merciful" where I am really just unwilling to be firm, including with my own nafs?
5. The hypocrite will cross the bridge with *no light of his own.* What light am I building *now,* in private, that no one sees?

### One-sentence distillation

The open enemy and the hidden enemy wear different faces but share one fate, because Allah judges the buried heart and not the shown face — so collapse the gap, become one true self, and run toward the only refuge that will not turn out to be the fire.

### The closing invitation

This verse came in a surah about a household, about a secret, about the gap between what is shown and what is hidden — and it is inviting you to do the bravest thing a person can do: **to be one person.** To let your hidden self and your shown self finally agree. Not because you are being watched, but because the exhaustion of the tunnel was never worth it, and the refuge at the end of it was always burning.

You are running toward something. You always were. This verse is simply Allah, in His mercy, stopping you on the road for a moment — *waghluẓ,* be firm, even with yourself — and turning your face, gently, toward home.

### A brief duʿā

*O Allah, You who gave the orphan shelter and named Yourself the refuge of the fleeing — close the tunnels in our hearts and make our hidden self and our shown self one. Let our maʾwā be Your Garden and not the fire, and let our maṣīr — what we are becoming — be a soul You are pleased to receive. Make us sincere when no one is watching, and firm where firmness is truth, and soft-hearted always. Āmīn.*
