# The Friends of Allah Are Not Defined by Spectacle but by Faith and Taqwa

## Surah Yunus (10:62-64)

[ayah:10:62] أَلَآ إِنَّ أَوْلِيَآءَ ٱللَّهِ لَا خَوْفٌ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

[ayah:10:63] ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَكَانُوا۟ يَتَّقُونَ

[ayah:10:64] لَهُمُ ٱلْبُشْرَىٰ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَفِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ ۚ لَا تَبْدِيلَ لِكَلِمَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

*Ala inna awliya'a Allahi la khawfun 'alayhim wa la hum yahzanun.*

*Alladhina amanu wa kanu yattaqun.*

*Lahumu al-bushra fi al-hayati al-dunya wa fi al-akhirah. La tabdila li-kalimati Allah. Dhalika huwa al-fawzu al-'azim.*

**Surely, the friends of Allah - there will be no fear upon them, nor will they grieve. Those who believed and used to have taqwa. For them is good news in the worldly life and in the Hereafter. There is no altering the words of Allah. That is the great triumph.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

After ayahs of warning, judgment, regret, witness, and total record, the heart needs to know what safety looks like.

Not imagined safety. Not the safety of denial. Not the safety of control. Real safety before Allah.

[PAUSE]

These ayahs answer with breathtaking simplicity. The awliya' of Allah are not introduced by hidden powers, private myths, or dramatic claims. They are introduced by two things so central and so demanding that the ego often looks past them: faith and taqwa. Then the surah gives them what every anxious heart wants most: no fear over what is coming, no grief over what has passed, and good news in both worlds.

The hook of the passage is this: **Yunus teaches that nearness to Allah is not a mystical category detached from obedience but a lived station of iman and taqwa, and that the real security flowing from that nearness is a divinely guaranteed freedom from ultimate fear and grief.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Awliya' Allah**: Nearness Is Named Through Belonging and Loyal Closeness

The passage opens:

**ala inna awliya'a Allah**.

Surely, the friends / allies / close ones of Allah.

[PAUSE]

Before the sentence delivers anything, it clears its throat. **Ala** is the particle Arabic uses to make a listener look up - the closest English is "listen" or "take note." Then comes **inna**, the particle of emphasis: "surely." Two alerts stacked on top of each other before a single piece of information arrives. The Qur'an does this when what follows is easy to miss or easy to get wrong. Both are true here.

The word **awliya'** carries nearness, loyalty, protective closeness, allied attachment. It is a deeply relational word. The root is about proximity - a *wali* is one who stands near, with nothing in between. Classical commentary keeps the word in that plain register rather than lifting it into mystery: al-Tabari glosses *awliya' Allah* as the helpers and supporters of Allah, and says directly that *wali* here means *nasir* - the one who backs you, the one who stands with you. Not a secret grade. A relationship of loyalty running in both directions.

But the ayahs immediately protect it from vagueness. They do not let us romanticize the title without definition.

The early commentators felt the pull of that title too. Al-Tabari notes openly that they differed over who deserves the name. One reported view is that they are people whose very appearance brings Allah to mind - the mark of goodness and quiet humility rests on them so visibly that seeing them reminds you of Him. Another keeps the door far wider: Ibn Kathir, commenting on this ayah, says Allah has already defined His friends Himself, so every God-fearing believer is a friend of Allah. The disagreement is worth knowing. It shows the tradition wrestling with the same temptation we have - to turn a description into an elite club - and it shows where the passage itself lands, because the very next ayah supplies the definition, and the definition is not a club.

### 2. **La khawfun 'alayhim wa la hum yahzanun**: Security Covers Future and Past Together

Then:

**la khawfun 'alayhim wa la hum yahzanun**.

No fear upon them, nor do they grieve.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the Qur'an's great formulas of security. Fear usually reaches forward toward what may come. Grief reaches backward toward what has passed or been lost. The ayah covers both horizons.

Notice how differently the two halves are built. Fear is negated as a *thing*: **la khawfun 'alayhim**, "no fear upon them," with the word for fear left indefinite - which in Arabic negation means not one fear of any kind. And the preposition is **'ala**, "upon." Fear is pictured as something that settles on a person from above, a weight pressing down. The ayah does not say they will not feel afraid; it says no such weight comes down on them at all. Grief, by contrast, is negated as an *action they do*: **wa la hum yahzanun**, "nor do they grieve," an ongoing present-tense verb. One is a burden lifted from over them. The other is a state that never takes hold inside them.

The classical readings also tell us which fear and which grief are meant, and they are more specific than a general calm. Al-Tabari reads it as: no fear upon them in the Hereafter from the punishment of Allah - and he gives the reason, that Allah is pleased with them, so He has secured them from that punishment - and no grief over whatever of this world passed them by. Ibn Kathir reads it the same way: no fear of the horrors ahead, no grief over anything left behind here. Al-Muyassar's one-line gloss says the same.

That is a more precise gift than it first sounds. The two things a human being can never fix for himself - what is coming that he cannot control, and what is gone that he cannot retrieve - are exactly the two things named.

That does not mean awliya' Allah never feel human emotion in dunya. It means the decisive existential fear and grief are removed from them under Allah's friendship.

### 3. **Alladhina amanu wa kanu yattaqun**: The Definition Refuses Spectacle

Then the ayah explains who they are:

**alladhina amanu wa kanu yattaqun**.

Those who believed and used to have taqwa.

[PAUSE]

This is stunning in its clarity. The awliya' are defined by iman and ongoing taqwa. Not by aura. Not by strangeness. Not by self-advertised spiritual specialness.

Grammatically, this is not even a new sentence. Al-Tabari points out that **alladhina amanu** is a description attached to **awliya'**, the way an adjective attaches to the noun it describes. He raises the technical question a student would ask - whether the phrase stands in the nominative or the accusative - and answers that it is nominative, following the word it describes. Read the two ayahs as one continuous statement and you hear it: *surely the friends of Allah, those who believed and kept being mindful of Him, no fear upon them, nor do they grieve.* The definition is not an afterthought appended to the announcement. It is welded into it. You cannot receive the title apart from the description; they arrive in the same breath.

And the form **kanu yattaqun** gives continuity. This was not a passing mood. Taqwa became a lived pattern.

The two verbs are deliberately different in shape. **Amanu** is a completed past: faith as something settled, an anchor already dropped. **Kanu yattaqun** - *kana* joined to a present-tense verb - is the standard Arabic way of saying "they used to keep doing this," an unbroken habit stretched across a life. Faith is stated once. Taqwa is stated as a repetition.

And the commentators refuse to leave taqwa as an atmosphere. Al-Tabari defines it here in working terms: being mindful of Allah by carrying out what He made obligatory and staying away from what He forbade. Al-Muyassar says the same in different words - obeying His commands, avoiding disobedience to Him - and glosses the iman as affirming Allah as true, following His Messenger, and accepting what he brought from Allah. So a two-word definition of a friend of Allah unfolds into something completely checkable: what you accept as true, what you do, and what you refuse to do.

### 4. **Lahumu al-bushra fi al-hayati al-dunya wa fi al-akhirah**: Good News Is Given in Both Homes

Then:

**lahumu al-bushra fi al-hayati al-dunya wa fi al-akhirah**.

For them is good news in the worldly life and in the Hereafter.

[PAUSE]

This is beautiful because it refuses a split imagination. Nearness to Allah is not only for later. The bushra reaches them already in dunya, and then continues into the akhirah.

Two small things in the wording carry weight. The phrase opens with **lahum**, "for them," placed ahead of the good news itself. Arabic fronts a word like this to reserve something: the bushra is theirs, assigned, already allocated. And **al-bushra** is definite - not "some good news" but *the* good news. The root of the word is the same one behind the word for the surface of the skin, because *bushra* is the kind of news that shows on a face before the mouth has reported it. It is news that changes how a person looks.

The exact forms of that good news may vary, but the point is that Allah does not reserve all reassurance for the final moment. He lets traces of divine glad tidings touch His people already here.

The commentators are careful here, and honest about their disagreement. Al-Tabari states plainly that the early scholars differed over what this worldly bushra is and what its description should be. The best-known reported position is that it is the good dream - the true dream a believing person sees, or that someone else sees about him - with Paradise as the bushra of the Hereafter. Others kept it broad: al-Muyassar simply says glad tidings from Allah in this life in whatever gladdens them, and in the next life the Garden. The ayah itself does not narrow it down, and neither should we. What is fixed is that some of the good news arrives early. The shape it takes is left open, and the tradition preserved more than one answer rather than settling on one.

### 5. **La tabdila li-kalimati Allah**: Their Security Rests on the Unalterable

Then:

**la tabdila li-kalimati Allah**.

There is no altering the words of Allah.

[PAUSE]

The negation is absolute in its very form. **La tabdila** places *tabdil* - the act of altering itself - under the kind of negation that denies a whole category. The sentence does not say "no one will alter the words of Allah," which would leave you scanning the horizon for whoever might try. It says alteration does not exist here. There is no such thing.

And **kalimat** is plural: words, not a single word. Every promise, every decree, every sentence He has spoken. Al-Muyassar reads the phrase as a promise-clause directly: Allah does not fail His promise and does not change it. So the good news of the previous line is not a hopeful forecast waiting to be confirmed. It has already been spoken, and speech from Allah does not get revised.

This is what makes the bushra stable. It is not fragile optimism. It is tied to words no one can revise. The believer's security rests not on mood, but on divine speech.

### 6. **Dhalika huwa al-fawzu al-'azim**: Triumph Is Redefined

Finally:

**dhalika huwa al-fawzu al-'azim**.

That is the great triumph.

[PAUSE]

One small word is doing heavy work: **huwa**, "it," sitting between "that" and "the great triumph." Arabic inserts this pronoun to lock a statement down - *that, and that alone, is the great triumph.* It is not one success among several. Everything else the word "winning" usually gets attached to is quietly demoted in a single syllable.

And **fawz** itself carries two motions: escaping what would have destroyed you, and reaching what you were after. Classical commentary spells both out - al-Muyassar explains that this is called the great triumph because it takes in rescue from everything a person dreads and the winning of everything a person wants and loves. Look at what that maps onto. Rescue from what is dreaded answers "no fear upon them." Winning what is loved answers "nor do they grieve." The last phrase of the passage is the first phrase of the passage, restated as a verdict.

The ayah redefines success. Real triumph is not gathered status, invulnerability, image, or worldly control. It is to stand under Allah's wilayah with the fear-and-grief problem fundamentally resolved by His promise.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: We Often Romanticize Wilayah and Underestimate Taqwa

Many people are attracted to the idea of being close to Allah, but less attracted to the daily discipline by which the Qur'an actually describes that closeness.

[PAUSE]

We like the language of spiritual intimacy, but we often imagine it detached from ordinary obedience. These ayahs refuse that fantasy. The awliya' are those who believed and kept practicing taqwa.

That means wilayah is nearer to honest faith, careful obedience, hidden sincerity, guarded boundaries, and repeated return than to anything theatrical. The Qur'an is rescuing the concept from spiritual vanity.

It helps to notice that "friend of Allah" functions here as a description, not as a title anyone hands out. The passage announces a category and then tells you at once what qualifies a person for it - and what qualifies is available to anybody. Ibn Kathir puts it in a single line: Allah has defined His friends Himself, and every God-fearing believer is one of them. That closes two doors at the same time. It closes the door on claiming the station for yourself on the strength of experiences or reputation, since the test named is faith and obedience, not sensation. And it closes the door on assuming the station belongs to other people - the rare, the visibly gifted, the historically famous - since the requirement was written in the plainest possible terms.

What is left is unglamorous and demanding. Al-Tabari's working definition of taqwa here - do the obligations, stay off the prohibitions - is not mysterious in the slightest, and that is exactly what makes it hard. A dramatic spirituality can be performed in bursts. This one has to be kept up, which is what **kanu yattaqun** was already saying: not a moment of taqwa, but a life of it.

### Theme 2: The Deepest Human Longing Is Freedom From Fear and Grief

Under many of our surface goals is one deeper longing: to be finally safe. To stop fearing what is ahead. To stop grieving in a way that swallows the heart. To rest.

[PAUSE]

These ayahs teach that this rest is not reached by control, accumulation, or denial. It comes through relationship with Allah built on iman and taqwa. Only then can the soul receive a kind of security that touches both future and past - no fear, no grief - because it is rooted in the nearness of the One who governs both.

And notice that the promise does not work by emptying a person's life of things worth fearing or grieving. Nothing here says the friends of Allah will never be frightened by a diagnosis or hurt by a loss. What the classical readings identify is narrower and deeper than that: the fear lifted is fear of Allah's punishment ahead, and the grief lifted is grief over the portion of this world that never came. Those two are the wounds nothing in this life can treat. You cannot secure your own ending, and you cannot go back for what you missed. The ayah does not tell you to stop caring about either one. It tells you both have been settled above you, by One who is pleased with you - which is the reason al-Tabari attaches to the security in the first place: they are safe from the punishment because Allah is pleased with them.

The rest of our fears stay negotiable, human, survivable. It is the two unsolvable ones that get answered here.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs announce the awliya' of Allah and immediately grant them freedom from ultimate fear and grief. They then define these close ones with disarming simplicity as those who believed and kept practicing taqwa. For them is bushra in this life and the next, and that promise is secured by words of Allah that cannot be altered, making this the truly great triumph.

[PAUSE]

Read the passage in reverse and its shape gets even clearer. The great triumph is guaranteed by unalterable words; the unalterable words guarantee the good news; the good news belongs to those who believed and kept being mindful; and those same people were introduced, at the very top, as having no fear and no grief. It is one chain, and the first link and the last link are the same claim said twice.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: have I made nearness to Allah feel more mysterious than the Qur'an makes it here? What would it look like for my longing for safety to be answered through deeper iman and steadier taqwa instead of through more control? And where do I still seek triumph in forms that cannot solve the fear-and-grief problem at its root?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **the friends of Allah are not a hidden class beyond the reach of ordinary believers, but those who live faith and taqwa long enough to stand under His nearness, His bushra, and His unalterable promise of ultimate security.**
