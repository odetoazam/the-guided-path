# Tawakkul Becomes Real When It Turns Into Du'a Under Pressure

## Surah Yunus (10:85-86)

[ayah:10:85] فَقَالُوا۟ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ تَوَكَّلْنَا رَبَّنَا لَا تَجْعَلْنَا فِتْنَةً لِّلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

[ayah:10:86] وَنَجِّنَا بِرَحْمَتِكَ مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ

*Fa-qalu 'ala Allahi tawakkalna. Rabbana la taj'alna fitnatan li-l-qawmi al-zalimin.*

*Wa najjina bi-rahmatika mina al-qawmi al-kafirin.*

**So they said: upon Allah we have relied. Our Lord, do not make us a trial for the wrongdoing people. And save us by Your mercy from the disbelieving people.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

It is one thing to say we trust Allah in principle. It is another thing to discover what trust sounds like when fear is active and danger is near.

Often the first proof that tawakkul has become real is not that anxiety disappears, but that the heart begins to speak differently. It stops circling only around the threat itself and starts turning that pressure into du'a.

[PAUSE]

That is what happens here. Musa told them to rely upon Allah if they were truly submitting. They answer not with philosophy, but with prayer. They declare tawakkul, then ask for two things: not to become a fitnah for oppressors, and to be saved by Allah's mercy from the disbelieving people.

It helps to remember who is speaking. Two ayahs earlier we were told that only a small group believed in Musa — a *dhurriyyah*, a handful from his people — and that they believed *'ala khawf*, on top of fear, afraid that Pharaoh and his chiefs would break them. So the voices in this du'a are not the confident majority. They are a frightened minority under a ruler the Qur'an describes as high-handed in the land and beyond all limits. That is the throat this prayer comes out of.

The hook of the passage is this: **Yunus teaches that tawakkul is not a vague spiritual posture but a state that quickly becomes du'a, asking Allah to guard both our faith and our fate under pressure.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **'Ala Allahi tawakkalna**: Their Reliance Is Declared Upward

They respond:

**'ala Allahi tawakkalna**.

Upon Allah we have relied.

[PAUSE]

This is beautiful because it shows Musa's instruction being received immediately. The word opens with *fa-* — the particle of "and right away." Musa says rely; and right away, they say we have relied. There is no gap in the sentence for hesitation to sit in.

The believers do not argue the point. They take the heart where he told them to take it. The preposition matters too: **'ala Allah**. Upon Allah. The weight is placed there.

And notice where those words sit. In Arabic you would normally expect the verb first — "we have relied upon Allah." Here it is flipped: *upon Allah* comes first, and only then *we have relied*. In Arabic that flip is how you say "and on no one else." Al-Muyassar spells out exactly this, glossing their words as: upon Allah alone, who has no partner, we depended.

There is one more quiet detail. Musa's own command in the ayah before used the same flipped order — *fa-'alayhi tawakkalu*, "so upon Him rely." They hand his own word order back to him. It is the sound of an instruction not merely obeyed but absorbed.

[PAUSE]

The verb itself is in the past tense: *tawakkalna*, we **have** relied. Not "we will try to." Not "we are working on it." They report it as something already done. Under that much fear, that is a striking way to speak.

The root is و-ك-ل, the root behind *wakil* — the agent you appoint to handle a matter you cannot handle yourself. When you appoint a wakil, you do two things at once: you decide you trust him, and then you actually hand him the file. Al-Tabari reads their statement along exactly those two lines — *bihi wathiqna*, in Him we placed our confidence, *wa ilayhi fawwadna amrana*, and to Him we handed over our affair.

That double meaning is worth holding. Confidence without handover is just an opinion about Allah. Handover without confidence is just exhaustion. Tawakkul is both: I believe He is able, and I have actually let go of the file.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir, commenting on Musa's command just before this, answers the obvious anxiety underneath it — what if I rely and it is not enough? He replies that Allah is sufficient for whoever relies on Him, and brings two verses to say it: *Is not Allah sufficient for His servant?* (39:36), and *whoever relies upon Allah, He is enough for him* (65:3). The one who takes Allah as his wakil does not need a second plan behind the first.

### 2. **Rabbana**: Reliance Opens Into Lord-centered Intimacy

Then:

**rabbana**.

Our Lord.

[PAUSE]

Tawakkul is not cold abstraction. It does not sound like stoic detachment. It turns into relational speech. They are not merely appealing to raw power. They are calling upon the nurturing Lord who tends, raises, and governs them.

That choice is worth noticing. What they are about to ask for is a display of overwhelming power — hold back a tyrant, break a grip. Yet they do not reach for a name of might. They reach for *Rabb*, the one who raises and provides and looks after. They ask the strong thing from the tender name.

And the address is stripped bare. Not "O our Lord" with the calling particle spelled out — just *rabbana*, the particle dropped. In Arabic, dropping it is the speech of someone standing close. There is no formal distance being kept here. This is the voice of people who are already near enough not to shout.

### 3. **La taj'alna fitnatan li-l-qawmi al-zalimin**: They Fear Being Turned Into a Trial

Then the first request:

**la taj'alna fitnatan li-l-qawmi al-zalimin**.

Do not make us a fitnah for the wrongdoing people.

[PAUSE]

This is a profound prayer. They are not only asking for personal comfort. They are asking that their condition not become a source of confusion, emboldening, humiliation, or apparent vindication for the oppressors.

Look at the verb: *taj'ala*, from ج-ع-ل, to make one thing **into** another. They are not asking Allah to remove a trial from them. They are asking Him not to turn *them* into one. In their own prayer they are the raw material, and they are asking not to be shaped into that particular object.

[PAUSE]

The word *fitnah* carries the image of putting metal in fire — heating it until what is really in it comes out. A fitnah is anything that turns up the heat until the truth of a person is exposed.

And here is something the passage does quietly. Two ayahs earlier, when we were told the small group believed while afraid, the fear was described with this very same root: they were afraid that Pharaoh *would put them to fitnah*. So the root has already been used once, pointed at them — they were the ones in danger of being tested. Now they take that same word and pray in the other direction: do not make **us** a fitnah for **them**. The word turns around in their mouths. They came in afraid of being tried, and their prayer is that they not become the trial.

[PAUSE]

What exactly did they fear? Here the classical commentators genuinely differ, and al-Tabari says so outright — he records that the scholars of interpretation disagreed about precisely what they were asking to be spared.

Al-Tabari's own reading is the striking one: *do not put these people to the test through us.* On this reading the fitnah lands on Pharaoh's people, and the believers are the instrument. They are asking Allah not to let their own crushing become the thing that hardens the tyrant further.

Al-Muyassar lays two readings side by side. First: do not give them victory over us, because that victory would become a fitnah *for us*, pulling us away from our religion. Second: or else the disbelievers themselves would be tried by their own victory, and would say — *if they had been upon truth, they would not have been beaten.*

[PAUSE]

That second sentence is worth sitting with, because it is one of the oldest arguments in human history and it is still in circulation. Winning is read as being right. Losing is read as being wrong. The believers are praying against a conclusion, not only against a defeat.

So the prayer runs in two directions at once, and both are worth praying. Do not let our suffering break our own faith. And do not let our suffering hand the wrongdoer an argument.

Sometimes a believer's public crushing becomes a fitnah for the wicked: they read it as proof that their way is right, or as permission to harden further. The believers ask Allah to protect them from becoming fuel for the moral confusion of the oppressor.

### 4. **Wa najjina bi-rahmatika**: Rescue Is Requested Through Mercy

Then:

**wa najjina bi-rahmatika**.

Save us by Your mercy.

[PAUSE]

Notice first that this is joined to what came before with a simple *wa*. It is one breath, one du'a, in two movements: first a request that something not happen, then a request that something does. And notice the order. They ask about the *meaning* of their situation before they ask about their own survival. Most of us would have reversed that.

The verb *najjina* comes from ن-ج-و. The simple form of this root means to get out, to come through. But the form used here is the intensified one — not "let us slip away" but "pull us out." Al-Tabari's gloss makes the picture physical: extricate us, he says, *from the hands* of the disbelieving people. Hands. A grip you cannot open by yourself.

[PAUSE]

This is subtle and beautiful. Rescue could have been asked in the language of power alone. But they ask through **rahmah**. As though they know that even deliverance from tyrants is not something they are owed. It is a mercy-gift from Allah.

The little *bi-* in front of *rahmatika* is the "by means of" preposition. They are naming the instrument of their own rescue — and the instrument they name is one of Allah's attributes. Not their patience. Not their sincerity in believing while everyone else stayed silent. Not even Musa's miracles, which they had just watched. When it came time to name what would get them out, they named His mercy.

That is a remarkable thing for the only people in Egypt who had believed to say. They had the strongest case anyone could have made. They did not make it.

### 5. **Mina al-qawmi al-kafirin**: The Real Divide Is Ultimately Moral and Theological

The passage ends:

**mina al-qawmi al-kafirin**.

From the disbelieving people.

[PAUSE]

The problem is not only political oppression. The Qur'an keeps returning the matter to its deeper truth. The oppressive structure is rooted in kufr. Rescue is not merely escape from bad rulers. It is rescue from a people shaped by refusal of Allah.

Notice that the same word — *al-qawm*, the people — appears in both ayahs, but with a different description attached each time. In the first request they are *al-zalimin*, the wrongdoers: this is what they do sideways, to other human beings. In the second they are *al-kafirin*, the disbelievers: this is what they are upward, before Allah. One people, seen from two directions. The Qur'an will not let this be filed away as merely a political conflict, and it will not let it be abstracted into a purely spiritual one either.

[PAUSE]

And it is worth knowing what "save us" actually meant on the ground that day. Both al-Tabari and al-Muyassar name the people concretely — Pharaoh and his chiefs — and both give the same reason the prayer was urgent: these were the people who had them in forced labor, driving them at grueling work and putting them to the most degrading menial service.

So this is not a poetic prayer about spiritual bondage. It is a prayer said by people who would go back to that labor the next morning. When they said *save us*, they meant the work, the hands, the humiliation, the whole daily arrangement.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: True Tawakkul Immediately Teaches the Tongue How to Pray

We sometimes speak about tawakkul as though it were only an inward feeling. But these ayahs show that once the heart begins to rely, the tongue begins to ask. Trust and du'a are deeply intertwined.

[PAUSE]

This can be a useful self-audit. When pressure increases, what language comes out of us? Do we only narrate the threat, or do we begin addressing Allah from inside it? Tawakkul becomes audible.

### Theme 2: Believers Should Care About the Moral Meaning of Their Public Fate

This prayer is remarkably mature because it is not self-enclosed. They care not only whether they survive, but what their visible condition will mean in the moral imagination of the oppressor.

[PAUSE]

That is a deep lesson. Sometimes we pray only for relief. But the Qur'an teaches us also to pray that our trials not become confusion for others, nor ammunition for falsehood.

It is also a lesson in how carefully they were thinking. They understood that the oppressor was not only going to hurt them — he was going to *interpret* them. Their bodies were going to be read as evidence for something. So they prayed about the reading, not only about the bodies.

### Theme 3: Trust Has Two Halves, and Most of Us Only Do One

Al-Tabari's double gloss on their words gives us a quiet diagnostic tool. Trust, he says, is *we placed our confidence in Him* and *we handed our affair over to Him*.

[PAUSE]

Most of the time our difficulty is not the first half. We usually do believe, in the abstract, that Allah is able. The difficulty is the second half — actually letting go of the file. We keep rehearsing the problem, keep running the scenarios, keep holding the case in our own hands while announcing that we have handed it over.

The test is simple enough to apply. After I said I trust Allah with this, did my thinking about it change at all?

### Theme 4: The Answer Came, but Not in the Shape They Asked For

There is something instructive in what happens immediately after this du'a. They ask to be pulled out from the hands of the disbelieving people — and the very next ayah does not part a sea or open a border. Allah reveals to Musa and his brother to settle their people in houses in Egypt, to make those houses places of prayer, to establish the salah, and to give the believers good news.

[PAUSE]

They asked for the exit. They were first given a qiblah.

The rescue would come. But the first thing handed to them was not the escape route — it was a way to be a worshipping community inside the very place they had asked to leave, and a promise attached to it. Sometimes the answer to "get me out" begins with "here is how to stand before Me while you are still in."

## Closing Synthesis

The believers answer Musa's command with a direct declaration of reliance upon Allah. That reliance immediately unfolds into du'a: asking not to become a fitnah for the wrongdoing people and pleading for rescue through Allah's mercy from the disbelieving people.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: what does my trust sound like when I am actually under pressure? Do I know how to turn fear into du'a, or do I mostly turn it into rumination? And when I ask for rescue, do I ask only for relief, or also for Allah to protect the moral meaning of my trial?

[PAUSE]

And a second question, smaller but sharper. When I name what will get me out of my situation, what do I name? My own effort, my own good record, my own sincerity? Or, like them, His mercy — the only thing they named while standing on the strongest case anyone in Egypt could have made.

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **real tawakkul does not remain a slogan; it becomes prayer that asks Allah to guard the believer from both outward destruction and from becoming a source of confusion in the hands of the unjust.**
