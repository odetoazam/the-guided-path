# Even After Rescue, Provision, and a Good Settlement, Knowledge Can Become a New Site of Division

## Surah Yunus (10:93)

[ayah:10:93] وَلَقَدْ بَوَّأْنَا بَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ مُبَوَّأَ صِدْقٍ وَرَزَقْنَـٰهُم مِّنَ ٱلطَّيِّبَـٰتِ فَمَا ٱخْتَلَفُوا۟ حَتَّىٰ جَآءَهُمُ ٱلْعِلْمُ ۚ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَقْضِى بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ فِيمَا كَانُوا۟ فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

*Wa la-qad bawwa'na Bani Isra'ila mubawwa'a sidqin wa razaqnahum mina al-tayyibat. Fa-ma ikhtalafu hatta ja'ahumu al-'ilm. Inna rabbaka yaqdi baynahum yawma al-qiyamati fima kanu fihi yakhtalifun.*

**And We certainly settled the Children of Isra'il in a good settlement and provided them from the good things. Then they did not differ until knowledge came to them. Indeed your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they used to differ.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

We often imagine that once oppression ends and provision arrives, the hardest part is over.

But the Qur'an regularly shows that some of the deepest moral tests begin after rescue. A people can be delivered from tyrants, settled in goodness, fed from what is wholesome, and still discover that the next trial is not persecution from outside, but division from within.

[PAUSE]

Look at where this ayah sits. One verse earlier, Fir'awn's body is pulled from the water and kept as a sign for those who come after him, and the passage immediately adds that many people are heedless of Allah's signs. So the surah has just told us that a plain, physical, undeniable sign does not automatically produce belief. Now, in the very next breath, it tells us that plain, clear knowledge does not automatically produce unity. The two verses run on the same track: the problem was never the quality of what Allah gave. It was the condition of what received it.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is brief, but severe. Bani Isra'il are given a truthful settlement and good provision. Yet their اختلاف emerges not in the absence of knowledge, but after knowledge comes. The unresolved matter is then handed to Allah's final judgment.

The hook of the passage is this: **Yunus teaches that external rescue and generous provision do not automatically produce inward unity, and that knowledge itself can become the arena of division when hearts are not healed enough to receive it rightly.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Bawwa'na... mubawwa'a sidqin**: Settlement Is Described Through Truthfulness

The ayah says:

**bawwa'na Bani Isra'ila mubawwa'a sidqin**.

We settled the Children of Isra'il in a settlement of truth.

[PAUSE]

Notice first how the sentence is built. The verb and its object come from the same root, بوأ. Allah does not say "We gave them a place." He says, in effect, *We settled them a settling.* The verb is the kind that means to put someone into a place, to house them, to establish them somewhere. Repeating the root doubles the weight: the settling was real, deliberate, and entirely His doing. They did not find this land. They did not win it by cleverness. It was assigned.

[PAUSE]

That root has already appeared in this same surah, and the echo is worth hearing. Back when Bani Isra'il were still living under Fir'awn in fear, Musa and Harun were told, *tabawwa'a li-qawmikuma bi-Misra buyutan* — take dwellings for your people in Egypt — and in the same breath they were told to make those houses places of prayer and to establish the prayer. So the first "dwelling" in this surah was a frightened one, taken under pressure, and even then it was supposed to be oriented toward worship. Here, six verses later, the frightened arrangement becomes a granted homeland. The same root that named their hiding place now names their inheritance.

[PAUSE]

Then there is the word **sidq**. Truthfulness, soundness, uprightness. The dwelling is not described only as safe or comfortable; it is described as *true*. It is not a morally ambiguous gift, not stolen ground, not a place that will turn out to have been a trap. It is a real, good, fitting settlement.

This exact phrase, *mubawwa'a sidqin*, occurs only here in the entire Qur'an. The Qur'an is sparing with this construction. It speaks of a tongue of truth, a seat of truth, an entrance and an exit of truth — and here, a dwelling of truth. Each time, "of truth" is Allah's own stamp on something He gives, marking it as genuine rather than borrowed or hollow.

[PAUSE]

And there is a quiet bracket around this surah worth noticing. Surah Yunus opens by telling the Prophet ﷺ to give good news to those who believe that they have *qadama sidqin* — a footing of truth — with their Lord. That phrase, too, occurs only once in the Qur'an, and it is here in Surah Yunus. The surah begins with a *footing* of truth promised to believers with their Lord, and near its end recalls a *dwelling* of truth already granted to an earlier community in this world. One is a standing that cannot be lost. The other, as we are about to see, was kept and then squandered.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators differ on where exactly this settlement was. Al-Tabari renders the phrase as "dwellings of truth" and reports two positions side by side: that it refers to Sham together with Jerusalem, and that it refers to Sham together with Egypt. Ibn Kathir gives the same range — Egypt and Syria, around Jerusalem — and notes that when Fir'awn and his army were destroyed, the land they had ruled passed into the hands of those they had ruled. He reads it alongside the verse that says Allah made the people who had been oppressed inherit the east and the west of the land which He had blessed. Al-Muyassar simply says: a good and chosen dwelling in the lands of Sham and Egypt.

The disagreement over the map does not change the point. Whatever the borders, the ayah insists on the quality of the grant. It was true ground, and it came from Him.

### 2. **Wa razaqnahum mina al-tayyibat**: Provision Comes in a Wholesome Register

Then:

**wa razaqnahum mina al-tayyibat**.

We provided them from the good things.

[PAUSE]

The word **tayyibat** is morally textured. Not just abundance, but wholesome goodness. The Qur'an is establishing that what they received from Allah carried both generosity and purity. Al-Muyassar makes this explicit: the provision was *halal* and *tayyib* — lawful as well as pure — and it came from the good things of a blessed land.

That matters more than it first appears. A community can be rich on food that is filthy in how it was obtained. Here the ayah closes that escape route in advance. Nothing in their material life was tainted. The house was true, the food was clean.

[PAUSE]

There is a pattern in where this exact phrase turns up. *Wa razaqnahum mina al-tayyibat* appears three times in the Qur'an, and all three times it sits inside a sentence about a lineage being honoured, opening the same way: *wa la-qad* — "and We certainly." We certainly honoured the children of Adam... and provided them from the good things. We certainly gave the Children of Isra'il the Book, the judgment, and prophethood... and provided them from the good things. And here: We certainly settled the Children of Isra'il in a dwelling of truth... and provided them from the good things.

So this phrase is never a throwaway line about groceries. Every time the Qur'an says "We provided them from the good things," it is listing the evidence of a dignity conferred. It is Allah putting the receipts on the table.

[PAUSE]

Which sets up the turn that follows, and makes it hurt.

### 3. **Fa-ma ikhtalafu hatta ja'ahumu al-'ilm**: Division Erupts After Knowledge

Then the startling line:

**fa-ma ikhtalafu hatta ja'ahumu al-'ilm**.

They did not differ until knowledge came to them.

[PAUSE]

This overturns an easy excuse. We often imagine people divide only because they do not know enough. But here knowledge arrives, and difference follows. That means the issue cannot be reduced to mere informational deficiency. Hearts, desires, status, ego, and unwillingness to submit all have to be considered.

The verb itself carries the shape of the problem. **Ikhtalafu** comes from the root خلف, and it is the form of the verb that means parties taking up positions against one another — not one person quietly holding an opinion, but people setting themselves opposite each other. It is division as a social act, done together, in both directions.

[PAUSE]

And the commentators are careful about what they divided *over*. Al-Muyassar reads it as the matter of their religion — not politics, not land, not commerce, but *din*. The one thing that was supposed to hold them together is the thing they broke over.

He then adds a phrase that is worth sitting with. He calls what came to them the knowledge that *required* their gathering and their unity. That is the sharp edge of this ayah. The knowledge was not neutral information that they happened to disagree about. It was the very thing whose whole function was to unite them. It arrived carrying an obligation to come together, and they used it to come apart.

[PAUSE]

Now look at how rare this sentence is. The exact wording *fa-ma ikhtalafu* — "then they did not differ" — occurs only twice in the whole Qur'an: here, and in Surah al-Jathiyah. And the phrase *ja'ahumu al-'ilm*, "knowledge came to them," occurs four times: here, in Aal Imran, in ash-Shura, and again in al-Jathiyah.

Read those four together and something becomes visible. In three of them, the Qur'an names the cause of the split outright: **بَغْيًۢا بَيْنَهُمْ** — out of rivalry and overreach between themselves. Envy. Wanting to be above one another. That is the Qur'an's own diagnosis, stated in its own words, of why people who have been given clarity still fracture.

[PAUSE]

In our ayah, that diagnosis is left out. Here, the ayah stops at the fact and says nothing about the motive.

The verse in al-Jathiyah is the near twin of this one — same phrase about provision from the good things, same "then they did not differ," and a closing sentence that is word for word identical to this one. And that twin supplies exactly the two words this one withholds: *baghyan baynahum*. So the Qur'an has not hidden the answer. It has placed it one verse away, in the sister passage, for the reader who keeps looking.

That is a mercy in the way it is arranged. Standing in front of this ayah alone, you are made to ask the question — *why would knowledge divide people?* — before you are handed the answer. And the answer, when you find it, is not about the knowledge at all. It is about wanting to win.

[PAUSE]

One more detail. Our ayah says they did not differ *hatta* — until — knowledge came. Its twin says they did not differ *except after* knowledge came. Al-Muyassar, explaining our verse, actually reaches for that second wording to gloss the first. Either way the sequence is fixed and undeniable: the knowledge came first, the splitting came second.

### 4. **Yaqdi baynahum**: Some Differences Are Deferred to the Final Court

Then:

**inna rabbaka yaqdi baynahum yawma al-qiyamati**.

Your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection.

[PAUSE]

This returns the matter to Allah. Not every historical dispute is resolved inside history. Some are carried all the way to the final court, where neither inherited claim nor communal self-justification will protect anyone.

The verb **yaqdi**, from the root قضي, is not "to have an opinion" or even "to rule." It carries the sense of finishing a thing off, closing it, leaving nothing hanging. Which makes it the exact opposite of what the community was doing. Their side of the sentence is *kanu fihi yakhtalifun* — a past-tense "they were" attached to an ongoing present-tense verb, which in Arabic paints a habit that kept repeating: they kept on differing, generation after generation, with no end in sight. His side of the sentence is one decisive act that ends it. Endless argument on one side; a single final word on the other.

[PAUSE]

And notice who is being addressed. **Inna rabbaka** — *your* Lord. Not "their Lord," not "Allah" in the third person. In the middle of a verse about an ancient community, the grammar swings around to speak directly to the Prophet ﷺ. This is Meccan revelation, delivered to a man watching his own people split over a message he knew was true. The line is a report about Bani Isra'il and, at the same time, a hand placed on his shoulder. Al-Muyassar reads the final clause with exactly this in mind: what they differed over includes the matter of *you*, O Messenger — and on that Day the deniers go one way and the believers the other.

Some commentators go further and identify part of "the knowledge" that came to them as what their own Scripture contained about the coming of this Prophet ﷺ. That is a reading drawn from the commentators rather than something the ayah states outright, and it is worth naming as such. But it does explain why the very next verse in the surah turns and tells the Prophet ﷺ that if he is in any doubt about what was revealed to him, he should ask those who were reading the Scripture before him — and then reassures him that the truth has come to him from his Lord. The knowledge itself is still sound and still consultable. It is the people holding it who divided.

[PAUSE]

There is also an answer inside this same surah to the obvious question: if the dispute is settled, why not settle it now? Earlier in Yunus, the Qur'an says that humanity was one single community and then differed, and that were it not for a word that had already gone forth from your Lord, it would have been decided between them right there concerning what they differ in. So the delay is not indifference. It is a deliberate word already spoken — a stay of judgment that leaves room for people to turn back. The final clause of our ayah, incidentally, appears in exactly one other place in the Qur'an, and it is that twin verse in al-Jathiyah. The Qur'an says this sentence twice, and both times about the same community.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Rescue From External Tyranny Does Not Cure Internal Disease

It is tempting to think that if only hostile circumstances were removed, a community would automatically become healthy. The Qur'an repeatedly refuses that simplification.

[PAUSE]

This ayah warns us that a people can inherit good space and wholesome provision while still carrying inner conditions that later rupture into division. Environment matters, but it is not the whole story.

And this ayah goes out of its way to remove every environmental excuse before the division is even mentioned. The house was true. The food was lawful and pure. The land was blessed. The oppressor was gone, and his body had been kept as a public sign. Ibn Kathir's note is worth holding here: the very territory Fir'awn had ruled passed to the people he had crushed, in fulfilment of Allah's promise that the oppressed would inherit the land He had blessed. Everything on the outside had been fixed.

[PAUSE]

So when the fracture comes, there is nowhere to point. Not at poverty. Not at persecution. Not at bad conditions. The only remaining variable is the inside of the people themselves.

That is an uncomfortable mirror for any community that has ever told itself it would be united if only the pressure let up. Sometimes the pressure is what was holding it together, and its removal simply reveals what was always there. A shared enemy can imitate brotherhood for a long time.

[PAUSE]

There is also something to notice in the earlier instruction that their houses in Egypt be made places of prayer. From the beginning, in this surah, the dwelling was never meant to be only shelter. It was meant to be oriented — a space with a direction, built around worship. A settlement of truth that stops facing Allah is still a good house. It has simply lost the thing that made it worth being given.

### Theme 2: Knowledge Without Purified Hearts Can Multiply Dispute

The ayah's sequence is haunting precisely because it names knowledge before division. We should let that unsettle us. More access, more literacy, more discourse, more detail, more argument - none of these automatically produce unity if the soul is still dominated by rivalry, ego, or selective submission.

[PAUSE]

The danger is especially subtle because people can then use knowledge itself as the material of their conflict while imagining they are honoring it.

And the Qur'an, in the parallel verses, is blunt about the engine underneath. *Baghyan baynahum.* Rivalry between them. The problem is not that two sincere people read a text and reached different conclusions — the Qur'an is not condemning honest difference here. The problem is people who want position, who want to be the ones who are right, who need the other party to be wrong. Knowledge is simply the nearest available weapon. Give that heart clarity and it will find a way to fight with it.

[PAUSE]

Ash-Shura describes the same disease with a different verb — they *split apart* after knowledge came to them, out of rivalry between themselves — and then adds that those who inherited the Book afterwards were left in unsettling doubt about it. That is the downstream cost. One generation's ego-driven argument becomes the next generation's confusion. The children inherit the split without inheriting the certainty.

[PAUSE]

This is where the commentators' phrase lands hardest. The knowledge that came to them was the knowledge that *obliged* them to come together. It was the cure. And a cure that is taken by a heart set on winning does not just fail; it becomes the occasion of the illness getting worse. That is the specific horror of this verse. Nothing went wrong with the medicine.

[PAUSE]

Yet the very next verse in the surah refuses to let this collapse into cynicism about knowledge itself. The Prophet ﷺ is told that if he doubts, he should ask the people who have been reading the Scripture. The people divided; the Book did not stop being true. So the lesson is not "knowledge is dangerous, stay away from it." The lesson is that knowledge is not enough on its own, and that the missing piece has never been more information. It is a heart that is willing to lose an argument.

## Closing Synthesis

Bani Isra'il are given a settlement described through truthfulness and are provided from wholesome things. Yet their later اختلاف does not wait for ignorance; it follows the arrival of knowledge. The ayah then leaves the unresolved matter with Allah, who will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning what they kept disputing.

[PAUSE]

Hold the two "truth" words of this surah together. It opens by promising believers a footing of truth with their Lord — something held with Him, out of reach. It arrives here at a dwelling of truth granted on the ground, in history, to a community that then broke apart inside it. What is kept with Allah is safe. What is handed to us is a test.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: do I still imagine that more knowledge alone will heal every division? What inner diseases in me could turn even good gifts and true knowledge into new occasions of conflict? And do I remember that some unresolved disputes are not ultimately settled by louder argument, but by Allah's final judgment?

[PAUSE]

And one more question, taken from the word the Qur'an supplies in the sister verse: in the last argument I had about religion, was I trying to arrive at what is true, or trying to be the one who was right? That is the whole distance between knowledge that gathers and knowledge that divides.

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **Allah may give a community truthful settlement, wholesome provision, and knowledge, yet if the heart is not disciplined those very gifts can become the setting of division whose final resolution belongs only to Allah.**
