# Faith Can Begin Small, Shaking, and Afraid - Then Be Taught Tawakkul

## Surah Yunus (10:83-84)

[ayah:10:83] فَمَآ ءَامَنَ لِمُوسَىٰٓ إِلَّا ذُرِّيَّةٌ مِّن قَوْمِهِۦ عَلَىٰ خَوْفٍ مِّن فِرْعَوْنَ وَمَلَإِي۟هِمْ أَن يَفْتِنَهُمْ ۚ وَإِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ لَعَالٍ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَإِنَّهُۥ لَمِنَ ٱلْمُسْرِفِينَ

[ayah:10:84] وَقَالَ مُوسَىٰ يَـٰقَوْمِ إِن كُنتُمْ ءَامَنتُم بِٱللَّهِ فَعَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلُوٓا۟ إِن كُنتُم مُّسْلِمِينَ

*Fa-ma amana li-Musa illa dhurriyyatun min qawmihi 'ala khawfin min Fir'awna wa mala'ihim an yaftinahum. Wa inna Fir'awna la-'alin fi al-ard wa innahu la-mina al-musrifin.*

*Wa qala Musa ya qawmi in kuntum amantum bi-Allahi fa-'alayhi tawakkalu in kuntum muslimin.*

**But none believed in Musa except a young offspring group from his people, upon fear of Fir'awn and their chiefs lest he put them through trial. Indeed Fir'awn was surely exalted in the land, and indeed he was surely among the excessive ones. And Musa said: O my people, if you have believed in Allah, then upon Him place your trust, if you are truly submitting.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

Sometimes we imagine that once truth has been publicly vindicated, faith should spread easily.

But the Qur'an is more realistic than our triumphal fantasies. Truth may be clear, falsehood may be exposed, and still the social cost of believing can remain terrifying. People do not live only inside arguments. They live inside regimes, families, punishments, vulnerabilities, and fear.

[PAUSE]

These ayahs honor that reality without surrendering to it. The believing group around Musa is small and afraid. Fir'awn's elevation in the land is real. His excess is real. So Musa does not shame the believers for their vulnerability. He teaches them the next necessary station: if you have believed in Allah, then rely upon Him.

[PAUSE]

And notice what the ayah does with their fear before Musa says a word. It does not talk them out of it. It confirms it - twice, and both times with the heaviest emphasis Arabic has: *and indeed Fir'awn was surely high in the land, and indeed he was surely among those who go past every limit.* These frightened people were not exaggerating. Allah puts on record that the man they were afraid of was exactly as large as he looked to them.

That is a strange kind of comfort, and it is the honest kind. The passage does not shrink the tyrant. It relocates the trust.

The hook of the passage is this: **Yunus teaches that early faith may begin under fear and fragility, but true iman must be educated into tawakkul, because submission to Allah is not complete while trust still remains chained to the tyrant's power.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Fa-ma amana li-Musa illa dhurriyyatun**: Faith Begins as a Small, Vulnerable Cluster

The ayah says:

**fa-ma amana li-Musa illa dhurriyyatun min qawmihi**.

None believed in Musa except a small offspring group from his people.

[PAUSE]

Look first at the small letter attached to Musa's name. They believed **li-Musa**, with a *lam*, not *bi-Musa*. In Arabic those are two different acts. To believe *bi-* something is to place your faith in it. To believe *li-* someone is to take his word as true - to credit him, to accept that what he is reporting is real. So the ayah is measuring something very specific: after the staffs, the ropes, the collapsed magic and the magicians on their faces, how many people were willing to say *this man is telling the truth*?

And the answer is delivered by subtraction. The sentence is built as a flat negative with a single exception carved out of it: **none believed... except**. Arabic uses that shape when it wants to close a door and leave one gap in it. The default position of the ayah is that nobody believed. The believers appear only as the exception clause.

[PAUSE]

The word **dhurriyyah** gives the scene a tenderness and vulnerability. It suggests a smaller, younger, less socially armored group. The Qur'an is teaching us not to despise small beginnings. Sometimes the earliest carriers of truth are not the established and protected, but the exposed and impressionable.

Classical commentators did not all hear the word the same way, and al-Tabari says so openly: at this exact word he stops and notes that the scholars of interpretation differed over what *dhurriyyah* means here. He records the view of some that it simply means *the few* - a remnant, a handful, a number small enough not to matter to anyone counting. Others take the ordinary sense: offspring, the young, the children of that generation. Either reading leaves the same picture. Whatever else this group was, it was not big and it was not powerful.

[PAUSE]

There is a second and larger disagreement hiding in three letters: *min qawmi-**hi***, from **his** people. Whose people? The natural reading is Musa's own, and al-Muyassar takes it that way and spells it out - a dhurriyyah from his people, from the Children of Israel. But Ibn Kathir carries another reading, reported by al-'Awfi from Ibn 'Abbas, in which the small believing group came from among Fir'awn's own followers. On that reading, the handful who believed were defectors from inside the machine.

The wording keeps both doors open, and one detail is part of why. The ayah says they feared *Fir'awn and* ***their*** *chiefs* - *mala'ihim*, a plural "their" where a simple "his" would have been tidier. Readers have long noticed the seam.

We do not have to settle it, and the passage does not ask us to. Either way the point stands and even sharpens: the people who believed were the ones with the least protection, whether they were the enslaved population or the ones breaking ranks with power. Believing cost them something that believing did not cost anyone else.

### 2. **'Ala khawfin**: Faith and Fear Can Coexist at the Beginning

Then:

**'ala khawfin min Fir'awna wa mala'ihim an yaftinahum**.

Upon fear of Fir'awn and their chiefs lest they put them through trial.

[PAUSE]

This is psychologically merciful. The ayah does not wait for fearlessness before recognizing faith. They believe **'ala khawf** - under fear, with fear pressing on them. Their fear is not denied. It is named.

That matters because some people assume that if fear still exists, faith must be defective in its entirety. But the Qur'an often deals more gently with beginnings. Fear may remain while the heart is still turning.

[PAUSE]

Two small things in the wording carry that gentleness. The preposition is *'ala* - on, upon, on top of. Not "with fear" walking beside them, but *on* fear, as a surface they were standing on the whole time they believed. And *khawfin* is left indefinite, unmeasured. The ayah does not tell us how much fear. It does not need to; it simply lets the quantity stay unspecified and large.

Then notice who is doing the reporting. This is Allah describing them. He could have described a group who believed and then adds, with disappointment, that they were also afraid. Instead the fear is folded into the sentence as part of the scene, and the verb He uses for what they did is still *amana*. They believed. It counted.

### 3. **An yaftinahum**: Power Threatens Not Only Bodies but Faith

The concern is:

**an yaftinahum**.

Lest he put them through fitnah.

[PAUSE]

This is deeper than generic harm. **Fitnah** is testing, burning, coercive trial, pressure that exposes what is inside. Tyrannical power does not only want outward compliance. It wants to destabilize loyalty itself.

[PAUSE]

The commentators do not leave this vague, and what they fill in is worth knowing. Ibn Kathir says the fear was that Fir'awn and his people would force them **back** into kufr - not that they would be hurt, but that they would be pushed into undoing what they had just done. Al-Muyassar is more concrete still: that he would put them to fitnah *by torture*, and so turn them away from their religion.

So this is not atmospheric intimidation. It is a machine with a stated output. Pain is the instrument; the target is the din. A regime that only wanted obedience could have stopped at obedience - people can be made to bow without being made to believe. Fir'awn wanted the inside of them too.

That is what makes the word *fitnah* the right one here. You do not put metal in fire to change its shape only. You put it in fire to find out what it is, and to make it become something else.

[PAUSE]

And note the grammar of who is acting on whom: *an yaftina-**hum***, that he might put **them** to fitnah. In this ayah the believers are the object of the verb. Hold onto that, because two ayahs later, when they finally open their mouths to pray, they will pick this same word up and point it the other way.

### 4. **La-'alin fi al-ard**: Fir'awn Is Not Merely Strong, But Exalted Against Right

Then the ayah diagnoses Fir'awn:

**wa inna Fir'awna la-'alin fi al-ard**.

Fir'awn was surely exalted in the land.

[PAUSE]

This is not praise. It is a description of dominating elevation, self-exalting rule, a height that becomes oppressive. The believers' fear is not irrational. They live under someone socially towering over them.

[PAUSE]

The word is *'alin*, from the root ع-ل-و, height. It is not a verb - it is a participle, a description of a standing condition. He is not doing something high at the moment; he *is* a high one, and that is simply the shape of the world the believers wake up in. Al-Muyassar glosses it with two words: *jabbar mustakbir* - a tyrant, swollen with his own greatness. Height that has stopped being a position and become a way of handling people.

And *fi al-ard* - in the land. His elevation had a territory. There was no province to slip away to, no corner of the country outside his reach. That geography is exactly why the fear in the previous clause is not neurosis. Fear is only irrational when the danger is imaginary.

[PAUSE]

Now look at how the sentence is armed. **Wa inna Fir'awna la-'alin**. There is *inna* at the front, and then an extra *la-* on top of the predicate. Arabic stacks those two when it wants to shut down any possibility of doubt - it is roughly the difference between "he was powerful" and "he most certainly was powerful, and let no one tell you otherwise."

And then the ayah turns around and does it again in the very next breath, with the same two tools, about the same man. Two fortified sentences back to back.

Think about who is being addressed by that. A frightened person often half-hopes to be told the threat is smaller than it feels. The Qur'an refuses. It insists on the size of the threat, under emphasis, twice - and only then does Musa speak. The relief that arrives in the next ayah is not going to come from shrinking Fir'awn.

### 5. **Wa innahu la-mina al-musrifin**: Tyranny Is Excess That Has Lost All Bounds

Then:

**wa innahu la-mina al-musrifin**.

He was surely among the excessive ones.

[PAUSE]

**Israf** is overstepping measure, going beyond bounds, spending and acting without restraint. Fir'awn's political evil is therefore tied to a deeper spiritual condition: excess without limit. Tyranny is not only domination. It is diseased transgression at scale.

[PAUSE]

Notice that he is not called *musrif*. He is called one **of** the musrifin - *min*, from among them. The ayah does not hand him a private label; it files him into a category that has other members. He is a specimen of a type. That is quietly deflating for a man whose whole project was to be without equal, and quietly useful for us, because a type can recur. There has never been only one of these.

Al-Muyassar also names the field the excess was happening in. Fir'awn was among those who went past the limit *in kufr and in corruption*. So *israf* here is not extravagance with money or appetite. It is a man who has stopped acknowledging that there is a line anywhere - in what he may claim about himself, in what he may do to people.

That is worth pausing on, because this same root shows up elsewhere in the Qur'an for very ordinary human immoderation: eating past the point, spending past the point. Here the identical word is used of a man running an empire. The Qur'an is naming the small domestic disease and the imperial one with a single term. What is at the bottom of both is the same refusal: the limit was there, and he went past it.

### 6. **Fa-'alayhi tawakkalu**: Belief Must Ripen Into Reliance

Then Musa says:

**in kuntum amantum bi-Allahi fa-'alayhi tawakkalu**.

If you have believed in Allah, then upon Him rely.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the Qur'an's great تربيه movements. Musa does not say: if you believe, you will feel no fear. He says: if you believe, direct your trust properly. The next step after frightened faith is trained tawakkul.

[PAUSE]

Look at the shape of what he says. There is one command in the middle - *rely upon Him* - and it is bracketed on both sides by the same conditional: *if you have believed... if you are submitting*. The instruction sits hemmed in between two tests. Musa is not stacking a new obligation on top of iman. He is telling them what the iman they already claim is supposed to be producing.

And the verb inside the condition is past tense: *in kuntum* **amantum**, if you *have* believed. He is not interrogating whether they believe. He takes it as settled - the ayah before already said they did - and then asks what a settled belief should now be doing with their fear.

[PAUSE]

The order of the words carries the weight. **Fa-'alayhi tawakkalu** - literally *so upon Him rely*, with the phrase "upon Him" pulled to the front of the command. In Arabic, moving a phrase forward like that is how you say "and on no one else." He is not asking them to add Allah to their list of hopes. He is asking them to empty the list.

Notice too which preposition he now uses. In ayah 83 they believed *li-Musa* - they credited his report, they took his word. Now Musa asks about believing *bi-Allah*, faith placed in Allah Himself. He is moving them off himself and onto the One he was sent to point at. Being believed was never the goal; a messenger's job is to not become the destination.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari fills in the reassurance underneath the command. Trust in Him, he writes, and hand yourselves over to His command - because He will never forsake the one who belongs to Him, and never abandon the one who has relied on Him.

That is a direct answer to the exact fear of the previous ayah. Musa's reply to people terrified of a real tyrant is not that the tyrant is weak. He has just been told, under double emphasis, that the tyrant is not weak. The reply is that the One they are handing themselves to does not drop people.

### 7. **In kuntum muslimin**: Submission Is Tested at the Level of Trust

He closes:

**in kuntum muslimin**.

If you are truly submitting.

[PAUSE]

This links Islam and tawakkul intimately. Submission is not only verbal assent or outward belonging. It is the practical surrender of one's safety calculus to Allah.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari's gloss makes the state very physical. *Muslimin*, he says, means those who are *mudh'inin* to Allah in obedience - yielding, giving in, no longer resisting. It is the word you would use for an animal that has stopped pulling against the reins, or a man who has stopped arguing. Submission in that sense is not a badge worn on the outside. It is the moment the resistance goes out of you.

Which makes Musa's sentence quietly severe. He has taken the most portable claim a person can make - *I believe, I submit* - and attached a test to it that cannot be faked from the tongue. Where is your trust actually parked? If it is still lodged in Fir'awn's mood this week, then whatever else has been said, something has not yet been handed over.

[PAUSE]

It is also worth remembering that he is saying this to the Children of Israel. The word *muslim* here is not the name of a later community; it is the plain description of anyone who gives himself up to Allah. Every prophet's people have been asked the same question in the same word.

And then the passage does something it rarely does. It lets us hear the answer. Musa's condition is put to them at the end of ayah 84 - and the very next ayah opens with their reply.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Qur'an Respects Fragile Beginnings Without Letting Them Stay Fragile Forever

This passage is so beautiful because it neither mocks fear nor canonizes it. The believing group is small and under threat. The Qur'an names that honestly. But Musa does not leave them there. He takes their real, trembling iman and teaches it the next discipline.

[PAUSE]

This is how spiritual growth often works. Allah does not always ask us to begin with perfected states. But He does ask that what is real at the beginning be educated, strengthened, and redirected.

[PAUSE]

It is worth noticing how little the ayah asks of them before calling it iman. They are few. They are young or unarmoured or both. They are standing on fear. They may not even be from the community you would expect. And the sentence still says: *they believed*. Nothing is subtracted from the verb because of the conditions around it.

But it is equally worth noticing that the passage does not stop there and call the fear beautiful. One ayah later Musa is already moving them. Honesty about a beginning is not the same as contentment with it.

### Theme 2: Tyrants Want to Own Not Only the Public Square but the Believer's Imagination of Safety

Fir'awn's real power is not only external punishment. It is the way his regime colonizes the imagination. People start believing that survival, provision, dignity, and future all remain locked inside his approval or anger.

[PAUSE]

Musa breaks that spell with one sentence: if you have believed in Allah, rely upon Him. Tawakkul becomes liberation from the tyrant's monopoly over what feels safe.

[PAUSE]

But the ayah will not let us romanticize this, and we should not either. The threat was not merely a story people had been told. The commentators say plainly what the fitnah was going to consist of: punishment applied until religion was let go. There was a real apparatus behind the spell.

That is why what Musa does is harder than positive thinking. He is not telling them the danger is in their heads. He is telling them that even a danger that is entirely real does not get to be the thing they lean on. The tyrant keeps his power over their bodies. What he loses is his monopoly over their sense of where safety comes from.

### Theme 3: Accurate Fear Is Not the Opposite of Faith

Put the two halves of ayah 83 next to each other and something quietly enormous appears. First: they believed, while afraid. Then, immediately: and indeed Fir'awn *was* high in the land, and indeed he *was* one of the boundary-breakers - both under the strongest emphasis Arabic can carry.

[PAUSE]

Allah does not correct their fear. He corroborates it. Their assessment of the danger was, as far as the ayah is concerned, accurate.

That should relieve a particular kind of pressure many believers carry. We often assume that feeling the weight of a situation is itself a failure of faith - that if I were really trusting Allah, the threat would look smaller to me. This passage does not work that way. Faith here is not a distortion of the danger. It is a decision made in full view of it.

The believer's task is not to misjudge the size of what is in front of him. It is to be accurate about the danger and still hand the matter over. That is harder, and it is the only version worth having, because a trust that depends on underestimating the problem breaks the moment the problem is seen clearly.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs show that only a small, vulnerable group believed in Musa, and they did so under real fear of Fir'awn and his elite lest they be driven into fitnah. Fir'awn's elevation and excess are named plainly, and then Musa moves his people from frightened belief toward rightly directed trust: if you have believed in Allah, then rely upon Him, if you are truly submitting.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: where is my own faith still real but frightened? What regime, person, institution, or imagined consequence still seems to own my safety more than Allah does? And if I say I believe and submit, how much of my practical trust has actually been transferred to Him?

[PAUSE]

A second question, smaller and more uncomfortable. When I am afraid, do I quietly assume the fear disqualifies me - that I will get around to relying on Allah once I have first managed to stop being scared? This ayah puts the two in the opposite order. The fear was still there when the belief was counted, and the instruction to rely came while the fear was still running.

And a third, from the two prepositions. In ayah 83 they believed *li-Musa*; in ayah 84 they are asked about believing *bi-Allah*. It is possible to be genuinely convinced by a teacher, a book, an argument, a community, and still not have moved the weight onto Allah Himself. Whose word am I crediting, and whom am I actually leaning on?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **Allah can begin a community of faith in small and fearful hearts, but He does not leave them there; He teaches them tawakkul so that submission becomes stronger than the fear of tyrants.**
