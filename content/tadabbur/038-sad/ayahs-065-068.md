# Only a Warner: The One Who Overwhelms and the One Who Forgives

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first, slowly, the way it would have fallen on the ears of people who heard it for the first time.

> **قُلْ إِنَّمَآ أَنَا۠ مُنذِرٌ**
> *Qul innamā anā mundhir*
> Say: I am only a warner.

> **وَمَا مِنْ إِلَـٰهٍ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ ٱلْوَٰحِدُ ٱلْقَهَّارُ**
> *wa-mā min ilāhin illā-llāhu-l-wāḥidu-l-qahhār*
> And there is no god whatsoever except Allah, the One, the Overpowering —

> [ayah:38:66] رَبُّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفَّـٰرُ
> *rabbu-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa-mā baynahumā-l-ʿazīzu-l-ghaffār*
> Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything between them, the Mighty, the Endlessly Forgiving.

> **قُلْ هُوَ نَبَؤٌا۟ عَظِيمٌ — أَنتُمْ عَنْهُ مُعْرِضُونَ**
> *Qul huwa naba'un ʿaẓīm — antum ʿanhu muʿriḍūn*
> Say: It is a tremendous tiding — from which you are turning away.

### Rebuild the world

To feel the weight of these words, you have to stand where they were spoken.

By the time we reach the sixty-fifth verse of Surah Sad, the surah has done something extraordinary. It has marched an entire gallery of prophets past us. Dawud, who was given a kingdom and a voice that made the mountains echo. Sulayman, who was given the wind and command over what no human had commanded. Ayyub, who was given suffering and then restoration. King after king. Gift after gift. Power after power.

And then — at the end of all that grandeur — the camera turns to one man standing in the streets of Makkah. No kingdom. No wind under his command. No mountains echoing his name. Just a man, surrounded by people who mock him, who have called him a sorcerer and a liar in the opening verses of this very surah, who have walked away from his gatherings saying *"this is nothing but a fabrication."*

And what is this man commanded to say about himself, right after the parade of kings?

Not *"I am like them."* Not *"I too have been given power."*

**"Say: I am only a warner."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that placement for a moment. After Dawud's kingdom and Sulayman's wind, the final prophet is told to define himself by a limitation. *Only.* Just this. Nothing more.

There is a strand running through this whole surah — a quiet argument with people who kept demanding that the truth arrive on their terms, wrapped in the kind of power they could see and bow to. And the answer the surah gives, here at its hinge, is to strip the messenger down to almost nothing and let the weight fall entirely on the One who sent him.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then let it pull us forward.

In these four short verses, the Prophet ﷺ is told to say something about *himself* that shrinks him — *only a warner* — and in the very same breath to say something about *Allah* that is almost too large to hold. Two divine names that, on the surface, seem to pull in opposite directions. A God described as **utterly overpowering** — and then, three words later, as **endlessly forgiving.**

How does the One who overwhelms everything also forgive everything? And why is *this* — this particular pairing — called *the tremendous news* that you are somehow managing to turn your face away from?

Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Innamā* — the word that builds a wall

Let me teach you a small piece of Arabic before we use it, because the entire opening of this passage turns on it.

In English, when we want to limit something, we add words around it. "I am *nothing more than* a warner." "I am *just* a warner." It takes a little phrase to fence the meaning in.

Arabic has a single tool that does this with surgical precision: **إِنَّمَا — *innamā*.** Grammarians call it a *particle of restriction*, *adāt al-ḥaṣr*. What it does is build a wall. Everything inside the wall is affirmed. Everything outside the wall is denied. When you say *innamā anā mundhir* — **"I am only a warner"** — you are not just describing yourself. You are drawing a boundary and saying: *inside this boundary is all of me; outside it, nothing.*

Think of how we use the word "just" when someone is reaching for more than they should. "I'm just the messenger." We say it to refuse a responsibility that isn't ours. That is exactly the work *innamā* is doing here.

Now look at the word it fences in.

**مُنذِرٌ — *mundhir.*** Its root is *n-dh-r* (the Leeds corpus confirms this), and the word sits in what Arabic calls **Form IV**, the active participle of *andhara* — "to warn, to give notice of danger." A *mundhir* is not someone delivering an opinion. He is someone who has seen the flood coming and runs through the village shouting. The word carries urgency and it carries *love* — you only warn the people you want to save.

Classical commentators were precise about what exactly was being warned of. Al-Jalalayn glosses this *mundhir* as *mukhawwif bi-l-nār* — "one who strikes fear of the Fire." Not a general alarm, but a pointed finger at a specific direction: *this way leads to the Fire.* Al-Tabari, reading the historical scene, hears the address directed specifically at the mushrikeen of Quraysh — and specifies that the Prophet ﷺ was warning them *bayna yaday ʿadhābin shadīd*, "before an imminent, severe punishment." The flood is not distant. It is already gathering at the edge of the horizon. And Tabari adds, with a pastoral urgency that moves through the centuries: *bādirū ḥulūlahu bi-l-tawbah* — "hasten to meet its arrival with repentance." The warning is not the last word. Embedded inside the warning is an invitation: *there is still time to turn.*

So the sentence reads: **"I am, and am only, the one who warns you of danger."**

[PAUSE]

Now — let's read the absence. This is where it opens.

Everywhere else in the Qur'an, when the Prophet ﷺ is described in his mission, he is almost always given *two* titles, paired together: **بَشِيرٌ وَنَذِيرٌ** — *bashīr wa nadhīr*, a bearer of glad tidings *and* a warner. The good news and the warning, side by side, like two hands.

Here — one hand. Only *mundhir.* The glad-tidings half is gone.

Why?

Stay with me, because the answer is in who he is talking to. He has been commanded to *say* this to people who have already mocked him, already turned away, already called the message a lie. To a heart that is racing toward a cliff, you do not begin with *"good news awaits you."* You begin with **"stop — there is an edge ahead."** The withholding of the glad tidings is not coldness. It is precision. It is the word a doctor uses when the patient is still pretending he is not sick.

And notice what the restriction does for the *messenger himself.* By fencing himself into *only a warner*, the Prophet ﷺ removes himself from the center. *I do not own your guidance. I cannot force your heart. I am not your judge or your savior. I am the voice at the edge of the cliff. What you do with the warning — and the One behind the warning — that is between you and Him.*

The smallness of the messenger is the doorway to the greatness of the One who sent him. He shrinks so that your eyes lift past him. And the instant your eyes lift past him — the sentence hands you God.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār* — the name that leaves no room

The moment the messenger steps back, the verse fills the space he left with the largest possible subject: **وَمَا مِنْ إِلَـٰهٍ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ** — *"and there is no god whatsoever except Allah."*

Listen to the Arabic structure, because it is more emphatic than any English can carry. *Wa-mā min ilāh* — the little word **مِنْ — *min*** here is what grammarians call *min al-istighrāqiyya*, the *min* of total exhaustion. It sweeps the entire category. Not "there is no god *for you*," not "no god *that we worship*" — but **"there does not exist, anywhere, of the entire genus of 'god,' a single one — except Allah."** It is a denial so complete it leaves no crack, no exception, no footnote.

Al-Tabari gives this phrase its full semantic weight: the verse is saying there is no object of worship that *deserves* worship, that it is *appropriate* to worship — except Allah alone. The denial is not merely numerical. It is qualitative. No other candidate even meets the criterion for candidacy.

And then Allah is named twice over: **ٱلْوَٰحِدُ ٱلْقَهَّارُ — *al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār.***

Let me teach you the first.

**ٱلْوَٰحِدُ — *al-Wāḥid*** (root *w-ḥ-d*, confirmed) — *the One.* But not "one" as a number you could count toward two. *Al-Wāḥid* means the One who has no second, no partner, no division within Himself, nothing comparable beside Him. Oneness not as quantity but as *uniqueness.*

Now the second name — and this is where it becomes overwhelming.

**ٱلْقَهَّارُ — *al-Qahhār.***

The root is *q-h-r* (Leeds confirms it). To *qahara* something is to dominate it so completely that it has no power of resistance left — to subdue, to overpower, to bring entirely under control. But look at the *shape* of the word. It is not the simple form *qāhir* (one who overpowers). It is **قَهَّار — *qahhār*** — the intensive pattern Arabic calls *faʿʿāl* (*ṣīghat al-mubālagha*). This pattern multiplies the meaning. It is the difference between "one who forgives" and "one who forgives *and forgives and forgives.*" The *faʿʿāl* form says: *this is not an action He performs; this is what He IS, totally, permanently, without exception.*

So *al-Qahhār* is not "the one who can overpower." It is **"the One whose overpowering is absolute, total, and unceasing — over everything, with no exception, forever."**

Al-Muyassar captures it exactly: *al-Qahhār — alladhī qahara kulla shay'in wa ghalabah* — "the One who has subdued every single thing and overcome it." Nothing stands outside His grip. The kings in this very surah — Dawud, Sulayman, with all their power — were themselves subdued. The mockers in the streets of Makkah, with all their pride — subdued. Death itself will be subdued.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel why these two names had to come *together*?

*Al-Wāḥid* tells you there is no one *beside* Him. *Al-Qahhār* tells you there is no one who could *resist* Him even if there were. The first removes every partner; the second removes every rival. Together they leave you standing in a universe with exactly one center of power, and it is not you, and it is not anything you could bargain with.

This is the answer to the whole quarrel of the surah. The people wanted a god they could negotiate with, share the throne with, fit alongside their idols. The verse says: *there is no room.* Not because the door is locked, but because the space is already, entirely, infinitely full.

### Element Three: *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* — the name that should not fit beside the last one

And now the verse does something that should stop your heart.

Having told you that Allah is *al-Qahhār* — the One who overpowers everything without exception — the very next verse adds: **ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفَّـٰرُ** — *al-ʿAzīz al-Ghaffār* — **"the Mighty, the Endlessly Forgiving."**

Notice the first of these before we arrive at the second. **ٱلْعَزِيزُ — *al-ʿAzīz*** (root *ʿ-z-z*) is often rendered simply as "the Mighty," but al-Jalalayn gives it a more precise gloss: *al-ghālib ʿalā amrih* — "the One who prevails in His command." This is not power as raw force. It is power as *unfailing efficacy*. His decree always lands. His will is never frustrated. Nothing, in the end, obstructs what He has ordained. *Al-Qahhār* subdues every rival; *al-ʿAzīz* ensures every command of His prevails completely. Together they are two angles on the same sovereign certainty.

Now the second name. **ٱلْغَفَّـٰرُ — *al-Ghaffār.***

The root *gh-f-r* means to cover, to shield, to veil over — which is why the same root gives us the *mighfar*, the helmet that covers and protects the head. To forgive, in Arabic, is to *cover* a thing so completely it is as if it was never seen.

And notice the shape again. It is not *ghāfir* (one who forgives). It is **غَفَّار — *ghaffār*** — the *exact same intensive pattern* as *qahhār.* The same mold. The same *faʿʿāl* form that means *totally, permanently, without exception, forever.*

[PAUSE — sit here]

Do you see what Allah has done?

He has taken the two names and poured them into the *identical grammatical shape.* *Qahhār* and *ghaffār.* Listen to them: they even rhyme — *al-qahhār… al-ghaffār* — the long *ā* and the rolling *r*, twins in sound. He is telling you, in the very *architecture* of the words, that these two are not in tension. They are equals. They are the same magnitude. Whatever totality, whatever endlessness, whatever absolute completeness belongs to His overpowering — *the very same* belongs to His forgiving.

He does not overpower more than He forgives. He does not forgive less than He overpowers.

Al-Muyassar brings this to its pastoral point: *al-Ghaffār* forgives "the sins of those who repent and return to His pleasure" — *man tāba wa anāba ilā marḍātih*. That small addition changes something. The forgiveness is not indifferent to direction. It is total *for* those who turn toward it. Which means the question the verse is quietly pressing is never: *Is there enough forgiveness?* The *faʿʿāl* form answers that permanently: there is more than enough, forever, for every soul. The question is only whether you will *turn toward it.*

And that is what makes verse 68 — "from which you are turning away" — so precise and so devastating.

This is what we almost never believe in our bones. We carry around a quiet arithmetic that says power and mercy trade off — that the more dominant a being is, the less tender; that strength is purchased with coldness. We've met human power and it taught us this. The strong man forgives rarely; that's how he stays strong.

The grammar of this verse demolishes the arithmetic. *Qahhār* and *ghaffār*, cast in one mold, set side by side, rhyming. **Maximal power and maximal mercy, in the same Being, at the same intensity, with no discount applied to either.**

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So look at what the linguistic construction has built for us. *Innamā* shrank the messenger to *only a warner* — and the moment he stepped back, the verse filled the space with the One beside whom there is no other (*al-Wāḥid*), against whom there is no resistance (*al-Qahhār*), whose command never fails (*al-ʿAzīz*), and then — in the same breath, in the same grammatical shape — the One whose forgiveness is just as total, just as inexhaustible, endlessly available to every soul that turns (*al-Ghaffār*).

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange thing we human beings keep doing in front of it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The God you don't have to choose between

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a wound almost everyone carries, and it usually came from someone who was supposed to be both strong and safe. A father who was powerful but you could never be sure of his mercy. A boss whose authority was real and whose forgiveness was not. We learn, very early, that power and tenderness rarely live in the same house. And so we build our picture of God out of the only materials we have — our experience of human power — and we end up worshipping, without realizing it, a God we are forced to *choose between.*

Some of us pick the *Qahhār* alone — and end up with a God we walk on eggshells around. A God of consequence so absolute there seems to be no warmth, no room to fall and get up, no covering at the edge of failure. We live in the religion of barely being enough. Every mistake feels final. The grip is so total in our minds that we forget there is also a door.

Some of us pick the *Ghaffār* alone — and end up with a God so forgivingly indifferent that our choices quietly stop mattering. We borrow against a forgiveness we've never actually moved toward. There is no edge anywhere in our theology, so we stop watching our step.

Both of these are broken pictures. And both — this is the crucial thing — emerge from reading *half a verse.*

The verse refuses the division. The grammar refuses it. *Qahhār* and *ghaffār*, poured into the same *faʿʿāl* mold, set in sequence, rhyming — they are not two different modes the Divine cycles between, softening and hardening by turns. They are one Being described from two angles simultaneously. The same One who overwhelms everything without exception is the same One whose forgiveness nothing exhausts. He does not become less sovereign when He forgives. He does not become less forgiving when He subdues. Both are absolute. Both are total. Both are always true at the same time.

[PAUSE]

Here is what that means practically.

You cannot hide from Him — *al-Qahhār* makes that true. But you can *turn toward* Him — *al-Ghaffār* makes that enough.

The overpowering is not a threat designed to make you despair. It is the foundation that makes the forgiveness *real.* Because only the One whose grip nothing escapes can offer a forgiveness that actually settles the whole account — not just looks away, not just pretends, but actually *covers*, with the authority of the One who owns everything being covered. The helmet protects because it is made of something stronger than what it shields against.

And the forgiveness is not a loophole that undermines the overpowering. It is a *door* — real, open, inexhaustible — inside the same universe where nothing escapes His knowledge or His hold.

This is the God you don't have to choose between.

### Theme Two: *Naba' ʿAẓīm* — the news that is too big to look at

[ayah:38:67] قُلْ هُوَ نَبَؤٌا۟ عَظِيمٌ
*Qul huwa naba'un ʿaẓīm*
Say: It is a tremendous tiding.

The word here is **نَبَأ — *naba',*** and it is worth pausing over its choice. Arabic has another word for news or message: *khabar.* Classical scholars noted that *khabar* is information of any kind, large or small. *Naba'* carries weight — it refers to a report of such consequence that it cannot be received and set aside. Elsewhere in the Qur'an, Surah al-Naba' opens with *ʿamma yatasā'alūn ʿan al-naba' al-ʿaẓīm* — "What are they asking about? The tremendous *naba'.*" The same term. The same modifier. The word belongs in the register of announcements that divide time into before and after.

And what is this *naba' ʿaẓīm* exactly? Ibn Kathir frames this passage under the heading: *"The Message of the Messenger ﷺ is a Great News."* The message itself — the tawhid, the resurrection, the whole of the Quran — *this is it.* Not a report *about* something that will arrive. The message *is* the event. The tremendous news is not elsewhere; it is these four verses and everything behind them.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the second half of the sentence.

[ayah:38:68] أَنتُمْ عَنْهُ مُعْرِضُونَ
*Antum ʿanhu muʿriḍūn*
From which you are turning away.

The verb is from the root *ʿ-r-ḍ* — and the image embedded in the root is important. *Iʿrāḍ* is not simply ignoring something. It is turning the *face.* It is a deliberate bodily movement — the way you swivel your head away from something you do not want to see. Not blindness. Not ignorance. *Active aversion.*

And notice the grammatical form: **مُعْرِضُونَ — *muʿriḍūn*** — this is a nominal predicate, a description of state, not a record of a single past act. It is saying: *this turning away is what you are. It is an ongoing condition, a posture you have settled into, a way of standing in the world.*

The tremendous news is here. The One who both overwhelms and forgives — completely, permanently, in equal measure — has sent someone to say so. And the response is to face the other direction.

There is no anger in the verse at this point. Only observation. *Say: It is a tremendous tiding. You are turned away from it.* The sentence is almost quiet, almost tired, the way a physician is quiet when a patient refuses the only treatment that works.

[PAUSE]

What makes the *iʿrāḍ* so staggering, in the light of everything that came before, is the arithmetic of the loss.

The news being turned away from is not merely information. It is the news that the One who holds everything in an unbreakable grip is also the One whose forgiveness is bottomless for everyone who turns back. Al-Muyassar says of *al-Ghaffār*: He forgives "the sins of those who repent and *return.*" Return is the operative word. The forgiveness is not a passive current that flows regardless of direction. It meets the one who moves toward it. The covering exists. It is total. It is permanent. But you have to face it.

So the terrible irony of *muʿriḍūn* is this: they are doing the one thing that, of all things, leaves them standing outside the one thing that would cover what they most fear being exposed for.

Turning away. From the news. That the covering is absolute.

### Theme Three: The weight of the *qul*

Step back and notice that the Prophet ﷺ is commanded to *say* both of these things. *Qul* — "Say" — opens verse 65 and verse 67. The command to speak is itself a burden.

In verse 65, he is told to say something that makes him small: *I am only a warner.* In verse 67, he is told to say something that calls out people he is trying to reach: *It is a tremendous news, and you are turned away from it.* Neither of these is an easy thing to say. The first requires willingness to be exactly what you are and nothing more, without borrowing authority or consolation from elsewhere. The second requires naming the problem clearly, to the people who have the problem, when naming it could anger them further.

This is the task the *mundhir* is given. Not to resolve the tension. Not to soften the news until it stops being news. To say it. Clearly, honestly, with the smallness of *innamā anā mundhir* and the courage of *huwa naba'un ʿaẓīm.*

The messenger's smallness is not weakness. It is clarity. He is not the message. He is the voice that carries it. And the One he carries it from — the One behind the restriction, behind the *innamā* — is the Wāḥid, the Qahhār, the ʿAzīz, the Ghaffār. The voice steps back. And what is left is enormous.

## CLOSING

Four verses. Twenty-one words.

A gallery of prophets just passed — kings, gifts, wind-commands, suffering, restoration. And here, at the hinge of all of it, the last prophet is told to compress himself into a single function: *warner.* Only. And then the One who sent him is named in four ways that together cover the entire space of what is true.

One. Overpowering. Prevailing-in-command. Endlessly forgiving.

The tremendous news is not complicated. There is one God. He holds everything. His will never fails. And for the one who turns back — there is no sin deep enough, no distance great enough, no accumulation large enough to exhaust what He can cover.

That's it. That is the news.

[PAUSE]

And the last thing this passage says, before the great narrative of Adam and Iblis begins, is not a threat. It is a diagnosis. The tremendous news is available. The messenger has been sent. The words have been spoken, clearly, without ornament, without even the *bashīr* half to soften the entry point.

*You are turned away from it.*

Not: *you will always be.* Not: *there is no return.* Just: *this is the posture you are currently in.*

Every posture can change. The *ghaffār* — the covering, total and inexhaustible — is still there. Al-Tabari heard it this way when he wrote his commentary: the answer to the warning is *tawbah*, and the call to hasten toward it is still in the text, still open, still addressed to anyone who has ears and chooses, finally, to turn.
