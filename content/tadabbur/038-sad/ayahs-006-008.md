# Be Patient Over Your Gods

## When the Machinery of Virtue Serves Falsehood

---

> [ayah:38:6] وَٱنطَلَقَ ٱلْمَلَأُ مِنْهُمْ أَنِ ٱمْشُوا۟ وَٱصْبِرُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ ءَالِهَتِكُمْ ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَشَىْءٌ يُرَادُ
>
> *wa-anṭalaqa l-malaʾu minhum ani mshū wa-ṣbirū ʿalā ālihatikum, inna hādhā la-shayʾun yurād*
>
> "And the chiefs among them went off, [saying]: *Walk on, and be steadfast over your gods. Indeed, this is a thing intended.*"
>
> — Sūrah Ṣād 38:6–8

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sound of a Door Closing

Let me set the scene, because everything depends on it.

A delegation of the most powerful men in Mecca has come to a meeting. Some of the narrations place it in the home of Abū Ṭālib, the Prophet's ﷺ uncle, who is sick and dying. The chiefs of Quraysh — the lords of the trade routes, the keepers of the Kaʿba, the men whose word moved the city — have gathered. And they have just heard, again, the thing they cannot bear to hear. *Qūlū lā ilāha illā Allāh.* Say: there is no god but God.

One sentence. That is the whole ask. Not a tax. Not a surrender of territory. Not a tribute. One sentence that would make them, for the first time in their lives, equal before something larger than themselves.

And they get up to leave.

Now, the Quran could have simply told us *that* they refused. It does something far rarer. It lets us hear what they said **to each other** as they walked out the door. Not their public speeches. Their private exhortation. The thing powerful men say to one another in the corridor when the meeting has rattled them more than they will admit.

And here is the strangeness I want you to feel before we go any further. Listen to the very first thing they tell each other:

*"Walk on. And **be patient** over your gods."*

**Be patient.** *Iṣbirū.* From *ṣabr* — the word that, in the mouth of this same Quran, is the crown of the believer. The word Allah pairs with prayer as the two great supports of the soul. The word that buys Paradise. *And here it is, being used by the enemies of the message, to encourage each other to hold the line against the truth.*

[PAUSE]

Why would the disbelievers need patience at all? You don't need to steel yourself against a lie. You don't need to grit your teeth and endure in the face of something obviously false. You only brace yourself like that against something that is **pulling at you**. Something working on you from the inside.

That is the thread we are going to follow. Because by the end of these three verses, Allah is going to reach past everything these men said — past their theories, past their objections, past their bravado — and name what was actually happening in their chests. And what He names will not be what they thought it was.

Stay with me. The chiefs thought they were walking away from a man. They were walking away from something inside themselves.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Wa-anṭalaqa*: The Word for Being Set in Motion

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the shape of the verb is carrying meaning that the translation throws away.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — what scholars call *forms*, numbered I through X. Each form bends the basic meaning of a root in a particular direction. Form II often intensifies. Form IV often makes something happen to another. And **Form VII** — the pattern *infaʿala* — has a special flavor. Grammarians call it *muṭāwaʿa*: the form of **a thing yielding to an action done upon it.** You push a door (Form I), and the door *yields and swings* (Form VII, *infataḥa*). You break a stick, and the stick *snaps* — the snapping is Form VII. It is the grammar of something **being set into motion** by a force.

Now look at how the verse opens:

> **وَٱنطَلَقَ ٱلْمَلَأُ** — *wa-anṭalaqa l-malaʾu*

*Anṭalaqa.* From the root *ṭ-l-q* — and this is **Form VII**, confirmed. We could translate it flatly as "they went off." But the form tells us more. This is not a calm, deliberate departure. Al-Qurṭubī, opening this very word, says plainly: *al-inṭilāq al-dhahāb bi-surʿa* — "*inṭilāq* is going **with speed**." It is a launching. A storming out.

And feel the family this root belongs to. From *ṭ-l-q* comes *ṭalāq* — divorce, release, the cutting of a bond. From it comes *ṭalīq* — one set free, let loose. From it comes the *inṭilāq* of an arrow leaving the bow. The whole root vibrates with the sense of a tie being released and a thing **propelled** away.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The grammar that means "a thing yielding to a force" — Form VII — is now describing the chiefs. On the surface they are the powerful ones; they decide; they lead. But the very verb chosen for their exit quietly says: *they were set in motion.* Something propelled them out of that room. They did not so much *choose* to leave as get **launched** — by their own agitation, by the pressure of a sentence they could not answer and could not accept.

The most powerful men in Mecca, and the word for them is the word for a snapped cord, a released arrow, a door blown open by a wind it did not summon.

Hold that. We will come back to what the wind was.

---

### Element Two — *Imshū wa-ṣbirū*: Two Commands That Mirror Two Paths

As they are launched out the door, they turn to one another and give two orders. Listen to them, because they are imperatives — direct commands — and they come as a pair:

> **أَنِ ٱمْشُوا۟ وَٱصْبِرُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ ءَالِهَتِكُمْ**
>
> *ani mshū wa-ṣbirū ʿalā ālihatikum*
>
> "Walk on — and be steadfast over your gods."

Two imperative verbs. *Imshū* — from *m-sh-y*, "to walk" — go, keep moving, proceed. And *iṣbirū* — from *ṣ-b-r* — be patient, be steadfast, endure.

Let me take them one at a time, because each one is doing quiet, devastating work.

**First, *imshū* — "walk."**

Why "walk"? They were already leaving. Why issue a command to do the thing they are already doing? Because *imshū* here is not about their feet. It is the language of **a path**. "Keep going on your way. Stay your course. Don't deviate." Al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both gloss it exactly this way: *amḍū ʿalā mā kuntum ʿalayhi* — "proceed upon what you were already upon, and do not enter into his religion."

This is the Quran's recurring image, and it is no accident that it surfaces here. The whole of guidance is a *path* — *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, the straight road we ask for seventeen times a day. To be guided is to *walk* a certain way. And here are men commanding each other: **keep walking the old way.** There are two roads in this verse, though only one is named — and the command *imshū* is the chiefs choosing their road and bracing each other to stay on it.

**Now the second command. And this is where the floor opens.**

*Iṣbirū ʿalā ālihatikum.* **Be patient over your gods. Be steadfast upon them. Cling. Endure for their sake.**

[PAUSE]

I need you to sit inside the strangeness of this. *Ṣabr.* Feel even the sound of it — the heavy emphatic *ṣ*, the clenched *b*, the *r* that holds. It is a word that *sounds* like a jaw set against pressure, like a body braced. And it is, in the vocabulary of this very Book, the supreme virtue. Allah says *innamā yuwaffā l-ṣābirūna ajrahum bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — the patient are given their reward without measure. He says *wa-staʿīnū bi-l-ṣabri wa-l-ṣalāh* — seek help through patience and prayer. *Ṣabr* is how the believer holds the line when everything pulls him off it.

And here, that exact word — that exact crown — is placed in the mouths of the men rejecting revelation, to brace each other to hold the line **for idols.**

Here is what we have to learn from this, and it is uncomfortable: **ṣabr, by itself, is morally empty.** It is a mechanism. It is the capacity to set your jaw and not move. And a mechanism takes its entire worth from **what it is aimed at.** Steadfastness upon the truth is the noblest thing a soul can do. Steadfastness upon falsehood is the most stubborn thing a soul can do. The endurance feels *identical from the inside.* The clenched jaw is the same jaw. The refusal to be moved feels the same whether you are standing on rock or standing on sand.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what this means? You cannot tell, from the *feeling* of your conviction, whether you are right. The chiefs of Quraysh felt every bit as principled, every bit as steadfast, every bit as *strong* as a martyr. They were exhorting each other to virtue. They were calling each other to *ṣabr.* And they were utterly, catastrophically wrong.

The strength of your grip tells you nothing about whether you are holding gold or holding a coal.

We will build a whole theme on this. For now, mark the word and let it trouble you.

### Element Three — *La-shayʾun yurād*: The Passive That Hides an Agent

Now they give their reason. And the grammar of their reason is the most haunting thing in the passage.

> **إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَشَىْءٌ يُرَادُ**
>
> *inna hādhā la-shayʾun yurād*
>
> "Indeed, this is a **thing** that is **intended**."

Two words to open carefully: *shayʾ* and *yurād.*

First, *shayʾ* — "a thing." From the root *sh-y-ʾ.* It is the vaguest noun in the language — the word you reach for when you will not, or cannot, name what you mean. And notice what they reduce the message to. Not "this call." Not "this truth" or "this lie." A *thing.* They take *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — the axis the heavens and earth turn on — and they shrink it to *a thing.* Something murky. Something with an angle.

Then the verb: *yurād.* From the root *r-w-d.* This is **Form IV in the passive, imperfect tense** — confirmed. The active form, *arāda*, means "to will, to intend, to want"; from it comes *irāda*, the will. And here it is passive: *yurād* — "**it is willed**," "**it is intended**," "something is being aimed at."

Now — the passive in Arabic, just like in English, does one specific thing: **it deletes the doer.** "The window was broken" — by whom? The sentence won't say. Passive voice hides the hand.

So when the chiefs say *inna hādhā la-shayʾun yurād* — "this is a thing being *willed*" — **whose will is it?** The grammar refuses to say. And the commentators record that the chiefs themselves filled that blank in more than one way. Al-Baghawī preserves the spread: some meant *yurādu binā* — "this is something aimed **at us**," a scheme to dominate us. Some meant *yurādu bi-Muḥammad an yamlik* — "what Muḥammad is really after is **to become king.**" In every reading, their meaning is the same suspicious instinct: *somebody wants something here. This monotheism is a play for power. There is a human will hidden behind it, working on us.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the trembling irony, and it lives entirely in that passive verb.

They were **right** that something was being willed. They were catastrophically **wrong** about whose will it was.

They scanned the situation and detected a hidden agent — and assumed it was Muḥammad, grasping for a throne. They could not conceive of a will operating above the human level. So they read the deleted doer as *a man like themselves, wanting what they would want* — power, rank, dominion.

But the passive is wide open. *Shayʾun yurād* — "a thing is being willed." Yes. **By the One whose will set the stars in their courses.** Something *was* being willed in Mecca in those years — the overturning of every idol, the leveling of every false hierarchy, the rescue of a people from the worship of stone. A will *was* moving. It simply was not the will they suspected. They felt the pressure of an intention bearing down on their world — *and they were not wrong to feel it* — but they mistook the wind of Heaven for the scheming of a man.

The grammar that they used to accuse a human of plotting is the very grammar that, read with open eyes, confesses the truth they were fleeing: **a thing is being willed, and the One willing it is not in that room as their equal.**

---

### Bridge: From Their Words to Allah's Verdict

So we have heard them. Watch the shape of their speech across these three verses, because it is all of one piece. They storm out (*anṭalaqa*). They brace each other (*imshū wa-ṣbirū*). They diagnose a plot (*shayʾun yurād*). Then they pile on the objections: *"We have not heard of this in the latest creed"* — the appeal to precedent, *we've never heard such a thing.* *"This is nothing but a fabrication"* — *ikhtilāq.* And finally, the objection that gives the whole game away: *"Has the Reminder been sent down upon **him** — out of all of us?"*

Every line of it sounds like confidence. *Inna hādhā* — "indeed this is..." *In hādhā illā* — "this is nothing but..." The grammar of certainty. The cadence of men who know exactly what they think.

And then Allah speaks. And in two short strokes He reaches past every word they said and names what was actually underneath.

This is no longer about their vocabulary. *Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself.*

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Counterfeit of Steadfastness

Let me start where you live, not where the verse is.

You have watched someone you love defend a position that is hurting them. Maybe it was a person they should have left. Maybe it was a grudge, a story about their family, a financial hole they kept digging. And the striking thing — the *maddening* thing — was never that they were weak. It was that they were **strong.** They held their ground with real courage. They endured real cost for it. They would say, with a steady voice, *"I'm not going to let anyone change my mind."* And you wanted to shake them, because all that magnificent firmness was poured into the wrong vessel.

Psychologists have a clumsy name for one corner of this. They call it the *sunk cost* trap, or *identity-protective cognition* — the well-documented finding that the more a belief becomes part of who we are, the more we defend it not by examining it but by **bracing against anything that threatens it.** The threat to the belief gets experienced as a threat to the self. So we do not weigh the new evidence. We *endure* it. We set our jaw and wait for it to pass.

That bracing has a name in this verse. It is *ṣabr.*

*Iṣbirū ʿalā ālihatikum.* Be steadfast over your gods.

[PAUSE]

The chiefs of Quraysh were not lazy men or stupid men. They were strong men exercising their strength in exactly the wrong direction. And the Quran does not say they *failed* to be steadfast. It says they *succeeded* — at the wrong thing. Their *ṣabr* was real. It was just aimed at idols.

Now follow this one step further, because here the verse turns a key. **Why does falsehood need patience at all?**

Think about it. If their gods were obviously, comfortably true, they would not need to *exhort* each other to endure for them. You don't form a huddle in the corridor and steel one another to keep believing the sun will rise. You don't brace yourself against the obvious. **You only need to summon steadfastness against something that is pulling at you.** *Ṣabr* is the response to *pressure.* And what was the pressure? It was the truth, working on them. It was that one sentence they could not refute and would not accept, tugging at the edges of everything they had built.

So the very command "be patient over your gods" is a confession. It is the sound of men who have felt the ground move and are gripping the rail. **You do not need patience to hold what is solid. You only need patience to hold what is slipping.**

[PAUSE]

And this turns the mirror on us, hard.

We tend to treat our own *conviction* as evidence. "I feel so strongly about this — it must be right." "I've never doubted it — that's how I know." But the chiefs felt strongly. The chiefs never wavered in public. The strength of the grip told them nothing. It told them only that they were *holding on* — not *what* they were holding.

So the question this verse leaves in your hands is not "Are you steadfast?" Almost everyone is steadfast about *something.* The question is: **What are you being patient over?** What is the thing you have decided, in advance, that no argument will be allowed to move? What is your *ālihatakum* — your "gods," the position you defend by enduring challenges to it rather than examining them?

Because here is the test the verse hands us, and it is a sharp one. The next time you feel that familiar bracing — the jaw setting, the "no one is going to change my mind on this" — treat the *bracing itself* as information. Not proof you're wrong. But a flag worth raising. *Why does this need defending by endurance? What is pulling at it that I am refusing to look at?* The truth does not usually require you to clench against it. It requires you to open.

**The practical change for tomorrow:** when you notice yourself going rigid in a disagreement — when the goal silently shifts from *finding what is true* to *not losing* — pause and name it. Ask the chiefs' question of yourself: *Am I being patient over a god?* That single moment of suspicion toward your own firmness is the crack through which guidance enters. The chiefs of Quraysh never let that crack open. That is the whole tragedy of verse six.

### Theme Two — "Why Him, Out of All of Us?": The Ego's Veto Over Truth

Now to the objection that gives everything away. Listen again:

> **أَءُنزِلَ عَلَيْهِ ٱلذِّكْرُ مِنۢ بَيْنِنَا**
>
> *a-unzila ʿalayhi l-dhikru min bayninā*
>
> "Has the Reminder been sent down upon **him** — from **among us**?"

Begin, again, where you live.

You have heard a true thing and rejected it because of *who said it.* Be honest. A piece of advice that was completely correct — but it came from your younger sibling, or the colleague you don't respect, or the relative who has no right to lecture *you* — and so you waved it away. Not because it was wrong. Because of the *ranking.* "Who are *they* to tell *me*?" We even dress this up as wisdom. We call it "considering the source." And sometimes that is wisdom. But very often it is the ego, quietly vetoing a truth it cannot bear to receive from a hand it considers beneath it.

That is precisely the objection here. Look closely: the chiefs do not say "the message is false." They have, in fact, just *run out* of arguments about the message — "we never heard such a thing," "it's a fabrication." Now they fall back on the last line of defense, and it has nothing to do with content. *"Has the Reminder been sent down on **him** — out of all of **us**?"* Their grievance is **rank.** Among all the great men of Mecca — the wealthy, the noble, the lords of the tribes — *why this one?* Earlier in the surah they had marveled at the same thing: *a-jāʾahum mundhirun minhum* — that a warner had come *from among themselves.* The intolerable thing was not the warning. It was that the warner was a *peer.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what has happened. The question has slid, without their noticing, from *"Is it true?"* to *"Is it fair that it came to him and not to me?"* And those are completely different questions. The first is about reality. The second is about the self. The ego cannot evaluate truth; it can only evaluate *status.* So when the ego is in charge of the inquiry, every question about *what is real* secretly converts into a question about *where do I rank.*

And now — watch Allah's response. Because He does not engage their objection on its terms at all. He does something far more surgical. He reaches past the words and names the disease:

> **بَلْ هُمْ فِى شَكٍّ مِّن ذِكْرِى ۖ بَلْ لَّمَّا يَذُوقُوا۟ عَذَابِ**
>
> *bal hum fī shakkin min dhikrī — bal lammā yadhūqū ʿadhāb*
>
> "**Rather** — they are in **doubt** about My Reminder. **Rather** — they have not yet **tasted** My punishment."

That word **bal** — "rather," "nay" — is a particle of *retraction* in Arabic. It cancels what came before and corrects it. *"Forget what you just heard them claim. Here is what is actually going on."* And He uses it *twice*, each one cutting deeper than the last, peeling the layers back to the root.

The first *bal* demolishes their entire performance: *bal hum fī shakkin min dhikrī* — "**rather, they are in doubt.**" [PAUSE] Sit with the violence of that. For three verses these men have spoken nothing but certainty. *Indeed this is a plot. This is nothing but fabrication. We have never heard such a thing.* And Allah, in five words, strips the costume off all of it: **it was doubt the whole time.** The bluster was not the opposite of doubt. The bluster was doubt *wearing armor.* Remember the *ṣabr* from verse six — the bracing, the gripping the rail? Now you know what they were bracing against. They were in *shakk*, in doubt, and the loud certainty was the noise a doubting heart makes to drown out its own questions.

This is the architecture of the whole passage, and now we can see it whole. **The louder the certainty, the deeper the doubt it was covering.** The exhortation to *be patient* was evidence of the pull they felt. The reduction of the message to a "plot" was evidence they could not refute it on the merits. The grievance "why him" was evidence they had nothing left but wounded rank. Every confident line was a tell. And Allah, reading the heart, names what the words were built to hide.

Then the second *bal*, deeper still: *bal lammā yadhūqū ʿadhāb* — "**rather, they have not yet tasted the punishment.**" Here the diagnosis bottoms out. *Why* are they in doubt? Not because the evidence is thin — the surah opened by swearing *by the Quran, full of Reminder.* They are in doubt because doubt is *comfortable* right now. Nothing has *cost* them anything yet. And notice the verb Allah chooses: *yadhūqū* — "**taste**," from *dh-w-q.* Not "see," not "hear," not "be told." **Taste.**

[PAUSE]

Why taste? Because taste is the most intimate, the most undeniable, the most *first-person* of all the senses. You can argue about something you only saw from a distance. You can dispute a report you only heard. But you cannot argue with a taste in your own mouth. Taste is knowledge that has entered you. It is the end of all debate. The whole problem with these men is that they are treating the gravest reality in existence as a topic for the corridor — something to be clever about, to wave away with "we never heard of this." Allah says: the day is coming when it stops being a *topic* and becomes a *taste.* And on that day there will be no doubt, because doubt cannot survive contact with the thing itself. The trouble is that the certainty will have come too late to be of any use.

Here is the unbearable mercy hidden in even this warning. *Lammā* — "**not yet.**" *They have not yet tasted it.* The door, at the moment these words were revealed, was still open. The "not yet" is the space in which repentance still lives. Their doubt was a choice they were still making, day by day, because nothing had forced their hand. They had time. The *ṣabr* they were spending on idols could have been spent walking the other road. The grip could have been opened.

[PAUSE]

**The practical change for tomorrow:** Take Allah's diagnostic method and turn it on yourself, gently. When you find yourself rejecting something — a correction, a religious obligation, a hard truth about your own conduct — run His two questions in order. First: *Am I actually certain it's false, or am I in shakk and shouting to cover it?* (Listen for the bluster; bluster is the sound of doubt.) And second, deeper: *Am I rejecting this because it isn't true — or because it hasn't cost me anything yet?* Because so much of what we "doubt" is simply what we have not yet had to *taste.* The believer's task is to let the truth land *before* it becomes a taste — to act on what is real while it is still merely *known.* That is the whole difference between the people of the corridor and the people of the road.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at what these three verses actually are, because the structure is the proof of everything we've said.

They are a **bluff, and its calling.**

Verses six through the first half of eight are entirely the speech of men *performing certainty.* Every device is a device of confidence: the storming exit, the bracing commands, the knowing diagnosis of a plot, the appeal to precedent, the flat verdict "nothing but a fabrication," the dismissive "why *him*?" Read on their own, these are the words of men who have it all figured out.

Then comes the *bal.* Twice. And the second half of verse eight reveals that the entire confident edifice was hollow. *They are in doubt.* They simply *had not yet tasted* the thing that would end the doubt. The whole performance of certainty was scaffolding around an empty center.

And the surah itself confirms this from its opening. Sūrah Ṣād begins by swearing *by the Quran, possessing **dhikr** (Reminder)* — and then immediately names the disease: *bali lladhīna kafarū fī **ʿizzatin** wa-shiqāq* — "rather, those who disbelieve are in **pride** and schism." Pride. *ʿIzza* — the false sense of being above. Hold that against verse eight's *min bayninā* — "out of all of *us*?" The pride named in verse two **is** the grievance voiced in verse eight. And the *dhikr* sworn by in verse one is the very *dhikr* they doubt in verse eight: *bal hum fī shakkin min **dhikrī.*** The surah opens by exalting the Reminder and naming pride; our passage shows pride **rejecting** that very Reminder. The frame and the picture are the same insight, drawn twice.

### Two Roads to One Truth

So our linguistic findings and our themes turn out to be a single discovery seen from two sides.

The word *ṣabr* — the crown of virtue placed in the mouths of idolaters — taught us that **steadfastness is a mechanism, worth only what it is aimed at, and that the need to brace yourself is a confession of the pull you feel.** And Allah's verdict — *bal hum fī shakk* — confirmed it from above: their steadfastness *was* a bracing against doubt. The grammar and the verdict meet.

The passive *yurād* — "a thing is being willed" — taught us that **they sensed a will bearing down on their world and mistook the Will of Heaven for the scheming of a man.** And the warning *lammā yadhūqū* — "they have not yet tasted" — confirmed it: there *was* a reality bearing down, real enough to be tasted one day, and they had reduced it to a corridor rumor. The will they refused to recognize was the only Will that was ever truly in the room.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the position I defend by *enduring* challenges to it, rather than by examining it? What is my *"be patient over your gods"*?
2. When I feel my conviction hardening in an argument, can I tell the difference between standing on truth and simply refusing to lose?
3. Is there a true thing I have waved away because of *who* said it — a *"why him, out of all of us?"* I've never admitted to myself?
4. Where in my life am I "in doubt" not because the evidence is thin, but because acting on the truth has not yet *cost* me anything?
5. What do I already *know* that I am waiting to *taste* before I act on it — and what would it mean to act now, while it is still merely known?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The chiefs walked out commanding each other to "be patient over your gods," and in that single borrowed word — *ṣabr* — they confessed what Allah would then name aloud: that the loud certainty of falsehood is only ever the armor worn by a heart that is secretly in doubt, still waiting, by mercy, to taste what it refuses to know.

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not, finally, about the chiefs of Quraysh. It is a mirror held up to the most respectable mechanism in your own soul: your firmness. Your principles. Your refusal to be moved. The Quran does not ask you to become a person who is easily swayed. It asks you to become a person who knows the difference between a grip on rock and a grip on sand — and who is brave enough to suspect his own grip when he feels it clench. Let the bracing become a question instead of a wall. That is the open door the chiefs walked past. It is still open for you. *Lammā* — *not yet.*

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who named the doubt beneath their certainty — name mine to me before it is too late to be of use. Do not let me spend my steadfastness on idols of my own making, nor let me mistake the noise of my own pride for the voice of truth. Open my grip. Let me act on what I know of Your Reminder while it is still light upon the heart, and not wait until it must be tasted. *Āmīn.*
