# Forgive Me, Then Give Me

## A Prayer Spoken in the Wreckage of a Test

---

## INTRODUCTION — Standing Where Solomon Stood

Let me read you the ayah first. Then I want to slow everything down, because there is a man here, and the man has just failed a test, and what he says next is one of the most carefully ordered prayers in the entire Quran.

> قَالَ رَبِّ ٱغْفِرْ لِى وَهَبْ لِى مُلْكًۭا لَّا يَنۢبَغِى لِأَحَدٍۢ مِّنۢ بَعْدِىٓ ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ

*Qāla rabbi-ghfir lī wa-hab lī mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī, innaka anta-l-Wahhāb.*

**"He said: My Lord, forgive me — and grant me a kingdom such as will not befit anyone after me. Indeed, You — You are the Bestower."**

Now. Before we touch a single word, we have to rebuild the room he is standing in. Because if you come to this ayah cold, it sounds almost arrogant. A prophet asking God for a kingdom *no one else can ever have*? That sounds like ambition. That sounds like a man who wants to be unmatched.

But look at the verse just before it. Allah says: *"And We certainly tried Solomon, and We cast upon his throne a body — then he turned back."* (38:34)

A trial. Something happened. The classical commentators preserve more than one account of what the trial was — al-Muyassar relays the well-known narration that Solomon once swore he would father a generation of horsemen who would fight in Allah's path, and he did not say *inshāʾ Allāh*, "if God wills" — and so of all his oaths, only a fragment came to pass, a single child laid upon his throne. Other narrations differ. The Quran itself does not name the trial. It leaves it veiled. It only tells you the *shape* of what happened: a man of immense power was tested, was humbled, and **turned back.**

[PAUSE]

So picture him. This is not a king on a high throne issuing a decree. This is a prophet in the aftermath of being humbled — in that raw, quiet moment after you realize you overstepped, after the floor has dropped out from under your certainty. The kind of moment where you can't pretend anymore.

And *that* is when he opens his mouth to pray.

Here is the hook. Here is what I need you to hold onto for the next hour. A man who has just been humbled asks God for the greatest kingdom in human history — and somehow this is not greed. Somehow the order of his words turns what looks like the most worldly request imaginable into one of the purest acts of worship in the Quran.

**The secret is not in *what* he asks for. The secret is in the order he asks for it, and in the Name he uses to ask.**

Let's go find it.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Order — اغْفِرْ Before وَهَبْ

Let me teach you something simple about how requests work before we go near the ayah.

Imagine you've wronged someone you love. You broke their trust. And now you come to them, and you need something from them — a favor, a real one. There are two ways to walk into that room.

The first way: you walk in and ask for the favor. And *then*, maybe, if it comes up, you apologize.

The second way: you walk in, and before you ask for anything, you say — *first* — "I'm sorry. Forgive me." And only after that, with the air cleared, do you ask.

These are not the same person. The order reveals what the heart cares about most. The first person wants the thing, and the apology is instrumental — a tool to get the thing. The second person wants the *relationship* repaired before anything else, and the favor comes second because, honestly, it matters less.

Now look at Solomon. Look at the exact sequence.

> ٱغْفِرْ لِى — *ighfir lī* — "Forgive me."
>
> وَهَبْ لِى — *wa-hab lī* — "and grant me."

**Forgiveness first. Kingdom second.**

The root of the first verb is *gh-f-r* (غفر), and the corpus confirms it: an imperative, a command-form of request — *ighfir*, "cover over, forgive." The root *gh-f-r* in the Arabic lexicon carries the sense of *covering*, *shielding* — the same root gives us *mighfar*, the helmet that covers and protects the head in battle. To ask Allah for *maghfira* is to ask Him to cover your fault, to draw a shield over it.

And notice — this is the very first thing out of his mouth after *Rabbi*. Before the wind, before the jinn, before the empire that would become legendary, the first word of his petition is: **cover my fault.**

Let's sit with this.

Here is a man about to ask for the largest gift Allah ever gave a human being in this world. And he does not lead with the gift. He leads with his need to be forgiven. The trial in verse 34 humbled him, and the very first motion of the humbled heart is not "give me" — it is "**clean me first.**"

Notice something even more precise. He does not say "forgive me *so that* You may give me a kingdom." There is no *so that*. There is no transaction. The forgiveness is not a down-payment on the kingdom. They are two separate requests, and the forgiveness comes first simply because *that is what matters most.* The relationship before the gift. The cleansing before the crown.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's remarkable. al-Qurtubi raises exactly the objection a skeptic would raise. He asks, in effect: *how could a prophet — a prophet! — ask God for worldly dominion, when the world is something Allah Himself has belittled and warned against?* It's the obvious question. Isn't asking for a kingdom the very definition of loving the dunya?

And the answer the scholars give, as al-Qurtubi records it, reframes the entire request. The kingdom Solomon asks for is not asked for as *possession*. It is asked for as **an instrument** — "to fulfill the rights of Allah, to govern His dominion justly, to establish His limits, to uphold His signs, to make His worship manifest, to enforce a just law among His creation." The kingdom is not the prize. The kingdom is the *tool* for a life of service.

And do you see now why the order matters so much? *Ighfir lī* first — *forgive me* — is what guarantees that the *mulk* that comes second is sought for Allah and not for the self. The istighfar is the purification of the intention before the intention is even spoken. A man who asks forgiveness first cannot be a man asking for a kingdom out of ego, because he has just confessed his own lowliness in the very same breath.

The order is the proof of the heart.

**What this single sequence reveals: the cleansed heart asks to be cleaned before it asks to be crowned — and that order is what makes the crown permissible to ask for at all.**

### Element Two: وَهَبْ and ٱلْوَهَّاب — Asking the Giver by the Name of Giving

Now I want to show you something in this ayah that, once you see it, you cannot un-see. It is a kind of quiet architecture — a circle the verse draws around itself.

First, a concept. In Arabic there is a specific word for a *gift* — *hiba* (هِبة), from the root *w-h-b* (وهب). And *hiba* is a very particular kind of giving. It is not a wage. It is not a reward you earned. It is not compensation for something you gave first. *Hiba* is a gift given **freely** — purely from the generosity of the giver, with no obligation, no exchange, no "you did this so here is that." When a king gives a soldier his pay, that is not *hiba* — that is owed. When a king, out of nothing but his own bounty, lifts a stranger from the dust and enriches him for no reason at all — *that* is *hiba*.

Now hold that, and watch what Solomon does.

His petition is: **وَهَبْ لِى** — *wa-hab lī* — "**gift** me." Not "pay me." Not "reward me." Not "give me what I deserve." The verb is *hab*, the imperative of *wahaba* — *give me freely, as a pure gift.*

And then watch how he closes the entire prayer:

> إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ — *innaka anta-l-Wahhāb* — "Indeed, You — You are **the Bestower.**"

The same root. *W-h-b.* He asks the **Wahhāb** to **wahab**.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what he just did? He did not ask for the kingdom and then tack on a compliment to God at the end. He asked Allah for a *hiba* — a free gift — and then named Allah by the very attribute of *free-gifting.* The request and the Name are the same root. He is, in effect, saying: *You are the One whose very nature is to give freely — so give to me freely.* He asks the Giver **by His giving.** He leverages the Name to obtain the thing the Name describes.

This is one of the most beautiful forms of *duʿāʾ* in the Quran — to call on Allah by the Name that matches your need. The thirsty man calls on *al-Razzāq*, the Provider. The lost man calls on *al-Hādī*, the Guide. And Solomon, asking for the ultimate free gift, calls on *al-Wahhāb*, the ceaseless Giver of free gifts.

And there is more in that final Name than first appears. *Al-Wahhāb* is not built on the plain pattern. It is the *faʿʿāl* form — صيغة المبالغة, the form of intensification, of abundance. *Wāhib* would mean "a giver." *Wahhāb* means **the One who gives, and gives, and gives** — whose giving is not an event but a constant outpouring, a permanent attribute. It is the difference between someone who *did* a generous thing and someone whose whole being *is* generosity.

So when Solomon ends with *innaka anta-l-Wahhāb*, listen to the logic underneath it. He is not flattering. He is reasoning. He is saying: *the size of my request does not strain You. I asked for a kingdom such as no one else will have — but You are not a giver who can be emptied. You are al-Wahhāb. The One whose giving never runs dry. A request this large is nothing against a Name this vast.*

[PAUSE]

And now I want you to feel the full circle of the verse. It opens with *Rabbi* — "my Lord," from the root *r-b-b*, the One who nurtures, sustains, raises, and provides. It moves through *ighfir* — cover me. It moves to *wahab* — gift me. And it closes on *al-Wahhāb* — the eternal Giver. The whole prayer is bracketed by giving. It begins in the hands of the One who sustains and ends in the hands of the One who bestows. The man in the middle, asking, is held on both sides by the generosity of God.

**What these two words reveal, sitting together: Solomon does not demand a wage from a master — he asks a free gift from the Freely-Giving, and he names God by the very generosity he is hoping to receive. The request is shaped to fit the Giver.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: لَّا يَنۢبَغِى — The Word That Refuses to Mean Only One Thing

This is the phrase that makes people uneasy. Let's go straight into it.

> مُلْكًۭا لَّا يَنۢبَغِى لِأَحَدٍۢ مِّنۢ بَعْدِىٓ
>
> *mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī* — "a kingdom that **lā yanbaghī** to anyone after me."

The word is **يَنۢبَغِى** — *yanbaghī*. The corpus marks it precisely: root *b-gh-y* (بغي), and it is **Form VII**, the *infaʿala* pattern. Let me teach you what Form VII does before we translate it.

In Arabic, the verb is built on a root, and then the root is poured into *forms* — patterns that bend its meaning. Form VII, the *infaʿala* form (the *n* slipping in, *yan-baghī*), very often carries a sense of something happening *of itself*, a natural disposition, something that *issues* or *follows* on its own. So *yanbaghī* does not simply mean "is allowed." It carries the sense of **what naturally befits, what is fitting, what properly belongs to** something — what *suits* it by its very nature.

So *lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī* means, at its core: a kingdom that **does not befit anyone after me.** That does not *suit* anyone else. That is not *fitting* for anyone to come.

Now here is the richness — and this is where you have to read carefully, because the phrase genuinely opens in two directions, and the classical commentators split along exactly this seam.

**The first reading.** al-Jalālayn takes *min baʿdī* to mean *siwā-ī* — "other than me." So: a kingdom that befits *no one but me.* A unique, unrepeatable dominion. And the question immediately arises: isn't that just wanting to be the greatest? And the answer, as al-Baghawī preserves it, is stunning. Several of the early authorities said Solomon asked for a uniquely unmatched kingdom **so that it would be a sign of his prophethood** — *āyatan li-nubuwwatihi, dalālatan ʿalā risālatihi, muʿjiza* — a miracle, a proof. A kingdom so unprecedented that no ordinary king could ever claim to have achieved it by ordinary means. The wind obeying him. The jinn building for him. This was not to be *envied*; it was to be *evidence* — a living sign pointing past Solomon to the God who gave it.

**The second reading.** al-Baghawī also preserves the words of ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ, who read it completely differently and far more tenderly: *"He means — grant me a kingdom that You will not strip from me at the end of my life and hand to another, the way it was once stripped from me earlier."* On this reading, Solomon — freshly humbled by the trial, having just *tasted* loss — is not asking to outshine future kings at all. He is asking for **security.** *Don't take it back this time. Don't let me lose it again.* The "after me" is not about other people; it is about Solomon's own fear of a second fall.

[PAUSE]

Now — the methodology of this kind of reflection is not to make you pick one. These are not two competing theories where one wins. They are two camera angles on the same humbled man, and the Arabic is *deliberately* wide enough to hold both. Hold them together and look at the single human being they reveal:

Here is a man who has just been broken by a test. And in his prayer he asks for a kingdom that is, at once, **a sign so unique it could only come from God** (so that no one mistakes it for his own achievement), *and* **a gift so secure he will never lose it again** (because he knows now, in his bones, how easily it can be taken). Both readings are confessions of dependence. Both say the same thing underneath: *I cannot hold this myself. Only You can make it unique. Only You can make it secure. It is entirely in Your hands.*

And notice the absence sitting right in the middle of the phrase. *Li-aḥadin* — "to *anyone*" — is indefinite, unspecified, wide open. He does not name a rival. He does not say "no king of Egypt" or "no emperor to come." He leaves *aḥad* — *anyone* — utterly open, which is what lets the phrase breathe in both directions at once. The unspecified word is doing the work. The gap is the meaning.

**What this one word reveals: Solomon asks for a kingdom that does not "befit" another — and the word is built wide enough to mean both 'a sign no one could counterfeit' and 'a gift I will never again lose.' Either way, it is the prayer of a man who has learned he holds nothing on his own.**

### The Bridge

So look at what these three pieces of language have shown us. The **order** — *forgive me* before *give me* — revealed a heart that wants cleansing before crowning. The **doubled root** — *hab* answered by *al-Wahhāb* — revealed a man asking for a free gift from the Freely-Giving, naming God by the very generosity he hopes to receive. And the **wide word** — *lā yanbaghī* — revealed a request that, however large, is a confession of total dependence.

Three different windows. One man. One posture: a king on his knees.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about Solomon's words — but about you, and about reality itself. Because this ayah is not finally about an ancient kingdom. It is about the relationship every one of us has to the things we want, the things we have, and the One who gives them.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Thing You Forgot to Ask For First

Let me start where you live.

You have a list. Everyone has a list. The job, the marriage, the house, the health back, the child, the visa, the diagnosis to come back clean, the business to finally turn. You carry it. And when you pray — if you pray — that list is usually the *content* of the prayer. "Ya Allah, give me this. Ya Allah, fix that." We come to God the way we come to a vending machine, with our requests already loaded, pressing the buttons.

And here is the thing none of us notices about our own prayers: **what we ask for first reveals what we actually worship.**

Not what we *say* we worship. What we *actually* do. Because the heart leads with what it loves most. If the first, most urgent, most repeated cry of your prayer is always *the thing* — the job, the spouse, the relief — then somewhere underneath, the thing has become the point, and God has become the means to the thing.

Now come back to Solomon. The single most powerful human being of his age, standing in the moment when he could ask for *anything*, and Allah is clearly about to say yes — look at the verses that follow, the wind, the jinn, an empire — and what does he reach for *first*?

**اغْفِرْ لِى.** Forgive me.

Not the kingdom. The kingdom comes second, and it comes almost casually, in the same breath. The thing he leads with — the thing that could not wait, the thing that came before the greatest gift in human history — is *his standing before God.* Clean me first. Cover my fault first. *Then* we can talk about kingdoms.

[PAUSE]

This is the diagnostic. This is the question the ayah hands you. **When the floor of your prayer drops away and only the most urgent thing is left — what is it?**

For most of us, if we're honest, the istighfar is the throat-clearing. It's the polite preamble we rush through to get to the real business — the asking. "Astaghfirullah, astaghfirullah, *now* — ya Rabb, about that job…" The forgiveness is the formality. The favor is the point.

Solomon inverts it. For him the istighfar *is* the point, and the kingdom is almost an afterthought — important, yes, but downstream. And remember *why* this is the order: because he had just been humbled. The trial in verse 34 stripped away the illusion that he was self-sufficient, and a heart that has truly tasted its own lowliness cannot help but lead with *cover me.* The man who leads with his sins is the man who has stopped pretending he doesn't have any.

So here is the practical turn — what changes about tomorrow. The next time you raise your hands, before the list, before the buttons, try leading the way Solomon led. Not as a formula, not as throat-clearing — but slowly, meaning it: *Ya Allah, before I ask You for anything, forgive me. Cover what I have done. Repair what is between us.* And watch what happens to the list after that. Watch how the things you wanted get quietly re-sorted, how some of them shrink, how the relationship moves to the front of the line where it was always supposed to be.

**The heart that asks to be cleaned before it asks to be given is a heart that has remembered who the gift is actually for.**

### Theme Two: You Own Nothing — and That Is the Best News You'll Hear

Let me ask you a strange question. What do you actually *own*?

Your body? It's aging without your permission, and one day it will stop, and you will hand it back. Your money? You came in with none and you will leave with none, and everything in between is on loan. Your children? They are entrusted, not possessed; they have their own souls and their own appointment with God. Your achievements, your reputation, your mind, your very next heartbeat — which of these did you manufacture? Which of these can you guarantee for even one more hour?

We walk around with a deep, unexamined assumption that the things in our hands are *ours.* That we earned them, that we hold them, that they belong to us by right. And this assumption is the source of an enormous amount of our suffering. Because if I *own* a thing, then losing it is a robbery, an injustice, a wound. The owner is always afraid — afraid of the loss, gripping, anxious, defending what is "his."

Now watch how Solomon relates to the greatest pile of "possessions" any human ever held.

He asks for a kingdom — and he asks for it as **وَهَبْ** — as a *hiba*, a free gift. He does not say "give me what I have earned." He does not approach Allah as a creditor collecting a debt, as if his prophethood or his worship had purchased him a kingdom. He comes empty-handed and asks for a *pure gift*, from **ٱلْوَهَّاب**, the One whose nature is to give freely.

And then read the answer Allah gives, three verses later. After granting him the wind and the jinn and the empire, Allah seals it with this:

> هَٰذَا عَطَآؤُنَا فَٱمْنُنْ أَوْ أَمْسِكْ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ (38:39)
>
> "**This is Our gift** — so give freely or withhold, without reckoning."

*Hādhā ʿaṭāʾunā.* **This is Our gift.** Allah Himself names the kingdom a *gift* — *ʿaṭāʾ.* Not a wage Solomon earned. Not a possession Solomon owns. A *gift* that remains, always, the property of the Giver. Solomon is handed the keys, but the house is never his. He is made a steward, never an owner.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what this does? Do you see the freedom buried in it?

Because here is the trap of the owner: the owner cannot give without pain. To the owner, every act of generosity is a subtraction, a loss from "his" pile. But look what Allah says to the *steward* — *fa-mnun aw amsik bi-ghayri ḥisāb* — "give freely or withhold, without reckoning." Solomon is told he can pour out this wealth without fear, *because it was never his to lose in the first place.* You cannot bankrupt yourself giving away what was always a gift. The steward gives freely precisely *because* he owns nothing. The very thing that terrifies the owner — letting go — is effortless for the one who never claimed ownership at all.

This is the great reversal of the ayah. We think ownership is power and dependence is weakness. Solomon shows us the opposite. The man who *grasps*, who says "mine," lives in fear of the loss. The man who receives everything as *hiba* — pure gift from al-Wahhāb — holds it all with an open hand, and an open hand cannot be pried apart, because it is already open. He has nothing to defend. The kingdom can come, the kingdom can go; the wind that obeys him today can still itself tomorrow; and through all of it he remains exactly what he was when he prayed this prayer — a servant standing before the Giver, asking, *cover me, give me, You are the One who gives.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the most quietly devastating part. Remember ʿAṭāʾ's reading of *lā yanbaghī* — that Solomon, having just *lost* his kingdom once in the trial, was pleading, "don't let me lose it again." A man terrified of losing what he holds. And what is Allah's answer to that fear? Not "yes, I'll make it permanently yours." The answer is *deeper* than that. The answer is: *it was never yours. It is Our gift. And once you understand that — once you stop being the owner and become the steward — the fear of loss has nothing left to feed on.* You cannot lose what you never owned. You can only watch the Giver give and the Giver take, and trust that the Giver is *al-Wahhāb*, the One whose giving never ends.

So the practical turn — what this changes about tomorrow. Take the one thing you grip hardest. The thing whose potential loss keeps you up at night. The child, the health, the security, the person. And try, just for a moment, to rename it. Not "mine." Say instead: *hiba.* A gift. On loan from al-Wahhāb. Held, not owned. Entrusted, not earned. And notice the loosening in your chest when you do — because the anxiety you feel about that thing is, almost always, the anxiety of an owner who is afraid. Become the steward, and the fear has nowhere to stand.

**The one who receives everything as a free gift from the Bestower is the only one who is finally free to hold anything at all — because he has stopped pretending it was ever his to lose.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture — How the Whole Verse Was Always One Prayer

Now that we have done the work, stand back and look at the shape of the whole thing, because it was holding together all along.

The verse opens with **رَبِّ** — *Rabbi* — "my Lord," from *r-b-b*, the One who sustains and nurtures and provides. And notice even here: he says *Rabbi*, not *yā Rabbī*. The vocative particle *yā* — "O" — is dropped. And in Arabic, dropping the "O" is the speech of *closeness*. You say "O So-and-so" to someone across the room; you drop it for someone right beside you. He does not call to a distant God. He speaks to the One who is *right here.*

And from that intimate opening, the prayer moves in a perfect arc:

- **رَبِّ** — my Lord, my Sustainer (the One who provides)
- **ٱغْفِرْ لِى** — forgive me (the relationship, first)
- **وَهَبْ لِى مُلْكًا** — gift me a kingdom (the request, second, and named as a free gift)
- **لَّا يَنۢبَغِى لِأَحَدٍ مِّنۢ بَعْدِى** — that befits no one after me (a confession of total dependence — only You can make it so)
- **إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْوَهَّابُ** — You are the Bestower (the request handed back to the Giver's own nature)

Do you see it? The prayer is **bracketed by God's generosity on both ends** — it opens in the hands of *al-Rabb*, the Sustainer, and closes in the hands of *al-Wahhāb*, the Bestower. The petition — the istighfar, the kingdom, the dependence — is held in the middle, cradled on both sides by the giving of God. The grammar itself is a picture of a servant surrounded.

And the answer in verse 39 completes the architecture from the outside: Solomon asks *wahab* — gift me — and Allah replies *hādhā ʿaṭāʾunā* — this is Our gift. The prayer asks in the language of *hiba*; the answer responds in the language of *ʿaṭāʾ.* Request and response rhyme. The Giver gave exactly in the register the servant asked.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The **order** — forgive me before give me — *is* the first theme: the heart that leads with cleansing before crowning. The **doubled root** — *hab* and *al-Wahhāb* — *is* the second theme: everything is a free gift, held by a steward, never owned. And the **wide word** *lā yanbaghī* sits between them as the hinge — the confession of dependence that makes the istighfar sincere and the stewardship possible. One man, one posture: a king who has discovered, in the wreckage of a test, that he is a servant — and that being a servant of *al-Wahhāb* is the richest thing a human being can be.

### Questions to Carry

1. When the noise falls away and only the most urgent cry of my prayer is left — what is it? The thing, or the One who gives the thing?
2. Do I treat istighfar as the throat-clearing before the "real" request — or as the request itself?
3. What is the one possession I grip hardest? And what would change in me if I renamed it *hiba* — a gift on loan, never owned?
4. Solomon asked for power *as a tool for service.* When I ask for more — more money, more reach, more influence — is it for the service, or for the having?
5. Allah told Solomon: "give freely or withhold, without reckoning." Do I hold what I've been given with the open hand of a steward, or the closed fist of an owner?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**A humbled king asks to be forgiven before he asks to be crowned, and names the kingdom a free gift from the Freely-Giving — teaching us that the cleansed heart leads with the relationship, and the freed heart owns nothing, because everything was always a gift from al-Wahhāb.**

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to wait for a kingdom to pray this prayer. You already hold gifts greater than thrones — a beating heart, a working mind, the people who love you, the breath you just took without asking. The invitation is small and enormous at once: the next time you raise your hands, lead the way Solomon led. *Forgive me first.* Then ask. And ask the way he asked — not as an owner collecting a debt, but as a servant standing before the Bestower, knowing that the One you're asking is *al-Wahhāb*, the One whose giving never, ever runs dry.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

O Allah, *al-Wahhāb* — before we ask You for anything, forgive us; cover what we have done and repair what is between us. Teach our hearts to lead with You and not with our wants, and let us hold every gift You give with the open hand of a servant who knows he owns nothing — because everything, always, was Yours to give. *Innaka anta-l-Wahhāb.*
