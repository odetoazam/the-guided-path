# By Your Own Might

### A reflection on Surah Sad, verses 77 to 82

### Rebuilding the world

To stand inside these verses, you have to feel where they sit.

This is not a story being told for the first time to a curious child. When these words came down in Makkah, they came to a man — the Prophet ﷺ — surrounded by chiefs. The notables of Quraysh. Men of lineage and wealth who had looked at this orphan from among them and said, in effect: *we are better than him. Why should the message come through him? Why should we bow to what he carries?*

And Surah Sad opens on exactly that energy. **ص ۚ وَٱلْقُرْآنِ ذِي ٱلذِّكْرِ** — and then immediately: *bal alladhīna kafarū fī ʿizzatin wa shiqāq* — "rather, those who disbelieve are wrapped in arrogance and defiance" (38:2). The whole surah is a meditation on what arrogance does to a soul. It parades the prophets who submitted — Dawud who fell down repentant, Sulayman who turned back to his Lord, Ayyub who endured — a gallery of the great precisely *because they bowed.*

And then, at the very end, the surah gives you the archetype. The original. The first refusal in the history of creation. It reaches all the way back, before there were Arabs or chiefs or even human beings with names, to a single moment in the unseen when the entire host of angels fell down in prostration — and one being would not.

[PAUSE]

Hold that picture. Every angel, faces to the ground. And one figure, standing. Just standing. The whole cosmos in submission, and a single vertical line of refusal cutting through it.

That refusal has already happened by the time our verses begin. Iblis has already said his terrible sentence — the sentence that, the scholars tell us, is the root of every sin that has ever been committed since: *anā khayrun minh* — "**I am better than him.**" Three words. He was asked to bow to Adam, and he ran a comparison, and the comparison came back in his favor, and so he refused.

Our six verses are what comes *after* the refusal. They are the sentencing. And then — and this is the part that should stop you — they are a *negotiation.* The condemned being speaks back. Asks for something. And is *given* it.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to carry into this reflection as a question you genuinely cannot yet answer:

When the most defiant creature in existence has just been cursed and expelled — and he turns and *asks God for a favor* — **why does God say yes?**

And when that same creature, having been granted the favor, opens his mouth to declare total war on every human being who will ever live — **why does he swear his oath of war by the very might of the One he is at war with?**

Sit in the discomfort of those two questions. Because the answers are not what you expect, and they are sitting inside the grammar of these words, waiting.

Let's go and find them.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: رَجِيمٌ — the word with three faces

Let's begin with a word you have said thousands of times without ever stopping to look at it.

Every time you open the Qur'an, every time you begin to pray, you are taught to say: *aʿūdhu billāhi mina-sh-shayṭāni-r-***rajīm**. "I seek refuge in Allah from Shaytan, the *rajīm*." It is one of the most repeated words on the tongue of a believer. And here, in 38:77, we find where the title is first pronounced over him. This is, in a sense, the verse where Shaytan *receives his name.*

> **فَإِنَّكَ رَجِيمٌ** — *fa-innaka rajīm* — "for indeed, you are *rajīm*."

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning, because you need it to see what's here.

Arabic words grow from three-letter roots, like a plant from a seed. The root here is **ر-ج-م**, *r-j-m*. And its core, physical, you-can-touch-it meaning is: **to throw stones at something. To pelt. To stone.** A *rajm* is a stoning. A *rujma* is a heap of stones piled on a grave. The root is heavy and physical — it is the sound of rocks being hurled.

Now here is the grammar. The word *rajīm* sits on a pattern Arabic calls *faʿīl*. And one of the well-documented behaviors of this pattern is that it can carry a *passive* meaning — it describes not the one who does the action, but the one to whom the action is *done.* *Jarīḥ* (on this pattern) doesn't mean "one who wounds" — it means *the wounded one.* *Qatīl* doesn't mean "killer" — it means *the slain.* *Asīr* means *the captured.*

So *rajīm* does not mean "the one who throws stones."

It means: **the one who is stoned. The one who is pelted. The one at whom things are hurled.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land. The title we give Shaytan — *al-rajīm* — is not a title of power. It is a title of *receiving.* He is the one upon whom the throwing is done. The eternal target.

Now. Here is where it opens into something breathtaking. When the classical scholars asked, *pelted with what? Stoned by what, exactly?* — they did not all answer the same way. And rather than treat their answers as competing theories where one must be right and the others wrong, I want you to hold them the way you'd hold three photographs of the same mountain taken from three sides.

**The first face.** Al-Jalālayn, plain and direct: *rajīm* here means **maṭrūd** — *driven off, expelled, banished.* The image beneath the stoning is the oldest human image of rejection there is: a figure being driven out of the camp by a hail of thrown stones. You don't argue with someone you're expelling. You don't escort them. You *pelt* them until they are gone. *Rajīm* is the one chased out under a rain of rejection. He is, at his core, **the cast-out.**

**The second face.** Al-Qurṭubī adds a layer that lifts your eyes to the sky: *marjūm bi-l-kawākib wa-sh-shuhub* — **pelted with the stars and the shooting flames.** This is the cosmic dimension. Elsewhere the Qur'an describes the shayāṭīn trying to creep up toward the heavenly assembly to steal a hearing, and being driven back by *shuhub* — burning meteors hurled like missiles (37:7–10, 67:5). So *rajīm* is not a one-time expulsion. It is an ongoing condition. The very stars of the night sky are, in this reading, an active defense system — and he is forever the thing being shot down. Look up at a falling star, and you are looking at the meaning of his name.

**The third face.** Al-Muyassar gives the subtlest of all: *marjūm bi-l-qawl* — **pelted with words. Stoned by the curse itself.** That the rejection is not only physical expulsion and not only cosmic fire, but *linguistic* — that every time a believer says *aʿūdhu billāhi mina-sh-shayṭāni-r-rajīm*, a stone is thrown. The name is self-fulfilling. We pelt him with the very title every time we seek refuge from him.

And this third reading is not a modern observation; it runs deep in the earliest transmitted commentary. Al-Ṭabarī preserved the report of Qatāda — one of the great second-generation exegetes, a man who knew companions of the Prophet ﷺ — who transmitted simply: ***al-rajīmu: al-laʿīn*** — "the *rajīm* is the *laʿīn*." The pelted one is the accursed one. The two titles fuse. To be stoned by words is to be cursed; to be cursed is to be pelted. So when God says *rajīm* and then immediately adds *laʿnatī*, He is not adding a second punishment to the first — He is drawing out what was already latent in the name.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what we have now? Not three rival meanings — **one reality seen in three depths.** He is driven out of the place (expulsion). He is driven back from the heavens (the stars). And he is driven down by the word of every believer who names him (the curse). Cast out of *where* he was, barred from *where* he wanted to climb, and struck by *what* the faithful say. The same throwing, operating on three planes at once.

And here is the thing I most want you to feel. We *fear* Shaytan. We speak of his power. But his own name, the name God gives him in the very verse of his sentencing, is a name of **utter passivity.** He is not the archer. **He is the target.** Whatever he does to us, the deepest truth encoded in his title is that things are eternally being *thrown at him.* He is losing. He has been losing since the first stone.

Now hear the sound of the verse around it. *Fa-khruj* — "get out." Say it. *Fa-KHruj.* That *khāʾ* — the harsh, scraping sound made by forcing air up against the back of the throat — is itself an act of expulsion. The command to leave *sounds like leaving;* it is a word you have to push out, almost spit out. And then *rajīm* — the hard *jīm,* the long stretched *ī,* the closing *mīm* — a word with weight, a word like a thrown stone landing.

One word. **The mightiest enemy we imagine is named, by God, as the one who is forever pelted, driven, and struck down.**

[PAUSE — let it sit]

### Element Two: مِنْهَا — "out of *it*" — and the thing God did not name

Now look at one tiny word, two letters joined to a pronoun, that almost everyone reads straight past.

> فَٱخْرُجْ **مِنْهَا** — *fa-khruj* **minhā** — "get out **of it.**"

**Of *it.*** Of *what?*

Here is a principle I want to teach you, and it may change how you read the entire Qur'an: **read the silences as carefully as you read the words.** When Arabic uses a pronoun — *it, her, them* — that pronoun must point back to something. It must have a referent. And usually the referent is obvious; it was just named. But sometimes God places a pronoun whose referent He *does not state* — and the not-stating is not an accident. It is an opening.

*Minhā.* The *hā* is feminine. "Get out of *her,* of *it.*" And the scholars, looking back through the verse for what this "it" refers to, found that God had left it open — and so they offered what they saw.

**Reading one:** *of the Garden.* This is the plain sense given by al-Jalālayn, al-Ṭabarī, al-Baghawī — *minhā ay mina-l-janna.* Get out of Paradise. The place of nearness. The dwelling is closed to you.

**Reading two:** *of the heavens* — *mina-s-samāwāt.* A wider expulsion. Not just a garden but the entire elevated realm, the company of the angels, the whole region of closeness to God. Cast all the way down.

**And then a third reading** — and al-Baghawī preserves it and a scholar he cites calls it *taʾwīlun ṣaḥīḥ,* a sound interpretation — that takes your breath. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Abū al-ʿĀliya understood *minhā* to mean: **get out of the *form* you are in.** *Mina-l-khilqa allatī anta fīhā.* Out of *yourself.* Out of the very shape and beauty of the creation you were.

Listen to the logic al-Baghawī records, because it is devastating. Iblis became arrogant *because of his form.* He boasted of what he was made of — *I am made of fire, he of clay.* His whole sin was a sin of *self-image.* He looked at his own constitution and found it superior. **So God changed the very thing he was proud of.** *Fa-ghayyara-llāhu khilqatahu* — God altered his form, *fa-swadda wa qabuḥa baʿda ḥusnih* — so he was blackened and made hideous after his beauty.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. He was proud of his form, and the punishment fit the crime with terrible precision: *get out of the form.* The very thing you exalted yourself by is the very thing taken from you. He worshipped his own beauty, so his beauty was the thing removed.

Now — *why* would God leave *minhā* unspecified? Why not just say "the Garden"?

Here is what the unstated pronoun invites us to consider. **Because all three are true at once, and naming only one would have made the expulsion smaller than it was.** He was driven out of the *place* (the Garden). He was driven out of the *realm* (the heavens). And he was driven out of *himself* (the form). The open pronoun lets the expulsion be **total** — a banishment from where he stood, from where he aspired, and from who he was. There is nothing left of his old life. Not the location, not the altitude, not even the face in the mirror.

And now notice an asymmetry that will haunt you once you see it. God leaves *minhā* — the place of expulsion — *vague.* But the very next thing, He makes intensely, possessively *specific*:

> وَإِنَّ عَلَيْكَ **لَعْنَتِى** — *wa-inna ʿalayka* **laʿnatī** — "and upon you is **My** curse."

Not "a curse." Not "the curse." **My** curse. *Laʿnatī.* The *ī* on the end is the most personal letter in Arabic — *mine.* The place he is thrown out of is left unnamed, almost as if it no longer matters where he was — but the curse that follows him is the most intimate, personal wound of all. It is *His.*

### Element Three: لَعْنَتِى — My curse, My motion

Now open the word *laʿna* itself, because what it means in the root is more active, more violent, than the English word "curse" suggests.

The root **ل-ع-ن**, *l-ʿ-n*, carries the sense of *driving to the outermost edge.* To curse in Arabic is not just to pronounce condemnation from a distance; it is to *push away,* to *expel to the margins.* A *laʿīn* is not merely one who has been condemned — he is one who has been **removed from the camp entirely**, sent beyond the perimeter, placed outside the circle of belonging. The word has motion in it. It is a verb that keeps moving.

And here the classical sources give us something of rare precision. Al-Muyassar, in its concise and careful glossing of this very verse, renders *laʿnatī* not as simply "my curse" but as: ***ṭardī wa ibʿādī*** — **My driving-out and My making-far.** Two verbs, not one state. *Ṭard*: the act of chasing out, of refusing entry, of pushing through the gate and bolting it behind. *Ibʿād*: the act of *increasing the distance*, of making yet further, of widening the gap with every moment that passes.

[PAUSE]

Let that distinction settle. We might imagine the laʿna as a sentence pronounced once, recorded in a book, and then filed. A fact about Iblis, static and historical. But al-Muyassar's reading insists on something different: **the laʿna is an ongoing divine action.** Every moment is another act of *ṭard* — another expulsion renewed. Every moment is another act of *ibʿād* — the distance between him and God growing, not fixed. The curse is not a wall built once. It is a wall that keeps being extended, that keeps being pushed outward, that never lets the distance close.

And this continues — *ilā yawmi-d-dīn.* Until the Day.

Al-Jalālayn glosses *al-dīn* here with characteristic precision as ***al-jazāʾ*** — recompense, the settling of accounts. The root *d-y-n* carries a cluster of meanings that circle around the same axis: debt, repayment, measurement, judgment. The word *dayn* (debt) shares this root. The word *dāna* (to be owed, to be subject to) shares this root. *Yawm al-dīn* is the day when every measurement is taken, every debt called in, every account balanced to the last fraction. It is not merely "judgment day" in a vague sense — it is the day of **precise reckoning.** The curse extends through all of history, across every moment of time, until the day when time itself is summed up and every number is final.

And here is the thing that catches the breath: the curse is not specified to end *on* that day, but *until* it. *Ilā yawm al-dīn.* The Day of Recompense is when the entire structure of history is finalized — and whatever the laʿna becomes on that day, it is absorbed into that final accounting. The expulsion that has been ongoing throughout history meets, on that day, its eternal verdict.

### Element Four: رَبِّ — the address that cannot be taken back

And now something utterly astonishing. The cursed, expelled, pelted being — the creature whose form has been altered, whose dwelling has been stripped away, who is being pushed further from God with every passing moment — opens his mouth. And the first word he speaks is:

> **رَبِّ** — *Rabbi* — "**My Lord.**"

Stop there. Do not read past it.

He just called God *his Lord.* In Arabic, *rabb* carries the full weight of ownership, nurturing, sovereignty, and sustaining care — the One who raised you, shaped you, has authority over you, provides for you. To say *rabbi* is to acknowledge a relationship of *total dependence.* And Iblis says it.

He does not say "O Power." He does not say "O Creator." He does not negotiate with some abstract force. He says *Rabbi* — **My Lord.** He claims the relationship even in the act of rebellion. He knows exactly who God is and what God's claim on him is. And he acknowledges it. Fully. And *still* he refuses to submit.

This is the most devastating diagnosis in the passage. Because it means his sin is not ignorance. It is not disbelief in the *existence* or *sovereignty* of God. Classical commentators across the tradition agree on this: **Iblis is not an atheist.** He is something far more specific and far more dangerous. He is the one who *knows* the truth completely and *still* chooses himself. He calls God "My Lord" and in the same breath asks God to grant him time to wage war on God's beloved creation.

Knowledge without submission. Acknowledgment without obedience. This is *kibr* in its purest, most distilled form.

[PAUSE]

And we feel the weight of this differently when we recognize that this is precisely the Quraysh chiefs' condition too. They are not atheists. They know the Kaʿba. They know Ibrahim. They know the traditions of prophecy. They know the Qur'an is extraordinary. Their rejection is not ignorance — it is *ʿizza*, arrogance, the refusal to bow that feels like dignity to the one who refuses. And so the archetype in the surah is not chosen at random. It is surgical. **This is you,** God seems to say to every chief of Quraysh who heard it. *This is the image of what you are doing.*

### Element Five: فَأَنظِرْنِى — asking to be delayed

Now the request itself. The morphology here is worth slowing for.

> **فَأَنظِرْنِى** — *fa-anẓirnī* — "then grant me respite / delay me."

The verb is *anẓara*, the Form IV (causative) of the root **ن-ظ-ر**, *n-ẓ-r*. The basic root means *to look at, to see, to wait, to observe.* The Form IV causative makes the action *happen to someone else* — it means *to cause someone to wait, to give them a delay, to hold their case open.* When you say *anẓirnī*, you are saying: **make me be one who is waited upon. Extend the waiting. Hold the verdict in suspension.**

What he is asking for is not mercy in the conventional sense. He is not asking for forgiveness; he made no gesture of repentance. He is asking for *time.* Operational time. He accepts the sentence. He does not contest the expulsion or the curse. What he contests is *immediacy.* He wants the execution of final judgment delayed so that he can act within the interval.

And notice what he asks for. *Ilā yawmi yubʿathūn* — **until the Day they are raised.** Not "until I've had a chance to make amends." Not "until the next generation." Until the resurrection itself. He wants the maximum interval. He wants to operate for the entire length of human history.

The scholars note the audacity of this. Here is a creature who has just been expelled and cursed, and his first move is to ask the One who expelled and cursed him for the longest possible term of extension — essentially requesting unlimited access to humanity for the entire span of creation. And the remarkable thing — the thing that should shake you — is that the answer comes back: *yes.*

### Element Six: مِنَ الْمُنظَرِينَ — classified, not singled out

But look carefully at *how* God says yes.

> **فَإِنَّكَ مِنَ الْمُنظَرِينَ** — *fa-innaka mina-l-munẓarīn* — "so indeed, you are among those granted respite."

God does not say: "You, specifically, are given this." God does not address Iblis as an exception, as a unique case, as a special arrangement being made for him. Instead — and this is a detail the classical scholars found significant — **God places him in a category.** *Mina-l-munẓarīn.* Among *the* respited ones. Among those who are already in this classification.

This plural matters. It tells us that Iblis is not a one-off, not an unprecedented edge case that God is improvising for. He is being placed within the framework of divine *qadar* — the decree — as one member of a broader category of things in the created order that have been given extension, given interval, given the rope of time before the final reckoning comes. He does not get a special deal. He gets slotted into what was already true of creation: that time is a given, that interval is the structure of this world, that things are not immediately finalized.

The word *munẓarīn* is on the *mufaʿʿalīn* pattern — it is the plural passive participle. They are the *waited-upon*, the *delayed*, those over whom the suspension hangs. And Iblis is among them. Not above. Not uniquely treated. **Among.**

There is a kind of humbling in this. Even in the granting, God does not elevate him. He is processed. He is categorized. He takes his place in a classification God had already established for the created order.

### Element Seven: يَوْمِ الْوَقْتِ الْمَعْلُومِ — the known day, and the modification

But now look at what the grant *actually* says — because God does not simply repeat Iblis's request back to him.

Iblis asked: *ilā yawmi yubʿathūn* — until the Day they are raised.

God grants: *ilā yawmi-l-waqti-l-maʿlūm* — until the Day of the **appointed, known time.**

These are not identical phrases. And the classical scholars noticed.

The word **وَقْت**, *waqt*, means a *precisely defined time, a fixed moment, a specified slot in the sequence.* It is time made specific — time with coordinates, time that has been bounded and measured. And the modifying adjective **مَعْلُومٌ**, *maʿlūm*, means *known* — a passive participle from the root for knowledge. **The appointed time that is known.**

Known by whom? Known to God. Not disclosed to Iblis. Not disclosed to us.

Al-Ṭabarī records the scholars' disagreement on this point, and it is worth sitting with rather than resolving too quickly. Some held that *yawm al-waqt al-maʿlūm* is essentially the same as the resurrection — that the two phrases point to the same moment and the change is stylistic rather than substantive. Others held that there is a genuine distinction: that the "known appointed time" is specifically the moment of the first blowing of the Trumpet — the point at which Iblis himself perishes — *before* the resurrection proper, so that the respite ends fractionally earlier than Iblis requested, at the moment everything in creation dies, not at the moment of their rising.

Whether or not these two days are separated by an interval is a question the scholars left open, because the Qur'an does not specify. But what is not open is the fact that **God did not simply ratify Iblis's requested terms.** God modified the phrasing. God substituted His own formulation for the one Iblis proposed. And in doing so, God reminded Iblis — and reminds us — of something important:

**The timing belongs to God.** You may ask for the maximum; the maximum is defined by One who holds a calendar you cannot read. The respite is real. The interval is real. But its boundaries are *known* — not by the one who requested them, but by the One who granted them.

[PAUSE]

There is something quietly sovereign in this substitution. Iblis asks for resurrection day as if he is naming the terms. God takes the terms back. *The time is known.* It was always known. And you, standing in the interval of your request, do not know what we know.

---

### Element Eight: فَبِعِزَّتِكَ — the oath that proves everything

And now we arrive at the verse the entire reflection has been building toward. The verse the title came from.

Iblis has been expelled. Iblis has been cursed. Iblis has been granted his time. And now he opens his mouth to declare what he intends to do with it.

> **قَالَ فَبِعِزَّتِكَ** — *Qāla fa-bi-ʿizzatika* — "He said: Then by **Your might** —"

Stop there. Before the declaration. Just the oath.

The root **ع-ز-ز**, *ʿ-z-z,* is one of the most charged roots in the Qur'an. *ʿIzza* is invincibility, indomitability, a might that cannot be overcome from outside. It is not raw power — it is *secured, unassailable, impenetrable strength.* It is the word used of God as *al-ʿAzīz*, the Ever-Mighty. It is the quality that means *nothing outside of God can diminish God.* Total self-sufficiency of strength.

And Iblis swears by it.

[PAUSE]

Think about what this means — really think about it. You are at war with someone. You are about to declare your campaign of total war against everything that person loves. You want to swear an oath that your declaration is real, that your commitment is total. And you swear by the *might of the person you are at war with.*

Why would you do that?

Because you have nothing else to swear by.

That is the answer the classical tradition gives, and it lands with the weight of a stone. **Iblis has no independent might.** He has no *ʿizza* of his own. Whatever operational capacity he has was given to him — given by the very One whose might he now invokes. He was created. He was sustained. His intelligence, his subtlety, his capacity to whisper and suggest and deceive — all of it was breathed into him by the same God against whom he is now declaring war. He cannot swear "by my own power" because he has none that is *his.* Every atom of his existence is borrowed.

So in the very act of declaring total war, **Iblis involuntarily testifies to divine sovereignty.** He cannot make his oath without acknowledging God's might. He cannot launch his campaign without invoking God's attribute. He wanted to be independent; the grammar of his own declaration proves he isn't.

This is what the title was pointing at: *the anatomy of a pride that is made to borrow the power it wars against.* You cannot fight God with God's own strength without the fight itself becoming a testimony to the strength of the One you cannot overcome.

And the early commentators preserved something even more pointed: note that Iblis does not say *bi-qudratī* — "by my own power." He does not say *bi-ḥīlatī* — "by my cunning." He says *bi-ʿizzatika* — "by *Your* might." As if the ʿizza of God is the very arena within which all things move, including rebellion itself. Nothing escapes it. Not even the declaration of war against it.

[PAUSE — let this sit for a long moment]

### Element Nine: لَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ — the total claim, and its preemptive undoing

Now the declaration itself.

> **لَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ** — *la-ughwiyannahum ajmaʿīn* — "I will surely lead them **all** astray."

The verb is *aghwā*, Form IV of the root **غ-و-ي**, *gh-w-y*. The basic root carries the sense of *ruin, perdition, going astray into loss.* To go where you should not go and arrive somewhere that destroys you. The Form IV makes it causative: *to cause another to go there. To lead someone into that ruin.* It is not deception in the neutral sense — it is *the act of ruining someone by misdirection.* A ghāwī is one who has already been ruined; *aghwā* is making someone else ruined.

And he claims he will do it to **أَجْمَعِينَ** — *all of them. Every single one.* The word *ajmaʿīn* is the emphatic, comprehensive plural — it leaves no exceptions, no stragglers, no category set aside. He is claiming total coverage. Every human being who will ever exist.

Now listen for what comes immediately after this declaration in the next verse (38:83):

> **إِلَّا عِبَادَكَ مِنْهُمُ الْمُخْلَصِينَ** — "except Your servants among them — the *mukhlasīn.*"

God's response does not argue the point. It does not debate whether Iblis is capable of what he claims. It simply states: ***except.*** An exception. Already named. Already secured. Already *known* — precisely the word used in the "known appointed time" just verses before.

And classical commentators across the tradition drew attention to this word: *mukhlasīn.* It is the passive participle — not *mukhlisīn* (those who make themselves sincere) but *mukhlasīn* (those who have been made sincere, those from whom sincerity was drawn out, those whom God purified). The sincere servants are not self-made. They are *chosen-out.* They were extracted from the mass before Iblis even finished his boast.

[PAUSE]

The total claim is punctured before it is complete. *All of them* — and in the very next verse, *not all of them.* God does not fight the declaration with a counter-declaration. He simply names the exception as if it is obvious, as if it was always the case, as if the *mukhlasīn* were already removed from the field before Iblis ever set foot on it.

This is the structure of divine protection. It is not reactive. It is not a response to Iblis's plan. It was established before the plan was announced.

## PART 2: WHAT THE PASSAGE BUILDS

### The anatomy of expulsion

Step back now from the individual words and see the shape of what has happened.

A being of extraordinary creation — one the tradition describes as the most devoted worshipper among the jinn, who had climbed through worship to a station near the angels — looked at Adam and performed a calculation. He weighed himself and found himself superior. And on the basis of that calculation, he refused the command of God.

The calculation was about *origin.* Fire versus clay. He evaluated himself by *what he was made of.* And here is the thing: the calculation was not entirely wrong in its facts. Fire is, in the physical world, more volatile, more energetic, more apparently powerful than clay. If you are evaluating by material constitution, his logic has a surface coherence.

But the logic was wrong in its *premise.* Because the question was never: *what are you made of?* The question was: *will you obey?* And he answered the question he wanted to be asked instead of the question he was actually given.

This is *kibr* in its structural form. **Arrogance is not the belief that you are great. It is the belief that your greatness exempts you from obedience.** It is the insertion of the self between the command and the response, as a filter, as a judge, as a veto. It is looking at God's instruction and running it through your own evaluation first — and acting on the evaluation rather than the instruction.

And what God's response teaches us — in the stripping of the Garden, the stripping of the heavens, the stripping of the form, the ongoing *ṭard wa ibʿād* of the laʿna — is that there is no floor beneath the one who falls by pride. Every layer peels away. Not because God is vindictive, but because **what pride exalts, reality corrects.** He was proud of his station — the station is removed. He was proud of his form — the form is changed. He was proud of his proximity to the divine — the proximity becomes its opposite. The punishment is not a punishment *added* to his condition; it is his condition *revealed.*

### The wisdom in the respite

But now the question we began with. *Why does God say yes?*

The grant of respite to Iblis is not an anomaly in the divine plan. It is a *component* of it. Because what is being built in human existence is not a race between creatures who cannot fail — it is a space in which real choice becomes possible. And real choice requires real pressure. A test with no adversary is not a test.

What God grants to Iblis is the operational room to function as exactly what humanity needs him to be in order for human life to have its full weight: **a real, intelligent, motivated, patient adversary.** Not omnipotent — but real. Not invincible — but present. Not everywhere in the way God is everywhere — but accessible enough that when you choose against him, the choice means something.

The *munẓar* (the one given respite) is also the *mumtaḥan* (the one being used as the instrument of testing). The grant to Iblis is a gift to humanity. It is what makes the space between birth and death matter, what makes repentance meaningful, what makes the *mukhlasīn* the *mukhlasīn* rather than simply the well-born.

And here is the architecture of divine wisdom that the passage encodes: **God gives Iblis respite by placing him within a known structure.** The time is bounded (*yawm al-waqt al-maʿlūm*). The protected category is already defined (*mukhlasīn*). The tools of his campaign are borrowed (*bi-ʿizzatika*). And his title names his ultimate condition, not his temporary capacity (*rajīm* — the forever-pelted).

He is given a real role to play, with real parameters, within a structure God did not hand him the blueprints to.

### The borrowed sword

The theological center of these six verses — the thing the title has been circling since the beginning — is the sword Iblis picks up to wage his war.

He swears by God's might.

Consider what this means for how you experience temptation. When whispering comes — when the voice suggests that you are better than this person, that you deserve more than this circumstance, that the comparison favors you, that submission is beneath you — that voice is not drawing on a power independent of God. **Every capacity Shaytan has to whisper was given to him.** Every subtlety, every tailoring of the message to your specific weakness, every precise timing — all of it is *borrowed.* He is operating on borrowed time (the respite), toward a borrowed end (the undoing of God's beloved), with borrowed tools (faculties given by God), invoking a borrowed authority (God's own might as the ground of his oath).

The Arabic grammatical structure of his oath is instructive. The *bāʾ* in *fa-bi-ʿizzatika* is the *bāʾ* of swearing — the same preposition used when you swear an oath by something. In classical Arabic, you swear by something *greater than yourself*, something that has authority over the declaration you're making, something that will hold the oath in place even if you waver. When Iblis swears *bi-ʿizzatika*, he is using God's might as the *guarantor* of his oath of war.

And guarantors, by definition, are above the thing they guarantee.

He cannot escape it. Even his declaration of total war against humanity contains, embedded in its grammar, an acknowledgment of who is above him.

[PAUSE]

This is not a rhetorical trick. It is the deepest theological reality these verses encode. **Pride, carried to its ultimate expression, still cannot unseat the truth it denies.** Iblis said "I am better than him" — but he cannot say "I am independent of God." He can refuse to bow, but he cannot refuse to be created. He can swear war, but he must swear by the strength of the One he wars against. The cosmos is structured in such a way that rebellion is parasitic on the very sovereignty it rebels against.

And that means: **he cannot win.** Not ultimately. Not structurally. Whatever victories he scores in individual moments — and he will score them — the architecture of his campaign is built on borrowed materials that can be called back at any moment.

## CLOSING MOVEMENT

### What Iblis does not understand about you

Here is what the passage ultimately wants you to know — and it is, perhaps, the most practical thing in it.

Iblis declared total war on *all of them.* But the very next verse names the exception. And the exception is not defined by bloodline, or nationality, or length of beard, or eloquence of speech. The exception is defined by a single word: *mukhlasīn.* Those whose sincerity God has purified. Those who have been *made* sincere from within.

This means the protection is not primarily a matter of your strength against his — it is a matter of your *orientation.* Are you turned toward God? Are you, even haltingly, even with failures and returns and failures again, *moving in that direction?* Because the one who is turned toward God has placed themselves in the category Iblis himself announced as outside his reach.

He announced his own limitation. He drew the boundary himself: *all of them — except the mukhlasīn.* And now you know what being *mukhlasīn* means: it is to be the one whose sincerity God has taken into His own keeping. To be extracted from the mass. To be *His* — because that possessive is the only one that matters, and it is the mirror image of the possessive in *laʿnatī:* just as the curse is *His,* the protection is *His,* and both operate with an intimacy and a persistence that nothing in creation can override.

### The sound we started with, now heard differently

Go back to the opening. Listen again.

*Qāla fa-khruj minhā fa-innaka rajīm.*

You hear *rajīm* differently now. Not as a title of threat but as a title of **already-defeated.** The one forever pelted. The one against whom God's curse is an active, ongoing motion of *ṭard wa ibʿād* — expulsion and increasing distance. The one who swears by power he does not own. The one who declared total war with a built-in exception he himself named.

*Wa-inna ʿalayka laʿnatī ilā yawmi-d-dīn.*

And upon him is *My* curse. Until the Day when all accounts are *settled.* Until every debt of *jazāʾ* is paid. He does not escape the counting. He simply has a longer interval before the final tally.

*Qāla rabbi fa-anẓirnī.*

He calls God "My Lord" even now. Knowledge without submission. The most dangerous condition — because it means the door to return was always open, always visibly open, and the refusal was therefore always perfectly chosen, never accidental, never ignorant.

*Fa-innaka mina-l-munẓarīn.*

Among those given time. Classified. Placed within the structure. Not above it.

*Ilā yawmi-l-waqti-l-maʿlūm.*

Until the known time. Known by God. Unknown to him. The limits were always God's to set.

*Fa-bi-ʿizzatika la-ughwiyannahum ajmaʿīn.*

By *Your* might. Borrowed. Acknowledged. Involuntary testimony embedded in the war cry itself.

[PAUSE — final pause]

### A word for the road

These six verses travel from expulsion to oath. From sentence to declaration. But the declaration — as we have seen — is already answered in the seventh verse that follows, before we even reach it. The exception is already secured. The mukhlasīn are already named. The time is already known.

You do not have to be afraid of an enemy who must borrow his weapons from the One protecting you.

You do not have to be paralyzed by a threat sworn on an authority that is not his to swear by.

You do not have to count yourself among the *all* — because the very one who said "all of them" immediately told you where the wall is. And it is not a wall you build. It is a wall God builds, around those who are *His.* The possessive is the whole protection. *Laʿnatī* — My curse upon him. *Mukhlasīn* — My purified ones, set apart.

The one who cannot stop being *rajīm* — driven back, struck down, forever the target — is not the final word in this story.

You are.
