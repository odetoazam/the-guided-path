# The Body on the Throne

### Surah Sad, Ayah 34

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first. In Arabic:

> **وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَٰنَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِۦ جَسَدًۭا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ**

Transliteration:

> *wa-laqad fatannā Sulaymāna wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan thumma anāb.*

And in plain English:

> "And We had certainly tried Solomon, and We placed upon his throne a body; then he turned to his Lord."

Nine words. That's the whole verse. Nine words to tell you about the single most extraordinary collapse-and-return in the life of the most powerful human being who ever lived.

### Let me rebuild the world you need to stand in.

Forget, for a moment, the children's-storybook version of Sulayman — the one with the talking ants and the smiling hoopoe bird. That's real, but it's not the world of *this* verse. The world of this verse is **power**. Total, unrivaled, planet-scale power.

This is a man who, a few verses later, will be handed the wind itself — *"so We subjected to him the wind, blowing gently by his command wherever he directed"* (38:36). The jinn worked for him as builders and divers. He understood the speech of birds. And in his own du'a — which comes in the very next verse — he asks for *"a kingdom such as will not belong to anyone after me"* (38:35). Not "a great kingdom." A kingdom **so singular that history will never repeat it.** And Allah grants it.

So hold that in your mind. The peak. Not a peak — *the* peak. The highest a human being has ever sat in this world. There is no throne above this throne.

Now look at where this verse falls in the surah. Surah Sad is a gallery. Allah is walking the Prophet ﷺ — who is exhausted, rejected, grieving in Makkah — through a portrait hall of prophets. And one word keeps getting stamped under each portrait like a signature: **awwāb** — *the one who keeps coming back.* Dawud is *awwāb* (38:17). Ayyub is *awwāb* (38:44). And of Sulayman, in this very passage, Allah says — *"how excellent a servant! Indeed, he was awwāb"* (38:30).

That's the theme of the whole gallery. Not "how excellent these men were at never falling." **How excellent they were at returning.**

### The Hook

And here is the strange thing — the thing I need you to feel the weight of before we go a single step further.

Allah tells us that He tested Sulayman. Allah tells us He *"placed upon his throne a body."* A body. *Jasadan.* On the seat of the most powerful kingdom in the history of the earth — a body.

And then... Allah tells us almost nothing else. He does not tell us what the body was. He does not tell us what the test was. He does not tell us what Sulayman did wrong. The most detail-rich Book ever revealed suddenly goes silent at the exact moment your curiosity is screaming the loudest.

[PAUSE]

Why would Allah do that? Why empty out the one verse where you most want the details?

Stay with me. Because the silence is not an absence of meaning. **The silence *is* the meaning.** And by the end, I think you'll see that this verse was never really about Sulayman's throne.

It was about yours.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Jasadan* — "A Body"

Let's start with the strangest word in the verse. *Jasadan.* "A body."

**First, the concept — before we touch the word.**

In Arabic, as in English, you can refer to a human form with several different words, and they are not interchangeable. There's a word for the living, functioning person — the self that eats and walks and breathes. And there's another kind of word — one that points specifically to the *form* without the life in it. The shell. The figure. The thing that has the shape of a person but not the spirit of one.

In English, think of the difference between saying *"someone was sitting in the chair"* versus *"a figure was slumped in the chair."* The second one makes your skin crawl a little, doesn't it? Because *"figure"* quietly tells you: something is missing. The shape is there. The life may not be.

Arabic has exactly this distinction. And the word Allah chooses here — **jasad** — is the second kind.

**Now, the word itself.** *Wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan.* "And We cast upon his throne — a *jasad*."

Here is what's remarkable, and you can verify it across the whole Quran. The word *jasad* appears only a handful of times in the entire Book — and almost every time, it means **a form that has no life in it.**

Listen. When the people of Musa took the golden calf to worship, Allah says they took *"ʿijlan jasadan lahu khuwār"* — *"a calf, a jasad, having a lowing sound"* (7:148, and again 20:88). A *jasad.* A body-shape. Metal. Hollow. Making a sound but utterly without a soul. The very word is chosen to tell you: **this thing they bowed to was a shell.**

And listen to this one — Allah defending His prophets against the mockers who said, "What kind of messenger eats food and walks in the markets?" Allah replies: *"And We did not make them jasadan that did not eat food, nor were they immortal"* (21:8). A *jasad*, here, is precisely a body that **does not eat** — a form without the living functions of a living being.

So now come back to Sulayman's throne with those echoes ringing.

*Wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi jasadan.*

Allah did not say He placed *a man* on the throne. He did not say *a person* or *a king* or even *an enemy.* He said He placed **a jasad** — and the word itself, everywhere else in the Quran, carries the scent of **lifelessness.** A form. A shell. Power's shape with power's life drained out of it.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. The most powerful seat on earth. And what does Allah put on it, to test its owner? Not a rival army. Not a plague. Not a dragon. **A body.** Just... a still, silent body, where the king should be.

Picture it the way the original listener would have. You walk into the great hall. The wind-servant is waiting outside. The jinn have built the palace. The birds are overhead. And on the throne — the seat of all of it — sits a *thing* in the shape of a man, going nowhere, doing nothing, ruling nothing.

**What is a body on a throne?**

It is *power that cannot move.* It is authority with no one home. It is the terrifying, naked truth of every throne ever built, made visible for one moment: that the throne does not give life to the one who sits on it — the one who sits on it was only ever *borrowing* his life, and the moment the loan is called in, what remains on the seat of all that power is **a jasad.** A shape. A shell.

### A word about the stories — and the silence.

Now, you may have heard dramatic stories about what this body was. And the classical commentators preserve several. Let me give you the composite, because each is a camera angle on the same reality — and because the *disagreement itself* is going to teach us something.

Al-Jalalayn relays the well-known narrative that a jinn took Sulayman's ring — the seat of his authority — and sat on his throne in his form, while Sulayman wandered unrecognized. Al-Baghawi preserves a long account, traced through Wahb ibn Munabbih, of Sulayman losing his kingdom for a period. Al-Qurtubi records the tradition from Ibn ʿAbbas about a legal case where Sulayman's heart inclined toward one party. And al-Muyassar foregrounds a different reading entirely: that the *jasad* was a half-formed child.

Now — be careful and be honest here. Many of these detailed narratives are *isrā'īliyyāt*, stories that entered the commentaries from earlier traditions, and the scholars themselves transmit them with caution and disagreement. They are not all of equal weight, and the Quran itself **names none of them.**

But there is one anchor that stands on much firmer ground. In a sound, *marfūʿ* hadith — a narration traced directly to the Prophet ﷺ in Bukhari and Muslim — the Prophet ﷺ said that Sulayman once declared he would go around to [seventy, in one wording] of his wives, *"each one of whom will bear a horseman who will fight in the way of Allah."* His companion said to him, *"Say: in shā' Allāh"* — *if Allah wills.* But he did not say it. And none of them bore a child except one woman, who bore **half a person** — *shiqq rajul.* The Prophet ﷺ then said: *"Had he said 'in shā' Allāh,' they would all have fought in the way of Allah."*

A half-formed body. *Shiqq* — half. A *jasad* with the loan of full life never granted.

Do you feel how that reading marries the word? The man who, for one instant, leaned on *his own* power — *I will,* without *if Allah wills* — is shown, on the very seat of his power, the limit of his power: a half-formed, lifeless form. The throne could not complete what Allah did not will to complete.

[PAUSE]

But here is the deepest point — and it lives in the **silence.** Notice that even with this authentic hadith, the Quran *still does not spell it out.* It says *jasadan* — indefinite, undescribed, *"a body."* No "the body of so-and-so." No explanation. Just: a body.

The commentators disagree *because Allah left a gap.* And we have to ask: why would the Most Eloquent leave a gap exactly here?

Because if Allah had named the body — told you it was a jinn, or a child, or a usurper — you would file it away as a *story about Sulayman.* A historical curiosity. Something that happened, once, to someone else, long ago.

By leaving it **a body** — undescribed, indefinite, universal — Allah makes it a mirror. *Jasadan* is not the body of one man. It is the body that sits on **every** throne the moment its owner forgets Who lent him his breath. It is the shape of *your* authority, *your* career, *your* health, *your* household — the day the life is withdrawn and only the form remains.

The vagueness is not Allah withholding information. **It is Allah handing you the verse.**

That single word — *jasadan* — reveals this: *all worldly power is a borrowed breath wrapped around a shell, and the throne never belonged to the one who sat on it.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Anāb* — "Then He Turned"

Now look at how the verse ends. After the test. After the body on the throne. Three letters of Arabic, one word: *anāb.* **"He turned."**

**The concept first.**

There are two completely different ways a human being can respond to falling. You know both of them from your own life.

One is the spiral. You fail, and the failure becomes a story you tell yourself about who you *are.* "I always do this. I'm just like this. There's no point." The fall becomes an identity. You sink into it.

The other is the *return.* You fail — and instead of sinking, you **turn around and go back.** Back to the door you came from. Back to the One you belong to. The fall, in this mode, is not a destination. It's a pivot.

The Quran has a precise word for the second one. And to understand it, you have to feel the root it's built from.

**The word and its root.** *Anāb* comes from the root **n-w-b** (ن-و-ب). And here is the beautiful thing: this is the same root from which Arabic gets the word *nawba* — **a turn, a shift, a bout** — and *nā'ib* — a **deputy,** the one who *takes his turn* standing in for another. (You may even know a related modern form: a parliamentary representative is a *nā'ib.*) The whole family of this root circles one core idea, attested in the classical lexicons: *to return, time after time. To come back around, again and again.*

*Nāba* — Ibn Manẓūr and al-Rāghib record — is *to return repeatedly,* to come back **turn after turn,** the way a recurring fever returns, the way the night-shift comes back around to the same watchman. It is not a single arrival. It is **cyclical, habitual coming-back.**

So when Allah closes Sulayman's test with *anāb*, He is not merely saying "he repented once." He is saying: **he turned back — the way he always turned back. He came around again, the way the truly devoted always come around again.** This is *inābah* — and al-Rāghib defines it precisely as *al-rujūʿ ilā Allāh* — the return to Allah, with repentance and sincerity of deed.

Now hold that against the word stamped over Sulayman's whole portrait in this surah: **awwāb** (38:30) — *the one who keeps returning.* Do you see? The verb at the end of verse 34, *anāb*, is the *awwāb* in action. The title told you who he was. This word shows you the title being earned — in real time, in the worst moment.

[PAUSE]

Notice something now. Notice what Allah does **not** say.

Allah does not tell you what Sulayman repented *for.* There's no object. No confession spelled out. No itemized sin. Just — *thumma anāb.* "Then he turned."

Read that absence carefully, the way we read the absence in *jasadan.* If Allah had detailed the sin, you'd be busy *judging the sin.* Was it serious? Was it minor? Was it fair? You'd turn the verse into a courtroom about Sulayman.

By naming only the **turning** and not the failing, Allah trains your eye on the one thing that actually matters for *you.* Not the precise anatomy of the fall — **the fact of the return.** The Quran is not interested in cataloguing what broke him. It is interested in showing you *what he did next.* He turned.

And one more thing — the word *thumma.* "Then." In Arabic, *thumma* is not the quick "and then" of *fa-.* It carries a sense of **interval, of sequence with a gap.** The test happened. The body sat on the throne. There was a space — a real, lived space, the disorienting space of a man confronting the shell of his own power... *thumma.* **Then.** He turned.

The return is allowed to come *after* the descent. It does not have to be instant to be real. There is room, in the grammar itself, for the journey back to take a moment.

What does this single word reveal? That **the mark of the beloved servant is not an unbroken record — it is an unbreakable direction.** He may fall. But he is always, always *facing home.* And when the test is over, the needle of his heart swings back to its Lord like a compass swinging back to north.

### Element Three (briefly): *Fatannā* and *Kursiyy* — Who Gets Tested, and Where

Two quick observations to frame the others, because the *who* and the *where* of this verse are not incidental.

First: *fatannā.* **"We tested."** From the root *f-t-n* — a heavy word. *Fitnah* is the testing of gold *in fire,* the trial that separates the real from the fake by putting it under heat. And notice the staggering thing: the one put in the fire here is **a prophet.** A king. Allah's own beloved servant, the *awwāb.*

We tend to imagine that closeness to Allah buys you *exemption* from trial. This verse says the opposite. *We* tested **Sulayman** — the favored one, the singular king. The fire is not a sign of Allah's distance. **The hottest fire is reserved for the most precious gold.** Your test is not evidence that He has forgotten you. It may be evidence of exactly how seriously He is treating you.

Second: *kursiyyihi.* **"His throne."** The root *k-r-s* carries the sense of something **compacted, firmly established, solid** — a *kursī* is a seat built to hold weight, to stand firm. And *that* is the cruel precision of the image: on the most *solid, established, firm-footed* seat human power has ever known, Allah lays down a *jasad* — a thing with no firmness in it at all. The firmest throne. The most lifeless occupant. The contrast is the whole sermon.

### The Bridge

So look at what these words have shown us, just as words. *Fatannā* — the beloved is the one who is tested. *Kursiyy* — on the firmest throne. *Jasadan* — a lifeless shell, undescribed so it can be everyone's. *Thumma anāb* — then, after the space of the fall, the eternal return.

The construction itself draws an arc: **summit → emptying → return.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About *your* summit, *your* emptying, and the only move that has ever mattered when you reach it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Hollow Crown — When You Reach the Top and Find a Body on the Throne

Let me start where you already live.

There is a feeling that almost no one talks about, because it sounds ungrateful, and because it terrifies us. It is the feeling of **arriving** — and finding it empty.

You worked for the promotion for nine years. You got it. And three Tuesdays later, you're sitting in the corner office, and there's this hollowness you can't explain. You bought the house. You married the person. You hit the number in the bank account you swore would make you feel *safe.* And you got there — and the safety wasn't there. Something was sitting on the throne you'd built, but it wasn't the joy you'd promised yourself. It was a kind of... shape of joy. A shell.

Psychologists have names for pieces of this — the *arrival fallacy,* the *hedonic treadmill,* the strange depression that hits high achievers at the moment of triumph. But the names just describe it. They don't explain it. **Why** is the summit so often hollow?

Surah Sad, verse 34, explains it.

Sulayman had the realest summit a human being has ever had. Not a metaphor. The wind. The jinn. A kingdom with no equal in history. If *any* throne on earth could be full — could finally satisfy, finally be *enough* — it would be his.

And Allah, who loves him, lets him see what is actually sitting on it. *Jasadan.* A body. A lifeless form.

[PAUSE]

Here is the mercy in it. Allah is not telling Sulayman, *"Your kingdom is worthless."* He is telling him — and you — *"Your kingdom was never alive. It never had the power to fill you. It was always a borrowed shape. The life you keep trying to get FROM the throne can only come from the One who put you on it."*

The hollowness you feel at the summit is not a malfunction. **It is information.** It is the *jasad* on the throne, showing itself. It is reality whispering that you climbed the right ladder against the wrong wall — that no created thing, however high, has *life* in it to give you, because life is not a property of thrones. Life is a loan from the Living One, *al-Ḥayy.*

And this reframes the whole panic of the hollow summit. When you reach the top of something and feel that cold emptiness, the worldly voice says: *climb higher. The next summit will be full.* That's the treadmill. That's the lie that keeps the machine running. But the verse says something else. The verse says: **the emptiness is the throne telling the truth.** Stop looking for a fuller throne. There isn't one. Sulayman had the fullest one possible and it still sat a body on it.

So what do you do tomorrow with this?

Here is the specific, practical shift. The next time you achieve something — close the deal, pass the exam, get the recognition — and you feel that strange flatness underneath the success, **do not run from it and do not chase the next thing to fix it.** Stop. Name it. Say to yourself: *this is the jasad on the throne. This is my proof that the thing I just got cannot give me life. Where, then, is the Life?* And turn — even for thirty seconds — to the One who lent you the whole climb. Let the hollowness drive you not to despair, and not to the next summit, but **back to Him.**

Which is precisely the next word of the verse.

### Theme Two: *Thumma Anāb* — Your Greatness Is Not Your Record. It's Your Direction.

Now the second human door. And this one is for everybody who has ever lain awake hating themselves for the same failure they swore they'd never repeat.

We carry a quiet, crushing theology in our bones — most of us absorbed it without ever being taught it. It goes like this: *good people don't fall. The closer you are to God, the cleaner your record. So every time I sin, every time I slip back into the thing I hate, it's proof that I'm not really one of the good ones. The relapse is the verdict.*

And that theology is a trap, because it makes every fall into a final exam you've already failed. So you spiral. The sin becomes shame, and shame whispers, *don't even turn back — what's the point, you'll just do it again* — and the shame keeps you *away* from the only door that could heal you.

Now look at the man in this verse.

This is **Sulayman.** Prophet. King. Beloved of Allah. Stamped *awwāb* — "ever-returning" — by Allah Himself, in this very passage. The standard of human excellence. And what does Allah, in nine words, choose to tell us about him?

That he was **tested.** That something went wrong enough to be called a *fitnah.* That a lifeless body sat on his throne. **And that then — he turned back.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that does to the bad theology.

If even Sulayman — *Sulayman!* — has a verse in the Quran whose engine is *test, fall, return,* then the fall was never the disqualifier. **Look at what Allah praises him for.** Not "he never erred." Allah praises him as *awwāb* — *the one who keeps coming back.* The title of honor is built *out of* the returning. Which means the returning *requires* something to return *from.*

Remember the root, *n-w-b.* Returning *turn after turn.* The honor isn't in a flawless straight line. The honor is in the **needle that keeps swinging back to north** no matter how far it gets knocked. The *awwāb* is not the man who is never knocked off course. He is the man who is **never pointed anywhere but home.**

This is the entire message of Surah Sad's gallery of prophets. Allah is consoling His Messenger ﷺ — and through him, you — and the consolation is not *"the great ones never struggled."* The consolation is: **the great ones were defined by their return.** Dawud, *awwāb.* Ayyub, *awwāb.* Sulayman, *awwāb.* The hall of fame is a hall of returners.

So here is where the bad theology dies. Your relapse is not the verdict. **Your relapse is the occasion for the one move that Allah actually honors.** The sin is not where your worth is decided. Your worth is decided by the direction you face *after* the sin. And the door — *thumma anāb* — is open after the test. There is a *thumma,* an interval, a space. You don't have to return in the first instant to return truly. You just have to **turn.**

What does this change about tomorrow?

The specific application is this. The next time you fall into the thing you hate — the sin you're ashamed of, the habit you swore off — Shaytan will hand you the spiral: *"You again. You're hopeless. Don't even bother turning back."* And in that exact moment, I want you to remember one word: **anāb.** And I want you to refuse the spiral and make the turn — *immediately,* not after you've "earned" it, not after you've punished yourself enough, not next week when you feel "clean." Right then. In the dirt of the fall, with the shame still hot, turn your face to Him.

Because the turning *is* the greatness. Not the clean record. **The compass that won't point anywhere but home.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and look at the shape of the nine words, because the structure is the proof.

> *wa-laqad* **fatannā** Sulaymāna — We **tested**
> *wa-alqaynā ʿalā kursiyyihi* **jasadan** — We laid a **body** on the throne
> *thumma* **anāb** — then he **turned back**

**Test → Emptying → Return.** Three beats. And notice the perfect symmetry of the bookends: the verse opens with an action of *Allah upon Sulayman* (*We tested... We placed*) and closes with an action of *Sulayman toward Allah* (*he turned*). Allah moves toward the servant with a trial; the servant moves back toward Allah with *inābah.* The verse is a **circuit** — divine descent meeting human return. That is the entire spiritual life, drawn in nine words.

### Bringing It Together

See now how the two halves of our journey were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **words** showed us: *jasadan* — a lifeless shell — undescribed, so that it could be every throne; and *anāb* — the habitual, compass-needle return.

The **themes** showed us: the throne is hollow because life was never its to give (that's the *jasad*); and the only thing that fills the hollow is to turn back to the Living One (that's the *anāba*).

It's one motion. **When you discover the body on your throne — that is your invitation to turn.** The emptiness of the summit and the return to Allah are not two lessons. They are the front and back of a single door. Sulayman saw the shell of his power, and *in that instant* the needle swung home. *Jasadan* — *thumma anāb.* The body on the throne was not his ruin. **It was the thing that turned him back.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I reached a summit, felt the hollowness, and mistaken it for a reason to *climb higher* — when it was really an invitation to turn back?
2. What "throne" am I asking to give me a life that only Allah can give?
3. When I fall, which theology runs my heart — *the fall is the verdict,* or *the turning is the greatness?*
4. Is my needle pointed home? Not: have I never strayed — but: when I stray, do I face anywhere else?
5. If Allah praised His beloved as *awwāb* — the returner — what does it mean that I have been treating my returns as failures instead of as the very thing He honors?

### One-Sentence Distillation

At the summit of the only kingdom of its kind, Allah let Sulayman see a lifeless body on his own throne — power emptied of the life it never owned — and the prophet's greatness was not that he never fell, but that, every time, **he turned back.**

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what might shift after tonight. Two things, and they are one thing.

When you reach the top of something and feel the cold hollowness underneath the success — don't run, and don't climb higher. **Recognize the body on the throne.** Let it tell you the truth: that no created thing has life to give you. And let that truth turn you.

And when you fall — when you fail at the very thing you swore you'd conquered — don't accept the spiral. **Make the turn.** Immediately. In the dirt. Because the compass that points home through every storm is not a lesser faith than the one that never strayed. According to Allah Himself, stamping that word over His most beloved kings — **it is the highest one.**

You don't have to be flawless. You have to be *awwāb.*

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who tested Your beloved Sulayman and then received his return — show us the body on every throne we build, before it deceives us, and let the sight of it turn us back to You and not away. Make us *awwābīn* — of those whose needle swings only home. And when we fall, You who love the returning, do not let our shame keep us from Your door; let *thumma anāb* be written over our lives as You wrote it over his.

Āmīn.
