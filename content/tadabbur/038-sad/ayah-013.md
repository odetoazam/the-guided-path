## The Ayah

**وَثَمُودُ وَقَوْمُ لُوطٍ وَأَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ ۚ أُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلْأَحْزَابُ**

*Wa Thamūdu wa qawmu Lūṭin wa aṣḥābu l-Aykati — ulā'ika l-aḥzāb*

And Thamud, and the people of Lut, and the companions of the thicket — those were the parties.

---

## Introduction: The Archive Opens

Picture the city of Makkah in the years before the hijrah.

The Prophet ﷺ has been receiving revelation for some years now. He has been calling his people. And the response from the tribal leadership — the elders of Quraysh, the men who run the trade routes and guard the Kaabah and hold the social order together — is not debate. It is not earnest disagreement. It is consolidation.

They are forming a front.

The gathering is not about theology. It is about identity, economics, inheritance, prestige. The chiefs are saying to each other: *has he made all the gods into one God? This is a strange thing indeed.* The subtext: if this spreads, everything we have built together collapses. Our unity. Our position. Our world.

And the Prophet ﷺ is watching this happen. Watching the coalition form. Watching the people he loves — his neighbors, his tribesmen, people whose children grew up alongside his children — close ranks against him.

And then Allah speaks.

"Be patient over what they say."

And then — as if reaching into the oldest possible archive — Allah opens the record. One by one, He names them.

The people of Noah, before you. Aad. Pharaoh of the stakes.

[PAUSE]

And then, in the ayah before us today — three more.

Thamud. The people of Lut. The companions of the thicket.

And then six words — seven Arabic words — that change what all of these names mean:

**أُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلْأَحْزَابُ.**

*Those were the parties.*

Here is the question I want to sit with you in today. Six civilizations. Different continents. Different eras. Different cultures. Different sins, even. Thamud killed a she-camel. Lut's people pursued what they pursued. The companions of the thicket cheated their scales. These are not the same civilization by any measure you and I would normally use.

And Allah gathers all of them — across all that difference — under a **single word.**

What is in that word that makes it the perfect verdict for all of them? And what does the third group in this list — these unnamed "companions of the thicket" — reveal about a kind of spiritual danger that does not look like danger at all?

That is where we are going.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

---

### The Word That Contains a Diagnosis

Before I show you what's in this ayah, let me teach you an Arabic word.

The word is **صَاحِب** — *sahib.*

You may know this word as "companion" or "friend." But the Arabic is doing something more precise and more profound than either of those English words captures.

The root is **صَحِبَ** — *ṣaḥiba* — which means to accompany someone. Not to encounter them. Not to know them. To accompany them — in the sustained, ongoing, habitual sense. To travel with someone. To keep company with someone so persistently that the separation between you and them begins to dissolve.

This is why the companions of the Prophet ﷺ are called **الصَّحَابَة** — the Sahabah. Not just "people who were around him." Not acquaintances. People who had accompanied him so deeply, so consistently, so completely that they were defined by that companionship. His company shaped them. The proximity changed them at the level of character. They became who they were through sustained proximity to him.

A sahib is not the person you met. A sahib is the presence you have traveled with so long that you cannot fully imagine yourself without it.

Now come back to the ayah.

**وَأَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ**

The companions of the Aykah.

الأيكة — the Aykah. Al-Jalalayn identifies it as الغيضة — the dense thicket, the lush grove. Al-Muyassar fills in the same picture from another angle, rendering the phrase as the people of "the trees and the orchards" (al-ashjār wa-l-basātīn) — not a single thicket but a whole standing canopy of cultivated abundance. A civilization of abundant trees, of prosperous land, of wealth and trade built in and around this forested, lush environment. These are the people who received the prophet Shu'ayb, peace be upon him — a people known in other passages as the people of Midian, people whose sin was commercial: they cheated the scales, gave short measure, defrauded the people they traded with.

Notice: Allah could have called them **قَوْمُ شُعَيْب** — the people of Shu'ayb, as He names others by their prophet. He does this elsewhere. But here, for this group, He chose a different naming entirely:

**أَصْحَابُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ.** The companions of the thicket.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that.

They are not merely *located near* the thicket. They are not simply *inhabitants* of a forested region. The word is **aṣḥāb** — the plural of sahib. They are the companions of the thicket. They have kept company with it in the deepest sense — so long, so completely, so habitually, that it has shaped who they are. The abundant lushness of their land is their sahib. Their identity is formed in constant proximity to it.

And Shu'ayb, peace be upon him, came to them. He did not come to take the thicket from them. He came to say: give full measure. Honest weight. There is a scale beyond your market scale. There is an accounting beyond your ledger.

And they said — and this is in Surah Al-A'raf, Surah Hud — something revealing: *"Does your prayer command you that we should leave what our fathers worshipped, or that we do what we like with our wealth?"* Your religion is yours. Our commerce is ours. These two things do not speak to each other.

[PAUSE]

That wall — between the spiritual and the material — is not built in one day. It is the product of a long companionship. When the thicket has been your sahib for long enough — when your civilization's identity, your daily rhythms, your sense of self, your children's futures are all inseparable from this abundant, lush prosperity — you begin to perceive the world only in its terms.

What the thicket makes real is real. What the thicket cannot see is theoretical.

And so when Shu'ayb comes and speaks of invisible accountability — of scales that Allah holds, of a reckoning beyond the market — his words land on a consciousness that has been shaped, by long companionship, to see only one kind of scale. The material world is their sahib. And the sahib relationship has done what long companionship always does: it has formed their perception.

Here is what invites us to consider something important: the naming "companions of the thicket" is not merely geographic. It is a spiritual diagnosis. Embedded in the name is the pathology. They did not fail because they were wealthy. They failed because the wealth had become their constant companion — and that companionship gradually, imperceptibly, colonized what they could receive.

[PAUSE — significant]

This is a remarkable thing for Allah to do: to encode the spiritual diagnosis in the name itself. Not "the cheaters of Midian." Not "the people who refused Shu'ayb." Companions of the thicket. The identity, the orientation, the root of what followed — all held in the word **aṣḥāb.**

---

### The Word at the End

Now let me teach you a second word — the word the whole list has been building toward.

**الأحزاب** — *al-aḥzāb.*

The root is **حزب** — *ḥizb.* And a ḥizb, in its Arabic semantic field, is not just any group. It is a party, a faction, a coalition — specifically one that has organized itself around a shared project. The verb **تَحَزَّبَ** means to form factions, and it carries with it the preposition *ʿalā* — against. To form a coalition *against* something. Al-Baghawi, in his tafsir of this passage, uses exactly this phrase: **الذين تحزبوا على الأنبياء** — those who formed coalitions *against* the prophets. The directionality is built into the word.

A ḥizb, in the Quranic register, is not a neutral gathering. It is an organized opposition. A coalition whose glue is shared resistance.

And notice: **ٱلْأَحْزَابُ** — with the definite article ال. Not just "parties." *The* parties. The lam of definition — ال التعريف — points to a specific, established, canonical entity. These are not one instance of a type. They are *the* representative instance. The definitive example of what this category looks like.

Now look at the grammar of the full sentence.

أُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلْأَحْزَابُ.

**أولئك** — "those." The distal demonstrative. Not هؤلاء — not "these," which would be proximate, nearby, present. **أولئك** points to something at a remove — historically distant, morally at a distance. The word itself is placing them elsewhere while the speaker points.

And then: the classification.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the sentence is — and what it is not.

This is a nominal sentence — **جملة اسمية** — a sentence with no verb. In Arabic, a nominal sentence makes an equation. It does not narrate an event. It does not say "they *became* the parties" or "they *acted as* parties" or "they *were called* parties." It says: they *are* the parties. Present tense. Definitional. The state is being asserted, not a process described. A nominal sentence in Arabic carries a permanence that a verbal sentence does not — it describes a nature, not an occurrence.

The absence of a verb is doing work.

[PAUSE — major]

Think about what Allah is doing here. He has listed six civilizations. He has named Thamud — the people who carved their homes into mountains, who killed the she-camel of Salih. He has named the people of Lut — their particular transgression famous across prophetic history. He has named the companions of the Aykah — the prosperous, thicket-dwelling merchants of Shu'ayb.

Different times. Different places. Different peoples. Different specific sins.

And He does not say: "each of them was defeated." He does not say: "Allah punished all of them." He does not narrate. He **classifies.**

**Those — were — the — Parties.**

The naming is the verdict. The classification carries the entire trajectory: the formation, the opposition, the defeat, the record in the eternal archive.

Al-Baghawi makes the move explicit. He writes: "So Allah informed that the mushrikeen of Quraysh are **a party from among these parties** (حزب من هؤلاء الأحزاب)." The contemporary audience — the Quraysh who are forming a coalition against the Prophet ﷺ right now, in Makkah, in the years this surah was revealed — they are being enrolled in this list. They are the latest chapter of the catalogue. They are not unprecedented. They are not unique. They are الأحزاب — the parties — doing what all the parties before them did.

Al-Muyassar frames it directly in the consolation register: "those armies of deniers are defeated armies, just as those before them were defeated." And it glosses الأحزاب with two verbs worth holding together — these are the nations who *taḥazzabū* upon disbelief and denial *wa-jtamaʿū ʿalayhi*: they factionalized around it and then converged upon it. First the splitting into a party, then the gathering of that party into a single front. The ahzab are already, by their very naming, announced as defeated — because the Ahzab pattern always ends the same way.

[PAUSE]

And al-Qurtubi notes something worth pausing on: there is a genuine scholarly difference in the qira'at — how the name of this group is read. Some readers (Nafi', Ibn Kathir, Ibn Amir) read **لَيْكَةَ** without the hamza and with a different voweling. Others read **الأيكة** with the hamza. The scholars have discussed whether these refer to the same community by two names, or whether something more specific is intended in each reading. What both readings agree on — what is beyond scholarly dispute — is that these are the people of Shu'ayb, and that their defining feature, whatever we call the place, was their civilization of lush, tree-dense abundance.

The forest, or the land of the forest. The thicket, or the name derived from the thicket. The prosperity is the constant.

### The Bridge

Let me name what the linguistic work has shown us before we go deeper.

The word **أصحاب** embeds a diagnosis: a civilization defined by its intimate, identity-forming companionship with material abundance — so complete that it shaped what they could see and what they could not.

The word **الأحزاب** — a nominal sentence, no verb, definitive article — issues a classification across six different peoples, six different eras, six different expressions of the same underlying orientation: they organized, collectively, against the truth their messenger brought. And the Quraysh, hearing this, were being told: you are the next chapter. The archive is still open.

Two different words. Two different kinds of observation.

Now let's ask what they are both pointing toward — not about grammar, but about reality itself.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

---

### What We Companion, We Become

Let me begin where you live.

Think for a moment — not dramatically, but honestly — about what you reach for first in the morning. Not what you intend to reach for. What your hand finds before your mind is fully awake. What occupies the background of your attention when no one is watching, when you have a spare moment, when the day goes quiet.

For many people it is a device. A feed. A particular social world.

For others it is the status of a project — the background hum of whether something at work is going well or badly.

For others it is the opinion of particular people — the constant, low-level monitoring of whether certain people think well of them.

For others it is financial state — the number, the account, the security.

None of these are, in themselves, evil. A career is not haram. Awareness of your finances is responsible. Caring what people think is human. A thicket is not haram.

But here is the question the Aykah people are asking across the centuries:

**What has become your sahib?**

Not what you have chosen consciously. But what has, through the slow accumulation of daily priority and habitual orientation and incremental investment of attention, formed itself into your constant companion — the presence that travels with you, that shapes your perception, that you can no longer quite imagine yourself without?

[PAUSE]

Because the Quranic diagnosis is specific. It is not that the Aykah people were villains. It is not that they were irredeemably corrupt. It is that prosperity had become their sahib — and the sahib relationship does something to you that no single dramatic decision does.

A sahib shapes your perception.

When you have traveled with someone — or something — long enough, their way of seeing gradually becomes your way of seeing. The sahib does not announce this. It does not happen in a moment. But over time, you find that the world looks like what your companion sees. The categories they use become your categories. The things they value register as important; the things they overlook become invisible.

Shu'ayb, peace be upon him, did not come to strip the Aykah people of their lush land. He came to add a dimension they had stopped seeing — the scale of divine accountability alongside the scale of the market. He came to say: these two things belong together.

And they said: your religion has nothing to do with our commerce.

[PAUSE]

The wall between those two words — religion, commerce — did not go up overnight. It was built brick by brick through the daily choice of companionship. The thicket was visited daily. The market was attended daily. The scale of the market was touched and measured and trusted daily. The scale of Allah — intangible, beyond the reach of prosperous hands — gradually receded from the perceptual field.

Not because they decided to reject it. But because they had made something else their sahib.

Here is what this invites us to consider about our own moment: we live inside systems designed — quite deliberately, by the standards of modern design — to become your sahib. The app is designed to be opened first. The notification is engineered to create a pull that mimics the pull of a companionship. The algorithm learns you and then reflects you back to yourself, becoming more and more fitted to your existing shapes — and thus, more and more invisible as a shaping force.

The question is not: are you addicted? That question misses the depth of the diagnosis.

The question is: what has companioned you into an inability to see certain truths?

[PAUSE — pause here, let it sit]

What would bounce off you — what would you be unable to fully receive — because a particular sahib relationship has already organized your inner world to see things a certain way?

The practical movement here is not austerity. It is not the rejection of prosperity or technology or comfort or community. The Quran does not ask the Aykah people to leave the thicket. Shu'ayb asks them to hold it honestly — to weigh it alongside what it cannot weigh itself.

The invitation is the practice of interrogating your sahibs. Not once, dramatically. But regularly, honestly, before the companionship has gone so deep that the inventory is no longer possible.

What is your thicket? Has it become your sahib? And if so — what scale has it made invisible to you?

### The Shape of Every Coalition Against Truth

Let me describe something you have seen many times.

When a new idea challenges an established order — scientific, social, cultural, spiritual — the first response is rarely pure, individual intellectual disagreement. There may be some of that. But what happens almost always, and almost immediately, is **coalition-forming.**

People who previously had little to do with each other discover their shared opposition. The economic elite and the social conservatives who might otherwise have nothing to say to each other find a common front. The academics who disagree on almost every question discover that they agree on resisting this particular intrusion. People whose interests are normally in tension suddenly find an alignment — not around something they share, but around something they both want to stop.

Social scientists have a name for this: negative partisanship. The phenomenon where group identity coheres and sustains itself more powerfully around shared opposition than around shared affirmation. The "against" becomes the glue. The coalition is held together not by what its members believe together but by what they are organized to resist together.

This is not a modern discovery. The Quran named it thousands of years ago.

**الأحزاب.**

[PAUSE]

Look again at what Allah is doing in this ayah and the verses around it.

He surveys six civilizations. He reaches across eras and geographies and cultures and specific transgressions. And He says: these all belong to one category.

Not because they committed the same sins. Not because they shared a culture. Not because they had the same social structures or the same motivations or even the same understanding of what they were refusing.

They are one category because of the **structure of their response** to truth. They organized — collectively, with social reinforcement, with group identity at stake — around the shared project of resisting what the messengers brought. The coalition was their common form.

Al-Baghawi's phrase cuts through everything: **الذين تحزبوا على الأنبياء** — those who formed factions against the prophets. The Arabic preposition *ʿalā* — against. Not around a shared belief. Not toward a common good. **Against.** The opposition was the organizing principle.

[PAUSE]

And this is where it becomes personally urgent, not merely historically interesting.

Al-Tabari, opening his comment on these verses, fixes the referent of the catalogue's first word so it cannot drift into the safely distant past. *Kadhdhabat qablahum* — "denied, before them." Before *whom*? Al-Tabari answers without hesitation: before "these mushrikūn of Quraysh, the ones who say, *has he made the gods into one God?*" — quoting back the Quraysh's own astonished words from the surah's opening (38:5). The grammar of the entire list, on al-Tabari's reading, is built to answer a question the Quraysh themselves had just asked aloud. They wanted to know who had ever heard of such a thing. The catalogue is the reply: everyone. Before you. Repeatedly.

Al-Baghawi applies it directly to the Quraysh, to the Prophet ﷺ's contemporaries: you are a party from among these parties. The catalogue is not closed. The category is live. Every generation produces its ahzab — coalitions that form, around shared opposition, against whatever divine truth that moment demands.

Notice what makes this specific and searching: the ahzab are not always the people who have sat down and formally decided to reject God. The ahzab are often people who have simply found each other around a shared resistance. The merchant class that could not afford to let the scales of accountability into their markets. The social order that could not afford to let the prophet's message destabilize its structures. The intellectual consensus that could not afford the disruption of prophetic claims.

They did not necessarily think of themselves as a coalition against truth. They thought of themselves as protectors of what they had built, defenders of what they understood, guardians of what they valued.

[PAUSE]

But the structure of the coalition — what it was organized *against*, what it reinforced in its members, what it made it socially costly to question — that was the diagnostic fact.

Here is what all the ahzab have in common across the centuries, and what invites the most honest self-examination:

The coalition is most powerful just before its collapse.

The Ahzab who besieged Medina — in Surah 33, the surah that takes this word as its name — were the largest military coalition the Arabian Peninsula had assembled. Multiple tribes, consolidated by Meccan leadership and external allies, surrounding the Muslim community with an overwhelming force. The word الأحزاب in that surah names the confederacy at the height of its apparent power.

And it was defeated.

Thamud, at the moment they killed the she-camel of Salih, were at the peak of their architectural achievement — a civilization of mountain-carved dwellings, of organized power and pride. The aykah people were prosperous and organized. Pharaoh had the most bureaucratically developed military state in the ancient world.

The coalition always feels, from the inside, like a guarantor of stability. The numbers are on its side. The social reinforcement is on its side. History — as far as it can see — is on its side.

And the Prophet ﷺ, in Makkah, watching the Quraysh coalition form around opposition to what he had brought — Allah is showing him the long view. You are watching the early stages of a pattern you already know the end of. The archive is full of the same entry.

[PAUSE — major pause, hold this]

But here is what closes the loop between the two themes — between the sahib of the thicket and the coalition of the ahzab.

The Aykah people are both.

They are the companions of the thicket — individually, each of them has made abundant prosperity their sahib, their identity companion. The lush land is what they travel with. The market scale is what they see with.

And they are الأحزاب — collectively, together, they have formed a coalition around the shared project of separating Shu'ayb's accountability from their commercial world.

These are not two separate conditions. They are the same condition at two scales.

**What begins as a personal sahib relationship with the world — gradually, through the reinforcement of community, through shared interest, through the social comfort of people who see things the same way — becomes an ahzab.**

The individual who companions the thicket finds others who companion the same thicket. Their shared vision reinforces itself. The wall between their world and the prophet's accountability becomes not just personal but communal, not just assumed but defended, not just preferred but organized.

[PAUSE]

This is the architecture the ayah reveals. And it is why the word **أولئك الأحزاب** — with no verb, a pure nominal equation — is the perfect classificatory verdict. It is not saying what they did. It is saying what they became, at the level of nature, through the slow and reinforcing work of collective companionship with the wrong things.

The naming IS the verdict.

## Closing Synthesis

---

### The Architecture

Now that the linguistic and thematic work is done, look at the structure of this catalogue as a whole.

38:12: Noah's people. Aad. Pharaoh of the stakes.
38:13: Thamud. The people of Lut. The companions of the Aykah.

Six civilizations in two verses. And the architectural move: the first group of three is named. The second group of three is named. And then, at the end of the second verse, the verdict covers **all six.**

This is not a conclusion drawn only from the three named in verse 13. **أُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ** — "those" — is pointing back across both verses. All six are gathered. And the classification falls on all of them equally.

The variety is the point. Allah is showing you: the category of الأحزاب is not limited to any one type of civilization, any one type of sin, any one era or geography. Military pride (Aad). State power (Pharaoh). Stone-carved permanence (Thamud). Sexual transgression (Lut's people). Commercial fraud built on abundance (Aykah). The substrate of the coalition differs in every case.

And notice that Pharaoh, too, arrives in this catalogue under an encoded name — ذو الأوتاد, "the one of the stakes." Al-Tabari pauses here to record that the early scholars actually differed over what the stakes were: some held that he possessed great structures of pegs and stakes upon which entertainments were staged for his amusement — a portrait of a man whose whole sense of stability rested on driven-in supports — while another well-known view holds that the stakes were instruments of cruelty, the pegs to which he fastened those he tortured. The reading is genuinely contested; what is not contested is the *method*. Here again a tyrant is summoned into the record under a name that carries its diagnosis folded inside it — exactly as the companions of the thicket were named by what had companioned them.

What does not differ is the structure: organized, collective, identity-reinforcing resistance to what the messenger brought.

And all six — the powerful and the prosperous, the militarily dominant and the commercially successful — receive the same one-word verdict.

The word absorbs all their differences and retains only what mattered.

And then the very next verse closes the file on all six at once: *in kullun illā kadhdhaba l-rusula fa-ḥaqqa ʿiqāb* — "there was not one of them but denied the messengers, so My punishment fell due." Notice the plural: not "denied *his* messenger," but denied الرُّسُل — *the messengers,* all of them. Each of these peoples historically met only the single prophet sent to them, yet the Quran charges each with rejecting the entire line. Al-Muyassar draws out why this is exact and not loose: to refuse the one messenger sent to you is to refuse the single message every messenger carried — so the denial is never merely local, never a quarrel with one man. And the verb that seals it is *ḥaqqa* — the punishment did not simply arrive; it *became due,* a settled right, the earned consequence of a verdict the word الأحزاب had already pronounced.

Ibn Kathir gathers this whole stretch under a single heading: it is a *reminder* of those who were destroyed among the nations before. And the verses that immediately follow refuse to let the reminder settle into the safety of history. They pivot, hard, to the present: *these* deniers — the ones listening now — "await only a single blast (ṣayḥa) that will have no pause, no second chance" (38:15). The archive of the ahzab was never opened as a chronicle of the dead. It was opened as a mirror held up to Makkah — and the gap between the ancient verdict and the contemporary listener is precisely as wide as a single shout.

---

### These Two Words Are One Insight

The linguistic work and the thematic depths are pointing at the same truth from two directions.

**أصحاب** (from صحب): what you have made your constant companion has shaped what you can see and receive. The intimacy of long-traveled companionship forms perception — and when what you have companioned is the material world to the exclusion of divine accountability, you become, without drama, unable to receive the prophet's message.

**الأحزاب**: when individuals who have formed these sahib relationships with the material world find each other, reinforce each other, and organize their shared resistance — the personal spiritual condition becomes a coalition. A faction. A classified entry in the eternal archive.

One diagnosis is individual. One is communal. But they are the same disease at two scales, and the Quran names both in a single ayah with devastating economy.

### Questions to Carry

These are genuine invitations to self-examination. Not rhetorical. There is no answer I will give you. These questions are for the space between here and what comes next.

**One.** What have I made my constant companion — what sahib relationship has formed, not through a conscious decision but through the slow accumulation of daily priority — and what truths has that companionship made harder for me to receive?

**Two.** Am I part of any coalition — social, professional, cultural, ideological, online — whose organizing principle is, at its core, shared opposition to something? And is that "something" possibly a truth I am enrolled in resisting?

**Three.** The Aykah people could not hear Shu'ayb's call not because they were especially wicked, but because the question "what matters most" had already been answered for them — by long companionship — before Shu'ayb arrived. What has already answered that question in me? And was it the right thing to answer it?

**Four.** The six civilizations in this catalogue are radically different from each other by every external measure. The Quran says they are one category. This means the classification does not depend on the specific content of what we are attached to or what we oppose — it depends on the structure of our orientation. Am I in the habit of comparing myself to the obviously, dramatically wrong ahzab — reassuring myself I'm not like them — while failing to ask whether I share the underlying structure?

**Five.** If Allah were to name my generation the way He named the companions of the Aykah — not by our achievements, not by our institutions, but by what we have made our sahibs — what name would be accurate?

---

### The Distillation

Six civilizations, across every difference of culture and era and specific transgression, are gathered under one word — **الأحزاب** — because what mattered at the end was not what they had built or possessed, but what they had made their companions and what they had organized themselves to resist.

---

### The Invitation

What shifts after this is not dramatic.

It does not require renouncing your career or your prosperity or your community. It does not demand austerity from the abundance of your life.

What it asks for is something quieter and more sustained: **the habit of interrogating your sahibs.**

The companions of the Aykah did not fail in one great visible moment. They failed in the small, unremarkable, daily choices to companion certain things and to hold other things at arm's length. The coalition formed gradually — one shared conversation at a time, one reinforced assumption at a time, one social cost attached to questioning at a time.

The invitation is to interrupt that formation — not dramatically, but regularly.

Ask who and what you are becoming companions with.

Ask whether any coalition you belong to has "organized against an accountability you haven't yet fully faced" as its actual, functioning glue.

Ask what name Allah would give your generation based on habitual orientation, not announced belief.

Not as self-torment. As honest accounting — the kind that, received while there is still time to adjust the scales, is mercy.

### Du'a

يا الله،

You named the companions of the Aykah by what they had made their constant companion — and in the name, You placed the diagnosis.

We ask You for the grace to be honest about our sahibs — about what we have accompanied so long, so quietly, so habitually, that it now shapes what we can and cannot see.

Protect us from the ahzab pattern — from the coalitions that form around us, softly, through shared comfort and mutual reinforcement, whose hidden organizing principle is resistance to what You are asking of us.

Make us companions of Your Book, companions of Your remembrance, companions of those who are orienting themselves toward You — so that if our names are ever gathered under a word in the eternal archive, it is a word worthy of the companionship You offered us.

آمين
