# The Shout With No Second Milking

## When Mockery Becomes the Last Disguise of Fear

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing two short verses in front of you. Listen first to the sound of them, even before the meaning.

> [ayah:38:15] وَمَا يَنظُرُ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ إِلَّا صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً مَّا لَهَا مِن فَوَاقٍ
>
> *Wa mā yanẓuru hāʾulāʾi illā ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan mā lahā min fawāq*
>
> **"And these await nothing but a single shout — for which there will be no respite, no return, no second chance."** (38:15)

> [ayah:38:16] وَقَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَا عَجِّل لَّنَا قِطَّنَا قَبْلَ يَوْمِ ٱلْحِسَابِ
>
> *Wa qālū rabbanā ʿajjil lanā qiṭṭanā qabla yawmi-l-ḥisāb*
>
> **"And they said, 'Our Lord, hasten for us our share before the Day of Account.'"** (38:16)

Two sentences. Sixteen words. And inside them, an entire portrait of the human heart in the act of refusing what it cannot quite stop believing.

### Rebuild the world

To feel where these words land, you have to stand where the first listeners stood.

Surah Sad opens in the thick of the Meccan confrontation. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling his own people — his cousins, his tribesmen, the men he grew up beside — to one God. And just a few verses before this one, they have answered him. They called him *sāḥir kadhdhāb*, "a lying sorcerer" (38:4). They asked, with a sneer, "Has he made all the gods into one God? This is an astonishing thing" (38:5). And then their leaders walked off, telling each other to stay firm, to hold the line, to give nothing away.

Then Allah does something. He reads them the roll call of the dead. Verses 12 and 13: the people of Nuh, ʿĀd, Pharaoh of the stakes, Thamūd, the people of Lūṭ, the dwellers of the thicket. Nation after nation. Every single one of them stood exactly where you are standing now, Quraysh. Every single one of them said exactly what you are saying. And every single one of them is gone — a name in a warning, dust under the trade routes you walk to Syria and Yemen.

So now the camera turns. After that long corridor of ruins, Allah turns to the living men of Mecca who are still sneering, and He asks — not of them, but *about* them, as if pointing — **so what, exactly, are these people waiting for?**

That is the moment. Verse 15 is the answer to a question hanging in the air after a tour of graveyards.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I want you to hold the question with me for the next hour.

The verse uses a verb of *seeing* to describe people who are *waiting*. It compresses the end of the entire world into a single, indefinite, almost casual-sounding noun — "a shout." And then it reaches for one of the strangest, most rural, most tactile words in the whole Qur'an to describe what that shout will *not* have — a word borrowed from the udder of a camel.

And then — this is the part that should unsettle you — these same people, having been told this, **do not fall silent. They make a joke of it.** They turn to the very Lord they are denying and say: go on then. *Hurry it up.*

Why would anyone mock the thing they are most afraid of? Why does the verse send them *looking* when they think they are in control? And what does a camel's udder have to do with the end of the world?

Stay with me. None of these are decorations. Each one is a door.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yanẓuru* — The Verb of Seeing That Means Waiting

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because if you don't have it in your hand, the verse will pass you by.

There is a root in Arabic, **naẓara (ن-ظ-ر)**, and its first and most natural meaning is *to look, to see, to direct your gaze.* It is the root behind *naẓar* — vision, sight, the act of considering something with your eyes. It is the same family of meaning that gives us *naẓariyya*, a "theory" — something arrived at by looking carefully. When you really *look* at something, weigh it, consider it, you are doing *naẓar*.

But Arabic has a second, quieter use of this same verb. To *look toward* something coming can shade into *waiting* for it. The way in English we might say, "I'm looking for the bus," when really we mean we are standing there waiting for it. The eye that is fixed on the horizon, watching for what arrives — that watching becomes waiting.

Now open the verse.

> **وَمَا يَنظُرُ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ**
>
> *Wa mā yanẓuru hāʾulāʾ*

Every one of the classical commentators — al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi — stops here and says the same thing in the same breath: **يَنظُرُ here means يَنتَظِرُ — they are not "looking," they are "awaiting."** Al-Qurtubi says it plainly: *yanẓuru bi-maʿnā yantaẓir.* "These" — *hāʾulāʾ* — he tells us, are the disbelievers of Mecca. They await nothing now but the shout.

So at the surface, this is simple. "These people are waiting for nothing but a single shout."

But let's sit with the *choice* of word, because Allah did not have to use *naẓara*. There is a perfectly ordinary, unambiguous verb for waiting — *intaẓara* — and indeed the commentators have to reach for it to explain this one. So why does Allah reach instead for the verb of *seeing*?

Notice something. These are the people who, a few verses earlier, would not look. They would not do *naẓar* — they would not consider, would not weigh the evidence, would not turn their gaze on the ruins of ʿĀd and Thamūd and let it land. They closed their eyes to the only kind of looking that could have saved them: reflection.

And here is the quiet justice in the word. The people who refused to *look* are now described with the verb of looking — but emptied of all its power. Their *naẓar* has collapsed into mere *waiting*. They are not surveying their situation like masters. They are not the ones deciding, plotting, holding the line, as they imagined back in verse 6. Grammatically, they have been turned into people who simply *stand there* while something approaches them.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. There are two kinds of *naẓar* a person can do in this life. There is the *naẓar* of reflection — the active, searching gaze that looks at the signs and reasons toward the Maker. And there is the *naẓar* of the condemned — the passive stare of someone who has run out of moves and is simply watching the clock. **Every human being is doing one of these two.** You are either looking *at* the signs, or you are merely waiting *for* the shout. There is no third posture. The man who will not reflect has not escaped *naẓar* — he has only been demoted to its emptiest form.

And notice the grammar of the sentence: **وَمَا يَنظُرُ ... إِلَّا** — "they await *nothing except*." This is the Arabic structure of *ḥaṣr*, of restriction, of total confinement. *Mā ... illā* — "not ... except" — slams every other door. It is the same construction as *lā ilāha illā Allah*: nothing, nothing, nothing — except this one thing. So the verse is not saying the shout is *among* the things they await. It is saying it is the *only* thing left on their horizon. Every other future they imagine — their trade, their alliances, their standing, their plans to outlast this preacher — all of it is struck through. Behind every plan, one event is waiting. And it is the only real one.

What this single verb reveals: **the refusal to reflect does not free you from the future; it only blinds you to the single event that is actually coming.**

### Element Two: *Fawāq* — The Word Borrowed From a Camel's Udder

Now we come to the strangest word in these two verses, and I want to earn it slowly, because if I just tell you the meaning you will nod and move on and lose everything.

First, the structure. The shout is described as **صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً** — *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan*, "a single shout." Hear the word *ṣayḥah* itself: the hiss of the *ṣ*, the open *ay*, the scraped guttural *ḥ* at the back of the throat. It is a word that *sounds* like a scream tearing the air. And it is **indefinite** — *ṣayḥah*, "a shout," not "the shout." There is something terrible in that indefiniteness. The thing that ends the world is left grammatically small, almost offhand — *a* shout, *one* of them. The commentators are unanimous: this is *nafkhat al-qiyāmah*, the blast on the Trumpet, the cry that ends the age. And it is **wāḥidah** — one. Just one. Not a campaign, not a siege, not a war. One sound. The contrast with all of human striving could not be sharper: against every army Mecca could raise, against all its gods and gold, the answer is *a single shout* — and that is already more than enough.

Now the word I have been promising you.

> **مَّا لَهَا مِن فَوَاقٍ**
>
> *mā lahā min fawāq*

"It has no *fawāq*."

What is *fawāq*? Here is where you must let the Arabs take you out to the desert, to the side of a she-camel at milking time.

The root **fāq (ف-و-ق)** carries the sense of *return, recovery, the interval in which something comes back.* And the classical lexicons fix the concrete image precisely: *al-fawāq* is **the interval between two milkings of a she-camel** — the span of time you must wait, after you have drawn the milk, for the udder to fill again so that you can draw a second time. The camel is milked; the milk *returns*; you milk again. That gap, that pause of refilling, that little window of recovery — *that* is *fawāq*.

Sit with how earthy this is. Allah, describing the cosmic and the unseen — the blast that ends creation — reaches for the most ordinary, daily, rural experience His listeners had: standing at the flank of a camel, waiting for the milk to come back.

Why? Because *fawāq* names a precise idea that abstract language struggles to hold: **the in-between time. The pause that contains the promise of a second chance.** The whole of a herdsman's patience lives in that word — the confidence that if you wait, the udder fills, and you get to draw again.

And the verse says: **this shout has no fawāq.** *Mā lahā min fawāq.* Notice the *min* — "not for it *any* fawāq at all." It is the *min* of total negation, the same construction that sweeps the category to zero. Not a little. Not a moment's worth. None.

Now watch the commentators circle this word like men describing the same mountain from different valleys — and notice that they are not disagreeing, they are *building*:

- **Ibn ʿAbbas and Qatāda** said *fawāq* here means **rujūʿ** — *return.* The shout, once it comes, does not come back, is not turned away; there is no returning from it. Al-Baghawi records this: the sound will not be repeated and there will be no return from it.
- **Mujāhid** said it means **naẓra** — *a glance, a respite,* a small look's worth of delay. Not even the time it takes to glance up.
- **al-Ḍaḥḥāk** said **mathnawiyya** — a *turning-back, a reversal,* something that could be undone.

Lay them side by side and you do not get three competing definitions. You get one reality seen from three angles. The shout that has no *fawāq* is the event that has **no second milking** — no interval in which mercy refills, no pause in which you regather yourself, no return, no reversal, no respite, no glance's worth of delay, no doing-it-again. It comes once. It is complete. It does not refill.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing I most want you to feel. Al-Baghawi even pauses to note that the word is read two ways — *fawāq* with a *fatḥa* (the dialect of Quraysh) and *fuwāq* with a *ḍamma* (the dialect of Tamīm) — *two readings, one meaning.* Even the tribes who could not agree on a vowel agreed on the terror underneath it.

Now lift the image to where it points. **Your entire life — this whole world — is the fawāq.** This dunya *is* the interval between milkings. It is the pause in which the udder refills, the window in which return is possible, the time when you can still draw again from the well of mercy, still turn back, still recover, still glance up and change. *That* is what this life is: one long *fawāq*, one great in-between, granted to you so that you can come back.

And the shout ends the *fawāq* forever. When it comes, the interval is over. There is no more refilling. The udder of mercy — if you will let me keep the image — is drawn dry at last, not because Allah's mercy ran out, but because the *time* in which it could be drawn has closed.

So the verse is not, in the end, about the shout. The shout is the stepping stone. **The verse is about the interval you are standing in right now.** It is whispering: *you think you are waiting for the shout. You are wrong. You are living inside the fawāq, and the fawāq is the gift, and the gift is running.*

[PAUSE]

What this one rural word reveals: **the mercy of this life is not that punishment is far — it is that there is still an interval, a refilling-time, a chance to draw again. The catastrophe of the shout is precisely that it is the end of the interval.**

### Element Three: *ʿAjjil … Qiṭṭanā* — The Grammar of Mockery

Now to verse 16, where the same people open their mouths — and what comes out is the most psychologically revealing thing in the passage.

> **وَقَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَا عَجِّل لَّنَا قِطَّنَا**
>
> *Wa qālū rabbanā ʿajjil lanā qiṭṭanā*

Two words deserve your attention.

First, **عَجِّل** — *ʿajjil*. This is the imperative of a Form II verb, from the root *ʿajila*, "to hasten, to be quick." And Form II — the doubled middle root, *faʿʿala* — characteristically *intensifies* or *causes* the action. The plain verb means "to hurry"; the Form II *ʿajjala* means "to make something come quickly, to speed it up, to rush it forward." So they are not saying "let it come." They are saying, with their hands open in mock-supplication, **"speed it up for us. Bring it forward. Rush it."**

Second, **قِطَّنَا** — *qiṭṭanā*, "our *qiṭṭ*." This word repays slow attention. The root **qaṭṭa (ق-ط-ط)** means *to cut.* A *qiṭṭ* is, at root, a *portion that has been cut off* — and from there, because in the old world documents were written on cut pieces of parchment, it came also to mean a *written allotment, a decree, a record, a share assigned to you.* You can almost hear the meaning in the sound of it: the doubled emphatic *ṭ*, *qiṭṭ* — clipped, abrupt, cut off short, a syllable that stops like a blade coming down. The very phonetics of the word enact a *portion sliced off.*

So what are they asking for? Classical commentators report the *qiṭṭ* here in two main senses, and again they layer rather than contradict: it is **naṣīb** — your *share, your allotment* — specifically their share of the punishment they had been threatened with; or it is, as the same root carries elsewhere, the *record, the written account* of one's deeds, the page that will be handed out on the Day. Either way the meaning bites: **"hasten our portion — our cut, our sentence, our page — bring it now."**

And then the tail of the sentence, which is the key that unlocks the whole mood:

> **قَبْلَ يَوْمِ ٱلْحِسَابِ** — *qabla yawmi-l-ḥisāb* — **"before the Day of Account."**

Now put it all together and hear the tone. They are saying: *Our Lord — speed up our share — before the Day of Reckoning even arrives.*

[PAUSE]

This is not a prayer. This is a sneer dressed as a prayer.

Think about what they are actually doing. The Prophet ﷺ has warned them of a Day of *Ḥisāb*, a Day of Account. Their response is to *mock the very idea* by demanding their portion *in advance.* "You say there's a reckoning coming? Fine — bring our cut of it *now,* before your so-called Day. We dare you. We can't even wait." It is the swagger of a man who taunts a doctor's diagnosis by lighting another cigarette in the waiting room. It is dark, defiant comedy. They are using the language of supplication — even calling Him *rabbanā,* "our Lord" — as a weapon of ridicule.

And here is a detail you must not miss, because the absence and the presence are both speaking. **They call Him "our Lord."** *Rabbanā.* Even in the act of mockery, even while denying His Day, the word that leaves their mouths is *Rabb* — Master, Sustainer, Owner. They cannot fully shake it. The tongue confesses what the heart is straining to deny. You do not say "*our* Lord" to something you have genuinely concluded does not exist. The mockery itself is built on the foundation it claims to be demolishing.

What this construction reveals: **mockery is not the opposite of fear. Mockery is one of the costumes fear wears when it cannot afford to be seen.**

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Yanẓuru* told us these people are not the masters of their fate they imagine — they have been reduced from those who *look* to those who merely *await.*

*Fawāq* told us that what they are awaiting is the end of the one thing that matters most: the interval, the refilling-time, the window of return that this whole life *is.*

And *ʿajjil qiṭṭanā* told us how they respond to that knowledge — not with reflection, not with terror that turns to repentance, but with a joke, a dare, a sneer aimed at the Day itself.

The words have shown us the *anatomy* of a particular human posture. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about seventh-century Mecca — but about the heart, including yours and mine, right now.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: We Are All Living Inside the Fawāq

Let me start not with the verse, but with something you already know in your body.

You have had the experience of an interval that you treated as if it were forever. The semester before the exam, when the exam was "ages away," until suddenly it was Sunday night. The years of your twenties when your parents seemed permanent fixtures, until one day you are holding a phone and someone is saying *you should come home now.* The free afternoon you meant to use well, that dissolved into nothing, and then it was dark. We are *experts* at mistaking the interval for the permanence.

There is a reason for this, and modern psychology has a clumsy name for it: we discount the future. The mind treats the present as solid and real and the future as a thin, theoretical rumor — even when the future is *certain.* We will pay more to avoid a small pain now than a large pain later. We will trade a future we are sure of for a comfort we can feel. The herdsman's word *fawāq* names exactly the gap our minds are built to misjudge: the interval between now and the next reckoning, the refilling-time we treat as if it had no end.

Now let the verse illuminate this.

The Qur'an does not say to the people of Mecca, "Beware the shout because it is far." It says, in effect, "You are *inside the fawāq* and you do not know it." The whole drama of the verse is not the shout — the shout gets a single indefinite word, *ṣayḥah.* The drama is the *interval* that has been quietly handed to them and that they are spending on mockery.

Stay with this, because it reorganizes everything. We tend to think of this life as the *real* thing and death as the interruption. The verse inverts it. **This life is the interval. This life is the fawāq — the pause for refilling, the window of return.** Death and what follows are not the interruption of reality; they are reality, and this is the merciful pause before it.

And that reframes mercy itself. We usually imagine Allah's mercy as the *absence* of punishment — "He hasn't punished me, so He is being merciful." But the verse points at a deeper mercy: the mercy is not that the shout is far. The mercy is that **there is still an interval at all.** Every breath you draw right now is the udder refilling. Every morning you wake still able to turn back is *fawāq* — recovery-time, return-time, draw-again-time. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah stretches out His hand by night so that the sinner of the day may repent, and stretches it out by day so the sinner of the night may repent — until the sun rises from the west. That is *fawāq* described in motion: an interval deliberately held open so that return remains possible.

[PAUSE]

So let me ask you, through a human door. When was the last time you treated an ordinary, uneventful day as a *gift of interval* — as borrowed refilling-time you had been granted to come back, to draw again from mercy? Or do your ordinary days feel like the permanent ground of reality, with "the reckoning" filed away as a rumor about the far future?

Here is the quiet terror and the quiet invitation of the word, held together: the shout has *no* fawāq — but you, right now, *do.* You are standing in the interval the disbelievers wasted. The fact that you are reading these words, that you can still feel the pull of return, that the udder is still refilling — that *is* the mercy. It is not promised to refill tomorrow.

What this changes about tomorrow: stop waiting for a "sign" or a crisis to begin returning to Allah. **The uneventful day is the sign.** The fact that nothing has happened yet *is* the fawāq, and it is the only one you are guaranteed. The practical application is almost embarrassingly simple: treat one ordinary day this week — not a Ramadan, not a calamity, just a Tuesday — as explicitly borrowed interval. Pray one prayer in it as if it were the last draw from the udder before the shout. Because for someone, somewhere, today, it is.

### Theme Two: Mockery Is the Costume Fear Wears

Now the harder, more intimate theme — because verse 16 is not really about the men of Mecca. It is about a move the human heart makes when it is cornered, and we all make it.

Begin where you live. Think about how people respond to a truth that frightens them and that they are not ready to face. They rarely argue it carefully. More often, they *laugh at it.* The diagnosis comes, and the person makes a joke about the gown the hospital gives you. The marriage is failing, and one spouse turns everything into sarcasm. The deepest fears in a friend group are precisely the things that get the most jokes — death, failure, loneliness, judgment. We do not mock what we are indifferent to. *Nobody mocks the existence of unicorns.* We mock what has gotten close enough to threaten us.

Psychologists call this a defense — gallows humor, dismissal, contempt — and its function is protective. Mockery puts distance between you and a truth you cannot afford to let near. If I can *laugh* at the reckoning, then I have made it small, and if it is small, it cannot crush me. The sneer is a wall thrown up in a hurry.

Now hear verse 16 with new ears.

> **رَبَّنَا عَجِّل لَّنَا قِطَّنَا قَبْلَ يَوْمِ ٱلْحِسَابِ**
>
> "Our Lord, *rush* our portion to us, before the Day of Account."

This is the gallows humor of Mecca. They have just been told of a Day of *Ḥisāb,* a reckoning with no second milking. And they cannot bear the weight of taking it seriously, so they do the cornered heart's oldest trick: they make it a dare. *"Bring it now. Speed it up. We can't even wait for your Day."* They shrink the infinite down to a punchline, because a punchline cannot sentence you.

But here is the movement, the thing the verse exposes that the mockers themselves could not see.

**They called Him *Rabbanā.*** *Our Lord.* You cannot mock the reckoning without addressing the Judge. The joke requires the courtroom to exist. Their sarcasm is parasitic on the very reality it is trying to kill. And this is the tell — this is how you know the mockery is fear and not conviction. A man who has genuinely concluded there is no Day does not stand and *demand* his portion of it. He simply forgets it. He goes home. The *energy* of the mockery, the *demand,* the *dare* — all of it betrays a heart that has not actually managed to disbelieve. It has only managed to *not face* what it half-believes. The mockery is the proof of the fear.

[PAUSE]

Let this sit, because it cuts close. The loud rejection of God in our own time so often has exactly this texture. Not calm, settled indifference, but heat. Demand. The dare flung at the sky — *if You're real, do something.* The mockery that returns, again and again, to the very thing it claims is nothing. You do not spend that much energy on nothing. *The intensity is the confession.* As one observer of the human heart put it, the opposite of faith is not doubt — doubt can be the most honest form of seeking. The opposite of faith is the *contempt* that refuses to look, because looking is the one thing it cannot survive.

And now turn the blade gently inward, because this is not only about the loud atheist. The same move lives in believers, quieter, in a different costume. We do it whenever we keep a truth at arm's length by making it light. The reminder of death that we deflect with "morbid much?" The brother who speaks sincerely about the Hereafter and we ease the discomfort with a joke. The pull toward prayer at 3am that we talk ourselves out of with a wry "I'm not that guy." Each of these is a tiny *ʿajjil qiṭṭanā* — a small sneer that buys us distance from a truth getting uncomfortably close. We are rarely Pharaohs. But we are often, in small ways, men keeping the reckoning at a comfortable distance with a smirk.

What this changes about tomorrow: when you next notice yourself *deflecting* a spiritual truth with humor, dismissiveness, or "later" — stop and ask the diagnostic question this verse hands you. *Am I dismissing this because it is false, or because it is true and too close?* The contempt is a signal, not a conclusion. The thing you are most tempted to wave away with a joke may be precisely the thing your heart already knows it must face. The practical step is small and brutal: the next time a reminder of the Hereafter makes you reach for a deflection, do not reach. Sit in the discomfort for sixty seconds without softening it. That discomfort is your *fawāq* doing its work — the interval is still trying to bring you back.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two verses are built, now that we have lived inside them.

Verse 15 is Allah's voice, describing reality from above: *these people await nothing but a single shout, an event with no second milking, no interval, no return.* It is the verse of **truth.**

Verse 16 is their voice, captured and replayed: *rush our portion, before the Day of Account.* It is the verse of **response.**

And the two fit together like a lock and its key turned the wrong way. Verse 15 tells them the interval is closing. Verse 16 shows them spending the interval on a sneer at its closing. The very breath they use to mock the reckoning is a draught of the *fawāq* they are mocking. **They are using the gift to deny the Giver.** They are spending the refilling-time ridiculing the One who is refilling the udder.

The linguistic journey and the themes turn out to be the same insight seen from two sides:

- The word *fawāq* showed us, from the side of *grammar,* that this life is an interval of return that the shout will end.
- The theme of mockery showed us, from the side of the *heart,* what we do with that interval when we cannot face its meaning: we make it small with a joke.

One discovery. *You are living in the interval, and the way you treat its closing reveals everything about you.* Some, like the men of Mecca, treat it with a sneer. The invitation of these verses is to treat it, instead, as what it is: borrowed time, held open by mercy, for the one purpose of return.

### Questions to Carry

1. If this ordinary day were named honestly — not "an ordinary day" but "an interval of mercy granted so I can return" — what would I do differently in the next twelve hours?
2. What spiritual truth do I most reliably deflect with a joke, a "later," or a change of subject? And is it because it is false, or because it is true and close?
3. When I imagine "the reckoning," do I file it as a far-off rumor, or do I feel that I am already *inside the fawāq* before it?
4. The men of Mecca still said *Rabbanā* — "our Lord" — even while mocking. Where in my own life does my tongue or my habit still confess a truth that the rest of me is avoiding?
5. If the shout has *no* second milking, and this life *is* the milking-time — what am I drawing from the udder of these days? Mercy and return, or distraction and delay?

### One-Sentence Distillation

These people are not the masters they imagine but mere waiters for a single shout that ends forever the interval of return this life *is* — and rather than face it, they spend that very interval mocking its arrival, because mockery is the last disguise of a fear too large to look at.

### The Closing Invitation

I will not tell you that you must fear the shout. The Qur'an has already placed it in front of you; you have heard it. What I will invite you toward is something gentler and more urgent than fear.

You are in the *fawāq.* Right now. The udder is refilling. The interval is open. That is not a threat — it is the single most hopeful fact about your situation, because the people in verse 15 had exactly the same interval, and the only thing they did wrong was fail to recognize it for what it was.

So let the next reminder that comes close not be met with a deflection. Let the next ordinary day not be spent as if it were permanent ground. Draw from the udder while it fills. That is all repentance is — coming back to draw again, in the interval that was opened for exactly that.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who held open the interval when You could have let the shout fall — let me not be among those who spend the *fawāq* mocking its closing. Make my ordinary days into return-time, and let my tongue confess You in worship before it can ever confess You in fear. And when the single shout comes that has no second milking, let me have already drawn deeply from Your mercy, in the days when the drawing was still possible. *Āmīn.*
