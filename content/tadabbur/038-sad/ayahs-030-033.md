# What an Excellent Servant

## Introduction: The Largest Kingdom Ever Given to a Human Being

Let me set the scene before we open a single word.

There has never been, and according to the Quran's own testimony there will never again be, a kingdom like the one this man held. He asked his Lord for a dominion that would not befit anyone after him — *mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī* — and it was granted. The wind ran beneath his command. The language of birds was opened to him. Armies of jinn and men and birds were marshalled into ranks. Molten brass flowed for him like a spring. He spoke, and the world rearranged itself.

If you wanted to build the human being least likely to remain humble — give him all of that.

Now hold that picture, because here is what Allah chooses to say about him. Not "what a king." Not "what a conqueror." Not even "what a prophet." When the curtain rises on Sulayman in Surah Sad, the very first thing Allah says about the most powerful human being who ever lived is four small words:

**نِعْمَ ٱلْعَبْدُ** — *niʿma al-ʿabd* — "What an excellent **servant**."

[PAUSE]

And then we are shown a scene. It is late afternoon. Magnificent war-horses — bred, trained, poised — are paraded before him. And something happens in his heart as he watches them. He loves them. He loves them so much that, by his own confession, the time slips. The sun sinks behind its veil. And then he says three words — *ruddūhā ʿalayya*, "bring them back to me" — and does something to those horses that scholars have debated for fourteen centuries.

Here is the question I want you to carry into this reflection. Why, at the absolute summit of human power, does Allah hand Sulayman the title of a *slave*? And what is it about this strange, intimate scene with the horses that earns him — in the very same breath — the description *innahu awwāb*, "he was ever-returning"?

Stay with me. The answer reorganizes what we think greatness is.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: وَوَهَبْنَا — The Verb That Tells You Nothing Was Earned

Let me teach you something about how Arabic gives things before we look at how Allah gives Sulayman.

There are several verbs in Arabic for transferring something from one hand to another. There is *aʿṭā* — to give, neutrally. There is *manaḥa* — to grant. And there is the root we meet here: **و-ه-ب**, *wahaba*. This is the root of *hiba* — a gift in the purest sense. A *hiba* in the language, and later in Islamic law, is a transfer of ownership with no return expected, no price attached, no debt incurred. It is the one kind of giving that the receiver did absolutely nothing to deserve. You cannot earn a *hiba*. The moment you earn it, it stops being one.

Now hear the verse:

**وَوَهَبْنَا لِدَاوُۥدَ سُلَيْمَٰنَ** — "And **We gifted** to Dawud, Sulayman."

Notice what is the gift. It is not the kingdom — that comes later in the passage. The first gift named is **a son**. Sulayman himself is the *hiba*. Before he is a king, before he commands the wind, before he is anything the world would recognize as powerful, he is introduced to us as something *given* — freely, gratuitously, into the open hands of his father.

Let's sit with this, because there is a quiet correction folded into the grammar.

The world's instinct is to look at a man like Sulayman and say: *look what he achieved.* The verb *wahabnā* says: look what he was **handed**. The wind was handed. The dominion was handed. The wisdom was handed. The very *existence* of the man — his being a son to Dawud at all — was handed. There is not a single rung on the entire ladder of Sulayman's greatness that he climbed by his own manufacture. Every rung was set under his foot by Another.

[PAUSE]

And here is why that matters for everything that follows. A man who knows he is a *hiba* — a man who knows that what he holds was placed in his hand and not generated by it — that man can let go of it without shattering. Because it was never his in the foundational sense. The horses, in a few verses, will test exactly this. Can he hold something beautiful with the open hand of one who received it, or will his fingers close around it?

The verb has already told us. He is a man who knows what it is to receive a gift. Watch how he gives one back.

## Element Two: نِعْمَ ٱلْعَبْدُ — The Verb of Praise and the Title Withheld

Now I want to teach you a small, beautiful piece of Arabic grammar, because the entire weight of this passage hangs on it.

Arabic has a special category of verb whose only job is to praise. The chief of them is **نِعْمَ**, *niʿma*. It is not like a normal verb. It is what the grammarians call a *fiʿl jāmid* — a "frozen" verb, locked into one form, that exists for a single purpose: to issue the highest grade of commendation. When you say *niʿma X*, you are not narrating an event. You are pronouncing a verdict. It is the linguistic equivalent of a gavel coming down: *excellent. Approved. The finest of its kind.*

And the structure has three parts. The verb of praise, *niʿma*. Then the category being praised — here, **ٱلْعَبْدُ**, "the servant." And then, crucially, the specific person being praised — *al-makhṣūṣ bi-l-madḥ* — which in this verse is left **unspoken**, hovering just off the page, understood by everyone to be Sulayman.

So the construction is: *niʿma al-ʿabd* — "What an excellent **servant** [is he]."

Now read it the way the grammar makes you read it. Allah is bringing down the gavel of supreme praise. And the category He files Sulayman under — the box He ticks — is **al-ʿabd**, the slave.

[PAUSE]

This is where I want you to read the **absence**, because the absence is louder than the presence.

Think of every category Allah could truthfully have filed him under. *Niʿma al-malik* — what an excellent king. True. *Niʿma al-nabī* — what an excellent prophet. True. *Niʿma al-ḥākim* — what an excellent ruler, *niʿma al-mujāhid*, *niʿma al-ḥakīm* — every one of them factually accurate for Sulayman. Allah passes over all of them. The single category selected, out of all the true ones available, is the lowest-status word in the human vocabulary: **the slave.**

Here's what is remarkable. In human ranking, "servant" sits at the bottom and "king" sits at the top. The whole architecture of worldly value is built on getting *away* from servanthood and *toward* mastery. And Allah takes the man who reached the absolute ceiling of mastery — more mastery than any human before or since — and praises him by pointing at the floor. By naming the one thing about him that has nothing to do with his crown.

Why? Because in the economy of the heavens the ranking is **inverted**. *ʿAbd* is not the bottom. *ʿAbd* is the summit. The highest a creature can rise is to become, fully and without remainder, the servant of his Lord — which is exactly why, when Allah praised His Messenger Muhammad ﷺ at the two highest moments of his life, the night of the Miʿrāj and the giving of revelation, He called him *ʿabdihi* — "His servant." Not His prophet in that instant. His servant.

So *niʿma al-ʿabd* is not Allah being modest about Sulayman. It is Allah telling you where the real altitude is. The kingdom was the scaffolding. The servanthood was the building.

And notice one more thing the grammar does. The classical commentators read *innahu awwāb* — "indeed he was ever-returning" — as the **reason** the title was earned. As al-Qurṭubī puts it plainly, *awwāb* here means *muṭīʿ*, obedient; and al-Muyassar glosses *niʿma al-ʿabd* as *Sulaymān*, with the praise grounded in his constant turning back to Allah. The excellence of the servant is not measured by the size of his kingdom. It is measured by the frequency of his return. Which brings us to the third word.

## Element Three: أَوَّابٌ — The Form That Means He Did It Again, and Again, and Again

Hold the word in front of you: **أَوَّابٌ**, *awwāb*.

The root is **أ-و-ب**, *awb*, and it means simply: *to return, to come back.* From it comes *iyāb* — the return journey, the coming-home. But Allah does not say Sulayman *āba* — "returned." He does not even use the ordinary intensive. He uses one of the most emphatic patterns the Arabic language possesses.

Let me teach you the pattern, because it is doing all the work.

Arabic has a family of forms called **صيغ المبالغة** — the forms of hyperbole, of intensity. When you want to say not just that someone does an action but that the action has become *who they are* — that they do it constantly, habitually, to a degree that defines them — you pour the verb into one of these moulds. One of the most powerful is the pattern **فَعَّال**, *faʿʿāl*, with that doubled, drumming middle letter. *Ghaffār* — not one who forgives, but the One whose very nature is endless forgiveness. *Tawwāb* — the One who turns to His servants over and over without exhaustion. And here, on the human side: ***awwāb*** — one who returns, and returns, and returns.

The doubled letter is not decoration. In Arabic the doubling of a consonant carries the doubling of meaning — repetition, intensity, recurrence built into the very sound of the word. You can *hear* the returning in it. *Aw-wāb.* The tongue goes back over the same point.

This is precisely why al-Jalālayn glosses it as *rajjāʿ* — itself an intensive form — *"one who returns constantly, in glorification and remembrance, at all times."* And al-Muyassar: *kathīr al-rujūʿ ilā Allāh wa-l-ināba ilayh* — "abundant in his returning to Allah and his turning back to Him."

Now here is what I need you to *feel* about this word, because it is one of the most merciful diagnoses in the entire Quran.

*Awwāb* does not mean "one who never left." [PAUSE] Think about that. You do not *return* to a place you never departed from. The word contains, built into its own structure, the assumption of distance. To be *awwāb* you must have, at some point, drifted — even slightly. The horse passage is about to show us exactly that drift: a beloved thing pulls his attention, the afternoon slides, the sun goes down. *Awwāb* is not the title of a man who is incapable of being distracted. It is the title of a man who, the instant he notices the distance, **closes it.** Again. And again. And again.

[PAUSE]

Do you understand what this means for you? The praiseworthy servant in the Quran is not the one who never wanders. There is no such human being. The praiseworthy servant is the one who has made *returning* into a reflex — so practised, so habitual, that the gap between drifting and coming back has shrunk to almost nothing. That is the *faʿʿāl* pattern living in a human heart. Not "I never fall." But "I have fallen and risen so many times that rising has become the most natural motion of my soul."

And now watch — because the surah is about to make this word into the spine of everything.

### The Bridge

Three words. *Wahabnā* told us nothing was earned; it was all received. *Niʿma al-ʿabd* told us that the title which outranks "king" is "servant." *Awwāb* told us that the mark of the excellent servant is not flawlessness but the reflex of return.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about a human heart standing in front of something it loves.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Afternoon the Time Slipped Away

Let me start where you live, not where Sulayman lived.

You sat down to do one good thing — and you looked up and an hour was gone. You opened your phone to check one message and the evening dissolved. You began admiring something genuinely good — a project, a passion, a beautiful thing you'd worked for — and somewhere in the admiring, the *aṣr* came and went, the moment of remembrance you'd meant to keep just... passed, behind the veil of your own absorption.

That is not a small modern problem. That is *the* problem of the loving heart. And the Quran sets it, of all places, inside the experience of a *prophet*.

Read the scene again:

**إِذْ عُرِضَ عَلَيْهِ بِٱلْعَشِىِّ ٱلصَّٰفِنَٰتُ ٱلْجِيَادُ** — "When, in the late afternoon, the noble standing steeds were displayed before him."

Look at the horses themselves, because the language adores them. **ٱلصَّٰفِنَٰت** — *al-ṣāfināt* — are not just horses. A *ṣāfin* is a horse so finely bred and so disciplined that when it stands at rest it poises on three legs and lifts the very tip of the fourth hoof to the ground — a posture of contained, electric readiness. And **ٱلْجِيَاد** — *al-jiyād* — are the swift, the superb, the word sharing its very root-family with *jawād*: excellence, generosity, nobility. These are not horses you glance at. These are horses that *stop the breath.* And they are paraded *bi-l-ʿashiyy*, in the golden, slanting light of late afternoon — the most beautiful hour to watch a beautiful thing.

And these horses are not a sin. This is the thing we must understand before we go a step further. They were instruments of *jihād fī sabīlillāh* — the cavalry of a kingdom raised for the cause of God. Loving them was loving an instrument of God's path. That is why Sulayman calls them *al-khayr* — **the good.** *Aḥbabtu ḥubba l-khayr* — "I have loved the love of *good things.*" He is not confessing to loving something forbidden. He is confessing to something far more searching: he loved something *genuinely good* so intensely that, for a span, it stood between him and the One who gave it.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how much closer to home that is? It is easy to police the love of obvious evil. The harder, lifelong work is watching the love of *good* things — your family, your work, your knowledge, your service, your halal beautiful life — and noticing the exact moment when the gift begins to eclipse the Giver. When *al-khayr* begins to crowd out *dhikr rabbī*.

And here we reach the most debated phrase in the passage, and I will be honest with you about the debate rather than flatten it, because the scholars genuinely differed and that difference is itself instructive.

**فَقَالَ إِنِّىٓ أَحْبَبْتُ حُبَّ ٱلْخَيْرِ عَن ذِكْرِ رَبِّى حَتَّىٰ تَوَارَتْ بِٱلْحِجَابِ**

There is a first reading, carried by many of the classical commentators, in which *ʿan dhikri rabbī* means "*away from* the remembrance of my Lord" — that Sulayman, absorbed in reviewing his cavalry, let the time of a devotion slip until *tawārat bi-l-ḥijāb* — "it disappeared behind the veil," the sun setting behind the horizon. On this reading the verse is a moment of a prophet catching himself.

And there is a second reading, also classical, in which *ʿan dhikri rabbī* means his love for these horses *arose out of* the remembrance of his Lord — that he loved them precisely *because* they served God's cause — and *tawārat bi-l-ḥijāb* describes the horses themselves galloping until they vanished from sight on the review-ground.

Here I want to be careful, because this touches the *ʿiṣma*, the divinely-protected integrity, of a prophet. The scholars who held the first reading did **not** mean Sulayman committed a sin. Many held that no obligatory prayer was lost at all — that the "remembrance" was a specific litany or a voluntary devotion of that hour, and that the lesson lies not in a transgression but in a prophet's exquisite sensitivity to the *faintest* drift of his heart. What for you and me would not even register as a lapse, a prophet treats as worthy of decisive correction. That is not weakness in him. That is the calibration of a heart so attuned to God that a hair's-breadth of distance feels like a chasm.

And *that* is the lesson that survives every reading: **he noticed.** Whatever exactly slipped, he caught it. The sun went behind the veil and instead of letting the moment pass with a shrug, he names it out loud — *innī aḥbabtu*, "I — *I* have loved" — taking it fully upon himself. No excuse. No blaming the horses. No blaming the hour. Just clear-eyed ownership of his own heart's motion.

This — *this* — is *awwāb* in action. Not a man who never drifts. A man who, the instant he feels the pull, turns the whole apparatus of his will around to face God again.

### The Response — and What He Was Willing to Cut

**رُدُّوهَا عَلَىَّ ۖ فَطَفِقَ مَسْحًۢا بِٱلسُّوقِ وَٱلْأَعْنَاقِ** — "'Bring them back to me,' and he began *mashan* over the legs and the necks."

Again, the scholars differ on *masḥ*, and again the difference is a gift, not a problem.

On one reading, *masḥ* is its most common meaning: to **pass the hand over**, to stroke. Sulayman, on this view, calls the horses back and runs his hand affectionately down their legs and necks — reaffirming a love that is *for the sake of God*, dignifying the very instruments of His path. On the second reading, *masḥ* carries its harsher lexical sense — to **strike, to smite** — and Sulayman, having seen how even this good thing competed for his heart, has the horses brought back and offers them up, severing his attachment to the very thing that distracted him, giving the most beloved of his possessions away from himself entirely.

Notice that under *both* readings the man does the same essential thing: he refuses to let the beautiful good thing keep its grip on him. Whether by drawing it close in a love re-anchored to God, or by cutting it away from himself altogether — either way, he reasserts that *dhikr rabbī* sits on the throne of his heart and the horses do not.

[PAUSE]

So let me bring this all the way home to your afternoon.

When the time slips — and it will, today, this week — the question is never "how do I become a person who never gets absorbed?" You won't. Even a prophet got absorbed by the slanting light on a beautiful thing. The question is: **what do you do with the horses?** When you finally look up and see that the good thing has eaten the hour you owed your Lord, do you let it slide and tell yourself it was harmless — or do you say, as he said, *innī*, "it was me," and then *do something decisive* to put God back on the throne?

The practical application is almost frighteningly concrete. Identify *your* horses. Not the haram things — those are the easy enemy. Identify the *good* thing you love so much that it quietly eats your remembrance: the work you're proud of, the screen, the person, the project, the pursuit. And build into your life a *ruddūhā ʿalayya* — a deliberate motion of bringing it back under God's authority. For some that is cutting it down to size. For others it is re-consecrating it: *I love this because it serves Him, and the moment it stops serving Him, I let it go.* Either way — you, like Sulayman, decide who sits on the throne before the sun goes down.

## Theme Two: Why the Servant Outranks the King

Now let me take you to the deeper current under this whole scene — the thing the title *al-ʿabd* was pointing at the entire time.

Begin where you live. Almost every one of us has built our sense of who we are on top of what we *do* and what we *hold*. I am my job. I am my role. I am my achievements, my family, my reputation, my competence. We don't usually notice we've done this — until one of those pillars is removed. Until the job ends, the role is taken, the achievement fails, the thing we held is pried from our hands. And then the panic that comes is not really about the lost *thing*. It is the panic of a self that was *built on* that thing and now has nothing to stand on.

Sulayman held more than anyone. The largest kingdom in human history. If identity-built-on-holding were a stable foundation, his would have been the most stable that ever existed. And yet the Quran refuses to define him by any of it. Not *malik*. Not *nabī* in that opening breath. **ʿAbd.** Servant.

Why? Because *ʿubūdiyya* — servanthood — is the one identity that **cannot be taken from you.** Think about it. They can take your kingdom. They can take your horses, your health, your role, your name. But no one in the heavens or the earth can remove your status as the servant of your Lord — *unless you hand it away yourself.* It is the single foundation that no loss can crack. It does not depend on what you hold. It depends only on Whom you face.

[PAUSE]

This is why, of all titles, Allah chose the one that looks like the *bottom*. Because the servant has already let go of the illusion that he owns anything — and so he has nothing left to lose. The king is terrified, always, of losing the kingdom. The servant lost it before he started; it was never his; it was a *hiba*, remember — handed to him, never generated by him. And a man who knows that can stand in a palace and in a ruin with the very same face.

Now watch how the Quran proves this — not with an argument, but with **architecture**, which I'll lay out fully in a moment. Because the most stunning thing about this title is not that Allah gave it to Sulayman. It is *who else* in this very surah receives the identical words.

Here is the paradox to sit with before we get there. We think the goal is to *rise* — to acquire, to master, to climb until we are the ones in charge. The Quran whispers something that takes a lifetime to hear: the goal is to *descend* — to become so completely a servant that there is no self left to defend, no kingdom whose loss could break you, no horse whose pull could enthrone itself in your heart. The freest human being in this passage is not free *because* he commands the wind. He is free because, commanding the wind, he still knows himself to be a slave.

The practical application here is quieter than the first, and it goes deeper. The next time you feel your sense of self rising or collapsing on the back of something you achieved or lost — stop and say the four words Allah said. *Niʿma al-ʿabd.* Not "what a success." Not "what a failure." *I am a servant.* Practise relocating your identity, one deliberate act at a time, off the shifting ground of what you hold and onto the one ground nobody can take: *Whose* you are. That is the foundation Sulayman stood on. It is offered, free of charge, to you.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture: One Refrain, Three Lives

Now let me show you the proof that was hidden in plain sight, and let me show it to you only now — because you needed to do the work above first to feel its weight.

Surah Sad gives us a gallery of prophets, and three of them stand in a row: Dawud, Sulayman, Ayyub. Watch what Allah says at the seal of each portrait.

Of **Dawud** — the king-prophet, given the kingdom and the Psalms (v. 17): *wa\-dhkur ʿabdanā Dāwūd... **innahu awwāb*** — "remember **Our servant** Dawud... indeed **he was ever-returning.**"

Of **Sulayman** — given the largest dominion any human ever held, our verse (v. 30): ***niʿma al-ʿabd** ۖ **innahu awwāb***  — "**what an excellent servant** — indeed **he was ever-returning.**"

And of **Ayyub** — fourteen verses later (v. 44), the man who was *stripped of everything*: his wealth gone, his children gone, his health gone, his body a ruin — what does Allah say of him? *innā wajadnāhu ṣābiran ۚ **niʿma al-ʿabd** ۖ **innahu awwāb*** — "We found him patient — **what an excellent servant — indeed he was ever-returning.**"

[PAUSE]

Look at what Allah has done. Word for word — *niʿma al-ʿabd, innahu awwāb* — the **identical** verdict falls on Sulayman at the zenith of having *everything*, and on Ayyub at the nadir of having *nothing.* And the same root-words — *ʿabd*, *awwāb* — crown Dawud in between.

Do you see the argument the structure is making? Allah lined up three lives that span the entire human spectrum: maximum power, maximum dominion, maximum loss. Three conditions that the world insists are *completely different* — the throne, the empire, the ash-heap. And over all three, Allah writes the **same two words.** Because the one variable that decides a human being's worth is *none* of the things that differ between these three men. It is not the size of the kingdom or the depth of the suffering. It is the single thing they share: they were **servants who kept returning.**

Wealth could not corrupt that. Power could not inflate it. Catastrophe could not destroy it. The refrain is identical because *the thing that matters is identical* — in the palace and in the ruin alike.

## Bringing It Together

Now feel how the three words from Part 1 and the two themes from Part 2 are one insight seen from different sides.

*Wahabnā* — nothing was his; it was all gift. Which is exactly *why* he could be *al-ʿabd* without panic: you cannot lose what you never claimed to own. Which is exactly *why*, when the horses pulled at his heart, he could say *ruddūhā ʿalayya* and put the gift back under the Giver — he was *awwāb*, the one who closes the distance the instant he feels it open. The gift, the servanthood, the returning, the horses, the refrain across three prophets — it is all a single sentence spoken from five directions: **the only thing in a human life that survives both abundance and ruin is the heart that keeps coming home.**

## Questions to Carry

1. What are *my* horses — the genuinely *good* thing I love so much that it quietly eats my remembrance? Can I name it honestly?
2. When the time slips and I look up and the moment with my Lord is gone — do I say *innī*, "it was me," or do I reach for an excuse?
3. Is my sense of who I am built on what I *hold* — or on *Whose* I am? Which one would survive if the holding were taken away?
4. The praiseworthy servant is not the one who never drifts but the one who has made *returning* a reflex. How fast is my return? How wide has the gap grown between drifting and coming back?
5. If Allah were to seal *my* life with a single verdict, the way He sealed three prophets' lives — is there anything in me that He could call *awwāb*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

At the summit of the greatest kingdom ever given to a human being, Allah's praise for Sulayman is not "what a king" but "what a servant" — because the only thing that outlasts both the gift of power and the loss of everything is a heart that keeps returning to its Lord.

## The Closing Invitation

You will not leave today and become a person who never gets absorbed by the slanting light on a beautiful thing. Neither did a prophet. What you *can* leave with is a smaller, truer ambition: to shorten the distance between the drift and the return. To make *ruddūhā ʿalayya* — *bring it back under His authority* — a motion your soul knows by heart. Not to never love the horses. But to never let them take the throne. That is not a burden laid on you. It is an invitation to the one foundation nothing can crack: to be, like the three servants of this surah, *awwāb* — one who always, always comes home.

## A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who gave Sulayman and called him *servant* before You called him *king* — relocate our hearts onto that same ground. When the good things we love begin to eclipse Your remembrance, give us the sight to notice and the will to bring them back to You before the sun goes down. And of all the verdicts that could be written over our lives, let *awwāb* be ours — make returning to You the most natural motion of our souls. *Āmīn.*
