# Some Paltry Host: How a Single Empty Word Routs an Army

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Six words. Listen to them.

> **جُندٌ مَّا هُنَالِكَ مَهْزُومٌ مِّنَ ٱلْأَحْزَابِ**
>
> *Jundun mā hunālika mahzūmun mina-l-aḥzāb.*
>
> "[They are but] a paltry host, [bound to be] routed right there — just another of the factions."

Six words. And one of them, by the strict reckoning of the grammarians, **does nothing.** It carries no meaning of its own. It points to no object. It is, in the language of classical Arabic grammar, *ṣila* — a connective, a filler, an "extra" word. The grammarians call it *mā zāʾida*: the redundant *mā*.

Hold onto that. A word that does nothing.

Because here is what we are going to discover together tonight: that "empty" word is the hinge on which the entire ayah turns. It is the difference between an army and a joke. It is the sound of God's contempt for something the whole of Mecca was trembling before. By the time we are finished, I want you to feel how Allah can crush an empire with a syllable that, on paper, means nothing at all.

### Rebuild the world

Step back into the moment this was revealed.

The Prophet ﷺ is in Mecca. He is, at this point, a man under siege — not by armies, but by something heavier. By mockery. By the slow, grinding contempt of his own people. Surah Sad opens with God describing them: **بَلِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ فِى عِزَّةٍ وَشِقَاقٍ** — "the disbelievers are in *ʿizzah* and *shiqāq*" (38:2). In *might*. In *glory*. In a towering, self-assured sense of their own importance — and in *defiance*, in splitting away, in opposition for opposition's sake.

Picture them. The leadership of Quraysh. Old money. Old names. Custodians of the Kaʿba, masters of the trade routes, the aristocracy of all Arabia. When they walked through Mecca, the ground felt like it belonged to them. And here is this man — one of their own, no army, no wealth, no faction — telling them their gods are nothing, that they will stand before a Reckoning, that everything they have built is sand.

And they laughed. The surah records their laughter. "A lying magician." "Has he made the gods into one God? This is an astonishing thing." They could not be touched. They were *fī ʿizzah* — wrapped in might like armor.

Now imagine you are the Prophet ﷺ, watching this wall of arrogance, carrying a message you *know* is true, and being treated like a fool by men who hold all the visible power.

What does God say to him?

He does not say "be patient." Not here. He does not say "they will see." He does a stranger thing. He leans down toward this mountain of Meccan pride — these men *fī ʿizzah*, in might — and He files them, on the spot, under a single word.

### The hook

Tonight we are going to ask three small questions that turn out to be enormous.

Why does Allah insert a word that means nothing — *mā* — right in the middle of describing the most powerful men in Arabia?

Why does He call them "defeated" — *mahzūm* — using a form of the word that describes something already finished, when the defeat had not yet happened and there was, at that moment, not the faintest sign it ever would?

And why does He end by sliding them into a category — *al-aḥzāb*, "the factions" — without telling us, yet, who else is in it?

Three questions. Stay with me. Because the answers, taken together, are one of the most devastating quiet acts of demolition in the entire Qurʾan.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that does nothing — *jundun mā*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah.

In English, when we want to belittle something, we reach for tone, or for a dismissive little add-on. We say: "Oh, he's got some little *job* downtown." We don't say which job. The vagueness *is* the insult. "Some" and "little" don't add information — they *subtract dignity*. You've heard a parent say of a bully, "He's just some *kid*." That "some" is doing emotional work while carrying no factual content at all.

Arabic has a tool exactly for this. It is the particle **مَّا** — *mā*. Among its many uses, there is one the grammarians call *mā* of *ibhām* and *taḥqīr* — the *mā* of vagueness-unto-contempt. You attach it to an indefinite noun, and it casts a shadow of "some sort of… and not much of one."

Now look at the ayah.

> **جُندٌ مَّا**
>
> *Jundun mā.*

Two things are already happening before the contempt-*mā* even arrives.

First, the word **جُندٌ** — *jundun*, "a host," "a troop," "an army" — is *indefinite*. Not *al-jund*, "the army." Just *jundun*. A host. Some host. Already it is anonymous, unnamed, one-of-many.

And then, [PAUSE] — *mā*.

Imam al-Qurṭubī, in his commentary, tells us plainly: *"mā" ṣila* — the *mā* here is a connective, an "extra." Al-Jalālayn says the same: *ṣila*. By the rules of grammar, you could lift it out of the sentence and the grammar would not break.

But here is what I want you to feel. **It is precisely the word that "means nothing" that carries the contempt.**

Think about what Allah is doing. The whole of Mecca looks at these men and sees *ʿizzah* — might, glory, weight. And Allah says: *jundun mā.* "Some… little… host." Some paltry band. A nothing-army. The vagueness is deliberate. He will not even dignify them with a definite article. He waves a hand at them the way you'd wave at a fly.

Notice something. We started the surah at 38:2 with **عِزَّة** — *ʿizzah*, might, the grandest word for power and self-importance. And nine ayahs later, the very same men are **جُندٌ مَّا** — "some paltry host." [PAUSE] That is not a description. That is a *demotion*. That is God taking the word they wear like a crown — *might* — and quietly replacing it with *some little troop*.

Here is what is remarkable. We tend to think God's power shows itself in the loud words — in *thunder*, in *fire*, in armies of angels. But watch where it lives *here*. It lives in a particle the grammarians call "extra." A word that, on the page, does nothing. Allah's contempt for human arrogance is so total that He can express it in the one part of speech that carries no meaning. He doesn't need a weapon to belittle them. He needs a *filler word.*

What this single particle reveals: **the gap between how power sees itself and how the Owner of power sees it is so vast that it fits inside an empty syllable.** They were *fī ʿizzah*. To God they were *jundun mā* — some little nothing.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The defeat that already happened — *mahzūm*

Now the second word. And to feel it, you need one piece of grammar.

In Arabic, you can describe an action with a verb — "he defeated them," "they will be defeated" — and that ties the action to a *time*: past, present, future. But Arabic has another tool: the *participle*. The **اسم المفعول** — *ism al-mafʿūl*, the passive participle — does not describe an *action in time*. It describes a *state*. A settled condition. A label that has become part of the thing.

In English: there is a difference between "the glass was broken" (an event, last Tuesday) and "the glass is *broken*" (a state — that is simply what it now *is*). "Broken" is the participle. It has stopped being something that *happened* and become something the glass *is*.

Now the ayah does not say *sa-yuhzamūn* — "they will be defeated," a future verb. It does not say *huzimū* — "they were defeated," a past event. It says:

> **مَهْزُومٌ**
>
> *Mahzūm.* "Defeated." "Routed."

The participle. Not an event. A *state*. A label.

[PAUSE] Now sit with the timing of this. When was this revealed? In *Mecca*. Years before any battle. The Muslims at this point are a hunted minority. There is no army of believers. There is no Badr. There is no battlefield on the horizon. By every visible measure, the people *fī ʿizzah* are winning, and they will go on winning, and there is nothing — *nothing* — to suggest otherwise.

And Allah does not say "they will lose." He says they *are* — already, now, as a settled fact of their being — **mahzūm**. Routed. As though the defeat were behind them rather than ahead of them.

Here is what is happening. Allah is describing the future with the grammar of the finished past, because to the One outside of time, the outcome is not a *prediction* — it is a *fact already in hand*. When God calls them *mahzūm* in Mecca, He is not forecasting the weather. He is reading you the verdict that has already been entered. The battle hasn't been fought because, from where He stands, the battle's result is as fixed as something that happened a thousand years ago.

Al-Baghawī preserves a report from Qatāda on exactly this. Qatāda said that Allah informed His Prophet ﷺ, *while he was in Mecca*, that He would rout the host of the idolaters — and Qatāda links it to the verse **سَيُهْزَمُ ٱلْجَمْعُ وَيُوَلُّونَ ٱلدُّبُرَ** — "the host shall be routed, and they shall turn their backs" (54:45). And, al-Baghawī tells us, "its interpretation came to pass on the Day of Badr." A promise spoken in the weakest hour, in the grammar of a done thing — and years later, in the dust of Badr, the world caught up to the word.

Now let me give you the image buried inside this root, because it is exquisite. The root *h-z-m* (هزم). Al-Qurṭubī notes the everyday Arabic usage: *tahazzamat al-qirba* — "the waterskin caved in" — *idhā inkasarat*, when it collapses and breaks inward.

Picture a waterskin. Full, taut, swollen, firm to the touch. It looks solid. It looks *full*. And then it is struck, or it dries, and it *caves* — it folds inward on its own emptiness and collapses into a wrinkled, useless thing.

[PAUSE] That is *hazm*. That is the word God chose. And do you see what He is saying about *ʿizzah*?

The might of Quraysh was a *full waterskin*. Taut, swollen, impressive to every eye. And God is telling the Prophet ﷺ: it is already *mahzūm* — already caved. The collapse is not a future blow from outside. The emptiness was always *inside*. Arrogance is a skin stretched tight over nothing, and it only takes one touch from the Hand that made it to fold it inward.

What this word reveals: **what looks most full is, in the sight of God, already collapsed — and He can say so in Mecca, years early, because He is naming a state, not predicting an event.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The lineage they joined — *mina-l-aḥzāb*

The last phrase. **مِّنَ ٱلْأَحْزَابِ** — *mina-l-aḥzāb*. "Of the factions." "Among the confederates."

Let me open the root. *Ḥ-z-b* (حزب). A *ḥizb* is a band, a faction, a group of people who *cluster together* around a cause. We still use the word today — in modern Arabic a political party is a *ḥizb*. The root carries the sense of people who bind themselves into a bloc.

Now notice the grammar. The men of Quraysh are not called *a* faction here, standing alone. They are called *min* — "of," "from among" — **al-aḥzāb**, *the* factions, definite, plural, a known category. As though Allah is reaching for a folder that *already exists*, a folder with a name on it, and dropping Quraysh inside.

And what is striking is what the ayah does *not* do — yet. It names the category — *the* factions — but it does not, in this ayah, tell us who else is in the folder. It leaves the file open. It makes you ask: *which* factions? Who came before? Who else got this label?

And then — only in the very next ayahs — Allah opens the folder and reads the names:

> **كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَعَادٌ وَفِرْعَوْنُ ذُو ٱلْأَوْتَادِ ۝ وَثَمُودُ وَقَوْمُ لُوطٍ وَأَصْحَٰبُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ ۚ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ ٱلْأَحْزَابُ**
>
> "Before them the people of Nūḥ denied, and ʿĀd, and Pharaoh — lord of the stakes — and Thamūd, and the people of Lūṭ, and the dwellers of the Thicket. *Those* were the factions." (38:12–13)

[PAUSE] There it is. *Ulāʾika-l-aḥzāb.* "*Those* were the factions."

Do you feel what just happened to Quraysh? They thought they were the *first* — the new power, the present masters, the men of the hour. And Allah has just told them: *you are not new. You are not even interesting. You are the latest entry in a very old list.* Nūḥ's people. ʿĀd. Pharaoh, with all his stakes and pyramids and armies. Thamūd, who carved homes into mountains. Each one, in their day, *fī ʿizzah*. Each one certain they were the exception. And every single one of them is now a *name on a list of the destroyed* — a closed file, a settled state, *mahzūm*.

This is the genius of putting *mahzūm* — the settled-state participle — right before *al-aḥzāb*. Allah is saying: the reason I can call you "already routed" is that you have joined a category whose every member is *already routed*. You stepped into a lineage with a perfect, unbroken record of collapse. The verdict on the group has been entered seven times over. You are simply the eighth signature on a document that always ends the same way.

What this phrase reveals: **arrogance always believes it is unprecedented; Allah's answer is to show it is utterly precedented — one more iteration of a pattern that has never, once, ended differently.**

### The bridge

Look at what these three words have built, standing together.

*Jundun mā* — you are not as big as you think. Some paltry host.

*Mahzūm* — you are not as full as you look. Already caved in, already collapsed around your own emptiness.

*Mina-l-aḥzāb* — and you are not as new as you imagine. Just the latest of the factions, joining a line with a flawless record of ruin.

Three quiet demolitions. No thunder. No armies. Just an "empty" particle, a participle, and a plural noun. [PAUSE]

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way reality itself is built.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The arithmetic of arrogance

Let me start where you live.

You have met the full waterskin. You may *be* the full waterskin some days. We all are.

Think of the most intimidating person you have ever stood in front of. The boss who could end your career with a sentence. The relative whose disapproval could freeze a whole room. The institution, the system, the *power* that made you feel small and made itself feel permanent. There is a particular feeling that radiates off real worldly power: the feeling that it *cannot be touched*. That it is simply a fact of the landscape, like a mountain. You don't argue with a mountain. You arrange your life around it.

That feeling has a name in this surah. *ʿIzzah.* And the people of Quraysh were drowning in it, and projecting it, and the believers around them were *feeling* it the way you feel it from your mountain.

Now here is the question the ayah forces. *What is that feeling actually measuring?*

Because watch what Allah does. He does not deny that they have power. He does not say "they are weak men." He grants them the whole surface — the wealth, the names, the custody of the Sanctuary, the laughter, the control. And then He looks straight through it and says: *jundun mā.* Some paltry host. A full skin stretched over nothing.

[PAUSE] Here is the arithmetic. Worldly power is real — but it is *borrowed*, every gram of it, and it is *temporary*, every second of it. ʿĀd had more of it than Quraysh. Pharaoh had *vastly* more — *dhū-l-awtād*, "lord of the stakes," with monuments that still stand four thousand years later. And the participle on every one of them is *mahzūm*. So if the mightiest faction in human history is, in the final ledger, *caved in* — then the feeling of untouchability is not measuring strength. It is measuring *the thickness of the skin over the emptiness.*

Let me make this sharper, because it cuts toward us, not just toward Pharaoh.

The most dangerous thing about *ʿizzah* is that it feels *exactly the same* from the inside whether it is real or hollow. Pharaoh did not wake up feeling like a doomed faction. He woke up feeling like a god. The taut waterskin does not know it is empty — it feels firm, it feels full, right up until the touch that folds it. Which means the feeling of being untouchable is *not evidence* of being untouchable. It is, if anything, the very symptom the Qurʾan is warning about. The fuller the skin feels, the more you should wonder what is actually inside it.

And now turn the blade inward, gently. Where in *your* life are you the full waterskin? Where have you mistaken a borrowed, temporary thing — your health, your reputation, your competence, your certainty that you've got time — for something solid? Because the ayah is not only about Quraysh. The ayah is a *lens*. It teaches you to look at any swollen, taut, impressive-feeling power — including the pride swelling in your own chest — and ask: *is this full, or is this caved-in and just hasn't been touched yet?*

What does this change about tomorrow? **One practice: when you feel the swell of ʿizzah — that surge of "I cannot be touched, I have arrived, I am above this" — name it as a waterskin.** Say it inwardly: *jundun mā.* Some paltry host. Not to crush yourself, but to let the air out gently *before* the touch comes that lets it out violently. The believer deflates his own arrogance daily, in small honest acts, so that he is never the swollen skin awaiting the blow.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: "You are not the first" — the consolation hidden in a threat

Now I want to turn this ayah completely around, because it has two faces, and we have only seen one.

To Quraysh, *jundun mā… mahzūm… mina-l-aḥzāb* is a threat. A demotion. A verdict.

But remember *who else is listening.* The Prophet ﷺ. Grieving. Mocked. Watching the wall of *ʿizzah* and carrying a truth no one will receive. And to *him*, these same six words are not a threat at all. They are an *embrace.*

Let me bring this into your world first.

There is a particular loneliness in being the one who sees clearly while everyone around you is certain you're a fool. The student who knows the textbook is wrong and is failed for saying so. The whistleblower. The person who left a toxic family system and is told *they* are the problem. The believer in a setting where faith itself is the joke. There is a voice that comes in those moments — and it does not say "you are wrong." It says something worse: *"You are alone. No one has ever stood where you stand. This is unprecedented, and you will be crushed by it, and no one will even remember."*

That is the deepest cut of opposition. Not "you are wrong." *"You are alone, and you are the first."*

Now listen to what Allah says to the loneliest man in Mecca. *Mina-l-aḥzāb.* "They are *of the factions*." And then He reads the names. Nūḥ. ʿĀd. Pharaoh. Thamūd. Lūṭ. Shuʿayb's people.

Do you see the gift? [PAUSE] Allah is saying to His Prophet: *you are not the first. Look — Nūḥ stood exactly here. For nine hundred and fifty years he stood here, facing a wall of ʿizzah, mocked, alone. Lūṭ stood here. Shuʿayb stood here. Every prophet I have ever sent has stood in this exact spot, facing this exact wall, hearing this exact laughter.* The thing the enemy uses to isolate you — "you are unprecedented" — Allah reverses into the deepest comfort: *you are in the most honored lineage that has ever walked the earth, and you already know how every chapter of this story ends.*

This is why the surah does what it does next. After this ayah, Surah Sad pours out story after story of the prophets — Dāwūd, Sulaymān, Ayyūb, Ibrāhīm, and the rest. The whole surah becomes a long arm around the Prophet's shoulder: *here is your family. Here is everyone who carried what you carry. Here is how each of their stories ended.*

And here is the movement that takes my breath. The *same fact* that terrifies the arrogant *comforts* the faithful. To the proud faction, "you are one of the aḥzāb" means *your collapse is guaranteed.* To the lonely prophet, "they are one of the aḥzāb" means *their collapse is guaranteed.* One pattern. Two completely different emotions, depending entirely on *which side of the truth you are standing on.* The pattern of history is a verdict to the one resisting it and a promise to the one carrying it. [PAUSE]

Let me sit with the paradox, because it is real and I don't want to rush past it. If the defeat of the arrogant is so certain that God states it in the past tense, years early — then why the *waiting*? Why did the Prophet ﷺ have to endure years more of Mecca before Badr? Why, if the verdict is entered, the long delay in the sentence?

I don't think the Qurʾan lets us answer that fully, and I won't pretend to. But notice this much. The verdict was given *in Mecca* — in the weakness, not after the victory. Allah did not wait until Badr to tell him "you won." He told him "they are *mahzūm*" while the believers were still being dragged across the sand. Which means the gift was never the victory alone. The gift was *the certainty during the wait.* To know the ending while you are still in the middle of the suffering — that is the consolation. The delay between the verdict and the sentence is exactly the space in which faith is built, the space where you live on the *word* of God before you can see the *act* of God.

What does this change about tomorrow? **When you feel unprecedented and alone in carrying what is true — costly honesty, an unpopular stand, faith in a faithless room — reach for the list.** You are not the first. You are *mina* a lineage — the prophets, the truthful, the patient — and the wall you are facing is *mahzūm*, already caved, whether or not you live to see it fold. Live on the verdict during the wait. That is the whole of the believer's strength: to act on the ending God has already named.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architectural reveal

Now stand back and look at the shape of what we've walked through, because the surah built an arch and we can finally see the keystone.

It opens at 38:2 with one word for the disbelievers: **عِزَّة** — *ʿizzah*. Might. Glory. The full waterskin, taut and swollen.

It arrives at 38:11 with the answer: **مَهْزُوم** — *mahzūm*. Caved in. The same skin, folded inward on its emptiness.

*ʿIzzah* → *mahzūm.* Might → routed. The entire opening movement of the surah is an arch that springs from the word the proud give themselves and lands on the word God gives them. And the keystone — the small stone at the very top that holds the whole arch in place — is that "empty" particle, *mā.* *Jundun mā.* The contempt-syllable that means nothing and decides everything. Remove it and the arch is just a sentence. Leave it, and it is a demolition.

### Bringing it together

See how the two halves were always one.

The linguistic journey showed us: an empty word carrying total contempt, a past-tense grammar laid over a future event, and a fresh power filed into an ancient folder.

The thematic journey showed us: arrogance is a swollen skin over emptiness, and the very pattern that condemns the proud consoles the faithful.

They are the same insight from two directions. **The gap between how a thing feels from inside and how it stands before God is so vast that God can express it in a syllable — and that gap runs in both directions.** The proud man feels mighty and is *mahzūm.* The lonely believer feels crushed and is *of the most honored line on earth.* The feeling is never the measurement. Only God's word is the measurement. And His word, here, fits in six syllables, one of which "means nothing."

### Questions to carry

- Where in my life am I the full waterskin — mistaking a swollen feeling of security for something actually solid?
- When I feel untouchable, do I read that as a sign of strength — or, as this ayah teaches, as the very thing to be wary of?
- When I carry something true and costly and feel utterly alone in it, do I reach for the lineage — *you are not the first* — or do I let the loneliness convince me I'm unprecedented?
- Can I learn to live on a verdict God has given before I see Him carry it out — the way the Prophet ﷺ lived on *mahzūm* through every year before Badr?
- Whose *ʿizzah* in my life have I been arranging myself around like a mountain — when before God it is *jundun mā*, some paltry host?

### One-sentence distillation

What the world wraps in *might* and what stands before God as a *paltry, already-routed band* are the same thing seen from two ends — and the believer's strength is to read reality by God's word, not by how the power feels.

### The closing invitation

Tonight, let one thing shift. Not a resolution to be made and broken. Just a new pair of eyes. The next time you feel the swell of pride, or the next time you stand before some towering worldly power and feel small — try the lens of this ayah. Ask of the pride in your chest, and of the mountain in front of you, the same quiet question: *full, or caved-in and just hasn't been touched yet?* Let *jundun mā* be the phrase that lets the air out gently. And when you carry truth and feel alone, let *mina-l-aḥzāb* remind you which list *you* are on — and how the other list always ends.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who see the emptiness inside what looks full and the honor inside what looks defeated — let me never be the swollen skin awaiting Your touch. Deflate my pride gently, by my own honest hand, before it folds beneath Yours. And when I carry what is true and feel alone in it, steady me on Your word: that the wall is already caved, that I am of an honored line, and that the One who named the ending in the weakest hour will carry it out in His. Āmīn.
