# Remember the Ones Who Remembered

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with the sound of them before we say anything about meaning.

> [ayah:38:45] وَٱذْكُرْ عِبَـٰدَنَآ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ وَإِسْحَـٰقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ أُو۟لِى ٱلْأَيْدِى وَٱلْأَبْصَـٰرِ

>
> *Wadhkur Ismāʿīla wa-l-Yasaʿa wa-Dhā l-Kifli wa-kullun mina l-akhyār.*

### Rebuilding the world

To feel why these verses were given, you have to feel the room they entered.

Surah Sad is a Makkan surah, and it opens in the middle of a fight. The leaders of Quraysh have just walked away from the Prophet ﷺ. They are astonished that one man would claim that the gods are one. They mock him. They call the message *a fabrication*, *the last religion*, *something made up*. The surah carries the sting of that rejection in its opening lines.

And then something remarkable happens. Allah does not answer the mockery with an argument. He answers it with **a procession of faces**.

He brings Dawud, who was given the kingdom and the wisdom and the decisive speech — and who was also tested, and turned back to his Lord. He brings Sulayman, with the horses and the wind and the whole sweep of dominion — *an excellent servant*. He brings Ayyub, stripped of everything, sitting in his affliction for years — and the account ends, just two verses before ours, with the words **نِعْمَ ٱلْعَبْدُ إِنَّهُۥٓ أَوَّابٌ** — *an excellent servant; indeed he was one who constantly turned back*.

That word — *servant*, **ʿabd** — is still ringing in the air when our passage begins. And the very first thing Allah says is: **وَٱذْكُرْ عِبَادَنَا** — *And remember Our servants*.

So picture the Prophet ﷺ in the hardest years of his mission. The doors closing. The insults landing. And his Lord leans in, as it were, and says: *Let Me show you who walked this road before you. Let Me show you what they were made of. And let Me tell you the one thing that made them what they were.*

This is not a history lesson. This is a Lord consoling His beloved by showing him his family — the family of all who carry the truth.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hold as we walk in.

In four verses, Allah is going to do two things. He is going to **command** the Prophet to *remember* these men — *wadhkur*, twice. And He is going to tell us the single quality He distilled them down to, the one thing He calls their *purity*.

And that one quality — the essence of why they deserve to be remembered forever — is that **they remembered.**

Remember the ones who remembered.

Stay with that. Because once you see what they remembered, and what it did to them, you will never look at your own forgetfulness the same way again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — أُولِي ٱلْأَيْدِي وَٱلْأَبْصَارِ: hands and eyes

Let me teach you something simple about Arabic before we touch the words, because it will change what you hear.

In Arabic, the body is a vocabulary. The language does not only use a word like *strength* in the abstract. It reaches for the part of the body that *does* the thing, and lets that part of the body stand for the whole capacity. The **hand** — *yad* — is the limb that grips, builds, gives, strikes, holds. So across Arabic, "hand" comes to mean *power, capacity, the ability to actually do.* When you say in English "he had a hand in it," you already know this instinct. The hand is doing.

And the **eye** — *baṣar* — is the organ that sees. But "seeing" in Arabic splits into two streams: the eye that catches light, and the eye of the heart that catches *truth*. *Baṣīra* — from the same root — is insight, discernment, the ability to see *into* a thing and know what it really is.

Now hold those two — hand and eye — and listen to how Allah describes Ibrahim, Ishaq, and Ya'qub:

> **أُولِي ٱلْأَيْدِي وَٱلْأَبْصَارِ**
>
> *ulī l-aydī wa-l-abṣār* — **possessors of the hands and the eyes.**

Notice the word **أُولِي** — *ulī*. It is not a casual word. It means *owners of, possessors of, the people characterized by.* It comes attached to the defining trait of a group. When the Quran says **أُولِي ٱلْأَلْبَابِ** — *possessors of intellect* — it means: these are the ones whose very identity is intellect. So here: these men are *the possessors of the hands and the eyes.* Their very identity is hand and eye.

Let's sit with what that means. The classical commentators are remarkably united here. Ibn Abbas, as al-Baghawi and the Jalālayn report, reads **ٱلْأَيْدِي** — the hands — as **ٱلْقُوَّة**: *strength, power — specifically strength in the obedience of Allah.* And **ٱلْأَبْصَارِ** — the eyes — as **ٱلْبَصَائِر فِي ٱلدِّين**: *insight in the religion, vision in the matter of God.* Qatāda and Mujāhid say it plainly: *they were given strength in worship, and sight in the faith.*

So the physical image is hands and eyes. But the image is a stepping stone. Watch where it leads.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. Allah did not say they had *strength*. He could have. He did not say they had *insight*. He could have. He named the **limbs**. He named the **hand** and He named the **eye**. Why descend into the body?

Because strength and insight, as abstractions, float. They are concepts. But a hand is something that *grips and acts now*, and an eye is something that *sees now*. By naming the limbs, Allah is telling you these were not men of *potential*. These were men of **exertion**. The strength was in the hand — meaning it was spent, used, applied. The vision was in the eye — meaning it was looking, awake, discerning in real time.

Now notice the **order**, and notice that there are **two** of them, paired.

Strength without vision is a blind power. It is a strong hand swinging in the dark — it does damage, even with good intentions. How many people have enormous capacity, drive, energy — and aim it at the wrong thing entirely? They have hands and no eyes.

And vision without strength is a beautiful paralysis. It is an eye that sees the path perfectly and a body that cannot take a step. How many people see exactly what is right, can describe it eloquently, can diagnose every flaw — and never *do* a single thing? They have eyes and no hands.

The prophets were given **both, fused.** *Possessors of the hands and the eyes.* The vision told the strength where to go, and the strength carried the vision into the world. The eye aimed; the hand struck true.

[PAUSE]

And here is the layer beneath even that. Where did the strength go, according to the commentators? Into **obedience.** Into *worship*. Not strength to conquer, not strength to accumulate — *strength in the service of God.* And where did the eyes look? Into the **religion of God** — to see Him, to know Him, *al-maʿrifa bi-llāh*, as al-Baghawi puts it.

So the most powerful capacity a human being can have, and the clearest sight a human being can possess, were both turned in one direction: toward their Lord. The hand worked for Him. The eye looked for Him.

That is what *ulī l-aydī wa-l-abṣār* reveals. Not multi-talented men. Not impressive men. **Integrated** men — whose whole power and whole sight pointed the same way.

### Element Two — أَخْلَصْنَاهُم بِخَالِصَةٍ: purified with a purity

Now we come to a sentence that, in Arabic, almost folds in on itself — and that folding is the meaning.

> **إِنَّا أَخْلَصْنَاهُم بِخَالِصَةٍ**
>
> *Innā akhlaṣnāhum bi-khāliṣatin* — **Indeed We purified them with a pure quality.**

Let me teach you the root first, because everything hangs on it. The root is **خ-ل-ص** (*kh-l-ṣ*). At its base it means: *for a thing to become pure, clear, unmixed — for it to be freed of everything that is not itself.* Gold that has had every other metal burned out of it is **khāliṣ**. Milk that comes out, as the Quran says elsewhere, *pure* between blood and dung — *khāliṣan* — drinkable, with nothing foreign left in it. This is the word for **the thing with nothing else mixed into it.**

From this root comes a word you already love: **ikhlāṣ** — sincerity. And now you can feel what sincerity actually *is*. It is not a warm feeling. It is **purity of intention** — an act done for God with nothing else stirred into it. No showing off, no side-motive, no audience. Just God. Unmixed.

Now look at the verse again, and notice that Allah uses the root **twice in a single breath**:

**أَخْلَصْنَاهُم** — *We purified them* — the verb.
**بِخَالِصَةٍ** — *with a pure thing* — the noun.

He purified them with a purity. He made them unmixed by means of an unmixed quality.

This is not redundancy. In Arabic, when you take a verb and follow it with a noun from the same root, you are **intensifying and specifying** at once. It is like saying, in English, "We cleansed them with a cleansing" — except Arabic does it with elegance, and the second word adds a crucial detail the first one lacked.

Because here is the question the doubling forces you to ask: *purified them with what?* What was the purifying agent? When you refine gold, you purify it *with fire.* So what is the fire that Allah passed these men through to burn out everything that was not pure?

The next two words answer it. And the answer will stop you.

[PAUSE]

> **ذِكْرَى ٱلدَّارِ**
>
> *dhikrā d-dār* — **the remembrance of the Abode.**

The thing He purified them *with* — the fire, the refining agent, the *khāliṣa* — was **the remembrance of the Abode.** The remembrance of the Home. The constant, living awareness of the Hereafter — the final dwelling.

Let me make sure you feel the grammar of this, because it is doing something precise. **ذِكْرَى** — *dhikrā* — is from the same root as *wadhkur*, the command that opened the passage: *remember.* But *dhikrā* is not the act of remembering once. The form carries a sense of *abundant, repeated, settled remembrance* — a remembering that has become a state, not an event. And **ٱلدَّار** — *the Abode* — with the definite article, is not just "a home." When Arabic says *the* Abode, *the* Dwelling, with no further description, it means **the real one. The lasting one.** This world is the place you pass through; *al-Dār* is the place you arrive at.

The Jalālayn presses further on what *dhikrā al-dār* actually encompasses. The commentator's gloss is careful: remembrance of the Hereafter and **working for it** — *dhikruhā wa-l-ʿamal lahā.* The remembrance and the action it produced are one thing, inseparable. Which is exactly what you would expect from men who were *possessors of the hands and the eyes* — the eye was aimed at the Abode, and the hand was already moving.

So here is the full sentence, unfolded:

*We made these men pure — and the thing We purified them with was their unbroken, settled, ever-present remembrance of the final Home — a remembrance that could not stay interior, that expressed itself in action, that moved their hands precisely because it had seized their eyes.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has just told us about the human soul?

He has told us **what purifies a person.** Not effort in the abstract. Not even worship in the abstract. The refining fire — the *khāliṣa* — is *keeping the Hereafter in view.*

Because think about what mixes *into* our intentions and dirties them. Why do we show off? Because we are thinking about people *here*. Why do we cut corners? Because we want a result *here*. Why do we hold grudges, chase status, fear the wrong things? Because *this world*, the temporary house, has filled our whole field of vision, and the real Home has slipped out of frame.

The moment the real Home comes back into view, the impurities have nowhere to hide. You cannot show off for an audience you have stopped believing in. You cannot sell your soul for a house you know you are leaving. The remembrance of the Abode is the fire that burns the foreign metals out of the gold. **Remember where you are going, and your reasons for doing things get purified by themselves.**

That is the engine of these men. That is what made the hand strong in obedience and the eye clear in the religion — they could not stop seeing the Home.

But notice, precisely, how this *dhikrā* came to be in them. The verse does not say they cultivated it through discipline alone, or trained themselves into it by force of will. It says **أَخْلَصْنَاهُم** — *We purified them.* The subject is Allah. The divine first person plural, the *naḥnu* of majesty: **We.** Al-Muyassar is explicit on this point: Allah *placed* the remembrance of the Final Abode in their hearts — He singled them out with this as a great distinction, making the *dhikrā* a gift settled within them, not merely a virtue they earned. The purification was divine initiative before it was human achievement. This does not diminish their exertion; it grounds it. They worked *from* a remembrance that was given.

And then — and this is the detail al-Muyassar presses — that remembrance did something beyond purifying them privately. They *called people to the Hereafter and reminded them of it*. The private *dhikrā* became a public *tadhkīr*. The inward fire became the outward call. The same root — *dh-k-r* — but now directed outward at humanity. They remembered, and that remembering overflowed into their mission until everyone around them was being reminded.

Which means: the *wadhkur* Allah gives to the Prophet ﷺ is not a separate thing from what these men themselves were doing. It is the next link in an unbroken chain. The prophets remembered the Abode, and it made them reminders to their peoples. The Prophet remembered them, and the Quran carries their story forward. You and I read these words now, and the chain of *dhikr* has reached our moment.

Major pause.

### Element Three — وَٱذْكُرْ … ذِكْرَى: remember the ones who remembered

Now lift your eyes from the single words and look at the **shape** of the whole passage. Because the shape is itself a message, and it is easy to walk right past it.

The passage opens with a command to the Prophet ﷺ:

**وَٱذْكُرْ** — *And remember.* Root: *dh-k-r*.

It closes, three verses later, with the same command repeated:

**وَٱذْكُرْ** — *And remember.* Same root, same form, an imperative.

And what sits in the exact center, as the reason these men are worth remembering at all?

**ذِكْرَى ٱلدَّارِ** — *the remembrance of the Abode.* Same root: *dh-k-r*.

[PAUSE]

Let that land. The command is *remember.* The reason is *they remembered.*

In four verses, Allah has built a perfect architectural mirror. He commands the Prophet ﷺ to remember these men — and the reason these men are worth remembering, the very thing that made them what they were, is that they could not stop remembering the Home they were headed toward. *Wadhkur* and *dhikrā* — the same root, the same three letters, the same act of turning the mind toward what matters — weave through the passage like a golden thread.

This is not accident. This is instruction. The way you become like them is to remember them. And the way they became what they were is to remember *the Abode.* The path runs: you remember them, they remembered that — and that chain of remembering will begin to change you, just as it changed them.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, in his commentary on this passage, pauses over the very first word in a way that opens a slightly different window. He notes that while the overwhelming majority of Quranic reciters — *ʿāmmat qurrāʾ al-amṣār* — read **وَٱذْكُرْ عِبَادَنَا** (*Our servants*, plural), the Makkan reciter Ibn Kathir read it as **وَٱذْكُرْ عَبْدَنَا** — *Our servant*, singular. Al-Tabari explains his reasoning: on the singular reading, the one servant first addressed is Ibrahim, and Ishaq and Ya'qub are mentioned afterward because they are his descendants — honored by the light of his lineage, his children and grandchildren carrying the same quality forward.

Both readings are legitimate. And they open slightly different windows onto the same truth. The plural says: here are three servants, each tested separately, each purified individually, each given strength in the hand and vision in the eye — three distinct testimonies to what a purified soul looks like. The singular says: here is one root, and the quality of *dhikrā al-dār* flowed through a household, down through generations — Ibrahim, then Ishaq, then Ya'qub — as if the remembrance of the Abode was itself an inheritance, a patrimony of the soul bequeathed from father to son.

Either way, you are looking at a family. The family of those who remembered.

### Element Four — ٱلْمُصْطَفَيْنَ ٱلْأَخْيَارِ: chosen precisely because they were pure

We have now crossed into the verse that makes the connection explicit.

> **وَإِنَّهُمْ عِندَنَا لَمِنَ ٱلْمُصْطَفَيْنَ ٱلْأَخْيَارِ**
>
> *Wa-innahum ʿindanā lamina l-muṣṭafayna l-akhyār* — **And indeed, with Us, they are among the chosen, the best.**

Two words: **ٱلْمُصْطَفَيْنَ** and **ٱلْأَخْيَارِ.** Let me give you the roots.

**ٱلْمُصْطَفَيْنَ** is from the root **ص-ف-و** (*ṣ-f-w*). At its base: *to be clear, clean, unmixed — to be the pure element that rises to the surface after the turbid has settled.* When you skim the cream from the top of milk, you are taking what is *ṣāfī* — the clear, the settled, the pure. *Muṣṭafā*, the pattern here, means *the one chosen precisely because he is the purest* — the one selected after everything impure has been removed and only excellence remains.

**ٱلْأَخْيَارِ** is the plural of *khayr* — goodness, excellence — in its superlative force. These are the people *of the good*. Goodness is not merely something they do; it is what they are constituted of.

Now hold both of those — and hold the verse before them. The verse before said: *We purified them with a purification* — **أَخْلَصْنَاهُم**. Root: **خ-ل-ص** (*kh-l-ṣ*). And now this verse says: they are **ٱلْمُصْطَفَيْنَ** — the selected pure ones. Root: **ص-ف-و** (*ṣ-f-w*).

Do you see the movement? Two adjacent verses, two adjacent roots — both meaning *purity*. **خ-ل-ص**: to be freed of everything that is not itself, to become unmixed. **ص-ف-و**: to be the clear, the selected, the one that rises as the pure element.

The logic is this: *because* Allah purified them with *dhikrā al-dār* — because the remembrance of the Abode burned out every mixed motive and every impurity of intention — *therefore* they became the ones chosen as the purest. The *ikhlāṣ* (purification) led directly to the *iṣṭifāʾ* (selection). They were not chosen first and then given a quality. They were given a quality — the remembrance of the Home — and the purity that quality produced is exactly why they were chosen.

The selection was the harvest of a lifetime of *dhikrā.*

[PAUSE]

And the phrase **عِندَنَا** — *with Us* — is worth a moment. In Arabic, *ʿinda* conveys presence, proximity, standing in the estimation of. When Allah says they are *with Him* among the chosen and the best, He is not only making a statement about their status. He is telling us *where they are.* They are with Him. The men who spent their lives keeping the Abode in view now stand in the presence of the One the Abode leads to. The *dhikrā al-dār* was the direction. *ʿIndanā* is the arrival.

Major pause here.

### Element Five — وَٱذْكُرْ إِسْمَاعِيلَ وَٱلْيَسَعَ وَذَا ٱلْكِفْلِ: three more, and all among the best

The passage closes with a second *wadhkur*, and three more names.

> **وَٱذْكُرْ إِسْمَاعِيلَ وَٱلْيَسَعَ وَذَا ٱلْكِفْلِ ۖ وَكُلٌّ مِّنَ ٱلْأَخْيَارِ**
>
> *Wadhkur Ismāʿīla wa-l-Yasaʿa wa-Dhā l-Kifli wa-kullun mina l-akhyār.*
>
> **And remember Isma'il, al-Yasa', and Dhul-Kifl — and all are among the best.**

Isma'il we know. He is Ibrahim's son — the one who was laid down for sacrifice and who said *do what you are commanded; you will find me, if Allah wills, among the patient.* He is, of his own testimony, the son of his father's faith. Whatever Ibrahim was given by way of *dhikrā al-dār*, Isma'il received it and lived it on the knife-edge of the same trial. Al-Yasa' is Elisha in the prophetic lineage — the successor of Ilyās, known for constancy, for continuing the work of those who came before him when continuity itself was the test.

And then there is **ذُو ٱلْكِفْلِ** — *Dhul-Kifl.*

The name is itself a word. **ٱلْكِفْل** — *al-kifl* — from the root **ك-ف-ل** (*k-f-l*), which means: *to guarantee, to take responsibility for, to stand surety for something, to fulfil a pledge completely.* *Dhul-Kifl* is *the one of the guarantee* — the one who took something on, who made a pledge and kept it.

Who was he? Here the classical scholars differ, and the Quran itself does not name his people or his time. Some say he was a prophet sent to a particular community; others say he was a righteous man who made a solemn pledge — to fast, to pray, to judge with patience and without anger — and who fulfilled that pledge without wavering until the end. The scholars collect these reports without settling firmly on one reading.

What the Quran does settle is this: he is *min al-akhyār* — among the best. Whatever the specifics of his pledge, the quality it points to is the same quality running through the entire passage. A person who makes a commitment to the Abode and keeps it. A person whose hand moves and whose eye sees. A person whose *dhikrā al-dār* was not a philosophy he occasionally visited but a covenant he honored in the daily texture of his life.

And then the final phrase, sweeping all three and closing the gallery: **وَكُلٌّ مِّنَ ٱلْأَخْيَارِ** — *and all are among the best.* Not some of them. Not the ones who succeeded visibly. All. The same word — *akhyār* — that sealed the first triad in verse 47 now seals the second in verse 48. The gallery is complete. The hall of the best is a hall for everyone who lived inside *dhikrā al-dār.*

## PART 2: WHAT THIS DOES TO THE ONE WHO RECEIVES IT

### The consolation that is also a commission

We need to return to the room where these words first landed.

The Prophet ﷺ is in Makkah. He is being mocked by his own tribe. The people he grew up among, whose faces he knows, are calling his revelation a fabrication. And his Lord sends him — what? Not a refutation. Not a defense. A gallery.

*Look at Ibrahim. Look at Ishaq and Ya'qub. Look at Isma'il and Dhul-Kifl. Look at all of them. Now look at what they had in common.*

They kept the Abode in view.

This is not comfort in the soft sense. It is something more demanding than that. Because the consolation carries an implication: *you are walking the road they walked. The road has always looked exactly like this. Mockery is the weather of prophethood — it comes with the territory. And the men who walked it before you walked it because they were not looking at the people mocking them. They were looking at the Home.*

The *wadhkur* — *remember them* — is therefore both solace and summons. Solace because you are not alone on this road. Summons because you are being asked to adopt the same orientation that sustained them: *dhikrā al-dār*, the remembrance of the Abode, the eye kept on the real destination while the hands do the work of this one.

[PAUSE]

### What the remembrance of the Abode actually does

Let me try to say precisely what *dhikrā al-dār* means when it is working.

It is not the same as thinking about death. Thinking about death, on its own, can produce anxiety, paralysis, a kind of morbid preoccupation. That is not what the prophets had.

*Dhikrā al-dār* is the settled awareness that **this world is not the final frame.** The frame is the Hereafter — *al-Dār*, the real dwelling. This world is the passage. And the critical shift that happens when you genuinely absorb this is not that you become gloomy about the present; it is that you become *free* in it. The approval of people loses its leverage over you, because you can see that the people who approve and disapprove of you are all passing through the same passage. The accumulations that seem so important — status, wealth, the comfortable arrangement of this temporary life — lose their power to distort your choices. What remains is what actually matters: the work, the worship, the turning toward the real Home.

This is the purifying mechanism. This is why *dhikrā al-dār* is the *khāliṣa* — the thing that makes the soul *khāliṣ*, unmixed. Because the moment the real destination is clear, all the false urgencies begin to dissolve. And you are left with what is true.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question that makes this personal: what is your relationship to *al-Dār*?

Not: do you believe in it intellectually? Most people who read these words believe in the Hereafter as a theological fact. The question is whether it is *in view*. Whether it is the horizon your day is oriented toward, or a doctrine you hold somewhere in the back of your mind while your attention is fully captured by this world, this week, this problem, this opinion, this screen.

The prophets were purified because the Abode was not merely a doctrine they held. It was the direction they faced. Every prayer turned them toward it. Every act of obedience was aimed at it. Every sacrifice made sense because it was made in the light of the real Home.

The question is not whether you believe in *al-Dār*. The question is whether *al-Dār* has enough of a grip on your imagination, your choices, your daily hours — whether it has become a *dhikrā* rather than just a belief — to begin the work of purification that the prophets knew.

[PAUSE]

### The names themselves are a command

There is a final thing I want to give you before we close.

Allah does not just describe these men. He names them. Ibrahim. Ishaq. Ya'qub. Isma'il. Al-Yasa'. Dhul-Kifl. Six names, spoken individually, lined up in a passage that could have simply said *the prophets before you were patient.* But it did not. It gave you the names.

Why?

Because a name is a face. And a face makes something a person rather than an idea.

When you say *the prophets were patient* — that is an abstraction. You can nod at an abstraction and move on. But when you say *Ibrahim* — that is the man who left his home and his family and his language and his gods, and walked into a desert because his Lord told him to. That is the man who laid his son down. That is the man whose *dhikrā al-dār* was not a philosophy but a set of decisions made under pressure, year after year, one after another, until a nation of faith grew from his single act of surrender.

When you say *Ya'qub* — that is the man who wept for decades for a son he did not know was alive. That is the man whose eyes went white from grief and who said *I only complain of my sorrow to Allah.* His *dhikrā al-dār* is what kept that sentence from becoming something else. He remembered where everything would be resolved. He kept the eye on the Home, even when the eye could not stop weeping.

The names land because each one carries a story, and each story is a different angle of light on the same truth: the remembrance of the Abode held these men together when everything else was coming apart.

[PAUSE]

And now the passage ends with a command you are reading, right now.

**وَٱذْكُرْ** — *And remember.*

The command that opened the gallery also closes it. The double *wadhkur* — remember Ibrahim and his lineage, remember Isma'il and those who came with him — frames everything. But here is what I want you to notice about what happens when that command reaches you.

Allah told the Prophet ﷺ to remember these men. The Prophet ﷺ remembered them and recited them, and his words entered the Quran. You are now reading the Quran, and the *wadhkur* has reached your moment. The command is no longer addressed only to a man in Makkah in the seventh century. It is addressed to you, now, in whatever hour you are sitting in.

Remember the ones who remembered.

And the instruction is clear: remember *what* they remembered. Not only their stories, not only their virtues — those are the surface. What you are being given, through the act of remembering them, is contact with their *dhikrā al-dār.* Their remembrance of the Home was the engine. Your remembrance of them — when done with attention, with the linguistic journey fully entered, with the names felt as faces — can become a doorway into that same orientation.

The chain of *dhikr* has not broken. It began with Allah placing the remembrance of the Abode in the hearts of His purified servants. It moved through their obedient action. It overflowed into their calling of their peoples to the Home. It reached the Prophet ﷺ as a command and a consolation. It reached us as a scripture that is still alive. And now, in whatever ordinary hour comes next after you close this, it offers you the same fire that made those men what they were.

Keep the Abode in view. Let the rest get purified.
