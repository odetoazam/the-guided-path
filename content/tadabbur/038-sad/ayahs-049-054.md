# The Beautiful Return: How the Prophets' Reward Becomes Yours

## Introduction: The Word You've Been Walking Toward

Let me give you the verses first, and then let me tell you something about where they sit — because where they sit is half the meaning.

> [ayah:38:49] هَـٰذَا ذِكْرٌ ۚ وَإِنَّ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ لَحُسْنَ مَـَٔابٍ
>
> *Hādhā dhikr. Wa-inna lil-muttaqīna la-ḥusna maʾāb.*
>
> **"This is a reminder. And indeed, for the God-conscious is a beautiful place of return."** (38:49)

And then the description unfolds — gardens whose gates are already flung open, people reclining, calling for whatever fruit they wish, companions beside them, and at the very end a sentence that lands like a seal pressed into wax:

> [ayah:38:54] إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَرِزْقُنَا مَا لَهُۥ مِن نَّفَادٍ
>
> *Inna hādhā la-rizqunā mā lahu min nafād.*
>
> **"Indeed, this is Our provision — for it there is no running out."** (38:54)

Now here is what you need to know to feel this.

For most of Surah Sad — verse after verse before this one — Allah has been walking us through a gallery of His prophets. Dawud, who was given a kingdom and a voice that the mountains echoed. Sulayman, who was given dominion over the wind. Ayyub, who lost everything and was given it back. Ibrahim, Ishaq, Yaʿqub, Ismaʿil. A long, luminous procession of the greatest human beings who ever lived.

And as Allah honors each one, He keeps reaching for the same word. About Dawud: *innahu awwāb* — "he was one who kept returning" (38:17). About Sulayman: *niʿma al-ʿabd, innahu awwāb* — "what an excellent servant; he kept returning" (38:30). About Ayyub, after all that suffering: *niʿma al-ʿabd, innahu awwāb* — "what an excellent servant; he kept returning" (38:44).

**Awwāb. Awwāb. Awwāb.** Like a bell rung three times. The one who returns. The one who turns back. The one whose whole life is a motion of coming home to Allah.

Al-Tabari observed that this entire gallery was addressed first to the Prophet ﷺ himself — *tā'assā bihim*, "draw comfort from them; follow their path in patience toward what you are enduring for the sake of Allah." The prophets' stories carry more than historical glory. They are medicine, administered directly to the one bearing the weight of prophethood in a city that had not yet believed. And because the Quran carries its occasion into eternity, the same medicine reaches every person who comes after — in every season of loneliness, resistance, or doubt.

[PAUSE]

So hold that word in your hand. *Awwāb.* The returner.

Because now the surah turns to us, and it says: *wa-inna lil-muttaqīna la-ḥusna* — **maʾāb.**

And here is the hook. I want you to hear it before we explain it. The word for "place of return" — *maʾāb* — and the word that described every single prophet — *awwāb* — **come from the same root.** They are the same idea wearing two different clothes. One is the person. One is the destination.

The whole surah has been quietly building a single equation. And in these six verses, it finally completes it. Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مَآب — The Destination That Was Hiding in the Verb

Before we touch the word, let me give you a small grammatical tool, because Arabic does something here that English can do too — we just don't notice it.

In English, we take a verb and we make a *place* out of it. "To bake" gives us "a bakery." "To park" gives us "a parking" lot. "To dwell" gives us "a dwelling." The action becomes a location — the place where that action happens, or the place that action arrives at.

Arabic does this with breathtaking economy. It's called the *ism makān*, the noun of place. You take the bones of a verb and you reshape them into a destination.

Now look at the verb that has been ringing through this entire surah.

The root is **أ-و-ب** (*a-w-b*). The verb *āba, yaʾūbu* means: **to return.** To come back. To turn back toward something.

From this root, Allah built the word He used for the prophets — *awwāb* (أَوَّاب). This is an intensive form. Not just "one who returns" but **one who returns again and again and again.** A person whose default motion, whenever they drift, is to turn back. Sin, and turn back. Forget, and turn back. Suffer, and turn back. The awwāb is not a person who never leaves — he is a person who **always comes home.**

And from that *same* root — أ-و-ب — Allah builds the noun of place: **maʾāb** (مَآب). The place of return. The destination of all the returning. The home that the homecoming is heading toward.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened?

For dozens of verses, Allah described the prophets by their *motion* — they were *awwāb*, always turning back. And now, in verse 49, He names their *destination* using the very same root. The returners (*awwāb*) arrive at the return (*maʾāb*).

**The person and the place are made of the same letters.** Because they are made of the same reality. The one whose life is return — what could his final home be, except the place of return?

Let's sit with this, because it gets deeper.

When you call someone an *awwāb*, you are describing a thousand small motions across a lifetime. Every time they caught themselves and turned back. Every late-night *istighfār*. Every moment they were pulled toward something low and chose, instead, to come home. None of it dramatic. Most of it invisible. A quiet, repeated turning.

And Allah is telling us: **all of that turning was going somewhere.** It wasn't motion in a circle. It was motion toward a place. Every return was a step on a road, and the road has a destination, and the destination has a name — *maʾāb* — and it is *ḥusn*, beautiful.

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say *lahum jannah* — "for them is a garden." He could have. Instead He chose the word that rhymes, in its very root, with who they had to *become* to deserve it. He named the reward with the same letters as the work. The destination carries the fingerprint of the journey.

Here is what this single word reveals: **the beautiful place of return is not a prize handed to a stranger. It is the natural arrival point of a life spent returning.** You do not stumble into the *maʾāb*. You walk there, one *awb* — one turning-back — at a time.

[PAUSE]

Now watch what the surah does immediately after naming the reward. Having said *ḥusna maʾāb* — "a beautiful place of return" — it does not leave the phrase as a beautiful abstraction. The very next verse elaborates: *Jannāti ʿadn.* Al-Tabari noted this structural move precisely: *Jannātu ʿadn: bayānun ʿan ḥusni l-maʾāb* — "the Gardens of ʿadn are an *explanation* of the beautiful place of return." The surah names the reward, then immediately translates itself. It is its own tafsir.

The word *ʿadn* carries the meaning of permanent settled dwelling — to take up residence without departure. Arabic draws on this same root for where minerals are found embedded deep in the earth, remaining there across ages (*maʿdin*, a mine — the thing that stays). So *Jannāt ʿadn* are not gardens you visit. They are gardens where the very concept of leaving has been removed. The *maʾāb* is not just any homecoming — it is the homecoming where departure becomes impossible, because the word has no meaning there.

The Companions felt the weight of this. Al-Tabari records that Umar ibn al-Khattab once asked Kaʿb al-Ahbar: *"What is ʿadn?"* Kaʿb replied: *"O Commander of the Faithful, palaces in Paradise made of gold."* The question itself tells us something. The word arrested them. The most confident men who ever lived sat with *ʿadn* and had to ask. The *maʾāb* is a destination remarkable enough to make even the most grounded of souls reach toward it, open-handed, and ask: *what exactly is this?*

But I have not yet shown you the most stunning thing about this word. For that, we need to read it against the rest of the surah. Hold *maʾāb* in your mind. We will come back to it — fittingly — at the very end.

### Element Two: ذِكْر — The Word That Means Both "Reminder" and "Renown"

Look at how the passage opens. Two words.

> هَٰذَا ذِكْرٌ
>
> *Hādhā dhikr.* — "This is a *dhikr*."

The root is **ذ-ك-ر** (*dh-k-r*). And here is the beautiful problem the translator faces: this one word carries *two* meanings at once, and Arabic lets you hold both in the same breath.

The first meaning is **reminder, remembrance.** A *dhikr* is something that brings a truth back to your mind. This is the sense in which the Quran calls itself *adh-dhikr* — the Reminder. So "this is a *dhikr*" means: *this account of the prophets is a reminder for you. Let it remind you. Let it move you.*

But there is a second meaning living inside the same word, and the classical commentators reach straight for it. *Dhikr* also means **mention, renown, honor — to be spoken of well.** Al-Qurtubi explains *hādhā dhikr* as *dhikr jamīl fī'd-dunyā* — "a beautiful mention in this world, an honor by which they are remembered forever." Al-Baghawi reads it as *sharaf* — nobility, honor. Al-Jalalayn is precise: *dhikr lahum bi-thanāʾ al-jamīl hunā* — "a mention of them with beautiful praise *here*," in this world, in this very recited Book. The word *hunā* — "here" — matters: the honor does not wait for the afterlife to begin. It lands in the dunya, spoken in mosques by people who never met these prophets, across centuries and continents. Al-Muyassar broadens the address still further: *hādhā l-Qurʾān dhikrun wa-sharafun laka ayyuhā l-rasūl wa-li-qawmika* — "this Quran is a reminder and an honor for you, O Messenger, and for your people." The beautiful mention does not belong only to the prophets being named. It flows to the one who carries this Book forward, and to the community that receives it.

So when Allah says *hādhā dhikr*, He is saying two things in one stroke:

**This is a reminder for you** — *and* — **this is their honor.** This is the beautiful remembrance the prophets earned. Their names, spoken with reverence, in a Book that will be recited until the end of time.

[PAUSE]

Now watch how these two meanings fold into each other.

How did the prophets earn this *dhikr* — this everlasting honorable mention? Go back to where their section began. Allah commanded the Prophet ﷺ: *wadhkur ʿabdanā Dāwūd* — "**Remember** Our servant Dawud" (38:17). The same root again. *Udhkur.* Remember him. Mention him.

So the prophets *remembered* Allah their whole lives — they were *awwāb*, constantly turning back to Him in remembrance — and now Allah is having *them* remembered, forever, in His Book. They made *dhikr* of Him; He makes their *dhikr* eternal.

This is the trade. You spend a life remembering Allah in private, in the dark, when no one is watching — and Allah answers by making your name a *dhikr jamīl*, a beautiful mention, spoken aloud across centuries by people who never met you.

Here's what's remarkable: notice where this word sits in the architecture of the surah. The surah *opens* — verse one — with *ṣād, wal-Qurʾāni dhī adh-dhikr* — "Sad. By the Quran, full of *dhikr*" (38:1). And here, as the prophets' gallery closes, Allah says again: *hādhā dhikr.* The surah is bracketed by the word. It opens by swearing by the Quran of remembrance, and it seals the prophets' honor by calling it remembrance. The whole structure is held between two utterances of the same word.

What this single word reveals: **to remember Allah is to be remembered by Him.** The Quran is *dhikr*; the prophets who lived by it became *dhikr*; and the invitation, sitting right there in the grammar, is that you can enter that same economy. Remember Him — and become unforgettable.

### Element Three: مَا لَهُ مِن نَّفَادٍ — Reading the Absence

Now I want to take you to the last verse of the passage, because it ends with something easy to read past — and the meaning is hiding in what is *missing*.

> إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَرِزْقُنَا مَا لَهُ مِن نَّفَادٍ
>
> *Inna hādhā la-rizqunā mā lahu min nafād.*
>
> **"Indeed, this is Our provision — for it there is no *nafād*."**

The word is **نَفَاد** (*nafād*). It means: running out. Depletion. Exhaustion. The state of a thing coming to its end. When a well runs dry, when the money runs out, when the food is finished — that is *nafād*.

And here is the grammatical detail I want you to feel. Allah does not simply say "Our provision is abundant" or "Our provision is vast." He could have. Instead, He defines the provision **by something that is absent from it.** *Mā lahu min nafād* — literally, "there is not, belonging to it, any running-out."

Notice the structure: *mā... min...* In Arabic, putting *min* after the negation *mā* is an intensifier. It doesn't just say "no running out." It says "not the *slightest trace* of running out. Not a hint of an ending. Not one drop of depletion anywhere in it."

[PAUSE]

Notice too what surrounds this seal. The verses just before it have described people reclining in the gardens — al-Muyassar fills out the image as reclining on adorned couches, asking for whatever their souls desire and their eyes find pleasure in. Not a fixed menu. Not a measured allotment. Whatever the soul desires. The provision is responsive to the one drawing from it, not constrained by what is available. And after all of that — after the responsive, generous, desire-fulfilling picture of abundance — the passage closes not with a quantity claim but with a duration claim: *it does not run out.* Allah could have said "Our provision is vast." He chose instead to say the ending is not there.

Stay with me, because this is doing something profound.

Allah is describing the provision of Paradise not by adding a feature, but by **subtracting a flaw.** And He chose, of all the flaws He could remove, the one specific flaw that haunts every single pleasure you have ever known.

Think about it. What is the one thing true of every joy in this life? **It runs out.** The meal you love — you take the last bite, and it's over. The vacation — the last morning comes. The laughter with people you love — the evening ends, and eventually *they* end, or you do. The defining shadow over every earthly delight is *nafād*. Depletion. The clock running down. Even in the middle of joy, some quiet part of you is already grieving its end.

Read the absence carefully. Allah is not promising you *more* of what you have. He is removing the **one thing** that poisons what you have. He is naming the precise ache — the *this-will-end* — and saying: *that is gone. That is not here. Mā lahu min nafād.*

This is *istidlal* at its most tender. Allah points at something you can feel right now — the way every pleasure carries its own ending inside it — and uses it as a stepping stone to a reality you cannot yet imagine: a joy with no ending inside it. A provision with the *nafād* surgically removed.

What this phrase reveals: **the difference between the gardens of this world and the Gardens of return is not quantity. It is the absence of the ending.** You already know abundance. You have tasted beautiful things. What you have never tasted — not once, not for a single second — is a beautiful thing with no *nafād*. That is the new thing being promised.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us.

*Maʾāb* told us the reward is a **destination** — and that the destination is shaped like the journey: the returners arrive at the return.

*Dhikr* told us the reward is a **remembrance** — that those who remembered Allah become eternally remembered by Him.

And *nafād* told us the reward is defined by an **absence** — the removal of the one flaw that shadows every joy we've ever known.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what it means to spend a human life turning back toward something, and what waits at the end of all that turning.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Only Homecoming That Doesn't Hurt

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a particular feeling that human beings carry — a quiet, lifelong ache. It's the longing to **return.** To go back somewhere. Maybe it's the house you grew up in. Maybe it's a version of yourself before things got complicated. Maybe it's a relationship, a city, a season of life when you felt whole. There is a reason "home" is one of the most emotionally loaded words in every human language. We are, all of us, trying to get back to something.

And here is the cruel thing about every return we attempt in this world: **it never fully works.**

You go back to your childhood home, and it's smaller than you remember, and strangers live there now. You reconnect with an old friend, and you both know it's not the same. You try to return to who you were before the loss, before the betrayal, before the diagnosis — and you find that door is locked. You cannot un-know what you know. Every homecoming in this life is shadowed by the gap between what you hoped to find and what is actually there. The return is always, somehow, a little bit of grief.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the verse again. *Wa-inna lil-muttaqīna la-ḥusna maʾāb.* "For the God-conscious is a **beautiful** place of return."

The word *ḥusn* — beautiful, good, lovely — is not decoration here. It is the whole point. Because Allah is naming the one return in all of existence that is **purely, completely beautiful.** No gap. No grief. No "it's not the same." A *maʾāb* that is only *ḥusn*, with nothing bittersweet folded into it.

And here is what the surah has been teaching us about how to get there. Remember the equation. The *awwāb* — the one who returns — arrives at the *maʾāb* — the place of return.

This means the great homecoming at the end is not a single event. **It is a practice.** Every day, you are either practicing return or you are not.

What is *tawba*? We translate it "repentance," but the word literally means **to turn back, to return.** What is your daily *istighfār*? A small return. What is every prayer — five times a day, turning your whole body toward one direction? A rehearsal of return. What happens when you catch yourself sliding toward something low, and you stop, and you turn back to Allah? That is one *awb*. One turning. One stitch in the fabric of becoming an *awwāb*.

Here is the paradox, and I want you to let it sit without rushing to resolve it: **the people Allah calls *awwāb* are not the people who never left. They are the people who always came back.** Dawud is called *awwāb* in the same surah that recounts his mistake (38:24). Ayyub is called *awwāb* after a lifetime that included the most devastating losses. The title is not for the spotless. It is for the *returners.*

Do you understand what that means for you?

You are not disqualified by the leaving. **You are defined by the returning.** The drift is not the story. The turning-back is the story. And every single turn — even the one you'll make later today, quietly, when no one knows — is a step on the road to a *maʾāb* that is pure *ḥusn*.

[PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow?

This: stop measuring your faith by how far you've drifted, and start measuring it by how quickly you return. The awwāb is not faster at avoiding the fall. He is faster at getting back up. Make the *return* short. That is the whole practice. Fall, and turn back the same hour. Forget, and remember the same day. Make returning the reflex of your life — and you are not just earning the *maʾāb*. You are already, in miniature, doing the thing Paradise is made of.

### Theme Two: The Cure for the Anxiety You Didn't Know You Had

Let me name a feeling you have, even if you've never put words to it.

Underneath your enjoyment of almost everything good, there runs a thin current of anxiety. Call it **scarcity.** It's the voice that says: *this won't last.* You're on a beautiful trip, and somewhere in the back of your mind, a counter is already running down the days. You're holding a sleeping child, and you're already aware they'll grow. You're at a table full of people you love, and a quiet dread whispers that not all of these chairs will be filled forever.

Psychologists have a name for the way this distorts us. When we believe a good thing is limited, we can't fully *be* in it — we're too busy guarding it, rationing it, bracing for its end. Scarcity doesn't just take the thing away at the end. **It steals the thing while you still have it,** by making you unable to rest inside it.

And here is the deepest layer of it: **everything in this world is governed by *nafād*.** Depletion. Every resource, every pleasure, every relationship, every breath you take, is being drawn down from a finite supply. The dunya is, by its very nature, a place of running-out. That is not a defect in your particular life. It is the law of the place.

[PAUSE]

Now stand inside the final verse.

> إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَرِزْقُنَا مَا لَهُ مِن نَّفَادٍ
>
> **"Indeed, this is Our provision — for it there is no running out."**

Allah does not say "you will have a lot." He has already, in the verses before, described abundance — fruit, drink, gardens, companions, gates flung open. But abundance alone would not cure the anxiety, because you can be drowning in abundance and *still* be afraid it will end. The rich are not free of scarcity-anxiety; often they have more of it, because they have more to lose.

So Allah does something abundance can never do. He reaches into the structure of the joy and **removes the ending.** *Mā lahu min nafād.* The counter is switched off. The clock in the back of your mind — the one that has been running your entire life, the one you've never once been able to silence — stops.

Think about what that does to the *experience* of every pleasure in that place. The companions of equal age, the fruit, the drink, the reclining in peace — every one of those is being enjoyed by someone who, for the first time in their existence, **is not afraid of it ending.** They can finally do the thing you have never been able to do in this world: be *completely present* in a joy, with no part of the soul braced for its loss.

That is the gift hiding in the grammar. Not just provision — provision *with the fear removed.*

[PAUSE]

And notice the timing of the verse before it: *hādhā mā tūʿadūna li-yawmi al-ḥisāb* — "This is what you are promised for the **Day of Account**" (38:53). The Day of *ḥisāb* — the day of reckoning, of counting, of accounts being settled and *closed.* The whole logic of dunya is accounting: things added up, drawn down, balanced, exhausted. And right *after* invoking the great Day of Counting, Allah names a provision that **cannot be counted down.** The day everything is finalized is the gateway to the one thing that is never finalized.

So what changes tomorrow?

This: notice the scarcity-voice when it speaks. When you feel that thin dread under a good moment — *this won't last* — let it become a *dhikr*. Let it remind you. Say to yourself: *yes, this will end. This is the dunya; this is the place of nafād. But it points to a place with no nafād, and I am working my way home to it.* Let the very thing that steals your peace — the ending of things — become the thing that turns your heart toward the place where nothing ends. Use the scarcity as a stepping stone. That is exactly what Allah designed it to be.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Reward That Travels Down the Mountain

Now let me show you the structure of this surah, because the linguistic work we've done makes it visible, and it is staggering.

Go back through the prophets' gallery and watch one phrase.

About **Dawud**, after his story, Allah says: *wa-inna lahu ʿindanā la-zulfā wa-**ḥusna maʾāb*** — "and indeed, for him with Us is nearness and a **beautiful place of return**" (38:25).

About **Sulayman**, after his story, Allah says the same: *wa-inna lahu ʿindanā la-zulfā wa-**ḥusna maʾāb*** — "nearness and a **beautiful place of return**" (38:40).

These were the elite. Prophets. Kings. Men given kingdoms and winds and miracles. And they were promised *ḥusna maʾāb.*

And then comes verse 49. After the prophets, after the gallery, Allah turns and says:

> وَإِنَّ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ لَحُسْنَ مَآبٍ
>
> "And indeed, for **the God-conscious** is a **beautiful place of return.**"

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? **The exact phrase given to Dawud and Sulayman is now given to you.**

*Ḥusna maʾāb* — 38:25, the reward of a prophet-king. *Ḥusna maʾāb* — 38:40, the reward of a prophet-king. *Ḥusna maʾāb* — 38:49, the reward of **anyone with taqwā.**

The reward travels down the mountain. What was reserved, in the text, for the greatest of all human beings is, three verses later, extended in identical words to the *muttaqīn* — to every soul who guards itself and keeps returning. The grammar of the muttaqīn confirms it: the word is Form VIII of the root و-ق-ي — *ittaqā* — a **self-directed** verb, "to guard *oneself*." It is not a station you are born into. It is a thing you *do* to yourself, daily, by returning. And the moment you do it, the prophets' reward becomes yours, word for word.

That is the architecture. The surah spends its length lifting the prophets up — and then bends down and hands their crown to you.

### Bringing It Together

Here is everything in one frame.

The prophets were *awwāb* — returners. They earned a *dhikr* — an eternal honorable remembrance. And their reward was *ḥusn maʾāb* — a beautiful place of return, a provision with no *nafād*.

And the surah's quiet, devastating claim is that **the same road is open to you.** Become a returner — an awwāb — and you walk toward the same destination, named with the same letters: *maʾāb.* Remember Him, and become *dhikr.* And the place you arrive will have the one feature no earthly joy has ever had: it does not run out.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same truth from two directions. The grammar says: *the returner arrives at the return, the rememberer becomes remembered, the joy has its ending removed.* The heart says: *I am always trying to get home, I want to matter beyond my death, I am tired of every good thing ending.* And these verses answer all three aches with one promise — and then prove the promise is for you by handing you, in identical words, the reward of a prophet.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you drift from Allah, how long do you stay away before you turn back? What would it mean to make the return *shorter* — to become not someone who never falls, but someone who always comes home quickly?

2. The prophets became an eternal *dhikr* by remembering Allah in private. What would your life look like if you believed that the unwitnessed acts — the quiet *istighfār*, the unseen turning-back — were the very thing being made unforgettable?

3. Where in your life is the scarcity-voice loudest — *this won't last*? Could that exact anxiety become a reminder that turns your heart toward the place of no *nafād*?

4. Dawud and Sulayman were promised *ḥusna maʾāb*. So are you, in identical words. Do you live as though the prophets' reward is genuinely within your reach — or as though it belongs to someone holier than you could ever be?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The one who keeps returning to Allah in this life — the *awwāb* — is promised the only homecoming that is purely beautiful and never runs out, in the very same words once reserved for the prophets.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to be Dawud. You don't have to be spotless. The door — *muffattaḥatan lahum al-abwāb*, "the gates flung open for them" — opens for *returners.* So let this be the shift: stop treating your faith as a record of how far you've fallen, and start treating it as a practice of how faithfully you return. Every turn back is a step toward the *maʾāb*. You are not far from home. You are simply being invited, again, to turn around.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, make us of the awwābīn — those who always return to You, however far we drift. Let the remembering we do in private become a beautiful remembrance with You that never fades. And bring us, at the end of all our returning, to that beautiful place of return — Your provision that knows no running out. Āmīn.*
