# The Test That Climbed the Wall
### Surah Sad, Ayahs 21–25 — Dawud and the Speed of Return

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first in Arabic, and then let it land slowly in English.

> وَهَلْ أَتَىٰكَ نَبَؤُاْ ٱلْخَصْمِ إِذْ تَسَوَّرُواْ ٱلْمِحْرَابَ
>
> *Wa hal atāka nabaʾu-l-khaṣmi idh tasawwaru-l-miḥrāb.*
>
> **"And has the news of the litigants reached you — when they climbed over the wall of the private chamber?"**

That opening word — *hal*, "has it reached you?" — is not quite a question. Al-Jalalayn notes that the rhetorical function here is *taʿajjub wa-tashwīq*: wonder and the arousal of desire to hear. Not *did you know* but *stop what you are doing. Let me tell you something that will arrest you.* The Quran is leaning in before it speaks.

And the scene unfolds: two men appear inside a locked room where a king has gone to be alone with his Lord. He is alarmed. They say: *do not fear.* They lay out a dispute — ninety-nine ewes against one. He gives a verdict. And then, in a single breathtaking instant, the king understands something about *himself* that no one in the room said out loud — and he collapses to the ground, asking forgiveness.

I want you to feel how strange this is before we go anywhere near the words.

### Rebuild the world

To stand inside this passage you have to first understand who Dawud was to the people hearing this surah.

Dawud — David — was not a minor figure. He was a prophet *and* a king, which almost no one in scripture is. Allah gave him a kingdom, gave him wisdom, gave him the Zabur, made the mountains and the birds sing with him, softened iron in his hands like wax. And of all the things He could have praised, look at what Allah singled out four ayahs before this one:

> *"…dha-l-ayd; innahu awwāb"* — "possessor of strength; indeed he was ever-turning-back to Allah." (38:17)

Hold those two words together, because the whole passage lives in the tension between them. **Strength.** And **turning back.** The mightiest man of his age. And the one always returning.

Now — Dawud was famous for one thing above all in the memory of his people: he was the perfect judge. When two people had a dispute that no one could untangle, they came to Dawud, and Dawud's verdict was *justice itself.* He was, to his world, what we would call the supreme court and the wisest sage rolled into one human being.

So hold that picture. This is the man the test is coming for. Not in his weakness. In his **strength.**

And the surah itself is being revealed into a specific pressure. The Prophet ﷺ in Makkah is surrounded by men who mock him, who turn their backs, who say *"is this the one chosen among us?"* The surah opens with their arrogance and their refusal. And in the middle of all that contempt, Allah keeps telling stories of prophets who had *every reason* to be arrogant — strength, kingdoms, power — and who instead kept falling to the ground in return. The strand running through this surah is: **the great ones were great because they bent.**

### The Hook

So here is what I cannot stop thinking about, and I want it to bother you the way it bothers me.

Two men come to the most just judge who ever lived. They bring him a case. He listens. He rules. His ruling, on the facts as presented, is *correct.* And the moment he finishes ruling — he understands he has failed a test, and he throws himself on the ground begging forgiveness.

What did Dawud see? [PAUSE]

The men never accused him of anything. The verdict was right. There was no crime in the room. And yet the just judge, the awwāb, the one whose justice was legendary — *something* in that room caught him, and he knew it instantly, and he fell.

To understand what he saw, we have to do something the passage is quietly inviting us to do. We have to read not only the words that are present — but the one voice that is missing.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Tasawwarū:* They Did Not Come Through the Door

Before I show you the word, let me teach you a small thing about how Arabic builds verbs, because it is going to matter enormously.

In Arabic, you can take a *noun* — a thing — and turn it into a *verb*, an action. We do this in English too, clumsily. We take the noun "to shelter" and say "they sheltered." We take "wall" and say someone "walled themselves in." When a verb is *built out of a noun like that*, the noun is still sitting inside the verb. The thing is still there, embedded in the action. You can't say "they walled themselves in" without the *wall* being physically present in the sentence's bones.

Now look at the word the Quran chooses for how these men arrived:

> **تَسَوَّرُوا — *tasawwarū***

This is a verb. But its root is **س-و-ر**, *s-w-r*, and that root is a **noun**: *sūr* — **a wall.** Not a fence, not a door, not a gate. The high outer wall of an enclosure. A barrier built precisely to keep what is outside, outside.

So *tasawwarū* is the verb made out of "wall." The most faithful rendering is something like: **"they walled-themselves-over."** They scaled the barrier. They came over the top of the very thing whose entire purpose was to stop them from coming.

Let's sit with this, because the Quran could have simply said *"they came to Dawud."* It says that in the very next ayah — *idh dakhalū ʿalā Dāwūd*, "when they entered upon Dawud." So why, in the first ayah, this strange and specific verb about climbing a wall?

Notice something. A door is the *permitted* way in. A door has a threshold, a knock, an announcement, a moment where the one inside can say *"who is there"* and prepare himself. The classical commentators note exactly this: al-Jalalayn says the men were prevented from entering through the door — *muniʿū-d-dukhūla ʿalayhi mina-l-bāb li-shughlihi bi-l-ʿibāda* — because Dawud had withdrawn for worship, and so they came over the wall instead.

The word the Quran uses for the visitors — *khaṣm*, **ٱلْخَصْمِ** — also carries a quiet grammatical strangeness. Al-Tabari notes that *khaṣm* is a verbal noun, a *masdar*, the kind that does not naturally pluralize — like the Arabic words for witness or traveler. The Quran announces them as a single word, *al-khaṣm*, and then immediately shows them as plural with *tasawwarū*, "they climbed." One singular word for multiple people. That grammatical collapse — the many folded into a grammatical one — will matter when we meet them again at the end of this element.

Here is what is remarkable. **The test did not knock.** [PAUSE]

The test did not come through the door, in daylight, announced, on the day Dawud had set aside for hearing cases. It came **over the wall**, into the one space he had sealed off for himself and his Lord, on the day he had given to God. It bypassed every defense precisely *because* it came where he felt most protected.

Stay with me, because this is the physical image pointing at something unseen.

Think about where you are spiritually ambushed. It is almost never at the front door. You are *braced* at the front door. When you walk into an obviously tempting situation, your guard is up — you knocked, you saw it coming. The thing that actually takes you down is the one that **comes over the wall** — into the space you'd marked as safe, on the day you thought you were doing everything right, dressed not as a temptation but as something with a legitimate claim on you. *"Just judge between us."* What could be more righteous than that?

The verb itself is telling you: **the trial that reaches the awwāb does not look like a trial.** It looks like a knock you're allowed to answer. Except it didn't knock. It came over the wall.

That is the first thing this single, strange word reveals — that the deepest tests enter through the breach you didn't think to guard, because you'd already classified that ground as won. [PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Al-Miḥrāb:* The Room Whose Name Means Battle

Now look at *where* they climbed into.

> **ٱلْمِحْرَاب — *al-miḥrāb***

We translate it softly: "the chamber," "the sanctuary," "the prayer-niche." And it is that — al-Jalalayn glosses it plainly as *masjiduhu*, Dawud's place of worship, his private oratory. The room where he prayed.

But listen to the root. *Miḥrāb* comes from **ح-ر-ب**, *ḥ-r-b* — and that root, in its plainest and oldest sense, means **war.** *Ḥarb* is *battle.* The very same letters that build "war" build the word for the most peaceful room in the house — the place of prayer.

Let that collision sit in your chest for a moment. The room of worship is named after **combat.** [PAUSE]

Why would the Arabs — why would the Quran — call the sanctuary by a word soaked in war?

Because the place where you stand before Allah is not a place of rest. It is the front line. The miḥrāb is where the human being does battle — not against an enemy across a field, but against the enemy that climbs over *his own* wall: the nafs, the whisper, the self that wants. The masjid of the heart is a battlefield. That is why the prophets *withdrew* to it, the way a soldier goes to the position he must hold.

Now feel the architecture of the scene the way the Quran has built it. Dawud has withdrawn into the **room of battle** to fight the only war that matters — the war within himself, in private, before his Lord. And it is into *that* room, the room he had sealed for exactly this struggle, that the test **climbs over the wall.**

Do you see what the Quran has done with two words? It has told you that **the trial finds you in the foxhole.** Not when you've laid down your arms — when you've taken them up. Not on your worst day — on the day you withdrew to be your best. The litigants don't ambush Dawud in the throne room or the marketplace. They ambush him in the *miḥrāb*, the place whose very name confesses that worship is warfare.

What this invites us to consider is uncomfortable and freeing at once: the moments you feel most spiritually secure — *"I'm in the masjid, I'm on my prayer mat, I'm in my devotion, surely I am safe here"* — those are not the moments the war pauses. The word *miḥrāb* is whispering that those are the moments the war is **most real.** The wall gets climbed in the sanctuary.

So the first word told us *how* the test comes — over the wall, unannounced. This word tells us *where* — into the very room you built to fight it. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three — The Voice We Never Hear

This third element is not a word that is present. It is a voice that is **absent** — and in the Quran, an absence placed this precisely is never an accident.

Let me first teach you how a just judgment works, because the whole moral spine of this passage hangs on it. There is a principle so basic to justice that every legal tradition on earth has some form of it: **you do not rule until you have heard the other side.** In the Islamic tradition the Prophet ﷺ taught it to ʿAli with his own tongue: when two disputants sit before you, do not judge for the first until you have heard the second. Listen to *both.* Then rule. To rule on one testimony alone is, almost by definition, to risk injustice — because you have only seen half the picture.

Now read the scene again with that principle in your hand.

Two men come. *Khaṣmān* — two disputants. One of them speaks:

> *"This, my brother, has ninety-nine ewes, and I have one ewe; yet he said: entrust her to me — *akfilnīhā* — and he overpowered me in speech — *wa ʿazzanī fi-l-khiṭāb*."*

Pause on that phrase before moving forward. *Akfilnīhā* — "entrust her to me" — is from *kafala*, the root of guardianship and surety. The powerful man didn't say *give me your sheep.* He said *make her my ward.* The language of care, of stewardship, wrapping an act of predation. And *ʿazzanī* — "he overpowered me" — is from the same root as *ʿazīz*, the Mighty. His mere status, his social gravity, crushed the weaker man's capacity to argue back. He didn't need to coerce; he simply spoke and the smaller man's words fell silent.

It is a moving complaint. Ninety-nine against one. The greedy strong man, the wronged weak man, silenced not by force but by sheer weight of presence. You can feel your own sympathy rushing to one side. And Dawud — the most just judge who ever lived — feels it too, and he rules:

> *"He has certainly wronged you in demanding your ewe in addition to his ewes…"*

Now. **Notice what is missing.** [PAUSE]

Where is the second man's voice?

We never hear it. The second litigant — the one just convicted — does not speak a single word in the entire passage. Allah, who could have given us his defense in one phrase, gives us **silence.** The accused is condemned and the text moves on, and his side of the story is a blank.

And here is the thing that makes the hair stand up: **that blank in the text is the exact shape of Dawud's mistake.** [PAUSE]

The Quran has not just told you Dawud judged hastily. It has *enacted* his haste in its own structure. The missing voice in the passage **is** the missing voice in the courtroom. You, the reader, were swept to a verdict on one testimony — and so were you made to walk, for a few seconds, in Dawud's exact footsteps. You ruled in your heart before the other man spoke. *We all did.* That is the genius of it. The absence is not a gap in the story; the absence is *the lesson, performed on you.*

This is why Dawud falls. The moment he announces his verdict, the silence of the second man strikes him — not because his ruling is factually wrong on the face of it, but because he is not just any man giving his opinion. He is the judge. And the judge's duty — the duty he has performed thousands of times — is to hear *both*. He didn't ask. He felt the pull of ninety-nine against one, the obvious wrongness of the powerful and the powerless, and he spoke.

The test didn't come as a temptation to rule *unjustly.* It came as a temptation to rule *too quickly in the name of justice.* The manipulation was elegant: wrap a trial in the language of equity itself — *"judge between us in truth, do not exceed, guide us to the even path"* — and the very righteousness of the request becomes the hook.

Before moving forward, I want to sit with the classical scholars here for a moment, because they preserve a detail that changes the architecture of this scene entirely.

Al-Tabari records — and this is the position of many of the early commentators — that these two visitors were not men at all. They were **angels.** Two angels, sent by Allah, to present Dawud with a parable he needed to hear about himself.

If that is true — and it is at least a significant strand of the classical tradition — then the second man's voice is absent for a reason that goes deeper than narrative compression. There is no second man. There was never a dispute about ewes. The ninety-nine and the one are not sheep; they are a mirror. The angels came to show Dawud a reflection of his own situation in the language of livestock, wrapped in the rhetoric of justice, so that he could see something true about himself with his own eyes — before any accusation was ever made. The ruling he delivers is his own *verdict on himself,* and he doesn't know it until the moment he finishes speaking.

That is the trial at its most elegant and most terrifying. It is not a test you can study for. It is a test that hands you the answer key in the form of a question you think you are grading for someone else.

And Dawud — the awwāb, the one always returning — saw it instantly.

### Element Four — *Ẓanna annamā fatannāhu:* The Instant He Knew

*Wa-ẓanna Dāwūdu annamā fatannāhu.*

Dawud perceived that We had only tested him.

Let's slow down and pick up that word *ẓanna* — **ظَنَّ**. In Arabic, *ẓanna* ordinarily means to suppose, to conjecture, to think something is probably true without being certain. If I say *ẓanantu anna-l-amra kadhā*, I am saying I *thought*, I *believed*, perhaps I was *guessing*. The word lives in the space between certainty and doubt.

And yet the classical commentators — and the Quran's own usage elsewhere — recognize that *ẓanna* sometimes tips over into something closer to *yaqīn*, to certainty. In a handful of precise contexts, the word carries the weight of a sudden and complete perception: not uncertain, not guessing, but a clarity that arrives in a flash, the way you suddenly *understand* something you were looking at the wrong way, and cannot go back to not seeing it.

That is how it is used here. Dawud did not *wonder whether perhaps* it might have been a test. He *realized* — all at once, without being told, in the moment — that the two figures and the case they presented and the verdict he had just pronounced were not what they appeared to be.

The word that comes after — **فَتَنَّاهُ**, *fatannāhu*, "We had tested him" — is from the root **ف-ت-ن**, *f-t-n*. And this root carries one of the most physical metaphors in the Arabic language for trial and refinement: the **fire-testing of gold.** *Fatana* is what the goldsmith does when he heats the metal to expose its impurities, to see what is real and what will burn away. The *fitna* is the test that reveals — not the test that destroys, but the test that separates what holds from what doesn't.

*Fatannāhu* — We fire-tested him.

And here is what strikes me about the construction. Allah does not say *We punished him* or *We caught him* or *We corrected him.* The word chosen is the goldsmith's word. The word of refinement. Which means: the test was not a trap. It was a furnace. Furnaces are not built to ruin the gold. They are built to purify it.

But what makes Dawud the *awwāb* he is — the *ever-returning one* — is not that he passed the test before it began. It is what happened in the two seconds after he understood.

*Fa-istaghfara rabbahu.* So he asked forgiveness of his Lord.

*Wa-kharra rākiʿan.* And fell down prostrate.

*Wa-anāba.* And turned back.

Three verbs. No interval between them. No deliberation, no waiting, no *I'll think about this and repent later.* The recognition and the return are a single motion. The understanding and the prostration are not separated by so much as a breath.

That is the speed that gives this passage its title.

### Element Five — *Wa-kharra rākiʿan:* The Fall

Let me spend a moment with the word **خَرَّ** — *kharra* — because it is not a gentle word.

*Kharra* means to fall suddenly, heavily, with the weight of the body dropping all at once. It is not the word for a graceful kneeling. It is not the controlled descent of someone who has planned to bow. It is the word for a collapse — the sound of something heavy hitting the ground. The classical lexicons give the sense of something toppling, the way a tall thing falls when it loses its balance.

Dawud *fell.* The most powerful man of his age, the judge whose verdict was law, the king who bent iron with his hands — he dropped. Not as a performance of humility, not as a ritual he observed when appropriate, but as the involuntary response of a soul that has seen something that knocks the legs out from under you.

*Rākiʿan* — the traditional translation is "bowing," from *rukūʿ*, the bow of the prayer. But notice the sajda marker in the text itself: ۩. This is a verse of prostration, a place where the reciter prostrates and the listener prostrates. The classical scholars note that *rākiʿ* here is being used — as it sometimes is in the Quran — to mean *sājid*, one who prostrates fully, face to the earth. He did not bow his head. He put his face to the ground.

There is something in that. Dawud, the king of kings, the judge of judges — face to the ground before his Lord. The physical position of prostration is the most extreme form of smallness the human body can assume: face down, the forehead touching the earth, the crown lower than everything else.

The test was designed around Dawud's greatness. His expertise in justice was the exact thing that made haste possible: the one who has judged a thousand cases accurately begins to trust the speed of his own pattern-recognition, and pattern-recognition can be manipulated. So the test used the very thing he was praised for — his wisdom, his quick perception — to catch him.

And his return used the thing that was at his very core. He was *dhal-ayd* — possessor of strength. And the strength was the strength to fall.

That is perhaps the most counterintuitive thing in this entire passage. The strength that makes a man great before Allah is not the strength that holds him upright. It is the strength that lets him hit the ground.

### Element Six — *Wa-anāba:* The Return, and the Architecture Closes

The last word of Dawud's repentance is **أَنَابَ** — *anāba*.

The root is **أ-ن-ب**, *ʾ-n-b*, and it carries a specific meaning in the Quran: to turn *toward* Allah, deliberately, completely, as a choice. It is related to but distinct from *tāba* — *tawba* is the repentance that turns *away from* the wrong; *inaba* is the turning *toward* the One you should have been facing all along. They often come together; here, the Quran singles out the *toward*.

*Wa-anāba.* And he turned back toward Allah.

And then comes the divine response. *Fa-ghafarnā lahu dhālik* — So We forgave him that. No hesitation on Allah's side either. The pattern is perfect: Dawud's recognition is instant, his return is instant, and the forgiveness is already there to receive him.

But look at what comes after the forgiveness, because the passage does not end with erasure:

> *Wa-inna lahu ʿindanā la-zulfā wa-ḥusna maʾāb.*
> "And indeed, for him is nearness to Us and an excellent place of return."

**Zulfā** — **زُلْفَى** — from the root *z-l-f*, to draw near, to come into close proximity. It is a word of intimacy, of being brought beside. After the fire-test, after the fall and the prostration and the return — his position in the sight of Allah is not simply restored to where it was. It is *closer.*

This is the Quranic grammar of transformation: the test that refined him has drawn him nearer than before he was tested.

And then the final word: *ḥusna maʾāb* — **مَآب**, the excellent place of return. Root **أ-و-ب**. [PAUSE]

Do you hear that? The very same root that described Dawud in 38:17 — *innahu awwāb*, "he was always returning" — appears now as the reward promised at the end of his story: *ḥusna maʾāb*, the beautiful destination of return.

The surah opened by calling him the *ever-returning one*. The story ends by promising him the *most beautiful place of return*. He was already a returner; the test made him a greater one; and the Word of Allah closes by saying: *the one who always returns will find, at the end, that the place he returns to is* **beautiful.**

The root is the same word at the beginning and the end. Dawud's nature and Dawud's reward are built from the same letters. He didn't earn something foreign to himself. He became, through the fire, more fully what he already was. And he was given, as his destination, the word that defined him.

That is the architecture of the passage. It is closed by the Quran with the precision of a hand returning a ring to a finger.

## PART 2: WHAT THE PASSAGE HOLDS FOR US

I want to name three things this story opens for the person who carries it home.

**On where the test finds you.**

The miḥrāb is the room whose name means battle. The test climbs the wall into the room of prayer. We already said that. But I want to stay with the practical weight of it.

One of the most common moments of spiritual harm in a person's life comes not in obvious temptation — in the moments of obvious temptation, we know to guard ourselves — but in the moments of spiritual confidence. The man who thinks he is settled in his faith, who has been righteous for long enough that he has stopped checking, who has judged correctly so many times that he trusts his first impression: that man is standing in the miḥrāb with the gate unguarded.

Expertise creates a specific vulnerability. The beginner asks questions; the expert fills in the blanks. The beginner says *tell me both sides*; the expert says *I can see where this is going.* The very things that mark genuine spiritual growth — deeper perception, faster recognition of patterns, greater confidence in one's own moral sense — are the same things that, unchecked, allow haste to masquerade as wisdom.

The men in the courtroom didn't ask Dawud to be unjust. They asked him to be just — *"judge between us with truth, do not exceed, guide us to the even path."* Everything they said was right. The problem was not the request. The problem was what Dawud did with it: he heard one side and spoke.

What did they ask for that he could have easily given them, and didn't?
A pause.
The space to hear the other man.
*One question.*

The test of the expert is not whether he can get the answer right when it's obvious. The test is whether, when the answer *feels* obvious, he still slows down to make sure.

**On the second voice.**

The second man never speaks. We already explored why. But I want to extend it outward, because this structural silence appears everywhere in our lives.

Every conflict you are pulled into has a voice you haven't heard. Every person who comes to you with a grievance about another person is presenting you with *khaṣm* without the other *khaṣm.* The story they tell may be entirely true. The wrong may be as clear as ninety-nine against one. And you may be right to feel what you feel about it.

The question is whether you *speak your verdict* before the second person has been in the room.

This is not only a principle of interpersonal justice. The Quran has hidden it in the structure of this story so that you *feel* it before you understand it — you were carried to a verdict, you felt the pull, you *knew* who was wrong — and then it invites you to notice: you did what Dawud did. Not maliciously. Not carelessly. You did it because the story was well-told and the pattern was obvious.

*That feeling* — of being pulled to a conclusion by a compelling account — is precisely the feeling to watch.

The tradition the Prophet ﷺ handed to ʿAli is exact: when two disputants sit before you, do not decide for the first until you have heard the second. What the Quran is teaching in this story is not just a rule for judges. It is a rule for the heart. The human mind will always want to resolve, to settle, to close the case. The spiritual discipline is the pause before the verdict — the deliberate act of holding the judgment open until the second voice has entered the room.

**On the speed of return.**

The last thing I want to name is the thing that gives Dawud his greatness, and I think it is often missed.

The common way to praise Dawud's repentance would be to say *despite his great sin, he repented.* But the Quran does not structure it that way. The Quran tells you Dawud's story not as a story of how terrible the fall was, but as a story of how fast the return was.

*Fa-istaghfara… wa-kharra… wa-anāba.* Three verbs, no pause, no deliberation, no interval. The moment of recognition and the act of return are one motion, like a knee that buckles the instant the weight becomes too great.

What makes a person *awwāb* — ever-returning — is not that they never fall. It is that they have trained their soul so completely in the direction of Allah that when they deviate, the deviation itself hurts, and the pain drives them back before they have time to construct a story about why they should wait.

The terrible thing about spiritual delay is that delay manufactures reasons to stay delayed. In the ten minutes after a wrong you know you've done, your soul begins building a case: it wasn't that bad, maybe I was right, maybe I should think about this more, maybe the timing isn't right. Every minute you wait gives the nafs more material to work with.

Dawud did not give it a minute. He did not give it a breath. The recognition and the prostration are a single act in the text — and I believe that is not compression. I believe that is the truth of what an *awwāb* looks like from the inside. There is no gap between knowing and returning because the soul that is truly oriented toward Allah finds the gap intolerable.

The Quran's word for the reward this earns is *zulfā* — nearness. Not restoration. *Nearness.* He ended the test closer to Allah than he began it. The fire-test did what fire-tests do: it burned away what stood between him and his Lord.

## CLOSING: THE SURAH SPEAKS TO THE PRESENT

Let me return one last time to where we started — to why this story appears here, in this surah, revealed into this moment.

The Prophet ﷺ in Makkah is surrounded by men who will not listen. Men of great worldly confidence who look at him and say: *is this the one chosen among us?* They have ninety-nine arguments and they hold them loudly. Their position is strong. Their mockery is coordinated. And the Prophet ﷺ — the man to whom this surah is addressed, the *you* of *wa hal atāka nabaʾu-l-khaṣm* — is being asked by Allah to receive this story.

What is Allah saying to him with it?

He is saying: *look at Dawud — the greatest of the strong, the possessor of might — and see what made him great. Not the ninety-nine. Not the throne. Not the swift verdict. The fall. The ground beneath the forehead. The anāba.*

He is saying: *the ones who mock you will not be mocked in return. They will be answered by something they cannot defend against: the steadfastness of a man who keeps returning.*

And He is saying to the Prophet ﷺ — and to us, reading this across fourteen centuries — that the trial does not come announced. It climbs the wall. It finds you in the miḥrāb. It wears the face of a legitimate question. And when you see it for what it is, there is only one right answer: *kharra rākiʿan wa-anāb.*

He fell. And he turned back.

The surah began by calling Dawud *awwāb* — ever-returning. It ends this passage by offering him *ḥusna maʾāb* — the beautiful return.

The one who is always returning will, at the end, arrive somewhere beautiful.

That is the promise hidden in the structure. And it is available — to the king, to the prophet, to the man surrounded by mockers, and to you, sitting with this passage now — equally, entirely, and without exception.
