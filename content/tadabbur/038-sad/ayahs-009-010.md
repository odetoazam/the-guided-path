# Who Holds the Keys to the Treasury?
### When the Ego Tries to Ration God's Grace

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the room they were spoken into.

> [ayah:38:9] أَمْ عِندَهُمْ خَزَآئِنُ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّكَ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْوَهَّابِ
> [ayah:38:10] أَمْ لَهُم مُّلْكُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ۖ فَلْيَرْتَقُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَسْبَـٰبِ

*Am ʿindahum khazāʾinu raḥmati rabbika-l-ʿazīzi-l-wahhāb. Am lahum mulku-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi wa mā baynahumā — fal-yartaqū fi-l-asbāb.*

"Or do they possess the treasuries of the mercy of your Lord — the Almighty, the Ceaseless Giver? Or do they own the dominion of the heavens and the earth, and all that lies between them? Then let them climb up by the ropes."

### Rebuild the world

To feel why this verse cuts the way it does, you have to stand in Mecca, a few years into the message, and listen to the actual complaint the leaders are making.

Go back four verses. The Quraysh elite are gathered, and they are *astonished* — not merely angry, **astonished** — that one of their own has stood up and called them to a single God. "Has he made the gods one God? This is a most strange thing" (38:5). And as they walk away, their chiefs say to one another: hold the line, stay with your gods, this is something *being engineered against us* (38:6-7).

Then comes the sentence that reveals everything. Verse 8:

> *A-unzila ʿalayhi-dh-dhikru min bayninā* —
> "Has the reminder been sent down upon **him** — out of all of us?"

Listen to where the emphasis falls. Not "is there a God." Not even "is there revelation." The stone in their throat is the word **him**. *Why him?* Out of all the men of Mecca — the older men, the wealthier men, the men of the great houses — why did the message land on Muhammad? There were men they considered more deserving. There is a verse elsewhere where they say it out loud: "Why was this Qurʾān not sent down upon a great man from the two cities?" (43:31). They had a candidate in mind. The message went to someone else.

So their disbelief, when you strip the theology away, is not really an argument about God at all. **It is an argument about distribution.** They have appointed themselves the committee that decides who is worthy of being chosen. And they have looked at the appointment and ruled it invalid.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Now here is what should stop us. When God answers this, He does not answer the way we would expect. He does not say, "He is worthy because of A, B, and C." He does not defend the choice on the merits. He asks them a question — and it is not a question about the Prophet at all. It is a question about **them**. About what they own. About whether the keys are in their pocket.

Two short questions, and then a single, devastating line of sarcasm — *climb up the ropes, then.*

What does ownership have to do with envy? What does a treasury have to do with prophethood? And why does God meet the smallest, most human pettiness — *why him and not me* — not with a rebuke about humility, but with a cosmic question about who holds the keys to the universe?

Stay with me. The answer rearranges something inside you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word أَمْ — The Particle That Turns a Statement Into a Trap

Let me teach you a small piece of Arabic before we touch the verse, because if you don't have it, the verse will pass you by as a simple question. It is not a simple question.

In Arabic there is a particle, **أَمْ** (*am*), and most of the time it functions like our English "or" — *did you take tea **or** coffee?* It sits between two options. But there is a second use, and the grammarians give it a name: **أَمِ المُنقَطِعة** — the *disjunctive* or "severing" *am*. In this second use, *am* does not connect two equal options at all. It cuts away from what came before and introduces something new with a force the grammarians say is equivalent to **بَلْ** ("rather, instead") *plus* a fresh question mark.

Here is an everyday example so you feel it. Imagine someone has been complaining for ten minutes that the manager promoted the wrong person, that the choice was unfair, that they deserved it more. And you turn to them and say:

"…Or do you own the company?"

Notice what that "or" just did. It didn't offer them a second option. It severed the entire complaint and exposed its hidden assumption. *You've been talking as if the decision were yours to make. So tell me — is the company yours?* The question is not looking for information. **The question is a trap, and the speaker already knows the answer.**

That is exactly the *am* of this verse. The grammarians have a term for this kind of question: **الاستفهام الإنكاري** — the *interrogative of denial*. It is a question shaped like a question but functioning as a flat negation. "Or do they possess the treasuries?" means, with full certainty: *they possess no such thing.*

Now open the verse. أَمْ عِندَهُمْ — *Or do they have…* And then the second one: أَمْ لَهُم — *Or do they own…* Two severing strikes in a row.

Let's sit with what those two *am*s are actually doing. They are picking up the Quraysh complaint from verse 8 — *why him and not someone of our choosing* — and they are severing it at the root. **You speak as though the appointment were yours to ratify. So let Me ask you a question.** Imam al-Qurtubi notes precisely this: that *am* here carries the sense of **التقريع** — *rebuke, reproach* — *because the speech is connected to the speech before it.* It is not a new subject. It is the answer to verse 8, and the answer takes the form of an exposed assumption.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable about answering an objection this way. God could have argued. Instead He **relocated the entire question**. The Quraysh thought they were debating *whether the choice was good*. The two *am*s reveal they were never qualified to be in that debate at all. You cannot critique the distribution of a treasury you do not own. The committee they appointed themselves to does not exist.

What this single particle reveals: **the objection to God's choices is almost never really about the choice. It is about a claim to authority that was never granted.** And the most efficient way to dismantle a false authority is not to argue with its verdicts — it is to ask, quietly, *who told you the keys were yours?*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: خَزَآئِن — The Treasury, and the Audacity of Standing at a Door That Isn't Yours

The next word is خَزَآئِن (*khazāʾin*). Let me open the root before the image, because the image is the whole point.

The root is **خ-ز-ن** (*kh-z-n*), and in the classical lexicons it means *to store, to hoard, to keep something safe under lock*. The **خِزَانة** (*khizāna*) is the storeroom, the vault, the place where precious things are held back from general circulation. A **خازِن** (*khāzin*) is the keeper of that vault — the treasurer, the one trusted with the keys. And خَزَآئِن is the plural: **the treasuries**. Not one vault — many.

So picture it concretely, the way the Arab listener would have. Not an abstraction. A great house, and deep inside it, sealed chambers. Storehouses. And in those storehouses, locked away, kept under guard, released only by the one who holds the keys — is *raḥma*. Mercy itself.

Let's teach our hearts to read this image instead of skating over it. What does it mean that mercy is kept in **treasuries**?

It means, first, that mercy is **abundant** — you do not build treasuries, plural, for something scarce. Al-Muyassar glosses it as the treasuries of *faḍl* — God's overflowing bounty. There is no shortage in the vault. The thing the Quraysh were fighting over as if it were one prize for one man is sitting in *storehouses*.

It means, second, that mercy is **owned**. A treasury has a proprietor. Things in a treasury are not lying in the open street for whoever grabs first. They are held, deliberately, by Someone — and released *by His decision*. The classical commentators — al-Jalalayn, al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi — all converge here, and it is striking how specific they get: the "mercy" in these treasuries, in this context, is **النبوة**, *prophethood*. Al-Baghawi calls them *mafātīḥ an-nubuwwa* — **the keys of prophethood** — *which they would give to whomever they pleased.* That last clause is the whole indictment. The Quraysh were behaving as though the keys were in *their* hands, to award to *their* candidate.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with me, because here is where the image turns on them.

The verse does not picture the Quraysh trying to break *into* the treasury. It pictures something subtler and more damning: it pictures them standing at the door **as if they were the treasurers**. As if the keys were already theirs. As if their job were to look at God's appointment and stamp it approved or rejected. *Am ʿindahum khazāʾin* — "Or do they have, in their possession, the treasuries…?"

And the moment you say it as a question, the absurdity collapses on them. *You? You have the storehouses of My mercy? You hold the keys?* These are men who do not control next year's rainfall, who cannot guarantee their own next breath, standing in the posture of the One who distributes prophethood across history.

Here's what's remarkable, and don't let it slip past. Notice God did not say *am ʿindahum khazāʾinī* — "the treasuries of **Mine**." He said خَزَآئِنُ رَحْمَةِ **رَبِّكَ** — "the treasuries of the mercy of **your Lord**," speaking to the Prophet ﷺ. In the very verse where they are objecting to *his* selection, God turns to him and says *your Lord*. The intimacy is placed exactly where the wound is. They asked, *why him?* And the answer includes, woven into its grammar, a quiet *because he is Mine, and the vault is Mine, and you were never the keeper.*

What this word reveals: **the things we most desperately compete over — recognition, calling, gifts, status — are not lying loose in the world to be seized. They are held in a treasury, by an Owner, released by His decision. To envy the one who received is to stand at a vault that was never yours and demand the right to sign the ledger.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْوَهَّابِ — The Two Names That Should Not Sit Together (and That's the Point)

Look at how the first verse ends. After "your Lord," two divine names, side by side: **ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْوَهَّابِ** — *al-ʿAzīz, al-Wahhāb.*

Let me open each one, and then let me show you why their pairing is the quiet climax of the whole verse.

**ٱلْعَزِيز** — *al-ʿAzīz.* The root is **ع-ز-ز** (*ʿ-z-z*). Its core sense in the lexicons gathers three threads that are really one: *might*, *unassailability*, and *rarity/preciousness*. Something *ʿazīz* is strong, cannot be overpowered, and — precisely because it is rare and hard to reach — is precious. The Arabs would describe land as *ʿazīz* when it could not be reached, a thing as *ʿazīz* when it was scarce and could not be had cheaply. So *al-ʿAzīz* is the One whose might cannot be breached, whose decisions cannot be overturned, who cannot be compelled or coerced by anyone. Al-Baghawi glosses it tightly: *al-ʿAzīz fī mulkihi* — **the Mighty in His dominion.** No one reaches over His head. No one revises His verdict.

**ٱلْوَهَّاب** — *al-Wahhāb.* The root is **و-ه-ب** (*w-h-b*), and it means *to give as a pure gift* — a **hiba**, a grant given freely, with no price, no exchange, no expectation of return. This is the most important thing about the root: a *hiba* is not a wage, not a trade, not a reward earned. It is sheer gift. And then notice the **form** of the word. It is not *wāhib* (a giver). It is **wahhāb** — the pattern فَعَّال (*faʿʿāl*), which in Arabic is **صيغة المبالغة**, the form of *intensification and abundance*. The doubled middle letter — that stress you hear, *wah-HAB* — is the sound of *giving and giving and giving.* Not one who gave once. The One who **gives ceaselessly, lavishly, by His very nature.** Al-Jalalayn and al-Baghawi both anchor it to the concrete instance at hand: He is the One who *wahaba* — bestowed, as pure gift — *prophethood upon Muhammad ﷺ.*

[PAUSE]

Now. Here is what I want you to notice, and it requires teaching one more small thing first.

In our ordinary human experience, **might and generosity pull in opposite directions.** Think about it honestly. The powerful man is the one who *withholds*. We expect strength to hoard, to guard, to extract. The one who gives freely we tend to picture as soft, as lacking the power to keep. In human life, the mighty rarely give, and the giver rarely stays mighty — give enough away and you are no longer powerful. Power and open-handedness sit at opposite ends of the table.

And God places the two names **directly next to each other**, with nothing between them: *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb.* The Unbreachable, the Lavish Giver. The One no one can compel — *and* the One who pours out gifts without being asked, without being paid, without running low.

Sit with why that pairing is impossible for a creature and natural only for God. *Why* does human power hoard? Because it is **finite**. What I give away, I lose. My strength is a fixed quantity; generosity depletes it. So the powerful protect their power by withholding. But God's might is not a fixed quantity that giving could drain. He is *al-ʿAzīz* — unassailable, undiminishable — *therefore* He can be *al-Wahhāb* without limit. **His giving costs Him nothing because nothing can reach Him.** The treasuries (from the verse before) never empty, because the Owner is *ʿazīz* — beyond depletion, beyond compulsion, beyond loss.

And now feel how the two names answer the Quraysh in a single breath:

- *al-ʿAzīz* answers their presumption: you cannot overturn My choice; My might is unbreachable; the appointment stands and you have no standing to revoke it.
- *al-Wahhāb* answers their envy: this was never a prize to be *earned* by the most qualified candidate of the two cities. It was a **gift**. A *hiba*. Given freely, by My nature, to whom I will. You were judging the selection by merit; **there was no merit committee, because it was never a wage. It was grace.**

[PAUSE]

What these two names reveal, together: **the security of mercy lies precisely in the fact that the One who gives it cannot be reached, pressured, depleted, or overruled.** A generous king who is weak will run out, or be robbed, or be forced to give to the wrong people. A mighty king who is stingy will give to no one. Only *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb* can guarantee a mercy that is both **boundless** and **safe** — flowing without end, and flowing by His decision alone, untouchable by every gatekeeper who would try to ration it.

### The bridge

So look at what these three pieces of language have built. The two **am**s severed the Quraysh's claim to be the committee. The **treasuries** exposed the absurdity of standing at a vault that was never theirs. And the two **names** revealed *why* the vault is safe and *why* its contents were never a prize to be earned: because its Owner is the Unbreachable who gives by sheer gift.

And there is one last clause we have not opened — the sarcasm of verse 10: *fal-yartaqū fi-l-asbāb*, "then let them climb up the ropes." We will come back to it in the synthesis, because it only lands once the themes are in place.

Now let's ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart, and about the One the heart keeps trying to negotiate with.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Committee in Your Chest — Why We Resent the Ones God Chooses

Let me start where you already live, far from Mecca.

There is a particular ache that almost no one admits to, and it goes like this. Someone you know — maybe someone you consider no more deserving than yourself, maybe less — receives something. The promotion. The marriage. The recognition. The ease. The opening you have been praying for, dropped into *their* lap. And before you can stop it, before any conscious thought, something in your chest tightens and says: *why them?*

Not *I'm happy for them.* Not even, at first, *I wish I had it too.* Something colder and more specific: ***why them and not me. They didn't earn it the way I would have. The selection was wrong.***

Name that feeling honestly, because the Qurʾān names it without flinching. That is **ḥasad** — envy. And the Qurʾān treats it not as a minor flaw but as one of the **engines of disbelief itself.** Read the verse before ours again: *a-unzila ʿalayhi-dh-dhikru min bayninā* — "the reminder, sent down on *him*, out of all of us?" Strip away the theology and that is just ḥasad with a robe on. The grand religious objection of the Quraysh, when you get to the bottom of it, is the same tightening in the chest you felt over a colleague's promotion. *Why him.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is what the Qurʾān does that nothing else does. It locates the **mechanism** of that envy. Look at what ḥasad secretly believes. When I resent that someone was chosen, I am — without ever saying it — sitting on a committee. I have appointed myself to a board of evaluation. I have reviewed the candidates. I have decided who *should* have received the gift. And then reality handed the gift to someone my committee did not approve.

Envy, at its root, is **a claim to a seat on a committee that does not exist.**

And this is exactly, *exactly*, what the two **am**s of our verse expose. *Or do they possess the treasuries? Or do they own the dominion?* God does not say to the envious heart, "be humble." He asks it a question about **jurisdiction**: *on whose authority is your committee seated? Where are your treasuries? Show Me your vault.* Elsewhere He says it even more nakedly — *am yaḥsudūna-n-nāsa ʿalā mā ātāhumu-llāhu min faḍlih* — "Or do they envy people for what God gave them of His bounty?" (4:54). Notice the same *am*, the same structure. **Envy is reframed, every time, as a dispute over ownership.** You are angry at a distribution. So produce your deed of title to the goods.

Sit inside this, because it is liberating once you see it. Every time you envy, you are suffering as though you were the manager of a vault — bearing the weight of distributions you did not make and cannot control, auditing a ledger that was never assigned to you. **Envy is the exhaustion of doing a job God never gave you.** It is standing at a treasury that isn't yours, furious that the keeper let the wrong man in, when *you do not even work here.*

And the moment you truly absorb the verse — *the treasuries are not yours, the dominion is not yours, the keys were never in your pocket* — something can finally **set down**. You are not on the committee. You were never on the committee. The distribution of grace across humanity is not your portfolio, not your burden, not your verdict to render. The tightening in your chest is the strain of a man trying to lift a weight that was never his to carry.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time the tightening comes — and it will come, over someone's gift you feel you deserved more — catch it, and say the verse to yourself as a question about jurisdiction. *Are the treasuries with me? Do I own the vault?* No. Then this distribution is not my file. Hand it back to the Owner. The relief is not in convincing yourself you deserved it too. The relief is in resigning from a committee you were never appointed to.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: You Don't Have to Win Mercy From the Gatekeepers

Let me bring you to the other side of the same coin, because this verse heals two wounds, not one — and the second is for the person who has been on the *receiving* end of human gatekeeping.

Here is the modern experience. Somewhere along the way, almost all of us absorb a quiet, corrosive belief: **that the good things are rationed by people, and you have to win them from those people.** The mentor decides if you're worth investing in. The community decides if you belong. The family decides if you measure up. The gatekeeper at every door — the institution, the inner circle, the one whose approval you crave — holds the key, and you stand outside performing, hoping to be let in. And when they withhold it, the message lands in the deepest part of you: *I was weighed, and found wanting. There wasn't enough, and I didn't make the cut.*

That belief is exhausting, and it is a kind of low-grade despair, and the Qurʾān demolishes it in five words: خَزَآئِنُ رَحْمَةِ رَبِّكَ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْوَهَّابِ.

Walk back through what we built. **The mercy is in treasuries** — *plural*, *abundant*, never scarce. The thing you have been told is rationed is sitting in storehouses. **The treasuries belong to *your Lord*** — not to the gatekeeper, not to the inner circle, not to the people whose approval you have been chasing. And the Owner is **al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb** — the Unbreachable who gives *as sheer gift*, by His nature, without a price and without a committee.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what each name does for the wounded one.

*al-Wahhāb* says: **the door you were standing at was never the real door.** Mercy is a *hiba*, a free gift — it was never a wage you had to earn from anyone. You have been performing for gatekeepers to win something that its actual Owner *gives away*. The Quraysh thought prophethood went to the most qualified man of the two cities; they were standing at the wrong door, auditioning for a post that was never awarded by merit. And so is anyone who believes God's mercy must be *won* from the people who guard the human gates.

And *al-ʿAzīz* says: **and no gatekeeper can intercept it.** This is the part that should make you breathe out. If the Giver were generous but weak, the gatekeepers could still get in the way — they could block the gift in transit, ration it, withhold it from the one God meant to receive it. But He is *al-ʿAzīz* — unreachable, unassailable. **No human gate stands between the Owner of the treasury and the one He decides to give to.** When *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb* opens the vault for a person, there is no committee on earth with the standing to close it. Ask the Prophet ﷺ himself — the whole assembly of Mecca's chiefs convened to reject his appointment, and the appointment stood, because the One who gave it is *ʿazīz*.

This is the staggering inversion. The very same verse that tells the *envious* "you don't own the treasury, so stop auditing the distribution" tells the *excluded* "you don't have to win it from anyone, because the One who owns it gives freely and cannot be blocked." **One truth — the keys belong only to al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb — sets the envious heart down and lifts the rejected heart up.**

Think of what falls away if you believe this. The endless performance for the approval of people who were never the keepers of anything. The despair when a human gate closes — as though that gate were the treasury itself. The verse relocates the keys *out of every human hand* and places them with the One who is both unable to run out and unable to be coerced. You are not at the mercy of the gatekeepers. You are at the mercy of *al-Wahhāb* — and that is the safest place a soul can stand.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** when a human gate closes on you — a rejection, an exclusion, a withholding of the approval you wanted — refuse the verdict it whispers. The closing of a human gate is not the emptying of the treasury. Take the request directly to the Owner. *Yā Wahhāb* — bestow it; *Yā ʿAzīz* — and let no gate stand in the way. You were never auditioning for the gatekeepers. You were always asking the Keeper of the vault.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: Two Questions and a Dare

Now stand back and see how the passage is built, because the shape is the proof.

God answers the Quraysh's *"why him?"* with a ladder of three rungs, and each rung is bigger than the last.

**Rung one — the treasuries of mercy** (verse 9). *Do you own the vault where prophethood is kept?* This is the specific charge: you are objecting to a distribution of grace as if you held the keys.

**Rung two — the dominion of the heavens and the earth** (verse 10). *am lahum mulku-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ.* Now He widens it. Forget the treasury of *mercy* for a moment — do you own the cosmos *at all*? The heavens, the earth, everything between? Because the right to distribute prophethood would belong only to the One who owns the whole estate. The argument zooms out from the single vault to the entire universe in one move.

**Rung three — the dare** (verse 10). فَلْيَرْتَقُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَسْبَٰبِ — *fal-yartaqū fi-l-asbāb.* "Then let them climb up the ropes." Let me finally open this. The verb *yartaqū* is from **ر-ق-ي** (*r-q-y*), to *ascend, climb*. And **ٱلْأَسْبَٰب** (*al-asbāb*) is the plural of *sabab* — whose root meaning in the lexicons is a **rope, a cord** — the thing you climb up by, and from there, *any means of access*. So the image is sharp and physical: *if you really own the heavens, then go ahead — find the ropes, scale them, climb up to the realm of decision and run the affairs of the cosmos yourselves. Award the prophethood to your candidate. We'll wait.* It is **أمر التعجيز** — *a command meant to expose incapacity*, a dare phrased as an order. The same way you would hand the reins to someone who keeps criticizing your driving: *fine — you steer.* And of course they cannot climb. They cannot reach the ropes. They have no access to the chamber of decision at all — which is the entire point. **The men who appointed themselves to the committee cannot even get into the building.**

### Bring it together

See now how the language and the themes are one insight from two directions.

The **linguistic** discovery was: two severing *am*s, a treasury that exposes a false claim of ownership, and two names — the Unbreachable and the Lavish Giver — that make God's mercy both boundless and safe.

The **human** discovery was: envy is the strain of sitting on a committee that doesn't exist, and despair is the strain of performing for gatekeepers who hold no keys. Both of them are cured by the *same* fact — **the keys belong only to al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb.**

They are the same sentence read from two seats. To the one who *resents* the chosen: *the vault is not yours, set down the ledger.* To the one who *aches to be chosen*: *the vault is not theirs either, so stop begging at their gate and ask the Owner.* And the dare at the end — *climb the ropes* — is the proof that seals both: no human being, envious or excluded, can reach the chamber where grace is distributed. **The keeping of the treasury is entirely, safely, beyond us — and that is not a loss. It is the best news a soul could receive.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I sitting on a committee that doesn't exist — auditing a distribution of grace, a gift someone else received, as though I held the keys to approve it?
2. Whose approval have I been performing for, as though *that person* were the keeper of the treasury — when the treasury was never in their hands at all?
3. When a human gate has closed on me, did I accept its verdict as if it were the emptying of the vault — or did I take my request to the Owner of the vault?
4. Can I hold the two names together in my own image of God — *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Wahhāb*, the Unbreachable who gives lavishly — or have I quietly assumed, like the rest of human experience teaches, that power withholds and only the weak give freely?
5. What would change in my chest, today, if I genuinely believed that the distribution of grace across every life I know is not my file to manage?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the ego presumes to ration God's grace — *why him, why them, why not me* — He answers not with a defense of His choice but with a question about ownership: *are the keys to the treasury in your hand?* — and the freedom of the believer is that they never were, because mercy is kept by the One who cannot run out and cannot be overruled.

### The Closing Invitation

Let this verse retire you from a job you were never hired for. You are not the keeper of the treasury of mercy — not over your own gifts, and not over anyone else's. You don't have to evaluate who deserved what. You don't have to win your portion from the gatekeepers. There is an Owner, and He is *al-ʿAzīz al-Wahhāb*, and the keys have never once left His hand. Resign from the committee. Step away from the gates. And bring what you long for straight to the One who gives — freely, abundantly, and beyond the reach of anyone who would tell you no.

### A Brief Du'a

*Yā ʿAzīz, yā Wahhāb* — You whose might cannot be breached and whose giving never runs dry: free our hearts from the strain of envying what You placed in other hands, and free them from begging at the gates of those who hold no keys. Teach us that Your treasuries are full, that Your gift is free, and that no one stands between You and the soul You decide to give to. Open Your vault to us — *Yā Wahhāb* — and let no gate on earth stand in the way — *Yā ʿAzīz*.
