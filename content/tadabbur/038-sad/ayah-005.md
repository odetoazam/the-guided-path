# When the One God Sounds Outrageous

## The Astonishment That Pointed the Wrong Way

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:38:5] أَجَعَلَ ٱلْـَٔالِهَةَ إِلَـٰهًا وَٰحِدًا ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَشَىْءٌ عُجَابٌ

**Transliteration:**
> *A-jaʿala-l-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidā? Inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿujāb.*

**Translation:**
> "Has he made the gods into one God? Indeed, this is a most astounding thing."

---

### Rebuilding the World

Let's go back and stand in the place where these words were first spoken — because if we don't, we'll hear them as a quaint historical complaint, the confusion of people long gone. And they are not that. They are us, on a hundred ordinary days.

Picture Mecca. Not the Mecca of pilgrimage — the Mecca of commerce, of clan, of a sanctuary ringed with idols, each one with a name, a lineage, a constituency. These were not abstractions. Al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt — these gods had real estate. They had festivals. They had families who had tended them for generations, whose identity and income and standing in the city were woven into them. To be a Meccan noble was, in part, to be a custodian of a god.

Now a man rises from among them. Not a stranger, not a foreigner — one of their own, of impeccable lineage, the one they had called *al-Amīn*, the trustworthy. And the classical commentators preserve the moment that lit the fuse. Among the narrations — al-Baghawi reports it in detail — the spark was the conversion of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb. It shook Quraysh. The believers rejoiced; the chiefs panicked. And so the assembly of leaders — al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra at their head, twenty-five of the most powerful men of the city by Baghawi's account — went to Abu Talib to demand he restrain his nephew.

And what was the unbearable thing? What was the demand that could not be tolerated?

Listen to verse four, the verse right before ours. *Wa ʿajibū an jāʾahum mundhirun minhum* — "and they marveled that a warner came to them from among themselves." They were astonished. And then verse five tells us the content of that astonishment, the exact shape of the offense:

**"Has he made the gods into one God?"**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the emotional temperature of that line. It is not a question seeking information. It is the sound of a man who has heard something so far outside his world that the only response is a kind of scandalized laughter. *Can you believe this? He wants to take all of them — all of our gods, the whole settled order of the heavens — and crush them down into one?*

To them, this was not theology. It was demolition.

---

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to carry into the next hour. These men were astonished. The verse is built, root and branch, around their astonishment — and we'll see in a moment that the astonishment is even more tightly woven into the words than it first appears.

But their astonishment was aimed at the wrong thing.

They marveled that the many should become One. They did not marvel that there had ever been many. They found the truth outrageous and the lie ordinary.

And the deepest question this ayah will ask us is not *were they wrong?* — of course they were wrong. The question is: **what have we made ordinary that should astonish us, and what astonishes us that is in fact the plainest truth there is?** Because the disease in this verse is not a seventh-century disease. It is the permanent reflex of a heart that has settled into the wrong shape.

Let's open the words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### Element One: The Question That Isn't a Question

Before we touch the ayah, let me put something ordinary in front of you.

Imagine a friend tells you he's quitting his stable job to go paint. And you say: *"You're giving up the salary? To paint?"* Now — are you asking him? Do you not know what he just told you? Of course you know. You heard him perfectly. The question is not a request for information. The question *is* your verdict. The grammar is a question; the meaning is "this is insane." You've dressed a judgment up as an inquiry, because a question carries more contempt than a statement. A statement says "I disagree." A question says "I can't even believe I have to take this seriously."

Arabic has a precise name for this. It's called *istifhām inkārī* — the interrogative of denial. A question whose real function is not to ask but to reject, to mock, to express astonishment that the thing could even be proposed.

Now look at how the ayah opens:

> **أَجَعَلَ** — *A-jaʿala* — "Has he made...?"

That tiny letter at the front — the *hamza*, the *a-* — is the interrogative. And everything about the context tells us this is not a sincere question. These are not students raising their hands. This is the *istifhām inkārī* in its purest form: a question fired like a weapon.

Notice what they did *not* say. They did not say "He has made the gods into one God, and he is wrong." A flat denial would have granted the claim the dignity of being a claim. Instead they reach for the question, because the question lets them perform their disbelief. **The interrogative is the sound of a worldview refusing to bend even far enough to argue.**

And here is the thing I want you to feel. There is a kind of rejection that is more closed than mere disagreement. To disagree, you must first let the idea in — you must hold it, examine it, find it wanting. But the mocking question keeps the idea out at arm's length. It never lets it in at all. It says: *I will not even host this thought long enough to refute it.*

[PAUSE]

How many of us have done exactly this — not with the message of the Prophet ﷺ, but with a hard truth about ourselves? Someone names the thing we most need to hear, and instead of letting it in to wrestle with it, we deflect with the scandalized question: *"You think I'm the problem?"* The question is a door we hold shut. It feels like engagement. It is the opposite of engagement.

That one letter at the front of the verse — *a-* — is the whole posture of a heart that has decided in advance.

---

### Element Two: The Verb That Transforms — *jaʿala* and Its Two Objects

Now let's go to the engine of the sentence. The verb.

> أَجَعَلَ **ٱلْءَالِهَةَ إِلَٰهًۭا وَٰحِدًا**
> *...jaʿala-l-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidā*

First, a small grammatical idea that unlocks everything here.

In English, some verbs take one object — "I saw the house." But some verbs take *two* objects, and when they do, they don't mean "I did something to two things." They mean "I turned the first thing *into* the second thing." Think: "They *named* the baby Yusuf." Baby and Yusuf are not two separate things acted upon. The sentence means: the baby *became* Yusuf. Or: "She *painted* the wall blue" — the wall *became* blue. Two objects, but one transformation.

The Arabic verb *jaʿala* — when it takes two objects like this — works exactly this way. The classical grammarians and the mufassirūn flag it precisely. Al-Qurtubi, commenting on this very ayah, says plainly: *mafʿūlān* — "two objects" — and then glosses the meaning: *ṣayyara-l-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidā*, "he *converted* the gods into one God." Not "he placed the gods and a God side by side." He *transmuted* them. Took the many and rendered them one.

So the accusation in their mouths is not "he believes in a different god." The accusation is **transformation**. *He is taking our gods — and doing something to them. He is melting them down.*

Now feel the violence in that, from where they stood. Their gods were not a list. They were a structure — a whole architecture of the cosmos, each deity with a domain, a clientele, a place in the order. And the verb *jaʿala* with its two objects says: he wants to take this entire intricate structure and collapse it into a single point.

[PAUSE]

But stay with me, because here the verse does something the careless reader walks straight past.

Look at the two objects again. Look at how they are dressed.

The first: **ٱلْءَالِهَةَ** — *al-ālihata* — "**the** gods." Definite. With the *al-*, the "the." And plural.

The second: **إِلَٰهًۭا وَٰحِدًا** — *ilāhan wāḥidā* — "**a** god, one." Indefinite. No "the." Singular.

Do you see what their grammar has done? In the mouths of the deniers, **the many gods are *the* gods — definite, settled, presupposed, the established furniture of reality.** And **the One God is *a* god — indefinite, novel, the strange newcomer, one option being floated.**

[PAUSE]

Turn that over. Their language has perfectly inverted the truth.

In reality, the One is the eternal, the necessary, the foundation of all being — the most "definite" reality there is. And the many are the invented, the contingent, the named stones. But in the *grammar of a heart settled in shirk*, it comes out backwards: the invented many are spoken of as *the* established fact, and the eternal One is reduced to *a* curious proposal. The definite article migrates to the lie. The indefinite clings to the truth.

This is not me reading something into the case-endings. This is what the words *are*. *Al-ālihata* — definite. *Ilāhan* — indefinite. And that asymmetry is a window straight into the diseased heart: **it treats what it is used to as reality itself, and treats reality itself as a novelty to be examined.**

We do this. We treat our scattered loyalties — career, image, comfort, the opinion of people — as simply "the way things are," the definite furniture of life. And we treat the total claim of the One God upon us as *a* consideration, *an* option, something to slot in among the others. The grammar of the deniers is alive in us whenever the world is "the" and God is merely "a."

[PAUSE]

One more thing before we leave the verb, and it is quiet but it matters. *Wāḥidā* — "one." The root is *wHd*, oneness. They could not even say the word without testifying to the very thing they were rejecting. The denial of *tawḥīd* cannot be uttered without using the word for One. The truth is so woven into reality that even the mouth that rejects it must borrow its vocabulary.

What this single verb reveals: **the call of Islam is not the addition of one more god to the shelf. It is the dismantling of the shelf.** And a heart built around the shelf will experience that dismantling as an assault.

[PAUSE — let that settle]

---

### Element Three: *ʿUjāb* — The Word That Overshoots Wonder

Now to the last word of the ayah, and it is the one that will tie the whole thing together.

> إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَشَىْءٌ **عُجَابٌ**
> *Inna hādhā la-shayʾun ʿujāb*
> "Indeed, this is a thing most astounding."

Look first at how heavily this verdict is armored. *Inna* — "indeed," the particle of emphasis. Then *hādhā* — "this." Then the *lām* of further emphasis on *la-shayʾun* — "certainly a thing." They are not muttering. They are pounding the table. *Indeed — this — is certainly — a thing —* and then the final word lands: *ʿujāb*.

Now we need to feel the morphology of that last word, because the entire point lives inside its shape.

The ordinary Arabic word for "amazing," "wondrous," is *ʿajīb* — عَجِيب. You'd use it for a beautiful sunset, a strange coincidence, a remarkable story. Common, everyday wonder.

But they did not say *ʿajīb*. They reached for a heavier mold. *ʿUjāb* — عُجَاب — on the intensive pattern *fuʿāl*. And the classical lexicographers mark the difference with surgical care. Al-Qurtubi quotes al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad — the founder of Arabic lexicography himself — drawing the exact line: *al-ʿajīb* is that which amazes; but *al-ʿujāb* is **that which has passed beyond the limit of amazement** — *alladhī qad tajāwaza ḥadda-l-ʿajab*. Al-Khalīl gives a parallel: *ṭawīl* means "long," but *ṭuwāl* means "so long it exceeds the bounds of length." The intensive pattern doesn't just raise the volume. It says *off the scale*. *Beyond what wonder can hold.*

So this is not "how curious." This is: **"This is monstrous. This is beyond the bounds of the believable. This breaks the meter of reality itself."**

[[PAUSE]]

And now hear the sound of it. *ʿU-jāb.* It opens on the *ʿayn* — that deep, constricted, guttural sound made far back in the throat, a sound that seems to come from somewhere effortful and strained. Then *jīm*, then the long, gaping *āb*. The word itself is a little difficult to say; it has a heaviness, a strain, a thing-too-big-for-the-mouth quality. The phonetics enact the meaning. They chose a word that *sounds* like a thing that overflows its container.

Now — here is the architecture. Here is why I asked you to remember verse four.

Verse four opened with: *wa **ʿajibū*** — "and they **marveled**." Root: *Ejb*.
Verse five closes with: *la-shayʾun **ʿujāb*** — "a thing most **astounding**." Root: *Ejb*.

[PAUSE]

The same root frames the entire passage. The two verses are bracketed, top and bottom, by wonder. *They marveled... a thing astounding.* The whole episode is a chamber of astonishment, and the root *Ejb* is the walls.

And so the verse forces the question on us — the question that is the heart of this whole reflection: **astonished at *what*?**

They were astounded that the gods should be made One. They poured the heaviest word in their vocabulary — *ʿujāb*, the off-the-scale word — onto *the truth*.

But where was their astonishment when there were many? Where was the *ʿujāb* at the spectacle of grown men carving a stone, naming it, and then begging it for rain? Where was the wonder that the very things they worshipped could not create a fly, could not defend themselves from a falling lamp, were dusted by the same servants who swept the floor? That — *that* — is the thing that should break the meter of reality. That should be the *ʿujāb*.

They aimed the strongest word they had at the One truth, and felt nothing at the ocean of absurdity they swam in every day.

**This is the precise anatomy of a misled heart: not the absence of wonder, but wonder pointed exactly backwards.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So look at what these eight words have shown us. A question that refuses to let the truth in the door. A verb of transformation whose own grammar betrays the inversion — the many made definite, the One made a stranger. And a word of astonishment, sounded twice across two verses, aimed with full force at the very thing that should have ended their astonishment forever.

The construction is a perfect X-ray of a particular soul: settled in the many, scandalized by the One, marveling in the wrong direction.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: Why the One Is Harder Than the Many

Let me start where you live, not where the ayah is.

Think about loyalty, and how we actually arrange our lives. Most of us, if we're honest, run on a kind of *distributed portfolio* of allegiances. A bit of ourselves invested in the career. A bit in how we look. A bit in our reputation. A bit in our family, our nation, our comfort, our pleasures. And there is a quiet, powerful logic to spreading ourselves out like this. It feels *safe*. If one investment fails — the job is lost, the looks fade, the reputation takes a hit — the others still stand. No single loss can level you. Diversification is wisdom in finance, and we instinctively apply it to the soul.

A portfolio of gods is *comfortable*. It hedges. No one of them owns all of you, so no one of them can demand all of you. You stay, in a sense, the manager of the fund. You allocate. You remain in control.

Now you can feel, can't you, the real terror underneath the Meccans' question. *"Has he made the gods into one God?"* This was not an intellectual difficulty. It was an existential one. To collapse the many into One is to put **everything in a single hand.** It is to surrender the diversification. It is to say: there is no hedge. There is no portion of me I am keeping back, no corner of loyalty held in reserve. **One.**

And the Qur'an knows this is the real difficulty. Listen to how it puts the same choice in the mouth of Yusuf, al*ayhi al-salām, speaking to two prisoners — verse 39 of his own surah:

> *"O my two companions of the prison, are diverse lords better, or Allah, the One, the Subjugator?"*
> *(a-arbābun mutafarriqūna khayrun ami-llāhu-l-wāḥidu-l-qahhār.)*

*Arbābun mutafarriqūn* — "scattered, divided lords." That word *mutafarriqūn* — fragmented, dispersed. Yusuf names the exact appeal: the lords are *many and scattered*, and there is a comfort in scatter. And against it he sets *al-wāḥidu-l-qahhār* — the One, the Overwhelming.

And here is the movement I want you to make with me. The Meccans heard "One God" and felt it as a *loss* — a stripping away, a demolition of their rich and varied heavens. But turn it over.

To serve many masters is not freedom. It is to be torn. The Qur'an gives us the image directly — Surah 39, verse 29 — of a man owned by several quarreling partners, each pulling him a different way, against a man belonging wholly to one. Which of them, the verse asks, is at peace? The man with many owners is not *more* free for having more owners. He is shredded among them. Every one of them has a claim, and the claims contradict, and he spends his life being pulled apart.

[PAUSE]

This is the great inversion the ayah exposes. They thought the many gave them *more* — more options, more safety, more selves. In truth the many gave them *less*: less peace, less wholeness, a self divided into warring shares. And the One they thought would *take* everything — the One is the only thing that gives a person back to himself. Whole. Unfragmented. Belonging in one direction.

This is why the convert often weeps at the *shahāda*, and the lifelong Muslim sometimes forgets why. *Lā ilāha illā Allāh* is not the subtraction of all the gods down to one. It is the gathering of a scattered soul into a single, restful, unbreakable belonging.

The Meccans were right that *tawḥīd* costs everything. They were only wrong about what it buys.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Notice, this week, the *mutafarriq* in you — the scatter. Catch the moments where you are serving competing masters: doing the deed for God *and* for the eyes of people, praying *and* protecting your image, generous *and* keeping score. That doubling, that being-pulled-two-ways, is the small daily texture of the many gods. And each time you catch it, you can make one small motion of consolidation — *for You alone* — and feel, even slightly, the scattered pieces draw back toward one center. That is *tawḥīd* not as a doctrine but as a practice of becoming whole.

[PAUSE]

---

### Theme Two: The Wonder Pointed Backwards

Now the second movement, and we begin again where you actually live.

There is a strange thing that happens to the human eye. Whatever it sees constantly, it stops seeing. The most extraordinary facts, if they are *familiar*, become invisible. You do not marvel at your own heartbeat — eighty times a minute, your whole life, without a single instruction from you. You don't marvel that the sun came up. You marvel at the *new* thing, the *strange* thing — and you go numb to the standing miracle precisely because it always stands there.

This is a mercy in small things. We couldn't function if we gasped at every breath. But applied to the great things, it becomes the most dangerous spiritual condition there is. **It means our sense of wonder — our *ʿujāb* — is calibrated by familiarity, not by truth.** We are astonished by the novel falsehood and bored by the eternal fact.

And this is *exactly* the disease the ayah diagnoses. Remember the architecture: *ʿajibū* in verse four, *ʿujāb* in verse five. Astonishment, top and bottom. They had wonder. The faculty was working. It was simply aimed backwards.

They found it *ʿujāb* — beyond the bounds of belief — that the many should be One. They found it perfectly normal that a man should pour his fear and hope into a thing he had carved with his own hands that morning. **The lie had become wallpaper. The truth had become an outrage.** Because the lie was familiar and the truth was new to them.

[PAUSE]

Now turn the mirror, gently, on ourselves — because this is not a verse about *them*.

What do we find *ʿujāb* today? What strikes the modern ear as strange, excessive, "too much"?

A young person who actually structures their day around five prayers — *that's* striking, *that's* a bit much, isn't it? Someone who won't take the interest, won't tell the convenient lie, won't be alone with what's forbidden — we feel a flicker of *ʿujāb*: how intense, how rigid, why so extreme? Total devotion to the One reads, to a fragmented age, as fanaticism. As the off-the-scale thing.

And what have we made wallpaper? A civilization that worships markets, images, the self, the algorithm — a thousand scattered *arbābun mutafarriqūn* with new names — and *that* is simply "the way things are." Definite. *Al-ālihata.* The settled furniture. We have made the worship of the many ordinary and the worship of the One strange. We have, with breathtaking precision, **reproduced the grammar of Surah Sad verse five** — the lie is definite, the truth is *a* curiosity — and we did not even notice we were quoting the deniers.

[PAUSE]

So the work of this verse on us is not to feel superior to the Meccans. It is to *recalibrate the instrument*. To take our sense of wonder down off its broken setting and reset it to truth.

Because once you reset it, the world detonates with the right astonishment. *That* — that I exist at all, that I was nothing and am now a self that can love and choose and turn back. *That* — that the One who needs nothing addresses me by revelation, sends a warner *minhum*, from among ourselves, lowering Himself to my level so I could understand. *That* — that I have spent years pouring my heart's fear and hope into things that cannot hold it, into reputation and provision and the opinions of people, while the One who actually holds everything stood waiting. *That* is the *ʿujāb*. That is the off-the-scale thing.

The Meccans were not lacking in wonder. They were lacking in *aim*. And so, often, are we.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Once a day — just once — catch yourself in an act of unconscious worship of the many. The reflexive check of how many people liked the thing. The flash of fear about money that you'd never feel about your standing with God. The instant you feel that, name it: *this is the wallpaper. This is what I've made ordinary.* And then deliberately move your wonder to where it belongs — pick one true thing, the smallest is enough, and let it actually astonish you. The heartbeat you didn't order. The verse that found you. The breath you're taking right now that you did not arrange. Reset the instrument, one act of wonder at a time, until the truth becomes astounding again and the lie becomes strange.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

---

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape of it, because the two verses are built as a single trap, and the trap is sprung by one root.

Verse four: *wa **ʿajibū** an jāʾahum mundhirun minhum* — "and they **marveled** that a warner came from among themselves." (Root: *Ejb*.)
Verse five: *...la-shayʾun **ʿujāb*** — "a thing most **astounding**." (Root: *Ejb*.)

The passage is sealed at both ends by astonishment. And between the two seals lies the content of the astonishment: *a-jaʿala-l-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidā* — the making of the many into One.

The structure *is* the argument. By framing the rejection of *tawḥīd* inside a double frame of wonder, the Qur'an is doing something merciless and quiet: it is showing you a faculty in perfect working order, aimed in the perfectly wrong direction. The deniers are not portrayed as dull. They are portrayed as *amazed* — fully alive to wonder — and that is precisely the indictment. **It is not that they could not feel astonishment. It is that they spent it all on the truth and saved none for the lie.**

---

### Bringing It Together

See now that the linguistic discoveries and the two themes are one insight, approached from two sides.

The grammar showed us the inversion: *the* gods, definite and settled; *a* God, indefinite and strange. The first theme showed us *why* a heart inverts like that — because the many feel safe and the One feels total, and a soul will call demolition what is in fact its only path to wholeness.

The morphology showed us *ʿujāb* — wonder overshooting its bounds, aimed backwards. The second theme showed us *that this is us* — a faculty of wonder miscalibrated by familiarity, astonished by devotion and bored by idolatry, reproducing the deniers' own grammar without noticing.

One insight, two doors: **a heart settled in the many will find the One outrageous, and will pour its wonder on exactly the wrong thing.** The cure for both is the same single motion — the gathering of the scattered self toward one center, which is also the resetting of wonder onto what is true.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life are the scattered gods *definite* — simply "the way things are" — while the One God is merely *a* consideration I fit in among them?

2. What do I find *ʿujāb* — excessive, intense, "too much" — when I see it in a devoted believer? And what does that flinch reveal about where my own wonder has gone numb?

3. Which masters are currently pulling me in contradictory directions, and what would it feel like to belong, in that exact matter, to One?

4. When a hard truth about myself comes to the door, do I let it in to wrestle — or do I hold the door shut with a scandalized question?

5. If I reset the instrument of my wonder to truth tonight, what is the first thing that would astonish me all over again?

### The Closing Invitation

This is not, in the end, a verse about ancient Mecca. It is a mirror held up to the permanent reflex of the human heart — the reflex to make the lie ordinary and the truth strange, to spread itself thin among many small masters because the One feels like too much to give.

The invitation is not to condemn the men who said these words. It is to notice how easily their words become ours — every time the world is *the* and God is merely *a*, every time devotion strikes us as excessive and distraction as normal. And then, gently, to begin the one motion that answers all of it: to gather the scattered self back toward its center, and to let the One God become astounding again, and the many gods strange.

You don't have to do it all at once. You only have to catch the inversion once, today, and turn it the right way around.
