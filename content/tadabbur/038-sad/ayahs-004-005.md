# The Wonder That Condemned Them

## Surah Sad, Ayahs 4-5

> [ayah:38:4] وَعَجِبُوٓا۟ أَن جَآءَهُم مُّنذِرٌ مِّنْهُمْ ۖ وَقَالَ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ هَـٰذَا سَـٰحِرٌ كَذَّابٌ
>
> [ayah:38:5] أَجَعَلَ ٱلْـَٔالِهَةَ إِلَـٰهًا وَٰحِدًا ۖ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَشَىْءٌ عُجَابٌ

*Wa ʿajibū an jāʾahum munḏirun minhum, wa qāla-l-kāfirūna hāḏā sāḥirun kaḏḏāb. A-jaʿala-l-ālihata ilāhan wāḥidā, inna hāḏā la-shayʾun ʿujāb.*

**"And they marveled that a warner had come to them from among themselves. And the disbelievers said, 'This is a magician, a relentless liar. Has he made the gods into one God? Truly this is an astounding thing.'"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Room Before the Words

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand inside the room where these words were spoken.

Makkah. A small, dense, tightly woven society where everyone knows everyone. There are no strangers here. The man now standing before them is not a wanderer who arrived on a caravan from some far country with a strange accent and a strange face. They have known him for forty years. They have watched him grow from a boy into a young man into the most trusted person in the entire valley. They gave him a name with their own mouths — *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy. When two tribes nearly went to war over who would lift the Black Stone, it was *his* judgment they all submitted to. They had handed him their disputes, their valuables, their secrets.

And now this same man — this known quantity, this familiar face — has begun to say something that splits the air of the city in two.

Here is what I want you to feel before we go further. When the message came, you would expect their objection to be about the *message*. You would expect them to argue theology — to say, "Your claims about God are wrong," or "Your account of the afterlife is mistaken." That would be an argument you could have.

But that is not where they begin.

[PAUSE]

They begin somewhere stranger. The very first thing the Quran records about their reaction is not an argument at all. It is an *emotion*. **They marveled.** They were *astonished*. And what astonished them was not the content of the warning — it was the *location* it came from. It came from *among themselves*.

Sit with how odd that is. The thing that should have made the message easiest to accept — *we know this man, he has never once lied to us* — became the very thing they could not get over.

And here is the hook, the question I want burning in you for the next hour: **Why would the most trustworthy man becoming a warner make people *more* resistant, not less? And why, of everything he said, was it the call to *one* God that struck them as the single most outrageous, unswallowable thing they had ever heard?**

The Quran is about to show us that their astonishment was not a reaction to the message. It was a confession about themselves. Let's go find it.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: مِّنْهُمْ — "From Among Themselves"

### Teaching the idea first

Let me start away from the ayah, with something you already know in your bones.

There is a strange law in human life: the closer wisdom comes to us, the harder it is to receive. A stranger tells you a hard truth and you consider it. Your own brother tells you the exact same truth and you bristle. The counselor on a screen says, "You need to forgive your father," and you nod. Your spouse says it at the kitchen table and you snap, "You don't understand."

We have a phrase for this. *No prophet is accepted in his hometown.* Familiarity, somehow, lowers the value of what is said. When the source is too close, the ego finds a way to discount it.

Hold that. Now look at the word.

### Opening the word

The ayah says a warner came to them — *munḏirun* — and then one word: **مِّنْهُمْ**, *minhum*. "From among them." From their own ranks. One of them.

Notice first the shape of *munḏir* itself. It comes from the root *n-dh-r*, and it is built on a form (Form IV, *anḏara*) whose work is to *deliver* something to someone — here, to deliver a warning of danger to a person who does not see it coming. A *munḏir* is not a debater and not a poet. He is the one who runs ahead of the flood shouting that the water is rising. His entire function is *care under pressure of time*. You do not warn people you are indifferent to. **The very word carries concern inside it.**

So the picture is: a man whose role is pure warning — pure "I am trying to save you" — and he is *minhum*, one of their own.

### Deepening

Now, why does the Quran bother to tell us he is *from among themselves*? Strip the word away. "They marveled that a warner had come to them." That sentence is complete. The message would stand without *minhum*.

So its presence is doing work. What work?

Here is the thing. *Minhum* is, on its face, a word of **mercy**. Allah is reminding them — and us — of a kindness. He did not send them an angel they could not look at, or a being from outside their experience whose every word would feel alien. He sent a warner *they already trusted*, speaking *their* language, carrying *their* memories, who understood their winters and their griefs from the inside. Elsewhere the Quran makes this explicit: *Allah conferred a favor upon the believers when He raised among them a Messenger from themselves* (3:164). **Minhum is a gift.**

And al-Jalālayn, glossing the word *mundhir* in this very verse, is precise about what the gift was warning them *toward*: the Prophet warned them *wa-yukhauwifuhum al-nāra baʿda al-baʿth* — of the fire after resurrection. That future-ness matters. To heed such a warning required trusting someone about a reality that had not yet arrived, a domain the eye cannot reach. God, in His mercy, ensured that the voice carrying this enormous invisible warning was the most visible, most trusted, most verifiable face in the city. The familiarity of the messenger was the guarantee for the unknowability of the message.

[PAUSE]

And they marveled at the gift as though it were an insult.

Stay with this, because it is the whole hinge. The exact feature that should have made belief *easiest* — *he is one of us, we know his face, we named him the Trustworthy* — is the exact feature their astonishment latched onto. They did not say, "How merciful that the warner is one we already trust." They said, in effect, "How *strange* that he should be one of us." The mercy registered to them as an affront.

Why? Because a warner from outside could be kept at a distance. A warner you can frame as exotic, as Other, makes no claim on your daily life. But a warner *from among yourselves* — a man who ate what you ate and walked where you walk and is in every visible way your equal — that man's message cannot be quarantined as "for other people." If *he* must bow to this truth, so must *you*. There is no escape hatch of difference. **The nearness that should have been a comfort became a cornering.**

This is the first thing *minhum* reveals: that the human ego, when it does not want a truth, will turn the very evidence *for* that truth into its grievance against it. The closeness that proves the mercy becomes the reason to reject the mercy.

What this single word reveals: **God placed His warning in the most familiar voice they had — and they used its familiarity as the excuse to dismiss it.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: وَقَالَ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ — When the Naming Is the Verdict

### Teaching the idea first

In ordinary speech, once you have named someone, you switch to a pronoun. "*Aisha* opened the door, and *she* sat down." You would never say, "*Aisha* opened the door, and *Aisha* sat down" — unless you were doing something deliberate. Repeating the full name where a pronoun would do is a signal. We do it to be emphatic, or formal, or to *brand* — "The *manager* approved it, and the *manager* will answer for it." The second naming lays responsibility on the title itself.

Classical Arabic rhetoric has a name for exactly this move: *waḍʿ al-ẓāhir mawḍiʿ al-muḍmar* — putting the **explicit noun** where a **pronoun** belongs. And the great commentators flag it whenever it appears, because it is never accidental.

### Opening the feature

Look at the grammar of our ayah. It opens: *wa ʿajibū* — "and **they** marveled." A hidden pronoun. *They.* The subject is carried inside the verb. So far, normal.

Then it continues: *wa qāla-**l-kāfirūn**a* — "and **the disbelievers** said."

[PAUSE]

Stop. By every rule of natural speech it should have said *wa qālū* — "and **they** said." The subject was already established two words earlier. The pronoun would flow. Instead the Quran lifts up a full, heavy, explicit noun and sets it down in the sentence: *al-kāfirūn* — **the disbelievers**.

Imām al-Jalāl, in his tafsīr, pauses on this very spot and names it precisely: *fīhi waḍʿ al-ẓāhir mawḍiʿ al-muḍmar* — "in it, the explicit noun is placed where the pronoun would be."

Al-Ṭabarī, commenting on the same grammatical choice, gives us the classical content of that label: those called *al-kāfirūn* here are *al-munkirūna waḥdāniyyata Allāh* — specifically, those who deny the oneness of God. The title in his reading is not diffuse unbelief; it carries a precision. The grammar brands this posture at the exact moment it opens its mouth, and the brand names what is being refused: tawhid itself.

### Deepening

So what is the effect?

Watch the two halves of the sentence. In the first half they are simply *they* — neutral, unnamed. *They marveled.* At that point they are just people, reacting, astonished. You could almost feel for them.

Then the sentence does something surgical. The moment they open their mouths to call the most truthful man a magician and a liar, the Quran *refuses* to let them stay anonymous. It will not say "*they* said." It strips away the soft pronoun and stamps the title on them: **the disbelievers said.**

Do you feel what just happened? The naming is the **verdict**. The grammar is telling you: *this utterance is not a passing opinion — this is what kufr sounds like.* To accuse the Trustworthy of being a liar is not a debating position; it is the very act that earns the name. The label is fastened to them at the exact instant of the slander, so that the words and the title become inseparable. **You want to know what disbelief is? Listen to what the disbelievers just said. That. That is what it is.**

[PAUSE]

And notice the mercy hidden even in the severity. By switching to the explicit noun, the Quran subtly separates the *act* from every individual heart. It does not say "Abū so-and-so said." It says *the disbelievers* — as a category, as a posture. The door is left open: stop saying this, and you are no longer described by it. The name is attached to the *stance*, not chained to the *person* forever. But while you stand in that stance, the grammar will name you by it.

What this feature reveals: **the Quran will let you remain an anonymous "they" while you merely wonder — but the instant you call truth a lie, it names you, because that act *is* the definition of the name.**

## Element Three: عُجَاب and كَذَّاب — The Intensive Forms, and the Ring of Wonder

### Teaching the idea first

Arabic can take a plain word and *crank up its volume* by reshaping it. This is called a *ṣīghat al-mubālagha* — a form of intensification. Take the simple word for "one who lies," *kāḏib*. Reshape it onto the pattern *faʿʿāl* — *kaḏḏāb* — and you no longer have "a liar." You have a *prodigious* liar, a *habitual* liar, a man whose whole craft is lying, lying as a profession. The doubled middle letter is like turning a dial: the meaning gets louder, denser, more relentless.

Hold onto that dial. We are going to watch them turn it.

### Opening the words — first, the accusation

The disbelievers say: *hāḏā sāḥirun kaḏḏāb* — "This is a magician, a liar."

Look closely, because the two words are not built the same way. *Sāḥir* — "magician" — is a plain active participle. Just "one who does *siḥr*." But *kaḏḏāb* — that is the dial turned to maximum. Not *kāḏib*, "a liar." **Kaḏḏāb.** A relentless, master, professional liar.

Now hold the two charges side by side and feel the contradiction.

A *sāḥir*, a magician, is someone whose words carry *real apparent power* — he bewitches, he produces effects, people are moved and swayed by what he does. That is precisely why, elsewhere, they feared his message would "separate a man from his brother." A magician's speech *works*.

A *kaḏḏāb*, a mere liar, is someone whose words carry **no power at all** — he is just making things up, empty, to be ignored.

[PAUSE]

So which is he? Is his speech so powerful it bewitches you, or so empty it deceives no one? **He cannot be both.** A man whose words have hypnotic force is not a man whose words are weightless lies. They have thrown two charges that cancel each other out — and that is exactly the signature of a heart that has already decided. When you have resolved to reject something *before* examining it, you do not need your reasons to be consistent. You only need them to be *many*. You grab every stone within reach and throw them all, even the ones that knock each other out of the air. **Incoherence is the fingerprint of motivated denial.**

### The ring closes

Now lift your eyes to the bracket around the whole passage. This is the structure the words have been building toward.

The passage *opens* with their reaction: *wa **ʿajibū*** — "and they **marveled**." Root *ʿ-j-b*: wonder, astonishment.

And the passage *closes*, one ayah later, with their own verdict on the message: *inna hāḏā la-shayʾun **ʿujāb*** — "truly this is an **astounding** thing." Same root, *ʿ-j-b* — but now turned onto the intensive pattern *fuʿāl*. *ʿUjāb* is not just "wondrous." It is *staggeringly, outrageously* wondrous. The dial again, turned to its limit.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the trap they have walked into? The passage is a **ring**, and the same root forms both ends of it. They **marvel** at the beginning (*ʿajibū*) — and the thing they declare *outrageously* marvelous at the end (*ʿujāb*) is... the oneness of God.

And here is the devastating turn. Look at how they pile up their emphasis on that final word. *Inna* — "truly." *La-* — the lām of emphasis — "indeed." *ʿUjāb* — the intensive. **Three layers of emphasis pressed upon a claim so bedrock, so unadorned, that the Quran offers it without elaborate proof — only the announcement itself: *the gods are one God*.**

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar, glossing this verse, renders their shock in a single devastating phrase: *kayfa yusayyiru al-ālihata al-kathīrata ilāhan wāḥidā* — "how does he *transform* the many gods into a single God?" The verb *yusayyiru* is Form II of *ṣāra*: not merely "reduce," but *cause to become*, *effect a change upon*. This is not the grammar of theological disagreement. In their hearing, tawhid was an *act being done to them* — a contraction of everything they had organized their world around into a single unmanageable point. Plurality was their inheritance, their economy, their identity; every god was a node in a web of relationships and custodianships and prestige. The call to one God did not feel like a different theology. It felt like erasure.

And that is what they called *ʿujāb*. Not "incorrect." Not even "offensive." But *inconceivable* — staggeringly beyond the range of what the mind is willing to absorb.

Here is the trap fully visible now. They opened with wonder at the messenger. They close with wonder at the message. The root is the same across both ends of the ring; only the target of the astonishment has shifted. But the wonder itself never became a window. It was always a wall. **The ring the passage traces is the ring of a heart that marvels and marvels — and moves nowhere.**

## PART TWO: WHAT THE ONENESS ACTUALLY THREATENED

There is a question the passage leaves underneath the surface, and we need to go down to it.

Why *this*? Of everything the Prophet said — the resurrection, the judgment, the accounting of every deed — why was *ilāhan wāḥidā*, "one God," the thing they called *ʿujāb*? Why not the description of the hellfire, which is vivid and terrifying? Why not the claim to revelation, which demands a kind of submission? Why does tawhid specifically land as the most inconceivable thing they have heard?

Because tawhid was not, for them, a theological claim. It was an announcement about *power*.

Makkah's sacred geography was built on multiplicity. Each god had its custodian tribe, its associated rituals, its share of the pilgrimage economy, its role in the social contracts that held the city together. The system worked precisely because no single deity — and no single lineage — held total authority. Plurality was the architecture of equilibrium. Every tribe had its access, its node, its piece of the divine arrangement.

*Ilāhan wāḥidā* — one God — collapses that architecture. It says: all those custodianships, all those contracts, all those claims, dissolve into one sovereignty. And if sovereignty is one, then the human arrangements built on its fragmentation are not sacred. They are contingent. They can be unmade.

This is why they do not say the Prophet is *wrong*. They say, through the verb *jaʿala*, that he has *done something* to their gods — caused this transformation, acted upon the landscape of the divine. The grammar of their complaint is the grammar of someone describing an injury, not a debate. **The call to tawhid was not an invitation to think differently. It was a dismantling of what they had built.**

[PAUSE]

And the Prophet — *minhum*, one of them — was the one saying it. Which meant there was no cultural distance to hide behind, no foreignness to use as a reason not to listen. The man who knew their world from the inside was telling them their world was arranged around a falsehood. That is not a claim you can set aside. It is a claim you have to destroy or accept. And so they reached for destruction: *sāḥirun kaḏḏāb* — pile every disqualification available, make the man impossible, so the message does not have to be answered.

Al-Ṭabarī notes that the Quraysh's underlying expectation had been even more specific: if a divine message were coming, it should arrive through *an angel sent down from the sky*. A celestial messenger would carry the foreignness they needed — something they could place at a safe remove, something that made no claim on their ordinary human equality. God's refusal to send an angel was already, in itself, an act of closeness. And closeness was exactly what they could not afford.

**They wanted a God at a distance. They received a warner from their own street. And the message he carried said that God was not at a distance at all — that all the intermediary arrangements they had built between themselves and the real, all the distributed gods and managed access points, were not roads. They were detours.**

## CLOSING: The Wonder That Is Still Possible

Let me bring this back to you, and I want to do it carefully, because there is something in these two ayahs that is not only about them.

The passage has named two wonders. The first is their wonder at the messenger — that he came from among themselves, in their language, through their history. The second is their wonder at the message — that God should be one, that all the fragmented authorities they had built their lives around should resolve into a single sovereignty.

Both wonders were available to them as *openings*. The familiar messenger could have been received as: *of course God would give us someone we already trust — that is mercy*. The oneness of God could have been received as: *of course there is only one source — that simplifies everything, clears every debt, liberates every attachment*. Both wonders, taken differently, would have led somewhere.

Instead, both became walls.

[PAUSE]

This is the human pattern the Quran is mapping here. And it is not only theirs.

Wonder can close or open. The wonder that says "I did not know the world was this large" — that is the beginning of the journey. The wonder that says "this is *ʿujāb*, inconceivable, too strange to absorb" — that is the end of it. The Quraysh had every reason to be moved. They had the most trustworthy man in the city, forty years of evidence, and a message compact enough to hold in a single breath: *lā ilāha illā Allāh*. And they marveled themselves out of it.

The question the passage leaves — quietly, without pressing — is this: **What are the places in your own life where wonder has become a wall?** Where "this is astonishing" has quietly become a reason not to move? Where the closeness of a truth — how near it is, how little escape it leaves — has produced in you the same *ʿajibū* that produced in them their *ʿujāb*?

[PAUSE]

Because the *minhum* is still happening. The mercy of God is still arriving in familiar forms. In a verse you have read a hundred times and suddenly understood differently. In an inconvenient conviction that surfaces at the wrong moment, in the wrong season, through the most ordinary of voices. The ego's reflex — across every generation — is to reach for *ʿujāb*. *This is too close. This changes too much. This is an astounding thing.*

The Quran preserved their words not to humiliate them but to name the movement, so that when you feel the wonder rising — the closing kind, the defensive kind — you will have a name for it. You will know what it is. And you will know what it cost the people who stayed inside it.

**The wonder that condemned them is available to every age. So, thank God, is the alternative.**
