# The Days That Belong to Allah

### Surah Al-Jathiyah, Ayah 14

> [ayah:45:14] قُل لِّلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ يَغْفِرُوا۟ لِلَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ أَيَّامَ ٱللَّهِ لِيَجْزِىَ قَوْمًۢا بِمَا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ

*Qul lilladhīna āmanū yaghfirū lilladhīna lā yarjūna ayyāma-llāhi li-yajziya qawman bimā kānū yaksibūn.*

**"Say to those who believe, let them forgive those who do not look forward to the days of Allah — so that He may recompense a people for what they used to earn."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me put you somewhere before we open a single word.

You are in Mecca. Not the Mecca of crowds and lights — the early Mecca, where being a believer is a daily exposure. You have no state, no army, no court you can appeal to. When someone curses you in the street, there is no one to complain to. When someone spits at your back, mocks your prayer, insults the people you love most — you absorb it. You go home with it sitting in your chest.

And here is the thing about a wound that cannot be answered: it doesn't heal. It *ferments.* It turns into a low, constant pressure behind the eyes. You start rehearsing what you'll do if it happens one more time.

There is a narration — and the classical commentators preserve it in more than one form, so let's hold it loosely — that this verse came down around a specific moment of exactly this kind. **Al-Baghawi reports, on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil,** that a man insulted ʿUmar ibn al-Khattāb in Mecca, and ʿUmar — who was ʿUmar — was on the verge of striking him down. **Al-Qurtubi narrates a similar account** of a man from Quraysh who reviled him. And **al-Qurazī and al-Suddī report** a broader occasion: that it was revealed about Companions in Mecca living under severe, grinding harassment from the idolaters. Notice the commentators don't agree on one single trigger — there are several traditions here, and I'm not going to flatten them into one. What they *agree* on is the climate: believers, wounded, with the instinct to hit back rising in their throats.

Into that climate, Allah sends down — not a comfort, not a complaint line, but an *instruction.*

And the instruction is the last thing the wounded heart wants to hear.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel before we go further. Read the verse again and notice who it is *about.* You forgive — whom? "Those who do not look forward to the days of Allah." It defines the offender not by what they did to you, but by *what they don't believe.* That is a strange way to describe the person who insulted you. Why does Allah identify your enemy by their **theology** and not by their **crime?**

Hold that question. Because the answer is the entire verse. And by the time we've earned it, this small instruction is going to turn out to be one of the most psychologically precise things ever said about why human beings can — or cannot — let go.

Let's begin.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Command That Refuses to Sound Like a Command

Let me teach you something about your own language first, before we touch the Arabic.

Imagine you want your child to clean their room. You have two ways to say it.

The first: **"Clean your room."** A direct order. The imperative. It lands as pressure — there is a commander and a commanded, and the gap between them is the gap of obligation.

The second: a parent who says, **"You're someone who keeps your space clean."** Notice what happened. There's no order in that sentence at all — grammatically it's just a description. But it's *more* powerful, not less. Because instead of pushing a command at the child from the outside, it reaches inside and names something as *who they already are.* It doesn't say *do this.* It says *this is what someone like you does.*

Keep that distinction in your hands. Now let's open the Arabic.

Allah is telling the Prophet ﷺ to deliver a command to the believers. The command is: forgive. Now — what is the most natural, expected way to say that in Arabic? It would be:

**قُل لِّلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا اغْفِرُوا** — *"Say to those who believe: Forgive!"* — with اغْفِرُوا, a direct imperative. An order, clean and frontal.

That is not what Allah said.

What He said is: **قُل لِّلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا يَغْفِرُوا** — and that word, **يَغْفِرُوا** (*yaghfirū*), is not an imperative at all. Grammatically it is a present-tense verb that has been put into what Arabic calls the **jussive** mood — the *majzūm*. Its ending has been clipped: the expected *nūn* of *yaghfirūna* drops away, and you're left with *yaghfirū.*

Now stay with me, because the grammarians themselves stood in front of this and asked: *why is it jussive? What clipped it?* **Al-Qurtubi lays out the readings side by side,** and rather than pick a winner, let me give you all of them at once — because each one is a different camera angle on the same astonishing thing.

**One reading:** the *yaghfirū* is jussive because it is the *answer to an implied condition* — the way Arabic says *"Stand — you'll get good"* (*qum tuṣib khayran*), where "you'll get good" is clipped because it hangs off "stand" as its consequence. On this reading the verse breathes like this: *the forgiveness is the natural result that flows from being told.* Tell them — and forgiveness follows, the way the consequence follows the condition.

**A second reading:** there's a hidden *lām* — *li-yaghfirū*, "so that they forgive" — the *lām* of purpose, quietly dropped.

**A third reading,** which al-Qurtubi attributes to ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā and says **Ibn al-ʿArabī preferred:** the sentence is answering a command that has itself been deleted. The full shape is: *"Say to them: *Forgive!* — and they will forgive."* The actual imperative — *ighfirū* — has been lifted out of the sentence, and all that remains on the surface is the *result:* *yaghfirū,* they forgive.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened across all three readings?

The direct order — *Forgive!* — has been **removed from the visible sentence.** Whether it was swallowed by a condition, hidden behind a *lām*, or deleted outright, the surface of the verse does not bark a command at the believers. It does something gentler and far more cunning. It describes the forgiveness as the thing that simply *happens* when you tell a believer. *Say to those who believe — and they forgive.* As if forgiveness is not an external burden laid on faith, but the **exhaust of faith itself.** Tell a believer, and forgiveness comes out the other side, the way warmth comes off something already lit.

Remember the parent who said, *"You're someone who keeps your space clean"?* This is the divine version. Allah is not standing over the wounded Companion shouting *forgive.* He is telling the Prophet ﷺ to inform them of **who they now are.** A person who believes — *yaghfir.* He forgives. It is no longer an order you must drag yourself to obey. It is a description of the species of person you have become.

And notice — this is mercy in the very *architecture* of the sentence. Because the hardest command in the human repertoire — *let go of the one who hurt you* — is exactly the command you cannot bear to receive as a shout. So Allah doesn't shout it. He folds it into your identity and lets it fall out as consequence.

**One word in the jussive — and the order to forgive has been transformed into the nature of the forgiver.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: "The Days of Allah" — أَيَّامَ ٱللَّهِ

Now look at the people you're being asked to forgive. They are described with a phrase, and the phrase is doing enormous work: **الَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ أَيَّامَ ٱللَّهِ** — "those who do not *look forward to* the days of Allah."

Let me first do the simple thing and then the deep thing.

The simple thing: **أَيَّام** (*ayyām*) is the plural of **يَوْم** (*yawm*), day. And **أَيَّامَ ٱللَّهِ** is a genitive construction — *the days of Allah*, the days that *belong* to Allah. Now here is a question worth slowing all the way down for: aren't *all* days Allah's days? Every sunrise is His, every Tuesday is His. So when Allah singles out certain days and stamps His own name on them — *the days of Allah* — He is pointing at something specific. What makes a day **Allah's day** in a way that ordinary days are not?

The commentators answer with one word: **وَقَائِع** — *His decisive events.* **Al-Jalalayn glosses *ayyām Allāh* as *waqāʾiʿuhu* — His happenings, His interventions.** The days when Allah's hand, normally hidden behind the smooth running of the world, suddenly becomes *visible.* The day a tyrant's sea closed over him. The day a people who felt untouchable were touched. The day the account that everyone assumed would never be collected — was collected.

This is the physical-to-spiritual move that the Quran makes constantly. A "day" is the most ordinary unit of your life — sunrise, sunset, the thing you cross off a calendar. But *the days of Allah* are days where the calendar tears open and you see, for a moment, what was underneath it the whole time: that there is an Owner, and He acts, and history is not just one thing after another. And the **greatest** of His days — the one the whole of this surah is leaning toward — is the Day when every community will kneel (*jāthiyah*) before His judgment.

So now read the description of your enemy again: they are **لَا يَرْجُونَ** — they do not *yarjūna* — these days.

Let's open *yarjūna.* The root is **rajā** (ر-ج-و), and its core meaning is **hope, expectation, anticipation** — to lean forward toward something coming. The dictionaries note something beautiful about this word: *rajāʾ* is the kind of hope that is braided with a little fear, because you are anticipating something whose arrival will weigh on you. That's why **al-Baghawi and the commentators gloss *lā yarjūna* here as *lā yakhāfūn*** — they do not *fear,* they do not reckon with, they do not *expect.* The person who *yarjū* the days of Allah lives tilted toward them, leaning into a future where the account comes due. The person who does *not* — lives flat. For them there is no day coming that will change the meaning of today.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what these two words have done *together.* **لَا يَرْجُونَ أَيَّامَ ٱللَّهِ.** The person who wounded you is being defined not by their insult but by their **horizon.** They are someone for whom no Day is coming. There is no reckoning on their calendar. And that — *that* — is why they could wound you so freely. **A person who believes nothing is ever collected will spend recklessly.** The cruelty is downstream of the theology.

And here is where the structure of the whole surah opens like a door. Ten verses later, Allah will let these very people speak. In **45:24** they say: *"There is nothing but our life in this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but Time (al-dahr)."* That is the creed of the one who *lā yarjū ayyāma-llāh.* No days of Allah — only the grinding wheel of *dahr,* impersonal time, which kills and is answerable to no one. **The person you are being asked to forgive in verse 14 is the person whose entire worldview gets quoted aloud in verse 24.** They are not your problem to solve. They are *time's* problem — and Time has an Owner.

## Element Three: "So That He May Recompense a People" — The Sentence That Won't Tell You Who

There is one more word, and it does its work by being *vague* — which, in the Quran, is never an accident.

The verse ends: **لِيَجْزِيَ قَوْمًۢا بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ** — *"so that He may recompense a people for what they used to earn."*

Two things to notice.

First, the **lām** on *li-yajziya* — the *lām* of purpose. So that. The forgiveness you extend is not the end of the matter; it is *for a reason.* It opens onto a purpose: *so that He may recompense.* Your letting-go is not the closing of the account. It is the **transfer of the account** to the One who will actually settle it. (And note, the commentators record a second recitation, *li-najziya* — "so that **We** may recompense" — same meaning, the divine voice shifting from third to first person, but the point stands either way.)

Second — and this is the load-bearing absence — look at the object: **قَوْمًۢا.** *A people.* Indefinite. Not *"them,"* not *"the disbelievers,"* not *"the ones who wronged you."* Just — *a people.* Undefined. And the verb of their earning, **يَكْسِبُونَ** (*yaksibūn,* from *kasb,* to earn, to acquire), is left wide open: recompensed *for what they used to earn.* Earn — good or bad? It doesn't say.

The classical commentators stood at this same vagueness, and **al-Jalalayn preserves the openness rather than resolving it:** the recompense could fall on the *disbelievers* for the harm they earned — *or* it could be the recompense of the *believers,* rewarded for the forgiveness *they* earned by restraining themselves. Read it one way and the verse ends with a warning to the cruel. Read it the other way and it ends with a wage for the patient.

Don't choose. **Both are true, and the indefinite *qawman* was built to hold both.** Here is the staggering thing the vagueness accomplishes: when you forgive, *two* accounts open at once. Theirs — for what they did. And yours — for what you swallowed. The same verb, *yaksibūn,* covers both, because in the economy of the days of Allah, **restraint is also an earning.** The harm they did is logged. And so is the fist you did not throw.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these words, laid side by side, have shown us.

The **jussive** *yaghfirū* told us forgiveness is meant to flow *out* of faith, not be forced onto it. The **days of Allah** told us the person who wronged you wronged you because, in their world, no account is ever collected. And the indefinite **qawman... yaksibūn** told us that the account *is* collected — by Someone else, on a Day you don't control, and that your own forbearance is being written into the ledger alongside their cruelty.

Now let's stop asking what these words reveal about *language* — and ask what they reveal about *reality itself.* About why a human being can let go of a wound, or can't.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Forgiveness Is Not Letting Them Off — It Is Handing the Case to a Higher Court

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a reason forgiveness is so hard, and it is not the reason people usually give. People say forgiveness is hard because the wound *hurts.* That's true, but it's not the core. Here is the core:

**We don't hold onto grudges because we want revenge. We hold onto them because we are afraid that if we let go, the wrong will go unanswered.**

Think about it honestly. The thing that keeps you up rehearsing the argument, replaying the betrayal — it's not really bloodlust. It's a kind of *bookkeeping panic.* A voice that says: *if I don't keep this on the books, no one will. If I drop it, they get away with it. My forgiveness becomes their acquittal.* That fear — that to forgive is to file the wrong under "settled" when it was never settled — is the chain. We stay angry as a form of *justice-keeping.* We become the unpaid security guard of our own wound, standing watch over it for years so that it is never forgotten, because we are terrified that to stop watching is to let injustice win.

Now hear what this verse does to that fear. It does not say *forgive, because it didn't matter.* It does not say *forgive, and let it go unanswered.* It says:

**يَغْفِرُوا ... لِيَجْزِيَ** — *forgive ... so that He may recompense.*

The forgiveness and the reckoning are in the **same sentence.** Your letting-go does not cancel the account. It *moves* it. **Al-Muyassar** captures the live mechanism: the believers pardon and overlook the harm done to them — *li-yajziya-llāhu hāʾulāʾi* — *so that Allah may repay these people for what they earned.* You are not the judge dropping the charges. You are the witness stepping back so the real Judge can take the bench.

This is the reframe that changes everything: **forgiveness is not closing the case. It is transferring it from a court where you are the exhausted, biased, powerless plaintiff — to a court where the Judge is never tired, never partial, never deceived, and never short of power to enforce the verdict.**

You were never qualified to hold that case anyway. You can't see the full file — what they were carrying, what they'll be asked, what was already taken from them. You can't enforce the sentence. And the holding of it is poisoning *you,* not them. The grudge is the one cup of poison you drink hoping the other person dies.

So the verse pries your fingers off the case one at a time. *You're afraid it'll go unanswered? It won't — liyajziya. You're afraid forgiveness means injustice wins? It doesn't — there is a Day, and that Day belongs to Allah.* The only reason you were clutching the wound was to make sure the universe didn't forget it. **The verse tells you the universe has an Owner, and He forgets nothing.**

This is the same instruction Allah gives elsewhere and it always carries the same logic. **In 42:40** — the recompense of an evil is an evil like it, *but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation, his reward is upon Allah.* **In 2:109** — *forgive and overlook until Allah brings His command.* Forgiveness in the Quran is never naïveté. It is always *deferral to a higher authority who has agreed to take the case.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time the wound comes back — and it will come back, because that's what wounds do — try saying the actual transfer out loud. Not *"it's fine"* — it wasn't fine. Say: *"This is not mine to collect. I am handing it to the Owner of the Days. He has the file. He doesn't lose files."* You are not pretending the debt is zero. You are changing whose name is on the account. And the strange peace that follows is the peace of a security guard who has finally been relieved of a shift he was never med to work.

## Theme Two: You Can Only Forgive As Far As You Believe in the Day

Now let's go to the deepest layer, because the verse has been quietly arguing something this whole time that we haven't said out loud yet.

Go back to the strange description. The people you forgive are *"those who do not look forward to the days of Allah."* We asked at the start: why describe your enemy by their *theology* and not their *crime?*

Here is the answer. **Because their theology is the diagnosis of their cruelty — and yours is the source of your power to forgive.**

Watch the two worldviews stand next to each other.

The one who does *not* expect the days of Allah lives in a closed universe. Nothing is coming. No account is collected. *Al-dahr* — time — grinds on and answers to no one (that's their creed, quoted in 45:24). In a universe like that, what is cruelty? It's *free.* It costs nothing. There is no later, no Owner, no ledger. So you spend it freely — you insult, you oppress, you crush, because the bill never comes. **A person with no horizon will burn down the present, because for them the present is all there is.**

And now — *you.* You are being asked to do the hardest thing a wounded person can do, and the verse has just told you exactly what makes it *possible.* You can forgive **precisely because you are the opposite of them.** You *do* expect the days of Allah. You *do* believe the account is collected. And that belief is not a sweet abstraction — it is the **specific psychological resource** that lets your fingers come off the wound. Because the only thing forgiveness costs you is the fear that the wrong goes unanswered — and that fear is *dead* in a person who knows the Day is real.

Do you feel the precision of this? **Your capacity to forgive is a direct readout of how real the Day of Judgment is to you.** They could not forgive even if they wanted to, because in their world there's no higher court to transfer the case to — it's their court or nothing, so they cling. *You* can forgive, because you have somewhere to put the case down.

This is why Allah defined your enemy by what they *don't* believe. He was holding up a mirror. He was saying: *look at why they wound — they have no Day. Now look at why you can let go — you have one. Don't become them. Don't let the wound collapse your horizon down to the size of theirs.* Because that is the real danger of being wronged: not the wound itself, but that **the wound tempts you to start living like someone who doesn't believe in the Day** — clutching, hoarding, settling scores in the only court you trust, which is your own. Revenge is, at bottom, a confession that you don't fully believe anyone else is keeping the books.

And notice how tightly this is woven into the surah. **45:14** — forgive those who don't expect His days. **45:24** — and here is what those people believe: nothing but this life, only time destroys us. **45:28** — and here is what they *missed:* "you will see every nation kneeling (*jāthiyah*)... called to its record." The surah is named after that kneeling. The whole structure is one argument: *the day they swore would never come, comes — and everyone kneels. So forgive now, from inside the knowledge they refused, that the Day is real.*

There is one more place this phrase lives, and it seals the point. **In 14:5,** Allah commands Mūsā: *"Bring your people out of the darknesses into the light, and **remind them of the days of Allah.**"* The same phrase — *ayyām Allāh.* To bring a people from darkness to light *is* to make the days of Allah real to them again. Darkness is the flat universe with no horizon. Light is the universe with a Day in it. **And the proof that the light has reached *you* — the test of whether the days of Allah are real to you and not just a phrase — is whether you can open your hand and let go of the one who wronged you.**

**What this changes about tomorrow:** when you find you genuinely *cannot* forgive someone — don't only ask, *why are they so cruel?* Ask the harder, more honest question: *how real is the Day to me right now?* Because the inability to let go is often not a failure of kindness. It's a failure of *certainty.* You're clutching the case because, in that moment, you half-suspect there's no higher court. So the repair for an unforgiving heart is not to try harder to be nice. It is to make the Day real again — to remind yourself of the days of Allah — until forgiveness falls out of you the way the grammar promised it would: *yaghfirū,* as the natural exhaust of faith.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Now stand back and see how the verse was built, because the order of its words is an argument in miniature.

**قُل لِّلَّذِينَ آمَنُوا** — *Say to those who believe.* It begins by naming you by your faith. Everything that follows is what faith *does.*

**يَغْفِرُوا** — *they forgive.* Not an order shouted at you — a description of your nature, the jussive that turned a command into an identity.

**لِلَّذِينَ لَا يَرْجُونَ أَيَّامَ ٱللَّهِ** — *those who do not expect the days of Allah.* The enemy defined by their missing horizon — the diagnosis of why they wound and the mirror of why you can let go.

**لِيَجْزِيَ قَوْمًۢا بِمَا كَانُوا يَكْسِبُونَ** — *so that He may recompense a people for what they earned.* The account does not vanish. It transfers. To the Owner of the Days, who collects from the cruel and pays the patient, and loses no file.

The verse moves from **your identity** → to **your action** → to **their blindness** → to **His justice.** It opens in faith and closes in the Day. And forgiveness is the bridge laid across the middle — the thing only a person who believes the *end* of the verse can perform the *middle* of.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **grammar** said: forgiveness should flow *out* of faith naturally (*yaghfirū*, the clipped command-become-nature). The **theme** said: it can only flow out of you to the exact degree that the Day is real to you. Same truth. The jussive *assumes* the believer has a horizon — that's *why* it can describe forgiveness as automatic. Take away the days of Allah and the grammar collapses; there'd be no reason for forgiveness to flow at all. **The mood of the verb depends on the reality of the Day.** Faith makes forgiveness natural — because faith means there's a higher court, and a higher court means you can finally put the case down.

## Questions to Carry

1. The grudge I am holding right now — am I keeping it because I want justice, or because I'm afraid that if I let go, no one else will collect it? What does that fear say about how real the Day is to me?
2. Who is the *qawman* — the "people" — whose account I've been trying to keep on my own books, when it was never mine to keep?
3. When I cannot forgive someone, is it really a failure of kindness — or a quiet failure of certainty that there is an Owner of the Days?
4. Have I let a wound shrink my horizon down to the size of the person who wounded me — started living, in this one corner of my heart, like someone who doesn't expect the days of Allah?
5. What would it feel like, tonight, to say out loud: *"This is not mine to collect"*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**You can forgive the one who wounded you — not because the wound didn't matter, but because you believe the days of Allah are real, and so you can hand the case to the Owner who never loses a file, never tires, and never lets an account go uncollected.**

## The Closing Invitation

There is a wound you've been guarding. You've stood watch over it for a long time, because some part of you believed that if you ever stopped watching, the wrong would simply dissolve into the air, unanswered. This verse is an invitation to relieve yourself of that shift. Not to declare the wrong was nothing — it wasn't. But to move it to the one court where it will be weighed perfectly, *bimā kānū yaksibūn,* down to the last earned thing. And to discover that the open hand was never the weak one. The open hand belongs to the person who knows the Day is coming — and can therefore afford to stop fighting today's battles as though they were the last word.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, Owner of the Days — make the reality of Your Day so present to my heart that forgiveness flows out of my faith the way You described it, not forced but freely given. Take from my hands the cases I was never able to judge or enforce, and let me trust that You collect every account and lose no file. And do not let any wound shrink my horizon down to the size of those who never expected to meet You. Āmīn.*
