# The Joke That Closes In

## When Mockery Becomes a Cage

---

> وَبَدَا لَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا عَمِلُوا۟ وَحَاقَ بِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ

> *Wa-badā lahum sayyiʾātu mā ʿamilū wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn.*

> "And there will appear to them the evils of what they did, and there will close in around them the very thing they used to mock."

---

## Rebuilding the World

Let me put you somewhere first. Before the verse, the room it was spoken into.

Picture Makkah, the early years. A man stands in the marketplace and says something strange and quiet: *there is a Day coming when all of this stops. When the buying and the selling and the boasting end, and every single person is made to stand and answer.*

And the response is not anger. Anger would be a kind of respect — anger means you took it seriously enough to be threatened. No. The response, again and again in this surah, is something colder than anger.

It is **a laugh.**

It is the raised eyebrow. The smirk. The "okay, sure." The man who turns to his friend and says — and the Qur'an records the exact sentence just one verse before ours — *"We don't really know what this 'Hour' is. We figure it's just a guess. We're not convinced"* (45:32).

Notice what mockery does there. It is not a counter-argument. They did not say "here is why resurrection is impossible." They said, in effect, *this isn't even worth arguing about.* Mockery is the weapon you reach for when you want to dismiss something **without having to engage it.** You wrap it in a joke so you never have to ask whether it's true.

That was the climate. A message of ultimate seriousness, met with a shrug and a smile. The deniers of Makkah had built a wall, and the bricks of that wall were jokes.

[PAUSE]

And then comes verse 33. Eleven words. And those eleven words do something to that wall.

**The Hook:** Here is what I want you to sit with before we even open the language. These people thought they were holding something *at a distance* by mocking it. They thought the joke kept the danger out there, away from them, harmless. The verse is about to tell us where that mocked thing actually goes. And it is the opposite of "out there." Stay with me — because the destination of a mockery is not where any of us would guess.

# Part One: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Word *Badā* — Things Do Not Begin, They Are Unveiled

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we touch the verse, because it changes everything that follows.

There is a difference between a verb that means *to create* and a verb that means *to appear.* In English we blur them. We say "the problem showed up," "the bill appeared," as if the thing came into existence at the moment we noticed it. But of course it didn't. The bill was always owed. It only **became visible** to you.

Arabic keeps this distinction sharp. The root **خ-ل-ق (khalaqa)** is to *create* — to bring from nothing into being. The root in front of us, **ب-د-و (badā)**, is something else entirely. *Badā* means **to become apparent, to emerge into view, to show itself.** The Bedouin (*badawī*) is named from this root — the one who lives out in the open, exposed, with nothing hiding him. *Al-bādiya* is the open desert where there is nowhere to conceal yourself.

So now look at how the verse opens:

> **وَبَدَا لَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا عَمِلُوا۟**
> *"And there appeared to them the evils of what they did."*

Allah did not say *khuliqat* — "the evils were created for them." He said **badā** — they **appeared.**

Let's sit with this. Because the difference is the whole spiritual physics of the Day of Judgment.

Their deeds were not manufactured on that Day. They were not invented as a punishment, conjured out of nowhere. **They were always there.** Recorded. Real. Present the entire time. What changes on that Day is not the existence of the deeds — it is the *visibility* of them. The lights come on. The veil that the person had drawn over their own life is pulled back, and the thing that was there all along simply **steps into view.**

Here is what is remarkable. This means the Day of Judgment, in the grammar of this word, is not primarily an event of *creation.* It is an event of **revelation.** Of seeing. The al-Qurtubi tradition glosses *badā lahum* as *ẓahara lahum* — "it became manifest to them" — and adds a single clarifying word that you must not miss: he says what appears is *jazāʾu sayyiʾāti mā ʿamilū* — **the recompense of their evil deeds.** Both the deeds *and* their consequence, which had been bound together the whole time like a seed and the tree folded inside it, finally show themselves.

[PAUSE]

Now bring it close. Where in your own life have you experienced *badā* — the appearing of what was always there?

The lump you felt and didn't get checked. It didn't *become* cancer the day the doctor named it. It was already that. The diagnosis was a *badā* — an appearing.

The marriage that "suddenly" ended. It did not suddenly end. The cracks were structural and old. The ending was the moment they *became visible* to someone who had been working very hard not to see them.

The debt you didn't open the envelope on. The number was always that number.

This is the terror and the mercy of *badā* at once. The terror: you cannot un-owe what you owe by refusing to look at it. Not-seeing is not the same as not-being. But hold onto the mercy in it too, because we will need it later: **if the Day is an unveiling and not a creation, then everything that will be unveiled is being written right now.** The file is open. You are still holding the pen.

What *badā* reveals: **on that Day nothing new is invented against you. Your life is simply shown to you with the lights finally on.**

## Element Two: The Word *Ḥāqa* — Not "Befell" but "Closed In From Every Side"

Now the second half of the verse, and the word that is the secret heart of the whole ayah.

> **وَحَاقَ بِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ**

Most translations give you *ḥāqa* as "befell them" or "surrounded them." That is not wrong, but it is so much flatter than what the Arabic is doing, and I want to slow all the way down here.

The root is **ح-ي-ق (ḥāqa)**. Let me first just let you *hear* it. Say it: *ḥāqa.* That opening **ḥ** is a sound made deep in the throat, a constriction, a tightening of the very passage of breath. And it ends on the **q** — *qāf* — another deep, back-of-the-throat, almost choked sound. The word is phonetically a closing. It begins in a tightened throat and ends in a swallowed stop. The mouth *enacts* the meaning. This is a word that sounds like something closing around you.

And that is exactly its meaning. The lexicographers tell us **ḥāqa** does not mean simply "to happen to" — it means **to encompass, to encircle, to descend upon and surround on all sides** so that there is no opening left to escape through. al-Qurtubi, in our sources, gives it precisely this double sense: *nazala bihim wa aḥāṭa* — **"it came down upon them and it surrounded them."** Both at once. It fell *and* it enclosed.

Think of the difference. If I say a problem *befell* you, that is a single blow from one direction — you could, in principle, step away from it. But if something **ḥāqa**'d you, it came at you from in front and behind and left and right and above, all at the same moment, until you turned in every direction and found only it. It is the difference between being *struck* and being *trapped.*

[PAUSE]

Now. Watch what it is that does this surrounding. Read the words again, slowly:

> *and there closed in around them* — **مَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ** — **the very thing they used to mock.**

Stop. Read that one more time and let it land.

The thing that surrounds them on every side, the thing that descends and encircles with no exit — is **the thing they mocked.**

Do you feel the reversal? They thought mockery *pushed the thing away.* That was the entire function of the joke — to create distance, to keep the warning out there at arm's length where it couldn't touch them. And the verse says: that exact thing, the punishment they laughed off, the Hour they called a "guess" — **that** is what now comes back and wraps itself around them on every side until it is all they can see.

The thing they thought they were holding *away* is the thing that ends up *all around.*

This is not a coincidence of vocabulary. This is the architecture of divine justice rendered in a single verb. What you mock, you do not escape. You invite. You give it your address.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more layer, and it is almost too precise. Look at the two halves of the verse and the prepositions Allah chose:

- *Badā* **لَهُمْ** *(lahum)* — the deeds appeared **TO them.** *Lahum* — "for them, to them." This is the language of *showing.* It is placed before their eyes. They are made to **look.**
- *Ḥāqa* **بِهِم** *(bihim)* — it closed in **AROUND them.** *Bihim* — "with them, upon them." This is the language of *enclosing.* It is placed around their bodies.

First they are made to **see** (the deeds appear *to* them). Then they are made to be **surrounded** (the punishment closes in *around* them). Sight, then encirclement. They are shown the truth, and then they are wrapped in its consequence. One verse, two motions: the eyes are opened, and then the cage is closed.

What *ḥāqa* reveals: **what you mock does not stay at a distance. You are giving it permission to one day surround you completely.**

## Element Three: *Yastahziʾūn* — The Grammar of a Habit, and a Future Spoken as Past

Two final features, smaller in size but they seal the whole thing.

**First: the shape of the word for mockery.**

The verb is **يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ** *(yastahziʾūn)*, from the root **ه-ز-أ (hazaʾa)**, to mock. But notice — the Leeds corpus confirms it — this is not the plain form of the verb. This is **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern. You can see it in the *ista-* at the front: *ist-ahzaʾa.*

What does Form X do? Among its core functions, it carries the sense of **actively seeking, deliberately undertaking, persistently doing.** It is not the accidental form. *Sa'ala* is to ask; *istaʿlama* (Form X) is to actively seek out information. The Form X adds intention, effort, a *leaning into* the action.

So *yastahziʾūn* is not a momentary chuckle that slipped out. It is **deliberate, sought-after, cultivated mockery.** Ridicule as a practiced posture. They *worked at* their scorn.

**And second: look at what wraps around that verb —** **كَانُوا۟** *(kānū),* "they used to," followed by the *imperfect* (ongoing) tense *yastahziʾūn.* In Arabic, *kāna* + the imperfect is the construction for a **continuous, habitual, repeated past action.** Not "they mocked once." But **"they kept on mocking. It was their way. Day after day, it was the thing they did."**

So the full picture of their mockery is now exact: it was *deliberate* (Form X) and it was *habitual* (kāna + imperfect). It wasn't a slip. It was a lifestyle.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the feature that ties the whole verse into a knot of certainty.

The two verbs that describe the Day of Judgment — *badā* (appeared) and *ḥāqa* (closed in) — are both in the **perfect tense.** The *past* tense. "It **appeared**." "It **closed in**." As if it has already happened.

But the Day of Judgment has not happened yet. So why the past tense?

This is one of the most powerful devices in all of Qur'anic Arabic — what scholars call the **prophetic perfect.** Allah describes a future event in the past tense **because its occurrence is so certain that it is as good as done.** To the One outside of time, who knows the end as He knows the beginning, the future Day is not a maybe. It carries the same fixedness as the past. You cannot change what has already happened — and Allah is telling you that this particular future has the same iron settledness as history.

Feel the contrast the verse builds: **their mockery is in the ongoing tense** — *yastahziʾūn,* always mocking, an open-ended habit they assumed would just continue. And **their reckoning is in the completed tense** — *badā, ḥāqa,* done, sealed, fixed. The thing they treated as endlessly open, Allah has already closed the book on. They thought they had all the time in the world to keep laughing. The grammar tells you the verdict is already written.

What the grammar reveals: **they treated the danger as an open question they could keep deferring with a joke. Allah describes it as a closed fact. The tense itself is the argument.**

## The Bridge

So look at what these eleven words, taken apart, have shown us.

We have a word — *badā* — that tells us the Day is not an invention but an **unveiling**; the lights coming on over a life that was always exactly what it was.

We have a word — *ḥāqa* — that tells us the mocked thing does not stay away; it **encircles**, comes back from every side, gives its consequence no exit.

And we have a grammar — Form X, the habitual *kāna,* the prophetic perfect — that tells us this mockery was a *cultivated way of living,* and that its reckoning is *already as fixed as the past.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself. Because the verse has named a spiritual mechanism that is not locked away on the Last Day. It is running in us right now.

# Part Two: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: Why We Mock — and Where the Mockery Goes

Let me start nowhere near the Qur'an. Let me start in a school hallway.

There is a kid who takes things seriously. He actually does the reading. He puts his hand up. He cares about something, openly, without irony. And there is, almost always, another kid leaning against the lockers who watches this and says, under his breath, just loud enough: *"look at this guy."*

Now ask yourself honestly: which of those two is afraid?

We think mockery is a sign of strength — the mocker stands above, looking down. But look closer at the *psychology* of it. **Mockery is the defense mechanism of someone who feels threatened by something he does not want to engage.** The kid against the lockers is threatened — not by the sincere boy, but by the *possibility that sincerity matters.* Because if it matters, then his own posture of not-caring is exposed as a posture. So he reaches for the cheapest, most efficient tool to make the threat go away **without having to think about it:** he laughs at it. The laugh says *I don't have to take this seriously, and neither do you.* It is a wall built of one brick, thrown up fast.

This is precisely what the deniers in this surah were doing. Go back to 45:32 — *"We figure the Hour is just a guess; we're not convinced."* That is not the language of someone who investigated and concluded. It is the language of someone who needed the question to *not be serious* — because if the Hour is real, then the entire architecture of their lives is wrong, and that is unbearable, so: *it's just a guess.* A shrug. A joke. The wall goes up.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement the Qur'an makes, and it is merciless and precise.

Mockery works by creating **distance.** That is its only trick. It pushes the threatening thing *out there,* away, into the category of "not worth my seriousness."

And the verse takes that exact mechanism and **inverts it at the destination.** *Wa-ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn* — the thing you pushed away **closes in around you.** The distance you manufactured collapses into total encirclement. The further you tried to hold it, the more completely it surrounds you in the end.

Why? Because mockery never actually deals with the thing. It only *hides* it. And we already learned from *badā* that hiding something does not make it not-there. It just delays the *appearing.* So everything you laughed off in order to avoid facing — it accrues. It waits. It is *there.* And on the Day the lights come on, it doesn't just appear politely in front of you. It comes back with the full weight of everything you refused to weigh, and it **surrounds you.**

[PAUSE]

Let me make this uncomfortably present, because the Day of Judgment is the *extreme* case of a law that operates daily.

The man who mocks therapy — "that's for weak people" — to avoid facing what's inside him. Does the inside thing go away? Or does it wait, and grow, and one day close in around his marriage, his sleep, his children?

The person who makes a running joke out of religion at every dinner — the eye-roll, the clever quip — have you noticed it is *always the same person,* always *needing* to make the joke? That need is a tell. You do not need to keep mocking what genuinely doesn't matter to you. The compulsion to keep laughing at it is the sound of a wall being *held up* against something that keeps pressing.

What we mock, we have refused to face. And what we refuse to face does not leave. It circles back. The verse just shows you the last lap.

The practical turn — and I want this concrete: **find the thing you keep making jokes about.** Not the things you criticize seriously — those you've engaged. The thing you *deflect with humor* every single time it comes near. That reflexive joke is a door you have nailed shut. This week, open it once. Just once. Ask, with no irony: *what if this is real and I have been laughing so I never have to find out?* That question is the opposite of *ḥāqa.* It lets you face the thing now, on your own terms, while it is small — instead of meeting it later when it surrounds you and there is no door left.

## Theme Two: The Mercy Hidden Inside the Terror of *Badā*

I want to end somewhere you might not expect — in *hope.* Because there is a mercy folded inside this frightening verse, and you only see it if you hold *badā* and *ḥāqa* together.

Start with the human experience of the bill coming due.

There is a particular kind of dread we all know: the dread of the thing you've been not-looking-at. The unopened envelope on the counter. The email you've left unread for three weeks because you *know* roughly what it says. The conversation you keep postponing. And here is the strange part of that dread — **the not-looking does not reduce the dread. It feeds it.** Every day you don't open the envelope, the imagined contents get heavier. Avoidance is not a painkiller. It is an interest rate. The debt of dread compounds.

That is the natural human condition this verse describes in its cosmic form. *Badā lahum* — it all *appears,* finally, with nothing left to hide behind. And for the one who spent a lifetime in avoidance, that appearing is pure terror, because *everything* arrives at once, and then *ḥāqa* — it surrounds, no exit.

[PAUSE]

But now turn the same word over and look at its other face.

If the Day of Judgment is *badā* — an **appearing** of what was always there — and not *khalq,* a creation of something new — then it tells you the most important fact about your situation *right now:*

**Nothing will appear on that Day that is not being written today.**

The file is not sealed. The lights are not yet on. The *badā* is in the future — and that means you are still in the chapter *before* the unveiling. Which means every deed in that file is, at this moment, **editable by repentance.** This is the whole logic of *tawba.* Allah promises, in another verse, that for the one who truly turns back, He will *transform their evil deeds into good ones* (25:70). The seed is not yet a tree. The envelope is not yet opened. You can still change what it will say when it is.

Do you see the mercy now? The very same mechanism that makes the Day terrifying — *that nothing is created then, only revealed* — is the mechanism that makes *today* the most precious thing you own. Because today is *inside the file.* Today is a line still being written. The terror of "it will all appear" is, read from this side of death, the gift of "it has not appeared **yet.**"

[PAUSE]

And here is where the two themes become one insight from two directions.

The mocker pushes the reckoning *away* — *I'll deal with it never; it's just a guess.* And in doing so, he wastes the one resource that the *badā* timeline gives him: **the time before the unveiling.** He spends his editable days laughing instead of writing. So when the file finally appears, he has added nothing to it but the mockery itself — which is exactly why, in the verse, *the mockery is what comes back to surround him.* He had years to put something else in the file. He put in jokes. The jokes are what *ḥāqa* him.

Whereas the one who takes the warning seriously *now* — who opens the envelope while it is still light, who faces the thing instead of deflecting it — is doing the only sane thing a person inside an editable file can do. **He is using the distance before the appearing to make sure that what appears is something he can bear to see.**

The practical turn: tonight, before you sleep, do a small private *badā* of your own. Turn the lights on yourself, gently, before they are turned on for you. Name one thing in your file you would not want to see appear. Just name it honestly to Allah. That act — voluntarily appearing your own deed to yourself and to Him, and asking Him to change it — is the single most powerful thing a person standing inside the editable chapter can do. It is choosing the small, survivable *badā* of repentance now, over the total, surrounding *badā* of regret later.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse as one shape, because it is built with terrifying symmetry.

> **وَبَدَا لَهُمْ** سَيِّئَاتُ مَا عَمِلُوا۟ ‖ **وَحَاقَ بِهِم** مَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ

Two halves. Two verbs of the Day, both in the prophetic perfect — *badā,* *ḥāqa* — the past tense of absolute certainty. Two relative clauses naming what comes back — *mā ʿamilū* (what they **did**) and *mā... yastahziʾūn* (what they **mocked**). And two prepositions doing precise, different work — *lahum,* it appeared **to** them; *bihim,* it closed in **around** them.

The first half is about their **actions** — and those are made to *appear,* placed before their eyes. The second half is about their **attitude** — the mockery — and that is made to *encircle,* wrapped around their bodies. Their deeds they are forced to **see.** Their scorn they are forced to **wear.** What they did stands in front of them; what they mocked closes in around them. The deed and the disposition both return — one to the eye, one to the whole self.

That is the architecture: **a life of two parts comes back in two ways, and neither part is escapable, because both are recorded by the same hand that has already written the verb in the past tense.**

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes were the same truth approached from two sides.

*Badā* taught us, in grammar, that the Day is an unveiling, not an invention — and the human heart taught us that what we avoid does not vanish, it waits to appear.

*Ḥāqa* taught us, in grammar, that the mocked thing encircles with no exit — and the human heart taught us that mockery is a wall we build to create distance, a wall that collapses into total enclosure at the end.

Two roots. One law: **what you will not face, you do not escape — you postpone, until the postponement runs out and it surrounds you.**

## Questions to Carry

1. What is the one thing I reliably deflect with a joke whenever it comes near? What am I keeping at a distance by laughing — and what would it cost me to face it seriously, once?
2. If the file of my life were unveiled tonight, exactly as it stands, which line would I most dread seeing *appear*? And what is stopping me from editing that line while it is still editable?
3. Where in my life am I treating an open, urgent question as if I have unlimited time to keep deferring it — speaking of it in the "I'll get to it" tense, when Allah may already have written it in the perfect?
4. Is my dismissal of something a *conclusion I reached* — or a *wall I'm holding up*? How would I tell the difference?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**What you mock to keep at a distance is precisely what will one day close in around you — and the deeds you hid even from yourself will not be created against you, only finally shown — so the time to face them is now, while the file is still open.**

## The Closing Invitation

This verse is not, in the end, about *them* — the laughers in the Makkan marketplace. It is a mirror held up to a mechanism that lives in every one of us: the reflex to deflect what threatens us, to laugh off what we cannot bear to weigh, to mistake *not-looking* for *not-there.*

The invitation is simply this: **turn the lights on yourself, gently, before they are turned on for you.** Open one envelope you've been avoiding. Face one thing you've been laughing past. Not out of dread — out of the deep mercy that the *badā* is still in the future, and the file is still in your hands. You are reading this on the editable side of the verse. That is not a small thing. That is everything.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who unveil what was hidden — let nothing appear against me on that Day that I did not turn back from in this one. I have laughed past things I should have wept over; soften my heart to take seriously what is serious, while there is still time to face it. And let what closes in around me on that Day be Your mercy, not my mockery. *Āmīn.*
