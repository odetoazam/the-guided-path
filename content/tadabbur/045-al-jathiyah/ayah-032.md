# We Only Assume: When the Refusal to Seek Becomes a Way of Life

## INTRODUCTION

Let us begin by sitting with the words themselves.

> وَإِذَا قِيلَ إِنَّ وَعْدَ ٱللَّهِ حَقٌّ وَٱلسَّاعَةُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهَا قُلْتُم مَّا نَدْرِى مَا ٱلسَّاعَةُ إِن نَّظُنُّ إِلَّا ظَنًّا وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُسْتَيْقِنِينَ

*Wa idhā qīla inna waʿda-llāhi ḥaqqun wa-s-sāʿatu lā rayba fīhā, qultum mā nadrī ma-s-sāʿatu in naẓunnu illā ẓannan wa mā naḥnu bi-mustayqinīn.*

"And when it was said, 'Indeed, the promise of Allah is true, and the Hour — there is no doubt about it,' you said, 'We do not know what the Hour is. We only assume an assumption, and we are not convinced.'"

### Rebuilding the world

To feel the weight of this ayah, you have to stand in Mecca, among a particular kind of person.

Not the violent persecutor. Not the mocker throwing stones. This is someone more familiar to us than that. A few verses earlier, in the same surah, the Quran has given us their creed in their own voice: *"There is nothing but our life in this world. We die, and we live, and nothing destroys us except time"* (45:24). This is the sophisticated denier. The man of the world. He is not stupid. He runs caravans, manages wealth, weighs evidence in the marketplace every single day. He demands proof for a debt of a few dirhams.

And someone comes to this person — a believer, a relative, perhaps the Prophet ﷺ himself — and lays before him a single proposition: *The promise of Allah is true. The Hour is coming. There is no doubt in it.*

Now watch what he does. He does not argue. He does not bring a counter-proof. He does not say "your evidence is weak, here is mine." He does something far more slippery. He reaches for a posture. He shrugs.

"We don't really know what this 'Hour' is. We figure it's all just guesswork. We're not convinced either way."

[PAUSE]

Notice that this is not the language of a debate. It is the language of a man backing slowly out of a room. And the Quran has preserved his exact words — not to inform us about him, but because we have all, at some point, stood in that doorway and said the very same thing.

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us. The Quran does not record their *arguments* against resurrection here. It records their *evasions*. And it turns out their evasion has a precise architecture — two certainties were offered to them, and they answered with three separate moves to escape. They worked harder to stay unsure than the truth had worked to reach them.

By the end of this ayah, the Quran will have exposed something we rarely admit about ourselves: that there is a profound difference between *not having* certainty and *refusing to seek* it. One of those is an honest condition. The other is a chosen way of life. Let's find out which one this ayah is really about.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "It was said" versus "you said" — the hidden hand and the owned denial

Before we touch the Arabic, let me give you a small picture from ordinary life.

Imagine a true statement and a false statement spoken in the same room. "The building is on fire" — true. "Nothing's wrong, sit down" — false. Years later, when we reconstruct what happened, notice how memory treats them differently. The true warning becomes ownerless — *someone* shouted fire, it doesn't matter who, what matters is that it was true. But the false reassurance gets a name attached to it. *You* told us to sit down. *You* said it was fine. Responsibility clings to the lie. The truth just floats there, belonging to no one and to everyone, because it was simply, plainly true.

Now look at the ayah, because Allah builds exactly this asymmetry into the grammar.

The truth arrives in the passive voice: **قِيلَ** — *qīla* — "it was said." From the root *q-w-l*, a perfect-tense verb, but built so that the speaker disappears. Who said that Allah's promise is true? The ayah doesn't tell us. It doesn't matter. The messenger recedes; the message simply stands. *It was said.* As if the truth needs no spokesman to be true.

And then the denial arrives in the active voice, with a face and a finger pointed at it: **قُلْتُم** — *qultum* — "**you** said." Same root, *q-w-l*, but now second-person plural, fully owned. Not "it was said that the Hour is doubtful." No. *You* said it. You, by name, in the plural, with your own mouths.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. When the truth is delivered, the grammar hides the messenger — because truth doesn't depend on who carries it. But when the denial is uttered, the grammar spotlights the speaker — because a denial is a *choice*, and choices have owners.

This is the first thing the ayah does to us. It quietly refuses to let the deniers be passive victims of confusion. They did not "end up" uncertain. They *said* something. **مَّا نَدْرِى** — "we do not know." That, too, is something *you said*. Even the claim of ignorance, here, is an active utterance — a thing chosen and voiced, not a fog that simply descended.

What this single grammatical contrast reveals: **truth does not need an owner to be true, but denial always has one.** You cannot hide behind "I just wasn't sure" when the not-being-sure was itself something you reached for and spoke aloud.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: "We only assume an assumption" — the prison built from one word

Let me first teach you two features of Arabic, plainly, because everything in this phrase depends on them.

The first is what grammarians call the **maṣdar / mafʿūl muṭlaq** — the cognate accusative. It is when a verb is followed by a noun from its own root. In English it feels strange: "to dream a dream," "to die a death," "to laugh a laugh." Arabic does it constantly, and it almost always does one of two things: it either *intensifies* the action, or it *narrows and defines* it — it pins the action down to exactly this and nothing more.

The second feature is the construction **إِن … إِلَّا** — *in … illā*. Here *in* is a negative particle ("not"), and *illā* means "except." Put them around a sentence and you create what is called *ḥaṣr* — restriction, total confinement. "There is nothing but X." It is a fence. It says: this, and not one thing outside of this.

Now hold both of those, and listen to what they say:

**إِن نَّظُنُّ إِلَّا ظَنًّا** — *in naẓunnu illā ẓannan.*

Word for word: *We do not think — except a thinking.* "We assume nothing but an assumption." Both words, the verb **نَّظُنُّ** and the noun **ظَنًّا**, come from the same root, *Ẓ-n-n* — the root of *ẓann*, conjecture, supposition, the guess that falls short of knowledge.

Look at what they have done to themselves with one sentence. They have taken the verb of guessing, and then chained a noun of guessing onto it — the cognate accusative pinning it down — and then they have built the *in … illā* fence around the whole thing. **We guess — and the only thing we do is guess — and even what we produce by guessing is nothing but a guess.**

[PAUSE]

This is a sentence that locks itself in from the inside. There is no door in it. They are not saying "we have a strong suspicion." They are not even saying "we doubt." They are declaring, as a settled fact about themselves, that conjecture is the ceiling of their world. Nothing rises above *ẓann*. They have made a wall out of a single root and then announced they cannot see over it.

And listen to how the word *sounds.* **نَّظُنُّ … ظَنًّا** — *naẓunnu … ẓannan* — that doubled, buzzing *nūn*, the sound humming back on itself, going nowhere, circling. The very phonetics enact a mind turning in place. Conjecture that loops.

There is a sister verse that turns this from a private failing into a cosmic law. In Surah An-Najm: **إِنَّ ٱلظَّنَّ لَا يُغْنِى مِنَ ٱلْحَقِّ شَيْـًٔا** — "Indeed, conjecture (*ẓann*) avails nothing against the truth (*ḥaqq*)" (53:28). Set that beside our ayah and the trap snaps shut. The believer offered them **حَقٌّ** — *ḥaqq*, truth, solid reality. And they answered with **ظَنًّا** — *ẓann*, conjecture. The Quran has already told us, elsewhere, that these two do not weigh the same. You cannot pay a debt of *ḥaqq* with the currency of *ẓann*. When reality finally arrives, "I figured it was probably nothing" buys you nothing at all.

What this phrase reveals: **a man can imprison himself in a single word and call the prison "open-mindedness."** "We only assume" sounds humble. It is in fact a wall, deliberately built, with the *in … illā* mortaring it shut.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: "We are not those who attain certainty" — the form of seeking, refused

Here is the final word, and it is the one that quietly diagnoses everything.

**وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُسْتَيْقِنِينَ** — *wa mā naḥnu bi-mustayqinīn* — "and we are not *mustayqinīn*."

First, the grammar of emphasis. The phrase is **مَا … بِ** — *mā … bi-*. The *mā* negates, and then this little **بِ** (*bi-*) is glued onto the predicate. In Arabic, that *bi-* is not carrying meaning — it is carrying *force*. It is an emphatic negation. Not merely "we are not convinced," but "we are not *in any way, not at all,* among the convinced." They slam the door and then lean on it.

Now the word itself: **مُسْتَيْقِنِينَ**, from the root *y-q-n* — the root of *yaqīn*, certainty, the highest rung of knowledge. But notice it is not the simple word for "certain." It is built on **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern — *istayqana*, the active participle *mustayqin*. (The corpus confirms it: this word, and only this word in the ayah, carries Form X.)

And Form X — the *istif'āl* pattern — characteristically carries the sense of **seeking, requesting, working toward, reaching out for** the meaning of the root. *Istaghfara* — to *seek* forgiveness. *Istaʿāna* — to *seek* help. So *istayqana* leans toward: to *seek out* certainty, to *come to attain* it, to arrive at *yaqīn* through a process. (That the form carries this seeking-and-attaining sense is the general nature of *istifʿāl*; the precise force here is what this construction invites us to weigh.)

Now feel what they have actually confessed.

They did not say "certainty was hidden from us." They did not say "we searched and found nothing." They said: *we are not, in any way, among those who have sought-and-reached certainty.* The very form of the word names a process — and they are placing themselves firmly outside of it. Not failed seekers. **Non-seekers.**

[PAUSE]

This is the hinge of the whole ayah. Go back and re-read their three statements with this in hand. "We don't know what the Hour is" — but did they try to know? "We only assume" — but is *ẓann* the best a serious person can do, or merely the least effort a person can get away with? And now: "we are not among those who reach certainty" — said with the very word that means *certainty-arrived-at-through-seeking.*

The word indicts them. The road to *yaqīn* was open. It is a road you walk — *istif'āl*, the form of effort. They simply declined to take a single step on it, and then reported their location — "not there" — as though it were a fact about the universe rather than a fact about their feet.

And listen, one more time, to the sound. **مُسْتَيْقِنِينَ** — *mus-tay-qi-nīn* — a long, effortful, drawn-out word, full of syllables you have to climb through to pronounce. The word for certainty-sought is *itself* hard to say. The very mouth has to work to get it out. It *sounds* like effort — the exact effort they refused to spend.

What this word reveals: **certainty about the ultimate things is not handed over like a parcel; it is the destination of a road you are invited to walk. And their tragedy was not that they failed to arrive. It was that they refused to set out.**

[PAUSE — major]

### The bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. The truth came *ownerless* because it needed no owner — *qīla* — while the denial was *owned*, chosen, spoken — *qultum*. They fenced themselves inside a single root, *naẓunnu illā ẓannan*, a wall mistaken for humility. And they placed themselves, with their own emphatic *bi-*, outside the road of seeking — *mā naḥnu bi-mustayqinīn* — non-travelers reporting that they had not arrived.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human heart manages a truth it does not want to face.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: "It's probably nothing" — the difference between not knowing and not seeking

Let me start not with the Hour, but with a body.

You feel something one morning. A lump. A pain that doesn't pass. An ache in a place there shouldn't be one. And a sentence arrives in your mind, fully formed, before you have thought about it at all: *It's probably nothing.*

Now be honest about that sentence. Is it knowledge? You haven't been to a doctor. You haven't run a single test. You have *zero* information. So "it's probably nothing" is not a conclusion — it is a *ẓann*, a guess. But notice what the guess is *doing*. It is not trying to find out. It is trying to let you *avoid* finding out. The guess exists precisely so that you will not have to pick up the phone and book the appointment — because the appointment might tell you something you cannot un-know.

[PAUSE]

The person who books the appointment is the *mustayqin* — the one who walks the road toward certainty even though it is frightening. The person who says "it's probably nothing" and stays on the couch is saying, with their whole life, *mā naḥnu bi-mustayqinīn* — "I am not among those who seek to know for sure."

Do you see it now? This ayah is not describing a strange, ancient Meccan merchant. It is describing the most ordinary human move there is: **when a possibility is too costly to confront, we don't deny it outright — we downgrade it to a guess, and then we use the guess as permission not to investigate.**

The deniers of Mecca did this with the largest possible truth. Resurrection. Standing before Allah. Their entire ledger of deeds opened in front of everyone. That is the ultimate "appointment." And so the entire apparatus of *ẓann* clicks into place — "we don't really know what this Hour is, it's all just speculation anyway, who can say." Not because the evidence was weak. Because the implications were unbearable.

Here is the movement I want you to feel. *Not having* certainty is an honest human condition; we are finite, we don't know everything, and Allah does not condemn finitude. But the ayah is not about people who lack certainty. It is about people who **organized their lives around not seeking it.** The Quran's quarrel is never with the question. It is with the man who manufactures a fog so he never has to find the answer.

And there is something almost merciful in how the Quran exposes this — because the cure is in the diagnosis. If the problem were "I genuinely cannot find certainty," there would be nothing you could do. But the problem named here is "I am not among the seekers." And *that* you can change tonight. The road is built on a verb of effort. You can take a step.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Catch the sentence "it's probably fine" the next time it arrives — about your health, your prayer, your marriage, your standing with Allah. And ask the one question the deniers refused to ask: *Am I saying this because I have looked, or because I am afraid to look?* If it is the second, that is not knowledge. That is the doorway they backed out of. Walk forward instead. Book the appointment — with the doctor, with the Quran, with your own soul.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: "We are not convinced" — when the heart sets the ceiling before the mind looks up

Now I want to go one layer deeper, into something modern psychology has a clean name for: **motivated reasoning.**

We like to believe the mind works in one direction: we gather evidence, weigh it, and *then* arrive at a conclusion. But researchers have shown again and again that the heart very often goes first. We *decide* what we want to be true — usually what is most comfortable, least demanding, least disruptive to the life we have already built — and then the mind goes to work as a lawyer, not a judge. It builds the case for the verdict the heart already wanted.

Watch this happen inside our ayah, in order.

The verdict the heart wants: *I do not want there to be a Day of accounting, because I have built a life that would not survive one.* That is 45:24 — "there is nothing but this worldly life." That is the wish.

Then the mind, the hired lawyer, gets to work and produces, in sequence:
- First, manufactured ignorance: *"We don't know what the Hour even is"* — pretend the concept is too vague to engage.
- Then, forced equivalence: *"We only assume an assumption"* — flatten the believer's *ḥaqq* and your own evasion onto the same level, as if both are mere guessing, so neither side can claim more.
- And finally, the closing argument: *"We are not convinced."*

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing I most want you to see. "We are not convinced" *sounds* like the honest conclusion of someone who weighed the evidence and found it wanting. But the grammar already told us the truth. The word was *mustayqinīn* — the form of *seeking*. They were never convinced because they were never *trying* to be convinced. The verdict came first. The "not convinced" was not where the journey ended. It was decided before the journey ever began.

This is the quiet terror of the verse, and the reason it is preserved for us and not just for them. The most dangerous form of unbelief is not the one that shouts "there is no God." It is the one that calmly says "I'm just not convinced" — while having arranged its entire inner life so that conviction can never arrive. It has set the ceiling of the room at *ẓann* and then complained, sincerely, that it cannot see the sky.

We do this in miniature all the time. We "research" the decision we already made. We "stay open-minded" about the obligation we don't want to fulfill — keeping it permanently in the category of "still thinking about it" so it never becomes a duty. We tell ourselves we're being careful and rigorous, when really we are being *motivated* — protecting a verdict the heart issued long ago.

The antidote the Quran models is breathtakingly simple and it is the opposite of their move. It is *yaqīn* sought honestly: to let the evidence come *before* the verdict. To walk the road of *istif'āl* with your hands open instead of clenched. To dare to ask "what if this is true?" and *follow the question* instead of strangling it in its crib with a shrug.

Notice the mercy here too. The believer's offer was **لَا رَيْبَ فِيهَا** — "no doubt in it." This is the same phrase Allah uses for the Quran itself at the very opening of Surah Al-Baqarah: **ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْكِتَابُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ** — "This is the Book, no doubt in it" (2:2). The same divine guarantee — *lā rayb* — is stamped on the Book and on the Hour. The certainty was *offered*, freely, with Allah's own seal on it. Their tragedy was not a lack of supply. It was a refusal of delivery.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Find one place where you have been "still deciding" for a suspiciously long time — a prayer you keep meaning to make regular, a sin you keep meaning to leave, a relationship with the Quran you keep meaning to begin. Ask yourself with brutal honesty: *Am I actually weighing this, or did my heart decide long ago and is now just stalling?* Then do the thing that proves the heart no longer sets the ceiling: take one concrete step toward the conclusion you've been avoiding. Conviction, in matters of the soul, often *follows* the first step rather than preceding it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architectural reveal

Now stand back and look at the whole shape of the ayah, because the architecture is the proof of everything we have said.

The truth was offered in **two** affirmations:
1. **إِنَّ وَعْدَ ٱللَّهِ حَقٌّ** — "the promise of Allah is *true*" (*ḥaqq*)
2. **وَٱلسَّاعَةُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهَا** — "and the Hour — *no doubt* in it" (*lā rayb*)

Two clean statements. Reality, and the absence of doubt.

And the evasion answered with **three** descents:
1. **مَّا نَدْرِى مَا ٱلسَّاعَةُ** — manufactured ignorance ("we don't know what it is")
2. **إِن نَّظُنُّ إِلَّا ظَنًّا** — reduction to conjecture ("we only guess")
3. **وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُسْتَيْقِنِينَ** — refusal of the road ("we are not seekers of certainty")

[PAUSE]

Two truths met with three evasions. Let that asymmetry land. They expended *more* effort escaping the truth than the truth expended reaching them. It takes one breath to say "the Hour is real, no doubt in it." It took them three separate maneuvers to wriggle out from under it. **Denial is not lazy. It is industrious.** It works overtime. The man who "just isn't sure" is often the hardest-working man in the room — it costs enormous energy to keep a truth this size at bay.

And notice the descent itself is a staircase going *down*. From *ḥaqq* (truth) they step down to *ẓann* (conjecture), and from *ẓann* they step down to the refusal even to seek. Each rung lower than the last. The Quran lets us watch a soul climb down a ladder it could just as easily have climbed up — because the very same word, *mustayqinīn*, that names their refusal also names the road back.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two sides.

The grammar showed us that truth is *ownerless* and denial is *owned* — *qīla* versus *qultum*. The themes showed us why: because denial is a *choice* the heart makes before the mind looks. The grammar showed us a wall built from one root — *naẓunnu illā ẓannan* — and the themes showed us that we build that wall ourselves, to avoid an appointment we are afraid to keep. The grammar showed us a word of *seeking*, refused — *mustayqinīn* — and the themes showed us that the road of seeking is still open, tonight, to anyone willing to take one honest step.

It all converges on a single, uncomfortable, liberating truth: **the gap between you and certainty is not a gap in the evidence. It is a gap you can choose to cross.**

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life have I said "it's probably nothing" — not because I looked, but because I was afraid to look?
2. Is there a truth about my soul, my deeds, or my death that I keep filed under "speculation" precisely so I never have to act on it?
3. When I say "I'm not really sure," have I actually *sought* — walked the road of *istiqān* — or have I simply declined to set out and called my standing-still a conclusion?
4. Did my heart issue a verdict long ago, and is my mind now just building the case for it?
5. The certainty was *offered* — *lā rayba fīhā*. Am I refusing delivery of something Allah is holding out to me?

### One-sentence distillation

The deniers were not lost because certainty was unavailable to them — it was offered with Allah's own seal — but because they reduced the greatest truth to a guess and placed themselves outside the road that leads to *yaqīn*, mistaking their refusal to seek for an honest inability to find.

### The closing invitation

This ayah is not, in the end, about them. It is a mirror held up to the most ordinary motion of the human heart — the shrug, the "I guess," the comfortable fog we manufacture so the demanding truth never quite resolves into focus. The invitation is not to suddenly *feel* certain. It is gentler and harder than that: it is to become someone who *seeks* — a *mustayqin* — to let the evidence walk in before you have already shown it the door, to dare to ask "what if it's true?" and then follow the question wherever it leads. The road is built from a verb of effort. You are invited to take one step on it today.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You stamped Your promise with truth and Your Hour with *no doubt* — do not let me answer Your certainty with a shrug. Make me of those who *seek* until they find — *al-mustayqinīn* — and do not let my heart pass its verdict before my eyes have looked up. Save me from the comfortable fog of "I guess," and bring me to the clear morning of *yaqīn*. Āmīn.
