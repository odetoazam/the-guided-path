# Built for the Verdict

### *Surah Al-Jathiyah, Ayah 22*

> [ayah:45:22] وَخَلَقَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَلِتُجْزَىٰ كُلُّ نَفْسٍۭ بِمَا كَسَبَتْ وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ

*Wa khalaqa-Llāhu as-samāwāti wal-arḍa bil-ḥaqqi wa li-tujzā kullu nafsin bimā kasabat wa hum lā yuẓlamūn.*

**"And Allah created the heavens and the earth in truth — and so that every soul may be recompensed for what it has earned, and they will not be wronged."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me take you back into the room where this verse first landed.

There was a way of thinking in Makkah — and it has never really left the human race — that went something like this: *Life is just this. You're born, you struggle, you die. Time grinds everyone down equally. The good man and the cruel man both end up as dust. There is no scale, no court, no final accounting. Whatever you can grab, grab it. Whoever you can step on, step on them. Because in the end, nothing is weighed.*

The Qur'an names these people just two verses later. They said: *"There is nothing but our worldly life — we die and we live, and nothing destroys us but time"* (45:24). *Mā yuhlikunā illā ad-dahr.* Only time. The universe, to them, was indifferent matter. A machine with no judge.

And right before our ayah, in verse 21, Allah surfaces the moral conclusion these people had quietly drawn. He asks:

*"Or do those who commit evil deeds think that We will make them like those who believe and do righteous deeds — equal in their life and their death?"*

Sit with that question for a moment, because everything hangs on it. The criminal had made a calculation. Deep down he assumed: *In the end, the murderer and the martyr come out the same. The one who built and the one who burned — same grave, same silence, same nothing.* He had flattened the moral universe into a single grey plane where nothing finally distinguishes the saint from the savage.

[PAUSE]

And then comes verse 22. Notice — Allah does not answer this with a threat. He does not begin with hellfire. He answers the materialist on the materialist's own ground. He points to the very thing the materialist worships — the cosmos, the heavens, the earth, the sheer physical machinery of existence — and He says: *look again at what you think is meaningless matter.*

**"And Allah created the heavens and the earth in truth."**

Here is the hook, and I want you to feel its strangeness before we unlock it. Allah is about to argue for the certainty of *moral* justice — the weighing of every soul — by pointing at the *physical* sky. As if the structure of the galaxies and the verdict on your life were somehow the same fact, spoken twice.

How can the existence of the heavens prove the certainty of your reckoning?

That is the question this verse will answer. Stay with me.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One — بِٱلْحَقِّ: The Two-Letter Word That Refuses an Empty Universe

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word.

In Arabic, one of the smallest, most unassuming particles is the letter **ب** — the *bā'*. It is a preposition, and like the English "with" or "by" or "in," it can do many quiet jobs. It can mean accompaniment — *I came with my brother.* It can mean instrument — *I wrote with a pen.* It can describe the manner or state in which something is done — *he spoke with honesty,* meaning he spoke truthfully, in a truthful way.

Now look at the phrase: **بِٱلْحَقِّ** — *bil-ḥaqq.* The *bā'* fused to *al-ḥaqq*, "the truth." Two letters and a noun. Easy to read straight past.

But here is what the *bā'* is doing. Allah did not say He created the heavens and the earth *and* truth, as if truth were a separate object placed beside them. He said He created them **bil-ḥaqq** — *with*, *by*, *in* truth. The truth is the medium of their making. It is woven into the act of creation itself, the way honesty is woven into honest speech. You cannot peel the truth off the heavens and find the heavens still standing underneath. The truth is in the grain of the thing.

Let's sit with this.

What does *al-ḥaqq* actually mean here? The classical commentators are remarkably consistent, and they unlock it for us by telling us what it is *not*. Al-Qurtubi glosses it as *bil-amr al-ḥaqq* — "by the true matter," the real purpose, as opposed to the empty one. And the wider Qur'an supplies the contrast in unmistakable terms. In Surah Sad: *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them in vain* — *bāṭilan"* (38:27). In Surah Ad-Dukhan, just one surah before this one: *"We did not create the heavens and the earth and what is between them as players. We created them only in truth — illā bil-ḥaqq"* (44:38–39).

So *al-ḥaqq* here stands against three things: against *bāṭil* (falsehood, the void), against *laʿib* (play, the trivial), against *ʿabath* (pointlessness). To say the cosmos was made **bil-ḥaqq** is to say, with the full weight of the word: *this was not a toy. This was not an accident. This was not a machine spinning for no reason. This has a point, and the point is real.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel what just happened to the materialist's argument.

He looked at the same sky and said: *meaningless matter, indifferent time.* Allah looks at it and says: *bil-ḥaqq* — saturated with purpose, structured by truth, the very opposite of empty. The two of them are staring at the identical heavens. One sees a void. The other sees a verdict being prepared.

And here is the structural genius of the verse — something the grammar makes plain. Al-Jalalayn notes that *bil-ḥaqq* is grammatically attached to *khalaqa*, "created," and that it points to Allah's power and His oneness. The truth in the making of the heavens is itself a sign — an evidence — of the One who made them and of the reality that this making is going somewhere.

What this single two-letter particle reveals is staggering: **the universe is not morally neutral. Truth is built into its foundations the way load-bearing steel is built into a tower.** And if truth is in the foundation, then what truth always demands — that things be set right, that accounts be settled — must be coming too.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two — وَلِتُجْزَىٰ: The Letter That Tells You Why the Sky Exists

Now watch what Allah hangs onto that same act of creation next. He says: **وَلِتُجْزَىٰ** — *wa li-tujzā.*

Let me teach the structure first.

In Arabic there is a particle — the letter **ل**, the *lām* — that, when placed before a verb in a particular form, means *"in order that," "so that," "for the purpose of."* Grammarians call it the *lām of taʿlīl*, the *lām* of purpose-giving, the *lām* of reason. When you see it, you are being told *why* something exists or was done. *I came to the door li-aftaḥahā — in order to open it.* The *lām* answers the question: for what end?

Al-Qurtubi confirms it for us exactly: *"wa li-tujzā — ay wa li-kay tujzā"* — "and so that," "in order that," every soul be recompensed.

Now read the two phrases together as the grammar joins them, with the connecting *wāw*:

*khalaqa... bil-ḥaqq* — He created in truth —
*wa li-tujzā kullu nafs* — **and so that** every soul be recompensed.

Do you see what these two clauses are doing, side by side? They are two answers to the same question: *Why the heavens and the earth?* Answer one: **bil-ḥaqq** — because of truth, in truth. Answer two: **li-tujzā** — so that every soul be paid back for what it earned.

[PAUSE]

Let this land. The recompense of your deeds is not presented here as an afterthought to creation. It is presented as one of the *reasons creation happened at all.* The Day of Judgment is not an epilogue stapled onto the universe once the story is over. According to the grammar of this verse, **the weighing of your soul is part of why the sky was raised in the first place.**

Here is the analogy that carries this, and I want it to do real work.

Imagine you walk into a vast building. The ceilings soar. The architecture is breathtaking. And at the center of it all is a single elevated bench, a witness stand, a place for the accused, and a great pair of scales carved above the door. You would understand instantly: *this is a courthouse.* Every column, every corridor, every beam was raised *for the sake of* what happens at that bench. The building is not a courthouse that happens to contain a courtroom. The courtroom is *why the building exists.* Remove the trial, and the architecture loses its reason.

The *lām* of purpose in **لِتُجْزَى** is telling you: the heavens and the earth are that building. The verdict on your soul is that bench. The cosmos was raised *for the sake of* the reckoning. The materialist walked into the courthouse and called it an empty warehouse.

And now notice the verb itself — **تُجْزَى**, *tujzā.* It is passive. "So that every soul *be recompensed.*" Allah does not say *li-najziya* — "so that *We* recompense." He removes the actor from the front of the sentence and leaves only the act and its certainty.

Why hide the One who pays?

Because the point here is not *who* delivers the verdict — that is never in doubt — the point is that the verdict *is delivered.* The passive voice strips away everything except the inevitability of the outcome. It is the difference between saying "the judge will sentence the prisoner" and simply saying "the prisoner will be sentenced." The second somehow feels more final, more woven into the order of things — as though it will happen whether you can name the judge or not. **The grammar makes the recompense feel less like a decision someone might make and more like a law the universe cannot break.**

What these two letters — the *bā'* of truth and the *lām* of purpose — reveal together is this: the cosmos was built truly, and it was built *for* the verdict. Truth is its fabric; recompense is its purpose. They are not two facts. They are one fact, stated from two sides.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three — كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ... بِمَا كَسَبَتْ ... وَهُمْ: The Soul, the Earning, and the Shift to "Them"

Now we come to the heart of the recompense, and there are three precise things in the Arabic I need you to see.

**First: كُلُّ نَفْسٍ — *kullu nafsin* — "every soul."**

The word *kull* means "every," "each." It is distributive — it does not gather people into a crowd; it picks them out one by one. Not *the souls will be recompensed* as a herd, but *every single soul*, individually, each one separately. And *nafs* — the soul, the self, the person — is grammatically singular and feminine. The verse zooms all the way in. From the immensity of *the heavens and the earth* in the first half — the largest things imaginable — it narrows in a single breath to *one soul.* Yours. The same truth that holds the galaxies in place is now bending its full attention onto one individual self.

**Second: بِمَا كَسَبَتْ — *bimā kasabat* — "for what it has earned."**

The root here is *kasaba* — *kasb* — and this is one of the most precise words in the Qur'an's vocabulary of accountability. *Kasb* is not what merely happens to you. *Kasb* is *acquisition* — it is earning, the way you earn a wage. It is the deed you reached out and took, the act you committed and that now belongs to you the way wages belong to the worker who put in the hours. The Qur'an uses this exact word elsewhere with chilling balance: *"It shall have what it has earned (kasabat), and against it shall be what it has earned (iktasabat)"* (2:286). You are paid in the currency of your own doing.

And notice the tense. *Kasabat* is **perfect** — completed, done, in the past. *Tujzā* — "be recompensed" — is **imperfect**, ongoing, yet to come. The earning is finished; the payment is pending. There is a gap between them.

This is the farmer's gap. Let me give you the analogy. A farmer goes out in the spring and sows. The sowing is *kasb* — done, complete, the seed is in the ground and cannot be un-sown. Then comes the long silence of the season. And then, in its appointed time, the harvest — *tujzā* — comes up, exactly and only from what was planted. You do not harvest what you did not sow, and you do not fail to harvest what you did. The gap between sowing and reaping does not mean the seed was forgotten. It means the season is not yet complete.

The materialist mistook the gap for absence. He sowed cruelty, saw no immediate harvest, and concluded there would never be one. But *kasabat* is perfect and *tujzā* is imperfect — the deed is filed, sealed, complete; the harvest is simply not yet in season.

[PAUSE]

**Third — and this is the most delicate stroke in the whole verse — وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ — *wa hum lā yuẓlamūn* — "and they will not be wronged."**

Watch the pronoun. The verse has been speaking in the singular: *every soul* (*nafs*), *what it earned* (*kasabat*) — singular, singular, singular. And then suddenly: **هم** — *hum* — "they." Plural.

This is a deliberate grammatical turn — the Arabic rhetoricians call this kind of shift *iltifāt*, a sudden pivot in person or number that jolts you awake and carries meaning in the very turning. Why does Allah move from "every soul" to "they"?

Because the two halves are doing two different consolations.

The singular — *every soul, by what it earned* — **individualizes the justice.** No one hides in the crowd. No one is judged by association, by tribe, by the company they kept. Each *nafs* stands alone before its own ledger. That is the part that should make you tremble.

Then the plural — *and they will not be wronged* — **collectivizes the reassurance.** It sweeps back out to take in the whole gathering, the entire human mass standing on the Day, and it lays one blanket guarantee over all of them at once: not one of you, in all this multitude, will be done an atom of injustice.

And again — look — **لَا يُظْلَمُونَ** is passive: "they will not *be* wronged." The wronger is, once more, unnamed. The verse refuses even to construct a sentence in which someone could be the agent of injustice against them. The grammar itself will not give injustice a place to stand.

Now hear what *ẓulm* means at its root. *Ẓulm* — to wrong — is, in the classical lexicon, *to put a thing in other than its rightful place.* To give the deserving less than they are owed, or to charge the innocent with what they did not do. *Lā yuẓlamūn* therefore means two things at once, and both are mercy: the good will not be **shortchanged** — not one ounce of their reward withheld — *and* the accused will not be **overcharged** — not one sin laid on them that they did not earn. Elsewhere the Qur'an spells out the precision: *"We place the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be wronged at all; even if it be the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it"* (21:47).

[PAUSE]

So now bring the three together. **Every soul** — you are not a statistic. **By what it earned** — you are judged by your own hand, not your neighbor's. **And they will not be wronged** — and in all of it, across the whole of humanity, the scale will not slip by the weight of a seed.

That is the full shape of the recompense the heavens were built to deliver.

---

### Bridge

Look at what these three movements of grammar have shown us. The *bā'* of *bil-ḥaqq* told us the universe is woven from truth, not emptiness. The *lām* of *li-tujzā* told us that the verdict on the soul is *why* the universe stands. And *kullu nafsin bimā kasabat wa hum lā yuẓlamūn* told us exactly how that verdict will be rendered — individually, by one's own earning, and without the faintest shadow of injustice.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about how you are going to live tomorrow morning.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One — The Universe Is Not Indifferent (And Your Sense of Justice Was Never a Mistake)

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of pain that almost everyone carries, and most people never name it precisely. It is the pain of watching someone get away with it. The colleague who lied and got promoted. The relative who was cruel and died comfortable in his bed, mourned and praised. The regime that crushed thousands and was never brought to a single courtroom. You watch this, and something inside you protests. Something says: *this is not right. This cannot be the end of it. There must be more than this.*

Modern thought has a name for that protest, and it is not a flattering one. It calls it a *cognitive bias.* The "just-world fallacy." A comforting illusion the human brain invented to cope with a universe that, in cold fact, doesn't care. On this view, your hunger for justice is a glitch — a leftover survival instinct projecting meaning onto meaningless matter. The mature thing, you're told, is to grow out of it. The universe is indifferent. Get used to it.

That is *exactly* the worldview of the man in verse 21 — the one who assumed the wicked and the righteous come out the same. And here is what I want you to feel: **this ayah does not argue with that man's despair by appealing to your feelings. It tells you your feelings were reading the universe correctly all along.**

[PAUSE]

The verse says the heavens and the earth were created **bil-ḥaqq** — in truth, with purpose, the opposite of empty. So when you look at injustice and your whole being recoils and insists *this is not the final word* — you are not malfunctioning. You are not coping. You are *perceiving the grain of the cosmos.* Your moral sense is not a bias laid over an indifferent universe. It is an echo of the truth the universe was actually built from.

Think about what it would mean if the materialist were right. If the universe is truly *bāṭil* — empty, in vain — then your outrage at cruelty has no more standing than your preference for one color over another. Nothing is *owed*. The torturer and the rescuer are simply two arrangements of atoms, neither better. On that view you cannot even *coherently* be angry at injustice, because "injustice" would just be a word for "things I personally dislike."

But you cannot live that way. Nobody can. The moment someone wrongs *you*, the philosophy evaporates and the deep certainty roars back: *that was wrong, and it should be set right.* That certainty is not weakness. According to this ayah, it is the most accurate instrument you own. It is the *ḥaqq* in the foundations, registering in your chest.

[PAUSE]

Now let me push one layer deeper, because there is a paradox here worth sitting in.

If the universe is built for justice, *why the delay?* Why does the cruel man die comfortable? Why is the harvest so far from the sowing?

Remember the grammar: *kasabat* is perfect, *tujzā* is imperfect. The deed is done; the payment is pending. The gap is real — but the gap is not absence. It is *season.* And here is the mercy hidden inside the delay that we rarely notice: that same gap, which lets the cruel man walk free a while longer, is *also* the space in which **you** are still sowing. If the harvest came the instant the seed touched the soil — if every sin detonated on contact — there would be no room left for repentance, no time to plant something better over the weeds. The delay that troubles you when you watch the wicked is the very same delay that is, right now, keeping your own door of return open.

The universe is not indifferent. It is patient. And patience can look like indifference only to someone who has mistaken the gap for the end.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It means you can stop performing the exhausting role of cosmic prosecutor. So much of our bitterness is the unconscious belief that *if I don't keep the account, no one will.* That every wrong I don't personally avenge will simply vanish unpaid. This verse lifts that weight off your shoulders. The account is kept — *bil-ḥaqq*, in a ledger more precise than any you could keep, by One who will not let a mustard seed slip. You are released from the role of judge. You are freed to be merciful, to let go, to forgive — not because the wrong didn't matter, but because you finally trust that it is *held.*

## Theme Two — *Kullu Nafs*: The Terror and the Mercy of Standing Alone

Now I want to take you somewhere more uncomfortable, because this verse does not only comfort the wronged. It also stands *you* before the bench.

Let me begin again where you live.

We are creatures who hide in crowds. It is one of our oldest instincts. When something goes wrong in a group, watch how the language shifts: *mistakes were made. Everyone was doing it. It was the culture. The system. The times.* We have a thousand ways to dissolve the individual self into the collective so that no single soul has to carry the weight. Psychologists call it diffusion of responsibility — in a crowd of bystanders, each person feels less obligated, because surely someone else will act, surely the blame is spread thin enough that none of it lands on me.

And there is real comfort in that diffusion. To be one of many is to be, in a sense, no one in particular. To be unaccountable.

Then comes the word **كُلُّ نَفْسٍ** — *kullu nafsin* — and it does something the modern mind finds almost unbearable. **It dissolves the crowd.**

*Kull* — every. *Nafs* — single soul. The grammar refuses the herd. It picks you out, one at a time, and stands you alone. On that Day there is no "everyone was doing it," because the verse does not say *the souls* will be judged collectively — it says *every* soul, *by what it* — singular, feminine, *it* — *earned.* Your earning. Not the culture's. Not the times'. Not the crowd you stood in while it happened. Yours.

The Qur'an makes this aloneness vivid elsewhere: *"And all of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone — fardan"* (19:95). The crowd you hid in your whole life is gone. The diffusion is over. There is only the soul and its ledger.

[PAUSE]

This should frighten us a little. It is *meant* to. The whole verse exists to answer a man who was counting on the crowd — counting on being lumped in, on the wicked and the righteous being averaged together into one undifferentiated grey. *Kullu nafsin bimā kasabat* is the demolition of that hope. There will be no averaging. There will be no statistical blur. Each soul, alone, by its own hand.

But — and here is where you must not leave too early — the very same word that terrifies is, from the other side, the deepest mercy in the verse.

Think about what *individual* accountability actually protects you *from.*

It protects you from being damned by association. In every human court, the innocent are swept up with the guilty. Whole families punished for one member's crime. Whole peoples blamed for the acts of a few. Reputations destroyed because of the company one kept. The collective is *brutal* to the individual — it crushes the one for the sins of the many, and it lets the guilty one vanish into the many. *Kullu nafsin bimā kasabat* abolishes both injustices at once. You will *not* be charged for what your father did, your nation did, your community did. *Bimā kasabat* — by what **it** earned. Only your own hand can write in your ledger.

And then the seal: **وَهُمْ لَا يُظْلَمُونَ** — *and they will not be wronged.* The double guarantee we uncovered in the grammar. The righteous will not be **shortchanged** — every hidden kindness, every unseen tear, every quiet act no human ever witnessed or thanked you for, is on the ledger, in full. And the accused will not be **overcharged** — not one sin you did not commit, not one weight that was not truly yours, will be laid against you.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the strange double motion of this? The same word that strips away your hiding place is the word that guarantees you perfect, personal, exquisitely precise justice. The terror and the mercy are the *same fact* seen from two angles — exactly as *bil-ḥaqq* and *li-tujzā* were the same fact seen from two angles.

To stand alone before the bench is terrifying *because* no one will carry your weight for you. To stand alone before the bench is merciful *because* no one's weight will be dumped onto you. The aloneness cuts both ways, and a just God would not have it any other way.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Two things, and they pull in beautiful opposite directions.

First: it means *your hidden deeds matter infinitely.* Every act you do when no one is watching — the prayer in the empty room, the charity no one knows about, the temptation you walked away from in private, the resentment you swallowed and replaced with a prayer — none of it is lost in the crowd. *Bimā kasabat.* It is all earning, all filed, all coming back to you in full. The unseen life is the real life.

Second: it means you can *stop carrying ledgers that were never yours.* So many of us are crushed under inherited guilt and borrowed shame — the weight of a family's failures, a community's hypocrisies, a history we did not write. This verse hands those ledgers back. You answer for *your* earning. Carry that. Carry it seriously. But put down what was never yours to carry. *Wa hum lā yuẓlamūn* — you will not be wronged, and that means you will not be charged a single sin that your own hand did not earn.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole verse — because it is built with a symmetry we could not have appreciated at the start.

The verse opens at the **largest possible scale**: *the heavens and the earth* — the entire cosmos, everything there is. And it closes at the **smallest possible scale**: *every single soul* — you, one self, standing alone. The verse travels from the galaxies to your heartbeat in a single breath.

And what connects the two ends? A single word, sitting at the hinge: **بِٱلْحَقِّ** — *bil-ḥaqq.* Truth.

The truth in the heavens and the earth → and the truth in the weighing of every soul. The same *ḥaqq* runs from one end of the verse to the other, from the widest thing to the most intimate. The verse is telling you, in its very architecture, that **the justice coming for your soul is made of the same substance as the structure of the universe.** They are not two truths. They are one *ḥaqq*, spanning from the outermost edge of creation to the innermost chamber of your accountability.

That is the architecture. The cosmos and the conscience, bridged by a single word.

## Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic discoveries and the themes turned out to be the same insight, approached from two roads.

The **grammar** showed us: *bil-ḥaqq* — the universe is woven from truth, not emptiness. The **theme** showed us: your hunger for justice was never a bias — it was your soul reading that truth correctly.

The **grammar** showed us: *li-tujzā* — the verdict is *why* the heavens were raised. The **theme** showed us: the universe is not indifferent; it is a courthouse, patiently in session.

The **grammar** showed us: *kullu nafsin bimā kasabat wa hum lā yuẓlamūn* — individual earning, no one wronged. The **theme** showed us: the terror of standing alone and the mercy of standing alone are the same gift.

The materialist looked at the sky and saw a void. We have looked at the same sky, through the same words, and seen a verdict being prepared *bil-ḥaqq* — and our own restless conscience turned out to be its faithful witness.

## Questions to Carry

Let these stay open. Do not rush to answer them.

1. When I watch someone "get away with it," what does my refusal to accept that — *really* — tell me about what I believe the universe is made of?

2. If the cosmos was raised *li-tujzā*, for the sake of the verdict — am I living as someone walking through a courthouse, or as someone wandering an empty warehouse?

3. *Kasabat* is perfect, *tujzā* is imperfect. What did I sow today that I will one day, certainly, harvest? Would I want this season's planting back?

4. Whose ledger am I carrying that was never mine to carry — and what would it feel like to finally set it down before the One who said *wa hum lā yuẓlamūn*?

5. *Kullu nafsin* — alone. When the crowd I hide in dissolves, what does my unwitnessed life — the deeds no one but Allah ever saw — actually look like?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The same truth Allah wove into the fabric of the heavens and the earth is the truth that guarantees not one soul's earning will go unweighed — the cosmos is a courthouse built for your verdict, and your own thirst for justice is the proof that you already know it.

## The Closing Invitation

Here is what I would invite to shift in you after sitting with this verse.

Stop looking at the sky as a backdrop. The next time you step outside at night and the heavens are above you, let the verse rise with them: *bil-ḥaqq.* Made in truth. Made for the verdict. And then let your eyes come back down — from the galaxies to the single soul that is you — and feel the *ḥaqq* run the whole distance, from the farthest star into your own chest.

And then live like someone who is *seen.* Not surveilled — *seen.* Seen by a Judge who will not shortchange your smallest hidden good and will not overcharge you a single sin you did not earn. There is a freedom in that the materialist will never taste: the freedom to do good that no one will ever know about, and to let go of wrongs you will never have to avenge — because the account is kept, the season is coming, and *they will not be wronged.*

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who created the heavens and the earth in truth — let that same truth settle into my chest, so that I live not as one wandering an empty world, but as one walking knowingly toward Your bench. Make my earning, when it is finally weighed, something I would be glad to harvest. And when I stand before You alone, by what my own hand has earned — deal with me by Your mercy before Your justice, for You have promised that not one soul will be wronged.*

*Āmīn.*
