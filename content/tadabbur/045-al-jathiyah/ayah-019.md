# The Crowd That Cannot Save You: Two Kinds of Belonging

## Introduction

Let me give you the verse first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single word of meaning.

> إِنَّهُمْ لَن يُغْنُوا۟ عَنكَ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍ ۖ وَٱللَّهُ وَلِىُّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ

*Innahum lan yughnū ʿanka mina-llāhi shayʾā. Wa-inna-ẓ-ẓālimīna baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ. Wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn.*

"Indeed, they will never avail you against Allah in anything. And indeed, the wrongdoers are allies of one another, while Allah is the Protector of those who are mindful of Him."

Now let me rebuild the world this verse landed in.

The verse just before it had done something enormous. Allah turned to His Messenger ﷺ and said: *"Then We placed you upon an ordained way — a sharīʿah — concerning the matter, so follow it, and do not follow the desires of those who do not know."* (45:18)

Picture what that command actually meant on the ground. The Prophet ﷺ is in a society where truth is a minority position. The people pulling at him — the leaders of Quraysh, the influential families, later the factions of Madinah — are not random individuals. They are *networked*. They have wealth. They have lineage. They have each other. When they call him toward their way, they are not just offering an opinion. They are offering belonging. They are offering safety in numbers. They are saying, in effect: *look how many of us there are, look how connected we are, look how much we can do for you — or to you.*

And here is the human pressure baked into that moment. When someone stands alone on the truth, and the falsehood in front of them is organized, allied, and powerful — every instinct in the body whispers: *maybe lean a little. Maybe these are the people who matter. Maybe their approval is the thing that will protect you.*

[PAUSE]

So verse 19 arrives to answer a question that was never spoken out loud, but was sitting in the air like humidity. The question is: *they have all this — all this alliance, all this power — does it count for anything?*

And the answer Allah gives is not a soft "be careful of them." The answer is a complete demolition of the premise. He takes the very thing that makes the crowd seem powerful — their numbers, their mutual loyalty — and shows that it is structurally incapable of doing the one thing that ultimately matters.

Here is the hook. By the end of this verse, Allah will have placed two kinds of belonging in front of you. One of them has many members and is utterly hollow. The other has only one Member named — and is the only real security in existence. And the hinge between them, the secret of which camp you end up in, is hidden in a single word that describes not what you *have*, but what you *do to yourself*.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Lan Yughnū* — The Root of Wealth, Drained to Zero

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word, because the whole force of this phrase depends on it.

In English, when we want to deny something will happen in the future, we say "they will not." It's a flat negation. But Arabic has a special tool — the particle **لَن** *(lan)* — that doesn't just deny the future. It *seals* it. *Lan* carries a tone of emphatic, decisive future negation. It's the difference between saying "this won't happen" and saying "this will *never* happen, don't even entertain it." When you hear *lan*, a door is being shut and bolted.

Now hold that bolted door in your mind, and look at the verb it's bolting:

> لَن يُغْنُوا
> *lan yughnū* — "they will never avail."

Here is where it becomes beautiful. The translation says "avail," but I want you to meet the root underneath. The verb *yughnū* comes from the root **غ-ن-ي** *(gh-n-y)*.

Do you know what else comes from that root?

*Ghinā* — wealth. *Ghaniyy* — the rich one, the self-sufficient one. *Al-Ghaniyy* — one of the names of Allah, The Absolutely Self-Sufficient, the One who needs nothing. This is the root of **richness, sufficiency, having-no-need**.

Let's sit with this, because it's doing something most translations cannot carry. The verb here is in Form IV — *aghnā / yughnī* — which takes that root of richness and turns it outward: *to make someone rich, to make someone free of need, to suffice someone, to be enough for them in the face of a danger.*

So when Allah says *lan yughnū ʿanka mina-llāhi shayʾā*, the literal architecture is something like: **"They will never be able to make you rich — to give you sufficiency, to be enough for you — against Allah, in anything at all."**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? The crowd presents itself as a source of *ghinā* — of enrichment, of resource, of "we can provide for you, we can cover you, we can be enough for you." That is the entire pitch of the powerful in-group. *Lean on us and you'll never lack.*

And Allah takes the exact root of that promise — the root of wealth and sufficiency — and bolts it shut with *lan*. The very thing they advertise, *enrichment, sufficiency*, is the precise thing they can never deliver where it counts. They cannot make you *ghaniyy* — free of need — before Allah. Not a little. Not partially.

Because look at the last word of the phrase:

> شَيْـًٔا
> *shayʾā* — "anything."

This is the seal on the seal. *Shayʾan* is indefinite, and placed here it functions to sweep the floor completely — "not in any thing, not in the smallest thing." It's the linguistic equivalent of turning your pockets inside out to show they're empty. Not "they won't help much." Not "they'll only partly avail you." **Anything. At all. Zero.**

And notice precisely *where* this zero applies: *mina-llāh* — "against Allah," "in the matter of Allah," in the face of Allah's reckoning. This is the crucial qualifier, and the scholars are unanimous on its sense. Al-Qurtubī, al-Baghawī, and the Jalālayn all gloss it the same way: *they will not repel from you anything of Allah's punishment.*

Here's what's remarkable about that qualifier. Human alliances are *not* useless in general. The crowd can genuinely help you against other humans. It can get you a job. It can defend your reputation. It can win you an argument, a war, an election. The verse doesn't pretend otherwise. It pinpoints the *one arena* where every horizontal alliance hits an absolute wall: **mina-llāh.** Before Allah. There, and only there, the most powerful network on earth has exactly the availing-power of a man trying to make himself rich with counterfeit money.

So the first thing this verse teaches us, just from three words, is this: **the very resource the crowd advertises — sufficiency, enrichment, having-your-back — is bolted to zero in the only place it ultimately matters.** *Lan yughnū... shayʾā.* They cannot make you rich before Allah by even a single thing.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Baʿḍuhum Awliyāʾu Baʿḍ* — The Closed Circle

Now Allah does something a teacher does. Having shown you that the crowd can't help you, He shows you *why* — by revealing what the crowd actually *is* on the inside.

> وَإِنَّ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍ
> *wa-inna-ẓ-ẓālimīna baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ*
> "And indeed, the wrongdoers — some of them are allies of others."

Let me teach the key word first, because everything hinges on it. The word is **أَوْلِيَآء** *(awliyāʾ)*, plural of **وَلِيّ** *(waliyy)*, from the root **و-ل-ي** *(w-l-y)*. And this root is one of the richest in the entire Qurʾan.

At its core, *w-l-y* means **nearness** — to be so close to something that nothing comes between you and it. From that single idea of closeness, the whole family blooms: a *waliyy* is a friend (one who is near you), a protector (one near enough to shield you), an ally (one who stands beside you), a guardian (one entrusted with you), even an heir and a master. Closeness that becomes responsibility. Closeness that becomes loyalty. Closeness that becomes *belonging to one another.*

So *baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* is not just "they cooperate." It's: **they belong to one another. They are each other's protectors, each other's loyalists, each other's people.** Al-Qurtubī unpacks *awliyāʾ* here precisely this way — *aṣdiqāʾ wa anṣār wa aḥbāb* — friends, supporters, beloveds. This is full-blooded belonging. The wrongdoers are not a loose collection of bad individuals; they are a *community*, bound by mutual loyalty.

Now — listen to the *shape* of the phrase. *Baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ.* "Some of them... of others." *Baʿḍ... baʿḍ.* The word loops back on itself. Say it aloud and you can hear the structure: it goes *out* to "some" and comes *back* to "some," out and back, a closed ring. The grammar itself draws a circle. They prop each other up — A leans on B, B leans on A — and the whole structure curves back into itself with **no point of exit and no anchor outside the ring.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the heart of it. What is *missing* from this picture?

Read the clause again. The wrongdoers have each other. They have alliance, loyalty, numbers, belonging. And there is **no mention whatsoever of Allah being with them.** Their entire support structure is horizontal. It is a ring of human beings holding each other up, and not one of them is holding onto anything *above* the ring.

This is why the first part of the verse said *lan yughnū... shayʾā.* Now you see the mechanism. A circle of people who all derive their security from *each other* has no security *at all* the moment a force from *outside* the circle arrives. They are like a group of drowning men clutching one another for support. Each one feels held. Each one *is* holding. And the entire cluster sinks together, because not one of them is attached to anything that floats.

Their loyalty is real. Their belonging is real. And it is **structurally worthless** against Allah — not because alliances are bad, but because *this* alliance has no vertical dimension. It is sealed off at the top.

And notice the word Allah chose to name them: **ٱلظَّـٰلِمِين** *(al-ẓālimīn)* — the wrongdoers, from the root **Ẓ-l-m**. Lexically, *ẓulm* is "to put a thing in other than its rightful place." And the very same root family gives us *ẓulmah* — **darkness**. Drink that in. The people in this closed ring are, by their very name, *those who have put things out of place* — and who dwell, by the resonance of their own title, in darkness. A ring of mutual loyalty, in the dark, holding onto each other and nothing else.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa-llāhu Waliyyu-l-Muttaqīn* — The Same Word, Reborn

And now comes the line that the whole verse has been building toward. After the closed dark ring, the verse breaks open:

> وَٱللَّهُ وَلِىُّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ
> *wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn*
> "while Allah is the Protector of those who are mindful of Him."

Notice first — and this is no accident — Allah uses **the same root again**. *Waliyy.* The wrongdoers had *awliyāʾ.* The mindful have a *waliyy.* The exact same word of belonging, of nearness, of protection. Allah is deliberately setting them on the same scale so you can weigh them against each other.

So weigh them. And watch every single thing invert.

The wrongdoers had *awliyāʾ* — **plural.** Many protectors. The muttaqīn have a *waliyy* — **singular.** One Protector. The crowd has *numbers*; the believer has *the One*. And the entire argument of the verse is that the singular outweighs the plural infinitely, because *Who* the One is changes everything.

The wrongdoers' alliance was *baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* — **mutual, reciprocal, circular.** Each one both gives protection and desperately needs it. It's a loan between bankrupt parties. But *wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn* is **one-directional.** Allah is the *waliyy* of the mindful — He protects, He needs nothing, He receives nothing back. He is *Al-Ghaniyy*, remember — the very root from the start of the verse — the One who needs no ally. His protection is pure gift flowing downward, not a debt circulating sideways.

The wrongdoers' ring was **sealed at the top** — no vertical line. The believer's belonging is **nothing but** the vertical line. Where the wrongdoers had a crowd and no God, the muttaqī has — in this verse — no crowd named at all, only God.

[PAUSE]

And here is the asymmetry I want you to feel in your chest. Look at what Allah gives each group, and look at what He *withholds*.

To the wrongdoers He gives a crowd — and withholds Himself.

To the muttaqīn He gives Himself — and (in the wording of the verse) does not even bother to name them a crowd.

Each group lacks exactly what the other has. And the verse's entire claim is this: **what the believer seems to "lack" — the crowd — is worth nothing** *(lan yughnū... shayʾā)* — **and what the believer has — the one Waliyy — is everything.** The trade looks lopsided to the eye. It is the most lopsided trade in existence — in the believer's favor.

Now — who exactly receives this? **ٱلْمُتَّقِين** *(al-muttaqīn)*. And I saved the most beautiful linguistic stroke in the verse for here.

*Al-muttaqīn* comes from the root **و-ق-ي** *(w-q-y)*, and this root means **to guard, to shield, to protect oneself, to place a barrier between yourself and harm.** That is what *taqwā* literally is at the root: not vague "piety," but **self-shielding** — the act of putting a guard between yourself and what would destroy you.

And the form Allah uses — *muttaqī*, the active participle of Form VIII *ittaqā* — carries a reflexive, self-involving sense: **one who takes guarding upon himself, who shields his own self.**

Now put it together with the word that sits right before it. *Wa-llāhu* — **waliyy** — *al-muttaqīn.*

The *muttaqī* is the one who **guards himself** (*w-q-y*).
And Allah becomes his **guardian, his protector** (*w-l-y*, *waliyy*).

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? **The one who takes up the work of shielding himself for Allah's sake is taken up by Allah as the One He shields.** You guard yourself — and the ultimate Guardian makes you His own. The two acts of protection lock together like a hand closing around a hand. Your small, effortful, daily self-guarding — turning away from a glance, biting back a word, refusing a sin when no one is watching — is met from above by the protection of the Lord of the worlds.

This is the hinge I promised you in the introduction. The secret of which camp you land in is *not* what you have. The wrongdoers *had* more — more numbers, more wealth, more alliance. The hinge is what you *do to yourself*: do you shield yourself for Allah, or do you dissolve yourself into the ring?

And al-Qurtubī, beautifully, tells us *what* the muttaqīn here are guarding themselves *from*: *alladhīna ittaqaw ash-shirka wa-l-maʿāṣī* — those who shield themselves from associating partners with Allah, and from disobedience. Guarding the *tawḥīd*. Refusing to be one more body in the dark ring.

### The Bridge

So look at what these words have built, line by line.

*Lan yughnū... shayʾā* — the crowd's advertised resource, sufficiency, drained to absolute zero where it counts.

*Baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* — and we saw *why*: their belonging is a closed horizontal ring, sealed off from above, drowning men clutching drowning men.

*Wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn* — and over against it, a single vertical line of protection, gift flowing down from the One who needs no one, to the one who guards himself for His sake.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the way you actually live, and ache, and decide, every single day.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Crowd That Affirms You Cannot Shield You

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a feeling almost every human knows, and it is one of the most powerful forces in the psyche. It's the feeling of *being inside the group.* When you walk into a room and you belong there. When your opinion is the opinion everyone around you already holds, and you feel that warm, frictionless sense of *being right because we are all here together.* Psychologists call it social proof — the deep, ancient instinct that says: *if many people around me are doing this, it must be safe; it must be true.*

That instinct kept our ancestors alive. If the whole tribe is running, you run first and ask later. We are wired, down to the bone, to read *the crowd* as *safety.* To read *numbers* as *truth.* To feel that being surrounded means being protected.

And here is what that instinct does to faith. It is the single most common reason people drift. Not an argument. Not a doubt they couldn't answer. A *room.* A circle of people for whom the deen is a joke, or a burden, or simply absent — and the slow, gravitational pull of wanting to belong in that room. You don't decide to leave the truth. You just lean, a degree at a time, toward the warmth of the ring. Because the ring *feels* like safety, and standing slightly outside it *feels* like danger.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the verse down onto that exact feeling.

*Wa-inna-ẓ-ẓālimīna baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ.* The wrongdoers are allies of one another.

What Allah is telling you is that **the feeling of safety the ring gives you is real, and the safety itself is a lie.** The warmth is genuine. The protection is counterfeit. You really do feel held — and the entire cluster is sinking. Because the ring's power runs in only one direction: sideways. It can shelter you from awkwardness, from loneliness, from being the odd one out at the table. It cannot shelter you from a single atom of what comes *mina-llāh.*

Think about how this reframes peer pressure. We usually imagine peer pressure as weakness — as caving. But the verse reveals it as something more like a *category error.* The person leaning toward the crowd is not making a moral failure so much as a *structural miscalculation.* They are seeking *ghinā*, sufficiency, security — a completely legitimate need — at the one address that is guaranteed to be empty: *lan yughnū ʿanka mina-llāhi shayʾā.* They are trying to withdraw protection from an account that has a zero balance in the only currency that will be accepted on the Day it matters.

And here is the part that should genuinely free you. The verse is not asking you to despise the crowd, or to become harsh, or isolated, or superior. It is simply removing an *illusion* — the illusion that their approval is load-bearing. Once you truly see that the ring cannot save you, the terror of stepping slightly outside it *dissolves.* What are you afraid of losing? Protection it never had the power to give. You are not sacrificing security by standing on the truth alone. You are declining to pay rent on a house with no roof.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete, because tadabbur that stays in the sky changes nothing.

Tomorrow you will be in a room — at work, in a chat group, among family, online — where the easy, affirmed, *everyone-agrees* position is one that quietly trades away something Allah asked of you. A bit of honesty. A bit of modesty. A bit of standing up for someone. And you will feel the pull: *but they all think this; but I'll be the odd one; but these are my people.*

Here is the practice. In that exact moment, ask yourself one question drawn straight from this verse: **"Can this room repel anything from me *mina-llāh*?"** Can these people, whose approval I'm about to buy, avail me even *shayʾan* — one thing — before Allah?

The honest answer is no. And the moment you truly hear that "no," the room loses its grip. Not because you stop loving people — but because you stop mistaking their warmth for a roof over your soul.

The crowd that affirms you cannot shield you. Only the One you guard yourself *for* becomes the One who guards you.

### Theme Two: You Guard Yourself, and the Guardian Takes You

Now let's go to the other side of the verse — the vertical line — because it answers a quiet ache that the first theme leaves open.

If I let go of the crowd, *who holds me?*

That's not a small question. It's the real fear underneath conformity. We don't cling to the ring because we love the ring. We cling because we are afraid of *empty space* — of standing with nothing and no one at our back. And the verse knows this. That is why it does not simply say "the crowd is useless" and leave you hanging in the void. It immediately fills the void with the single most reassuring statement in the human vocabulary:

*Wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn.* And Allah is the Waliyy of those who guard themselves.

Let me reach for where this lives in your experience.

Think about what it means to have one person in your life who is *truly* in your corner. Not many acquaintances — one *waliyy.* Someone with real power, who has decided that you are *theirs*, that your problem is their problem, that they will spend their strength on your behalf without needing anything back. Most people go their whole lives aching for even a fraction of that. We will trade enormous things — dignity, time, our very convictions — for the feeling of having someone powerful who has *claimed* us.

Now hear the verse. *Allah* — *Al-Ghaniyy*, the One who needs no one, who owns all power, against whom no crowd can avail *anything* — **that** One declares Himself the *Waliyy* of a specific kind of person. Not a distant well-wisher. *Waliyy* — near, loyal, protective, *yours.* The very word the wrongdoers used for their fragile mutual clutching, Allah uses for what He *is* to the believer — except now it is backed by the One who cannot fail.

[PAUSE]

But — and this is the part that turns reassurance into transformation — notice *who* receives this. Not "Allah is the Waliyy of everyone." Not "Allah is the Waliyy of those who feel spiritual." *Al-muttaqīn.* The ones who **guard themselves.** *W-q-y.*

And we already uncovered the secret hidden in that root. *Taqwā* is not a mood. It is an *act* — the act of placing a shield between yourself and what would harm your relationship with Allah. Small. Daily. Often invisible. The glance you lower. The lie you don't tell when it would have been easy and free. The prayer you guard when sleep is sweeter. The sin you refuse in a room where absolutely no one would ever know.

Here is the breathtaking exchange the verse encodes in its very roots. **You perform the small act of guarding yourself — *w-q-y* — and Allah responds with the infinite act of guarding you — *w-l-y*.** Your tiny shield, raised in private for His sake, is answered by the Shield of the Lord of the heavens and the earth. You guard a glance; He guards your heart. You guard your tongue; He guards your standing on the Day of Standing. The currencies are not equal. You give copper and receive the treasuries of the sky. That is not a transaction. That is a Lord overwhelming His servant with generosity disproportionate to anything the servant did.

[PAUSE]

And feel the difference in *texture* between the two kinds of belonging now.

The wrongdoers' belonging was *reciprocal and exhausting* — *baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* — everyone owing, everyone needing, everyone propping. It is the belonging of the networker who can never stop networking, the politician who can never stop calculating who owes whom. It is *work*, forever, because the moment you stop feeding the ring, the ring drops you.

The believer's belonging is *one-directional and restful.* Allah is *Al-Ghaniyy.* He does not need your protection. You can never put Him in your debt and you never have to. His *wilāya* over you is not a loan you must keep servicing. It is a gift that asks of you only one thing: that you keep guarding yourself *for Him.* And even that guarding — He helps you with it. There is a place to finally set down the exhausting arithmetic of belonging.

So the practice for this theme is the inversion of the last one. The first theme had you *withdraw* trust from the ring. This one has you *relocate* it. Tonight, pick one private shield — one small act of *taqwā* that only Allah will ever see. Not a public good deed. A hidden one. A glance you guard, a thought you refuse to feed, a small obedience no one will applaud. And as you do it, know with your whole heart what the verse is telling you: this hidden shield of yours is the very thing that places you under His. *Wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn.* You guarded yourself in the dark, for Him — and He has claimed you as His own.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how perfectly this verse is built. It is a single, balanced structure, and every piece answers another piece.

It opens with a **verdict on the crowd's power**: *lan yughnū... shayʾā* — they avail nothing.

It moves to the **diagnosis** — *why* nothing: *baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* — because their belonging is a closed horizontal ring, sealed off from above.

And it closes with the **alternative**: *wa-llāhu waliyyu-l-muttaqīn* — the open vertical line, the One Protector for the ones who guard themselves.

And watch the verse's deepest piece of engineering. The root of the *first* word's promise — *gh-n-y*, sufficiency — comes back as the silent attribute of the *last* clause's subject. The crowd cannot *make you rich (yughnū)* against Allah... precisely because the One who claims the believer is *Al-Ghaniyy*, the only One who is rich in Himself and can therefore enrich another. Sufficiency cannot come from the bankrupt ring. It can only come from the One who needs nothing. The verse begins by denying false sufficiency and ends, by implication, in the only true source of it.

And the *waliyy* root, used for the wrongdoers and then for Allah, forces the comparison the whole verse exists to make: plural fragile reciprocal allies, versus one needless faithful Protector. Same word. Opposite realities.

### The Two Discoveries Are One

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same insight approached from two directions.

The *words* showed us a closed dark ring of mutual clutching and an open line of gift from above.

The *themes* showed us that this is the actual shape of your daily life: the warm useless pull of the crowd, and the restful claiming protection of the One — with the hinge being not what you *have*, but the small private act of guarding yourself for His sake.

It is all one thing. **Belonging that runs sideways cannot save you. Belonging that runs upward is the only thing that can. And the doorway from the first to the second is *taqwā* — guarding yourself, so the Guardian takes you.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I paying for protection from a ring that has no roof? Whose approval am I treating as load-bearing when it can avail me *nothing* before Allah?

2. When I feel the pull to lean toward "what everyone thinks," can I ask the verse's question — *can this room repel anything from me, mina-llāh?* — and feel its grip loosen?

3. What is one private shield — one act of *taqwā* no human will ever see — that I have been neglecting? What would it mean to raise it tonight, knowing it places me under His protection?

4. Do I experience my belonging to Allah as *restful gift* — or have I turned even my faith into another exhausting ring I am anxiously feeding?

5. The wrongdoers had *more* — more numbers, more power, more alliance. Do I, in my heart, still secretly believe that *more* is *safer*? Or have I truly grasped that the One outweighs the many?

### The Distillation

> The crowd that affirms you cannot shield you from Allah; only the One you guard yourself *for* becomes the One who guards you.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to despise people, or to live alone, or to become hard. You are being invited to set down one illusion — that the warmth of the ring is a roof — and to discover that all along there was a roof above the ring, a *Waliyy* who needs nothing and claims you anyway. The space outside the crowd was never empty. It was where He was waiting. Step into it not with fear of losing the ring, but with the relief of someone who finally stops paying rent on a house with no roof — and walks into the shelter of the One who owns the sky.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, *Al-Ghaniyy*, the One who needs no ally and against whom no crowd can avail anything — let me never trade Your protection for the warmth of a ring that cannot save me. Make me of those who guard themselves for Your sake in the places no one sees, and by that guarding, take me as Your own. You are the *Waliyy* of those who are mindful of You — let me live, and die, beneath that single, sufficient protection. Āmīn.
