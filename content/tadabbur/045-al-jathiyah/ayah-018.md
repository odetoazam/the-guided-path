# The Road to Water: Why Everyone Is Following Something

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single meaning.

> **ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَٰكَ عَلَىٰ شَرِيعَةٍ مِّنَ ٱلْأَمْرِ فَٱتَّبِعْهَا وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَآءَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ**

> *Thumma jaʿalnāka ʿalā sharīʿatin mina-l-amri fa-ttabiʿhā wa-lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾa-lladhīna lā yaʿlamūn.*

> *"Then We placed you upon an ordained way of the matter — so follow it, and do not follow the desires of those who do not know."*

Thirteen words. On the surface, an instruction. Follow this; don't follow that. You could read it in four seconds and move on, and most of us have, a hundred times.

But I want to slow down, because hidden inside these thirteen words is one of the most penetrating descriptions of the human condition in the entire Quran. By the time we are finished, I want you to see that this ayah is not really a command at all. **It is a diagnosis.** It is Allah telling you something true about every single human being who has ever lived — including you, sitting here right now — that you have probably never had named so precisely.

Let me rebuild the world this verse landed into.

### The world behind the verse

The surah is Al-Jathiyah — "The Kneeling" — named for the day when every nation will be seen kneeling, called to its book of deeds. The whole surah is about a single fracture: the line between the one who lets the signs of Allah speak to him, and the one who has taken **his own desire as his god** (we'll meet that phrase later — it sits just a few verses down, in 45:23, and this verse is laying the foundation for it).

Now hold the immediate context. Just before our ayah, in verse 17, Allah speaks about an earlier people — the Children of Israel. And He says something startling. He gave them the Scripture. He gave them *al-ḥukm* — judgment, the ability to rule between truth and falsehood. He gave them prophethood. He gave them good provision. He gave them **clarity** in the matter of religion.

And then? *"They did not differ — until after the knowledge had come to them — out of envy between themselves."*

[PAUSE]

Read that again, because it overturns something we assume. We assume people go astray because they *lack* knowledge. Allah says: these people had the knowledge. The Scripture was in their hands. They fractured anyway. And the engine of the fracture was not ignorance. It was *baghy* — envy, the overreach of the self, desire dressed up as a position.

So they had knowledge, and desire defeated the knowledge.

**Then** — *thumma* — comes our verse. "Then We placed *you* upon an ordained way." The address turns, suddenly and personally, to the Prophet ﷺ. After a whole community that held the Scripture and still let desire tear it apart, Allah turns to His Messenger and sets him on a clear road, and warns him — *him*, the one receiving revelation directly — away from "the desires of those who do not know."

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows:

**Why warn the Prophet ﷺ — the man receiving the very words of Allah — about following desire? And why call the people he must not follow "those who do not know," when the people in the verse just before knew, and fell anyway?**

Something is being said here about knowledge and desire that is far deeper than "smart people are guided and ignorant people are not." Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three things to open. A single noun that re-describes your entire religion. A single verb, used twice, that re-describes your entire life. And a silence — a missing word — that re-describes everyone you are tempted to follow.

---

### Element One: شَرِيعَة — the place where you go down to drink

Let me teach you a way Arabic works before we touch the word, because it will change what you hear.

In Arabic, abstract words are almost never born abstract. They begin as something you can touch, see, walk on, taste — and the abstract meaning grows out of the physical one like a plant out of soil. The physical sense never fully leaves. It hums underneath. So when you want to understand what a word *really* says, you don't ask the dictionary for the abstract definition. You ask: **what could I see, in the desert, that this word first named?**

Now the word. Allah says He placed the Prophet ﷺ:

> **عَلَىٰ شَرِيعَةٍ مِّنَ ٱلْأَمْرِ** — *ʿalā sharīʿatin mina-l-amr* — "upon a *sharīʿah* of the matter."

*Sharīʿah.* You know this word. It has become, in our time, one of the most argued-over words on earth — a word that makes headlines, a word people use as a weapon, a word that for many evokes a courtroom, a punishment, a list of restrictions. A system of law. A fence.

Now let me show you what the word actually meant to the first people who heard it. The classical lexicographers — and al-Qurtubi states this directly in his commentary on this very verse — tell us that *sharīʿah* originally named one specific physical thing:

**It is the path that leads down to the water.**

[PAUSE]

*Mashraʿat al-māʾ.* Qurtubi's words: *"mawrid ash-shāriba"* — the place where the one who drinks comes down to drink. Picture it. You are in the desert. There is a river, or a spring. But the banks are not all reachable — there is rock, there is mud, there are steep drops, there are stretches where the water is there but you cannot get to it, and a stretch where you *can*. That accessible slope down to the living water — the worn path where people and animals descend to drink and live — **that** is the original *sharīʿah*.

Sit with this, because it is doing real work.

The root — *shīn-rāʾ-ʿayn*, ش ر ع — gives us the idea of a way *opened toward* something. The same root gives *shāriʿ*, a street — Qurtubi notes this too — because a street is a way that takes you *to* a destination. So *sharīʿah* is not "rules." At its root, *sharīʿah* is **the opened path to the thing that keeps you alive.**

Now feel the difference between the two pictures sitting in your head.

Picture one — the one our age handed us: *sharīʿah* is a wall. A boundary. A "no." Something that keeps you *from*.

Picture two — the one the word was born with: *sharīʿah* is the slope down to the river in a land that kills you without water. Something that keeps you *for*. It doesn't fence you out of life. **It is the one path that gets you to life.**

[PAUSE]

And now hear the verb that Allah pairs with it. He says *jaʿalnāka ʿalā sharīʿah* — "We *placed* you **upon** it." Not "We gave you a sharīʿah." Not "We sent you a law." He placed you *upon* it — *ʿalā* — the preposition of being on top of, standing on, supported by. You are standing on the path. The path is under your feet. It is the ground you walk on to reach the water.

Think about what that does to the believer's whole posture. The one who experiences the dīn as a fence spends his life with his back to it, straining away, feeling the "no" at his shoulders. The one who understands *sharīʿah* as the desert-path to water spends his life *facing forward*, walking it, because he is thirsty and it is the way to drink.

Here is what this single word reveals: **Allah did not place a wall around the Prophet ﷺ. He placed a road under him — and the road runs to water.** Everything that follows in the verse — "so follow it" — is now not the command of a jailer. It is the urging of someone who knows where the water is, in a desert where you will die if you wander off the path.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: ٱتَّبِعْ — the verb you are already obeying, right now

Now the second word. And this one I want to teach as a concept first, in your own life, before we let the ayah near it.

Here is a claim. I want you to test it against your own experience: **You are, at every waking moment, following something.** There is no neutral. There is no still point where a human being follows nothing and simply *is*. Your attention is always being led. Your steps are always tracking some scent. The only variable — ever — is *what* you are following.

Hold that thought. Now look at the verse, because Allah builds it on exactly this claim, and He builds it with surgical precision.

He uses one verb, *ittabaʿa* — to follow — and He uses it **twice**, back to back:

> **فَٱتَّبِعْهَا** — *fa-ttabiʿhā* — "so **follow** it"

> **وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ** — *wa-lā tattabiʿ* — "and do **not follow**..."

Same verb. Same root — *tāʾ-bāʾ-ʿayn*, ت ب ع, the root of *tabiʿa*, to come behind, to go after, to track. And notice the form: this is Form VIII, *iftaʿala* — *ittabaʿa*. In Arabic, Form VIII often adds a sense of taking the action *on yourself*, doing it with effort and engagement. *Ittibāʿ* is not drifting. It is the deliberate, effortful, self-involving pursuit — the way a tracker follows a trail, eyes down, committed, going wherever it leads.

So look at what Allah has done with the architecture of the sentence. He has not said "obey this and disobey that." He has not used two different verbs for two different relationships. He has used **the same act — following — and simply changed the object.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what that quietly concedes? It concedes the very thing I asked you to test a moment ago. The verse does not say: *follow the sharīʿah, or follow nothing.* It says: *follow the sharīʿah, or follow desires.* The choice is never between following and not-following. **Following is the constant. Everyone in this verse is following.** The Prophet ﷺ is told to follow. The ones "who do not know" are following too — they are following their *ahwāʾ*. The only thing the verse lets you choose is the object on the end of the verb.

This is so much more honest than the way we usually frame faith. We tell ourselves the story that there are two kinds of people: people who "submit" and follow a path, and free people who follow nothing and answer to no one. The Quran calmly dismantles that story in the space of six words. There are no free people in that sense. There is the one following the road to water, and there is the one following his desires. Both are following. Both have their eyes down on a trail. **One trail goes to the river. The other goes—**

— and to learn where the other trail goes, we have to open the third word.

---

### Element Three: أَهْوَاء — the word that means *falling*

This is the heart of it. Stay with me very closely here, because this is the word the whole verse has been walking toward.

The thing you must not follow is named: *ahwāʾ* — "**the desires** of those who do not know." *Ahwāʾ* is the plural of *hawā*. We translate *hawā* as "desire," "whim," "inclination," "caprice." All of that is true. But translation buries what the Arabic puts right on the surface, and what it puts on the surface will change how you feel about your own appetites for the rest of your life.

The root of *hawā* is *hāʾ-wāw-yāʾ*, ه و ي. And its core, attested meaning — you'll find it in Lisān al-ʿArab, you'll find Imam al-Rāghib build his whole entry on it in the *Mufradāt* — its core meaning is:

**To fall. To drop. To plummet from a height toward the depths.**

[PAUSE]

This is the same root, the same word, that opens Surah An-Najm: *"wa-n-najmi idhā hawā"* — "By the star when it **falls**" (53:1). The star that *hawā* is the star that drops out of the sky. When a thing collapses downward, when it plunges, when it descends from up to down — the Arabs said *hawā*.

And from this exact root — the root of *falling* — the Arabs drew the word for desire.

Let that detonate slowly.

Allah did not name your lower desire with a neutral word. He named it with **the word for what happens to a body that has nothing holding it up.** *Hawā* is the desire that operates the way gravity operates: it does not need a reason, it does not need to be argued for, it simply *pulls*, and the pull is always in one direction — **down.** You do not have to *do* anything to fall. Falling is what happens when you stop holding on. And *hawā* is the appetite that drags the soul the way gravity drags a stone: effortlessly, constantly, downward.

[PAUSE]

Now hold this against the word from Element One. *Sharīʿah* — the path you climb *down to reach water*, yes, but a path, a worn way, a thing with direction and a destination, *the ground under your feet.* And *hawā* — *falling*, with no path, no ground, no destination but the bottom. The one is a road. The other is a drop.

And there is one more thing in the grammar, and it is not decoration — it is the whole picture completing itself. Look at the number of each word.

*Sharīʿah* is **singular.** One path. *"Follow **it**"* — *fa-ttabiʿ**hā*** — a single feminine "it." One road, one water, one direction.

*Ahwāʾ* is **plural.** Not *hawā*, the desire — *ahwāʾ*, the desire**s**. Many. Scattered. Pulling in every direction at once.

[PAUSE]

Feel the asymmetry the grammar has built. On one side: **one** ascending path to **one** source of life. On the other side: **many** falls, in many directions, all of them downward. This is not a choice between two roads. It is a choice between *a* road and *no road* — between one way up to the water and a thousand ways down into nothing. The person following his *ahwāʾ* is not on a different path. He is not on a path at all. **He is falling, and he has mistaken the sensation of falling for the sensation of being free.**

That is what the other trail is. It was never a trail. It was the edge.

---

### The silence: لَا يَعْلَمُونَ — those who do not know *what?*

Now the missing word — and I told you at the start to read silences as carefully as sounds.

Whose desires must not be followed? *"The desires of **alladhīna lā yaʿlamūn**"* — "those who do not know."

In Arabic, the verb *yaʿlamūn*, "they know," is a verb that wants an object. To know is to know *something*. You expect Allah to finish it: those who do not know **the truth**, those who do not know **Allah**, those who do not know **the path**. The slot is open. The sentence is reaching for the thing they don't know.

And Allah leaves it empty.

[PAUSE]

*"Those who do not know."* Full stop. He does not tell you what they don't know. And in Arabic, when a verb that craves an object is deliberately left without one, the silence does something specific: it **universalizes**. It removes the limit. They do not know — *anything that matters here.* Not the path, not the water, not the destination, not the cost. The ignorance is not partial. It is total, with respect to the only thing the verse cares about: **where the road goes.**

And now — this is the part I most want you to catch — connect this silence back to the world we built at the start.

Remember verse 17. The people before — the ones who had the Scripture, the judgment, the prophethood — they *had* knowledge, and they fractured anyway, out of *baghy*, out of envy and overreach. So why, one verse later, are the people you must not follow called "those who do not **know**"?

Here is the answer the verse has been constructing this whole time:

**The moment you follow *hawā*, your knowledge stops counting as knowing.**

[PAUSE]

The people of verse 17 had information in their hands. But once they let desire steer — once *ahwāʾ* took the wheel — Allah no longer describes the desire-followers as people who "know and disobey." He describes them as people who *do not know.* Because in the Quran's reckoning, to *know* the path to water and then walk off the edge anyway is not really to know it. Real knowledge of where the water is *moves your feet toward it.* Knowledge that leaves you falling was never knowledge in the sense that counts. It was data. And *hawā* turns the most learned man, the moment it captures him, into one of *"those who do not know."*

That is why the Prophet ﷺ — the man receiving the very words of Allah, the most knowing of all — still needed to be told: *do not follow their desires.* Because the danger was never a shortage of information. The danger is *hawā*, which can hollow out any amount of knowledge from the inside and leave a learned man falling, certain the whole way down that he is the one who can see.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these thirteen words have shown us about words themselves. *Sharīʿah*: the dīn is not a fence but a path — the one path down to water in a desert that kills. *Ittabaʿa*, twice: you are always following something; the only choice you ever make is the object of the verb. *Ahwāʾ*: the alternative to the path is not another path but *falling*, many-directioned, downward, dressed up as freedom. And *lā yaʿlamūn*: the fall doesn't just take you somewhere bad — it un-knows you, it converts your knowledge into the ignorance of a man certain he can see as he drops.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the reality you wake up inside of every single morning.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The lie that you are not following anything

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You have been told, your whole life, by every screen and every song and every quiet assumption of the age you were born into, a single sentence. It comes in a hundred costumes but it is always the same sentence:

*Follow your heart.*

*Be true to yourself.* *Trust your gut.* *Live your truth.* *No one can tell you who to be.* *The only authority is you.* It is the water we swim in. It is so total that it doesn't feel like a claim anymore — it feels like simple sanity, and the alternative feels like a cage.

And underneath all of it is a hidden promise, the promise that makes the whole thing seductive: **the promise that if you stop following anyone else, you will be following no one. That you will be free. That "your heart" is a clean, neutral, sovereign place that belongs only to you, and that listening to it is the one form of guidance that isn't somebody else pulling your strings.**

Now hold that promise up against the verse. Because the verse looked straight at this promise fourteen hundred years before our age made it a religion, and it took it apart with one grammatical move.

Remember: *one verb, used twice, with only the object changed.* The verse does not offer you "follow the path" versus "follow no one and be free." There is no third door in the sentence. **There is only following.** The man who "follows his heart" is not the exception to following — *he is one of the two cases the verb names.* He is *fa-ttabiʿ ahwāʾ* — he is following desires. He did not escape the verb. He just put a different word on the end of it.

[PAUSE]

And here is where the root we uncovered does its most painful, most liberating work. The promise said: *your heart is a neutral, sovereign place.* But the Quran named that place *hawā* — **falling.** It is not neutral. It has a *direction*, and the direction is built into it the way down is built into gravity. So when the age tells you "follow your heart, it will lead you to yourself," the Quran answers: *that pull you feel — that effortless, reasons-not-required, it-just-feels-right pull — you have felt that exact sensation before. You felt it the last time you stepped off something high. It is not the feeling of freedom. It is the feeling of falling. And the reason it feels like freedom is that falling, for the first few seconds, feels exactly like flying.*

[PAUSE]

This is the thing no one warns you about. *Hawā* does not announce itself as bondage. Bondage you could resist. *Hawā* announces itself as *liberation* — as finally being free of all the people telling you what to do, free to just *follow yourself.* And that is precisely why it is the most dangerous master of all: it is the only master that convinces you that obeying it is the same thing as having no master. The drug, the scroll, the grudge you won't release, the relationship you know is wrong, the purchase, the rage, the second look — every one of them arrives wearing the same face. Not "obey me." Never that. Always: *this is what YOU want. This is you being true to you.* And you follow — *ittibāʿ*, eyes down, committed, tracking the trail — straight off the edge, narrating freedom the whole way down.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the question you ask at the moment of choice. The age trained you to ask: *what do I want? what does my heart say?* — as if the answer to that question were automatically the answer you should follow. The verse retrains you to ask a second question, the one *hawā* never wants you to ask: **"This pull I feel — is it a path, or is it a drop? Does it have a destination I could name, and would I want to arrive there — or does it just feel good to fall?"** Because *hawā* can never survive that question. The desire that is really a path will tell you where it goes and let you weigh it. The desire that is really a fall has no answer — it has only the pull, and the pull is the whole of its argument. Learn to feel the difference between a road under your feet and the lightness of a body with nothing holding it up. That lightness is not freedom. **In the language Allah chose, it is the precise opposite of standing on something.**

---

### Theme Two: The fence and the river

Now let me take you back into the desert, because the first word of our journey has a gift in it that we have not fully unwrapped, and it is a gift specifically for the heart that *resents* the dīn.

Be honest, in the privacy of your own chest. There is a part of almost every believer that experiences the commands of the religion as a *fence*. As a long list of "no." No to this food, no to this money, no to this glance, no to this freedom that everyone around you seems to enjoy. And even if you obey, the obedience can carry a low, secret ache — the sense that you are being kept *from* something, that the wall of the law stands between you and a fuller life that is happening on the other side of it. You're inside the pen. The good pasture is out there.

That feeling is not a sin. It is a *misunderstanding* — and it is the exact misunderstanding that the word *sharīʿah* was waiting to heal.

Because we proved, from the lexicon, what *sharīʿah* first meant. Not a fence. **The path down to the water.** And the difference between those two images is the difference between two entirely different Gods.

Imagine the desert. The real one — the one these words were revealed into. A place where the sun does not warm you, it *hunts* you. Where the single difference between a living man and a dead one, by nightfall, is whether he found water. In that place, imagine someone who knows the land draws a line for you in the sand and says: *here. This worn slope, this *sharīʿah* — walk down here. Not there, where it looks easier — there the bank is a sheer drop and the sand is quick and the easy-looking way ends in rock with no water at the bottom. Here. This is the way down to the river.*

[PAUSE]

Now. Is that person *fencing you in*? Is he restricting your freedom? Is the line he drew a wall keeping you *from* life?

You know the answer in your body. **He is the only reason you will drink.** Every "not there" he says is not a theft — it is a *not-there-because-there-is-death-there-and-I-can-see-it-and-you-cannot.* The line he draws is not the boundary of a cage. It is the edge of the only path that does not end in thirst. And the man dying of thirst does not experience that line as a restriction. He experiences it as **rescue**, and he runs down it.

This is the reframe the word performs, and it is total. Every "no" in the dīn — every prohibition you have ever resented — is one of two things, and the desert tells you which. It is either *"not there, that's a drop"* or *"this way, the water's this way."* The God who gave you *sharīʿah* did not build a pen around a thirsty man for the pleasure of watching him pace. **He drew the one line in a trackless waste that leads down to the river — and then He said, with everything urgent in the word *fa-ttabiʿhā*, "follow it." Not "submit to my fence." "Come and drink. This way. Quickly. I can see what you cannot.**"

[PAUSE]

And here is why this matters so much for verse 17, the one we have been circling the whole time. The people before had the Scripture and walked off the path *anyway* — and they did it because, somewhere along the way, they stopped seeing the law as the road to water and started seeing it as a fence to be argued with, climbed, negotiated, bent toward what they already wanted. *Baghy.* The moment the *sharīʿah* becomes, in your heart, a wall instead of a river-path, you will start looking for the gate in it — and *hawā* is always standing right there, by the gate you think you've found, smiling, saying *this way is easier, the bank's not so steep over here* — and the bank over there is a sheer drop, and *hawā*, remember, is just the Arabic word for **what you do off the edge of one.**

So what does *this* change about tomorrow?

Tonight, take the one command of Allah you most resent. The one that feels most like a fence. Don't argue with it. Just ask it one question — the question the word *sharīʿah* puts in your hand: **"If this is not a wall keeping me from life, but a line on the desert floor — then what is the drop it is keeping me back from, and where is the water it is pointing me toward?"** You may not see the answer at once. The man on the slope cannot always see the river from the top; sometimes he only sees the one who *can* see it, and trusts the line. But ask the question, and you will feel the whole posture of your obedience begin to turn — from a back braced against a wall, to a face turned toward water. From being kept *from*, to being led *to*. That turn is the entire difference between a religion you endure and a religion you *drink.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back, and watch the whole verse assemble itself, because the order of it is the argument of it.

> **ثُمَّ** — *Then.* The hinge. After the people who had knowledge and fell to envy.
> **جَعَلْنَٰكَ عَلَىٰ شَرِيعَةٍ** — *We placed you upon a path to water.* The ground under your feet.
> **مِّنَ ٱلْأَمْرِ** — *of the matter.* From Allah's own command — not built by you.
> **فَٱتَّبِعْهَا** — *so follow **it**.* One object. Singular. The road.
> **وَلَا تَتَّبِعْ أَهْوَآءَ** — *and do not follow **desires**.* Same verb. Plural object. The falls.
> **ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ** — *of those who do not know.* The silence. The un-knowing the fall produces.

See it now as one shape. The verse opens by reminding you that knowledge alone did not save the people before — they had it and fell. It answers that not with *more knowledge* but with a **path placed beneath the feet** — something to stand on, something that comes *from the matter*, from Allah's command, not from the self. Then it names the only real choice a human ever makes — the object of the verb *follow* — and sets the two objects side by side so their grammar exposes their nature: **one** path, singular, to water; **many** desires, plural, in free-fall. And it closes by telling you what the fall does to the one who chooses it: it doesn't just drop him, it *blinds* him — it turns him into one of "those who do not know," certain he sees, all the way down.

The two halves of our journey were never two. **The linguistic discoveries and the lived themes are one insight approached from two sides.** *Sharīʿah*-as-path and the river-not-the-fence are the same truth. *Ittabaʿa*-used-twice and the lie-that-you-follow-nothing are the same truth. *Hawā*-as-falling and follow-your-heart-is-the-feeling-of-flying are the same truth. The grammar *is* the diagnosis. Allah did not decorate a command with imagery. **He encoded the structure of the human heart into the structure of a sentence**, and then handed you the sentence.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. Don't rush to answer them. Let them stay open in you.

1. Right now — today — what am I actually *following*? Not what do I believe I follow. What does my attention, my money, my time, my anger actually *track*, eyes down, all day?
2. The last pull I called "being true to myself" — was it a path, or was it a drop? Could I have named where it was taking me — and would I have wanted to arrive there?
3. Which command of Allah do I experience as a fence? And have I ever once asked it where the water is?
4. The people I am quietly tempted to follow — the voices that feel free, that feel ahead of the curve — do they *know* where they're going? Or have they only learned to narrate the fall beautifully?
5. When *hawā* speaks to me, what costume does it wear? What is the exact sentence it uses — because it almost never says "obey me," it says *"this is you"* — and would I recognize it the next time it does?

### The one sentence

**You are always following something — and Allah has placed beneath your feet the one path that descends to water, so do not trade it for the scattered desires of those who, having let the fall un-know them, are only plummeting, and calling it freedom.**

### The invitation

Nothing here asks you to white-knuckle your way through a list of rules tonight. The shift this verse invites is smaller and far deeper than that. It is a shift in *what you think you are doing* when you obey, and *what you think is happening* when you don't.

You are not a free agent choosing between submission and liberty. You are a thirsty traveler in a desert, and there is exactly one slope down to the river, and there are a thousand easy-looking edges that are not slopes at all. The dīn is not the wall that keeps you pacing. **It is the line that keeps you alive.** And every desire that pulls you off it, however much it feels like flight, is named, in the language your Lord chose, after the one thing that is the opposite of standing on solid ground.

So the invitation is just this: **the next time you feel that effortless pull — stop, and ask whether you are walking, or falling.** That single question, asked honestly, is the path itself.

### A du'a from these words

*O Allah, You placed a path beneath the feet of Your Messenger and called it the way to water — place our feet upon it, and let us feel it as rescue and not as restraint. Teach us to know the pull of Your path from the pull of the fall, and do not let our desires un-know us. And when we feel ourselves dropping and calling it freedom, catch us, and turn our faces back toward the river.*

*Āmīn.*
