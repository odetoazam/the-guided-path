# Woe to the One Who Made Lying a Self

### Surah Al-Jathiyah, Ayah 7

> وَيْلٌ لِّكُلِّ أَفَّاكٍ أَثِيمٍ
>
> *Waylun li-kulli affākin athīm*
>
> **"Woe to every habitual liar, deeply steeped in sin."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing at the Threshold

Four words. That is the entire ayah. *Waylun li-kulli affākin athīm.*

In a Book where some single verses stretch across a page, here Allah delivers a complete verdict in a breath. And before we touch a single one of these four words, I want you to feel where we are standing when they arrive.

**Rebuild the moment with me.**

Just before this ayah, Allah has been doing something extraordinary. He has been pointing — at the heavens, at the alternation of night and day, at the rain that revives the dead earth, at the winds that shift and turn. Sign after sign after sign. And then He says: *"These are the signs of Allah which We recite to you in truth. So in what speech, after Allah and His signs, will they believe?"* (45:6)

Sit with the tone of that question. It is not a neutral inquiry. It is the tone of someone who has shown you everything — opened every window, poured out every proof — and watched you look away. *After this... after all of this... what is left? What more could possibly reach you?*

The question hangs in the air. And the very next word is the answer.

Not a new argument. Not another sign. A **verdict.**

*Waylun.*

Woe.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the hook — the thing I need you to hunger to understand before we go any further. The classical commentators tell us this ayah came down concerning specific, named men. Some say al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith. Some say al-Ḥārith ibn Kalada. Some say Abū Jahl and his circle. Particular people, with particular faces, who did particular things.

And yet the word Allah chose is **كُلّ** — *every.*

Not "woe to that man." Not "woe to those people." **Woe to *every* one who is like this.**

Something happens in that gap — between the specific person who triggered the revelation and the universal sentence Allah actually pronounced. The historical man becomes a *type.* And the question that should make every one of us go still is this: **what exactly is the type? What is it, precisely, that earns this word of doom — and could it, quietly, be describing me?**

Let's find out by opening these four words slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wayl* — The Sound of Ruin

Before we define this word, I want to teach you something about how Arabic — and especially the Arabic of the Qur'an — sometimes makes a word *sound* like the thing it names.

Think of words in your own language that do this. *Crash. Whisper. Thud. Murmur.* You can almost hear the meaning inside the sound. The mouth performs what the word describes.

Now say this word slowly, out loud if you can: **وَيْل** — *wayl.*

Feel what your mouth does. It opens — *way* — into a long, falling diphthong, and then closes down hard on the *l.* It is, quite literally, the shape of a cry. A wail. Listen to how close those two words even sound across languages. *Wayl. Wail.* The word for doom is built like the sound a person makes *under* doom.

[PAUSE]

So what does it mean? Here the commentators give us two layers, and I want you to hold both, because they are not competing — they are the same reality seen from two distances.

Al-Muyassar reads it as the *condition*: **"severe destruction and ruin."** A state of devastation.

Al-Qurṭubī gives us the *place*: he reports that **wayl is a valley in Jahannam.** Not just an abstract misfortune — a geography. A location with coordinates in the next life.

Now watch what Allah is doing with the physical here. He does not describe spiritual ruin in spiritual terms — He cannot hand you "the state of being severed from your Lord forever" as a concept your nerves can feel. So He gives you a *place.* A valley. Somewhere you could, in principle, stand. The physical image — the valley — is the stepping stone. The destination it points toward is a reality no map could hold: the condition of a soul that heard the truth and is now beyond its reach.

And notice one more thing, easy to miss. The word is **وَيْلٌ** — *waylun* — with the *tanwīn*, the indefinite ending, that little *-un.* In Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite this way, especially as an opening exclamation, swells it. It does not say "*the* woe," a known, measured quantity. It says woe of a kind beyond reckoning. **An unspecified, immeasurable woe.** The grammar itself refuses to put a ceiling on it.

So before we even know *who* this is for, we know the weight of what is coming. A measureless ruin, shaped like a cry, with an address in the Fire.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Affāk* — Not One Who Lied, But One Who Is a Liar

Now to the heart of it. Who is this for?

The first word of the description is **أَفَّاك** — *affāk.* The translations say "liar." That is true, but it is a thin slice of the truth, and I want to give you the whole thing. To do that, I need to teach you one of the most beautiful and dangerous features of Arabic morphology.

**Here is the concept first, in plain terms.**

Arabic can take a root — a cluster of meaning — and pour it into different *molds*, and each mold changes not just the word but the *intensity* and *permanence* of what it describes. There is one family of molds the grammarians call **صِيَغ المُبَالَغَة** — *the forms of intensification.* These molds take an action and turn it into a *characteristic.* They turn something you *do* into something you *are.*

Let me make this concrete in English. There is a difference between saying "he drinks" and calling someone "a drunkard." There is a difference between "she helps people" and "she is a giver." The second word in each pair isn't describing an event — it's describing a *self.* It says: this is not what the person did once; this is what the person *is*, habitually, by character, as a settled identity.

One of the most powerful of these intensive molds is **فَعَّال** — *faʿʿāl* — with that doubled middle letter. The doubling is not decoration. The doubling is *repetition made audible.* It is the sound of the action happening again, and again, and again, until it hardens into a trait.

Now look at the word: **أَفَّاك** — *affāk.* That is the *faʿʿāl* mold exactly. And listen to it: the doubled **ف** — *ff* — that catch and burst in the middle of the word. Say it: *af-fāk.* The repetition is *built into the mouth.*

[PAUSE]

So *affāk* does not mean "a person who told a lie." Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both gloss it simply: **كَذَّاب** — and notice, even their explanation uses the *same intensive mold* — *kadhdhāb*, not *kādhib.* Not "a liar" but **"a great and habitual liar,"** one for whom lying has become the medium they live in. Lying is not their accident. It is their atmosphere.

But there is a deeper layer in this specific root, and this is where the ayah opens all the way.

The root of *affāk* is **أ-ف-ك** *(a-f-k).* And in the lexicon, the core meaning of *ifk* is not simply "to speak an untruth." Its root sense is **to turn a thing away from its proper direction — to overturn, to flip, to invert.** The same root describes the cities of the people of Lūṭ that were **مُؤْتَفِكَة** — overturned, turned upside down, their foundations flipped to the sky.

Do you see what this does to our understanding?

[PAUSE]

The *affāk* is not merely someone who says false words. The *affāk* is **someone who takes the truth and turns it upside down.** Someone whose whole orientation is inversion — who meets the real and flips it, who hears what is upright and stands it on its head. The lie is the symptom. The disease is a soul that has made *overturning* its habit. Truth comes in right-side-up; this person reflexively flips it.

And remember where we are. Two ayahs before, Allah laid out sign after sign, "in truth." The *affāk* is the one who received all of that — uprightness, reality, truth poured out — and *turned it over.* Al-Qurṭubī says this verse is **"a threat against the one who abandons reasoning from His signs"** — *taraka al-istidlāl bi-āyātihi.* The signs came to lead him to the truth. He took them and overturned them.

This is no longer a stranger. This is a *capacity inside every human being* — the reflex to take what we know is true and find the angle that flips it.

### Element Three: *Athīm* — The Inward Soil Beneath the Outward Lie

The verdict could have stopped at *affāk.* It does not. Allah pairs it: **أَثِيم** — *athīm.*

This word comes from the root **أ-ث-م** *(a-th-m)*, the root of **إِثْم** — *ithm* — sin, wrongdoing, moral guilt. And *athīm* sits in another intensive mold, **فَعِيل** — *faʿīl* — the mold of a quality that has soaked all the way through, become saturated, settled deep. Al-Jalālayn glosses it precisely: **كَثِير الإِثْم** — *"abundant in sin,"* one *full* of it.

Now I want you to notice why Allah placed these two words *side by side*, and in *this order.* Because they are not a repetition. They are two halves of one anatomy.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. *Affāk* — the overturner of truth — describes what comes **out** of a person. The speech. The lie. The outward act of inversion that others can hear.

*Athīm* — the one steeped in sin — describes what is **within.** The inner soil. The saturated ground from which the lying grows.

And the order is exact. First *affāk*, then *athīm.* First what you see — the flipped truth on the tongue — and then, underneath it, the explanation: a self already so steeped in *ithm* that overturning the truth is the natural harvest of that soil. **The lie on the surface is fed by the sin in the depths.** You do not become a habitual overturner of truth by accident. You become one because the ground beneath has been saturated, drop by drop, with *ithm* — until falsehood is simply what such soil grows.

Here is what is remarkable, and a little frightening. Allah did not say "woe to the one who lied and sinned" — two events. He named **two settled characters fused into one person:** the outer habit and the inner saturation. This is not a snapshot of a bad moment. It is a portrait of a self that has been *built*, choice by choice, into something.

[PAUSE]

**The bridge.**

So look at what these four words, taken together, have shown us. *Wayl* — a measureless ruin shaped like a cry. *Li-kulli* — for *every* one, no exceptions, the specific man dissolved into a universal type. *Affāk* — not one who lied, but one who *is* an overturner of truth, by repetition, by habit, by character. *Athīm* — and beneath that habit, an inner ground saturated with sin until falsehood is what it naturally yields.

The grammar has built a person in front of us. Now let's ask what this person reveals — not just about words, but about the human soul itself, and about a danger that lives much closer to us than we'd like to admit.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How a Self Is Quietly Built

Let me start where you already live, far from any verdict.

You have, at some point, told a small story about yourself that wasn't quite true. We all have. Maybe it made a hard day sound a little more bearable. Maybe it made you look slightly better than you were. The first time, you *knew.* There was a small flinch inside — the quiet awareness: *that's not exactly how it happened.*

And then you told it again. And the flinch was smaller. And again. And by the tenth time, here is the strange and terrible thing — **you could no longer feel the seam between the story and the truth.** You believed it. Not because it became true, but because *you* became someone who no longer noticed the difference.

That experience — universal, ordinary, almost invisible — is the doorway into this ayah. Because that is precisely what the mold *faʿʿāl* describes. Not a lie. A *liar.* Not the act. The *self that the repeated act builds.*

[PAUSE]

Psychologists have a name for the early stage of this: cognitive dissonance. When what I do clashes with who I believe I am, the discomfort can be resolved two ways. I can change the behavior — hard. Or I can change the belief about myself — easy. And so, drop by drop, the self quietly reshapes itself around what it keeps doing. We don't usually decide to become dishonest. We just keep choosing the easy resolution until, one day, dishonesty is not something we do — it's the shape of the person doing it.

This is the deepest mercy and the deepest warning hidden in the grammar. Allah did not pronounce *wayl* on the one who lied. He never does that — a single lie, a single sin, is the territory of *tawbah*, of return, and the door is wide open. The *wayl* falls on the *affāk*, the *athīm* — the **settled self.** The one who let the act become an identity. The one who stopped flinching.

And here is the paradox I want to let sit, unresolved, because resolving it too fast would steal its power: **the more thoroughly you become the affāk, the less you can feel that you are one.** The doubled *ff* of habit muffles the alarm. By the time lying is your medium, you have lost the very instrument that would tell you something is wrong. The disease anesthetizes the nerve that would report it.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It relocates the entire battle. The battle is not at the *tenth* lie, when you no longer feel it. The battle is at the *first* — at the small flinch, the seam you can still detect. **The flinch is a mercy.** It is the soul's intact nerve, still reporting. The practical work of this ayah is not "do not become an affāk" — by the time that's a live question, the nerve is dead. The work is: *honor the flinch while you can still feel it.* When the small voice says *that's not quite how it happened* — stop there. That small discomfort is the most precious thing you own in that moment. It is the difference between an act and a self.

### Theme Two: The Sin of Hearing and Turning Away

Now the second theme, and for this I need you to notice what comes *after* our ayah — because Allah Himself completes the portrait in the very next verse.

45:8: *"He hears the signs of Allah recited to him, then persists in arrogance as if he had not heard them."*

Look at that closely. The *affāk athīm* is not someone who never encountered the truth. He is someone who **heard it** — *yasmaʿu* — the verses recited right to him, into his ears, in his own language. And then *persisted in arrogance* — *yuṣirru mustakbiran* — **"as if he had not heard."**

[PAUSE]

This reframes everything. We tend to imagine the people of *wayl* as the ignorant, the people who never got the message, the ones with an excuse. The Qur'an says the opposite. The *affāk* is defined by *reception.* He got it. It reached him. And he overturned it anyway. Remember the root — *afk* is *to turn truth from its place.* You can only turn away something that arrived. **The ifk presupposes the hearing.** The crime is not absence of truth; it is the *inversion of a truth that landed.*

Now let me bring this all the way home, because you and I live in the most acoustically saturated moment in human history.

We hear truth constantly. We scroll past the ayah. We listen to the reminder in the car. We read the verse, nod, and keep moving. We are *hearers* on an industrial scale. And the question this ayah forces — gently, but it does not let go — is: **what is the distance, in me, between hearing and turning?**

Because there is a quiet form of the *affāk* that has nothing to do with telling lies to other people. It is the one who hears the truth from Allah, recognizes it as true, feels its pull — and then, with a small inner motion, turns it over. *Yes, but not now. Yes, but not me. Yes, but later.* That small motion — *taking what landed as true and flipping it so it no longer obligates you* — that is *ifk* in its most intimate and most respectable disguise. We don't call it lying. We call it being busy. We call it being realistic. We call it "I'll get to it."

[PAUSE]

And notice the companion word again: *mustakbiran* — in arrogance — in the next ayah. What turns *hearing* into *ifk* is **pride.** Not stupidity, not lack of access. The refusal to be moved by something you know is above you. The *athīm* — the one saturated within — turns away not because the truth is unclear, but because submitting to it would cost the self something the self is unwilling to pay.

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes what I do in the *gap.* Every time a verse, a reminder, a truth from Allah lands on me, a clock starts. There is a window — short — between *hearing* and *response*, and in that window the soul either bends toward the truth or quietly turns it over and files it away. The practical work is to **close the gap.** To make hearing and heeding nearly the same motion. When the ayah lands and something in you says *that is true and it asks something of me* — do the smallest possible thing in response *immediately*, before the turning-reflex engages. One act of obedience, however small, in the first minute. That single act is the opposite of *ifk.* It is the truth, kept right-side-up.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing the Two Directions Together

Look at what we have found, from two completely different doors, arriving at one room.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a *self* — built by the doubled *ff* of repetition (*affāk*) out of a soil saturated with sin (*athīm*), pronounced over with a measureless, place-bearing ruin (*wayl*), and addressed not to one man but to *every* one of his type (*li-kull*).

The **thematic journey** showed us *how that self gets built* — drop by drop, flinch by ignored flinch, in the small gap between hearing a truth and turning it over.

They are the same insight from two sides. The grammar tells you *what* the dangerous self is. The themes tell you *how* you become it — and therefore, exactly where to stand to make sure you don't. The intensive forms are not describing a monster across the world from you. They are describing the *direction* of a road that begins with one small, unflinched-at inversion of a truth you heard today.

### The Architecture

And now see the architecture of the passage, which we could not have seen at the start. 45:6 — *the signs are recited to you in truth.* 45:7 — *woe to every overturner of truth, steeped in sin.* 45:8 — *he hears the signs, then turns away in arrogance.* The truth is recited (6). The verdict on its inversion is pronounced (7). The act of inversion is shown (8). The ayah of *wayl* is the hinge — the truth on one side, the turning-away on the other, and the woe poised exactly in between. **The structure itself enacts the moment of choice we each stand in:** the truth has been recited; now, which way will you turn it?

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, in the last week, did I feel the small *flinch* — the seam between the story and the truth — and tell it anyway? Is that nerve still working in me?
2. When a truth from Allah landed on me recently — a verse, a reminder — what was the distance, in time, between hearing it and turning it over to "later"?
3. Is there a truth I have heard so many times that I no longer *hear* it — that I have, quietly, turned upside down so it no longer asks anything of me?
4. The *athīm* is the inner soil beneath the outer lie. What am I letting saturate my inner ground, drop by drop, that I have not yet named as *ithm*?
5. If lying hardens into a self that can no longer feel itself — what daily practice keeps my soul's alarm *sensitive* rather than muffled?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Woe is pronounced not on the one who erred once, but on the one who, by ignoring flinch after flinch, made the overturning of heard truth into a self — and the only safe place to stand is the small, present moment where the truth has just landed and has not yet been turned.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to leave this reflection terrified of a verdict. You need to leave it *awake* to a direction. The *affāk* is not a category you are sentenced to — he is a road, and roads are walked one ignored flinch at a time. So the invitation is small and immediate: the next time a truth lands on you and something inside flinches, or whispers *not now* — **stop at that exact point.** Honor the flinch. Close the gap. Keep one truth, today, right-side-up. That single act is the whole of this ayah, lived in reverse.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who recite Your signs to us in truth — do not let us be among those who hear them and turn them over. Keep alive in us the small flinch that warns, before the heart grows deaf to itself. Save us from becoming a self we can no longer feel, and let every truth that reaches our ears find a short road to our limbs. Āmīn.
