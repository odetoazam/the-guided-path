# The Light That Divided Them

## When Knowledge Becomes a Battlefield

---

### INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with the sound of them before we touch the meaning.

> [ayah:45:17] وَءَاتَيْنَـٰهُم بَيِّنَـٰتٍ مِّنَ ٱلْأَمْرِ ۖ فَمَا ٱخْتَلَفُوٓا۟ إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا جَآءَهُمُ ٱلْعِلْمُ بَغْيًۢا بَيْنَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَقْضِى بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ فِيمَا كَانُوا۟ فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

*Wa ātaynāhum bayyinātin mina-l-amr. Famā-khtalafū illā min baʿdi mā jāʾahumu-l-ʿilmu baghyan baynahum. Inna rabbaka yaqḍī baynahum yawma-l-qiyāmati fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn.*

"And We gave them clear proofs of the matter. And they did not differ except after the knowledge had come to them — out of transgression between themselves. Indeed, your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they used to differ."

#### Let us rebuild the world this verse is speaking into.

Read the ayah just before it — 45:16. Allah is listing what He gave the Children of Israel, and it reads like an inventory of every blessing a community could want. The Book. *Al-ḥukm* — sound judgment, the ability to rule and discern. Prophethood — not one prophet but a long chain of them. Good, pure provision. And then the crowning line: **We favored them over the worlds.**

Picture that. A people handed clarity itself. Not left to grope in the dark, not abandoned to guess at right and wrong. They were given **bayyināt** — clear, shining proofs. The matter was settled for them. The map was drawn, the road was lit, the destination marked.

And what happened?

This is the moment our ayah responds to. After all of that — after the Book, the judgment, the prophets, the proofs — they **divided**. They fractured. They turned on each other.

Now here is the question that should stop us in our tracks. **Why?**

We have an easy answer ready. People disagree because they don't know enough. Confusion divides us. If only everyone had the facts, if only the truth were clear, we would all stand together. That is the story we tell ourselves. More information, more unity. Clearer truth, less conflict.

This ayah looks at that story and quietly takes it apart.

[PAUSE]

Because notice *when* the dividing happened. Not before the knowledge came. **After.** *Min baʿdi mā jāʾahumu-l-ʿilm* — "after the knowledge had come to them."

The clarity did not unite them. **The clarity was the very thing they fought over.**

And the verse tells us the reason in a single word — a word we will spend a long time inside today — a word that has nothing to do with the mind and everything to do with the heart.

Stay with me. By the end, I don't think you'll ever read the phrase "we just need to educate people" the same way again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Root That Appears Three Times

Let me teach you something about how Arabic works before we open the verse, because once you see it, this ayah lights up from the inside.

In Arabic, words grow from roots — usually three consonants that carry a core idea. And a writer of real artistry can take a single root and plant it several times in one sentence, in different forms, so that the whole sentence hums on one frequency. It's like a composer returning to the same three notes in different octaves. You may not consciously notice it. But you *feel* the unity.

There is a root in this ayah that appears **three times**. The root is **ب-ي-ن** (b-y-n). Its core meaning is *separation that makes things clear* — to distinguish, to make distinct, to set apart so the truth shows. From this one root come both the idea of **clarity** and the idea of **between**.

Now watch where it lands.

**First appearance:** *bayyināt* — **بَيِّنَات** — "clear proofs." The gift. The thing Allah gave them. Clarity itself, from the root b-y-n.

**Second appearance:** *baynahum* — **بَيْنَهُم** — "between them." *Baghyan baynahum* — the transgression that broke out **between** them.

**Third appearance:** *baynahum* again — **بَيْنَهُم** — "Indeed your Lord will judge **between** them."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened?

The same root — the root of *making clear*, the root of *between* — is woven through the entire verse three times. And it traces the whole tragic arc:

Allah gave them **clarity** (bayyināt).
That clarity became the ground of war **between** them (baynahum).
And Allah will finally **judge between** them (baynahum) on the Last Day.

The very thing meant to stand *between* them as common light — the bayyināt — became the thing that came *between* them as a wall. And the only one who can restore what should have been *between* them is the One who steps **between** them at the end.

One root. Three positions. **Clarity given. Clarity weaponized. Clarity restored.**

This is not decoration. This is the architecture of the whole verse compressed into three consonants. The Arabic is *telling you*, beneath the meaning of the words, that the gift and the wound and the healing are all the same substance. Light. What was given as light became a blade, and only Allah can melt the blade back into light.

Let that sit. One clear statement before we move on: **the proof that should have stood between them as a bridge became the line drawn between them as a border.**

### Element Two: The Single Word That Names the Disease

Now we come to the word I promised you. The diagnosis. One word, and the entire mystery of "why did the clarity divide them?" collapses into it.

The word is **بَغْيًا** — *baghyan*.

First, let me teach you the grammar, because the grammar itself is doing something beautiful.

In Arabic, you can describe an action, and then add a noun in a particular grammatical case — the accusative — that answers the question **"why?"** The grammarians call it *maf'ūl li-ajlih*, the object of *cause* or *purpose*. It's the word that tells you the *motive* behind the verb.

So the sentence runs: "they did not differ... except after the knowledge came to them" — and then, standing in that accusative, answering *why* — **baghyan**. Their differing happened **out of baghy**.

This single word, grammatically, is the answer to the entire question of the verse. Not "they differed because the text was ambiguous." Not "they differed because they were confused." They differed — *baghyan*. Out of **baghy**.

So what is baghy?

Let's go down to the root, because the root will undo you a little.

The root **ب-غ-ي** (b-gh-y) — in its most basic, innocent form — means simply **to seek. To desire. To want.** *Baghā yabghī* — he sought, he looked for something. It is one of the most ordinary human verbs there is. To seek is not a sin. To seek is to be alive. The child seeks the mother. The thirsty seek water. The believer seeks his Lord. We are all, every one of us, *seekers*.

So how does this gentle root — *to seek* — become the word for the thing that shattered a chosen people?

Here is the turn. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, in his *Mufradāt*, gives the precise definition: **baghy is seeking to exceed the proper measure** — *ṭalab tajāwuz al-iqtidār fīmā yutaḥarrā*. It is seeking that **overshoots the line.** The seeking is not the problem. The *overshooting* is the problem. Baghy is desire that will not stay within its banks.

And the lexicons — *Lisān al-ʿArab* — preserve images for this root that I want you to hold in your body, not just your head. They say *baghā al-jurḥ* — "the wound *festered*." It swelled past healthy flesh, corrupted, overflowed its bound. And they say *baghā al-māʾ* — "the water *rose and overflowed*," the river breaking its banks and flooding the land it was meant to nourish.

[PAUSE]

Sit with those two pictures.

A wound that swells past its edge until it poisons the body. A river that rises past its bank until it drowns the field it was supposed to feed.

**That** is baghy. It is not a foreign invader. It is something *good* — the river that gives life, the body's own flesh — that has *exceeded its proper measure*. The seeking soul, overflowing its bank.

Now bring it back. *Why* did they divide after the clarity came? Because the human drive to seek — to want, to aspire, to rise — **overflowed its banks**. The classical commentators are nearly unanimous on what the overflow looked like. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Jalālayn render *baghyan* as **ḥasadan** — *envy*. Imam al-Ṭabarī and the others speak of it as the will to dominate. Al-Muyassar puts it most concretely: it was *baghy of some against others, seeking elevation and leadership* — *ṭalaban lil-rifʿati wa-l-riʾāsa*. They wanted to be **above**. They wanted to be **seen** as the ones who were right. The seeking of status overflowed the seeking of truth, and flooded everything.

Here is the devastating part. **Knowledge gave the ego more to fight over, not less.**

When the truth is murky, there's little glory in claiming it. But when the truth is *clear* — when the bayyināt have arrived and everyone can see the proof — now being *the one who holds it* becomes a prize. Now there is rank to be won. Now "I am right" can become "I am *above* you." The very clarity that should have ended the argument raised the stakes of the argument. The light gave the baghy something to swell against.

And do you hear the sound of the word? **Baghyan** — that thick, choked *ghayn* in the throat, *bagh-*. Compare it to the soft, open, flowing *bayyināt*. The clear proof flows off the tongue like water; the transgression catches in the throat like something swelling, something congested, something that won't pass cleanly. The Arabic *sounds* like the festering it describes.

One clear statement: **they did not lack the truth. They resented sharing it. The disease was never in the head — it was always in the heart that wanted to be higher than its brother.**

### Element Three: The Verb That Opens and Closes the Cage — and the Word "Your"

One more piece, and this one shows you the master architecture.

There is a verb form in Arabic — **Form VIII** — that often carries the sense of *doing something among yourselves, reflexively, mutually.* The root **خ-ل-ف** (kh-l-f) at its base means *to be behind, to come after, to go against.* In Form VIII it becomes **ikhtalafa** — *to differ, to fall into mutual opposition* — each one turning his back against the other, each going *behind* and *against* his brother.

Now watch where this verb sits in the verse — because it forms a cage around everything.

It appears at the **beginning**: *famā-**khtalafū*** — "they differed" — in the **perfect** tense. Done. A completed historical fact. They fractured.

And it appears at the very **end**: *kānū fīhi **yakhtalifūn*** — "they used to keep differing" — and now it shifts to the **imperfect**, the tense of ongoing, unfinished, repeated action. *Kānū... yakhtalifūn* — "they *kept on* differing," disputing without end, the argument that never closes.

So the entire verse is locked between two walls of the same word: they *differed*... and they *kept differing*. The opening tells you it happened. The closing tells you it never stopped. The dispute is a sealed room with no earthly door. They went in, and the verse shows them still inside, still circling, still at each other — *fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn*, "concerning that which they would not stop differing over."

And then — into this sealed, hopeless, endless room — comes the one word that breaks the wall. Not their word. **His.**

*Inna **rabbaka** yaqḍī baynahum* — "Indeed **your Lord** will judge between them."

Notice the address. **Rabbaka** — "*your* Lord." The *kāf*, the "your," is spoken to the Prophet ﷺ. The verse has been describing, in cold historical distance, a faceless "them" who divided and divided and would not stop. And suddenly Allah turns His face to His Messenger and speaks to him directly, intimately: *your* Lord. The grammarians call this turn *iltifāt* — the sudden pivot of address — and it is never accidental.

Why turn to the Prophet here? Because he is the one who *knows this pain*. He is the one who brought the clearest bayyināt the world had ever seen — and watched them rejected, watched people divide over him *baghyan*, out of pure envy, exactly as their ancestors had divided over the proofs before. Al-Muyassar even voices the address openly: *inna rabbaka — ayyuhā-r-rasūl* — "your Lord, **O Messenger**, will judge."

It is consolation. After the long, grim picture of the endless dispute, Allah leans toward the one carrying the weight of it and says: *this is not yours to resolve. It is not yours to win. It belongs to your Lord, and He will close the room.* The verb *yaqḍī* — "will judge, will decree, will bring to final verdict" — is the door no human hand could open. The differing was sealed shut by their baghy. Only the Lord can break the seal.

One clear statement: **the dispute had no earthly exit — so Allah did not give them an argument to win. He gave His Messenger a Judge to trust.**

### Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. One root — *b-y-n* — tracing clarity into division into final judgment. One word — *baghy* — naming the disease as a swelling of the seeking-self past its bank, not a failure of the mind. And one verb — *ikhtalafū... yakhtalifūn* — caging the whole human story between a fracture and an endless argument, until "your Lord" steps in.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about us. About the way *you* and *I* fight. About what to do with the argument you will never win.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Truth Did Not Set Them Free — It Gave Them Something to Fight Over

Let me start where you actually live.

Think about the most bitter conflict you know of. Not between strangers. Strangers don't really wound each other. Think of the feud between two scholars in the same field. The split between siblings who grew up in the same house. The schism inside a single mosque, a single family, a single friendship that once shared everything. The break between two people who *agreed on almost everything.*

Have you noticed this? **The deepest divisions are not between people who are far apart. They are between people who are close.** The civil war is bloodier than the foreign war. The theological splits that drew the most blood in history were not between religions — they were between two readings of the *same* verse, two camps who shared the *same* book, the *same* prophet, the *same* God.

Why? We expect proximity to breed unity. The data of human history says the opposite. Proximity breeds the *fiercest* division.

This ayah tells you exactly why, and it is not flattering to any of us.

[PAUSE]

When people are far apart, there's nothing to *seek* from each other. No rank to win, no superiority to establish. But when people stand on the *same* ground, holding the *same* truth, looking at the *same* clear proof — now there is a prize. Now the question is no longer "what is true?" The question quietly becomes **"who is the truest? Who is the most right? Who stands above whom?"**

That is *baghy*. The seeking that overflowed its bank. And here is the trap that the Children of Israel fell into, and that we fall into every single day: **the clearer the truth, the more the ego can hide inside it.** When I'm defending something genuinely *true*, I get to feel righteous *while* I crush you. My baghy puts on the robe of the bayyināt. My need to be above you disguises itself as zeal for the truth. And I can no longer tell — *I genuinely cannot tell* — whether I am defending the proof or defending my throne.

This is why "we just need to educate people" does not heal the world. Information does not touch baghy. You can give two people the identical, perfect, shining proof — and watch them divide *harder*, because now each one has a brighter banner to march under. The knowledge came (*jāʾahumu-l-ʿilm*) — and it became ammunition.

Al-Muyassar adds the line that turns this whole verse around to face *us*: *wa fī hādhā taḥdhīrun li-hādhihi-l-umma an taslukā maslakahum* — "and in this is a **warning to this Ummah** not to walk their path." The verse is not a history lesson about *them*. It is a mirror held up to *us*. Every time this community has split — and it has split, and split, and split — it has almost never been for lack of knowledge. The knowledge was there. It was baghy. It was the seeking-self overflowing its bank, dressed up as defense of the truth.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It gives you a diagnostic question you can ask yourself in the middle of any disagreement — and it is a brutal one. Not "*am I right?*" You probably are, on some point. The question is: **"Do I want this person to *see* the truth — or do I want them to *lose*?"**

Because if some part of you wants them to *lose* — wants them to be exposed, humbled, put *below* you — then no matter how correct your position is, you are not carrying bayyināt anymore. You are carrying baghy. The proof has become a blade. And the verse has already told you where that road ends: in a sealed room of endless differing, *fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn*, until your Lord has to come and judge what you refused to lay down.

The practical shift is small and almost unbearable: in your next real disagreement, before you speak, locate the *seeking* in your own chest. Is it seeking the truth — or seeking to be *above*? Keep the seeking inside its bank. The moment you feel it begin to swell — to want their humiliation, not their guidance — that swelling *is* the baghy of this verse, beginning in you.

### Theme Two: The Argument You Will Never Win in This Life

Now I want to speak to a different kind of person. Not the one tempted to win — the one who is **exhausted from losing.**

There is a particular grief that the modern world barely has language for. It is the grief of the **truth that was never vindicated.** You were wronged, and the wrong was never acknowledged. You were right, and you were never proven right. Someone slandered you and walked away clean. A relationship broke over a lie, and the lie stands to this day as the official story. You sat in a dispute where you *knew* the clear truth — you had the bayyināt — and you watched the other side win the room anyway, *baghyan*, out of sheer will to dominate. And there was nothing you could do.

And you have carried it. For years, maybe. The unsettled account. The argument that has no ending, that replays in your chest at 3am, the *yakhtalifūn* that will not become past tense.

This ayah was revealed for you too.

Look at what Allah does *not* say. He does not say, "Don't worry, the truth will come out *in this life*." He does not promise you earthly vindication. The verse is painfully honest: they *kept* differing — *kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn* — the imperfect tense, the open wound, the argument with no earthly door. Some accounts will simply never be settled here. Some truths will go to the grave uncredited. The verse does not lie to you about that.

What it gives you instead is something deeper than winning.

*Inna rabbaka yaqḍī baynahum yawma-l-qiyāmati* — "Indeed your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection."

[PAUSE]

Look at the precision of the consolation. Three things, and each one is a balm.

**First:** *yaqḍī* — He will *judge, decree, bring to final verdict.* Not "discuss." Not "review." *Qaḍāʾ* is the gavel that ends all appeal. The room that had no earthly door — He opens it and closes the case forever.

**Second:** *baynahum* — He will judge **between them.** That root again — b-y-n. The very "between" that was violated, the space that should have held shared light and instead held a wall — *that exact space* is where He renders His verdict. He does not judge over their heads. He steps into the precise breach and settles what stood between.

**Third** — and this is the one that should bring you to tears — *fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn* — "concerning **that very thing** over which they used to differ." Not a general accounting. Not a vague cosmic settling-up. The *specific content* of the dispute is preserved. The exact thing you fought over, the exact wrong, the precise truth that no one would acknowledge — *that* is what He addresses. **Nothing is dropped. Nothing is forgotten. Nothing is too small to be filed.** The argument you gave up on, the account everyone else moved past, the truth that the world overwrote — it is being held, in full detail, in the record of the One who will judge *fīmā kānū fīhi yakhtalifūn*.

Do you feel the weight lift?

You do not have to win the argument now. You do not have to force the acknowledgment, manufacture the vindication, spend your one short life relitigating the unsettled case. **Your Lord** — and remember, He said *rabbaka*, *your* Lord, the One who is intimately yours — has already entered the verdict into His ledger. The case is not closed because it is *forgotten*. It is open because the *Judge has not yet ruled* — and when He rules, He will rule on *that very thing*.

The practical shift here is the gift of being able to **lay the argument down.** Not because you were wrong. Not because it didn't matter. But because it has been transferred to a higher court, and a higher court does not lose files. When the old grievance rises in you tonight — the unsettled account, the truth no one credited — you are allowed to say the words this verse puts in your mouth: *"My Lord will judge between us, on that very thing, on a Day I trust more than I trust this world's verdict. I can put it down now."*

That is not giving up. That is *qaḍāʾ* — handing the gavel to the only hand that was ever fit to hold it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now That We Can See It

Go back and look at the whole verse as one shape, because now every piece fits.

It opens with a **gift of light** — *bayyināt*, from the root b-y-n, clarity given.

It moves to a **fracture** — *baghyan baynahum* — and the b-y-n returns, but now it marks the wound: the space *between* them, violated by the seeking-self that overflowed its bank.

And it closes with a **restoration** — *yaqḍī baynahum* — the b-y-n a third time, the very space between them, now the place where the divine verdict falls.

Clarity → division → judgment. **Given between them, broken between them, healed between them.** One root, three times, carrying the entire moral history of a people — and of you.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discovery and the two themes are one truth seen from two sides.

The root *b-y-n* showed us, in the structure of the words, that the gift and the wound are made of the *same substance* — light becomes the blade when baghy gets hold of it. And that is exactly Theme One: **the clearer the truth, the more dangerous it becomes in the hands of an ego that wants to be above.** Knowledge did not unite them because knowledge cannot cure baghy; only the heart can.

And the iltifāt — the turn to *rabbaka*, "your Lord," sealing the verse with *yaqḍī* — showed us, in the grammar, that the human room has no door but the divine one. And that is exactly Theme Two: **you do not have to win the unwinnable argument, because your Lord will judge between you on that very thing.**

The same insight, twice: **stop swelling past your bank to defeat your brother — and stop drowning in the argument you cannot win.** Lay the seeking down inside its measure. Hand the verdict to the One who steps *between*.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. Bring to mind your most stubborn disagreement. Honestly — do you want the other person to *see* the truth, or to *lose*? What would change if you only wanted the first?

2. Where in your life has your *seeking* — your good, God-given drive to aspire and to rise — quietly overflowed its bank and started to flood the people closest to you?

3. Is there an old account, an unvindicated truth, that you have been relitigating in your chest for years? What would it feel like to formally transfer it to a court that does not lose files?

4. When you defend "the truth," can you tell the difference between defending the proof and defending your throne? How would you even know?

5. Al-Muyassar said this verse is a warning to *this* Ummah. Where do you see knowledge being used as ammunition among the people of the Qur'an today — and where do you do it yourself?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the clearest truth arrives, what divides people is never their minds but their egos — the seeking-self overflowing its bank — and the argument it creates has no earthly door, only the verdict of *your* Lord, who will judge between them on that very thing.

### The Closing Invitation

You came in, perhaps, believing what we all half-believe: that if the truth were just clear enough, we would stop fighting. This verse has gently dismantled that. The truth *was* clear. They had the bayyināt. They divided anyway — *baghyan baynahum*.

So the invitation is not "seek more clarity." You may already have all the clarity you need. The invitation is to watch the *seeking* in your own chest, and to keep it inside its bank — to notice the exact moment your love of the truth begins to swell into a love of being above, and to *stop it there*, while it is still small. And where the world has handed you an argument you can never win, the invitation is to lay it down — not in defeat, but in trust — into the hands of the One who judges *between*, on that very thing, on the Day that outranks every verdict here.

Not information. A different way to hold every disagreement you will ever have.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You gave us light and we have so often turned it into a blade — forgive us our baghy, the seeking that overflows its bank, the part of us that loves to be *above*. Keep our seeking inside its measure, and let our love of Your truth never curdle into love of our own standing. And for every account we could not settle, every truth no one would credit — we hand it to You, *our* Lord, who judges between Your servants on that very thing. We trust Your verdict more than the world's. Let us lay the argument down.

*Āmīn.*
