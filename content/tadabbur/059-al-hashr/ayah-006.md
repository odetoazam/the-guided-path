# The Shadow That Returned: Wealth Without Spurring

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single word.

> وَمَآ أَفَآءَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِۦ مِنْهُمْ فَمَآ أَوْجَفْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ خَيْلٍ وَلَا رِكَابٍ وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُسَلِّطُ رُسُلَهُۥ عَلَىٰ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

*Wa mā afāʾa-llāhu ʿalā rasūlihi minhum fa-mā awjaftum ʿalayhi min khaylin wa lā rikābin wa lākinna-llāha yusalliṭu rusulahu ʿalā man yashāʾ, wa-llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr.*

"And whatever Allah **restored** to His Messenger from them — you **spurred** for it neither horses nor riding-camels, but Allah gives His messengers power over whomever He wills. And Allah is competent over all things."

### Rebuild the world

Picture Madinah, a few years into the migration. A tribe living on the edge of the city — Banu Nadir — has broken faith with the Muslims. The community moves against them. But there is no great battle. No clash of armies on an open field. There is a siege, a tightening of pressure, and then — the tribe leaves. They pack what their camels can carry and they go. Behind them they leave date-palm gardens heavy with fruit, houses, wells, fortified dwellings, fields that took generations to cultivate.

And now there is wealth. Real wealth. Standing there, unguarded, suddenly belonging to no one.

Put yourself among the believers in that moment. Many of them are *muhajirun* — emigrants who left everything in Makkah and arrived in Madinah with nothing, depending on the generosity of their hosts. They have been poor for years. And here, in front of them, is a windfall the size of an entire tribe's accumulated property. The natural human instinct rises immediately: *this is ours, we won it, divide it among us — a share for every man, the way spoils are divided after a battle.*

And it is right here — at the height of that expectation — that the revelation comes down. Not to deny them. But to **rename what is happening to them.** [PAUSE]

### The Hook

There is a single word at the start of this ayah that quietly dismantles the entire instinct of the human heart toward what it possesses. The believers were about to call this wealth one thing. Allah calls it something else entirely — and the word He chooses comes from the same root as a phenomenon you have watched a thousand times without ever knowing it was speaking to you. It happens every afternoon. It involves a shadow.

Stay with me. By the end, you will not look at anything you own the same way again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَفَآءَ — the word that means *to return*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word, because the whole ayah turns on it.

In Arabic, words are built from roots — usually three letters — and the entire family of meanings that grows from that root stays connected, like branches from one trunk. When you learn that two very different-looking words share a root, you have not learned a coincidence. You have learned that the Arabs, and the One who revealed the Quran in their tongue, saw a hidden kinship between two things you thought were unrelated.

Now. The verb that opens this ayah is **أَفَآءَ — *afāʾa*.** The translations render it "gave" or "bestowed" or "conferred." Those are not wrong, exactly. But they are flattening something. Because *afāʾa* does not come from a root meaning *to give*. Watch.

The root is **f-y-ʾ (فيء).** And the core meaning of this root — its trunk — is **to return. To come back. To revert to where something belongs.** [PAUSE]

*Afāʾa* is Form IV of that root. In Arabic, Form IV typically takes a verb and adds a causative force — it means *to cause the thing to happen.* So if the root means "to return," then *afāʾa* means **"to cause to return. To restore. To bring back to its rightful place."**

So read the ayah again with that precision restored:

"And whatever Allah **caused to return** to His Messenger from them..."

Not *gave.* Not *granted as a prize.* **Returned.** As if it were always meant to be there, and had simply been, for a while, in the wrong hands.

Notice what this single morphological choice does. The believers were about to use the word *ghanīma* — spoils, gain, the root meaning of which is *to acquire, to profit, to win.* That word frames wealth as **something you seized that wasn't yours, and now is.** Conquest. Acquisition. The hunter and the kill.

Allah refuses that frame completely. He reaches for a different root — not *gain*, but **return** — and in doing so He tells you the wealth was never being *won.* It was being **restored to its proper owner.** The believer does not seize; the believer receives back what the King had always assigned to him. [PAUSE]

Now here is the thing I promised you. Here is why this root will change how you see the world.

The very same root, **f-y-ʾ**, gives Arabic its word for a particular kind of shadow. Not just any shade. The morning shade is called *ẓill.* But there is a specific word — **الفَيْء — *al-fayʾ*** — and the lexicons are precise about it: *al-fayʾ* is **the shadow that returns in the afternoon.**

Think about what a shadow does across a day. At dawn, shadows stretch long toward the west. As the sun climbs to its peak, the shadows shrink and vanish — at noon a thing may stand with almost no shadow at all. And then, in the afternoon, as the sun descends the other side of the sky, the shadow **comes back** — it lengthens again, now reaching east. That returning shadow, the one that comes back after having disappeared, the Arabs called *al-fayʾ.* The shade that **returned.**

Sit with this. Because it is the whole ayah in a single image.

When the afternoon shadow returns and falls across you and cools you — **did you make that happen?** Did you reach up and move the sun? Did you spur a horse, exert a muscle, strcategize, hustle, earn it? Or did you simply *stand where you were standing* — while a celestial body you do not command moved across a sky you did not build, and the shade returned to you as a gift of that motion?

[PAUSE]

That is *fayʾ.* That is what Allah is naming this wealth. **It came to you the way the afternoon shadow comes to you** — not because you generated it, but because the One who moves all things moved them, and the blessing fell across you while you stood. The same root. The same lesson. The shade that returns, and the wealth that returns — both arrive by a motion that is not yours.

What this single word reveals: **everything you possess is fayʾ.** It is shade that fell on you because the Owner of the sun decided where the light would land. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: أَوْجَفْتُمْ — the word that sounds like the effort it denies

Now the ayah does something abrupt, and I want you to feel the jolt of it.

Up to this point Allah has been speaking *about* parties in the third person: *Allah* restored, to *His Messenger*, from *them.* He, His, them. The grammar is keeping a respectful distance, describing events from above.

And then, mid-sentence, without warning, the address swings around and points directly at the listener:

**فَمَآ أَوْجَفْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ — "you spurred nothing for it..."**

*You.* Suddenly *you.* In Arabic this turning of the address — shifting from talking *about* someone to talking *to* them — is a known rhetorical move called *iltifāt*, and it always lands like a hand on the shoulder. It is the difference between hearing a sermon about "people" and suddenly realizing the speaker is looking straight at *you* and has been all along. The community wanted to make a claim — *we earned this.* And Allah turns, faces them, and addresses the claim head-on: **"YOU spurred nothing."** [PAUSE]

Now the word itself. **أَوْجَفْتُمْ — *awjaftum.*** The root is **w-j-f (وجف)**, and the lexicons — al-Qurtubi spells it out — define *wajaf* as **the hard, urgent, driving gait of a mount; speed, the agitated running of a horse or camel when it is pushed.** *Awjafa* is Form IV again — to *cause* the animal to break into that strained, galloping, panting run. To spur. To drive hard. To push your mount until it strains.

So the literal picture: "you did not **drive your animals hard** to get this." You did not mount up at dawn, you did not gallop across the desert, you did not exhaust your horses in the chase. You did not *spur.*

Now listen to the word as **sound.** *Aw-jaf-tum.* Feel the cluster in the middle — that *j-f* catching in the throat, the abrupt *t*, the whole word a little breathless and effortful, like a panted exhalation, like the sound of exertion itself. The word for *straining a mount into a hard run* **sounds like straining.** It has the texture of labor in its very consonants.

And what does Allah do with this strenuous, effortful, panting word? [PAUSE]

He **negates it.** *Fa-mā awjaftum* — "you did **not** spur." The word that sounds like effort is placed in the sentence precisely so that the word *not* can be set in front of it. The Quran summons the full sound of human striving — and then cancels it. You can almost hear the heart's claim of "*but we worked for this!*" rising up in the very phonetics of *awjaftum* — and then *mā*, *no*, lays it flat. [PAUSE]

And there is one more small jewel here. The ayah says *min khaylin wa lā rikābin* — "of **horses** nor **riding-camels.**" The grammarians, including al-Jalalayn, note that the *min* before *khayl* is *zāʾida* — an "extra" particle added not for new meaning but for **emphasis of total absence.** Its function is to say: *not even one.* Not a single horse. Not one camel. The negation is swept completely clean. Not "you did little"; rather, **"of striving, there was precisely none."**

And why both *khayl* and *rikāb* — horses and camels? Because between them they are the two instruments of human effort in that world: the horse, the animal of speed and combat; the riding-camel, the animal of the long journey and the carried load. Together they are a complete picture of *how a human being exerts himself to get somewhere and seize something.* And Allah lists both, only to put *neither* in your hands. The full toolkit of human striving — named, and emptied. [PAUSE]

What this word reveals: **your sweat is not the cause.** The ayah does not merely say the wealth came easily. It performs, in its very grammar and its very sound, the **erasure of human effort as the explanation** for what arrives in your life. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: يُسَلِّطُ — *but* — and the hinge of the whole ayah

So if not your spurring, your horses, your effort — then *what?* The ayah does not leave the space empty. It pivots on one of the most decisive words in Arabic:

**وَلَـٰكِنَّ — *wa lākinna* — "but rather."**

This is the word of contrast, of correction, of *no, instead, the truth is this.* It stands like a hinge in the middle of the ayah. On one side: everything that is *not* the cause — your horses, your camels, your striving. On the other side, after the hinge, the single real cause:

**وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُسَلِّطُ رُسُلَهُۥ — "but rather Allah gives His messengers power..."**

The verb is **يُسَلِّطُ — *yusalliṭu*** — root **s-l-ṭ (سلط).** You already know this root, even if you don't know that you do. It is the root of **سُلْطَان — *sulṭān*** — authority, dominion, the *sultan.* The core meaning is *dominance, overpowering authority, being set firmly over something.*

And here it is in **Form II** — the form that intensifies and makes-to-happen. *Yusalliṭu* means **"He sets [them] in dominance over; He imposes their authority; He empowers them decisively over."** It is an active, deliberate, sovereign act. Allah does not merely *permit* His messengers to prevail — He *installs* their dominance. He *sets* them over their enemies.

Now feel the architecture of the contrast Allah has built:

- *You* — and the verb of your effort is **negated.** *Mā awjaftum.* You spurred *nothing.*
- *Allah* — and the verb of His action is **intensive, active, sovereign.** *Yusalliṭu.* He decisively empowers.

The human verb is cancelled. The divine verb is doubled in force. That is the whole theology of provision compressed into two verbs facing each other across the word *but.* [PAUSE]

And then — *ʿalā man yashāʾ* — "over **whomever He wills.**" The root *sh-y-ʾ*, the verb of pure divine will. Not over whom you defeated. Not over whom your strategy outmaneuvered. Over whom *He* willed. As al-Muyassar explains it: Allah turns the hearts of the enemy, and they yield without a battle even being fought. The outcome was authored in the will of Allah long before any believer thought to reach for a sword.

And the seal of the ayah: **وَٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ — "And Allah is competent over all things."** The root *q-d-r*, divine power and measuring-out. *Kulli shayʾin* — *every single thing.* This is not a footnote. It is the foundation under everything that came before. Why did the wealth return without your spurring? *Because the One arranging it is powerful over every single thing* — including the thing you were sure only your effort could secure. [PAUSE]

### Bridge

So watch what these three words have done, standing together.

*Afāʾa* told you the wealth **returned** — it was never seized, only restored, like the afternoon shade. *Awjaftum*, negated, told you **your effort did not cause it** — and said so in a word that sounds like effort, only to cancel the sound. And *yusalliṭu*, after the great hinge of *but*, told you **what the real cause was** — the sovereign, decisive, doubled act of Allah's will, sealed by His power over every single thing.

Three words. One claim, dismantled. One reality, revealed.

Now let us ask what this construction reveals not just about a tribe's abandoned gardens in the seventh century — but about your life, your provision, and the deepest illusion the human heart carries.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The illusion that your striving is the cause

Let me start where you actually live.

You woke up this morning and somewhere in you is a quiet equation that runs all day, mostly below awareness. It goes like this: **effort in, reward out.** You work the hours, the money comes. You send the applications, the offer arrives. You spur the horse, you catch the prize. And the corollary, the darker half of the equation: *if I stop spurring, it all stops.* The outcome is mine because the effort was mine.

This is the water you swim in. The entire modern story of the self is built on it: the hustle, the grind, the self-made man, the idea that you are the author of your provision and the architect of your outcomes. And it is intoxicating, because it *feels* true. You *did* work. You *can* feel the strain in your own muscles. The horse was real and you really were the one spurring it.

[PAUSE]

And into exactly this equation, Allah dropped a controlled demonstration.

Look at what He did. He did not choose an easy example to make this point. He chose **wealth** — the very thing the human heart is most certain it earns. And He arranged a case in which wealth — a tribe's worth of it, gardens and homes and wells — arrived into the hands of the believers **with the human-effort variable set to exactly zero.** No battle. No charge. No spurred horse. Not one camel driven hard. *Mā awjaftum min khaylin wa lā rikābin.*

He ran the experiment with the effort removed — and the provision still came. [PAUSE]

Do you see what He is teaching? He is not saying *effort is bad.* He is not telling you to stop working. He is breaking the **causal link** that your nafs insists upon — the link that says *my striving is what makes the outcome happen.* He removed the striving entirely, kept the outcome, and let you watch. As if to say: *here is provision arriving with none of your effort in it — so that you will finally understand that even when your effort is present, it was never the cause. It was only ever present alongside the cause.*

Your spurring and your provision have been arriving together your whole life, and you concluded that one made the other. The fayʾ is the case Allah engineered to show you they were never welded. The outcome belonged to Him the entire time.

[PAUSE]

Now re-enter through a human door. Think of the thing in your life you are most proud of having *earned.* The degree. The career. The home. The recovery. And then trace backward, honestly, all the things that had to be true for your effort to bear fruit — and that you did nothing to arrange. The brain you were given. The era you were born into. The one teacher who saw something in you. The door that happened to be open the week you knocked. The health that held just long enough. The economy that didn't collapse that year. The thousand silent permissions from the Owner of the sun, without any one of which your spurring would have spurred *nothing.*

You spurred. You really did. **But you did not author the field your striving ran across.** That field is *fayʾ.* It was returned to you while you ran, and you mistook your running for the reason. [PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Not that you stop working — the Prophet ﷺ tied his camel and then trusted. It changes the **posture inside the work.** You strive with your full strength and your full sweat, *and* you hold the outcome with an open hand, knowing it was never yours to command. The anxiety that lives in the equation "*if I stop spurring it all stops*" — that anxiety is the disease, and this ayah is the cure. You spur because you were commanded to spur. The returning of the shade belongs to Him. Tomorrow, do your work — and let the result rest in the hand of the One who is *qadīrun ʿalā kulli shayʾ.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: To receive what returns, instead of seizing what you own

Here is the second movement, and it is about the posture of the heart toward everything already in your hands.

Begin again with the human reality. There are two ways to hold a thing. You can hold it as a **conqueror** holds the spoils — *this is mine, I took it, I won it, and the holding of it proves my worth.* Or you can hold it as a **steward** holds something *returned* into his care — *this came back to me, it was assigned to me by an Owner, and my holding of it is a trust, not a trophy.*

These are not just two attitudes. They produce two completely different human beings.

The conqueror who holds his wealth as *ghanīma* — as seized gain — is always tense. Because what was *taken* can be *taken back.* His grip is anxious, defensive, proud. He measures himself by the pile. He resents anyone who threatens it. And when it is gone, *he* is gone, because his self was fused to the holding.

[PAUSE]

The companions, in that raw human moment, leaned toward the first posture. They saw the wealth of Banu Nadir and the instinct rose: *divide it among us, a fighter's share for every man — we won this.* It is recorded in the classical commentary — al-Baghawi among others reports — that the believers came to the Prophet ﷺ expecting it to be apportioned the way battlefield spoils are apportioned. A completely human expectation. And not a sinful one. Just — a *conqueror's* reading of what had happened.

And Allah, gently and decisively, corrected the *word.* Not *ghanīma.* **Fayʾ.** Not *seized gain* — **returned trust.** [PAUSE]

Feel what that re-naming does to the heart. The moment you understand that the thing in your hands did not get *taken* by you but *returned* to you — by an Owner, for a purpose, on loan — the whole grip changes. The tension drains out of it. You cannot be anxious about losing what you never seized in the first place. You cannot be arrogant about a shadow that fell on you while you stood still. And you cannot be devastated when it lifts, because the One who returned it had every right to move the sun again.

This is why the very next ayah — 59:7 — immediately turns this returned wealth *outward*, distributing it to the poor, the orphan, the wayfarer, "so that it does not become a thing circulated only among the rich among you." Do you see the logic? *Because* it is fayʾ — returned, not earned — it flows. Wealth understood as *seized* gets hoarded; that is what conquerors do. Wealth understood as *returned by Allah* gets *released*, because the one holding it knows he is a riverbed, not a reservoir. The naming in ayah 6 makes the generosity of ayah 7 inevitable. [PAUSE]

Now bring it into your own house. Look around at what you have — the income, the people, the health, the years. The nafs files all of it under *ghanīma*: **mine, earned, won, owed to me.** And that filing is the source of so much of your suffering — the clutching, the comparing, the terror of loss, the entitlement that curdles into bitterness the moment something is taken.

The ayah invites you to re-file every single thing under *fayʾ.* Not *I earned this* — but *this was returned into my care.* Your wealth: returned, like the shade. Your spouse, your children: returned into your keeping for a season, never seized, never owned. Your very breath: shade that the Owner of the sun lets fall across you each morning, and will, one appointed afternoon, let lift. [PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practice, and it is small and total at once. Tonight, take one thing you are gripping as *ghanīma* — one thing you secretly believe you *earned* and are *owed* — and rename it, deliberately, as *fayʾ.* Say it: *this was returned to me. It is a trust, not a trophy.* And then do the thing that only the steward can do and the conqueror never can — **let some of it flow.** Open the hand the width of one act of giving. Because the surest proof that you have understood the word *fayʾ* is that, like the wealth in the very next ayah, the blessing in your hands begins to *move toward others* instead of pooling and stagnating in you.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Stand back now and see how the two halves of this reflection are the same truth approached from opposite sides.

The **linguistic journey** showed you, from the side of *cause*: your spurring did not produce your provision — the word *awjaftum* was summoned in all its effortful sound only to be negated, and the real cause was named after the great *but* — *Allah yusalliṭu*, the sovereign, doubled, willing act of the One powerful over every single thing.

The **thematic journey** showed you, from the side of *posture*: therefore hold nothing as a conqueror holds spoils. Hold everything as a steward holds what was *returned* — *fayʾ*, the afternoon shade that fell across you while the Owner of the sun moved a sky you do not command.

They meet in the middle, in one realization: **You did not cause it, so you cannot truly own it; you can only receive it, hold it as trust, and let it flow.** The illusion of self-causation and the illusion of absolute ownership are the same illusion. This ayah dissolves both with a single word built from the root that means *to return.*

### Questions to carry

1. What is the one thing in my life I am most certain I *earned* — and if I trace honestly all that had to be given to me for my effort to bear fruit, how much of even that is *fayʾ*?
2. Where in my life is the anxiety "*if I stop spurring, it all stops*" quietly running the show — and what would change if I truly believed the outcome was never welded to my effort?
3. Am I holding my blessings as a conqueror holds spoils, or as a steward holds what was returned into his care?
4. If everything in my hands is *fayʾ* — returned, on loan — what should be *flowing out* of my hands that is currently pooling and stagnating?
5. When the shade finally lifts from something I love, will I meet it as theft, or as the Owner moving His own sun?

### One-sentence distillation

What you have was never seized by your striving — it was *returned* to you, like the afternoon shadow that falls while you stand still and a sun you do not command moves across the sky — so hold it as a trust restored, not a prize earned.

### The closing invitation

Nothing here asks you to work less. The Prophet ﷺ spurred his own camel and tied it at night. The invitation is gentler and far more total than that: keep striving with your whole strength — and quietly unbuckle your heart from the outcome. Move through tomorrow doing your work fully, while knowing in your bones that the provision falling across your day is *fayʾ* — shade returned by the will of the One who is *qadīrun ʿalā kulli shayʾ.* Let that knowledge make you, at once, more diligent in the work and more peaceful about the result. And let it open your hand.

### A brief duʿā

O Allah, You who restore the shade after the sun has moved — teach our hearts that all we hold is *fayʾ*, returned into our care and never seized by our hands. Loosen our grip from what we did not author, free us from the anxiety of believing our spurring is the cause, and let what You return to us flow out to those You have placed in our path. You are *qadīrun ʿalā kulli shayʾ* — powerful over every single thing. Āmīn.
