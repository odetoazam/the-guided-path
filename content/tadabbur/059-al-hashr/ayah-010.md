# The Ones Who Came After: A Prayer Against the Shackle in the Heart

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Hold it in three forms.

**The Arabic:**

> [ayah:59:10] وَٱلَّذِينَ جَآءُو مِنۢ بَعْدِهِمْ يَقُولُونَ رَبَّنَا ٱغْفِرْ لَنَا وَلِإِخْوَٰنِنَا ٱلَّذِينَ سَبَقُونَا بِٱلْإِيمَـٰنِ وَلَا تَجْعَلْ فِى قُلُوبِنَا غِلًّا لِّلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ رَبَّنَآ إِنَّكَ رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

**The sound of it:**

*Walladhīna jāʾū min baʿdihim yaqūlūna Rabbanā-ghfir lanā wa li-ikhwāninā-lladhīna sabaqūnā bil-īmāni wa lā tajʿal fī qulūbinā ghillan lilladhīna āmanū, Rabbanā innaka Raʾūfun Raḥīm.*

**The meaning:**

> "And those who came after them say: *Our Lord, forgive us, and our brothers who preceded us in faith, and do not place in our hearts any rancor toward those who believed. Our Lord, indeed You are Full of Kindness, Most Merciful.*"

---

### Rebuild the world

To feel where this ayah lands, you have to feel where it sits.

Surah Al-Hashr is a surah of *aftermath*. Its name means "The Gathering" — but it opens onto a battlefield of consequences. A tribe has been exiled from Madinah. Houses are being pulled down "by the hands of the believers and the hands of the believers' enemies" (59:2). There is war booty to distribute. There are loyalties fracturing, hypocrites whispering, alliances collapsing. It is a surah about who gets *what*, and who belongs to *whom*.

And in the middle of all this distribution — land, wealth, spoils — Allah does something quiet and enormous. He stops talking about property and starts taking a portrait of His community. Not a snapshot of one moment. A portrait *across time*.

First, verse 8: **the Muhajirun.** The emigrants. The ones who walked out of Makkah with nothing — left their homes, their wealth, their families — chasing only "bounty from Allah and His pleasure." They gave up *everything they had.*

Then, verse 9: **the Ansar.** The Helpers. The people of Madinah who were already home. And Allah praises them for something almost impossible: they "love those who emigrated to them, and find in their breasts no need for what the others were given" — *and they prefer others over themselves, even when poverty is their own condition.* They gave from their *own* table.

Two circles. The ones who left everything. The ones who shared everything.

And then verse 10 — *our* verse — opens a third circle. And this is the moment I want you to feel. Because the first two circles are *closed*. The Muhajirun are a finite list of names. The Ansar are a finite list of names. History could have ended the portrait there: here are the great ones, the first ones, the ones who were *there*.

[PAUSE]

But Allah doesn't close the portrait. He opens a door.

**"And those who came after them..."**

That's you. That's me. That's every single human being who would ever say *lā ilāha illā Allah* from that day until the last day. The portrait of the believing community is left deliberately, breathtakingly *unfinished* — so that there is room in it for everyone who was not yet born.

### The Hook

Here is what I need you to sit with before we begin.

When Allah finally describes the people of the *future* — the ones who weren't there for the migration, weren't there for the sacrifice, weren't there for any of it — He does not describe them by what they *built*, or what they *conquered*, or what they *gave up*.

He describes them by **two sentences they say** and **one feeling they ask to be spared.**

And the feeling they beg to be protected from is not fear of their enemies. It's not poverty. It's not death.

It is something they ask Allah to keep *out of their own chest.* Something so dangerous that the entry ticket into the company of the Muhajirun and the Ansar — across fourteen centuries — is being free of it.

Let's find out what it is.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Sabaqūnā bil-īmān* — "They raced ahead of us into faith"

Let me teach you a small thing about Arabic before we open the word.

Arabic, like every language, gives you *choices* about how to describe the same fact. Imagine two runners. One crosses the finish line; the other comes second. You could say the second runner *lost*. You could say the first runner *beat* him. You could say the first runner *got there first*. Every one of those describes the identical race — but each one carries a completely different feeling about it. One is sour. One is competitive. One is simply... grateful that someone arrived.

Now look at how the later believers describe the Companions who came before them.

> ٱلَّذِينَ سَبَقُونَا بِٱلْإِيمَـٰنِ
> *alladhīna sabaqūnā bil-īmān*
> "...who **preceded us** in faith."

The root here is **سبق (s-b-q)**, and it is a *racing* word. *Sabq* is the word for winning a race, for getting to the front, for being out ahead. From this root comes *musābaqa* — competition, contest. From this root the Quran tells us to "race one another" (*sābiqū*) toward forgiveness (57:21).

[PAUSE]

So sit with this. The later believers are looking back at the Companions and they could have phrased it a hundred ways. They could have envied them: *they got what we'll never get.* They could have resented being born too late: *why them and not us?* They could have felt the ache of the second runner who didn't make the podium.

Instead they reach for a *racing* word — and they place themselves, with total contentment, **behind.**

*They raced ahead of us into faith.*

Notice something. There is no bitterness anywhere in that phrasing. To call someone "the one who got ahead of me" is only sweet if you genuinely *want them* to have gotten there. A bitter heart cannot say "they preceded me" without flinching. The bitter heart says "they were *lucky*." The clean heart says "they *won*, and thank God they did, because they lit the path I'm now walking."

And look at the little word *bi-* (بِ), "**in** faith" — or more precisely, "**with** faith." They didn't precede us in *wealth*, or *land*, or *status*. The only race the believer cares about being behind in is the race *into īmān.* That is the one finish line where it is a *joy* to come second, because the person ahead of you isn't your rival — they're your *ancestor in light.*

This is the first thing the ayah teaches about the later believers: **they look backward across time and feel no envy — only gratitude that someone ran ahead and left the trail warm.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Lā tajʿal fī qulūbinā* — "Do not *place* it in our hearts"

Here is the second word, and it changes everything about how we think we control our own hearts.

> وَلَا تَجْعَلْ فِى قُلُوبِنَا غِلًّا
> *wa lā tajʿal fī qulūbinā ghillan*
> "...and do not **place** in our hearts any rancor."

I want you to notice what they did *not* say.

They did not say, "and **we will remove** rancor from our hearts." They did not say, "help us *fight* the rancor in our hearts." They did not even say, "**take out** the rancor that is there."

They said: *Do not* ***put it there in the first place.***

The verb is **تَجْعَل (tajʿal)**, from the root **جعل (j-ʿ-l)** — to make, to place, to set, to render. And it's addressed to Allah. *You*, Lord — do not *set* this thing inside us.

Stay with me, because there is a whole theology of the human heart hidden in this one grammatical choice.

In Arabic the word for heart is **قلب (qalb)**, and it comes from the root **قلب (q-l-b)** — which means *to turn over, to flip, to overturn, to change from one state to another.* The heart is literally named after its own *instability.* It is "the thing that keeps turning." This is why the Prophet ﷺ would so often pray: *"Yā muqallib al-qulūb — O Turner of hearts — keep my heart firm upon Your religion."* He addressed Allah by the very name buried inside the word *qalb.*

[PAUSE]

So now feel the weight of what these believers understood.

They knew that the heart is not a fortress you simply *command.* You cannot bark an order at your own chest and make resentment evaporate. Anyone who has ever tried to *will* themselves to stop resenting someone knows this in their bones. You can decide it. You can resolve it at night. And you wake up and the bitterness is still *sitting there*, exactly where it was, because **feelings do not obey direct orders.**

The later believers understood something the modern self-help world is only now catching up to: *you cannot simply decide not to feel.* The heart is *acted upon.* It is turned, flipped, set, rendered — by something beyond your conscious will.

So what do they do? They go to the *One who places things in hearts* and they ask Him: **don't place this one.**

This is not passivity. This is the deepest possible realism about the human interior. They are not pretending they can sterilize their own hearts by gritting their teeth. They are taking the heart's helplessness *seriously* — and carrying it to the only One with His hand on the dial. *You set what is set in us. So, please — not this.*

What this invites us to consider is staggering: **the cleanness of your heart toward your brother is something you are meant to beg for, not something you are expected to manufacture.** The duʿā itself is the act of worship. Asking is the work.

[PAUSE — let that sit]

### Element Three: *Ghill* — the rancor that is also a shackle

Now we arrive at the word the whole ayah has been circling. The thing they beg not to have placed in them.

> غِلًّا
> *ghillan*
> "...any rancor."

The root is **غل (gh-l-l)**, and I need to walk you carefully through this root, because the Arabic is doing something English simply cannot.

In its emotional sense, *ghill* means the *hidden* rancor of the heart — not loud, explosive anger, but the quiet, buried, low-grade grudge. The resentment that *settles*. The kind you can carry for years while smiling. Al-Baghawī glosses it precisely as *ghishsh, ḥasad, wa bughḍ* — deceit, envy, and hatred — the cold sediment at the bottom of the chest. It's not the storm. It's the *silt.*

But here is what the root *also* carries, in the very same letters. From **غل (gh-l-l)** comes **غُلّ (ghull)** — and *ghull* is the **iron collar**, the **shackle**, the **chain** clamped around a captive's neck. The Quran uses this exact root for the *aghlāl* — the fetters — placed on the necks of the people of the Fire (as in 40:71, "when the shackles are around their necks"). The same three letters give you both *the grudge in the heart* and *the chain on the neck.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what the Arabic is telling you for free?

**Rancor is a shackle.**

It is not a weapon you wield against your enemy. It is a *chain* clamped around *your own neck.* You think the grudge is something you *hold.* The language tells you it's something that *holds you.* The person you resent walks around free, often unaware. You are the one bound. *Ghill* in the heart and *ghull* on the neck are the same root because they are, spiritually, the same object. The resentment you carry against your brother is iron, and it is locked on *you.*

And now look at one final detail — a detail of *absence* that is easy to read past.

The word is **غِلًّا (ghillan)** — and in Arabic it is *indefinite.* Not *al-ghill*, "the rancor." Just *ghill* — "**any** rancor." A trace. A speck. The smallest sediment.

They are not asking, "save us from the *great* hatreds." They are asking to be spared *any of it at all.* Not a grudge. Not a coldness. Not a flicker of "they got what I deserved." The duʿā is for a heart scrubbed *completely* clean toward the believers — down to the last grain.

[PAUSE]

And notice the precise target of that cleanness:

> لِّلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا
> *lilladhīna āmanū*
> "...toward **those who believed.**"

The shackle they fear most is not resentment toward their *enemies.* It's resentment toward **their own.** Toward the believers. Toward the family. This is the danger the ayah singles out — not that you'll hate the disbeliever, but that a quiet *ghill* will lodge itself between you and your *brother in faith*, and bind you both in iron without anyone noticing.

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words have built, standing together.

A people who race to be *grateful* their predecessors finished ahead of them — *sabaqūnā.* A people who know they cannot purify their own hearts by force, and so beg the Turner of hearts to *not place* the poison — *lā tajʿal.* And the poison itself: *ghill* — the hidden silt that is secretly an iron chain, and they want *not a grain* of it toward their own.

Three words. One portrait of a clean heart across time.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You belong to people you will never meet

Let me start where you already live.

You are wearing, right now, the labor of thousands of people you will never thank. Someone you'll never meet figured out how to purify water so that you didn't die of cholera as a child. Someone laid the cable that carries this very sentence to you. Someone, centuries ago, worked out the mathematics that lets your phone find a satellite. You are *standing on an invisible mountain* of people who came before you, did the hard part, and handed you a world already half-built.

And here is the strange thing about being a late arrival to anything good: it can curdle two ways.

It can make you *grateful* — *look what I was handed, look at the trail they left warm for me.* Or it can make you *resentful* — *they had it easier, they got the glory, I'm just cleaning up after them, why do they get the credit?*

The same inheritance. Two completely opposite hearts.

[PAUSE]

Now bring that into faith.

You did not invent *lā ilāha illā Allah.* You received it. It came to you down a chain of fourteen centuries of human beings who memorized it, taught it, died for it, carried it across oceans and through famines and under swords so that it could arrive — intact — at *you.* You are, in the most literal sense, **"the ones who came after."** *Walladhīna jāʾū min baʿdihim.* That is your name in this ayah.

And Allah is showing you exactly which heart the late-arriving believer is *meant* to have toward the ones who ran ahead.

Not envy. Not "they had the Prophet ﷺ and I didn't, so they got the easy version." Not the sour theology of the latecomer who feels cheated by being born too late.

But this: *Rabbanā-ghfir lanā wa li-ikhwāninā — Our Lord, forgive us, and forgive our brothers who raced ahead of us into faith.*

[PAUSE]

Feel how astonishing that is. The first thing the later believer does — before asking anything for *himself*, in the same breath — is **pray for the dead.** For people he never met. For people who cannot do a single thing for him in return. He widens his duʿā backward across time to include strangers whose only relationship to him is that *they believed first.*

And this is the door into the community. Al-Qurtubī records the words of Ibn Abī Laylā: the people are in three stations — the Muhajirun, the Ansar, and *"those who came after them."* And then he says: *"strive that you do not fall outside these stations."* The way you stay *inside* the believing community — the way you, born in the wrong century, still get to stand in the portrait alongside the Companions — is by carrying their memory with *love* and praying for their forgiveness.

This is why al-Muyassar draws the lesson straight out: this ayah is proof that *"the Muslim should remember his predecessors with good, and make duʿā for them."* Your membership in the ummah is not just *forward*-looking — it is an act of love sent *backward.*

So here is what changes about tomorrow. **The believers who came before you are not your rivals for a finite prize. They are your brothers — and the proof of your faith is that you pray for them.** The next time you read a name from the early generations, or the name of a scholar long dead, or even a grandparent whose faith reached you — don't measure yourself against them. Send them a *Rabbanā-ghfir lahum.* That single sentence is your signature in the portrait.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The thing that quietly disqualifies you is *internal*

Let me start, again, with something you already know in your body.

You have, at some point, carried a grudge against someone in your own circle. Not an enemy — your *own.* A sibling who got the praise. A friend who succeeded faster. A fellow believer at the masjid who seems to have it all together while you struggle. Someone in your *own community* whose blessing somehow felt, in the dark of your chest, like a subtraction from *you.*

And you know exactly what that feeling does. It does not hurt *them.* They are off living their life. It sits in *you.* It poisons the gathering you're both in. It makes the duʿā stick in your throat when you try to ask good for them. It is, precisely, a *shackle* — and you are the one wearing it.

[PAUSE]

Now. Reread the ayah and notice what these believers are *most afraid of.*

They are at war. The whole surah is exile, siege, spoils, hypocrites, enemies on every side. There are a thousand external dangers they could have prayed about. And what is the *one* thing they beg Allah to keep out of their chest?

Not their enemies' swords. **Their own resentment toward their own brothers.**

*Wa lā tajʿal fī qulūbinā ghillan lilladhīna āmanū.*

This is the ayah's most unnerving insight. The danger that can quietly cost you your place in the company of the believers is not *out there.* It's the silt in your own heart toward the people standing next to you. You can survive every external enemy and still be *shackled from the inside* by a grudge against a brother.

And here is the paradox I want you to let sit, without rushing to fix it.

These are *believers* praying this. People of faith. People described by Allah in praise. And *they* are the ones who feel they need to beg not to have *ghill* placed in their hearts toward other believers. **The closer you get to faith, the more seriously you take the danger of this hidden rancor — not less.** It is not the sin of the faithless. It is the sediment that can settle even in a sincere chest, precisely because the people closest to you are the ones whose blessings are easiest to envy.

[PAUSE]

So why does the ayah end the way it does?

> رَبَّنَآ إِنَّكَ رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ
> *Rabbanā innaka Raʾūfun Raḥīm*
> "Our Lord, indeed You are Full of Kindness, Most Merciful."

Sit with *which* names they choose to close on. They have just confessed the heart's helplessness — *we cannot clean ourselves; don't let the chain be placed in us.* And they seal the prayer with **رَءُوف (Raʾūf)** and **رَحِيم (Raḥīm).**

*Raʾūf*, from the root *r-ʾ-f*, is the most *tender, intimate* form of compassion — the gentleness you show to someone fragile, someone who *cannot help themselves.* It is mercy bending low over weakness. And *Raḥīm* is the enveloping, sustaining mercy that wraps the believers.

Why these two names, here? Because a heart that has just admitted *"I cannot purify myself — You must do it"* needs to know it is asking the *right* God. Not a distant judge who says "fix your own heart." A *Raʾūf* — One whose very nature is to be tender toward the one who cannot manage on their own. They are saying: *We bring You our helpless, silt-stained hearts — and we know You are exactly the kind of Lord who handles helpless things with gentleness.*

[PAUSE]

So what changes about tomorrow?

Two things. First — **stop trying to white-knuckle your resentment, and start praying it out.** When you feel the cold grudge settle toward a brother or sister in faith, don't just command yourself to stop. You can't. Take it to the Turner of hearts in the exact words of this ayah: *Rabbanā lā tajʿal fī qalbī ghillan lilladhīna āmanū.* Make the duʿā *the* response. That is what the ayah teaches is the believer's actual tool.

Second — and this is concrete — **build the habit of asking forgiveness for the believer you're tempted to resent.** It is almost impossible to carry *ghill* toward someone you are sincerely praying *Allah forgive them* over. The duʿā and the grudge cannot occupy the same chest. The ayah hands you the antidote *inside the same sentence* as the diagnosis: you defeat *ghill* toward the believers by asking *maghfira* — forgiveness — *for* the believers.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now step back and see the whole structure, because it was being built the entire time.

Surah Al-Hashr draws the believing community as **three concentric rings:**

- **Verse 8 — the Muhajirun:** they gave up *everything they owned.*
- **Verse 9 — the Ansar:** they shared *everything they had,* and loved the newcomers, and *"found no ghill in their breasts"* for what others were given.
- **Verse 10 — those who came after:** us. And we are not asked to give up our homes or share our wealth. We are asked for *one thing* — the very thing the Ansar were praised for in the verse before.

Did you catch it? **Verse 9 praises the Ansar for having no resentment in their breasts. Verse 10 has the later believers** ***praying*** **for exactly that same clean heart.** The Ansar *achieved* it; the latecomers *beg* for it. The thread running through all three rings — emigrant, helper, and everyone after — is not sacrifice, or wealth, or even being present at the founding.

It is **a heart free of ghill toward your brothers.**

That is the spine of the whole community across all of time. The Muhajirun proved it by leaving. The Ansar proved it by sharing without envy. And you — the one who came after — prove it by *praying* for the ones ahead of you and *begging* not to resent the ones beside you.

### Bring it together

Now watch the linguistic discoveries and the themes become a single point.

The *racing* word — *sabaqūnā* — was gratitude pointed **backward** through time, toward the dead who ran ahead. The *placing* word — *lā tajʿal* — was helplessness pointed **upward**, toward the only One who can clean a heart. And *ghill*, the shackle-grudge, was the danger pointed **inward**, toward your own chest and your own brothers.

Backward in gratitude. Upward in helplessness. Inward in vigilance.

That is the entire posture of "the ones who came after." That is *your* posture. The believer born too late for the migration is given a way to stand in the portrait anyway: love those ahead of you, beg the One above you, and guard the heart within you.

### Questions to carry

1. When I think of the believers who came before me — the Companions, the scholars, my own elders in faith — is my first instinct *gratitude that they ran ahead*, or a quiet sense that *I was cheated by being born late*?
2. Is there a believer — in my family, my masjid, my circle — whose blessing has felt, in the dark of my chest, like a subtraction from me? Is there *ghill* I've been carrying and calling something else?
3. Have I been trying to *will* my heart clean by force — and failing — when I was meant to *beg* it clean instead?
4. When was the last time I made duʿā of forgiveness for someone I was tempted to resent? Could those two things even survive in my chest at the same time?
5. If my membership in the believing community is signed by how clean my heart is toward my brothers — what does my signature look like right now?

### One-sentence distillation

The proof that you belong to the believers — across all of time — is a heart that races to be grateful for those who came before you, and begs the Turner of hearts to keep it free of even a grain of rancor toward those who stand beside you.

### The closing invitation

You came after. That is not a loss. The ayah names it as a *station* — a place in the portrait reserved for you specifically. But the door into that station is narrow in exactly one way: it does not admit a shackled heart. So let this ayah become a prayer you actually pray, in its own words, in the moments you feel the silt settling. Not to perform purity — but because you finally believe what the verse believes: that the heart is *given*, not forced, and the One who gives it is *Raʾūf, Raḥīm.*

### Brief duʿā

*Rabbanā, forgive us, and forgive our brothers who raced ahead of us into faith. Yā Muqallib al-Qulūb — O Turner of hearts — do not place in our chests a single grain of rancor toward those who believed; lift the iron from our necks before it locks. Innaka Raʾūfun Raḥīm — for You are exactly the kind of Lord who handles our helpless hearts with gentleness.* Āmīn.
