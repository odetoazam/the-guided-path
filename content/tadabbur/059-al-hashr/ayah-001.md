# The Stones Were Already Glorifying

### Surah Al-Hashr, Ayah 1

> سَبَّحَ لِلَّهِ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*Sabbaḥa lillāhi mā fī-s-samāwāti wa mā fī-l-arḍ, wa huwa-l-ʿAzīzu-l-Ḥakīm.*

**"Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah — and He is the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me take you to Madinah, to a specific anxiety in the air.

There was a tribe living on the edge of the city — Banu Nadir. They were skilled, wealthy, and above all, *fortified*. They lived in strongholds: thick walls, high palms, date gardens that fed them, positions that could be defended. When the Prophet ﷺ first came to Madinah, they entered into a treaty with him: we will not fight you, and we will not fight against you.

Then they broke it. They conspired. And when the consequence came, they did what every secure people do when threatened — **they retreated behind their walls.** They climbed into their fortresses, sealed the gates, and waited. Their confidence was not foolish, by the ordinary calculus of the world. They had stone. They had height. They had supplies. They had every material reason to believe: *these walls will hold.*

The Qur'an is about to tell the story of how those walls did *not* hold — how a people certain of their security were uprooted and sent into exile, "al-Hashr," the gathering, the driving-out, which gives the surah its name.

Now — here is what should stop us.

[PAUSE]

A surah about a military expulsion, about treaty-breaking and siege and exile, about walls and weapons — **does not open with any of that.** It does not open with the conspiracy. It does not open with the army. It does not open with the Prophet ﷺ at all.

It opens with the *entire cosmos*. It lifts your eyes off the fortress and points them at the sky and the earth at once, and it makes a single, enormous statement: everything in the heavens and everything on the earth is *already* glorifying Allah.

Why begin a story about human security with the glorification of galaxies?

And there is a second thing hiding here, in the grammar, that the English completely loses. The verb "glorifies" — in the Arabic it is not in the present tense. It is in the *past*. Not "is glorifying." **"Already glorified."** Something has already happened. Something is already finished. Before the siege, before the walls fall, before a single thing the surah is about to describe — a verdict has already been rendered by all of creation.

Stay with that. We are going to find out what was already finished.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Is Already Over — *Sabbaḥa*

Let me first give you the concept, plainly, before we touch the word.

In any language, tense is not just *when* — it carries *certainty*. Think of the difference between someone saying, "I will pay you back," and someone placing the money in your hand and saying, "It's done. It's paid." The first is a promise stretched into the future, still open, still capable of failing. The second is a closed fact. You cannot argue with what is already done.

Arabic has this same distinction, and it makes it sharply. There is the imperfect tense — *yusabbiḥu*, "is glorifying," ongoing, present, stretching forward. And there is the perfect tense — *sabbaḥa*, "glorified," completed, sealed, behind us.

Now look at the word that opens our surah:

> **سَبَّحَ** — *sabbaḥa*

This is the perfect tense. The completed one. The sealed one.

Here is why a scholar of the Qur'an sits up at this. There is a small family of surahs that all open by declaring that creation glorifies Allah — sometimes called *al-Musabbiḥāt*. And they do not all use the same tense. Several of them — Surah As-Saff, Surah Al-Jumuʿah, Surah At-Taghābun — open with *yusabbiḥu*, the present, ongoing form: creation *is glorifying*, continuously, now. But this surah, Al-Hashr, like Al-Hadid before it, opens with **sabbaḥa** — the past, the completed.

Why would Allah choose the *finished* tense for *this* surah?

Let's sit with the context. This is the surah of a people who trusted their walls. A people who looked at their fortresses and calculated: we are secure, the outcome is uncertain, we may yet prevail. They lived inside the *future tense*. Everything for them was still open, still to be decided, still defendable.

And Allah opens their surah by saying: **it is already done.**

The glorification of all things to Allah — the universal acknowledgment of His transcendence, His sovereignty, the surrender of every created thing to its Creator — is not a process still unfolding, still up for grabs. It is *sabbaḥa*. Completed. The verdict was rendered before the siege began.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the people in the fortresses. They think they are at the beginning of a story whose ending they might shape. The grammar tells us they are reading a story whose ending was *already written*. The stones they are hiding behind are part of "whatever is on the earth" — and those stones had *already glorified* the One the people inside were defying.

**The perfect tense is the verse quietly telling you: the contest you think is still open was decided long ago.**

Now — one honest note, the kind of note this work requires. That two tenses appear across these surahs is a documented fact of the text; you can open the muṣḥaf and see *sabbaḥa* here and *yusabbiḥu* in As-Saff. What that difference *means* is where the scholars reflect rather than simply report, and I have offered you one reading — the reading the surah's own subject matter invites. Hold it as illumination, not as the only possible camera angle.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word *Mā* — "Whatever," Not "Whoever"

Here is the second thing, and it is the kind of thing you would walk straight past in English.

The verse says, "*whatever* is in the heavens and *whatever* is on the earth." In Arabic:

> **مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ**

The word is **مَا** — *mā*, "whatever," "what." It is *not* **مَن** — *man*, "whoever," "who."

Let me give you the concept first. In Arabic, when you are speaking about beings with intellect and will — people, angels, the rational — the natural word is *man*, "who." When you are speaking about things without intellect — objects, animals, the inanimate, the broad mass of creation — the natural word is *mā*, "what." *Man* points at minds. *Mā* points at *everything*.

So which one does Allah reach for here, in a verse declaring that creation glorifies Him?

He reaches for **mā** — *whatever*. The word that sweeps up not just the angels, not just the conscious, but the *things*. The dust. The water. The mountains. The trees. **And the stones of a fortress.**

Al-Jalālayn notes something precise here: the use of *mā* — the word weighted toward the non-rational — is *taghlīb lil-akthar*, a giving-of-precedence to the majority. Because the overwhelming majority of what fills the heavens and the earth is *not* the conscious mind. It is the silent, the inanimate, the uncounted mass of created things. By choosing *mā*, Allah folds *all of it* into the act of glorification.

Now feel where this lands in the surah of Banu Nadir.

A people barricade themselves behind walls of stone. They place their trust, their security, their entire hope of survival in *things*. In matter. In rock and timber and height.

And the opening verse has already told them: **the very stones you are hiding behind are glorifying the One you are hiding from.**

[PAUSE]

Every brick in that fortress is, in its own mode that we cannot hear, declaring *subḥān Allah* — declaring Allah free of every imperfection, free of every rival, sovereign over all. The wall is not neutral. The wall is not on your side. The wall is *His*, and it knows it, and it is glorifying Him even as you lean on it for protection against Him.

There is no inert matter in this universe. There is nothing you can put between yourself and Allah that is not, itself, already in submission to Allah. The word *mā* closes every exit. It tells you: there is nowhere to hide, because the hiding place is a worshipper too.

This connects to a verse you may know — *"and there is not a thing except that it glorifies His praise, but you do not understand their glorification"* (17:44). The same truth. But here, in Al-Hashr, it is not abstract. It is aimed. It is aimed at people who trusted in *things* — and at us, who do the same.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Extra Letter — The *Lām* in *Lillāh*

This is the most delicate of the three, and the most tender.

The verse does not say *sabbaḥa-llāha* — "glorified Allah," with Allah as the direct object. It says *sabbaḥa **li**llāh* — with a small letter inserted: the **lām**, the "to" or "for."

> **سَبَّحَ لِلَّهِ**

Al-Jalālayn observes that grammatically this *lām* is *mazīda* — "added," extra. The verb *sabbaḥa* is fully capable of taking its object directly. Allah did not grammatically *need* this letter. And yet it is there.

Now — in the Qur'an, nothing is filler. When a structure includes a word the grammar did not strictly require, the meaning has not been padded; it has been *bent* in a particular direction. So what does this added *lām* do?

The bare construction — "glorified Allah" — emphasizes the *act* falling upon the object: the glorifying lands on Him. But *sabbaḥa lillāh* — "glorified *to* / *for* Allah" — carries the flavor of *dedication*, of offering, of the glorification being turned *toward* Him as its purpose and direction. It is the difference between *praising someone* and *offering your praise up to someone*. The little *lām* turns the act into a gift directed at a recipient. It is devotional rather than merely descriptive.

Let me put it in something you can feel. There is a difference between saying "I thanked her" and "I gave my thanks *to* her." The second has a posture in it — a turning, a handing-over, a directedness of the heart. The *lām* puts that posture into the cosmos. All of creation is not merely *describing* Allah's perfection from a distance; it is *turning toward Him and offering it up*.

[PAUSE]

And see how this completes the picture of the previous two elements. The tense told us the glorification is *finished and certain*. The word *mā* told us it is *total — even the stones*. And now the *lām* tells us it is *directed* — it is not noise, not ambient, not accidental. Every particle of creation is *oriented*. It has a face turned toward its Lord, and it is pouring its glorification *toward* Him.

So picture the fortress one more time. The people inside face each other, face the gate, face the enemy. The stones of their walls face *Allah*. The whole of creation has its face turned in a direction the besieged cannot see — and it is the very direction they are refusing to turn.

---

### Bridge

So three small features — a past-tense verb, a two-letter word, and one extra letter — have together told us something the English translation cannot carry.

The glorification of all things to Allah is **finished** (*sabbaḥa*), it is **total, down to the inanimate** (*mā*), and it is **directed, offered up, devotional** (*lillāh*). A universe that is already, completely, and intentionally surrendered.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the secret architecture of every human life that builds walls.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Fortress You Are Hiding Behind Belongs to Him

Let me begin not in Madinah, but in your life.

Everyone has a fortress. It is rarely made of stone. For one person it is a bank balance — a number that, when it climbs high enough, finally makes the breathing slow down. For another it is a title, a position, a reputation; a wall of *who I am to other people*. For another it is a relationship, a person who feels like cover from the open sky. For another it is a plan — a meticulously arranged future, every contingency mapped, so that nothing can surprise them.

We build these. We climb inside them. And we tell ourselves the same sentence Banu Nadir told themselves: *these walls will hold.*

Here is what we never notice. The thing we trust for security is itself a *created thing* — and therefore it is already in the hand of the One we are unconsciously trying to be safe *from*. Not safe from in hatred — safe from in the deepest sense: safe from ever having to fully, nakedly depend on Him. The fortress is a way of not needing God *quite* so much. The savings account is a hedge against having to say *yā Allah* with my whole chest.

And the opening of Al-Hashr walks straight into that hiding place and says: *whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah.* Your fortress is His worshipper. Your savings are His. Your reputation is His. Your carefully built future is His, and it is — in its own way — glorifying Him right now, oriented toward Him, while you orient yourself toward *it*.

[PAUSE]

Banu Nadir's tragedy, the Qur'an will tell us two verses later, is captured in an unforgettable line: they thought their fortresses would defend them from Allah — *and Allah came at them from where they did not expect*, and *they destroyed their own houses with their own hands*. The walls did not just fail. The walls became the instrument of the undoing.

This is the terrible logic of every fortress made of created things: **the more total your trust in it, the more total your exposure when it moves.** Because it was never yours. It was always His worshipper, on loan to you, glorifying Him the entire time you mistook it for your security.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It does not mean tear down the walls. You are allowed savings. You are allowed plans. You are allowed people you love. **It means do not mistake the wall for the protection.** The protection was never *in* the stone; it was always in the One the stone glorifies. The practical shift is small and enormous: when you feel the calm that your fortress gives you — the bank balance, the title, the plan — let that calm *travel through* the created thing and land on its Owner. Say, even silently: *this is Yours, and I am trusting You through it, not it instead of You.* Then, if He moves it, you have not lost your security. Your security was never standing on the wall.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Might That Knows What It Is Doing — *Al-ʿAzīz Al-Ḥakīm*

The verse ends with two of Allah's names, and they are placed together for a reason.

> **وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ** — "and He is the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."

Let me start where you live.

You have known power. And in your experience, power and wisdom almost never come in the same package. The powerful are so often *not* wise — the strong man who crushes because he can, the empire that flattens what it does not understand, the boss whose authority is real and whose judgment is reckless. Power, in human hands, tends to *outrun* wisdom. It acts before it understands. It is mighty and *blind*.

And the wise, in our experience, are so often *not* powerful — the gentle, perceptive person who sees exactly what should be done and has no means to do it. The counselor with no authority. The good idea with no funding. Wisdom, in human hands, tends to be *helpless*. It understands and *cannot act*.

We have organized half of human suffering around this split: **power without wisdom is tyranny; wisdom without power is futility.** We rarely meet the two married.

Now hear the verse. *Al-ʿAzīz* — al-muyassar glosses it beautifully: *the One who is never overcome*, never defeated, never resisted. Absolute, irresistible might. And immediately, in the same breath, *al-Ḥakīm* — al-muyassar again: the One who *puts every matter in its proper place*, whose wisdom orders His decree, His planning, His making, His law. Might that *never errs*. Power that is *perfectly informed*.

[PAUSE]

Feel why these two names close *this* surah's opening.

A people has just been uprooted. Their homes emptied, their tribe exiled, their fortresses thrown down. To the human eye, this is *power* — the raw exercise of force, one party stronger than another. And the danger, the lurking suspicion in the heart, is that maybe it is *only* power. Maybe might just wins, and wisdom has nothing to do with it. Maybe the universe is run by *al-ʿAzīz* alone — strength with no justice behind it.

So Allah will not let the name *al-ʿAzīz* stand alone. He weds it instantly to *al-Ḥakīm*. The might that emptied those fortresses was **not** arbitrary, not random, not mere force. Every part of it was *placed* — wise, measured, purposed, exactly right. The treaty-breakers were not crushed by blind strength; they were *answered* by a might that knew precisely what it was doing and why.

This is the deepest comfort and the deepest warning in the verse, held in two words.

The **comfort**: when your own life is uprooted — when a fortress you trusted is thrown down and it *feels* like raw, meaningless force has come at you from where you did not expect — these two names are Allah telling you that the might that moved your life is *never* divorced from wisdom. There is no event in your life that is power without purpose. *Al-ʿAzīz* never acts without *al-Ḥakīm* in the same breath. What looks like the universe simply being stronger than you is, in truth, the universe being *wiser* than you can yet see.

The **warning**: do not, like Banu Nadir, mistake His patience for His absence, or your fortress for His inability to reach you. He is *al-ʿAzīz* — He is never overcome, and no wall has ever held against Him. And He is *al-Ḥakīm* — when He moves, it will be at exactly the right moment, from exactly the direction you did not guard.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? When something is taken from you and the heart screams *this is unfair, this is just force, this is the strong taking from the weak* — let the second name catch the first before it falls. Train yourself to say both: *He is al-ʿAzīz* — yes, this had power behind it I could not resist — *and He is al-Ḥakīm* — and therefore it had wisdom behind it I cannot yet read. You do not have to *see* the wisdom to *trust* that the might was married to it. That marriage is the verse. Hold both names, and the same event that would have made you bitter becomes the place you learn to trust.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found, and watch the two halves of the verse become one truth approached from two directions.

The **first half** — *whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah* — dismantled every fortress. It told us, through the finished tense, the all-sweeping *mā*, and the dedicating *lām*, that all of creation is *already, totally, and devotionally* surrendered to Allah. Including the stones we hide behind. There is no created thing that is not His worshipper. There is nowhere to hide that is not, itself, in *sujūd*.

The **second half** — *and He is al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm* — told us *who* it is that all this creation is surrendered to: a Might that is never overcome, married to a Wisdom that never errs. So the One you cannot hide from is also the One you would never *want* to hide from, because His power is never blind and His wisdom is never helpless.

Put them together and the architecture is complete. **The first half tells you that you cannot escape Him; the second half tells you that you would be a fool to want to.** The universe that is closing every exit on you is closed by *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — the strongest and the wisest. Your inescapability and your safety are the *same fact*.

That is why a surah about a fortress opens with the cosmos. It had to relocate your sense of security *before* it told you the story of walls falling. It moves you, in eleven words, off the wall and onto the One the wall glorifies.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is my fortress — the created thing whose presence makes me feel I can finally breathe? And have I quietly mistaken *it* for my safety, instead of the One it belongs to?
2. The stones of Banu Nadir's walls were glorifying Allah even as they leaned on them. What am I leaning on right now that is, itself, in submission to Him?
3. When something was taken from me and it felt like raw, unfair force — did I ever let the name *al-Ḥakīm* catch the name *al-ʿAzīz*? Did I trust there was wisdom in it, even unseen?
4. If everything is *already* (*sabbaḥa*) glorifying Allah, what would change in me if I stopped living in the open, anxious future tense — and joined what is already, finally, decided?
5. Is there a place I am turning my face — chasing, defending, trusting — while creation around me turns its face toward Him?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Everything you would hide behind is already glorifying the One you are hiding from — and He is too mighty to be escaped and too wise to be feared.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to tear down your walls. You need only to see *through* them. The next time the calm of your fortress settles over you — the number in the account, the title, the plan, the person — let that calm not stop at the created thing. Let it pass through, the way the *lām* passes the glorification *through* creation and up to Allah, and let it land where it was always meant to land. Trust *Him* through the gift, not the gift instead of Him. Then nothing He gives can become an idol, and nothing He takes can become your ruin.

### A Brief Duʿā'

*O Allah, You are al-ʿAzīz — nothing has ever held against You; and You are al-Ḥakīm — nothing You do is ever without perfect wisdom. Let me not hide behind what is already in submission to You. Turn my face the way all creation turns its face — toward You — and let my security rest in You alone, never in the walls I build. Āmīn.*
