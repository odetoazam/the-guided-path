# The Fear That Points the Wrong Way

## When Walls Replace the Heart

---

### The Words Before Us

> لَأَنتُمْ أَشَدُّ رَهْبَةً فِى صُدُورِهِم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَّا يَفْقَهُونَ
>
> لَا يُقَـٰتِلُونَكُمْ جَمِيعًا إِلَّا فِى قُرًۭى مُّحَصَّنَةٍ أَوْ مِن وَرَآءِ جُدُرٍۭ ۚ بَأْسُهُم بَيْنَهُمْ شَدِيدٌۭ ۚ تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًۭا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌۭ لَّا يَعْقِلُونَ

*La-antum ashaddu rahbatan fī ṣudūrihim mina-llāh. Dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yafqahūn.*

*Lā yuqātilūnakum jamīʿan illā fī quran muḥaṣṣanatin aw min warāʾi judur. Baʾsuhum baynahum shadīd. Taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā. Dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā yaʿqilūn.*

*"You are a greater object of dread in their breasts than Allah. That is because they are a people who do not deeply understand. They will not fight you all together except from within fortified towns or from behind walls. Their violence among themselves is severe. You suppose them united, but their hearts are scattered. That is because they are a people who do not reason."*

---

### Rebuilding the World

Let's go back to Medina, to a year of broken trust.

A tribe that had lived in the city for generations — Banū al-Naḍīr — had entered into a covenant with the Prophet ﷺ. And then, quietly, that covenant began to rot from the inside. There were plots. There was an attempt on the Prophet's life. And there were whispered promises arriving from another quarter of the city — from the *munāfiqūn*, the hypocrites, men who wore the face of faith in the daylight and conspired in the dark. They sent word to Banū al-Naḍīr: *Stand firm. We are with you. If you are driven out, we go out with you. If you are fought, we fight at your side.*

It was a promise made by men who had no intention of keeping it.

So when the moment came, what did Banū al-Naḍīr do? They did not march out to meet the believers in the open. They withdrew. They climbed up into their fortresses, pulled the gates shut, and waited behind high stone walls — waiting for an army of allies that would never arrive. The hypocrites stayed home. The promise dissolved like mist.

Now — here is what I want you to feel before we read a single word of these verses. Picture that scene from the outside. A walled fortress. Armed men on the ramparts. Stone, height, defense, the appearance of strength. To any observer standing below, this looks like power. This looks like a united front. This looks like people who know exactly what they are doing.

And then Allah does something that no observer on the ground could ever do.

He opens the wall. [PAUSE]

He shows us not the fortress, but the *chests* of the men inside it. He shows us what is happening in the cavity behind the ribs. And what is inside does not match what is outside at all.

That gap — between the strength you can see and the condition Allah can see — that is the entire subject of these two verses.

---

### The Hook

Here is the thing that should stop you.

These men had walls. They had weapons. They had height and stone and the advantage of defense. By every visible measure, they had reason to feel secure.

And Allah tells the believers: *they are more afraid of you than they are of Me.*

Not "they fear you too." Not "they fear you somewhat." **More.** More than Allah.

How does a person end up fearing the created thing in front of them more than the Creator who holds all of it in His hand? How does a heart get so turned around that the smaller fear swallows the infinite one?

That is not a question about Banū al-Naḍīr. [PAUSE]

That is a question about every one of us. And the answer is buried in two small words at the end of these verses — two different words for "they don't understand" — that most translations flatten into the same English. Let's earn our way to them.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### Element One: The Dread That Pointed the Wrong Way

Let me teach you something about fear before we touch the verse.

In English we have basically one word — *fear* — and we stretch it to cover everything from "I fear God" to "I fear spiders." But Arabic refuses to be that lazy. Arabic has a whole family of fear-words, and each one has a different texture. There's *khawf* — the general apprehension of something bad coming. There's *khashya* — a fear laced with knowledge and reverence, the fear of someone who *knows* what they're standing before. And then there's the word Allah chose here.

**رَهْبَة** — *rahba*.

Sit with the sound of it for a moment. *Rah-ba.* It opens with a breathy, almost trembling *r-h*, and it carries in its root the sense of fear that makes you *recoil* — fear that makes you pull back, withdraw, flee inward. From this very root comes the word *rāhib*, a monk — one who withdraws from the world out of awe and dread. *Rahba* is not calm reverence. It is the dread that makes you shrink and run.

Now hear the verse:

> لَأَنتُمْ أَشَدُّ رَهْبَةً فِى صُدُورِهِم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ
>
> *"You are a greater object of dread in their breasts than Allah."*

Let's open this slowly, because the grammar is doing something exquisite.

First, notice the very first word: **لَأَنتُمْ** — *la-antum*. That little *la-* at the front is an oath-particle of emphasis. Allah is not casually observing this. He is swearing to it. *By all means — it is YOU.* And He fronts the word *antum*, "you," throwing it to the very front of the sentence for stress. **You** — you believers, you small band — *you* are the object of their dread.

Then: **أَشَدُّ** — *ashaddu* — "more intense, more severe." This is the comparative form from the root *sh-d-d*, the root of intensity, of something tightened and hardened. Hold onto that root. It is going to come back in the very next verse, and when it does, it will tell us something devastating.

And then the placement of the dread: **فِى صُدُورِهِم** — *fī ṣudūrihim* — "in their breasts."

Here's what's remarkable. Allah does not say "you are more feared *by them*." He says the fear is **in their chests**. He locates it. He gives it an address inside the body. Why?

Because He is about to perform a kind of spiritual surgery. He is opening the chest cavity and showing us where this fear actually lives — and showing us *what it has displaced.* In a chest that was made to hold the fear of Allah above all else, another fear has moved in and taken the larger room. The fear of a created people has grown *ashaddu* — more intense — than the fear of the One who created them.

Stay with this. The tragedy is not that they were afraid. Fear is natural. The tragedy is the **ratio**. The infinite has been ranked beneath the finite. The One who can do everything has become, in the geography of their chest, *less* frightening than the ones who can do almost nothing.

The classical commentators are careful here. Al-Baghawī glosses it plainly: *they dread you more intensely than they dread Allah.* Al-Jalālayn adds a piercing detail about *why* — because Allah delays His punishment, while the enemy in front of them feels immediate. **And there is the whole mechanism.** They feared what was near and visible over what was greater but unseen. The sword you can see today frightens you more than the Lord whose reckoning feels far away.

Isn't that exactly how the human heart miscalculates? We discount the infinite because it is patient, and we inflate the immediate because it is loud. [PAUSE]

What this one word *rahba* reveals — fear that makes you recoil and withdraw — is that their entire posture, climbing into fortresses and hiding behind stone, was *rahba* made visible. The architecture of the fortress was just the outer shell of the fear in the chest. They built walls because they were already walling off inside.

### Element Two: "You Think Them United — But Their Hearts Are Scattered"

Now the second verse opens, and Allah moves the camera.

He has shown us the fear inside the chest. Now He shows us what that fear does to a group of people who share it.

> لَا يُقَـٰتِلُونَكُمْ جَمِيعًا إِلَّا فِى قُرًۭى مُّحَصَّنَةٍ أَوْ مِن وَرَآءِ جُدُرٍۭ
>
> *"They will not fight you all together except from within fortified towns or from behind walls."*

Look at the conditions Allah lists. They will fight — but only **فِى قُرًۭى مُّحَصَّنَةٍ**, *fī quran muḥaṣṣanah*, "in fortified towns," or **مِن وَرَآءِ جُدُرٍ**, *min warāʾi judur*, "from behind walls."

That word **مُّحَصَّنَةٍ** — *muḥaṣṣanah*, "fortified" — comes from the root *ḥ-ṣ-n*, which gives us the *ḥiṣn*, the fortress. The doubled middle letter in its form intensifies it: not just walled, but *heavily fortified*, reinforced, made impregnable. And **جُدُر** — *judur* — is simply "walls," bare stone barriers.

Notice what Allah is doing. He is reducing their courage to its honest size. They have power, yes — but only borrowed power, the power of the *wall*, not the power of the *self*. Strip away the fortress and there is nothing to fight you with. Their boldness is entirely a function of how much stone stands between them and you. **Their bravery has an address, and the address is "behind something."** [PAUSE]

Then comes the line that turns the whole thing inside out:

> بَأْسُهُم بَيْنَهُمْ شَدِيدٌۭ
>
> *"Their violence among themselves is severe."*

There it is — **شَدِيدٌ**, *shadīd*, the very same root *sh-d-d* that described their *fear* in the previous verse. Remember? Their dread of you was *ashaddu*, "more intense." And now their *baʾs* — their might, their aggression, their fighting-spirit — is *shadīd*, "severe."

Do you see the cruel comedy of it? Their intensity exists. The fierceness is real. It's just **pointed in the wrong direction.** They have severe might — *baynahum*, "among themselves." Toward each other. The ferocity they cannot summon against the believers, they unleash on one another. The intensity that should have made them formidable is consumed in internal conflict. *Shadīd* against each other; trembling behind walls before you.

And now the verse delivers its masterstroke — one of the most psychologically precise sentences in the entire Qur'an:

> تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًۭا وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ
>
> *"You suppose them united, but their hearts are scattered."*

Let me teach you the two words at war in this sentence.

**جَمِيعًا** — *jamīʿan* — "all together, gathered, united." It's a smooth, full, gathering sound. It implies assembly, cohesion, one body.

**شَتَّىٰ** — *shattā* — "scattered, dispersed, diverse, in pieces." Listen to it. *Shat-tā.* It even *sounds* fractured — a sharp *sh*, a hard stop on the doubled *t*, a thing breaking apart in the mouth. The sound performs the meaning. This is a word for things flung in different directions, with no center holding them.

And Allah hangs the entire human error on a single verb: **تَحْسَبُهُمْ** — *taḥsabuhum* — "you *suppose* them, you *reckon* them, you *calculate* them to be." This is the verb of the estimate that turns out wrong. It is the verb of the surface reading. *You do the math from the outside and you conclude: united. One bloc. A solid front.*

And Allah says: your arithmetic is correct about the outside and blind to the inside.

**تَحْسَبُهُمْ جَمِيعًا** — on the outside, gathered.
**وَقُلُوبُهُمْ شَتَّىٰ** — but the hearts, scattered. [PAUSE — let this sit]

Here is something I don't want you to miss. In the first verse, the fear was located in the **ṣudūr** — the *breasts*, the chest, the outer chamber. Now, the disunity is located in the **qulūb** — the *hearts*, the innermost core. Allah moves us inward. First He showed us the fear sitting in the chest. Now He takes us all the way down to the heart — the true center — and shows us it is not one heart at all. It is fragments, *shattā*, each beating to a different rhythm, each afraid in its own corner.

This is why the wall mattered so much to them. People whose hearts are *shattā* cannot trust each other in the open field, where survival requires that the man beside you not run. So they need the wall to do what trust should have done. **The fortress is a substitute for the unity they don't have.** Stone holding together what the heart could not. [PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Two Endings — *Yafqahūn* and *Yaʿqilūn*

Now we arrive at the most beautiful detail of all, and it is the kind of thing you can only see when you slow all the way down.

Both verses end almost identically. Look:

Verse 13 ends: **ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَّا يَفْقَهُونَ** — "That is because they are a people who do not *yafqahūn*."

Verse 14 ends: **ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ قَوْمٌ لَّا يَعْقِلُونَ** — "That is because they are a people who do not *yaʿqilūn*."

Same structure. Same rhythm. Same "that is because they are a people who do not—"

And then **two different verbs for understanding.** [PAUSE]

A careless eye reads both as "they don't understand" and moves on. But Allah does not place two different words where one would do. So let us ask: what is the difference between *fiqh* and *ʿaql*?

**فقه** — *fiqh* — from the root *f-q-h* — is deep comprehension. It is to penetrate beneath the surface of a thing to grasp its inner reality, its *why*, its true nature. It's the same root that gives us *fiqh* as a science — the deep, penetrating understanding of the law beneath the rulings. *Fiqh* goes *under*.

**عقل** — *ʿaql* — from the root *ʿ-q-l* — is reason, the faculty that binds and restrains. Its root literally means to tie up, the way you'd tie a camel's leg so it doesn't wander. *ʿAql* is the basic rational faculty that connects cause to effect, that draws the obvious conclusion, that looks at the evidence in front of it and reasons correctly.

Now — watch *where* Allah places each one. This is the masterpiece.

Verse 13 was about their relationship to **Allah** — fearing the believers more than the Lord of the worlds. And what do they lack? **فقه** — the *deep* understanding. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both gloss it precisely: *they do not deeply grasp the measure of Allah's greatness and power* — *qadr ʿaẓamat Allāh*. Of course it takes *fiqh*, the deep faculty, to rightly fear the Unseen. To fear Allah more than the sword in front of you, you must *penetrate* past the visible world to the reality of the One behind it. That is the deeper sight. They didn't have it. So they feared what their eyes could see.

Verse 14 was about something completely different. It was about their **own condition** — their hidden disunity, their scattered hearts, the fact that their bravery was borrowed from walls. And what do they lack here? **عقل** — basic reasoning. Because you don't need deep mystical insight to figure out your own house is divided. You just need to *think*. The fragmentation was an observable fact about themselves, sitting right in front of them, and they couldn't even reason their way to *that*.

Do you see the precision? [PAUSE]

**The unseen reality of Allah's greatness requires *fiqh* — deep penetration — and they lacked it.**
**The visible reality of their own division requires only *ʿaql* — plain reasoning — and they lacked even that.**

So the verses indict them on two levels. They failed at the hard thing — grasping the majesty of the Unseen. *And* they failed at the easy thing — noticing the obvious about themselves. A people who can neither see deep nor think straight. The fear in verse 13 grew from a failure of *fiqh*; the fracture in verse 14 grew from a failure of *ʿaql*. Two diseases, two precise diagnoses, two perfectly chosen words.

And here is the quiet warning folded inside it: these are not separate failures. They are the *same* failure seen at two depths. When you stop fearing Allah as He deserves — a failure of *fiqh* — your inner world stops cohering — a failure of *ʿaql*. Lose your grip on the One, and you lose your grip on yourself. The scattered heart and the misplaced fear are root and branch of a single tree.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these three constructions have shown us. A word for fear that *recoils* — and a fear that grew larger toward the creation than toward the Creator. A pair of words, *jamīʿan* and *shattā*, exposing a unity that was real on the outside and shattered on the inside. And two final verbs, *fiqh* and *ʿaql*, diagnosing a heart that could neither see the Unseen nor reason about the seen.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not about Banū al-Naḍīr, not about a fortress that fell fourteen centuries ago — but about reality itself. About *your* chest, and *your* heart, and the walls you may be building right now.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Tyranny of the Nearer Fear

Let me start where you actually live.

You know this feeling. There is a thing you know is right — a prayer you should pray, a word you should say, a wrong you should refuse to go along with. And you know, *theologically*, that Allah is watching, that Allah's pleasure is the only thing that ultimately weighs anything. You *know* this. You could pass a quiz on it.

And then a person walks into the room. A boss. A friend group. A stranger whose opinion you've decided matters. And suddenly the fear of what *they* will think floods your chest and drowns out everything you knew. You stay silent. You go along. You shrink.

In that moment, *la-antum ashaddu rahbatan fī ṣudūrihim mina-llāh* — "you are a greater object of dread in their breasts than Allah" — that verse is no longer about ancient enemies. It is about you. The created thing in front of you frightened you more than the Creator above you.

Why does this happen to us? The verse already told us, through al-Jalālayn's insight: because **the immediate feels more real than the infinite.** The disapproving face is *here*. The reckoning feels *far*. The human nervous system was built to respond to the lion in the grass, not the consequence in the afterlife. So our raw, untrained fear flows toward whatever is nearest and loudest — and the nearest thing is almost never Allah, because Allah does not shout. He is *al-Ḥalīm*, the Forbearing. He delays. And we mistake His patience for distance, and His silence for absence, and we calibrate our fear to the wrong scale.

This is the deepest tyranny most of us live under and never name: **the tyranny of the nearer fear.** We are governed, hour by hour, by the smaller of two powers, simply because the smaller one is closer to our face.

And now hear what the verse implies as the cure. The problem was a failure of *fiqh* — deep perception. The fear was misplaced not because they were cowards but because they could not *see* who Allah is. So the cure is not "be braver." Bravery doesn't fix a vision problem. The cure is *fiqh* — to deepen your grasp of Allah's greatness until He becomes, in the geography of your chest, the largest thing there. Not because you forced fear of people *down*, but because you let the knowledge of Allah grow so *large* that every other fear shrank to its true, tiny size beside it.

This is the secret the believers in this very story lived out. A small band, outnumbered, facing fortified tribes and a city riddled with hypocrites — and they did not climb into walls. Why? Because the One they feared was bigger than everything they faced. When Allah is the largest thing in your chest, the lion in the grass becomes a kitten. Not because the lion shrank — because your eyes finally adjusted to scale.

So what changes tomorrow? Here is the practical thing. The next time you feel that flood of fear-of-people in your chest — that hot, shrinking dread of someone's judgment — pause and *name it*. Say to yourself: *this is the nearer fear, and it is lying to me about how big it is.* And then deliberately call to mind one attribute of Allah — His power, His seeing, His nearness to *you*. Just one. You are not trying to crush the small fear by force. You are inviting the large reality back into the room so the small fear can return to its actual size. That is *fiqh* in practice — and it is trainable, one moment of recalibration at a time. [PAUSE]

---

### Theme Two: The Fortress and the Fracture — On Unity That Isn't

Now the second theme, and this one is achingly modern.

*Taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa-qulūbuhum shattā.* "You suppose them united, but their hearts are scattered."

Let me bring this into your world.

We live in the age of the *visible group*. The group chat. The follower count. The community that looks, from the outside, perfectly cohesive — same opinions, same hashtags, same enemies, a united front presenting itself to the world. From below the wall, it looks like *jamīʿan*. Gathered. One.

And yet — has there ever been an age more *shattā*? More privately lonely behind its public unity? People who post about belonging and go home to an empty kind of isolation. Movements that share a slogan but not a single bond of real love. Families that look intact in the photograph and are *baʾsuhum baynahum shadīd* — their fiercest conflicts saved for each other, the people closest to them. The same intensity that should have built something, spent instead on tearing at one another behind closed doors.

The Qur'an diagnosed this fourteen centuries ago with surgical exactness. It told us that **outward gathering and inward scattering can coexist perfectly** — that you can have all the appearance of unity and none of its substance. And it told us *why* the appearance survives anyway: the **wall.** *Quran muḥaṣṣanah, min warāʾi judur.* When hearts are scattered, people need external structures to hold them together — because the internal bond is gone. Stone does the work that love used to do. The group chat does the work that brotherhood used to do. The shared enemy does the work that shared faith used to do.

Here is the haunting question this raises about our own lives. [PAUSE] What are *my* walls? Where am I relying on an external structure — a label, an institution, an online tribe, even a family name — to hold together a connection that has gone *shattā* on the inside? Because a wall can keep you standing in the same place as other people. It cannot make you *one* with them. The moment of real pressure — the open field, the test that demands the heart and not the appearance — is exactly when *shattā* hearts scatter and the borrowed strength evaporates. The hypocrites in this very story promised to march out, and when the field opened, they were nowhere. Their unity with Banū al-Naḍīr was *jamīʿan* in word and *shattā* in heart, and the truth came out the instant it cost something.

And remember the diagnosis Allah gave: *lā yaʿqilūn* — they don't even reason. They couldn't see their own fracture. That is the most dangerous part of inward scattering — **it hides from the people inside it.** From the outside *you* can see it: *taḥsabuhum jamīʿan* — even an outsider supposes them united. But the ones living it can't reason their way to the truth of their own condition, because the wall they built to fool you has fooled them too. The fortress that hides the fracture from the enemy hides it from the self.

So what does this change about how we live? Two things.

First, an honesty practice. Ask of your own communities and bonds — including the closest ones: *Is this jamīʿan all the way down, or only on the surface? Where am I letting a wall substitute for a heart?* Real unity, the kind the believers had, is not built from stone or slogans. It is built from hearts that genuinely fear and love the same One — which is why the surah's own believers were unbreakable. Their unity had no wall because it didn't need one. The bond was *internal*.

Second — and this is the constructive turn — if the disease is *shattā* hearts held by external walls, the cure is the opposite: build the *internal* bond and you will need fewer walls. Invest in the heart-connection, not the appearance of connection. One real act of reconciliation with a person you've been *shadīd* toward — one honest conversation, one swallowed pride, one genuine "I'm sorry" — does more for true unity than a thousand performances of it. Tear down one wall by repairing one heart. [PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

---

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at how these two verses are built, because the design is the proof.

Allah gave us two verses, and He ended each with the same frame — *dhālika bi-annahum qawmun lā...* — "that is because they are a people who do not..." Two matching pillars. And between them, He built a perfect descent **inward**:

- **Verse 13** locates the problem in the **ṣudūr**, the breasts — the *outer* chamber — and names it **fear**: a fear of the creation greater than the fear of the Creator. The diagnosis: a failure of **fiqh**, the deep perception of the Unseen.

- **Verse 14** descends to the **qulūb**, the hearts — the *innermost* core — and names it **fracture**: an apparent unity over a buried scattering. The diagnosis: a failure of **ʿaql**, the plain reasoning about the seen.

From chest to heart. From fear to fracture. From the *fiqh* that fails before the Unseen to the *ʿaql* that fails before the obvious. The structure itself walks us from the surface of the human being down to the core — and shows us that the rot at the surface and the rot at the core are one rot.

And the *sh-d-d* root stitches it all together: their fear of you is *ashaddu* — intense; their violence among themselves is *shadīd* — intense. The intensity was never the problem. The *direction* was. All that severe energy, pointed at walls and at each other, and never once where it belonged: in humble fear of the One who holds them all.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the lived themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

From the *words*, we learned that *rahba* misplaced, *jamīʿan* falsified, and *fiqh* and *ʿaql* both absent describe a single broken condition. From *life*, we learned that this is precisely how a heart breaks: it fears the nearer thing over the greater, and it builds walls to fake a unity it no longer feels.

And underneath both is one law of the inner life: **when the fear of Allah leaves its rightful throne in the chest, everything downstream falls apart.** The fear goes to the wrong object. The unity goes hollow. The strength goes sideways, spent against your own people. Lose your grip on the One, and you lose your grip on yourself.

The believers in this very surah are the silent counter-example standing in the frame the whole time. Outnumbered, walled-in by no fortress, holding no borrowed courage — and *unbreakable*, because the largest thing in their chests was Allah, and so their hearts were truly *jamīʿan*, with no wall required.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *nearer fear* that governs me most often — whose disapproval, whose face, whose judgment — and would I admit that, in that moment, it sits *ashaddu*, more intense in my chest, than the fear of Allah?

2. Where in my life am I relying on a *wall* — a label, a group, an appearance — to hold together a bond that has quietly gone *shattā* on the inside?

3. Is there someone toward whom my *baʾs* is *shadīd* — someone close to me who receives my sharpest edge, while the world sees only my calm front?

4. When I fail to fear Allah rightly, is it a failure of *fiqh* — that I have not let His greatness grow large enough in me to see Him truly?

5. What would change today if the largest reality in my chest were actually the largest reality?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the fear of Allah is dethroned from the chest, the heart fears the nearer thing over the greater and hides its own scattering behind walls — for a people who can neither perceive the Unseen nor reason about the seen.

---

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to become fearless. You are being invited to become *rightly* afraid — to let the One who deserves your awe grow so large in your chest that every smaller fear returns to its true, small size. And you are being invited to stop trusting walls — to look honestly at where you've let appearance stand in for the heart, and to repair one real bond instead of performing a hundred fake ones.

The fortress fell. The walls always do. What stands is the heart that fears Allah and is therefore truly joined to others who do. Build *that*, and you will never need a wall.

Grant us the* fiqh *to perceive Your greatness and the* ʿaql *to know our own hearts. And make our hearts truly* jamīʿan *— gathered upon love of You — so that we need no wall to hold us together.* Āmīn.
