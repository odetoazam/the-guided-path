# The Loud Oath and the Empty Tunnel

## Introduction: A Promise Whispered in the Dark

أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ نَافَقُوا۟
*Alam tara ilā alladhīna nāfaqū…*
**Have you not seen those who practice hypocrisy…**

Let me set the scene before we open a single word, because these two ayahs are not abstract. They are a recording. They are the transcript of a private conversation that the people having it believed no one else could hear.

Medina, the early years. The young Muslim community has a treaty with the Jewish tribes living in and around the city. One of those tribes, Banu Nadir, breaks faith — the sources describe a plot against the Prophet ﷺ himself. The community moves to expel them. And here is where our ayahs live. In the days before that expulsion, a group of men inside Medina — men who walked into the masjid, who prayed in the rows, who called themselves believers — sent word secretly to Banu Nadir.

The message was intoxicating. *Don't worry. Stand your ground. We are with you.* **If they drive you out, we walk out with you. If they fight you, we fight at your side. And we will never — never — let anyone talk us out of you.**

It was, on its face, the language of total loyalty. The kind of words you would weep to hear from a friend in your worst hour. Three promises, each one heavier than the last, each one sealed with the strongest oath-language the Arabic tongue possesses.

And then the moment came.

[PAUSE]

And not one of them moved.

Banu Nadir stood alone. The men who had sworn to die beside them were nowhere. The whisper in the dark turned out to be exactly that — air.

Now here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows. The Quran could have told this story in a single line: *the hypocrites promised help and broke their promise.* Done. We would have understood. But Allah does not do that. Instead, He preserves the promise word for word — every emphatic syllable of it — and then, in the very next breath, He dismantles it clause by clause, in the exact order it was given.

Why preserve the lie so carefully before exposing it?

What is Allah teaching us by quoting the empty oath in full?

Stay with that. Because by the end, I think you'll find that these two ayahs are not really about a tribe in the seventh century at all. They are about a thing that lives inside every one of us — and about the difference between the words we *say* and the words Heaven actually *records*.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Animal That Builds Two Doors

Let me start with the very word the Quran uses to name these people — because the word is a picture, and the picture is the whole diagnosis.

نَافَقُوا۟ — *nāfaqū* — "those who practiced hypocrisy."

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you where this word comes from, because Arabic words grow out of roots the way trees grow out of seeds, and the seed here is unforgettable.

The root is **ن-ف-ق** (n-f-q). And one of the things this root names in classical Arabic — documented in the old lexicons — is the burrow of a small desert animal, the jerboa. Picture a little creature that digs its home under the sand. But it does something clever. It does not dig a hole with one entrance. It digs a tunnel with **two** openings. There is the door it shows the world, the obvious one. And then there is a second exit, hidden, lightly covered over with a thin crust of earth, kept in reserve. When a predator comes scratching at the front door, the jerboa bursts out the back and is gone.

That hidden second exit. That is the *nafaq*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what the Quran has just done by choosing this word. It did not call these people "liars" — there is a word for that, and we will see Allah use it in a moment. It did not call them "cowards." It reached for the image of an animal that *builds escape into the architecture of its own home.* The munāfiq is not someone who happens to run away. The munāfiq is someone whose entire dwelling — whose entire self — is constructed around a concealed exit. The front door faces the believers: prayer, presence, warm words, loud oaths. The back door faces the other way, always open, always ready. The promise to Banu Nadir was made *through the front door, by a creature already crouched at the back.*

Now notice something about the grammar that deepens this. The form of the verb here, *nāfaqa*, is what Arabic grammarians call Form III — the pattern that characteristically carries the sense of an action **directed at, or engaged with, another party**; a back-and-forth, a facing-toward. It is the same pattern we will meet again in this very passage in the word *qūtiltum*, "you are fought" — combat, which by its nature is two-sided. The munāfiq, by the shape of the word, is not simply being false in a vacuum. He is *facing two ways at once.* One face toward the believers. One face toward their enemies. The word for hypocrisy in Arabic is built, at the level of its grammatical bones, out of two-sidedness.

Here's what's remarkable. We tend to think of hypocrisy as a moral failing — a flaw, a stain on an otherwise normal house. The Quran's word says something more structural and more frightening: it is an *architecture.* A way of building a self around an exit you never intend to close. And the test of such a self is not the words spoken at the front door. The test is what happens when the predator finally comes — when staying would cost something.

That is the diagnosis. Now watch the Quran put it on trial.

## Element Two: The Heaviest Oath Arabic Can Build — and Who Out-Swears It

Here is where the language becomes almost unbearable in its precision.

When the hypocrites make their promise, they do not say it casually. Listen to the machinery:

لَئِنْ أُخْرِجْتُمْ لَنَخْرُجَنَّ مَعَكُمْ
*la-in ukhrijtum la-nakhrujanna maʿakum*
**"If you are expelled, we will SURELY leave with you."**

Let me teach you the grammar, because once you see it you will hear it ringing.

Arabic has a way of swearing an oath without saying the words "I swear." It does it with two small additions that wrap around a verb like a vice.

First, the **lām al-qasam** — the "oath lām," a little *la-* prefix that signals: *what follows is sworn.* You hear it at the front of *la-nakhrujanna* and *la-nanṣurannakum.* It is the grammatical equivalent of raising your hand.

Second, the **nūn al-tawkīd** — the "nūn of emphasis," a heavy doubled *-nna* clamped onto the end of the verb. This is the sound of a fist hitting a table. *La-nakhruja-NNA.* *La-nanṣura-NNA-kum.*

When you put both around a verb — the oath-lām in front, the emphatic-nūn behind — you have produced **the single most emphatic affirmation the Arabic language is capable of constructing.** There is no stronger way to say a thing. It is "we will leave with you" turned up to a volume that cannot be exceeded. Roughly: *By God, we shall absolutely, without any doubt, leave alongside you.*

And they do it not once but twice — *la-nakhrujanna* (we shall surely leave) and *la-nanṣurannakum* (we shall surely help you) — and they fortify the space between with a third hammer-blow: وَلَا نُطِيعُ فِيكُمْ أَحَدًا أَبَدًا — *"and we will never obey anyone against you, ever."*

Look at that little phrase, because it is over-built on purpose. *Aḥadan* — "anyone," indefinite, sweeping in every possible person. *Abadan* — "ever," sweeping in all possible time. They didn't say "we won't listen to so-and-so." They sealed off *every* person across *all* of time. No one. Never. The promise is total in its persons and total in its duration. It leaves no crack.

[PAUSE]

Now. You have just heard human beings deploy the absolute maximum of linguistic certainty. Every gun loaded. Every emphasis fired. If words could bind a soul, these words would have welded these men to Banu Nadir forever.

And here is the moment the whole passage turns on. Watch what Allah does. He does not argue with them. He does not say "we shall see" or "they are probably exaggerating." He answers their maximal oath with an oath of His own, in the same emphatic register, and He wins it in five words:

وَٱللَّهُ يَشْهَدُ إِنَّهُمْ لَكَٰذِبُونَ
*wa-llāhu yashhadu innahum la-kādhibūn*
**"And Allah bears witness that they are liars."**

Feel the architecture of this. *Yashhadu* — He *testifies,* He bears witness. Not "Allah knows," though He does. *Allah testifies* — courtroom language, the language of sworn evidence given before judgment. And then His verdict comes wrapped in the same emphatic machinery they used: *inna* (truly) plus the lām of emphasis on *la-kādhibūn* — "they are most certainly liars." They built the strongest sentence a human can build to say *we are true.* Allah builds the strongest sentence to say *they are false.* And the two sentences stand side by side, and one of them is the speech of creatures with a hidden back door, and the other is the speech of the One from whom there is no exit at all.

Notice the word He chooses for them: *kādhibūn* — liars. Earlier He named them by their architecture, *nāfaqū.* Now He names them by their words, *kādhibūn.* The structure and the speech, both indicted, in two ayahs.

And here is what I want you to really feel. **The volume of an oath is not evidence of its truth.** Everything in us instinctively reads loudness as sincerity — the friend who swears the hardest must mean it the most. The Quran severs that wire completely. The loudest oath in the passage belongs to the emptiest hearts. The quietest line — Allah's five words — is the only true thing said. When a human heart is full, it often barely needs to speak. When it is hollow, it reaches for *abadan, aḥadan,* the lām, the nūn, piling emphasis on emphasis to manufacture a conviction it does not actually possess.

The over-swearing was never strength. It was the sound of the back door, rattling.

## Element Three: The Mirror — When Allah Demolishes a Sentence Clause by Clause

Now we arrive at the second ayah, and I need you to slow all the way down, because what Allah does here is one of the most precise things in the Quran, and if we rush we will walk straight past it.

The hypocrites made three promises, in order:
1. *If you are expelled, we will surely leave with you.*
2. *We will never obey anyone against you, ever.*
3. *If you are fought, we will surely help you.*

Watch how ayah 12 answers. Allah takes their oaths and lays His refutation directly on top of each one, in the **same order, with the same conditional words** — but with every emphatic weapon stripped away and replaced by bare, flat negation:

لَئِنْ أُخْرِجُوا۟ لَا يَخْرُجُونَ مَعَهُمْ
*la-in ukhrijū lā yakhrujūna maʿahum*
**"If they are expelled, they will not leave with them."**

They had sworn *la-nakhrujanna* — "we will SURELY leave," oath-lām and emphatic-nūn blazing. Allah answers *lā yakhrujūn* — "they will not leave." No oath. No emphasis. Just the plain present-tense fact. The grammatical contrast is the entire point: against their maximal affirmation He sets the calmest possible negation, and the calm one is the truth. He doesn't need to shout it down. He just states what will happen.

وَلَئِن قُوتِلُوا۟ لَا يَنصُرُونَهُمْ
*wa-la-in qūtilū lā yanṣurūnahum*
**"And if they are fought, they will not help them."**

They swore *la-nanṣurannakum* — "we will SURELY help you." Allah: *lā yanṣurūnahum* — "they will not help them." Promise number three, demolished in three words, the emphatic nūn of their oath answered by the simple *lā* of reality.

[PAUSE]

And now — *and now* — watch Allah do something He did not have to do. He has already refuted them. He could stop. The case is closed. But He adds a third clause that goes *past* refutation into something almost merciful in its thoroughness — He grants them, hypothetically, the very thing He just denied, and shows that even *that* would collapse:

وَلَئِن نَّصَرُوهُمْ لَيُوَلُّنَّ ٱلْأَدْبَٰرَ
*wa-la-in naṣarūhum la-yuwallunna l-adbār*
**"And even if they did help them, they would surely turn their backs."**

Here is the stunning move. Do you hear it? *La-yuwallunna* — Allah now uses **their own oath-machinery against them.** The oath-lām, the emphatic-nūn — the exact weapons they fired to swear loyalty — Allah picks up and fires to swear their *flight.* They said *la-nanṣuranna,* "we will SURELY help." Allah says *la-yuwallunna l-adbār,* "they will SURELY turn their backs." He out-emphasizes their emphasis. The one thing in this scenario that is genuinely certain — certain enough to swear by — is not their help. It is their running.

And look at the image He reaches for. *Yuwallūna l-adbār* — literally, *they turn the backs.* The *dubur* is the back, the rear. To "turn your back" in battle is the physical posture of the fugitive — the moment a fighter stops facing the enemy and shows his spine and runs. It is a *physical* picture. But it is pointing at something unseen, isn't it? The body turns its back because the *heart* never faced forward to begin with. The flight of the limbs in that final hour is just the visible shape of a conviction that was hollow from the first whisper. The back door of the *nafaq* — remember the jerboa — has finally been used. *Yuwallūna l-adbār* is what the second exit looks like from the outside.

And then the final clause, and it lands like a closing door:

ثُمَّ لَا يُنصَرُونَ
*thumma lā yunṣarūn*
**"Then they will not be helped."**

Read this one carefully, because it widens. Throughout, the verb *naṣara* — to help — has had clear subjects and objects: *we* will help *you,* they will not help *them.* But this last line goes **passive,** and drops the helper entirely. *Lā yunṣarūn* — "they will not be helped." Helped by *whom*? The verb leaves the helper unnamed — and in the Quran's grammar, when all earthly helpers have been stripped away and the verb falls silent about who is left, the One left is unmistakable. *Then they* — and the "they" has now swallowed both parties, the false friends and the ones who trusted them — *will receive no help from anywhere.* Not from each other. Not from Heaven. The whole tangled web of promised solidarity ends in a single passive verb with no one on the other side of it.

Three promises. Three refutations, in order, in the same grammar, stripped of emphasis. Then an escalation that out-swears the swearers. Then a closing line that goes silent about the only Helper who was ever real.

That is not a rebuttal. **That is a demolition performed with surgical care** — and we will see, at the very end, that its shape is itself a kind of proof.

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things. A *word* — *nāfaqū* — that diagnoses hypocrisy as an architecture with a hidden exit. An *oath* — *la-nakhrujanna* — that reveals how the emptiest hearts reach for the loudest emphasis, and how Allah's quiet testimony overturns it. And a *mirror* — ayah 12 — in which every emphatic promise is answered by bare fact, and the only thing worth swearing by turns out to be their flight.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about us. Because the hypocrites of Medina are long gone, and these two ayahs are still here, still being recited. They were preserved for a reason. Let's find the reason inside ourselves.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Louder the Promise, the Emptier the Hand

Let me begin not with the ayah but with something you already know intimately.

You have met the person who swears too hard. We all have. The acquaintance who says, "I would do *anything* for you, anything, you know that, right? Call me at 3 in the morning, I mean it, *anything*" — and then doesn't return your text when you actually need a ride. The colleague who promises, with his hand on his heart, that he'll have it done, *absolutely, one hundred percent, you have my word* — and the deadline passes in silence. There is a specific texture to these promises. They are *too* emphatic. They reach for absolutes — *anything, always, never, I swear* — and somehow the reaching itself makes you uneasy, though you can't say why.

Now you can say why. The Quran just told you.

The hypocrites of Medina spoke in *exactly* this register. *La-nakhrujanna. La-nanṣuranna. Aḥadan. Abadan.* Every word an absolute. And Allah's verdict on all of it was five words: *they are liars.* The Quran has handed you a diagnostic instrument, and it is this: **the volume of a commitment is often inversely related to its substance.** Not always — but often enough that the over-swearing should make you *listen harder, not relax.*

Why would this be? Why would the empty heart be the loud one? Let's sit with this, because the answer is about all of us, not just hypocrites.

When you are genuinely going to do something, the deed is already settled inside you. It has the quiet of a thing that is real. You don't need to convince yourself, so you don't need to convince the other person with a wall of emphasis. *"Yeah, I'll be there"* — and you are. But when something in you already knows you *won't* — when the back door is already open and your foot is already on the threshold of the second exit — a strange thing happens. The words have to do double work. They have to perform a conviction the heart does not feel. And performance is loud. The emphasis is not for the listener; it is the sound of a person trying, and failing, to convince *himself.* *Abadan* — "ever" — is what you say when, somewhere underneath, you already know you mean "until it costs me something."

This is why Allah preserved the oath in full before exposing it. He wanted us to *hear the loudness* so that we would learn to read it. He froze the over-emphatic promise in the Quran forever, like an insect in amber, and labeled it, so that every generation could study the sound of an empty oath and recognize it — in others, and, far more importantly, in ourselves.

Because here is where this stops being about the munāfiqūn and starts being about you and me. Turn the instrument around. When do *you* over-swear? Notice the prayers you make the loudest. Notice the *tawba* that comes with the most emphatic vows — *I will NEVER do this again, ya Allah, never, I swear, this is the last time, absolutely the last* — and notice how often the volume of the vow is matched only by the speed of its breaking. The loudness was not strength. It was the same hollow place the hypocrites spoke from, dressed in religious clothes. The heart that is genuinely turning often does so quietly. *"Ya Allah, help me. I'm weak. Don't let go of me."* No grand vow. Just a hand held out.

[PAUSE]

So here is the practical thing to carry into tomorrow, and it has two edges. **Outward:** when someone swears to you in absolutes, do not be moved by the volume — be patient and watch the deeds, because *Allah testifies* that words are cheap and the loudest are often cheapest. And **inward**, which is the harder edge: examine your own emphatic promises — to people and to Allah — and ask whether the loudness is conviction or compensation. Whether you are stating a truth that is already settled in you, or shouting at a back door you have not yet had the courage to close.

The cure is not to swear louder. The cure is to close the second exit — and then you will find you barely need to swear at all.

## Theme Two: An Alliance Is Only as Strong as the Truth It's Built On

Now let's widen the lens, because there is a second thing happening in these ayahs, and it is about why some bonds hold and others shatter at the exact moment of pressure.

Look again at the word the hypocrites use for the disbelievers of Banu Nadir: لِإِخْوَٰنِهِمُ — *li-ikhwānihim* — "to their **brothers.**"

Brothers. The Quran calls them brothers. The hypocrites prayed in the masjid with the believers, but Allah's word locates their true kinship elsewhere — with *alladhīna kafarū min ahli l-kitāb,* "those who disbelieved among the People of the Scripture." Why brothers? The classical commentators give the answer plainly: because the real bond between them was not blood and not treaty. **It was shared opposition to the believers.** Their brotherhood was built on a common *no* — on what they were all against. As one of the commentators puts it, He made the hypocrites their brothers *in disbelief,* because they were of the same kind.

And here is the law these two ayahs reveal, and it is a law that governs human relationships far beyond seventh-century Medina: **a bond built on a shared falsehood will fail at precisely the moment it is asked to carry weight.**

Think about what an alliance founded on a *negative* actually is. *We are together because we are both against them.* There is no positive substance holding it — no shared truth, no real love, no covenant before God. It is two people standing back to back in the dark, and the only thing keeping them together is the enemy in front of them. And the instant the cost of staying rises above the cost of leaving — the instant the predator scratches at the door — there is nothing in the bond itself to hold it. Each one bolts for his own second exit. *Lā yakhrujūna maʿahum.* They do not leave together, because they were never truly together. They were only *against the same thing,* and "against" is not a foundation. It is a temporary alignment.

This is why ayah 12 reads like a slow-motion collapse in stages, and I want you to feel the stages, because they map onto every false loyalty you have ever watched fall apart. First, *they won't even leave with them* — the cheapest form of solidarity, just walking out the same door, and even that fails. Then, *they won't help them* — the active form, standing and fighting, and that fails too. And then the Quran grants the impossible: *suppose they did help* — and even *then,* *la-yuwallunna l-adbār,* they turn and run. Allah walks the false alliance down every rung of the ladder and shows that there is no rung on which it holds. Not at the top, not in the middle, not even at the generous bottom of the hypothetical. A bond with no truth in it has no load it can bear, at any level.

[PAUSE]

Now contrast this — and the contrast is the whole spiritual point — with what the *believers* are built on. Just a few lines earlier in this same surah, Allah describes a different kind of bond: people who love those who came to them, who *prefer others over themselves even when poverty is their own portion.* That is a bond built on a positive — on *īmān,* on love for the sake of Allah, on a covenant that has the Real at the center of it. And the difference is everything. The false alliance fails *because* the enemy gets strong. The true bond holds *even when* the cost is highest — *even over their own hunger* — precisely because what holds it is not the enemy in front but the God above.

So ask yourself, gently: what are my own bonds built on?

We all have alliances of the *negative* kind, and they masquerade as friendship. The group chat held together by who you all dislike. The workplace solidarity that is really just shared resentment of management. The relationship whose main fuel is a common enemy — a person, a rival, an out-group. These feel intense. They feel loyal. They are not. And the test is the one ayah 12 administers: *what happens when staying costs something?* The bond built on shared grievance evaporates the moment the grievance is gone or the cost goes up. Each person turns his back. *Lā yunṣarūn.*

And here is the final, quiet turn of the knife — the last clause. *Thumma lā yunṣarūn.* "Then they will not be helped." When the false bond collapses, the parties don't just lose each other. They lose *help altogether*, because the only Help that ever truly holds — the *naṣr* that survives the worst hour — comes from the One they built their lives turned *away* from. They constructed an entire alliance around opposition to the believers and, by extension, to the believers' God. So when the structure falls, there is no one above it to catch them. The passive verb has no subject because, for them, there is no Helper left. *That* is the difference between *nasr* you swear to give each other and *nasr* that descends from Heaven: one of them runs out exactly when you need it, and the other is the only thing that never does.

So the practical application, the thing to do tomorrow, is an audit. Look at your three or four closest bonds and ask of each: *is this built on a shared love — of Allah, of good, of something true — or on a shared enemy?* Invest in the first. Be honest about the second. And build the one bond that can never turn its back: the bond with the only Helper whose help does not flee when the cost is high. Anchor a friendship in *that,* and you have built something *la-yuwallunna l-adbār* cannot describe — a loyalty that walks toward the cost instead of away from it.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture: A Refutation Built as a Proof

Now let me show you the shape of these two ayahs as a whole, because we have done the work, and now the architecture can become visible — and the architecture is itself the argument.

Ayah 11 is the *claim,* preserved in full, in maximal emphasis: three promises, plus a divine verdict.

Ayah 12 is the *refutation,* and it is built as a perfect mirror:

| The Oath (11) | The Reality (12) |
|---|---|
| "If you are expelled, we will **surely leave** with you" | "If they are expelled, they will **not leave** with them" |
| "We will **never obey** anyone against you, ever" | *(its substance answered by all that follows)* |
| "If you are fought, we will **surely help** you" | "If they are fought, they will **not help** them" |
| | *escalation:* "and even if they did help — they would **surely turn their backs**" |
| "And Allah **bears witness** they are liars" | *closing:* "then they will **not be helped**" |

Do you see it now? Allah did not refute their promise with a counter-argument. He refuted it with **its own structure, run in reverse.** He let them build their sentence at full volume, then walked back through it clause by clause and emptied each one — and then, where they had sealed their oath with their loudest emphasis, He took that same emphasis and turned it onto the truth of their flight. The form of ayah 12 *is* the proof. It says, in its very shape: *I heard every word you said, in the order you said it, and here is what each one was actually worth.*

This is why He preserved the lie in full before exposing it. A demolition this precise requires the original building to be left standing first. He quoted the empty oath completely so that we could watch it fall completely.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes are one insight seen from two directions.

The *word nāfaqū* — the burrow with two exits — and the *theme of the loud promise* are the same thing: the emphasis is loud precisely *because* the back door is open. A heart with no second exit does not need to shout.

And the *mirror of ayah 12* — every promise emptied, the alliance collapsing rung by rung — and the *theme of the false bond* are the same thing: a structure built on shared opposition has no load-bearing truth in it, so when Allah runs His finger down its clauses, each one gives way, until the final passive verb stands alone with no helper behind it.

One ayah is grammar. One is the human heart. They are the same ayah.

## Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. Where in my life do I *over*-swear — to people, to myself, to Allah — and is the loudness conviction, or is it the sound of a back door I haven't closed?

2. If I name my three closest bonds honestly: how many are built on a shared *love,* and how many on a shared *enemy*? Which would survive if staying suddenly cost me something?

3. When the predator scratches at my door — when faith or loyalty or honesty becomes expensive — which exit does my foot move toward?

4. Whose *naṣr* am I actually relying on? The help people swear to give me, or the Help that does not flee when the cost is highest?

5. If Allah were to *testify* — *yashhadu* — about the gap between my words and my deeds this week, what would the testimony say?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The loudest oath sworn from an empty heart is overturned by the quietest testimony of Allah — for loyalty built on shared falsehood collapses at the exact moment it is asked to bear weight, while the only help that never turns its back is the help that comes from the One we are tempted to build our lives turned away from.

## The Closing Invitation

Here is what might shift after this. Not a resolution to swear better — that is the munāfiq's own move, more emphasis over the same hollow. The shift is quieter and harder: to go and *find the second exit* in your own house. The promise you make too loudly. The bond you keep because of who it opposes. The vow to Allah you've broken so many times you now make it at maximum volume to drown out the memory. Find that back door. And instead of swearing louder at the front, walk to the back and close it.

A heart with no hidden exit does not need to shout its loyalty. It just stays — when it is expensive, when others have turned their backs, when staying is the whole test. *That* heart, Allah does not testify against. That heart, He helps. And His help is the one *naṣr* in all of existence that will never, *abadan,* turn its back on you.

## Du'a

O Allah, You who *testify* and are never deceived — close the hidden exits in our hearts before they betray us. Make our quiet truth louder in Your sight than our loud words, and let the love that binds us to others be a love built on You, so that when the cost is highest we walk toward it and not away. And when every other helper turns his back, be our *Nāṣir* — the Help that does not flee. *Āmīn.*
