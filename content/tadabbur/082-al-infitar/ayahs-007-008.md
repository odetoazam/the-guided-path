# The One Who Balanced You

## Introduction: The Question Before the Answer

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the world they fell into.

> **ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَكَ فَسَوَّىٰكَ فَعَدَلَكَ ۝ فِىٓ أَىِّ صُورَةٍ مَّا شَآءَ رَكَّبَكَ**
>
> *Alladhī khalaqaka fa-sawwāka fa-ʿadalak. Fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa rakkabak.*
>
> **"He who created you, then proportioned you, then balanced you — in whatever form He willed, He assembled you."**

Seven words. Three verbs in the first verse, each one beginning where the last one ended. *Created. Proportioned. Balanced.* And then a fourth verb closing the second verse: *assembled.* That is the whole passage. It is so short you could miss it on the way to something that looks more important.

But before we open a single one of these words, we have to stand where this verse stands. Because this verse is not a statement. **It is an answer.** And you cannot feel the weight of an answer until you have heard the question.

### Rebuild the world

Picture the surah from its first breath. *Idhā s-samāʾu nfaṭarat* — "When the sky is split open." The very name of the surah, **Al-Infitar**, means the tearing, the cleaving, the splitting apart. The ceiling of the world — the thing every human being since the first has looked up at and assumed was permanent, fixed, unbreakable — comes apart at the seams. The stars scatter. The seas are made to burst forth and pour into one another. The graves are overturned, the dead spilling upward out of the earth.

This is a surah about the dismantling of everything you assumed was solid.

And then, in the middle of that cosmic unraveling, the camera turns. It stops looking at the sky and the seas and the stars. It turns and it looks directly at **you**. And it asks one question — a question so personal, so quiet after all that thunder, that it lands like a hand on the shoulder:

> *Yā ayyuha l-insānu mā gharraka bi-rabbika l-karīm.*
>
> **"O human being — what deceived you concerning your Lord, the Generous?"** (82:6)

Sit in that question for a moment. *Mā gharraka* — what deluded you, what fooled you, what made you so confident, so casual, so forgetful? And notice the name Allah chooses for Himself at the end of the question. Not "your Lord, the Mighty." Not "your Lord, the Avenger." **"Your Lord, the *Generous* — al-Karīm."**

That is a strange word to end an accusation with. If someone were rebuking you, you would expect them to remind you of their power. Instead He reminds you of His *kindness*. As if to say: of all the lords you could have grown careless before, you grew careless before **the generous one?** The one whose entire posture toward you has been giving?

### The Hook

So the question hangs in the air. *What deceived you about your Generous Lord?* And here is what is stunning. The very next words — our two verses — are not a new subject. **They are the evidence for the prosecution.** They are Allah answering His own question by pointing at the one thing the heedless human can never put down, never leave at home, never escape:

His own body.

You want to know how generous your Lord is? *Look down. Look at your own hands.* The proof you have been ignoring is the very instrument you are using to ignore it.

Stay with me. Because in the next forty minutes, three small verbs are going to take us from the inside of a womb to the edge of the Day of Judgment — and they are going to make it very, very hard to ever again say the words "I am just an ordinary person" and mean them as something small.

# Part One: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Three Verbs That Climb a Staircase

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about how Arabic — and really how language itself — handles sequence.

In English, if I want to tell you about three things that happened one after another, I have to add connecting words: "He did this, **and then** he did that, **and after that** he did the third thing." The connectors are bulky. They sit between the actions like furniture.

Arabic has a single letter that does this work: the letter **fāʾ — فَ**. Attached to the front of a word, it means something like "and then, immediately following, as a direct next step." It is not a lazy "and." It is a staircase. Each *fa* is a step that could only come after the step below it. It carries **sequence** and it carries **consequence** — *this*, and so therefore *that*.

Now look at our verse, and watch the staircase:

> **خَلَقَكَ** — *khalaqaka* — He created you
> **فَ**سَوَّىٰكَ — *fa-sawwāka* — and then He proportioned you
> **فَ**عَدَلَكَ — *fa-ʿadalaka* — and then He balanced you

Three verbs. Two *fa*'s between them. Allah is not giving you a list. **He is giving you a sequence of craftsmanship.** He is letting you watch, in slow motion, the stages by which a human being is made.

Let's climb the staircase one step at a time.

### Step one: *Khalaqaka* — "He created you"

The root here is **خ-ل-ق (kh-l-q)**, and it is the great Quranic word for creation. But notice what it does *not* yet say. *Khalaqa* is the bringing-into-being. It is the raw fact of existence: you were not, and then you were. The classical commentators catch this immediately. Al-Jalālayn glosses it: *baʿda an lam takun* — "after you were nothing at all."

That is step one. Out of sheer nothingness — *being*. But notice: a thing can exist and still be formless. A lump of clay exists. Raw material exists. *Khalaqa* gets you *something*, but it does not yet get you *someone*. For that, you have to climb.

### Step two: *Fa-sawwāka* — "and then He proportioned you"

Here is the word I want us to slow down on, because the grammar itself is carrying meaning.

The root is **س-و-ي (s-w-y)**, which gives us the idea of *evenness, levelness, proportion, things being made equal and right*. But the verb here is not the simple form. Look at the doubled, stressed middle — *saw-wā*. In Arabic grammar this is **Form II**, and the corpus confirms it: this is *faʿʿala*, the intensive, deliberate, shaping form of the verb.

Let me teach you what Form II *does*, because this is the heart of it.

The simple form of a verb is a single clean action. Form II takes that action and makes it **intensive, repeated, applied with care over and over until it is complete**. If the simple verb *kasara* means "he broke," the Form II *kassara* means "he smashed to pieces" — broke and broke and broke. The doubled letter in the middle is, quite literally, the sound of an action being done thoroughly.

So *sawwā* is not "He made you even" as a single snap of the fingers. **It is "He proportioned you" as a patient, sustained, ongoing act of shaping** — adjusting, refining, bringing every part into right relation with every other part. Al-Qurṭubī unfolds it exactly this way: *sawwāka fī baṭni ummik* — "He proportioned you inside your mother's belly, and gave you two hands and two feet and two eyes and the rest of your limbs."

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the shift? Step one gave you existence. Step two is Allah, with the patience encoded right into the form of the verb, **shaping that existence into a body** — over weeks, over months, in the dark, cell upon cell, organ taking its place beside organ. The Form II is the womb. It is the nine months. It is the unhurried artistry of a Maker who does not rush His work.

And here is something to sit with. You were there for none of it. You contributed nothing. You did not know it was happening. The most intricate construction project in the known universe — a human being — was carried out *on* you and *for* you while you were less than a thought. That is what the verb *fa-sawwāka* is quietly holding.

### Step three: *Fa-ʿadalaka* — "and then He balanced you"

The final step, and the final *fa*. The root is **ع-د-ل (ʿ-d-l)** — and this is one of the most loaded roots in the entire Arabic language. It means **balance, justice, equilibrium, proportion, setting things in their right and equal place.** It is the same root from which Allah's justice — *al-ʿadl* — is named.

So what does it mean that He *ʿadalaka* — "balanced you"?

The commentators give us a beautiful, layered picture. Al-Jalālayn: *jaʿalaka muʿtadila l-khalqi mutanāsiba l-aʿḍāʾ* — "He made you balanced in form, your limbs in proportion to one another." And then he says something so concrete it almost makes you smile: *laysat yadun aw rijlun aṭwala min al-ukhrā* — **"so that one hand or one leg is not longer than the other."**

Stop and let the ordinariness of that land on you.

You have two arms that match. Two legs the same length. Two eyes set level in your face. Your features arranged in a symmetry so reliable that you have *never once thought about it* — and that thoughtlessness is the entire point of verse 82:6. *Mā gharraka* — what made you so casual? You woke up this morning balanced. You stood up without calculating it. The fact that you did not have to *think* about whether your legs were the same length — that seamless, silent symmetry — **that is *ʿadalak*. That is the verse, lived, before you even read it.**

### The composite: a hidden second reading

Now I have to show you something the classical scholars preserved, because it opens the verb even wider.

There were two recognized recitations — *qirāʾāt* — of this word. The majority read it **with the stress: *ʿaddalak* (with shadda)** — meaning exactly what we have described: *He balanced you, made your form symmetrical and even.* That is the reading of al-Jalālayn and the majority, and al-Qurṭubī notes the verse *"laqad khalaqnā l-insāna fī aḥsani taqwīm"* — "We created the human in the most beautiful proportion" (95:4) — stands behind it.

But the Kufan reciters — ʿĀṣim, Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī — read it **lightened: *ʿadalak* (no shadda)**, and al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record what this opens up: *amālaka wa-ṣarafaka ilā ayyi ṣūratin shāʾ* — **"He inclined you and turned you toward whatever form He willed"** — beautiful or plain, tall or short, this shape or that.

Now — and this is the methodology of the classical scholars — **do not pick one.** Hold both. They are two camera angles on a single act. One reading says: *He balanced you* — look at the symmetry He gave you. The other says: *He directed you into a form* — and notice that the form you received, out of every form He could have chosen, was a free gift of His will. You did not earn your shape. You did not request it. He *inclined* you toward it.

And that second reading is precisely what flows into verse eight: *fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa rakkabak* — **"in whatever form He willed, He assembled you."** The word *rakkaba* — again that doubled, deliberate Form-II shape — means He *composed* you, *fitted you together*, like a master assembling a thousand precise parts into one working whole.

[PAUSE]

So here is the staircase, complete:

**He brought you out of nothing. He shaped you, patiently, in the dark. He balanced you, limb against limb, until you were symmetrical and whole. And the form you walk around in — He chose it and composed it, freely, as a gift.**

Four verbs. And every single one of them was done *for* you, *to* you, *before* you could say thank you, ask permission, or even exist to object.

> *Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.*

# Part Two: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Generosity You Are Standing Inside Of

Let me start where you live, not where the verse is.

Think about the last time someone did something genuinely kind for you. Really kind. And think about how, in the moment, you felt it — the warmth, the gratitude, the urge to do something back. And then think about how, three weeks later, you had completely forgotten. The kindness became the new normal. It dissolved into the background of your life.

This is one of the most reliable facts about the human heart: **we stop seeing what is constant.** Gratitude is triggered by *change* — by the gift arriving. Once the gift stays, it stops registering as a gift and starts registering as a *given*. The new car becomes "my car." The job you prayed for becomes "my job, which is stressful, actually." The person you begged God for becomes the person you argue with.

Psychologists have a clinical name for this — *hedonic adaptation*. We adapt to our blessings until they go silent.

Now bring that back to verse 82:6. *Mā gharraka bi-rabbika l-karīm* — "What deceived you about your Generous Lord?" Do you see now what the deception *is*? It is not that the human being looked at God's generosity and rejected it. **It is that the generosity was so total, so constant, so woven into the fabric of his every breath, that it went invisible.** He adapted to it. He is *standing inside* the gift and asking, "What gift?"

And so Allah does the one thing that can break the spell. He does not point at the sky or the harvest or the rain — gifts that are at least *outside* you, where you might still notice them. **He points at the gift you can never get outside of.** Your own body. The verse forces the most intimate gift back into your field of vision: *He created you. He proportioned you. He balanced you. He assembled you into the form you are wearing right now, this second, as you read this.*

You cannot adapt your way out of your own spine. You cannot stop using the gift in order to ignore it — because the very act of ignoring requires the eyes He balanced, the brain He composed, the breath He is sustaining as you do it.

Here is the movement I want you to feel: **the verse is not informing you of a fact. It is staging an intervention.** It is taking the most overlooked object in your entire life — the body you live behind — and turning it back into what it always was: an unbroken, head-to-toe act of generosity from *al-Karīm*, the Generous One.

What does this change about tomorrow?

The next time you stand up from your bed — that ordinary, automatic motion — and your two legs, the same length, carry you across the room without a single thought — let that be your first *dhikr* of the day. Not a long one. Just: *He balanced me.* The verse is asking you to convert the most boring fact about yourself — that your body works — into the most reliable proof you own that you are *loved by a Generous Lord.* You do not need to go looking for evidence of His care. You are wearing it.

## Theme Two: The Body as Witness — Why the Gift Is Also a Summons

Now let me turn the verse, because there is a second face to it, and it is more sobering.

Begin with a feeling you know. The feeling of being *given* something — and the quiet weight that comes with it. When someone hands you their trust, their resources, their child to babysit, something shifts. The gift is also a *responsibility*. To receive is, somehow, to *owe*. We feel this instinctively. A gift with no strings still ties a thread to your conscience.

Now scale that up to its absolute maximum. What is the largest gift you have ever received? It is not your house or your wealth or even your family. **It is you.** It is the fashioning, the proportioning, the balancing, the assembling of the very self that receives everything else. Everything you have, you have *through* the body Allah composed. It is the gift that makes all other gifts receivable.

And here is the turn. Remember *where this verse sits.* It sits inside a surah about the sky splitting open and the graves overturning and the records being laid bare — *ʿalimat nafsun mā qaddamat wa-akhkharat*, "every soul will know what it sent ahead and what it left behind" (82:5). This is a surah about **the Day of accounting.**

So when Allah reminds you, in the middle of all that, *"He created you, proportioned you, balanced you"* — He is not only saying *look how generous I was.* He is also saying, with perfect justice, *and what did you do with what I gave you?*

[PAUSE]

Feel the two faces of the same verse pressing together. The hands He balanced so that "one is not longer than the other" — **what did those balanced hands reach for?** The eyes He set level in your face — **where did those level eyes look?** The form He assembled, freely, out of every form He could have willed — **whom did that form bow to?** The gift and the reckoning are the same gift. *al-Karīm* gave you a body that is, at one and the same time, His mercy and your witness. On that Day, Surah Fussilat tells us, your own skin will testify. The instrument of the gift becomes the instrument of the account.

This is why the verse is, finally, **a mercy and a warning braided into one rope.** It is a mercy because it tells the heedless human: *you are not random, you are not an accident, you were made on purpose by Someone generous.* And it is a warning because it tells him: *and precisely because you were made on purpose, you will be asked about the purpose.* The dignity and the responsibility are inseparable. To learn that you were lovingly, deliberately composed is to learn, in the same instant, that you were composed *for* something — and that you will be asked whether you lived for it.

What does this change about tomorrow?

Let the symmetry of your own body become a small, daily examination. Not a harsh one — a tender one. When you wash for prayer and water runs over hands and face and feet — over the very limbs the verse names — let it be the moment you ask: *these were given to me balanced and whole, on purpose, by the Generous One. Am I using them in a way I would be at peace to see written in my record?* You do not need to manufacture motivation from nowhere. **It is built into your wrists.** The summons is written into the structure of the gift.

# Closing Synthesis

## Bring It Together

Look at what the linguistics and the themes have done — because they are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The **three verbs** climbing their staircase — *created, proportioned, balanced* — were never just grammar. Each *fa* was a step deeper into the truth that **you are not a happening; you are a making.** Nothing about you was rushed, nothing was accidental, nothing was left to chance. The Form-II patience of *sawwā* and *rakkaba* — the doubled, deliberate, thorough shaping — is the linguistic proof of a love that took its time over you in the dark, where you would never see it and never thank Him for it.

And the **two themes** — the forgotten generosity, and the body as witness — are simply what that staircase *means* when a living human being stands on it. Because you were made with such deliberate care, you are surrounded — *clothed* — in a generosity you have stopped seeing. And because you were made with such deliberate care, you will be asked what you did with it.

The whole passage is *al-Karīm* answering His own question from verse six. *What deceived you about your Generous Lord?* And the answer is: nothing should have. The evidence was never far away. **It was never even outside of you.** You have been the proof the whole time.

## Questions to Carry

1. What is the most constant blessing in my life — the one so reliable I have stopped seeing it as a blessing at all? When did I last thank Allah for it specifically?
2. If my own body is the clearest evidence of *al-Karīm*'s generosity that I will ever hold, why is it so often the first thing I complain about and the last thing I thank Him for?
3. The hands He balanced "so that one is not longer than the other" — when I picture them on the Day they testify, what do I want them to say I reached for?
4. Where, exactly, has the generosity of my Lord become invisible to me precisely *because* it never stopped?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The body you have stopped noticing is the standing, head-to-toe answer to the question *"what deceived you about your Generous Lord?"* — a deliberate gift that is also a summons.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to leave this reflection with a grand resolution. Let it be small and physical. Tomorrow, when you first rise and your balanced limbs carry you without a thought — pause for the length of one breath. Notice the symmetry. Notice that it was *given*. And let that single breath of noticing be the beginning of un-forgetting. The verse is not asking you to find God somewhere far away. It is asking you to *stop overlooking Him in the nearest thing you own.*

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah — You who created me out of nothing, who proportioned me with a patience I will never fully understand, who balanced my every limb and assembled me, freely, into this form — let me never again be deceived about Your generosity while I am wrapped inside it. Open my eyes to the gift I am standing in, and let the body You composed be a witness on my behalf, not against me, on the Day the sky is split open.* Āmīn.
