# The Ache No Vehicle Can Reach

> **Surah Ghafir (40:79–81)**

*Allāhu-lladhī jaʿala lakumu-l-anʿāma li-tarkabū minhā wa minhā taʾkulūn. Wa lakum fīhā manāfiʿu wa li-tablughū ʿalayhā ḥājatan fī ṣudūrikum, wa ʿalayhā wa ʿala-l-fulki tuḥmalūn. Wa yurīkum āyātihi, fa-ayya āyāti-llāhi tunkirūn.*

## Introduction: The World These Words Arrived Into

Let's begin not with the words, but with the room they walked into.

By the time you reach the eightieth ayah of Surah Ghafir, you have been through an argument. Not a gentle one. This surah — the first of the seven *Ḥā-Mīm* surahs that sit together near the end of the Quran like a cluster of related letters from the same hand — opens with a warning that lands almost immediately: **مَا يُجَٰدِلُ فِىٓ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟** — "None dispute concerning the signs of Allah except those who disbelieve." That word — *āyāt*, signs — is the spine of the entire surah. Everything in Ghafir is a wrestling match over the *signs*. Who reads them. Who twists them. Who argues them away.

And the surah does not stay abstract. It gives you a face for the argument. It gives you Pharaoh — the man who looked at the most obvious signs in human history and said, in effect, *I see nothing but what I already believed.* And against Pharaoh it gives you one of the most quietly heroic figures in the whole Quran: **a believing man from the family of Pharaoh, who hid his faith,** and who stood up in that poisoned court and pleaded with his own people to think. To just *look*. To not argue away what was in front of them.

So by ayah 79, the theme has been set at a boil. The whole surah has been asking one question in a hundred forms: *what does it take for a human being to deny what is right in front of him?*

And then — watch what Allah does. After the pharaohs and the courts and the drowning and the fire, after all the cosmic disputation, Allah suddenly lowers His voice. He stops pointing at history. He stops pointing at empires. And He points at your dinner. He points at the animal you ride to work. He points at the boat.

Now feel this the way the first listeners felt it. These are people whose entire economy walked on four legs. The camel was not a quaint image to them — it was their car, their truck, their pantry, their tent-cloth, their milk, their savings account that could walk itself across a desert. A man's wealth was literally counted in *anʿām*, in livestock. When you told a seventh-century Arab "Allah made the *anʿām* for you," you were not offering him a nature documentary. You were naming the thing his whole survival stood on. You were putting your finger on the exact center of his sense of security.

So the argument that has been raging at the scale of Pharaoh and drowning armies now shrinks down to the size of the animal tied up outside his own house. And that is the trap being set — a merciful trap, but a trap. Because it is easy to argue about God when the subject is cosmic and far away. It is much harder when the subject is the meat on your plate and the beast that carries you home.

**Here is the hook. Hold it in your hand for the next hour.** In the middle of this list of ordinary gifts — riding, eating, benefits, ships — Allah slips in one phrase that has no business being in a list about transportation. He says these animals carry you *"to reach a need that is in your chests."* Not a need in your homes. Not a need at the market. A need **in your chests.** [PAUSE]

Why would He locate a need *there* — in the same place the Quran always puts the heart, the faith, the secrets, the grief? What does a camel have to do with your chest?

Sit with that. We are going to earn the answer slowly.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### First: The Word Order That Feeds You Before It Explains Itself

Before we touch the deep phrase, I want to show you something small, because the small thing teaches you how carefully these words are arranged — and once you see the care, you will trust the destination.

Look at the first ayah again:

<!-- ayah:40:79 -->
جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَنْعَٰمَ لِتَرْكَبُوا۟ مِنْهَا وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ

*"...that you may ride some of them, and some of them you eat."*

In Arabic, word order is not just grammar — it is emphasis. Whatever a speaker pushes to the front of a clause is the thing he wants your eye to land on first. It works the same way in English if you strain to hear it: *"Some of them, you eat"* hits differently than *"you eat some of them."* The first one makes you feel the *some of them.*

Now watch the two halves of this line. In the first half, the verb comes first: *li-tarkabū minhā* — "that you may **ride** — of them." Riding leads; the animals follow. But in the second half, it flips: *wa minhā taʾkulūn* — "**and of them** — you eat." Now the animals come first, and the eating follows.

<!-- morphology:40:79:9 root=ركب pos=V --> The verb *tarkabū* (ride).
<!-- morphology:40:79:15 root=أكل pos=V --> The verb *taʾkulūn* (eat).

Why the flip? The classical grammarians noticed this and it repays the noticing. Riding an animal is a *whole* engagement — you are on the whole beast, it carries all of you, so the verb *riding* naturally takes the lead. But eating is *partial* — you eat *some* of them, not all, not always, and the animal that carries you is usually not the one you slaughter. So Allah fronts the *minhā*, the "some of them," to whisper a limit into the sentence. **Even the gift He is describing, He describes with restraint.** The very word order is teaching you proportion: use them, but not all of them; ride, but eat only some.

I point this out not for its own sake, but so you feel the grain of the wood. Nothing here is loose. If the ordering of two ordinary verbs is this deliberate, then when we reach the strange phrase about the chest, we are not going to explain it away as a figure of speech. We are going to assume it is exactly as precise as everything around it.

### Second: The Need That Lives in the Chest

Now we go to the heart of it. Literally.

<!-- ayah:40:80 -->
وَلِتَبْلُغُوا۟ عَلَيْهَا حَاجَةً فِى صُدُورِكُمْ

*"...and that you may reach, upon them, a need that is in your chests."*

Let me give you the tools before I use them, because two words here are doing all the work.

The first is the verb *tablughū*, "that you may reach / attain / arrive at." <!-- morphology:40:80:9 root=بلغ pos=V --> Its root is *b-l-gh* — **بلغ** — and this is the same root from which the Quran gets the word *balāgh*, a *message that reaches its destination*, and *bāligh*, someone who has *reached* maturity. The root is about arrival — a thing traveling until it lands. Keep that in your ear: *bulūgh* is the completion of a journey. Something set out, and now it has arrived.

The second word is the object of that reaching: *ḥājah*. <!-- morphology:40:80:12 root=حوج pos=N --> Root *ḥ-w-j* — **حوج** — meaning a *need*, a *want*, a *thing lacking that presses on you*. And notice — Allah does not say *your* need, *al-ḥājah*, the definite, the specific one. He says *ḥājatan* — **indefinite. A need. Some need. Any need.** He deliberately leaves it unnamed and open.

Hold those two together and the sentence already tightens: *"that you may reach a need."* And then comes the phrase that has no business here:

*fī ṣudūrikum* — **"in your chests."**

<!-- morphology:40:80:14 root=صدر pos=N --> The word is *ṣudūr*, plural of *ṣadr* — the chest, the breast, root *ṣ-d-r*, **صدر**. And this word is not neutral in the Quran. When the Quran wants to talk about your body's front, it has words for that. *Ṣadr* is where the Quran keeps the *inner life.* It is *fī ṣudūrikum* that Allah says faith settles, that doubt festers, that secrets hide, that grief tightens, that whispers are cast. The chest, in Quranic language, is **the seat of what you carry that no one else can see.**

Now put the whole thing back together and feel the collision. Allah is describing *transportation*. Camels. Boats. The most physical, external, dusty thing in a human life — moving your body from one place to another. And right in the middle of it, He says the destination of all that moving is *"a need in your chests."*

Do you feel the seam? [PAUSE]

Because a camel cannot carry you to your *chest.* A camel carries you to a market, to a well, to a wedding in another valley, to your family three days' ride away. The *chest* is already here. It has been with you the whole time. It came on the journey.

And that is the whole point. Watch what the ayah just did, quietly, with a single preposition. It told you that **every external journey you have ever taken was really about something internal.** You told yourself you needed to *get somewhere.* But the thing that actually drove you into the saddle was not out there in the destination. It was a pressure *in here* — a want, a lack, a *ḥājah* lodged in the chest. The camel moved your body across the sand. But the thing that needed reaching was never in the sand. It was riding in your chest the entire time, and you were carrying it to a place you hoped would finally quiet it.

This is the Quran doing what only the Quran does. A science lesson can tell you what a camel is for. Only this can take the camel — the most ordinary object in that man's world — and use it to reach *inside his own chest* and name a longing he had never put into words. The animal is not the subject. **The animal is a device.** It is a physical thing Allah is using to point at an unseen thing: the restless, un-nameable need that every human being is quietly hauling from place to place, hoping the next place will hold the answer.

And remember — He left the need *indefinite.* *Ḥājatan.* A need. He did not fill in the blank, because the blank is different for every single person listening. For one man it is money. For a woman it is a child. For one heart it is honor, for another it is escape, for another it is simply peace. **The indefiniteness is the mercy.** By not naming your need, Allah named all of them. He reached into every chest at once.

### Third: You Are Not the Driver — The Passive That Puts You in Your Place

There is one more word, and it changes the posture of the whole passage.

<!-- ayah:40:80 -->
وَعَلَيْهَا وَعَلَى ٱلْفُلْكِ تُحْمَلُونَ

*"...and upon them and upon the ships you are carried."*

The verb is *tuḥmalūn.* <!-- morphology:40:80:23 root=حمل pos=V --> Root *ḥ-m-l*, **حمل** — to carry, to bear. And here is the thing your ear should catch, especially if you know a little Arabic: this verb is **passive.** Not *taḥmilūn* — "you carry." *Tuḥmalūn* — **"you are carried."**

Let that land. Through the whole passage the human being has been the subject of the verbs. *You* ride. *You* eat. *You* reach. You, you, you — the active agent, the one doing things, the one in the saddle with the reins in his hands, going where he decides to go. And then at the very last verb, Allah flips the voice, and suddenly *you are not the one doing anything.* **You are being carried.**

Think about what that reversal exposes. The man on the camel *feels* like the master of the situation. He chose the destination. He pulls the rein left, the animal goes left. His whole sense of the moment is *I am in control; this beast obeys me.* And Allah, with a single grammatical choice, quietly punctures it: *tuḥmalūn* — you are being carried. The animal beneath you is not really under your power; it is a creature Allah *made* to bear you, and at any moment its heart could stop, its leg could break, its temper could turn, and all your reins would be worth nothing. The illusion of the rider is that he is driving. The reality of the rider is that he is a passenger on something he did not make and cannot ultimately command.

And notice Allah pairs it: *ʿalayhā wa ʿala-l-fulk* — "upon them **and upon the ships.**" <!-- morphology:40:80:22 root=فلك pos=N --> The animals for the land; the ships for the sea. He has now covered the two great surfaces a human can move across. And on both of them — land and sea, camel and boat — the verb is the same. *You are carried.* There is nowhere you go under your own power. Cross the desert: you are carried. Cross the ocean: you are carried. The human being spends his whole life believing he is *traveling*, and Allah tells him the truer word is that he is being *borne* — held up, moment by moment, on things He fashioned and sustains.

This is not a small correction. It is the correction at the root of all denial. The Pharaoh of this very surah went wrong at exactly this point: he mistook *being carried* for *carrying.* He looked at a kingdom that was holding him up and called himself its lord. And here, gently, to ordinary people, Allah shows the same truth from the humble end — you, on your everyday animal, on your everyday boat, are as carried as any king. You never once drove.

### Fourth: "Which One?" — The Sound of Denial

And now the passage turns, and you should feel the turn like a hand landing on your shoulder.

<!-- ayah:40:81 -->
وَيُرِيكُمْ ءَايَٰتِهِۦ فَأَىَّ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ تُنكِرُونَ

*"And He shows you His signs — so which of the signs of Allah do you deny?"*

First the verb *yurīkum.* <!-- morphology:40:81:2 root=رأي pos=V --> Root *r-ʾ-y*, **رأي** — to see. But it is in the causative form — He does not merely *see*; He **makes you see.** *Yurīkum* — He *shows* you. And it is in the present tense, which in Arabic carries the ongoing, the continuous. Not "He showed you His signs" once, long ago. **He keeps showing you.** Right now. This morning, when you ate. This week, when you traveled. The showing never stops.

And then the question. And here we have to talk about who is being spoken to, because the address shifts under your feet. The whole passage until now has been *description* — "Allah is the One who made... and in them are benefits... you are carried." It reads almost like a gentle report of favors. And then, without warning: *fa-ayya āyāti-llāhi tunkirūn* — **"so which of the signs of Allah do you deny?"** The soft listing of gifts snaps into a direct, second-person challenge. The voice rises. You have been listening comfortably to a list of nice things God does, and suddenly the speaker turns, looks straight at you, and asks you to your face: *now — which one will you reject?*

Feel the design of that question. He does not ask *do* you deny. He asks *which.* *Ayya* — "which one?" <!-- morphology:40:81:7 root= pos=INTG --> It is the grammar of a challenge that has already surrounded you. It assumes you are standing in the middle of a room whose every wall is a gift — the food, the ride, the benefits, the safe passage, the need He named in your chest — and it asks you to *point.* Go on. Pick one. Which of these will you look at and say *this did not come from Him*? The riding? The eating? The very ache in your chest that sent you searching? **You cannot point,** because there is no single sign to reject — and that is the trap sprung shut. Denial was never about a particular sign that failed the test. Denial is a posture toward *all* of them.

And the last word tells you what that posture really is. *Tunkirūn* — "you deny." <!-- morphology:40:81:10 root=نكر pos=V --> Root *n-k-r*, **نكر.** Sit with this root for a moment, because it is not the ordinary word for "lie" or "reject." *N-k-r* is the root of *munkar* — the *evil, the reprehensible.* It is the root of *nakirah* — the *unknown, the strange, the unfamiliar.* And listen to the shape of the word in your mouth: *tunkirūn.* It is a hard word. The *k* and the *r* clatter against each other; there is nothing smooth in it. The sound of the word is as ungracious as the act it names.

But here is the deep thing hiding in that root. *N-k-r* means *to make something strange* — to treat as *unknown* the very thing you know perfectly well. It is the exact opposite of *ʿirfān*, recognition. So when Allah asks *fa-ayya āyāti-llāhi tunkirūn,* He is not asking "which sign have you never seen?" He is asking **"which of these utterly familiar things — your food, your animal, your daily passage — will you now pretend is strange to you? Which one will you look at, a thing you have known your whole life, and act as though you do not recognize its Giver?"**

That is what denial is, at the root of the word. Not ignorance. **Estrangement from the familiar.** Making a stranger of what you know best.

Now let us ask what all four of these words have shown us — not about grammar, but about reality itself.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### The Ache You Keep Trying to Drive To

Let me not start with the ayah. Let me start with a feeling you already know intimately.

There is a particular thought that runs through most human lives, and it goes like this: *once I get there, I'll be okay.* Once I finish the degree. Once I get the job. Once I get the better job. Once we move into the house. Once the baby sleeps through the night. Once the kids are grown. Once the debt is gone. Once I hit the number. There is always a *there*, some destination glowing on the horizon, and the whole engine of your days is built around reaching it, because you have quietly promised yourself that *when you arrive, the pressure in your chest will finally let go.*

And here is what everyone eventually notices, if they live long enough and stay honest. You *arrive.* You actually get there. The degree comes. The house closes. The number hits. And there is a strange, hollow week — sometimes only a day — where you feel it: *huh. It's the same chest.* The pressure did not lift. It migrated. Within a month it has attached itself to a new *there*, a new destination, and the engine fires up again and you are back on the road, driving toward the next place that promises to finally be enough.

Psychologists have a clinical name for the treadmill part of this — they call it *hedonic adaptation*, the way we absorb every gain back to baseline. And they are describing something real. But they describe the *mechanism*, the *what*. They can tell you that you keep resetting. They cannot tell you *why you were built this way* or *what the ache is actually for.* They can measure the treadmill. They cannot tell you what you were running toward.

Now — *"that you may reach, upon them, a need that is in your chests."*

Do you see what this ayah did fourteen centuries before the psychology? It looked at a man loading his camel to ride to some destination, and it told him the truth about his own trip: *the need you are riding toward is not at the end of the road. It is in your chest. It came with you.* The Quran located the ache in the one place the traveler never thought to look — inside himself — and in doing so it explained why no destination ever silenced it. **You cannot arrive at a place that will fix a thing you are carrying.** The camel can close the distance to the market. No camel closes the distance to the thing in the *ṣadr.*

But do not stop at the diagnosis, because a diagnosis alone would just be despair with footnotes. Look again at *who* is speaking this sentence. The whole passage is *"Allāhu-lladhī"* — **"Allah is the One who...."** The same One who made the camel that carries your body is the One telling you that your camel was never the answer. He gave you the vehicle *and* He named the ache the vehicle can't reach. Why would He do both? Why hand you the means of the journey and in the same breath tell you the journey won't satisfy you?

Because the ache was never a design flaw. **It is a homing signal.** The restlessness that no house and no number could quiet is not a bug in the human heart; it is the heart's built-in refusal to be satisfied by anything less than the One it was made to reach. Every destination fails you *on purpose.* The failure is the mercy. If any *there* had ever actually been enough, you would have stopped there, and made it your god, and never looked past it. The ache keeps you from parking your whole soul in a mortgage. It is the low, constant pull that will not let a created thing become your resting place.

So what changes tomorrow? Something very concrete. The next time you catch yourself in that old sentence — *once I get there, I'll be okay* — you have a choice about how to read the disappointment when *there* turns out not to be enough. You can read it as *I picked the wrong there* — and get back on the camel, and ride toward another one, forever. Or you can read it the way this ayah teaches: *of course this didn't fill it. Nothing here was ever going to. This ache in my chest has an address, and the address is not in this world.* And that single reframe turns a lifetime of quiet frustration into a lifetime of quiet direction. You stop being angry that the destinations keep failing you. You start being *guided* by the fact that they do.

### The Strange Thing You've Stopped Seeing

Here is a second thing you already know, from the inside.

Try to remember the first time you saw the ocean. Not a photo — the actual thing, the horizon of it, the size. If you were a child, you probably went quiet. Something in you understood, without words, that you were standing in front of something enormous. Now think about the last time you were at the beach. Odds are you spent part of it looking at your phone. Same ocean. The ocean did not shrink. *You* stopped seeing it.

This is one of the strangest and most reliable facts about being human: **familiarity blinds us far more thoroughly than distance ever could.** The things we see every day become, in a sense, invisible. Your own front door. The face of the person you have loved for twenty years. Your heartbeat, which has not missed an appointment since before you were born and which you have not thanked once this week. We do not lose the ability to see wonders. We lose the ability to see wonders *we have gotten used to.* The mind files them under "normal" and stops rendering them.

And now hear the question again with the root of it laid open: *fa-ayya āyāti-llāhi tunkirūn* — "so which of the signs of Allah do you *make strange*?" Remember what *n-k-r* actually means — not to disbelieve some far-off claim, but to treat the *familiar* as *unknown*, to look at what you know best and act as though you do not recognize it.

Do you see now why Allah, in a surah full of Pharaoh and drowning armies and cosmic disputes, chose to make His closing case out of *cattle and boats*? He could have pointed at the mountains. He could have pointed at the stars. Instead He deliberately pointed at the most *ordinary* things in the room — the animal tied up outside, the food already in your mouth, the boat you have boarded a hundred times. **Because that is exactly where denial hides.** No one denies the comet. Everyone is stirred by the eclipse. It is the *daily* sign, the one worn smooth by repetition, that we quietly stop counting as a sign at all. And Allah is saying: *that* is where you are most in danger. Not in front of the miracle you have never seen — in front of the miracle you see every morning and have stopped calling a miracle.

This is why the surah's warning at the top — *"none dispute the signs of Allah except those who disbelieve"* — has to be read together with this ending. Disputing the signs is not usually a dramatic act. It is rarely a man shaking his fist at the sky. **It is far more often the slow, comfortable numbness of a person surrounded by gifts he has entirely stopped noticing.** Pharaoh's denial and your distraction at the beach are, at the root, the same word: *nukr.* Making a stranger of the known. He did it with an empire. We do it with a plate of food and a functioning pair of lungs. The scale is different. The disease is the same.

And this is where the passive verb from earlier comes back and finishes the thought. *Tuḥmalūn* — *you are carried.* The reason we stop seeing the gift is that we have quietly reassigned the credit. We tell ourselves *I got here.* I drove. I earned. I arranged my own passage. And a person who believes he is the driver feels no wonder, because wonder requires acknowledging a Giver, and the self-made man has fired the Giver and taken the wheel. To *see the sign again* — to recover from *nukr* — you have to first accept *tuḥmalūn.* You have to admit you were being carried the whole time. The recognition of the sign and the humility of the passenger are the same movement of the heart.

So what does this actually change tomorrow morning? This one is almost embarrassingly small, and that is the point. You will eat something tomorrow. You will move from one place to another by some means you did not build and do not fully understand. This ayah is asking for a single act of resistance against the numbness: *notice one of them.* Pick one ordinary thing — the first sip of water, the fact that your legs carried you to the door, the animal or the engine that bore you across the city — and for one deliberate second, refuse to treat it as strange. Refuse the *nukr.* Recognize the Giver in it, out loud in your chest if not on your tongue. That is not a small religious nicety. **That is the whole difference between the posture of Pharaoh and the posture of a believer,** rehearsed in the space of a heartbeat, over a glass of water.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture: How the Trap Was Built

Now step back and look at the shape of the whole thing, because there is a design here you could not see until we had climbed all three ayahs.

Look at where Allah put you. He begins with the animals and says *lakum* — "for **you**." He gives you riding and eating. He gives you *manāfiʿ* — benefits, *for you.* He follows you *inside*, into your *ṣudūr*, your chests, and names the need He finds there. He puts you *upon* the animals and *upon* the ships — *ʿalayhā, ʿala-l-fulk* — and even there, at the height of your apparent mastery, He tells you *you are carried.* And then, and only then, when there is not one axis of your life He has not touched — your stomach, your travel, your labor, your longing, the land under you, the sea around you, your very chest — **only then does He ask the question.** *Which of the signs of Allah do you deny?*

That is the architecture. The three ayahs are not a list. They are a **closing net.** Allah surrounds the human being on every side — food and transport, body and heart, land and sea, the outer journey and the inner ache — and once the circle is complete and there is nowhere left to stand that is not a gift, He asks you to point at the one thing you'll call not-His. And you cannot. There is no exit from the room, because the room is made entirely of His signs, and *you are one of the things being held up inside it.* The question at the end is unanswerable not because it is clever, but because by the time it arrives, the evidence is already touching every part of you.

### The Two Roads Were One Road

And now see that the two themes we walked were never two. They were one insight approached from opposite doors.

From one door: the ache in your chest that no destination fills. From the other: the sign in front of you that you have stopped seeing. And here is where they meet — **both are failures to recognize where you already are.** The restless man cannot see that what he is chasing is not down the road; the numb man cannot see that what he is chasing has been beside him the whole time. One looks too far ahead. One looks straight through what is present. But both have lost contact with the same reality: that they are, right now, held up and provided for and surrounded by the constant, ongoing generosity of a God who *"keeps showing you His signs"* — *yurīkum*, present tense, never stopping. The cure for the restlessness and the cure for the numbness turn out to be the identical act: **to recognize the Giver in the given, right here, in the ordinary thing you are holding.**

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. What is the *there* you are currently driving toward — the destination you have quietly told yourself will finally settle your chest? And if you are honest: have you arrived at a *there* before, and felt it fail to settle you?

2. Which ordinary sign have you most thoroughly stopped seeing? Name the specific one — the thing so familiar it has become invisible.

3. Where in your life are you most convinced you are the driver? And what would change in how you carried it if you truly believed the word *tuḥmalūn* — *you are being carried*?

4. Denial, at the root, is making a stranger of the familiar. Where have you made a stranger of something — or someone — you actually know very well?

### The One Sentence

**The same God who gave you the vehicle to carry your body is the One who placed the ache in your chest that no vehicle can reach — so when He asks which of His signs you deny, the honest answer is that you cannot name one; you only stopped noticing.**

### The Invitation

So here is what this passage quietly invites — not commands, invites. Not a grand resolution. Just a re-opening of the eyes. Tomorrow you will eat, and travel, and want something you do not yet have. All three are in this ayah. Let one of them, just one, stop being invisible. Let the food be a sign again. Let the ache be a direction instead of a frustration. Let the fact that you were *carried* soften the fiction that you drove. You do not have to become a different person. You only have to stop making a stranger of what you already know.

### A Duʿā From These Words

*O Allah, You who made the beasts to bear us and the ships to carry us across the deep — we confess that we were being carried the whole time we thought we were driving. You placed in our chests a need that no road of ours could ever reach; turn that ache toward You, so that our restlessness becomes our guidance and not our torment. And O You who keep showing us Your signs without ever pausing — cure us of the blindness of the familiar. Let us not make strangers of Your gifts. Let us recognize You in the water, in the bread, in the breath — and answer Your question honestly: not one of Your signs do we deny. Āmīn.*
