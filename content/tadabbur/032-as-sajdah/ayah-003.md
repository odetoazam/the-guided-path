# The Warner No One Sent Before
## Mercy Disguised as Confrontation — A Reflection on Surah As-Sajdah, Ayah 3

---

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> أَمْ يَقُولُونَ ٱفْتَرَىٰهُ ۚ بَلْ هُوَ ٱلْحَقُّ مِن رَّبِّكَ لِتُنذِرَ قَوْمًا مَّآ أَتَىٰهُم مِّن نَّذِيرٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Am yaqūlūna iftarāh? Bal huwa-l-ḥaqqu min rabbika li-tundhira qawman mā atāhum min nadhīrin min qablika laʿallahum yahtadūn.

**Translation:**
> "Or do they say, 'He has invented it'? Rather, it is the truth from your Lord, that you may warn a people to whom no warner has come before you — so that perhaps they may be guided."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To enter this ayah, you have to feel something that's almost impossible to feel from where we're sitting today.

Imagine a city. Not a metropolis. A trading town, ringed by stone hills and desert, where everyone knows everyone's lineage going back five generations. The center of this town is a black cube draped in cloth, and around that cube, three hundred and sixty idols. Wood. Stone. Carved into shapes that mean something to the people who carved them. Some of these idols have names that go back centuries. Some of them have stories attached — stories of how they came from elsewhere, from older nations, from peoples who were said to have wisdom.

In this town, a man — known to everyone, trusted by everyone, called *al-Amīn*, "the Trustworthy" — begins to speak words. He says these words are not his. He says they come from beyond him. He says there is one God, not three hundred and sixty. He says there is a Day coming when every soul will stand alone and answer.

And the people — many of them — look at him and say:

*"He's making it up."*

That single accusation is the air this ayah is breathing in.

But here is what the listener of that moment needed to understand — and what we need to recover before we can hear what Allah is about to say. The people of Makkah were not random pagans. They were the descendants of Ibrāhīm, peace be upon him. They knew his name. They claimed his lineage. They circled a house he had built. But between Ibrāhīm and Muḥammad ﷺ — across centuries — **no prophet had been sent to them**. Other nations had received warner after warner. Banū Isrāʾīl had a chain of prophets stretching from Mūsā through Dāwūd through ʿĪsā. The Arabs of the Ḥijāz had inherited fragments. Echoes. Names. But no living voice. No one standing in front of them saying: *thus says your Lord.*

[PAUSE]

This is the silence into which the Quran arrives. And the very people who had been waiting in that silence — even if they didn't know they were waiting — turn around when the silence breaks and say: *you made this up.*

### The Hook

Allah's response to this accusation is one of the most extraordinary moves in the entire Quran. He does not defend His Messenger ﷺ the way you would expect. He does not list miracles. He does not threaten. He does not even argue.

He says one word — *bal* — and then redirects the entire conversation to a place the accusers were not ready to go.

He tells them why the Book came.

And the reason He gives is not what you'd think. It is not victory. It is not vindication. It is something far stranger — and far more tender — than anyone in that moment was prepared to hear.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Am* — The Question That Isn't Asking

Before we touch the ayah, let me show you something about how Arabic asks questions.

In English, when we want to ask "is it this, or is it that?" we use the word *or*. It's a neutral connector. It just offers an alternative. But Arabic has a particle that does something more specific. The particle *am*. And *am* doesn't just offer an alternative — it often carries the weight of **incredulity**. Of disbelief at what's being suggested. It's the question you ask when you already know the answer should be obvious, and yet someone is suggesting the opposite.

Imagine a parent comes home and finds the kitchen destroyed. Flour on the floor. A broken bowl. Their five-year-old standing in the middle of it all. The parent says: *"Did the dog do this? Or are you telling me YOU did this?"*

That second question — the one introduced by "or" — isn't really asking. It's exposing. It's putting the absurdity on display so the speaker can hear themselves.

Now look at the opening word of our ayah:

> **أَمْ يَقُولُونَ ٱفْتَرَىٰهُ**
> *Am yaqūlūna iftarāh* — *"Or do they say He has invented it?"*

Allah is not genuinely asking whether they're saying this. He knows they're saying it. The *am* is doing two things at once. First, it's connecting back to the previous ayah — *tanzīlu-l-kitābi lā rayba fīh*, "the revelation of the Book, no doubt in it." Allah has just declared the Book is beyond doubt. *Am* then says: *or are they really going to claim He invented it?* It puts the accusation right next to the declaration, and the absurdity speaks for itself.

Second, the *am* is carrying a quiet astonishment. The Book is so manifestly what it is — and yet there are people who, looking at it, will say *iftarāh*, he made it up. The particle itself is shaking its head.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah doesn't say "they say." He says *am yaqūlūna* — *or do they say*. The accusation isn't even quoted as a fact. It's quoted as a question hovering in the air, almost daring the accusers to claim it out loud.

This is the first piece of mercy in the ayah. Before the refutation comes, before the redirection comes, the accusation is held up to the light — *do you really want to say this?*

### Element Two: *Iftarāh* — The Shape of the Word Itself

Now let's look at the accusation. The verb they're accused of speaking is *iftarā* — from the root *f-r-y*.

In classical Arabic, this root has a physical origin. *Farā al-jilda* — to cut a piece of leather. To slice it, shape it, fabricate it into something it wasn't before. From this, by extension, the root came to mean to invent, to make up, to fabricate. Specifically — and this is critical — to fabricate **a lie about God**. Throughout the Quran, *iftirāʾ* almost always refers to inventing a falsehood and attributing it to Allah.

But notice the form of the verb here. It's not *farā*. It's *iftarā* — Form VIII. The added *t* in the middle of the word is a morphological device in Arabic that often indicates a kind of **deliberate, self-involved action**. It pulls the verb toward intentionality. It says: this isn't an accident, it isn't a slip, it isn't a mistake. *Iftarā* means he sat down, he plotted, he constructed this lie, piece by piece, and then claimed it came from elsewhere.

The accusation, in other words, is not casual. They are not saying "he's confused." They are not saying "he heard this somewhere." They are saying: *he is a deliberate fabricator. He cut this Book like you cut leather. He shaped it to his purposes. And he stamped Allah's name on it.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel the sound of the word. *Iftarāh*. Listen to the texture. There's a harshness in it — the *f* and the *t* knocking against each other, the dragged *rā* in the middle. It is not a smooth word. It has the texture of something jagged, something cut. The word *sounds* like the act it describes — a violent shaping of something into a shape it shouldn't have.

And Allah's response to this jagged accusation is a single, soft syllable.

*Bal.*

### Element Three: *Bal* — The Smallest Word, the Largest Pivot

This is the word I want you to stay with longest.

*Bal* is one of the smallest words in Arabic. Two letters. One syllable. And yet in the grammar of the Quran, it is one of the most powerful pivots a sentence can make.

In Arabic, *bal* is called a *ḥarf al-iḍrāb* — a particle of redirection. But the word *iḍrāb* itself is interesting. It comes from a root that means to *strike*, to *turn away from*. *Bal* doesn't just say "actually, on the other hand." *Bal* says: *strike that. Turn from that. We are leaving that conversation now.*

There are softer ways to disagree in Arabic. There are particles that mean "but" in a gentler sense. *Bal* is not gentle. *Bal* is a refusal to engage further with the premise that came before.

Now watch what Allah does with it.

The accusers have said *iftarāh* — he invented it. Allah could respond by arguing. He could say: *no, here are the proofs. Here are the miracles. Here is the eloquence. Here is the consistency. Here is the lineage of the Messenger.* He doesn't. He says:

> **بَلْ هُوَ ٱلْحَقُّ مِن رَّبِّكَ**
> *Bal huwa-l-ḥaqqu min rabbika* — *Rather, it is the truth from your Lord.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment.

The whole argument is dismissed in two letters. *Bal.* We are not going to have a conversation about whether this is invented. That conversation is over before it begins. The Book is *al-ḥaqq* — *the* truth, with the definite article, the truth itself, not a truth, not one truth among many. And it is *min rabbika* — from your Lord.

And here is where the linguistic precision becomes a kind of tenderness. Allah does not say *min Allāh*, from God. He says *min rabbika*, from **your Lord**. The pronoun shifts. In a single phrase, Allah pivots from the accusers to the Prophet ﷺ himself. The accusation came from them. The reassurance comes to him. *Your Lord. The One who nurtures you. The One who has been with you since before they ever heard your name.*

The word *rabb* in Arabic doesn't just mean "lord" in the sense of a master. It comes from a root that means to nurture, to raise, to bring something from a small state to a fuller state. Your *rabb* is the one who has been growing you, gradually, since you came into existence. *Min rabbika* means: this Book is from the One who has been forming you since before you knew you were being formed.

[PAUSE]

This is the second piece of mercy. The accusation came in public, loud, jagged. The reassurance comes in private, soft, intimate. *Your Lord. From your Lord.*

But Allah is not finished. Because if the ayah ended at *min rabbika*, it would still be about the Messenger ﷺ — about defending him. And the most stunning move is still ahead.

### Element Four: *Li-Tundhira* — The Purpose That Reframes Everything

After *min rabbika*, Allah adds a word that begins with the letter *lām*. *Li-tundhira*. The *lām* here is called the *lām al-taʿlīl* — the *lām* of purpose, of reason. It means: *in order that.* Or: *so that.*

This single letter does something extraordinary. It says: the Book did not come because of the accusation. The Book did not come to defeat the accusers. The Book came **for a purpose**, and that purpose was set long before the accusation was ever uttered.

And what is the purpose?

> **لِتُنذِرَ قَوْمًا مَّآ أَتَىٰهُم مِّن نَّذِيرٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ**
> *li-tundhira qawman mā atāhum min nadhīrin min qablika* — *that you may warn a people to whom no warner has come before you.*

The verb is *tundhira*, from the root *n-dh-r*. The root *n-dh-r* in classical Arabic carries a very specific texture. A *nadhīr* is not someone who threatens. A *nadhīr* is someone who **alerts you to danger that you cannot yet see**. The *nadhīr* in pre-Islamic Arabia was, in some usages, a scout. Someone who rode ahead of the caravan, saw an ambush in the distance, and came galloping back screaming a warning so that the caravan could prepare.

The *nadhīr* loves the people he is warning. That is why he is warning them. If he didn't care whether they lived or died, he would say nothing. The warning **is** the love. The warning is what love looks like when there is real danger and time is short.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. We have been trained, in modern usage, to hear the word "warner" and feel something cold. Authoritarian. A finger pointing. But the Arabic *nadhīr* is the opposite. The *nadhīr* is the one who runs toward you when everyone else is walking away. The *nadhīr* is the one whose voice shakes because he can see what's coming and you can't, yet.

Allah is telling His Prophet ﷺ: *the reason I sent you this Book is so you can run toward them.*

Now read the description of the people again:

> *a people to whom no warner has come before you.*

This is the part that should stop your breath.

Allah is saying: these accusers, these jagged voices, these people calling you a fabricator — these are not people I have given up on. These are people **I have been holding in My silence for centuries**, waiting for the right moment to send you. They have not been ignored. They have been *saved* for this. No warner came to them before you because *you* were the warner I was preparing for them.

[PAUSE]

The accusation has been completely transformed. They said: *you invented this to elevate yourself.* Allah says: *I sent this because I love them, and I have been waiting to love them out loud, through you, for generations.*

### Element Five: *Laʿallahum Yahtadūn* — The Word That Trembles

The ayah ends with one more word that we have to look at carefully.

> **لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ**
> *laʿallahum yahtadūn* — *so that perhaps they may be guided.*

The word *laʿalla* is one of the most theologically charged particles in Arabic. It is usually translated as "perhaps" or "so that." Grammatically, it expresses *tarajjī* — hope, expectation, anticipation.

Now here is the problem. *Laʿalla* expresses hope. But hope, in human use, implies uncertainty. *I hope it will rain* means *I do not know if it will rain.* So when Allah says *laʿallahum yahtadūn* — *perhaps they will be guided* — does Allah not know? Of course He knows. Allah's knowledge is absolute.

The classical scholars resolved this not by softening *laʿalla* but by deepening it. *Laʿalla*, when used by Allah, does not express His uncertainty. It expresses the **relational structure** He is establishing with the listener. It places the verb in the space of *should* and *could* and *might* — the space where human freedom lives.

Allah is not saying *I don't know if they'll be guided.* Allah is saying: *I am sending this Book in such a way that guidance becomes possible — and the doorway is open, and I will not force them through it.* The *laʿalla* is the architecture of human freedom inside divine sovereignty. It is Allah refusing to coerce.

And notice the verb. *Yahtadūn*, from the root *h-d-y*. But it is not *yuhdawn* — they are guided (passive). It is *yahtadūn* — Form VIII again, that *t* in the middle marking reflexive, self-involved action. *They guide themselves.* Or more precisely: *they take guidance, they accept guidance, they let guidance in.*

[PAUSE]

So the ayah ends with this trembling hope: *that they might let themselves be guided.* Allah has sent the Book. Allah has sent the Messenger ﷺ. Allah has provided the warning, which is love, which is mercy. And then Allah steps back and says: *the rest is between Me and them. I have opened the door. Perhaps they will walk through.*

This is the third piece of mercy. He doesn't force. He invites. He sends a warner because He loves them — but He will not override the very freedom that makes their love mean anything.

### Bridge

We came in expecting a defense of the Prophet ﷺ against an ugly accusation. What we found instead was something else entirely. We found Allah refusing to engage with the accusation at all. We found Him pivoting from the accusers to the Messenger, then from the Messenger to the deeper purpose, then from the purpose to a trembling hope.

The whole movement of the ayah is from confrontation to mercy. From their voice to His voice. From their question to His silence-broken-into-speech.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way Allah relates to people who reject Him.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When the Accusation Is Really a Confession

Let's start somewhere very human.

Have you ever been accused of something — and as the person was accusing you, you slowly realized that what they were describing was not you at all? That what they were describing was actually them?

Therapists have a word for this. *Projection.* When something inside us is too painful to face — guilt, shame, dishonesty, weakness — we sometimes export it. We see it in someone else and accuse them of it. The accusation becomes a mirror we hold up to someone else, but the face in the mirror is our own.

This is one of the deepest patterns of the human psyche. We are most likely to accuse others of the very thing we cannot face in ourselves.

Now look at the accusation in the ayah. *Iftarāh.* He has invented it. He has fabricated it. He has cut this Book like a piece of leather and shaped it to his purposes.

Who is more likely to make this accusation? Who is the kind of person who would suspect this?

[PAUSE]

It is the person who knows what it looks like to fabricate. It is the person who has, in their own life, shaped their religion to their purposes. The leaders of Quraysh had inherited a religion of Ibrāhīm and had, over generations, cut it, shaped it, refashioned it. They had added gods. They had added rituals. They had added narratives that suited their trade routes and their tribal politics. They themselves were the fabricators.

When they looked at Muḥammad ﷺ and saw a man receiving revelation, they could not imagine that revelation was real — because their own religious history was a history of invention. They projected. They saw, in him, the very thing they were.

And here is what Allah does in this ayah that no human accused person would do. He does not say: *you are the fabricators, not him.* He does not turn the accusation back on them. He simply says *bal* — and walks away from the conversation. He refuses to dignify the projection. He refuses to fight on the ground of the accusation.

And then He does something even more astonishing.

He still calls them *qawman* — a people. He does not call them *al-kāfirūn*, the disbelievers, even though they are. He does not call them *al-ẓālimūn*, the wrongdoers, even though they are. He calls them *a people*. A people **to whom no warner has come before you.**

[PAUSE]

He gives them a category that is defined not by their sin but by their condition. They are not the deniers. They are *the unwarned*. The ones who have been waiting in a silence they did not even know was a silence.

Think about what this does to your understanding of how Allah sees the worst of His creation. The very people who are accusing His beloved Messenger ﷺ of being a fraud — Allah, in the same breath, characterizes as *people who have been waiting for this Book*. Their accusation is the symptom of the very silence the Book has come to break.

**The practical application:** the next time someone in your life accuses you of something — especially something tied to your faith, your sincerity, your motives — pause before defending. Ask: *what is this person projecting? What in their own life is this accusation actually about? And what kind of silence are they sitting in that I am tempted to fill with argument when what they need is mercy?*

Most of the people who attack faith are not arguing with you. They are arguing with a wound. And the wound is often the absence of a warner — the absence, in their life, of someone who said *I see what's coming, and I love you enough to tell you.*

Sometimes the most prophetic thing you can say in the face of an accusation is *bal* — and then quietly, intimately, walk the person back to what's actually true.

### Theme Two: Mercy in the Shape of a Warning

There is something deeply counterintuitive in this ayah that I want us to sit with for a long time.

Allah's stated purpose for sending the Quran — in this ayah — is *li-tundhira*, *that you may warn*. Not *that you may give good news*. Not *that you may comfort*. *Warn.*

And yet everything we've uncovered about *nadhīr* tells us that warning, in the Quranic sense, is one of the highest expressions of love. The warner is the scout. The warner is the friend who tells you the truth that nobody else will tell you. The warner is the doctor who says *we need to talk about your test results* — and says it not because she enjoys saying it, but because she would rather you weep now than die later.

Modern culture has done something strange with the concept of warning. We have made *kindness* synonymous with *affirmation*. To be kind, in the contemporary view, is to make someone feel good about whatever they are choosing. To warn is to be *judgmental*. To say *this path will hurt you* is to be *unloving*.

The Quran reverses this entirely.

In the Quran, the one who watches you walk toward a cliff and says nothing is not kind. They are negligent. They are, in some sense, complicit. **Love without warning is not love. It is convenience.**

And this is why Allah, in this ayah, frames the entire prophetic mission as a mission of warning. Not because Allah delights in warning. But because warning is what love sounds like when the stakes are infinite and time is finite.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the part that should reach into your own life.

Who is the *nadhīr* in your life?

Not in the prophetic sense — that role is sealed. But in the smaller sense. Who is the person in your life who has loved you enough to warn you? Who is the person who, when you were drifting, said *I see what's coming and I want you to stop*?

If you can think of one, hold onto them. Thank Allah for them. They are rarer than you know.

And — harder question — for whom are *you* the warner? Whose drift have you seen? Whose silence have you sat next to, watching them walk toward something that will harm them, and said nothing because saying something felt awkward or risky or unwelcome?

The Quran is a warner. The Prophet ﷺ was a warner. Every inheritor of prophecy carries some of this function. Not to scold. Not to dominate. But to love enough to speak.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer here. The phrase *mā atāhum min nadhīrin min qablika* — *no warner came to them before you* — is doing something subtle. It is telling the Prophet ﷺ that this is **his** people, in a specific, intimate way. They have been waiting for him. The silence in their tradition was a Muḥammad-shaped silence.

And this applies to you too, in your own circle. There are people in your life who have not yet received the warning they need. And it is possible — likely, even — that you are the one who has been prepared for them. Not because you are special. But because the silence in their life is shaped like you. They will not hear it from a famous scholar. They will not hear it from a viral video. They will hear it from you, because you are the one who has been placed near them.

The ayah is telling the Prophet ﷺ: *your placement is not accidental.* And it is telling you the same.

**The practical application:** identify one person in your life who is sitting in a silence that you might be the one to break. Not with a lecture. Not with confrontation. With the soft, intimate voice of someone who loves them and sees what's coming. Pray for them tonight. And ask Allah to make you a *nadhīr* in the prophetic mold — not harsh, not preachy, but the kind of friend who would rather have an uncomfortable conversation than watch someone they love walk off a cliff.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Step back now and look at the whole ayah.

> *Or do they say He has invented it? Rather, it is the truth from your Lord, that you may warn a people to whom no warner has come before you — so that perhaps they may be guided.*

The ayah is built in three movements:

**Movement One:** The accusation is named. *Am yaqūlūna iftarāh.*
**Movement Two:** The accusation is dismissed and redirected. *Bal huwa-l-ḥaqqu min rabbika.*
**Movement Three:** The deeper purpose is revealed. *Li-tundhira qawman mā atāhum min nadhīrin min qablika laʿallahum yahtadūn.*

Notice the trajectory. The ayah begins with **their voice** — the voice of accusation, the harsh syllables of *iftarāh*. It moves to **His voice** — *min rabbika*, your Lord. And it ends with **their possibility** — *laʿallahum yahtadūn*, that perhaps they may be guided.

The very people whose voice opens the ayah are the same people whose guidance closes it. Allah has, in a single ayah, taken the accusers and turned them into the beloveds. He has shown that the people who call His Messenger a fraud are the same people for whom His Messenger was sent. Their rejection does not disqualify them. It is the very thing that makes the mission urgent.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic ayah showed us: *bal* refuses to fight the accusation, *min rabbika* personalizes the reassurance, *li-tundhira* reframes the purpose as mercy, *laʿalla* preserves human freedom.

The thematic depths showed us: accusations are often confessions, and warning is what love sounds like when the stakes are real.

Both are saying the same thing. **Allah does not respond to rejection with rejection. He responds with redirection. He does not abandon those who deny Him. He sends them a warner — because that is what mercy looks like when sin is real and the soul is at stake.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When someone accuses you of insincerity in your faith, what does the *bal* of your own life sound like? Do you fight on their ground, or do you redirect to *min rabbika*?
2. Who has been a *nadhīr* in your life — and have you ever thanked them?
3. Who, in your life, is sitting in a silence that you might have been prepared to break?
4. When you are the one accusing — of others, of yourself, of the past — what are you really projecting? What inside you is the accusation actually about?
5. If Allah refuses to coerce even the most arrogant denier into guidance — preserving the *laʿalla* even at the cost of their salvation — what does that tell you about how He is relating to your own resistance, right now, in whatever you are refusing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The accusation that the Book is invented is dismissed not with argument but with a single word, because Allah refuses to fight on the ground of the accuser — He simply reveals that the Book was sent out of love for the very people who deny it, that they might walk freely into a guidance He will never force upon them.

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this?

Perhaps the way you receive accusations against your faith. Perhaps the way you make them.

Perhaps the way you understand the word *warning* — as something tender, not something harsh.

Perhaps the way you look at the people in your life who reject what you believe. They are not your enemies. They are, in Allah's eyes, people who have not yet been warned — *by you*, in the way only you can warn them, with the voice that has been shaped for their particular silence.

And perhaps the way you understand yourself when you are the one resisting. Allah is not chasing you with thunder. He is offering you a Book. *Laʿallakum tahtadūn.* That perhaps you might let yourself be guided.

### A Brief Du'a

> O Allah, You who pivoted from accusation to mercy in a single word — teach us to pivot. When we are accused, let us not fight on the ground of our accusers; let us speak from where You speak. When we are tempted to accuse, let us first look in the mirror. Make us warners in the prophetic mold — soft of voice, urgent of heart, loving enough to tell the truth that no one else will tell. And in our own moments of resistance, when You stand at the door of our hearts saying *laʿallahum yahtadūn* — may we, finally, let ourselves be guided.
>
> Āmīn.
