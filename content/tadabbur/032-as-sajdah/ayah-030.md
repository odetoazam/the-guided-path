# Turn Away From Them and Wait — The Closing Command of As-Sajdah

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> فَأَعْرِضْ عَنْهُمْ وَٱنتَظِرْ إِنَّهُم مُّنتَظِرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum wantaẓir, innahum muntaẓirūn.

**Translation:**
> So turn away from them and wait. Indeed, they too are waiting.

---

## Introduction: The World This Ayah Closes

Imagine you have been speaking, for years, into a room that mostly will not listen.

You have brought them news so urgent it leaves you sleepless. You have warned them of a Day that is coming. You have told them about their own creation — that they came from a despised drop of fluid, that they will be returned to dust, that they will be raised again, that their faces will be lowered before their Lord, that the deepest secret in their chest will be exposed.

And what have they said back to you?

They have mocked. They have shrugged. They have repeated the same tired question they have asked a hundred times: *"When is this victory, if you are truthful?"* (32:28) — not as a sincere question but as a taunt. They are not asking because they want to know. They are asking because they want to silence you.

This is the Makkan period. This is the Prophet ﷺ standing in a city that has rejected him. This is a surah called **As-Sajdah** — "The Prostration" — a surah whose central image is the believer falling to the ground in awe of Allah's verses while the denier remains standing in arrogance.

For twenty-nine ayahs Allah has been answering them. He has reminded them of creation. He has described their death and resurrection. He has painted the scene of the criminals on the Day of Judgment with their heads bowed. He has contrasted the believer who falls in sujood with the rebel who turns away. He has even responded to their taunting question about "when" with a piercing reply: *"The day victory comes, faith will not benefit those who disbelieved..."* (32:29)

And now, after all of that — after all the proof, all the warning, all the answering — the surah ends.

It ends with six words.

Not a thunderclap. Not a final argument. Not a closing peroration.

Six words to the Prophet ﷺ.

[PAUSE]

What do you say to a Messenger who has spent himself on a people who will not hear?

That is the question this ayah answers.

And the answer is so quiet, so restrained, so structurally beautiful that we will spend the next hour trying to hear it properly.

**The hook:** Notice that this ayah contains the same root used twice — once as a command to the Prophet ﷺ, once as a description of his enemies. The Prophet is told to *wait*. His enemies are described as *those who are waiting*. Both sides — the truth and the falsehood — are doing the exact same verb. And yet they could not be more different. What is the difference between two people doing the same action? And what does it mean that Allah closes a surah this long with a verb so small?

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Fa-aʿriḍ* — The Command to Turn Away

Before we look at the word itself, sit with the strangeness of the instruction.

A prophet has been sent. A messenger has been commissioned. The whole purpose of his being there is to call them. And now, at the close of a long surah of calling, Allah says: **turn away from them.**

How do we make sense of this?

Let's start with a concept the listener already knows. Think of a parent and a teenager mid-argument. The parent has explained, pleaded, reasoned, raised their voice, lowered their voice. The teenager has crossed their arms. There comes a moment — every parent knows this moment — where continuing to speak is no longer love. Continuing to speak becomes its own kind of damage. The parent says, *"I've said what I needed to say. I'm going to step out of this room now."*

That stepping out is not abandonment. It is not surrender. It is not even silence forever. It is the recognition that words have reached the edge of what words can do. The next move belongs to someone else — to the teenager, to time, to a moment that has not yet arrived.

Hold that image. Now look at the Arabic.

**فَأَعْرِضْ** — *fa-aʿriḍ*. The *fa-* at the beginning is a connector — "so," "therefore," "in light of all that has come before." It tells us this command is not arbitrary. It is the conclusion of an argument. Allah has just spent twenty-nine ayahs presenting the case. Now, the *fa-* says: in light of all of that, do this.

The verb *aʿriḍ* comes from the root **ʿ-r-ḍ** (ع-ر-ض). The base meaning of this root in classical Arabic dictionaries is something wide, something with breadth — the side of a thing, the width of it, the broad face of something. From this root comes the noun *ʿarḍ* (width) as opposed to *ṭūl* (length).

So when you *aʿriḍ ʿan* someone, what are you literally doing? You are turning your *side* toward them. You are no longer facing them frontally. You have rotated so that your face is no longer in their direction. They are now beside you, not in front of you.

Stay with that physical picture for a moment.

To face someone is to be in conversation with them. To face someone is to give them your attention, your front, your engagement. When the Prophet ﷺ stood before the Quraysh and recited to them, he was *facing* them — they were the object of his speech, the target of his concern, the focus of his energy.

To *aʿriḍ ʿan* them is to rotate. To stop facing them. Not to walk away forever. Not to hate them. Not even necessarily to be silent. Just — to stop pointing yourself at them.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. This same verb — *aʿriḍ* and its forms — has been used elsewhere in the Quran to describe what the *deniers* do. Allah describes them as *muʿriḍūn* — those who turn away. They turn away from the verses of Allah. They turn away from the reminder. They turn their side to the truth.

And now, at the end of As-Sajdah, the Prophet ﷺ is being told: *you* turn your side now. *You* aʿriḍ.

But notice — and this is everything — when *they* turn away, they are turning away from **truth**. When *he* turns away, he is turning away from people who have rejected truth. The verb is identical. The moral content is opposite.

This is one of the Quran's quiet techniques: it uses the same word for two parties to expose how different the same action can be depending on what you are turning *from*.

What this invites us to consider is this: turning away is not, in itself, good or bad. To turn from the truth is the deepest crime. To turn from those who have crushed the truth — after you have given them everything — can be the deepest mercy. The Prophet is not being told to harden. He is being told to *release*.

There is a tafsir tradition that reads *fa-aʿriḍ* here not as a permanent disengagement but as a *strategic* one — a release of pressure, a stepping back from the futile front of the argument so that other doors can open. Other classical readings note that this kind of *iʿrāḍ* is itself a form of preaching: the silence after a long argument can do work that more words cannot.

These are not competing readings. They are the same instruction from different angles. Step back. Release the pressure. Let the silence speak. Let what you have already said settle into hearts that the noise of more words would only crowd out.

[PAUSE]

One more thing about *aʿriḍ ʿanhum* before we move on.

Notice the *ʿanhum* — *from them*. Not *from this matter*. Not *from the message*. From **them**. The people. Specifically the ones the surah has been addressing.

The Prophet is not being told to give up the mission. He is being told to release this particular set of hearts to Allah. The message continues. The Quran continues. The other people, the ones who have not yet heard, the ones whose hearts are still soft — they are still ahead. But *these* ones, the entrenched ones, the ones who have heard everything and chosen mockery — turn your side from them. Let Allah handle them now.

This is the first half of the closing instruction. **Stop facing the wall.**

But Allah does not stop there. Because to stop facing them is not the whole picture. If the Prophet just turned and walked away in despair, that would be one thing. But Allah gives him something to do *in* the turning. He gives him a posture for the silence.

### Element Two: *Wantaẓir* — And Wait

**وَٱنتَظِرْ** — *wantaẓir*. "And wait."

Before we open this word, teach yourself the concept that lives inside it.

In English, "waiting" can mean almost anything. You wait at a red light. You wait in line at the grocery store. You wait for a kettle to boil. The word is so common it has almost no texture. It can mean bored. It can mean impatient. It can mean passive.

Arabic does something more careful here.

The verb *intaẓara* comes from the root **n-ẓ-r** (ن-ظ-ر). And the base meaning of *naẓara* — the root verb — is **to look**. To see. To gaze. The eye is involved. *Naẓar* is a glance, a look, a sight.

Now, *intaẓara* is the eighth-form derivation of this root. The eighth form in Arabic (the *iftaʿala* pattern) often carries a reflexive or sustained meaning — to do the action upon oneself, or to engage in it actively and continuously.

So *intaẓara* is not "to look" the way you look at something passing by. It is to **hold your gaze** in a particular direction. To keep looking. To remain in the posture of one whose eyes are pointed toward something that has not yet arrived.

Do you feel the difference?

"Waiting" in English is often what you do *between* the things that matter. *Intiẓār* in Arabic is itself a posture of attention. It is the watchman on the city wall, eyes fixed on the horizon, waiting for the messenger to crest the hill. It is the mother whose ears tune to every car door because her son is coming home from war. It is the eyes of someone who knows what is coming and is keeping watch for it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah has just done.

He told the Prophet ﷺ: *aʿriḍ* — turn your side from them.

Then immediately: *wantaẓir* — and **look**. Keep your gaze fixed.

If you only had the first command, you would imagine the Prophet ﷺ defeated, dejected, slumped. Turn away. End of story.

But the second command lifts his head. Don't just turn from them — turn *toward* something else. Don't just stop facing the wall — start facing the horizon. Your gaze is no longer on the deaf crowd. Your gaze is on what Allah is bringing.

This is one of the most psychologically precise instructions in the Quran. Allah does not tell the Prophet to *not look at them*. He tells him to *look at something else*. Because a heart cannot be told simply to release. A heart can only be told to release **into** something else.

You cannot stop facing one direction unless you are given another direction to face.

The *iʿrāḍ* is the rotation away. The *intiẓār* is the rotation toward.

Together they form one motion. The Prophet ﷺ pivots. He turns his side from those who would not hear, and he turns his eyes toward the One who has promised that everything He has spoken will come to pass.

What this invites us to consider is the discipline of the prophetic gaze. He is not told to argue more. He is not told to plead more. He is not even told to pray for them more (at this moment, in this surah). He is told to **look up**. The work has been done. The verses have been delivered. Now keep your eyes on the horizon, because what is coming is coming, and it is coming on Allah's schedule, not theirs.

### Element Three: *Innahum Muntaẓirūn* — They Too Are Waiting

And now we arrive at the most stunning move of the ayah.

**إِنَّهُم مُّنتَظِرُونَ** — *innahum muntaẓirūn*. "Indeed, they too are waiting."

Allah has just commanded the Prophet ﷺ to be a *muntaẓir* — one who waits, one who watches the horizon. And now, in the same breath, He describes the enemies of the Prophet using the exact same word: *muntaẓirūn*. They are also watchers. They are also waiters.

The root is the same. The grammatical form is the same. *Muntaẓir* is the active participle from the same eighth-form verb. The Prophet is a *muntaẓir* (singular). They are *muntaẓirūn* (plural). Linguistically, both parties are doing the same thing.

[PAUSE — let that sit]

Why would Allah do this?

Let's slow down. Teach the concept first.

There is a literary device — not unique to Arabic but particularly cherished in classical Arabic rhetoric — called *mushākala*, which is when the same word is used for two opposing parties to reveal something. It is the rhetorical equivalent of putting two photographs side by side. The frame is identical. But what is inside the frame could not be more different.

Allah is putting the Prophet ﷺ and his enemies in the same frame. Both are waiting. Both have their eyes on a horizon. Both are watching for something to come.

But what are they waiting *for*?

The Prophet ﷺ is waiting for the promise of Allah. He is waiting for the help that has been guaranteed. He is waiting for the unfolding of what his Lord has already told him is coming. His waiting is built on certainty. His horizon has a sun on it that is rising.

They are waiting too. They are waiting for him to fail. They are waiting for him to be silenced. They are waiting for some accident of history to remove him. They are waiting — though they do not know it — for the very thing he is waiting for. Because the thing that is coming for him is help. The thing that is coming for them is consequence. **And it is the same event.**

Let's sit with this.

The Day of Judgment is one Day. The victory of the truth is one victory. The unfolding of Allah's promise is one unfolding. When it arrives, it arrives for everyone. But for the one whose face has been pointed toward it in trust, its arrival is the meeting of a beloved. For the one whose back has been turned to it in mockery, its arrival is the collapse of every excuse.

The Prophet ﷺ and his enemies are watching the same horizon.

[PAUSE]

Notice also a subtle thing about the verb form. The Prophet is given the *imperative* — *wantaẓir*. Wait. An instruction. A choice. He is *commanded* to wait, which means his waiting is an act of obedience and faith.

The enemies are described with the *active participle* — *muntaẓirūn*. They are already, by their nature, waiting. Their waiting is not commanded. It is simply what they are doing whether they know it or not. They imagine themselves to be in control, to be the actors, to be the ones with leverage. But Allah names them: you too are just *waiting*. You are not driving anything. You are not bringing about anything. You are standing on a piece of ground watching a horizon — exactly like the man you mock.

The difference is not whether you are waiting. Everyone is waiting. **The difference is what you are waiting for, and whether you know what is coming.**

There is a classical reading that points out a quiet asymmetry here. The Prophet is told to wait — *and his waiting is filled with content*. He has been given the news. He knows the promise. He has been told what the horizon holds. The deniers are described as waiters — *and their waiting is empty*. They do not know what is coming. They imagine they are waiting for something good for themselves. They are not. They are waiting and they do not know what for.

What this invites us to consider is the difference between **conscious waiting** and **unconscious waiting**. To be alive is to be a waiter. Every human being is on a horizon-watch. The question is whether you know what is on the horizon, and whether you have prepared for what is actually coming, or whether you are simply standing there imagining that whatever arrives will arrive in your favor.

### Bridging from Word to World

Let's pause and look at what these three words have given us.

*Aʿriḍ* — rotate your side away from those who will not hear.
*Wantaẓir* — fix your gaze on the horizon of Allah's promise.
*Innahum muntaẓirūn* — and know that they are doing the same action you are, but for the opposite outcome.

The whole surah closes on a single human posture: the believer turned, gaze fixed, watching. Six words. A complete spiritual orientation.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Discipline of Releasing What You Cannot Convert

Here is something every parent, every teacher, every spouse, every da'i, every human being who has ever tried to change someone knows in their bones.

There comes a moment in every relationship of unequal conviction where you reach the floor of what your words can do.

Imagine a parent who has tried, for years, to bring a child back to prayer. They have left books. They have left voice notes. They have left long, careful, loving texts. They have sat at the kitchen table and tried to explain. They have cried. They have apologized for their own failures. They have done everything they know how to do.

And the child has heard it all. And the child has chosen, for now, not to come.

What is left for that parent to do?

Most parents try one of two things. Either they keep pushing — and the pushing itself becomes the wedge that drives the child further. Or they collapse — they give up, they grow cold, they let the relationship become a series of empty meals and unanswered messages.

Both responses are forms of misery. And both are exactly what *fa-aʿriḍ wantaẓir* refuses.

The third way — the prophetic way — is to **rotate your side and lift your eyes**.

Stop facing the wall of their refusal. That is the *iʿrāḍ*. It is not coldness. It is not withdrawal of love. It is a recognition that you have done what you were sent to do. You have delivered the message. You have left the door open. The next move is not yours. The next move belongs to a Mover who is not you.

And then — lift your eyes. That is the *intiẓār*. Fix your gaze on the horizon where help comes from. Believe that the same Allah who softened your heart is capable of softening theirs. Believe that the time you cannot see is being measured by Him. Believe that the silence between you is not empty — it is a space He is working in.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the hardest disciplines in the spiritual life.

We are taught — rightly — to do our best. We are taught to call, to invite, to plead. But we are rarely taught what to do when the calling has reached its ceiling. And so we get stuck. We either rage against the unconverted heart or we abandon it. We either become bitter dā'is or we become silent spectators of our own loved ones drifting.

Allah, at the end of As-Sajdah, gives the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, gives us — a third option.

You are not the converter. You are the messenger.

When you have delivered the message, you have done your work. The conversion belongs to the One who turns hearts. And the only sane posture, having done your work, is to release the outcome to the One who owns it, and to keep your eyes on the horizon He has promised.

**The application is concrete.** Think of the one person in your life whose heart you have been trying to move. The estranged sibling. The drifting friend. The parent who will not listen. The spouse who will not soften. The child who has chosen another road.

You have spoken to them. You have prayed for them. You have done what you could.

Tonight, after isha, can you do *fa-aʿriḍ wantaẓir*? Can you rotate your side from the wall of their refusal — not from love of them, but from the wall — and lift your eyes? Can you say: *Ya Allah, I have done what I can. I release them to You. I will keep my gaze on the horizon where Your help comes from. I will not let my heart curdle in the waiting. I will wait the way the Prophet ﷺ was told to wait — with my eyes lifted.*

That is the first thing this ayah is teaching.

### Theme Two: Everyone Is Waiting — The Only Question Is For What

Now let's go to the second theme, which is bigger and stranger and more universal.

*Innahum muntaẓirūn.* They too are waiting.

Here is a claim about reality that this ayah is quietly making: **every human being is in a state of waiting, whether they know it or not.**

The most arrogant disbeliever in Makkah, sneering at the Prophet ﷺ, mocking the promise of resurrection — Allah calls him a *muntaẓir*. A waiter. Even his mockery is a form of waiting. Even his certainty that nothing is coming is itself a posture of someone standing on a horizon, watching.

This is not metaphor. It is metaphysics.

Time is a corridor. We are all walking down it. None of us is stationary. None of us is the cause of our own arrival. Every breath we take is a breath that is bringing us closer to something we did not choose and cannot stop. The question is not *whether* you are waiting. You are. The question is: **what are you waiting for, and have you faced the right direction?**

Think of two people on a train.

One has bought a ticket. They know the destination. They have packed for it. They have a friend waiting at the other end. They are reading a book, perhaps, or looking out the window, but underneath everything they do, there is a quiet certainty: *I am being carried to a place where I am known, where I am expected, where I belong.*

The other has wandered onto the train without knowing where it goes. They have not bought a ticket. They have no idea what city is at the end of the tracks. They are loud, perhaps, or distracting themselves, or laughing too much, because the truth — that they do not know where they are going — would be unbearable to face. But the train is moving exactly as fast for them as for the first passenger.

Both are travelers. Both are arriving. The train does not care which one bought a ticket.

[PAUSE]

This is what *innahum muntaẓirūn* is saying.

The disbelievers of Makkah did not think of themselves as waiters. They thought of themselves as actors. They thought they were *doing* something — running their economy, protecting their idols, plotting against the Prophet, managing their reputations. They thought of the Prophet ﷺ as the one who was waiting — waiting for help that would never come, waiting for a promise that was a fantasy.

Allah inverts the picture in one word.

You think you are the active ones. You are not. You are also standing on the platform. You are also watching the horizon. The Day is coming for you exactly as it is coming for him. The only difference is that he sees the dawn rising and you think the darkness will last forever.

**The deepest application of this theme is self-diagnostic.**

Ask yourself, honestly: what am I actually waiting for?

Not what I say I am waiting for. Not what I post on social media that I am waiting for. What does my heart, when no one is watching, actually fix its gaze on?

Some of us are waiting for a promotion. Some are waiting for a marriage. Some are waiting for the moment our parents finally approve. Some are waiting for our children to grow up so we can rest. Some are waiting for a body that does not hurt. Some are waiting, secretly, for someone we love to die so that an inheritance comes or a chapter closes.

These are all human waitings. They are not necessarily evil. But notice — every one of them is *finite*. Every one of them is on a horizon that ends inside this life.

The Prophet ﷺ was told to wait for something else. He was told to wait for what is on the other side of every other horizon. He was told to keep his gaze fixed past the marriage, past the inheritance, past the promotion, past the death, past everything that this world can deliver — onto the moment when Allah's promise meets His messenger.

The disbelievers were also waiting. But every horizon they fixed their eyes on was a horizon that would shatter on the Day. The wealth was waiting to be taken. The reputation was waiting to be exposed. The power was waiting to be transferred. They were waiting for things that the Day itself would consume.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question the ayah asks of every listener, and we should not rush past it:

**If everyone is waiting, what is the horizon you are facing?**

If your heart is fixed on something that the Day will swallow, then you are facing the wrong direction even if you do not know it. The Day will come and your horizon will collapse under its own weight.

If your heart is fixed on what Allah has promised, then no event in this world — no betrayal, no loss, no humiliation, no slow public defeat — can move your horizon. Because what you are watching for is on the other side of all of it.

**The practical application:** before sleep tonight, ask yourself one question and answer it with brutal honesty: *what is the thing I most desperately want to arrive?* Whatever that thing is, ask: *is it on a horizon that the Day will preserve, or one the Day will destroy?* If you find your gaze fixed on the latter — and most of us will, most of the time — then this ayah is giving you the smallest, gentlest correction. Rotate. Lift. Wait the way the Prophet ﷺ was told to wait. Your horizon was always too small.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Six Words

Now that we have done the work, look at the structure.

*Fa-aʿriḍ ʿanhum* — turn from them.
*Wantaẓir* — and wait.
*Innahum muntaẓirūn* — indeed, they too are waiting.

Notice the architecture. The middle word — *wantaẓir* — is mirrored in the final word — *muntaẓirūn*. Same root. Same form-family. The Prophet's verb is doubled in the description of his enemies. The ayah forms a kind of arch: a turning-away on one side (*aʿriḍ*), a turning-away on the other (the implicit silence of *innahum* — they are not even named, just referred to as "them"), and in the center, the verb of waiting binding both parties together.

The Prophet ﷺ stands inside the verse the way he stands inside history — turned from the noise, eyes lifted, watching. The enemies are placed *around* him grammatically, mentioned only as a *them* and a *they*, depersonalized, fading into the background of a horizon they think they are running but are actually just standing on.

The whole surah, which is called *The Prostration*, ends with this image: a single believer in a posture of patient watching, while around him a crowd of mockers stands waiting too — for the same event, with opposite outcomes.

That is not a coincidence of word choice. That is the shape of the world the surah has been describing for thirty ayahs.

### Bringing It Together

Our linguistic work and our thematic work were the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic insight: *aʿriḍ* is rotation away, *intaẓir* is rotation toward, and the same waiting-verb describes both the believer and the denier — because both are doing the same physical action with opposite spiritual content.

The thematic insight: every human being is waiting, and the spiritual life is not about whether you wait but about what you face. The believer is taught to release what cannot be converted and to lift the eyes. The denier is exposed as a waiter who does not know what is coming.

The linguistic structure *is* the spiritual structure. Allah did not just describe these realities — He encoded them in the grammar.

### Questions to Carry

1. Is there a person in my life — a child, a parent, a friend, a spouse — whom I am still trying to *face* even though I have done all the speaking I can do? What would *fa-aʿriḍ wantaẓir* look like with them, this week?

2. What am I actually waiting for? Not the answer I would give in public — the answer my heart gives in the dark. Is that thing on a horizon the Day will preserve, or one the Day will destroy?

3. When I feel defeated in calling someone to good — when I feel my words are bouncing off them — do I rage, or collapse, or lift? Which posture does my body know best?

4. The Prophet ﷺ was given six words at the end of a long surah. What would it mean for me, at the end of a long day of trying to be heard, to close with the same six words?

5. The deniers were called *muntaẓirūn* — waiters who did not know what they were waiting for. Am I one of them in any quiet corner of my life? Where am I waiting without knowing what is actually coming?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> When you have delivered the message and they will not hear, turn your side from them, lift your eyes to the horizon Allah has promised, and know that they are watching the same horizon — they just do not know what is rising on it.

### The Closing Invitation

Surah As-Sajdah is the surah of falling — the surah of those who, when reminded of Allah's verses, fall prostrate before Him. It is a surah about which way the body goes when the truth lands. Some bodies fall in sujood. Some bodies stiffen in pride.

And the surah closes with a final body — the body of the Prophet ﷺ, neither falling nor stiffening, but **standing**. Standing with his side turned from the deaf crowd. Standing with his eyes lifted to the horizon. Standing in a posture so small it could fit in six words.

That is the posture this ayah is inviting you into.

Not to argue better. Not to grieve harder. Not to abandon. Not to rage.

To rotate. To lift. To wait the way a watchman waits — knowing exactly what is coming, exactly Who has promised it, and exactly which direction to face while the world around you stands waiting too, for the same dawn, without knowing.

### Brief Du'a

> *Ya Allah, teach us the discipline of turning our side from what we cannot move, without our hearts curdling in the turning. Lift our eyes, when we have spoken all we can speak, to the horizon You have promised. And when we find ourselves waiting — and we are always waiting — let our waiting be the waiting of the Prophet ﷺ at the end of As-Sajdah: turned, lifted, watching for You. Amin.*
