# Sides That Forsake the Bed

*On the Gravity That Beats Sleep*

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you slowly. In Arabic first.

**تَتَجَافَىٰ جُنُوبُهُمْ عَنِ ٱلْمَضَاجِعِ**
*Tatajāfā junūbuhum ʿani-l-maḍājiʿ*

**يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُمْ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا**
*Yadʿūna rabbahum khawfan wa-ṭamaʿā*

**وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنفِقُونَ**
*Wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn*

**"Their sides forsake their beds, calling upon their Lord in fear and hope, and from what We have provided them they spend."**

[PAUSE]

Before we step inside this verse, I want you to sit with where it lives.

Surah As-Sajdah is a short surah. It moves fast. By the time we arrive at this ayah, Allah has done something specific. In the verse just before — 32:15 — He describes those who, when reminded of His signs, fall down in prostration. They worship in full daylight, in the open, where the community sees them. The mosque. The congregation. The qibla.

And then He does something remarkable. He doesn't leave the camera there. He follows them home.

He follows them past the public worship, past the witnesses, past the social ritual. He follows them into their own houses. Into their own bedrooms. Into the hours when no one is watching. And He shows us what happens there.

This is the verse that no neighbor sees. This is the verse that no co-worker sees. This is the verse that not even the person's own family always sees. It is the verse of what happens between a soul and its Lord when the lights go off and the world goes to sleep.

And then — and you need to know this before we begin — what comes immediately *after* this ayah is one of the most stunning promises in the entire Quran. **"So no soul knows what has been hidden for them of joys of the eyes, as a recompense for what they used to do."** [32:17]

[PAUSE]

The hidden joy comes after the hidden worship. The reward that no soul has ever imagined comes after the action that no soul ever witnesses. There is a relationship between those two ayahs that this entire reflection is going to be about.

But here's the question I want to leave hanging over us — because we have to earn the answer.

Why does Allah, in describing the people whose night life is this beautiful, **not** say "they get up from their beds"? Why does He describe their *sides* doing the leaving? Why does the agency in this image belong to the body parts, not the person?

That choice is going to crack open the entire ayah. Stay with me.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tatajāfā* — The Word for "Forsake"

I want to teach you a piece of Arabic grammar before we open the word, because without it, the word stays closed.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns — what scholars call *abwāb* or "forms." Form I is the basic, default verb. *Kataba* — "he wrote." That's just the action. Plain. But Form II intensifies. Form III implies someone *with* you. Form IV is causative — making something happen to someone else. And Form VI — the one we're about to meet — is special.

Form VI carries the sense of **sustained, reciprocal, or strained action**. Something that doesn't happen in one motion. Something that has to be enacted, kept up, pulled against resistance.

Here's an everyday way to feel it. The Form I verb *baʿuda* simply means "to be far." But the Form VI *tabāʿada* means "to keep oneself far from something pulling you back" — a sustained distancing, almost a wrestling away.

Hold that in your mind. **A wrestling away.**

Now look at the word.

**تَتَجَافَىٰ** — *tatajāfā*.

This is Form VI of the root **ج-ف-و** (j-f-w). The root itself carries the meaning of *aversion, estrangement, withdrawal, a turning away.* Lexicographers describe it as a kind of dryness — the way two things that should be close pull apart and there is space between them where there used to be contact.

So Allah doesn't use Form I. He doesn't say "their sides leave their beds" — a single quick motion. He uses Form VI. He uses the sustained, effortful, wrestling-away form.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

The image is no longer "they got up." The image is a body in tension with a bed. A body pulling. A bed holding. And the verb is in the present tense — *yatajāfā*, ongoing — meaning this is not a one-time event. This is a recurring posture. This is what these people *do*, night after night.

But notice something even more striking. Who is the grammatical subject of this verb?

It is not **they.** It is not **the worshippers.**

The subject is **junūbuhum** — *their sides.* Their flanks. The part of the body that touches the mattress. The grammar gives the agency to the **side**, not the person.

This is what I asked you to hold from the introduction.

Allah is not saying "they pull themselves out of bed." He is saying **their sides pull away.** The flank, of its own seeming agency, is the actor.

Why?

Here's what this construction invites us to see. There comes a moment — for someone whose connection to Allah has reached a certain depth — when the leaving of the bed is no longer a willed cognitive struggle every single time. It becomes something more like a magnetic property of the body itself. The side knows. The side is unable to stay. The flank, having learned the weight of those nights, lifts on its own.

Imam al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* notes that *tajāfī* implies one thing being too constrained, too uncomfortable, in the place where it sits — so it lifts. Like a hot coal that won't lie still on a surface. There is a quality of *not fitting* in the place of comfort. The bed becomes too small to contain a soul that has tasted something larger.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The verse does not romanticize this. It is not a heroic image. There is **strain** in this word. *Tatajāfā* — the very sound of it. The dental repetition, *ta-ta-jā-fā*, like an effortful wrenching. The sides do not glide off. They have to *come unstuck*.

So picture this. The bed has gravity. Allah knows. He created the bed. He created the body that aches. He created the night that wraps around you like a blanket. And He is not pretending this is easy. He is saying: **in those whose sides have learned to come unstuck, here is what is happening.**

There is one more layer. The word for "beds" — **al-maḍājiʿ** — comes from the root **ض-ج-ع**, which means *to lie down on one's side.* It is the same body-position language as **junūb** (sides). So the verse is doing something almost poetic: it pairs **sides** with **places-of-lying-on-the-side.** The body and the bed share the same vocabulary. They are made for each other. The fit is perfect. And yet — the sides come unstuck anyway.

What this single grammatical choice reveals: **the leaving of the bed for the sake of Allah is not described as a triumph of mind over body — it is described as a transformation of the body itself.** The flesh has been taught a new gravity.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Khawfan wa Ṭamaʿan* — Fear and Yearning, Bare

Now stay with me, because the next word does something even stranger.

What are these people doing once they've come unstuck from their beds?

**يَدْعُونَ رَبَّهُمْ** — *yadʿūna rabbahum* — "they are calling upon their Lord."

That phrase by itself would be enough. They wake up. They call on Allah. We could move on.

But Allah does not move on. He gives us two more words. Two states. Two engines that are driving the call.

**خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا** — *khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan* — "in fear and in yearning."

I need to teach you something about Arabic grammar here, because if I don't, you will miss what is missing.

In Arabic, when you fear or hope or long for something, the language wants an object. *I feared* — feared what? *I hoped* — hoped for what? The verb wants completion.

Even the noun forms typically take a complement. *Khawfun min* — fear of. *Ṭamaʿun fī* — longing for. The mind expects the second half.

But Allah does not give us the second half.

He says: **fearing. Yearning.** And He stops.

[PAUSE]

Notice this. He does not say "fearing His punishment and yearning for His Paradise" — though that is theologically true. He does not say "fearing the Fire and longing for the Garden." He does not specify *what* they fear or *what* they long for.

He leaves the objects empty.

The classical commentators — al-Ṭabarī, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī — all noticed this opening. Some say: the fear is of His punishment and the yearning is for His reward. Some say: the fear is of being far from Him and the yearning is to be near Him. Some say: the fear is of His displeasure and the yearning is for His face.

Don't choose between these. Each of these is a camera angle on the same reality.

What Allah has done by leaving the objects unspecified is invite **every fear and every yearning** of a heart that has come awake into this call. The believer in the dark hour is not running a clean theological algorithm. The believer is a *full* heart pressed against its Lord. Whatever it fears — He receives. Whatever it yearns for — He receives.

The empty space after *khawfan* and *ṭamaʿan* is not a deficit. It is room. Room for everything the worshipper has carried into the night.

Here's what's also remarkable. Look at how the word *ṭamaʿ* works.

*Ṭamaʿ* is a stronger word than the English "hope." In other contexts in the Quran, the same root describes a kind of *eager longing* — even appears in negative contexts where someone covets what they shouldn't. It is hope with appetite. Hope that *wants*. Hope that leans forward with the whole body.

So the worshipper at night is not politely hoping. The worshipper is **leaning into Allah with appetite.** The body that just had to wrench itself away from a bed is now *aching toward* its Lord with the same intensity in the opposite direction.

[PAUSE]

There's a pairing here that I want you to notice. *Khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan* — fear and yearning, in equal measure. Not fear alone. Not yearning alone. Both. Together. In the same call.

The scholars of the heart — al-Ghazālī especially — describe these as the two wings of a bird. With only one wing, the soul cannot fly to Allah. Pure fear without hope becomes despair. Pure hope without fear becomes presumption. The Quranic worshipper, in this single ayah, is described as having both engines firing in the dark.

And both engines were *needed* to leave the bed. Notice the order. The body forsakes the bed *and then* calls in fear and hope. The fear and hope are what the body lifts itself toward. The bed has its gravity. The fear and yearning toward Allah have a counter-gravity. The verse is showing us, mechanically, *what kind of inner state actually lifts a body out of comfort.*

Not duty alone. Not guilt. Not habit. **Two specific, paired emotions** — being afraid of distance from Allah, and being hungry for nearness to Him.

What this reveals: **the engine of qiyām al-layl is not discipline. It is desire and dread. Discipline is the residue of those two emotions doing their work over time.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Mimmā Razaqnāhum* — The Provision Allah Names as His Own

The third linguistic element is small but does something enormous.

**وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَاهُمْ يُنفِقُونَ** — "and from what **We have provided them** they spend."

Two things happen in this clause that you would miss without slowing down.

The first: notice the **shift in pronoun.** Up until this point in the verse, Allah is talking *about* the worshippers in the third person — *their sides, their beds, they call.* And He calls Himself *rabbahum* — *their Lord.* He is the spoken-of God.

But suddenly, in this last clause, Allah is no longer being described. Allah is speaking as Himself. *Razaqnāhum* — *We have provided them.* The royal "We." The first person plural of divine speech. Allah is no longer the topic. Allah is the speaker.

In Arabic rhetoric, this is called *iltifāt* — *the turn.* It is when the grammatical perspective suddenly shifts, and the listener is made to feel the divine voice coming closer or pulling back. Here, Allah does it specifically at the moment of mentioning provision. As if to say: *do not lose sight of where the wealth came from. I am the one who gave it. Hear it in My voice.*

The second thing: the word **mimmā** — *from what.* This is **mim** of partition — *some of, a portion of.* Allah does not say *kulla mā razaqnāhum* — *all that We provided them.* He says **mimmā** — *from what We provided them.* From a portion. Some.

And He does not specify the amount.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this absence.

How much should the believer spend? How much is *enough*? The verse does not say. There is no percentage. There is no minimum. There is only **mimmā** — from what We gave them. The amount is left to the heart that already woke itself up at night out of fear and yearning. That heart, Allah trusts, will know.

And notice the order. First, the night prayer. Then, the spending.

This is not accidental. The verse is telling you the *direction of the flow.* What is received in the dark hour gets distributed in the daylight hour. The vertical worship — the call to the Lord — produces a horizontal worship — the open hand to creation. They are not two separate piles of good deeds. They are **one current** moving through the worshipper. He receives from above and pours out below.

And the word *yunfiqūn* itself — Form IV of **ن-ف-ق**, which in its base form means *to be used up, to be exhausted.* In Form IV, *anfaqa* means *to make something flow out, to spend.* The root carries the sense of something that does not stay. Wealth, in the Quranic worldview, is not meant to pool. It is meant to *flow.* The night vigil opens the channel; the spending is the water that comes through.

What this reveals: **the worshipper described in this ayah is not measured by what he keeps. He is measured by what he refuses to keep — both his sleep at night, and his wealth by day.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have stepped through three linguistic doors.

One: the body itself, taught a new gravity, coming unstuck from the bed.

Two: the call in the dark, powered by fear and yearning with no specified objects — meaning every fear, every longing of the awakened heart.

Three: the provision named in Allah's own voice, given in unspecified measure to be poured out as needed.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Bed Has Gravity — On the Physics of the Lower Self

I want to start this section not in the verse, but in your bedroom.

It is 3:47 AM. The alarm has not yet gone off. But something in you stirred. You opened your eyes. The room is dark. The blanket is heavy in exactly the right way. The pillow has formed itself to the shape of your head. The temperature is perfect. Your body is in that suspended state where every limb has found its rest. And in fifteen minutes, the call to prayer will begin — or, depending on the time of year, the time for tahajjud is right now.

Notice what happens in your body when you imagine this.

Your sides are heavy. Your sides do not want to move. The bed has gravity. **It has actual, physical gravity.** It is pulling on you. There is something almost magnetic about a warm bed at 4 AM. The longer you lie there, the stronger the pull becomes. Every minute you wait, the harder it gets to leave.

This is not weakness of faith. This is **physics of the nafs.** This is the way the lower self works.

The Quran is not naïve about this. Allah created the body. Allah created the bed. Allah created the night and the cold and the warmth and the weight. He is not asking you to pretend it is easy. He is asking you to know there are people whose bodies have learned to leave anyway — and to know what their inner state was when they leaned out.

[PAUSE]

Think about gravity in physics for a moment. Gravity is not something you can argue with. You don't talk gravity out of pulling on you. Gravity is just what mass *does* to space. The bigger the mass, the stronger the pull.

The lower self has its own version of this. Comfort, when you have lived inside it long enough, accumulates mass. Every night you sleep through, every prayer you delayed, every soft thing you reached for — adds mass. The pull gets stronger over time. This is why the longer someone has been distant from the night, the harder the night becomes.

But the verse is telling you something extraordinary about the believer it describes. **Allah did not change the gravity of the bed.** The bed still pulls. The body still resists. The leaving still requires *tajāfī* — that strained, effortful, sustained pulling-away. The grammar is honest. The Form VI is not glossed over.

What changed is that **a second gravity entered the room.** The gravity of *khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan.* Fear of distance from Allah, and yearning for nearness to Him. And that second gravity, in these people, became the stronger of the two.

This is the entire teaching of qiyām al-layl in three Arabic words.

You do not defeat the gravity of the bed by willpower. You defeat it by **growing a competing gravity.** And the competing gravity is grown the same way mass is grown — by accumulation. Every night you actually rise, the second gravity gains mass. Every fear you feel toward distance from Allah, the second gravity gains mass. Every longing you feel toward His face, the second gravity gains mass.

Until one night, the second gravity is heavier than the first — and your sides, on their own, **come unstuck.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what this reframes about the spiritual life. We tend to think: *I am bad at waking up for prayer because I lack discipline.* The verse is saying: *you are not waking up for prayer because the only gravity you have built is the gravity of comfort.* The cure is not more discipline. The cure is **a heart that has learned to fear and to long.**

Where does that heart come from? It comes from *exposure.* It comes from reading the Quran enough that the Day of Judgment becomes vivid. It comes from sitting with descriptions of the Garden until your soul aches for what is there. It comes from contemplating the names of Allah — *al-Wadūd, the Loving; al-Quddūs, the Holy; al-ʿAfuww, the Pardoning* — until those names create their own pull in your chest.

You do not get up at night because you are strong. You get up at night because you have become *full of something else.*

And this is why the verse describes the *sides* as the agent. Because by the time someone has lived inside fear and yearning long enough, the body itself is renovated. The body becomes a body that cannot stay. The flank that lay flat for so many years finds itself rising before the mind has fully chosen to rise.

What does this change about tomorrow?

If you want to rise for tahajjud, do not start by setting an alarm. Start by feeding the second gravity. Read one ayah about the Garden before you sleep. Read one ayah about the Fire before you sleep. Sit with one name of Allah for two minutes before you sleep. **Build mass in the right direction.** The alarm becomes irrelevant when the gravity has been transferred.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Two Worships That Must Travel Together

I want to open this second theme with something you have probably noticed in your own life.

You have met people who pray a lot but are stingy. And you have met people who are generous but never pray. And in both cases, something feels off. Something is incomplete. There is a half-life to each posture — a worship that has not become whole.

The Quran, again and again, refuses to let these two come apart. *Aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāta wa-ātū-z-zakāh* — *establish the prayer and give the zakāh.* They appear together so often that we stop noticing the pairing. But the pairing is the whole point. **There is no Quranic believer whose worship goes only upward, and there is no Quranic believer whose generosity goes only outward.** The real worshipper has both directions firing.

And this single ayah — 32:16 — is one of the most beautiful places where this pairing appears, because of how *physical* the pairing is.

Look at the architecture of the verse.

The first clause: *their sides forsake their beds.* That's the body, vertically, lifting itself out of comfort *toward* Allah.

The second clause: *calling upon their Lord in fear and yearning.* That's the voice, vertically, reaching up *toward* Allah.

The third clause: *and from what We have provided them they spend.* That's the hand, horizontally, opening *toward* creation.

[PAUSE]

The body rises vertically. The voice reaches vertically. The hand opens horizontally. The verse is a cross. A plus sign. A four-directional motion of the soul.

And the verse is telling you these are not three separate good deeds. They are **one motion.** They flow into each other. The body that learned to rise at night is the same body that, by day, cannot hold tightly to wealth. The voice that learned to cry out in the dark is the same voice that, by day, cannot say *no* to the one in need. The vertical worship literally *opens* the horizontal worship. The horizontal worship literally *validates* the vertical worship.

Here is the danger if you separate them.

Vertical worship without horizontal worship becomes a private spiritual luxury — *I and my Lord, in my room, and the world can fend for itself.* The Quran has hard words for this. *Wa lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi-l-miskīn* — *and he does not urge the feeding of the poor* — is described as a hallmark of one who *denies the Day of Judgment.* Severe.

Horizontal worship without vertical worship becomes hollow philanthropy — generosity without source. The hand opens but the heart never filled. And eventually, that hand closes again, because there is no spring feeding it. You cannot pour from a well that you never visit.

But put them together — vertical and horizontal, qiyām and infāq, the call and the giving — and you have something that the Quran calls a *muḥsin*, a *muttaqī*, a *ṣiddīq.* A complete person.

[PAUSE]

Now go back to the grammar.

Remember *mimmā* — *from* what We provided. Some, not all. Why does this matter so much here?

Because the believer who has been at qiyām al-layl is not *forced* into generosity. He is **freed** into it. The night vigil has put his wealth in perspective. He has stood before the One who provided it. He has felt his own smallness next to the Provider. By the time the morning comes and a brother asks for help, the calculation has already been done in his heart. The wealth, he has already given back to Allah in his soul. Now it just needs to flow through his hand.

This is why *infāq* in the Quran is so rarely paired with anxiety. The believers in this verse are not described as worried about their wealth running out. They have stood at night. They know Who provides. The same hand that closed around the blanket has learned to open, and the same hand that learned to lift from the bed has learned to lift toward another person's need.

What does this change about tomorrow?

Two practical movements.

First, if you find your generosity is shrinking — your patience for charity, your willingness to spend on others, your softness toward the poor — check your nights. Check your time with Allah in private. Generosity is downstream of intimacy with the Provider. You cannot squeeze it from a dry well.

Second, if you find your night worship is becoming spiritually heavy in a self-centered way — making you proud, making you withdraw, making you judge others — check your hand. Check who you fed this week. Check what your wealth actually did. **The night worship that does not flow into the day's giving has somewhere lost its current.**

The verse is telling you: these two must travel together, or neither one is what it appears to be.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now I want to show you something you couldn't have seen at the beginning.

The verse has three movements. Read it again in your head: *Their sides forsake their beds, calling upon their Lord in fear and hope, and from what We have provided them they spend.*

The first movement — the leaving of the bed — is about the **body.** The wrestling, the strain, the physical break with comfort.

The second movement — the call to the Lord — is about the **heart.** Fear and yearning. The two engines of the inner life.

The third movement — the spending — is about the **hand.** What goes out into the world.

**Body. Heart. Hand.**

The verse is a complete portrait of a worshipper. Not a body without a heart. Not a heart without a hand. All three, in sequence, in one current. The body that has been retrained, feeding a heart that has been awakened, expressing through a hand that has been opened.

And it is no coincidence that the very next ayah — 32:17 — promises a reward that *no soul knows.* Because what this ayah describes is, itself, a worship that almost no soul *sees.* The body coming unstuck at 3 AM has no audience. The fear and yearning in the dark has no audience. Even the giving, when done in the manner the Quran prefers, has no audience. The whole life is *hidden.* So the reward is *hidden* to match. The currency of unseen worship is paid in unseen joy.

### How the Pieces Fit Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The Form VI of *tatajāfā* — the wrestling-away — is the linguistic shape of *the bed has gravity.* Allah honored the difficulty by choosing that form. The grammar admits what we feel.

The empty objects after *khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan* — the unspecified fear and unspecified yearning — is the linguistic shape of *whatever a heart carries in the dark is received.* The grammar makes room for everything.

The shift to *razaqnāhum* — the divine first-person — is the linguistic shape of *the worshipper has been with the Provider, and the wealth flows from a known Source.* The grammar reminds you Whose hand fills yours.

The whole verse is, syntactically, what it describes spiritually: **a current that begins in the body and travels through the heart and exits through the hand, with Allah named as the Source in the middle of the motion.**

### Questions to Carry

I want to leave you with questions, not answers.

1. What is the actual gravity of my bed at 4 AM — and what mass have I built on the other side of the scale?

2. When I imagine *khawfan wa-ṭamaʿan* — fear and yearning toward Allah — do I feel anything in my chest? Or are those two words abstractions to me? What would it take to make them physical?

3. Is there a wall between my vertical worship and my horizontal generosity? Do they flow into each other, or do they live in two separate accounts of my life?

4. If Allah followed me home tonight — past the public worship, past the daytime persona — what would the camera see at 3 AM?

5. What is the reward 32:17 describes that I cannot even imagine? And is my life right now oriented toward earning it, or oriented toward what I *can* imagine?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**When the gravity of fear and yearning toward Allah grows heavier than the gravity of the bed, a body comes unstuck on its own — and the wealth that flowed in during the dark hour flows out as generosity in the daylight.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to wake up tomorrow at 3 AM.

But you do have to begin growing the second gravity.

Tonight, before you sleep, do one thing. Read one ayah slowly. Sit with one name of Allah. Picture one scene from the Day of Judgment. Picture one scene from the Garden. **Feed the second mass.** Let it gain a gram of weight tonight, and another gram tomorrow, and another the night after.

One day — not by force, not by alarm, not by guilt — your side will come unstuck on its own. And when it does, you will understand this verse from the inside.

And then go look at your wealth, and ask which portion of it is still in your grip out of fear of losing it — because that grip is the daylight test of the night you just had.

### Du'a

O Allah, do not let our sides love the maḍājiʿ more than they love standing before You. O Allah, make our hands light with what You have given us, and our nights heavy with calling on You, so that we may be among those whose reward You have hidden where no eye has seen.*

*Āmīn.*
