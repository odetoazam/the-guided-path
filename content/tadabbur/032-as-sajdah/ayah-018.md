# The Question That Refuses to Let You Sleep

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> أَفَمَن كَانَ مُؤْمِنًا كَمَن كَانَ فَاسِقًا ۚ لَّا يَسْتَوُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Afaman kāna mu'minan kaman kāna fāsiqā. Lā yastawūn.

**Translation:**
> "Is then the one who is a believer like the one who is a defiantly disobedient? They are not equal."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

To understand what happens in this ayah, you have to feel where the surah has just been.

Surah As-Sajdah opens at cosmic scale. Allah speaks of the heavens and the earth and what is between them — created in six days. He speaks of His command descending from heaven to earth and ascending back to Him on a day whose measure is a thousand years. He speaks of the human being — created from clay, then from a despised fluid, then shaped, then given hearing and sight and hearts.

And then the surah turns to a moment in the night.

It describes a particular kind of person: **تَتَجَافَىٰ جُنُوبُهُمْ عَنِ الْمَضَاجِعِ** — *their sides forsake their beds.* The verb is gentle and difficult at once. *Tatajāfā* — to pull away, to lift off, to peel back. The bed is warm. The body is heavy. And yet something in them lifts. They get up. They call upon their Lord in fear and in hope. They spend from what He has given them.

And then Allah says something staggering. **فَلَا تَعْلَمُ نَفْسٌ مَّآ أُخْفِيَ لَهُم مِّن قُرَّةِ أَعْيُنٍ** — *no soul knows what has been hidden for them of coolness of eyes.* A reward so vast and so private that no soul — not even the soul that earned it — knows what awaits.

Now hold that picture. Someone in the dark. Lifting their side from the bed. Whispering. Hidden. And Allah is preparing them for a reward no one on earth can imagine.

[PAUSE]

And then — immediately, with no warning — comes our ayah.

### The Hook

Allah asks a question. Not a statement. A question.

He does not say *the believer is better than the defiantly disobedient.* He does not announce a verdict. He turns to you, the listener, and He asks:

**Are they the same?**

Why? Why does the surah — at its very hinge — refuse to tell you the answer and instead demand you say it yourself?

Stay with that. Because what we're about to discover is that the form of the question is doing more work than the content. The way Allah asks this is the entire point.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Is Not a Question

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the ayah.

In English, when we say *"Is the rich man like the poor man?"* — that's a real question. The person could answer yes, or no, or "it depends." It's an open inquiry.

But Arabic has a rhetorical form called **al-istifhām al-inkārī** — the interrogative of denial. It looks like a question. It sounds like a question. But it is actually a denial dressed as a question. The questioner is not asking to find out. The questioner is asking because they want *you* to admit something you already know.

It's the difference between a teacher asking *"What's two plus two?"* — and a teacher asking *"You don't think two plus two equals five, do you?"* The second one isn't really seeking information. It's forcing you to recognize what you already know.

Now look at the ayah:

**أَفَمَن كَانَ مُؤْمِنًا كَمَن كَانَ فَاسِقًا**

The **ا** at the beginning — that's the hamza of istifhām, the interrogative particle. Followed by **فَ** — *fa* — *then.* "Is then..."

This is the istifhām al-inkārī. Allah is not asking because He doesn't know. He is asking because **you** need to say it.

Notice something. Allah could have said directly: *the believer is not like the defiantly disobedient.* That would have been a statement. You could nod along. You could agree intellectually. You could move on.

But Allah does not let you nod and move on. He turns the sentence into a question. And a question — a question demands an answer.

Here's what's remarkable. The moment Allah phrases this as a question, **you are no longer a spectator.** The ayah has reached out and grabbed you by the collar. You cannot read it without answering it. And the answer you give — silently, in your own chest — is the answer Allah was waiting for.

[PAUSE]

This is what istifhām al-inkārī does. It transfers the weight of the verdict from Allah's tongue to yours. He could have ruled. Instead, He asks — so that the ruling comes out of you.

And then, in case you tried to remain neutral, in case you tried to shrug — Allah closes the door. He answers His own question:

**لَّا يَسْتَوُونَ** — *they are not equal.*

Two words. Final. He let you answer first. Then He sealed it.

### Element Two: The Verb That Names the Crime

Now look at the two words being contrasted. **مُؤْمِنًا** — believer. **فَاسِقًا** — *fāsiq.*

We need to slow down on *fāsiq*. Because in our heads we've translated it into something soft. *Disobedient.* *Sinful.* *Wrongdoer.* But these translations have lost the texture of the original word.

The root **ف-س-ق** in classical Arabic was used to describe **a date that has broken out of its skin.** When the date matures and its outer skin cracks and the flesh bursts through — the Arabs said *fasaqat at-tamrah.* The date has *fasaqa-ed.*

Let that image sit with you.

It is not the date that simply fell off the tree. It is not the date that was eaten. It is the date that **broke its own covering.** Burst out of the boundary that was meant to contain it.

When the Quran applies this word to a human being, it is naming something specific. The *fāsiq* is not merely someone who slipped. The *fāsiq* is someone who **broke out of the covering of the command.** Someone who recognized the boundary and ruptured it. Someone whose inner desire grew so large that it split the skin that was meant to hold it.

Now feel the choice of word against the choice of its opposite.

The opposite of *fāsiq* in this ayah is not *muṭīʿ* — the obedient. It is not *ʿābid* — the worshipper. It is **mu'min** — the believer.

This is significant. The root **ʾ-m-n** carries the meaning of *security, safety, trust, settling.* The *mu'min* is the one who has found stability inside the boundary. Who has placed trust and rests within it. The *fāsiq* is the one who has burst through that same boundary.

So Allah is not contrasting two behaviors. He is contrasting two **postures of the self toward limit.**

One settles inside the limit and finds peace there.
One bursts through the limit and calls it freedom.

These are not just different actions. These are different orientations of the entire human being.

[PAUSE]

And here is where we have to be honest about the modern world. Our entire culture has elevated the second posture and called it growth. *Break the rules. Burst through the boundary. The skin that contains you is the obstacle to your becoming.*

The Quran is using the same image. The date that bursts its skin. And the Quran is saying: **that is the picture of moral collapse, not moral arrival.** The date that bursts its skin is the date that has begun to rot. The skin was protecting it. The boundary was the very thing keeping it whole.

The *fāsiq* is the human being who tore off the very thing that was keeping them whole.

### Element Three: The Verb at the End — *Lā Yastawūn*

Now look at how Allah closes the question. Two words.

**لَّا يَسْتَوُونَ** — *lā yastawūn.* "They are not equal."

The root **س-و-ي** means *to be even, level, equal, equivalent.* But notice the form of the verb. It is not *lā yatasāwayān* — using a different morphological pattern. It is **yastawūn** — Form VIII (iftiʿāl), the reflexive form, with the plural ending.

Two things to notice.

First — the form itself. Form VIII often carries the meaning of *taking on the action upon oneself.* So *yastawūn* doesn't just mean "they are equal" — it carries the sense of "they make themselves level, they settle into equivalence with each other." It's the act of becoming peers.

And Allah negates **this very act.** *Lā yastawūn* — they cannot even arrive at being level. The possibility itself is closed.

Second — and this is the one that stops you — the verb is **plural.** Not dual. Plural.

In Arabic, when you contrast two things, you typically use the dual: *lā yatasāwayān* — *the two of them are not equal.* That would be grammatically natural. The ayah just named two categories — the believer and the *fāsiq.* Two. You would expect dual.

But Allah uses the **plural.** *Lā yastawūn* — *they (plural) are not equal.*

What is the plural doing here?

The classical commentators noticed this. The shift to plural opens the category. It is not saying "this one specific believer and this one specific *fāsiq* are not equal." It is saying **every believer and every fāsiq across all of time** — they are not equal. The plural sweeps the entire human population into the verdict.

And there is something else the plural is doing. By using *yastawūn* — plural — Allah is including in the comparison not just the persons but **everything about them.** Their states. Their hearts. Their ends. Their reckonings. Their reward. Their loss. **None of it is equal.**

[PAUSE]

So in two words — *lā yastawūn* — Allah has done three things at once:
- Negated the very act of equivalence (Form VIII)
- Universalized the verdict (plural over dual)
- Swept every dimension of their existence into the inequality

The ayah began as a question. It ends as a verdict that cannot be appealed.

### Bridging Toward the Themes

We've seen three linguistic moves. The question that forces you to answer. The verb that names not a slip but a rupture. The two-word seal that closes every door of equivalence.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lie of "We're All Basically the Same"

Begin where you live.

There is a phrase you have heard a thousand times, in a hundred forms. *We're all basically the same.* *We're all doing our best.* *Who am I to judge?* *Everyone's path is valid.* *At the end of the day, we're all human.*

This phrase has a power to it. It produces a kind of relief. It dissolves discomfort. It lets you sit at a dinner table with anyone, anywhere, and feel that the fundamental differences between you do not actually matter — because, at the deepest level, you and the person across from you are the same kind of being doing the same kind of thing.

There is a part of this that is true. Every human being is created by Allah. Every human being carries dignity. Every human being is His servant whether they acknowledge it or not. That part is not what this ayah is denying.

What this ayah is denying is the **further** claim — the one our culture smuggled in alongside the first. The claim that because we are all human, our **trajectories** are all equivalent. Our **choices** are all equivalent. The way you live and the way someone else lives — these will meet at the same place because, ultimately, *we're all basically the same.*

[PAUSE]

Allah turns to you and asks: **are they?**

Are they really?

The one whose side lifted from the bed in the dark — and the one who tore through every limit Allah set — *are they the same?*

Notice what the ayah is **not** asking. It is not asking whether they are the same biologically. It is not asking whether they are the same in dignity at the moment of their creation. It is asking whether their **state**, their **end**, their **reality before Allah** — is the same.

And the answer Allah forces out of you, the answer He then seals — *lā yastawūn.*

Here is the part that has to land. The modern phrase *we're all basically the same* is not actually a statement about human nature. It is a statement about **consequence.** What it really means is: *what we do will not, in the end, produce radically different outcomes.* Because if outcomes really diverge — if some lives lead one place and other lives lead somewhere completely different — then "we're all basically the same" becomes a lie that comforts no one.

The phrase is an attempt to **flatten the moral landscape** so that we do not have to feel the vertigo of asymmetric ends.

And the ayah refuses the flattening.

This is not Allah being harsh. This is Allah being honest. Because if the believer and the *fāsiq* end up in the same place, then the believer's struggle was theater. The night vigil was theater. The forsaken bed was theater. The fear and the hope were theater. Everything the surah just described — *tatajāfā junūbuhum* — pointless.

The whole architecture of the moral universe collapses if we are all basically the same.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says *lā yastawūn*, He is doing something merciful. He is telling the one who got up in the night: **your getting up mattered.** He is telling the one who broke the limit: **your breaking mattered.** Neither of those acts is going to be dissolved by the polite fiction that we're all basically the same.

**Practical application:** The next time you feel the pull to flatten — to say "we're all the same" as a way of avoiding the discomfort of asymmetry — notice the pull. Notice what it is trying to protect you from feeling. And ask yourself: *am I flattening because it is true, or because it is comfortable?* The ayah will not let you have both.

### Theme Two: Why Allah Asked Instead of Told

Now go back to the question itself.

We've already seen, linguistically, that the question is not really a question. It is istifhām al-inkārī — a rhetorical denial. Allah already knows the answer. He is going to seal it Himself in the next breath.

So why ask?

Stay with this. Because something profound is happening here.

When you read or hear someone state a verdict, your mind can do something with it. It can file it away. It can disagree with it. It can intellectualize it. It can say *"that's their view"* and place it on a shelf. A statement comes at you from outside, and your inside has many strategies for managing what comes from outside.

But a question? A question reaches in and forces something out of you.

When Allah asks *are they the same?* — He is not adding new information to your mind. He is **extracting something that was already there.** He is forcing into the open the verdict your own fitrah — your own primordial nature — already knows.

[PAUSE]

This is the genius of the form. Allah does not need to convince you that the believer and the *fāsiq* are not equal. **You already know.** Every human being knows. The Quran's frequent use of rhetorical questions is built on this premise: there are truths so deeply embedded in the human soul that they do not need to be argued — they only need to be **drawn out.**

And this is why the question is followed by *lā yastawūn.*

Notice the order. Allah does not say *they are not equal — so are they the same?* That would be backwards. He asks first. Then seals.

Why?

Because in the moment between the question and the seal, **you answered.** Silently, in your chest, you said *no, they are not the same.* Maybe you did not even notice you said it. But you did. The grammar of the ayah forced it out of you.

And then *lā yastawūn* arrives — and it is no longer Allah telling you something. It is Allah **confirming** what just emerged from you. The verdict is now jointly yours.

This is how the Quran operates on the human soul. It does not pile information on top. It **excavates** what is buried beneath.

[PAUSE]

There is a second layer here. Reflect on who the original audience was.

This surah is Meccan. The people hearing it included those who were sneering at the believers — calling them weak, naive, deluded. The narrative of the Meccan elite was: *we are the same as them, or we are better than them. They are just people like us who happen to follow Muhammad. Tomorrow we'll all be in the same dust.*

And the ayah arrives and shatters that.

But notice — it doesn't shatter it by **attacking** them. It shatters it by **asking them.** It puts them inside the question. It puts them in the position of having to answer for themselves whether the believer and the one who burst the limits — are the same.

And here is the staggering part. **Even the fāsiq, hearing this ayah, has to answer.** Even the one being condemned is forced into the position of agreeing with his own condemnation. Because his own fitrah knows. His own primordial nature, before he buried it under his choices, knows that the one who lifted his side from the bed is not the same as the one who tore through the limits.

The ayah uses the *fāsiq* to convict the *fāsiq.*

**Practical application:** When you find yourself reading the Quran and a question appears — stop. Do not skim past it. The Quran asks questions for a reason. Answer it. Out loud, or in your chest. Allah is not asking because He doesn't know. He is asking because **you** need to. The questions of the Quran are mirrors held up to your soul. Do not pass them without looking.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at the whole ayah again, with everything we've seen.

**أَفَمَن كَانَ مُؤْمِنًا كَمَن كَانَ فَاسِقًا ۚ لَّا يَسْتَوُونَ**

Notice the structure. It has two halves, separated by a pause.

The first half is a **question that contains two parallel phrases.**
- *aman kāna mu'minan* — *the one who was a believer*
- *kaman kāna fāsiqan* — *the one who was a fāsiq*

Notice the parallel: *man kāna* / *man kāna.* Allah builds a perfect grammatical mirror. The two halves of the question are structurally identical. As if Allah is saying: **on the surface, the form of these two lives looks identical. Both are human. Both have lived. Both *kāna* — both were.**

The grammar tempts you, for half a breath, to see them as parallel. As equivalent.

And then — the seal. *Lā yastawūn.*

The mirror shatters.

The ayah is doing in its **form** what it is saying in its **content.** It shows you the surface symmetry of two lives — *aman kāna / kaman kāna* — and then reveals that the symmetry is illusion. They are not equal.

This is the architecture. The surface is parallel. The depth is asymmetric. You cannot judge by the surface. You have to wait for the seal.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **question form** (istifhām al-inkārī) and the theme of *why Allah asked instead of told* are the same. Allah uses the form of a question to extract the verdict from inside you because the truth of moral asymmetry is already buried in every human soul. The form is the theology.

The word ***fāsiq*** (the one who bursts the boundary) and the theme of *we're all basically the same* are the same. Our culture's flattening narrative treats the boundary as cosmetic — a social convention you can opt out of without consequence. The word *fāsiq* reveals that the boundary was structural — it was the skin that kept the fruit whole. To burst it is not freedom. It is the beginning of rot.

And the verb ***lā yastawūn*** — plural, sealing, final — is the answer to both. The plural sweeps every dimension of every life into the inequality. The seal closes the door on every appeal. The form denies even the act of becoming equivalent.

Three words do the work of an entire theology of accountability.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I hear the phrase *we're all basically the same*, what is it protecting me from feeling? And is what it is protecting me from — actually true?

2. The word *fāsiq* names the one who burst the skin that was holding them whole. Are there limits in my own life that I have been calling cages — when they were actually skin?

3. Allah asked instead of told. When did I last read a question in the Quran and actually stop to answer it — in my chest, before moving on?

4. The ayah comes right after the description of those whose sides forsake their beds. What does it mean that the first contrast Allah draws — between believer and *fāsiq* — is anchored to the one who got up in the night?

5. If the verdict is *lā yastawūn* — they are not equal — what is the most honest, unflinching answer I can give to the question: which side of this asymmetry am I on, today?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah turns the verdict into a question so that the truth your soul already knows — that lives lived inside the limit and lives lived bursting through it are not, and cannot be, the same — comes out of your own mouth before He seals it from His.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to make a grand resolution tonight. The ayah is not asking for that.

It is asking for a smaller, harder thing. The next time the flattening voice arises — *we're all the same, it doesn't really matter, who's to say* — let yourself feel the discomfort of asymmetry instead of dissolving it. Sit with the fact that the way you live this hour is producing a trajectory that is not the same as the trajectory the *fāsiq* is producing. Not because you are better. But because Allah has said *lā yastawūn* — and He does not say it cruelly. He says it so that the night vigil of the one who gets up in the dark is not made meaningless by the polite fiction that everyone ends up the same place.

Your getting up matters. Your staying down matters. Your choices today are not theater.

That is the gift the ayah is giving you. Not a sword. A scale.

### Du'a

O Allah — You who asked rather than told, who drew the answer from within us rather than imposing it from above — let the verdict our souls already know find its way into our lives, our nights, our limits. Make us of those whose sides lift from the bed in the dark, not of those who tore through what You set in place. And on the Day You make the asymmetry plain, place us on the side of *lā yastawūn* that You promised — coolness of eyes that no soul could have imagined. Āmīn.
