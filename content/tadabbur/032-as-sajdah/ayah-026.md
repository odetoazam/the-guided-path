# The Stones That Will Not Speak: Walking Through Ruins With Sealed Ears

## Introduction

### The Ayah

> أَوَلَمْ يَهْدِ لَهُمْ كَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا مِن قَبْلِهِم مِّنَ ٱلْقُرُونِ يَمْشُونَ فِى مَسَـٰكِنِهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَـَٔايَـٰتٍ ۖ أَفَلَا يَسْمَعُونَ

*A-walam yahdi lahum kam ahlaknā min qablihim mina-l-qurūni yamshūna fī masākinihim. Inna fī dhālika la-āyāt. A-falā yasmaʿūn.*

**"Has it not become clear to them—how many generations We destroyed before them, through whose dwellings they now walk? Surely in that are signs. Will they not, then, hear?"**

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah the way it was first heard, you have to stand on a road.

The trade route from Makkah to Syria did not run through empty desert. It ran past **al-Ḥijr**—the carved stone city of Thamūd. Houses cut into red sandstone cliffs. Doorways still framed. Lintels intact. Rooms you could walk into. A whole civilization frozen mid-breath, abandoned for over a millennium by the time the Quraysh's caravans wound past every spring and fall.

The merchants of Makkah did this trip twice a year. The *riḥlat al-shitāʾi wa-ṣ-ṣayf*—the journey of winter and summer—mentioned in Surah Quraysh. Their wealth came from these routes. Their children grew up hearing about al-Ḥijr the way our children grow up hearing about places they've seen on postcards a thousand times.

They knew the story. Every Arab knew the story. Thamūd was the people of the prophet Ṣāliḥ. They had been given a sign—the she-camel. They had hamstrung her. Allah had destroyed them. The houses they had carved in stone, thinking themselves untouchable, were still there. Empty. For more than a thousand years.

And the Quraysh walked past it.

Twice a year.

For generations.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Allah is asking a question in this ayah that, if you really sit with it, will unsettle you. Because the Quraysh had every piece of evidence a human mind could ask for. They had **physical proof.** Empty cities. Carved stone. Names of destroyed peoples preserved in their own poetry. Routes that ran *through* the ruins.

And it did nothing to them.

The question is not, "Why didn't they get more evidence?" The question Allah is asking is sharper than that. **What kind of evidence does evidence have to be, before it can guide a closed heart?**

And then the strangest word in the ayah—the one we'll spend the longest time on—lands at the end: *a-falā yasmaʿūn.* **Will they not hear?**

Not "will they not see." Not "will they not look." Not "will they not understand." Allah, describing people walking with their eyes open through visible ruins, asks about their **ears.**

Stay with that. Something is going on under the surface of this ayah, and it concerns you more than it concerns them.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We're going to open three things in this ayah. A missing subject. A present-tense verb that turns the listener into a defendant. And the final word that should have been something else.

### Element One: The Subject That Allah Did Not Name

*A-walam yahdi lahum*

The verb *yahdi*—**"does it/he not guide"**—is in the third person singular. In Arabic, every verb implies a subject. Sometimes the subject is named. Sometimes it's a pronoun fused into the verb. And sometimes—rarely, deliberately—the subject is left hanging, unspecified, *muḍmar*. The grammarians have a phrase for this: a hidden pronoun whose referent the reader must supply.

Let me show you how this works in English first, because the same trick exists in your language.

Imagine a parent says to a teenager, after a long, exhausting argument: *"Did this not teach you anything?"* You feel the weight of that sentence. But notice: *what* is "this"? The argument? The consequences? The week of grounding? The look on the parent's face? The parent has not specified. And the unspecified subject is precisely what gives the sentence its reach. *Anything* could be the teacher. **Everything** could be the teacher.

Now return to the ayah. *A-walam yahdi lahum kam ahlaknā…* "Does it not guide them—**how many** We destroyed?"

The classical commentators sat with this for centuries. Who is the implicit subject of *yahdi*?

Some said: **the destruction itself.** "Does the destruction not guide them?"

Some said: **the report of the destruction.** "Does the news, the historical record, not guide them?"

Some said: **Allah.** "Does He not guide them by means of how many We destroyed?"

Some said: **the walking through the ruins.** "Does this very walking not guide them?"

[PAUSE]

Now—here is where the methodology of the early scholars becomes a gift. When you find them differing like this, do not treat their opinions as multiple choice, where you pick one and discard the others. **Treat them as four camera angles on a single reality.** Allah did not specify the subject because *all of these are the subject.* The fact, the report, the divine pedagogy behind the fact, the embodied walk through it—each of these is meant to guide. And the silence of the verb is what lets all four enter at once.

This is what classical balāghah calls a *taʿmīm bi-l-ḥadhf*—a generalization achieved through omission. By naming no specific subject, Allah lets every possible subject in. Reality itself, in all its layers, is the teacher.

Notice something else. We talk in our era about *evidence*. We treat evidence as if it were a single category—a piece of information you either have or don't have. The ayah is teaching you something more demanding. Evidence is not a thing. **Evidence is a relationship.** It requires a fact, a witness to the fact, a report of the fact, and a receptive faculty in the one being addressed. Drop any one of those four, and the "evidence" becomes inert. A stone is just a stone.

This is the first thing the ayah is doing to us. It is asking: *what guides you?* And by leaving the subject blank, it is hinting: **if you are not being guided, it is not because the world is short on subjects of guidance. The world is overflowing with them. Something in you is the missing link.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Verb That Turns Tenses on You

*yamshūna fī masākinihim*

**"They walk in their dwellings."**

I want you to notice the tense. In Arabic, *yamshūna* is the *muḍāriʿ*—the imperfect, the present-continuous, the ongoing. Allah did not say *mashaw*—"they walked"—in the past. He said **they walk.** Right now. As we speak.

This is a deliberate choice, and it is doing something very specific. Let me show you with an analogy.

Imagine you are watching a thriller. The detective walks into a house. The director could cut to a flashback and show you, in the past tense of cinema, what happened in that house. Or—and this is the more devastating choice—the director could leave you in the present, with the detective walking slowly through rooms, and only gradually, through what is *still there*, let you understand what happened. Empty cribs. A coffee cup half-full. A photograph on the floor.

The second technique is more powerful because it puts you in the room. Past tense lets you watch. Present tense makes you a witness.

Allah is using the second technique on the Quraysh. He is not narrating the destruction of Thamūd. He is filming the Quraysh **walking through it.** Right now. *Yamshūna.* They are walking. As I am reciting this ayah to them, they are walking. As they hear it, they are walking. The destruction is past tense—*ahlaknā*, We destroyed, perfect tense, completed. But the walking is alive. The walking is now.

[PAUSE]

And the brilliance of this construction is that it does not stay locked to seventh-century Arabia. The *yamshūna* never stops. Every generation that hears this ayah is grammatically inside its present tense. **We** walk in those dwellings. We walk past the ruins of Soviet empires whose monuments are still standing in our parks. We walk past the abandoned mansions of dot-com millionaires. We walk past the empty churches in cities that once thought themselves Christendom forever. We walk past the gym memberships of people we knew who are dead. We scroll past the Instagram accounts of friends who have been gone for years and were sure, when they posted, that they were just getting started.

The *yamshūna* is the verb that will not let history be other people's problem. It is permanent present tense. It catches every reader.

There is one more thing to see here. Notice the word *masākinihim*—"their dwellings." The root is س-ك-ن, the root of *sakan*, dwelling, and *sakīnah*, tranquility, and *sākin*, the one who settles. A *maskan* is not just a house. It is the place you settled, the place you came to rest, the place you said: *here, I will stop moving.*

And Allah preserves the possessive: *masākinihim*, **THEIR** dwellings. Not "the abandoned houses." Not "the empty ruins." **Their** dwellings. The language still attributes the houses to people who are gone. As if to say: they thought it was *theirs*. They settled. They said, *here, I will rest.* And the language remembers their claim—even as their bodies are dust.

What is more humbling than walking through a place still grammatically owned by someone who no longer exists?

That is the trap of the *maskan*. The places we settle in flatter us into thinking we have arrived. The Arabic root knows: *sakan* is a kindness from Allah (Surah Ar-Rūm 30:21), but it is also a temptation. The one who settles forgets that he was, all along, a traveller.

### Element Three: The Word That Should Have Been "See"

*A-falā yasmaʿūn?* **"Will they not, then, hear?"**

Stop here. Read the ayah again in your mind. What sense organ would you have expected at the end?

Allah describes visible ruins. Carved stones. Empty houses. Doorways that the eye can stare into. He says they **walk through** them—an act of *embodied looking.* By every rule of natural speech, this sentence should end with: *a-falā yubṣirūn?* **Will they not see?**

In fact, the very next ayah, 32:27, ends exactly that way. It describes water flowing onto dry land, vegetation growing, livestock feeding, people eating—and Allah closes with *a-falā yubṣirūn.* **Will they not see?**

So Allah knows the word. Allah uses the word—**in the very next breath.** Which means the choice of *yasmaʿūn* in 32:26 is not a coincidence. It is a surgical decision.

Why does the ayah about **visible ruins** end with **hearing**?

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want to suggest, and I want you to feel it slowly.

A ruin is silent. That is what it is. A ruin is a building that has lost its voice. What once was filled with conversation and laughter and the recitation of poetry and the cries of children—is now empty stone. The wind moves through it. That is all.

To **see** a ruin is easy. The eyes register stone. The eyes register absence.

To **hear** a ruin is something else entirely. To hear a ruin, you have to listen to what the stones are *no longer saying.* You have to hear the silence as testimony. You have to understand that the very emptiness of the space is speech.

Allah is not asking the Quraysh whether they have functioning retinas. He is asking whether they have the inner ear that can hear the silence of an empty city. Because **the lesson is not in what is there. The lesson is in what is gone.**

The eye reports presence. The ear, in this ayah, is the organ that registers absence. And the inner ear—the one Allah is invoking—is the one that hears meaning, that hears warning, that hears the soft voice of revelation under the loud surface of the visible world.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most consistent patterns in the Qur'ān. When Allah describes the unbelievers, He often pairs them with the loss of hearing first. *Ṣummun bukmun ʿumyun*—"deaf, dumb, blind" (2:18). The deafness comes first. Why? Because revelation is sound before it is anything else. The Qur'ān is *qurʾān*—a recitation. *Tilāwah.* An audible event. To reject revelation is, first, to refuse to **hear**.

The eyes can be neutral observers. The ears are committed listeners. The eye can see and pass. The ear, when it truly hears, has been changed by what it heard.

When Allah asks *a-falā yasmaʿūn*, He is asking: are you receiving? Is anything getting in? Or have you walked past empty cities a thousand times with the inner ear sealed shut, and called yourself a witness?

Linguistic honesty here: the grammatical fact is that *yasmaʿūn* is the imperfect of *samiʿa*, root س-م-ع, used throughout the Qur'ān for both physical hearing and receptive listening. What I am suggesting—that the choice of "hear" over "see" in this specific ayah is doing thematic work because of the immediate juxtaposition with 32:27's *yubṣirūn*—is interpretive, but it is anchored in something the text itself does. The two ayahs sit next to each other. Allah used one verb here and the other one verse later. That asymmetry is a fact. What it *means* is the invitation.

### Bridging the Linguistic to the Real

So now we have three linguistic facts.

A subject left blank, so that everything can be the teacher.

A present tense that will not release us, so that every generation walks the same road.

A sense organ reversed, so that we are forced to ask whether we have ever truly **heard** anything we have **seen**.

These are not three observations. They are one observation in three keys. Allah is teaching us that **the problem of guidance is not informational. It is relational.** The world is not stingy with signs. We are stingy with receptivity. And the cure is not more evidence. The cure is the unsealing of an inner ear.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words—but about the architecture of a heart that can be guided.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Pedagogy of Ruins

Let me begin somewhere familiar.

You have lost people. Not necessarily to death. To estrangement. To addiction. To the slow betrayal of a friendship that just—stopped. You have stood in a kitchen and looked at a chair that someone used to sit in, and the chair was still there, and they were not, and you understood in your body that absence has a weight presence does not have.

You have walked through the room of a parent who died, after the funeral, after everyone left, and you have seen the half-read book on the nightstand, and the glasses folded by the lamp, and you have felt something teach you about your own mortality that no sermon ever could.

This is the *masākin* phenomenon. The dwellings of those who have gone speak more sharply than the speeches of those who are here.

The ancient Arabs, before Islam, knew this. Their poetry begins, almost as a genre rule, with *al-wuqūf ʿalā al-aṭlāl*—the standing at the ruins. The poet finds an old campsite. His beloved's tribe has moved on. The fire pits are cold, the tent stakes are gone, the ground is marked but empty. And he stands and weeps. The opening of nearly every great pre-Islamic ode is a man crying at an emptiness.

The Qur'ān comes to a culture that already understood, in its poetry, that **ruins are a teacher.** What the Qur'ān adds is this: the teacher is not nostalgia. The teacher is **theology.**

The pre-Islamic poet stood at the ruins and mourned the lover who was gone. The Qur'ān stands the listener at the ruins and asks: **who allowed this lover to be gone, who allowed this city to fall, who allowed this empire to end, and what does His permission of this teach you about the precariousness of your own continuation?**

The pedagogy of ruins is this: every empty place is asking you a question you do not want to answer. *Why are they gone, and why are you still here, and what makes you sure you will continue?*

[PAUSE]

Now sit with how this lands in our era.

We are surrounded by ruins. We just call them by other names. *Defunct*. *Bankrupt*. *Closed*. *Defunct social media platforms*. *Companies that were going to change the world*. *Empires that were too big to fail and failed*. We pass through them daily and we have developed a remarkable skill, which is the skill of looking at a ruin and noting only its quaintness, never its warning.

A friend tells you about a colleague who lost their job after twenty years. You feel a flicker of fear. Then you look at LinkedIn and you scroll past it. You scroll past the ruin. The dust does not settle on you. The lesson does not land.

Why?

Because in our era, we have built an information architecture that delivers ruins to us as **content.** Not as testimony. The function of content is to be consumed and discarded. The function of testimony is to change the witness. The Qur'ān is asking us whether we have turned the most serious teaching events in human history into mere content. Whether we now walk through the *masākin* of fallen people the way one scrolls through a feed.

[PAUSE]

What does this ayah change about how you live tomorrow?

It asks one thing of you. The next time something falls—a person, a project, a friendship, a company, a regime—**stand at the ruin for one extra minute.** Do not scroll. Do not flip the page. Do not move on with a polite note of acknowledgment. Stand there. Let the silence speak. Ask the question the ayah is asking. *What was this person sure of, and how was that surety dismantled, and what am I currently sure of that operates on the same assumptions?*

Just one extra minute. The discipline of the ayah is the discipline of staying in front of a closed door for longer than is comfortable.

### Theme Two: The Difference Between Looking and Hearing

There is a kind of looking that protects you from what you are looking at.

A doctor walks past a body in a hospital corridor without breaking stride. A soldier reads casualty reports over coffee. A pastor performs the eighth funeral of his career. A nurse takes a child's pulse at the moment of death and then signs a form. None of these people are heartless. They are people whose looking has been **insulated**—because to look without insulation, all day, every day, would dismantle them.

We all have this capacity. It is a survival faculty. The eye that does not let things in.

The Qur'ān, in this ayah, is not condemning looking. It is naming a specific kind of looking that has lost its connection to the heart. The Quraysh did not have eye damage. They had **heart damage that disguised itself as ordinary perception.** They looked at ruins and saw scenery.

What you and I have to understand, with some pain, is that this is the default setting of an unguarded human. **The longer you live, the more the eyes learn how not to let things in.** It is not malice. It is fatigue. It is the soul's version of calluses. The first time you see a homeless man on the street, it cracks you. The hundredth time, you cross the road thinking about your evening plans. You did not decide to become hard. The hardening happened to you while you weren't looking. Or rather—while you *were* looking, but no longer hearing.

[PAUSE]

This is why Allah, again and again, points to the **ear** as the organ of receptivity. Hearing is harder to insulate than seeing. The eye has lids. The ear does not. Sound enters whether you want it to or not, *unless* you have built an inner wall against it. And once you have built the inner wall, the wall is harder to remove than a habit. It becomes a feature of who you are.

The Prophet ﷺ described the sealing of the heart as a black dot that grows with each sin until it covers the heart entirely. *Al-rān*, the corrosion mentioned in Surah Al-Muṭaffifīn (83:14): "*kallā bal rāna ʿalā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūn*"—"No, rather a corrosion has built up on their hearts from what they used to earn." The corrosion is not a single act. It is the patient layering of dismissals. Every signal you did not let in is another particle in the wall.

Walking past al-Ḥijr for twenty years and learning nothing—that is the wall.

Scrolling past a friend's death for thirty seconds and feeling nothing—that is the wall.

Reading the Qur'ān every Ramadan for forty years and being unchanged—that is the wall.

[PAUSE]

So how does the wall come down?

The ayah suggests an answer in its final verb. *A-falā yasmaʿūn?* The opening of the ear is the cure. And the opening of the ear is not technical—it is dispositional. It is a willingness to be **moved.** It is the prior decision, made before any specific signal arrives, that you will not insulate yourself from what reality is trying to tell you.

There is a practical form of this. It is called *istighfār.* Not as a magic formula. As a practice of un-insulation. Every time you say *astaghfirullāh*, you are saying: *I have been the kind of person who walks past testimony without hearing it; soften me, return me to receptivity, do not let me become a man who only sees stones where stones are speaking.*

The Prophet ﷺ said he asked Allah's forgiveness more than seventy times a day. He was the most receptive heart in human history. And he was the most frequent *istighfār*-maker. The two are not in tension. The two are the same fact. **The ear that hears is the ear that constantly asks to have its hearing renewed.**

[PAUSE]

What does this theme ask of you tomorrow?

One specific practice. Before you sleep tonight, pick one piece of testimony from your day that you scrolled past. A friend's bad news. A stranger's grief. A worry your spouse mentioned that you brushed off. A news headline you flicked away from. Anything where your eye saw and your ear did not hear.

And sit with it for sixty seconds. Just sixty. Let it land. Let it ask you its question. Then say: *astaghfirullāh*. Not for the act of scrolling. For the **disposition** that scrolled. The disposition is what the ayah is naming. The disposition is what asks to be repaired.

Sixty seconds. Every night. For one week.

This is what *yasmaʿūn* means in practice. The ayah is not asking you to listen harder. It is asking you to **become a person whose hearing is intact.**

## Closing Synthesis

### The Three Pieces Are One Piece

Look at what we have.

A verb with no specified subject—because everything is meant to teach you.

A present tense that catches every reader in its grammar—because the ayah is filming **you** walking through your own ruins.

A sense organ named at the end—not the eye, but the ear—because the question was never whether the world was sending signals. The question was whether you were receiving.

These are not three discoveries. They are three angles on a single architectural fact: **the universe is constructed to guide. The constraint on guidance is not the world's silence; it is the listener's deafness.**

And the two themes we explored sit underneath this exactly. The pedagogy of ruins is the world doing its job—broadcasting testimony, leaving every empty kitchen and every empty city as a sign. The difference between looking and hearing is the human side—whether we have built the inner ear that can receive what the world is saying, or whether we have walled ourselves off and called the wall maturity.

### Questions to Carry

1. What ruin in your own life—a relationship that ended, a habit that destroyed someone you loved, a person who died sure of themselves—have you been walking through without standing still in front of it?

2. When did you last say *astaghfirullāh* not as a habit, but as a request to be **re-opened** to a piece of testimony you had walled out?

3. If your eye saw and your ear did not hear, what *kind* of evidence would still get through to you—and is it possible that nothing is left in that category, because the wall is now total?

4. The Quraysh walked past al-Ḥijr twice a year for generations. What is *your* al-Ḥijr—the testimony you pass routinely and have stopped registering?

5. If the present tense of *yamshūna* includes you—and grammatically, it does—what is your specific assignment in this ayah, and how would tonight be different if you accepted it?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**The world is overflowing with teachers; the question Allah is asking is whether the inner ear, which alone can hear them, is still intact in you.**

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to be more impressed by ruins. Allah does not need your astonishment. He is inviting you into a different relationship with the visible world—one in which the surfaces of things stop being scenery and start being **speech.** A relationship in which an empty house is not a sad fact but a sentence directed at you. In which a friend's failure is not gossip but a warning gift you've been handed.

This is what it means to be a believer who is awake. It is not extra knowledge. It is **restored hearing.** And it is available to you the moment you ask for it, and re-available every moment you ask again.

### Du'ā

*Allāhumma*, You who have made the universe speak in a thousand tongues to the heart that listens—open in us the ear that hears the silence of ruins, the warning under the visible, the testimony You have placed in every fallen city and every emptied chair. Do not let us walk through Your signs the way the Quraysh walked through al-Ḥijr—our eyes intact, our hearts sealed, our feet moving, our hearing gone. Make us the people of *a-falā yasmaʿūn*—the ones who, when the question is asked, answer **balā**: yes, we hear, and we have been changed by what we heard. *Āmīn.*
