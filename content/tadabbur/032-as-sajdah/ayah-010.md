# When the Question Names the Questioner

## Surah As-Sajdah, Ayah 10

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:32:10] وَقَالُوٓا۟ أَءِذَا ضَلَلْنَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ أَءِنَّا لَفِى خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍۭ ۚ بَلْ هُم بِلِقَآءِ رَبِّهِمْ كَـٰفِرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Wa qālū a-idhā ḍalalnā fī al-arḍi a-innā la-fī khalqin jadīd. Bal hum bi-liqā'i rabbihim kāfirūn.

**Translation:**
> "And they say, 'When we are lost in the earth, will we really be in a new creation?' Rather, they are — in the meeting with their Lord — disbelievers."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

To feel this ayah, we need to feel the air of the surah it's sitting in.

Surah As-Sajdah is short — only thirty verses — but it is one of the most concentrated arguments in the Quran about three things: that Allah created you deliberately, that He will return to you deliberately, and that what happens between those two points matters more than you think.

The surah opens with the heavens and the earth and the six days. It moves through the creation of the human being from clay, then the drop of fluid, then the hearing and seeing and hearts. Verse after verse, Allah is building a case — not legally, but almost architecturally. He is showing you the floor plan of existence. He is saying: *I made this. I made you. None of this is accidental.*

And then, in verse 10, He quotes the people who looked at all of this and said: **no.**

Not no with shouting. No with a question — the most sophisticated kind of refusal. A question that sounds reasonable. A question that sounds almost scientific.

*"When we are lost in the earth — when our bodies have broken down into dust and we have become indistinguishable from the soil — will we really be reassembled into a new creation?"*

It's a fair question if you're asking it honestly. The body decomposes. Atoms scatter. What you called "me" disperses across the ground until there is no boundary left between you and the dirt.

And so they say: how could that be put back together? How could that be *you* again?

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what we are going to discover.

The disbelievers in this verse chose a particular word for what happens to the body in the grave. They chose a word that sounds neutral — almost gentle. Almost poetic.

But it is the same word the Quran uses, again and again, for **going astray.**

The word they used to describe their bodies in the dust is the same word Allah uses, throughout this Book, to describe a heart that has lost its way.

And then Allah responds. Not by correcting their biology. Not by arguing about atoms. He responds with one of the smallest particles in the Arabic language — two letters — and with that particle, He turns their entire question inside out.

He says, in effect: *You think you are asking about your body. You are not asking about your body. And the thing you are afraid of has already happened to you — just not where you think.*

That is the ayah we are about to walk into.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word They Did Not Realize They Were Saying — *Ḍalalnā*

Before we open the verb, let's talk about something else.

Have you ever heard someone describe their own life using a word, and the moment they said it, you realized they had just told you something they didn't mean to tell you?

A person says, *"I just feel like I'm drifting."* And they meant it casually. They meant: I haven't decided on a career path. But the word *drifting* — when you sit with it — describes something else. It describes someone in water with no shore in sight, moving without choosing to move, going somewhere they did not pick. The word said more than they did.

Arabic does this with a kind of precision that English rarely matches. Arabic words are not chosen from a thesaurus. They are pulled from a root — and the root carries everything the word has ever meant, across every context. When you pick the word, you don't get to leave the root behind. The root comes with you.

Hold that idea. We're going to use it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what the deniers say. *A-idhā ḍalalnā fī al-arḍ.* "When we are lost in the earth."

The verb is **ḍalalnā** — *we became lost.* It comes from the root **ḍād-lām-lām** (ض ل ل).

In its physical sense, classical Arabic uses this root for things that disappear into something larger. The lexicographers cite the way Arabs spoke of water vanishing into sand — *ḍalla al-mā'u fī al-arḍ*, the water was lost in the earth. It seeps in. It becomes indistinguishable from what swallowed it. You could not point to a single drop and say *that one was mine.* It is gone. Dispersed. Absorbed.

That is the image the deniers are using for their bodies. Beautiful, in a way. *We will seep into the ground. We will be absorbed. We will be lost the way water is lost in sand.*

It almost sounds peaceful. It almost sounds like a poem.

But here is where the root will not let them go.

The same root — **ḍ-l-l** — is the Quran's primary word for **spiritual misguidance.** *Ḍalāl.* Going astray. Losing the way.

It is the word in the very last verse of Sūrat al-Fātiḥah, the verse every Muslim says seventeen times a day: *wa lā al-ḍāllīn* — "and not of those who have gone astray." Same root.

It is the word Allah uses, again and again, throughout the Quran, for a heart that has wandered off the path. *Man yashā'i Allāhu yuḍlilhu* — "whom Allah wills to send astray." Same root. *In tatabiʿ aktharu man fī al-arḍ yuḍillūka ʿan sabīli Allāh* — "if you follow most of those on earth, they will lead you astray." Same root.

Stay with me.

The deniers — the very people in this ayah — pick a word to describe what they think will happen to their bodies. They pick **ḍalalnā.** *We will be lost in the earth.*

And they did not realize what they were saying.

[PAUSE]

Because the same word, in the same Book, describes what is already happening to their *hearts.*

They are worried about a future loss — the body dispersing into soil. They are not worried about the present loss — the soul dispersing from its Lord.

They have chosen, of all possible Arabic verbs, the one verb that names both at once. They mean it physically. Allah hears it spiritually. And the irony — the gentle, devastating irony — is that **the thing they are afraid of has already happened. Just not in the dirt. In the chest.**

This is not me reading something into the word. The Quran uses this root for spiritual misguidance hundreds of times. Any Arabic listener in the 7th century, hearing the deniers say *ḍalalnā fī al-arḍ*, would have felt the second meaning thrum underneath the first. It is not a hidden meaning. It is the root showing itself.

What this invites us to consider: the words we choose to describe our condition often describe our condition more accurately than we know. When someone reaches for the word *lost* to describe their body's fate but cannot say it about their soul, the language has caught them. The vocabulary is honest even when the speaker is not.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Hinge — *Bal*

The deniers have spoken. They have asked their question. *When we are lost in the earth, will we really be in a new creation?*

Allah's response is a single word.

**Bal.**

Two letters in Arabic: ب ل. *Bal.*

Before we hear what it does in this ayah, let's understand what *bal* is.

In English, when someone makes a statement and we want to redirect it, we have several options. We can say *no.* We can say *actually.* We can say *but.* Each one is a slightly different move.

*Bal* in Arabic is something more specific than any of these. The grammarians call it a particle of **iḍrāb** — a particle of turning away, of redirection, of leaving one claim behind and moving to a different one. It does not just negate. It pivots. It says: *let's stop talking about that and talk about what's actually going on.*

You can hear it in everyday Arabic. Someone says: *Did you forget to call me?* And the other person says: *Bal, I was thinking about you all day.* It doesn't just say "no, I didn't forget." It redirects the conversation to what was really happening underneath.

That is the particle Allah uses here.

The deniers have constructed an objection. The objection has a specific shape: *we doubt the science of bodily resurrection. We doubt the mechanics of reassembly. Our bodies will be lost in the dust — how could they be a new creation?*

And Allah responds: **bal.**

Notice — He does not answer the question they asked.

He does not say, *Actually, here is how reassembly works.* He does not say, *Let Me explain the metaphysics of resurrection.* He does not say, *The atoms are not destroyed, only rearranged.*

He pivots.

He says: **bal hum bi-liqā'i rabbihim kāfirūn.** *Rather — they are, in the meeting with their Lord, disbelievers.*

Let's sit with what just happened.

[PAUSE]

They asked a question about *bodies.* He answered with a statement about *hearts.*

They asked a question about *biology.* He answered with a statement about *relationship.*

They asked, *Will we be a new creation?* He answered, *You don't actually care about that. The thing you can't accept is not the recreation. The thing you can't accept is the meeting.*

Here's what's remarkable. If you have ever been in a difficult conversation with someone — a real one, the kind that matters — you know that there is always a question on the surface and a question underneath. The person asks you something specific, but the specific question is not really the question. It is the polite version of the question. The deflected version. The version that is safe to ask out loud.

And sometimes — if you are listening carefully and you love them enough to risk it — you can say: *That's not what you're actually asking, is it?*

That is what *bal* does in this ayah.

Allah is saying to the deniers: I hear your question. I also hear the question underneath it. You think you are arguing about whether the body can be put back together. You are not. You are protecting yourself from something else. The thing you cannot face is not the mechanics of resurrection. The thing you cannot face is **meeting Me.**

Documented grammatically: *bal* is a particle of redirection, ending one claim and opening another. What this invites us to consider: when Allah uses *bal* to answer a question, He is often telling us that the question itself was the wrong question — and the right question was hiding underneath it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Word They Did Not Use — *Rabbihim*

There is one more thing we have to notice. And it lives in what is **absent.**

When the deniers spoke, they used eleven words. *A-idhā ḍalalnā fī al-arḍ a-innā la-fī khalqin jadīd.* Eleven words.

Read those eleven words again. Count how many times Allah is mentioned.

[PAUSE]

Zero.

Not once. Allah is not in their sentence. They do not say *will our Lord recreate us.* They do not say *will the One who made us once make us again.* They speak about the earth. They speak about themselves. They speak about a new creation, indefinite — *khalqin jadīd*, "a new creation," with no specified Creator.

The Creator is not named. The Creator is **edited out.**

And then look at Allah's response. *Bal hum bi-liqā'i **rabbihim** kāfirūn.* "Rather, they are, in the meeting with **their Lord**, disbelievers."

The word that did not appear in their sentence appears in His.

And it is not just any word. It is **rabbihim** — *their Lord.* Not *al-Khāliq*, the Creator. Not *Allāh*, the divine name. **Rabb.**

*Rabb* in Arabic carries the meaning of the one who nurtures, who sustains, who raises something from one state to a higher state, the master of relationship. A *rabb* is not a distant deity. A *rabb* is the one who has been with you, providing for you, raising you, ever since you began.

And the pronoun attached — *him*, "their" — makes it personal. *Their* Lord. The Lord who is theirs. The Lord who has been theirs all along, whether they acknowledged Him or not.

Notice what just happened across these two sentences. The deniers spoke about death in language so abstract that Allah did not even appear in it. *Lost in the earth. A new creation.* As if the earth itself were the agent. As if creation just sort of happened.

And Allah's response restores the *who.* He says: there is a meeting. The meeting is with *Me.* The meeting is with the One who has been your Lord this whole time. You can edit Me out of your sentence. You cannot edit Me out of reality.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer here. The deniers said *khalqin jadīd* — "a new creation" — and the word is **indefinite.** In Arabic, that little *tanwīn* at the end of *jadīd* signals that this creation is unspecified, generic, kept at arm's length. They are talking about it as a category, as an abstract possibility.

Allah responds with **liqā'i rabbihim** — definite, specific, possessive. Not *a* meeting. **The** meeting. With **their** Lord.

They held it at distance. He brought it close.

They made it impersonal. He made it relational.

They asked whether it would happen. He told them the only reason they're asking is because they don't want it to.

### Bridge

We have now seen three things.

We have seen that the deniers reached for a word — *ḍalalnā* — that named more than they meant, because the root for "lost in the dust" is the same root the Quran uses for "lost in the soul."

We have seen that Allah did not answer their question. He pivoted with *bal* — turning away from biology to reveal what was actually being defended against.

And we have seen that they spoke a sentence without Allah in it, and He answered with a sentence where He is named not as a distant Creator but as **their Lord** — the one whose relationship to them was never really in question, only in denial.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Argument Is Almost Never About the Argument

Sit with a moment from ordinary life.

Someone you love is upset. They tell you — clearly, sharply — that they are upset because you forgot to do a specific thing. The dishes. The text. The promise.

And you, if you are listening carefully, know that this is not really about the dishes.

You know that the dishes are the polite version. The dishes are the safe version. The dishes are the version they could speak out loud without having to say the harder thing — that they feel unseen, or unimportant, or alone.

If you respond to the dishes, the fight will continue. Because the dishes were not the problem. The dishes were the place where the real problem became visible.

This is one of the oldest patterns in human communication. **The argument is almost never about the argument.**

We do this with each other. We do it with ourselves. And — this is the part we don't talk about — **we do it with Allah.**

[PAUSE]

The deniers in this ayah are doing something every human being does, all the time. They have constructed a sophisticated objection. The objection sounds reasonable. The objection sounds like a question about mechanics — *how could a decomposed body be reassembled?* It is the kind of question you could put on a debate stage. It has the shape of an intellectual problem.

But Allah hears the question and responds to what's underneath it.

He says: *You are not actually wrestling with the mechanics. You are protecting yourself from the meeting.*

This is what *bal hum bi-liqā'i rabbihim kāfirūn* is telling us. The disbelief is not really about whether resurrection is *possible.* It is about whether the meeting is **desired.**

And here is where this ayah stops being about the deniers and starts being about us.

Because if you have ever postponed something you knew was right — and you noticed, in yourself, the elaborate machinery of reasonable-sounding objections you constructed to justify the delay — you have done what the deniers did. Maybe not at the scale of denying resurrection. But at the scale of a prayer you didn't pray, a conversation you didn't have, a repentance you kept deferring.

The objections sounded reasonable. *It's too late. The wudu is too cold. I'll do it later. I don't feel ready.*

These were the dishes.

Underneath them was something else. Maybe the fear of being seen. Maybe the discomfort of being changed. Maybe the unwillingness to face who you have actually been.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah does *not* do in this ayah. He does not say: *let Me prove resurrection to you.* He does not say: *here are seven evidences from biology.*

He could have. He proves it elsewhere in the Quran — many times. He talks about the dead earth coming back to life. He talks about the seed splitting open. He talks about His ability to recreate the very tips of your fingers.

He has those arguments. He just doesn't use them here.

Here, He uses **bal.** He pivots away from the argument because He is teaching us — through the response — that the argument was never the point.

The point was the heart's posture toward the meeting.

**Practical invitation:** When you next notice yourself constructing an unusually thorough objection to a thing you know is good — pause. Ask the question Allah's *bal* is teaching us to ask: *What am I really defending against? What is the meeting I don't want to face?*

It is not always Allah's meeting. Sometimes it is a meeting with a truth about yourself. Sometimes it is a meeting with a person you have wronged. Sometimes it is a meeting with the version of you that you have been postponing becoming.

But the pattern is the same. The argument is almost never about the argument.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: We Project Our Loss Outward

Now hold the other discovery.

The deniers used the word *ḍalalnā* — "we will be lost" — for what happens to their bodies in the dust. The exact word the Quran uses, hundreds of times, for the soul going astray.

There is a name in modern psychology for what they are doing. It is called **projection.** It is one of the oldest things human beings do. We take a quality that lives inside us — something we cannot bear to see in ourselves — and we attach it to something outside us. We point at the outside thing and say: *that is the problem.* Meanwhile the actual problem is still inside, unaddressed, growing.

A person who is full of contempt sees contempt everywhere in others. A person who has been unfaithful suspects unfaithfulness in their spouse. A person whose own soul has wandered away from its Lord — and who feels, in some half-conscious place, that this has happened — names the wandering somewhere else. Names it in the *body.* Names it in the *dust.*

The body has not wandered yet. The body is still alive, still walking, still talking. It is the soul that is already lost.

But the deniers cannot say that. So they put the loss into the future, and they put it into the wrong place. They say: *we are afraid of a loss that will happen to our bodies after we die.*

And Allah, with the gentlest possible response — *bal* — turns the loss right-side-up. *The loss you fear is not in the dust. The loss you fear is in your chest. And it is not in the future. It is right now.*

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most clarifying frames the Quran gives us for understanding ourselves.

When you feel a vague dread about something far away — death, the grave, the Day — pause and ask: *is this dread really about that distant thing? Or is something already happening in me, and I am giving it a name and a location that lets me keep my distance from it?*

Anxiety about death is sometimes — not always, but sometimes — a misplaced grief about the way we are living now. The body has not yet gone into the dust. But something else has. Something has dispersed. Something has seeped away. We feel its absence and we put a name on it that points outward.

The Quran's mercy here is staggering. Allah is not mocking the deniers for this. He is **naming it** for them. He is saying: *I know what you are doing. I know that the words you chose were truer than you knew. I am telling you what they meant — so that you have a chance, while you are still here, to do something about it.*

Because — and this is the hidden hope in the ayah — if the loss is in the heart, then it can be reversed in the heart. Dust cannot un-disperse itself. But a heart that has wandered can return. Today. This afternoon. In this moment.

The same Book that uses *ḍalāl* for going astray uses *hidāyah* for being guided back. They are paired. One does not exist without the possibility of the other.

So when Allah uses *bal* to redirect the deniers from their bodies to their hearts, He is not just exposing them. He is offering them the only door that is still open. *The dust is not the problem. The dust will do what dust does. The thing you can actually fix is the thing you have been pretending is in the dust.*

[PAUSE]

**Practical invitation:** Take any anxiety you have been carrying about something far away — a death, a future failure, a loss you fear. Sit with it for a moment. Ask: *is there something happening in my heart right now that I have been giving this future name?*

If there is, the future may be exactly what it will be. Allah's promises will arrive on their own schedule. But the heart-loss — the actual *ḍalāl* underneath the imagined one — can be brought to Him today.

That is what *bal hum bi-liqā'i rabbihim kāfirūn* is, in the end. It is a diagnosis. And every diagnosis Allah gives in this Book is also, simultaneously, an offer of cure. He only names what He wants to heal.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### How the Pieces Fit

Look at what we have, now, all together.

The deniers said eleven words. Allah responded with seven. And inside this brief exchange, the entire architecture is symmetrical.

They reached for *ḍalalnā* — a word that named more than they intended, because the root carries spiritual loss inside the physical loss they meant.

They constructed a question that **edited Allah out** — speaking of the earth as the agent, of creation as something indefinite, of themselves as if no Lord were involved.

And Allah responded with one word — *bal* — that pivoted away from the surface of the question to the heart of it; and then He **put back what they had taken out.** He named **their Lord.** He named **the meeting.** He restored the *who* they had erased.

The linguistic move and the theological move are the same move. They tried to make this about biology, far away, abstract, with no relationship inside it. He brought it back to **liqā'i rabbihim** — a meeting, with *their* Lord, that is the only thing this was ever about.

The word they did not realize they were saying named what was already true of them. The word He used named what was always true of Him.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you next notice yourself constructing a careful, reasonable objection to a thing you know is good — what is the *meeting* underneath it that you may not want to face?

2. Where in your life have you used words that named more than you meant? What were the words trying to tell you?

3. The deniers spoke an entire sentence about death without naming Allah. Listen to your own internal speech for one day. How often do you describe what happens to you as if the earth were the agent and Allah were not in the sentence?

4. *Bal* turns one claim into another. If Allah said *bal* to your most familiar complaint — the one you've been rehearsing for years — what do you suspect He would name underneath it?

5. The body's loss in the dust is unavoidable. The soul's loss is not. If today you could name one place where *ḍalāl* has crept in quietly while you were arguing about something else — what would you name?

### The Heart of It in One Sentence

*The disbelievers asked about their bodies because they could not bear to ask about their hearts, and Allah answered with one word — bal — to tell them, and us, that the loss they fear is not in the future and not in the dust, but in the chest, right now, and it can still be undone.*

### The Closing Invitation

This is not a verse to leave the deniers in. It is a verse to leave **ourselves** in.

We do what they did. We do it on smaller scales, and we do it more politely, but we do it. We construct careful questions about distant problems so that we do not have to address present ones. We use the language of the body when the soul is what is hurting. We speak sentences about our lives in which Allah, somehow, has been edited out.

And the mercy of this ayah is that Allah is still listening. He still hears the word underneath the word. He still answers with *bal*. He is still — even now, even with us — willing to pivot the conversation from the polite version to the true one, if we will let Him.

That is the invitation. Not to be more clever in our questions. To be more honest in them. To go to Him with what we actually fear, what we actually want, what we actually are.

He has already heard the version we don't say out loud. He is waiting for us to say it.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who hear the word beneath the word — when our objections are really avoidances, name them for us as gently as You named theirs. Do not let us speak sentences about our lives in which You are not in the sentence. And before the day our bodies are lost in the earth, return to You the parts of us that have already wandered. You are our Rabb. The meeting is with You. Let our hearts stop refusing it.*

*Āmīn.*
