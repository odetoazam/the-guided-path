**Translation:**
Indeed, your Lord is He who will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they used to differ.

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we need to go back to Makkah.

Not the Makkah of the Grand Mosque as it stands today. The Makkah of the seventh century — a city of trade routes and tribal honor, where words were weapons and reputation was everything. And into this city, a man stands up and says: *This speech is from Allah.*

The response was immediate. *You fabricated it. You are a poet. You are a soothsayer. You learned it from someone else.* The accusations came in waves — not just disbelief, but active, sustained, public mockery. The people who mattered in Makkah looked at the Prophet ﷺ and said: *We dispute this. We dispute you.*

Now hold that image.

Surah As-Sajdah opens with one of the most direct responses to that accusation in the entire Quran: *"The revelation of the Book — about which there is no doubt — is from the Lord of the worlds."* No preamble. No apology. Just a declaration, from the very first breath of the surah: this dispute has already been settled on the divine side. The question is only what will happen on the human side.

What follows across twenty-four ayahs is one of the Quran's most sustained portrait galleries of contrast. The human being formed from clay and breathed into with Allah's spirit — what do they do with that gift? The heart that opens to the Quran and weeps in prostration — and the heart that turns away. The person at the moment of death who says *"My Lord, return me"* — and the one who meets their Lord in the fullness of what they chose. Two groups, drawn and redrawn, from every angle.

And then, in the ayah just before this one — ayah 24 — a portrait of the highest station: *"And We made from among them leaders — guiding by Our command — when they were patient, and when they were certain of Our signs."*

Patient. Certain. These two words define the profile of the person who survived the dispute intact. Not by winning every argument. Not by silencing every opponent. But by bearing the weight of ongoing disagreement without losing their footing.

And then Allah says what we have just read.

**إِنَّ رَبَّكَ هُوَ يَفْصِلُ بَيْنَهُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ**

Here is what I want you to carry into this ayah before we open it linguistically.

There is something every honest person has experienced. A dispute that could not be resolved. An accusation you could not disprove. A truth you knew — with complete certainty — that no one around you would confirm. An argument that went on so long it became the wallpaper of your life.

What does this ayah say about that?

It says: the resolution has already been scheduled.

It just isn't today.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: هُوَ — The Word That Leaves No Room for Anyone Else

Let me teach you something about Arabic grammar before we open this word.

In English, when you want to emphasize the subject of a sentence, you stress your voice, or add "himself," or write in capital letters. Arabic does it differently — with a grammatical device so precise and so surgical it leaves nothing to interpretation.

In classical Arabic, there is a structure called the **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — the *pronoun of separation*. Here is how it works. In a normal Arabic sentence, you go straight from subject to predicate: *"Zayd is generous."* Simple. But when you insert a separate personal pronoun between the subject and what follows — *"Zayd — he — is generous"* — something shifts entirely. That inserted pronoun is not decorative. It is not emotional filler. It is the grammar of **exclusion**. It means: Zayd, and not someone else. Zayd, to the exclusion of others as candidates. The grammar itself draws a circle around one person and locks everyone else out.

Now look at what Allah says.

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ — *Indeed, your Lord.*
هُوَ — *He.*
يَفْصِلُ — *will judge.*

Your Lord — **He** — will judge.

That pronoun هُوَ, standing between "your Lord" and the action, is the ḍamīr al-faṣl doing exactly what it was built to do: eliminating every other candidate for this role.

[PAUSE]

Let that settle.

Every court you have ever appeared before. Every person who ever rendered a verdict about you. Every tribunal of public opinion that decided who was right and who was wrong. Every historical judgment that came down about movements and moments and people. Every parent who chose whom to believe. Every institution that decided who would be remembered well.

This word, هُوَ, looks at all of them and says: *not the final one.*

Notice how Allah addresses the Prophet ﷺ here — رَبَّكَ, **your** Lord. Not "the Lord." Not "Allah" in the abstract third person. Your Lord. The one in a specific relationship with you. The one who has been watching everything that has been said about you, everything that has been attributed to you falsely, everything that has been mocked and disputed. And that same Lord — **He alone** — will judge between them.

There is a reason this ayah comes after twenty-four ayahs of sustained disputation. After the Quran has been called fabricated. After mocking and arguing and refusing and disputing have filled the air of an entire city. All of that debate has a terminus. And the terminus has only one Judge. And this word, هُوَ, carved into the grammar, makes that absolute.

Not the tribunals of Makkah.
Not the court of time.
Not history's consensus.
Not the popular verdict.

**He.**

### Element Two: يَفْصِلُ — The Cut That Ends the Tangle

I want to spend time with a single Arabic root. Because it is one of the most extraordinary roots in the language.

ف — ص — ل

*Fāʾ. Ṣād. Lām.*

This root carries the idea of a clean, decisive separation. Not a negotiated compromise. Not a gradual fading. A cut. The kind that takes something previously joined and makes it two unmistakably distinct things.

Look at the family of words this root generates:

*Faṣīl* — a weaned child. The separation from the mother's milk is complete; there is no returning to the previous state.

*Fuṣūl* — chapters of a book. The text is divided into distinct units, each with a clear boundary, each not the other.

*Faṣāḥah* — eloquence. In the Arabic literary tradition, eloquence is not merely beautiful speech. It is *clear* speech — speech that separates what it means from what it does not mean, with such precision that no ambiguity remains. Speech that cuts through fog.

And *faṣl* in judgment means the definitive verdict. The decision that closes the case. Not an ongoing deliberation. Not a preliminary finding. The cut. Done. Over.

This is the word Allah chooses: **يَفْصِلُ**. Your Lord — He — will *cut clean between them.*

[PAUSE]

Now place that against the other verb this ayah contains.

يَخْتَلِفُونَ — *they used to differ.*

This verb comes from a completely different root: خ — ل — ف. And this root has an entirely different texture.

The base meaning of خ-ل-ف is: to be behind something. To follow in the wake of. To come after. When you take this root and put it into Form VIII — the reflexive, intensive pattern — *ikhtilāf* — you get people pulling in opposite directions from one another, each following a different guide, each going "behind" something different, each moving away from a shared center.

Ikhtilāf is not merely disagreement. It is the image of a rope being pulled from both ends simultaneously. It is dispersion. Scattering. People who cannot and will not converge.

And notice what tense each verb carries.

يَخْتَلِفُونَ — imperfect, present, ongoing. *They are still differing.* The disagreement is not past. It is alive right now. They are in it.

يَفْصِلُ — also imperfect, but future-certain. *He will cut.* Not "He has cut." Not "He might cut." The imperfect here carries inevitability: this action is coming. It is as certain as anything future can be.

Here is what the architecture of these two verbs together reveals:

The human condition: **ongoing, unresolved, scattering — present tense, right now.**
The divine action: **decisive, clean, cutting — future-certain, already scheduled.**

The ongoing present state of human ikhtilāf has an expiry date.

The faṣl is coming.

[PAUSE — let this land]

One more layer. The contrast between these two roots is not coincidental arrangement. Faṣl and ikhtilāf are structurally opposite. Ikhtilāf is people pulling in different directions from one another — dispersion, scattering, the centrifugal force of disagreement. Faṣl is cutting the knot between those directions — making visible which was true, ending the scattering with a decisive partition.

The surah chose the one Arabic word that is the exact counter-shape of the problem it is ending.

This is not poetry. This is precision.

### Element Three: فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ — Inside the Disagreement

There is something in this phrase I want to slow all the way down for.

فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

Most translations render this as: *"concerning that over which they used to differ."* Which is accurate. But the Arabic says something slightly more arresting than "concerning."

The preposition فِي means *in* — inside. And it appears twice in this short phrase.

فِيمَا — *in what*
فِيهِ — *in it*

Literally: *"in what — they were — in it — disagreeing."*

They are not merely arguing *about* something from a distance, standing outside the dispute and examining it. They are *inside* the disagreement. It surrounds them. They are immersed in it. The repetition of فِي — two uses of "inside" in four words — is doing something precise: it is wrapping these people within their argument. The disagreement is not an object they hold. It is a habitat they inhabit.

[PAUSE]

Notice the verb in the middle: كَانُوا — *"they were."* Past tense. They were inside it. And then the surah says they are *still* differing — يَخْتَلِفُونَ, imperfect, ongoing. So they were inside it — and they remain inside it. The disagreement became a kind of permanent residence.

This is a psychologically precise observation about what happens when a dispute goes on long enough.

When an argument has continued for years, it stops being something you are *doing* and becomes something you are *living inside*. The dispute about whether the Quran was truly from Allah was not merely an intellectual position that Quraysh held. It had become part of how they saw everything. It had become their lens. They were *inside* it — and could no longer see the walls of the container.

The divine faṣl will come from outside that container.

This is the one who will judge: not another party who is also inside the disagreement, carrying their own version of it. The One who stands entirely outside every human argument — who sees not just the dispute but the people immersed inside it, sees the full reality that the disputers cannot see from where they stand — will cut.

And when He does, the walls of the container will fall away.

### The Bridge

So what has the language shown us?

**هُوَ** tells us: there is one Judge, exclusively. Every other verdict — however powerful, however ancient, however widely accepted — is provisional.

**يَفْصِلُ** tells us: the judgment will be a clean cut — not a negotiation, not a splitting of the difference, but a decisive separation of what is true from what only appeared to be.

**فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ** tells us: people become so immersed in their disagreements that they lose the ability to see beyond them. They are inside something they cannot step outside of.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about Arabic, but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Living Inside Unresolved Truth

I want to begin with an experience before I begin with any idea.

You have been in this situation. Perhaps you are in it right now.

Someone said something about you that was not true. And the people who heard it believed it. You tried to correct it, and it did not help — or the people who could have spoken up for you stayed silent, or the evidence that would vindicate you was inaccessible, or disputed, or simply not persuasive to the people whose judgment mattered.

And you know the truth. And Allah knows the truth. And the wrong verdict stands.

Or a family dispute running for years — where the original wrong has long since been buried under layers of competing narratives. Or a business betrayal where the other party walked away looking like the reasonable one. Or a theological argument with someone who will never change their mind, no matter what evidence is placed in front of them. Or a marriage that ended, and the story that got told about what happened — and you know it is not the full story, and it never will be corrected properly, not in this world.

This is the experience this ayah is entering.

The Prophet ﷺ — the most truthful of all people, the one his own community called *al-Amīn* (the Trustworthy) before revelation came — was told that the speech he was carrying was fabricated by him. The thing he knew with more certainty than anything else in his life was the very thing most publicly, most viciously disputed. Imagine bearing that. Not for a day. For years. In a city where your word and your reputation were your entire world.

And Allah says to him, directly: **your Lord — He — will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection** concerning precisely this dispute.

He does not say: here is the argument that will finally silence them.
He does not say: here is the proof that will change their minds.
He says: **the resolution has already been scheduled.**

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here, because this can be misread as resignation. As giving up.

It is not.

The Prophet ﷺ kept conveying. He kept presenting the Quran. The believers kept calling people. The evidence kept being offered. The effort did not stop. What changed was not the effort. What changed was the *emotional relationship to the outcome* of that effort.

When you genuinely believe that the resolution is scheduled — that on a specific day, before the only Judge whose judgment is both final and true, the case will be heard completely — something shifts in you. You are freed from the exhausting project of *forcing* the resolution to happen now. Of needing every person you argue with to admit you're right before you can have peace. Of measuring your standing by whether today's tribunal rendered the correct verdict.

There is a word for this disposition. Not resignation. **Tawakkul** — active transfer of jurisdiction. You are not abandoning the truth. You are placing the truth in the hands of the One whose judgment you actually trust.

And this changes the quality of how you carry your disputes. The believer who has genuinely internalized this ayah is still engaged, still truthful, still pushing back where that is needed — but without the particular bitterness that accumulates when you feel the truth will be lost forever if you don't win the argument today. The rage of the person who believes that if they lose this argument, the truth loses with it permanently — that particular rage is what this ayah addresses.

The guided leaders of ayah 24 are described with two qualities held simultaneously: *ṣabarū* — they were patient — *wa kānū bi-āyātinā yūqinūn* — and they were certain of Our signs. Patient *and* certain. Not patient *instead of* certain. Both, together. Certain enough not to need external confirmation right now. Patient enough to wait for the confirmation that is already on its way.

This is one of the most practically transformative things a believer can carry: **you do not need to win the argument today. The final verdict is not today's to give.**

[PAUSE]

There is a question this raises, and I want to name it honestly rather than skip past it. Doesn't this become a justification for accepting injustice? For never pushing back? For quietism in the face of wrong?

Here is the distinction. There is pushing back through legitimate means — speaking truth, seeking justice, correcting falsehood, refusing to let lies stand unchallenged where you have the ability to challenge them. That is often obligatory. That is what the prophets did.

And there is the *emotional demand* that the resolution happen on your timeline, through your chosen method, producing the acknowledgment you need from the people you need it from. That is the weight this ayah releases you from.

You can act for justice without depending on justice being done in your lifetime. That is, in fact, the definition of prophetic conduct.

### Theme Two: Why the Day of Standing Is the Only Real Resolution

Now I want to take you to the phrase at the center of this ayah's second half.

يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ — *the Day of the Resurrection.*

Most translations render *qiyāmah* as "Resurrection" — which is correct. But the root is *qāma* — to stand. To rise. To take an upright, irreversible posture. Al-Qiyāmah is the Day when everything rises. When everything stands. When the option of remaining seated, of not showing up, of letting the case continue indefinitely by default — is removed.

Consider how every human system of resolution has a fatal flaw: it can be refused.

The accused can refuse to appear. The evidence can be concealed or destroyed. The judge can be corrupted. The verdict can be appealed, endlessly. And even if all of those fail — people can simply decline to accept the verdict. History can be rewritten. New interpreters arrive in later centuries and overturn the consensus. The powerful write the accounts, and the powerful's version survives.

This is not pessimism. It is simply true. Every human resolution is provisional.

But يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ is categorically different. Everyone stands — there is no absence, no non-appearance, no hiding behind unavailability. Everything is known — not the partial record that reached the court, but everything. The Judge has no stake in any party — no favoritism, no bias, no prior relationship that tilts the scale. And there is no appeal after the faṣl. The cut is final.

[PAUSE]

But here is the deepest layer of why divine faṣl is categorically different from every human form of resolution.

Human courts produce **verdicts about behavior**. Divine judgment produces **exposure of truth**.

Those are not the same thing.

A court can find you not guilty and you can know in your own heart that you were. A court can convict you and you know you were innocent. The verdict and the truth are related — but they are not identical. Human resolution operates on evidence, procedure, and the distribution of power. Divine resolution operates on *what actually happened*.

The word *faṣl* when used in the context of reality rather than merely law carries this sense: not just ruling on the dispute, but making visible which version of the story was the real one. Not just deciding who wins — but revealing what was always true to all who were ever part of the dispute.

This is why, for the believer, entrusting disputes to Yawm al-Qiyāmah is not merely hoping for a favorable verdict. It is trusting that reality will finally be *seen* — fully, completely, without the fog of human limitation, by every party, with no further ability to deny or reinterpret.

For the one who has been on the receiving end of sustained injustice — this is not a cold comfort. It is an immense one. Because what you wanted was never just to "win." What you wanted was for the truth to be *known*. To be fully visible. To be undeniable. And on that Day, it will be — not just known by you, but known by all.

[PAUSE — major pause]

There is one more thing I want to sit with before we leave this theme.

The ayah says: فِيمَا كَانُوا فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ — *"concerning that over which they used to differ."*

Notice: *used to*. Past tense. As though, from the vantage point of that Day, the disagreement is already finished. Already in the past.

But in our world, the disagreement is still happening. Yakhtālifūn — imperfect, present, ongoing.

So Allah is speaking from the perspective of eternity about something that is still very much alive in time. The dispute that feels so total, so present, so inescapable right now — is already, from the vantage of Yawm al-Qiyāmah, a past thing. Already described with past tense in the divine speech.

This is the believer's particular form of seeing. Not denial of present reality. But the capacity to hold both directions of time simultaneously — to know with certainty what has already been settled in the divine arrangement, even while living inside the apparent uncertainty of now.

The resolution is not missing. It is not delayed indefinitely. It is coming — and from the perspective of the One who speaks, it has already occurred.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Before we bring everything together, I want to show you something about this ayah's place across the entire Quran.

This same formula — *"your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they used to differ"* — appears in Surah An-Nahl (16:124) as well. Almost word for word. Two ayahs in two different surahs, spoken in different contexts, carrying the same essential announcement: the dispute has a resolution date.

But there is one difference. In An-Nahl 16:124, Allah uses the verb **يَحْكُمُ** — *He will rule, He will govern, He will issue the decree*. That root ح-ك-م emphasizes *authority* — the judge's right and power to pronounce the verdict. The governance of the one who rules.

Here in As-Sajdah, Allah uses **يَفْصِلُ** — *He will cut, He will separate, He will cut clean*.

Why the different word here?

Because As-Sajdah is a surah about *distinction*. From its first ayah to its last, it has been drawing lines. Between fabrication and revelation. Between the heart that opens and the heart that hardens. Between the one who prostrates and the one who walks away. Between the patience of the guided leaders and the mockery of those who dispute. The surah has been performing faṣl — separation, distinction — as its fundamental literary act, across every contrast it draws.

And now, in ayah 25, it announces: what this surah has been doing linguistically all along, Allah will do finally and completely on the Day of Rising.

The architecture of the surah is the message. The surah that kept making distinctions ends by announcing the final Distinction.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

What the grammar revealed and what the themes opened are the same insight, seen from two directions.

The ḍamīr al-faṣl (هُوَ) and the verb yafṣilu are both about the same reality: **exclusive, decisive, divine jurisdiction**. One is the grammar of *who*. One is the grammar of *what He will do*. Together: there is one Judge, and His judgment is a clean cut that ends the scattering.

The double فِي that wraps the disputants inside their arguments, and the theme of the believer living inside unresolved truth — they describe the same human condition. People inside disagreements that feel total and inescapable, unable to see beyond the walls of the container they are living in.

And the imperfect tense of yakhtālifūn — still happening, still ongoing, present — is answered by the imperfect tense of yafṣilu — coming, certain, already scheduled from the perspective of eternity. The ongoing human condition is met by the incoming divine action.

The whole ayah is a single movement: from the immersive, inescapable human ikhtilāf — to the precise, exclusive, inevitable divine faṣl.

### Questions to Carry

Take these as genuine invitations, not rhetorical closings:

1. What dispute in your life have you been trying to resolve on *your* timeline — and what would genuinely change if you transferred the jurisdiction to the One described in this ayah?

2. Is your certainty about truth actually certainty — or is it certainty *plus* anxiety about whether anyone else will confirm it? Can you separate those two things? What would the certainty feel like without the anxiety?

3. The guided leaders of ayah 24 were described with two qualities together: patience and certainty. Which one is harder for you right now, in the specific disputes you are carrying?

4. If you knew with complete conviction that every false thing ever said about you would be fully visible on Yawm al-Qiyāmah — would that change how you respond to it today? What specifically would be different?

5. Is there someone in your life whose wrongs toward you you have been holding tightly — partly because you do not trust that the resolution will ever come without your effort to force it? What would it mean to place that case with the Judge described in this ayah?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**Every dispute that cannot be resolved in this world has already been assigned a resolution date — and that date has the only Judge whose faṣl is both final and fully true.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

The surah placed this ayah immediately after the portrait of the guided leaders — patient and certain. Those two words are not describing emotional states. They are describing a relationship with time. Patient means: I do not need this resolved today. Certain means: I know it will be resolved.

That is what this ayah is inviting you into. Not to stop caring about truth. Not to stop seeking justice. But to carry your disputes with a different center of gravity — to stop needing every argument to reach its verdict tonight, because you know the final verdict is not tonight's to give.

The resolution is not missing. It is not indefinitely deferred. It is coming, as certain as the Day of Standing itself.

And the One who will cut will cut with knowledge of everything — including the things only you and He know.

*And let our certainty in Your faṣl on the Day of Resurrection be a source of rest for us in this world, and a light by which we walk quietly, steadily, toward the Day of meeting You.*
