# The Suppressed Vision and the Yaqīn That Came Too Late

## Surah As-Sajdah, Ayah 12

---

## Introduction: The Sentence That Cannot Finish

Let me read this ayah to you slowly. In Arabic first, then in English.

**وَلَوْ تَرَىٰٓ إِذِ ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ نَاكِسُواْ رُءُوسِهِمْ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ رَبَّنَآ أَبْصَرْنَا وَسَمِعْنَا فَٱرْجِعْنَا نَعْمَلْ صَـٰلِحًا إِنَّا مُوقِنُونَ**

*Wa law tarā idhi-l-mujrimūna nākisū ruʾūsihim ʿinda Rabbihim — Rabbanā abṣarnā wa samiʿnā fa-rjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan, innā mūqinūn.*

"If only you could see when the criminals are hanging their heads low before their Lord — 'Our Lord, we have seen and we have heard. So return us — we will work righteousness. We are now certain.'"

[PAUSE]

Before we step inside this ayah, I want you to rebuild the world it was first spoken into.

Makkah. The early years. The Prophet ﷺ is standing in a city that has heard the Quran for years now. They have heard the descriptions of the Last Day a hundred times. They have heard about the trumpet. They have heard about the standing. They have heard about the questioning. And they have, every single time, walked away saying some version of the same thing: *prove it. Show it to me. Let me see it with my own eyes, and then I'll believe.*

This was not an unusual demand. It was the demand of an entire civilization. The Quraysh wanted **proof in the form of sight.** They wanted Allah to roll back the curtain on the unseen and let them look. Until that happened, they would treat the Messenger's warnings as poetry — beautiful, perhaps, but optional.

And the surah that contains this ayah — Surah As-Sajdah — is one long, patient response to that demand. It opens with creation. It moves through death. It moves through judgment. And then it lands here, on this ayah, where Allah essentially says: *you want to see? Let me show you the moment you finally see. Let me show you what your demand looks like when it is granted — but granted too late.*

This is the hook I want you to hold as we walk in.

Allah begins this ayah with the words **wa law tarā** — "If only you could see..."

And then He never finishes the sentence.

[PAUSE]

The "if only you could see... what?" never comes. The grammatical structure is left open. Hanging. Like a door that opens onto something the language cannot follow into.

That is where we are about to walk.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Conditional That Refuses to End

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open this word.

In Arabic, when you say **law** — "if" — you are setting up what grammarians call a conditional. It has two halves. The first half is the condition: *if such-and-such were the case.* The second half is the consequence: *then such-and-such would follow.* The grammarians have a name for the second half. They call it the **jawāb** — the "answer" of the condition.

In English, we do the same thing. *If you had been there... you would have cried.* The first half sets up the scene. The second half tells you what would have happened. Both halves are required to complete the sentence.

Now. In normal Arabic, if you leave off the *jawāb*, your sentence is broken. It's like saying "If you had been there..." and then walking away. The listener is left waiting for the rest.

But there is one situation in which the classical rhetoricians say you are allowed — even encouraged — to leave off the *jawāb*. And it is this:

**When what would have followed is so overwhelming, so vast, so beyond capturing, that any specific answer you give would diminish it.**

You leave it off precisely because finishing it would make it smaller.

The Arabs would say, in praise of someone: *law taʿrifu fulānan...* — "If only you knew so-and-so..." And then they would stop. Because the moment you say "you would love him" or "you would admire him" or "you would never forget him" — you have reduced him to that one thing. Leaving the sentence open lets the listener's imagination expand into the silence.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah does.

**Wa law tarā** — "If only you could see..."

See **what** would happen to you? See **what** state you would be in? See **what** you would feel?

Allah does not finish it.

He does not say "you would weep." He does not say "you would tremble." He does not say "you would fall on your face." He does not say "your heart would break."

Because every single one of those is too small.

The scene that comes after **wa law tarā** is so overwhelming, so soul-shattering, that no specific response could contain it. So Allah leaves the door open and lets you fill it with whatever your soul is capable of imagining. And then, He suggests, *whatever you imagined — it is not enough.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. The verb **tarā** is in the second-person singular. *If only **you** could see.* Grammatically, this is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ. But every classical commentator notes that when Allah uses this construction, the address is being deliberately broadened. The "you" is the reader. The "you" is the listener. The "you" is me. The "you" is you.

The grammar reaches through the page and points directly at the person reading.

**If only you — yes, you, right now — could see what is going to happen.**

What this invites us to consider is this: there is a scene being described in this ayah that Allah believes, if we could only see it, would change us. Permanently. Completely. And the only reason He is describing it in words is because words are the only tool we have on this side of the veil. The actual sight is being held back from us. For now.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: *Nākisū Ruʾūsihim* — The Reversal of the Upright

Let me read you the next part again. *Idhi-l-mujrimūna nākisū ruʾūsihim ʿinda Rabbihim.*

"When the criminals are **hanging their heads low** before their Lord."

I want to sit with the word **nākisū** for a long time.

The root is *n-k-s*. And in classical Arabic lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib — this root has one core meaning: **to flip something upside down. To invert it. To reverse it from its natural orientation.**

A *naksa* is a relapse — when someone recovers from an illness and then "flips back" into it. A *manakkas* is something stood on its head. When the Arabs wanted to describe a man whose fortunes had reversed completely — who was once high and is now low — they used this root.

So **nākisū ruʾūsihim** does not just mean "their heads are down." It means **their heads have been flipped from their natural position.**

To understand the weight of this, you need to understand what the head means in Arabic.

In the Arabic imagination — and honestly, in the human imagination universally — the head held high is the symbol of dignity. Of self-possession. Of a person who knows who they are. The Arabs had an entire vocabulary of pride built around this: *rafīʿ ar-raʾs* (one whose head is raised), *ʿalī al-jabīn* (one whose forehead is high), *shāmikh al-anf* (one whose nose points upward). The head, in their poetry, is the seat of the *self*.

And in the Quran, the head held upright is what distinguishes the human from every other creature on earth. Allah says elsewhere that He made the human *fī aḥsani taqwīm* — in the most upright form. The vertical posture is the *human* posture. To walk upright is to walk as a human.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the criminals in this ayah.

They are not just looking down. They are not just embarrassed. The word **nākisū** is telling you their entire orientation has been **flipped**.

The human who walked through the world with their head held high, looking down on the message, looking down on the messenger, looking down on the believers, looking down on the warnings — that human is now standing in a position they have **never been in their entire life.**

Head hanging. Eyes on the ground. The very posture they refused to take in *rukūʿ*, in *sujūd*, in *duʿāʾ* — they are now locked into it permanently. Not by choice. By the gravity of the moment itself.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. In Surah As-Sajdah, just three ayāt after this one, Allah will describe the believers. And the description He chooses is **fall on their faces in prostration** (32:15) — the moment that gives this surah its name.

So in the space of a few ayāt, the surah shows you the two human postures:

The one who lowers his head **now**, voluntarily, in *sajdah*.
And the one who has his head lowered **then**, involuntarily, in *nuks*.

Every single human being will, eventually, end up in one of those two postures. The only choice is **which**. And the only choice is **when**.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is something almost unbearable. Every prostration we make now is an act of *choosing the posture before it is chosen for us.* Every time we lower our forehead to the ground while we still have the option to refuse, we are saying: *I would rather take this position willingly than have it taken from me.*

The believer rehearses the posture of judgment, daily, on their own initiative. The denier rehearses the posture of dignity, daily — and discovers, on that Day, that the dignity was never theirs.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: *Abṣarnā wa Samiʿnā* — The Order That Has Been Reversed

Now look at what they say. *Rabbanā **abṣarnā wa samiʿnā**.* "Our Lord, we have seen and we have heard."

I want you to notice the order. **Seeing first. Hearing second.**

In the Quran, when Allah describes His own knowledge — when He describes the human faculties — the order is almost always the reverse. *As-samīʿ al-baṣīr.* The All-Hearing, the All-Seeing. *Innī samīʿun baṣīr.* "Indeed I am Hearing, Seeing." *Jaʿalnā lahum samʿan wa abṣāran wa afʾidatan.* "We gave them hearing and sight and hearts." The sequence — across hundreds of ayāt — is **hearing, then sight.**

Why?

Because in the *dunyā*, this is how knowledge arrives. You **hear** about something before you **see** it. You hear about Allah before you see the signs of Allah. You hear about the Last Day before any sign of it appears. You hear about the Messenger ﷺ before you meet his teaching. Hearing is the *first* faculty of faith. It is the faculty by which the unseen reaches us.

That is why, in the *dunyā*, **hearing precedes sight.**

[PAUSE]

Now look at the criminals.

They flip the order.

**Abṣarnā wa samiʿnā.** "We have **seen** and we have **heard**."

What this is signaling — what every classical mufassir noticed — is that the criminals are speaking from a place where **sight has come first**. The veil has been pulled back. The unseen has become visible. The angels are visible. The Fire is visible. The throne is visible. Their own deeds, laid out before them, are visible.

And **only after seeing** are they now able to honestly say *we hear.*

Stay with this.

In the *dunyā* — when they had the chance to be saved — they had hearing without sight. Allah was giving them His speech through His Messenger. The words were entering their ears. And they refused to act on those words because they kept demanding: *show me, show me, show me.*

In the *ākhira* — when it is far too late — they finally have sight. And the moment they have sight, they suddenly "hear." Not because the sound has changed. But because **their refusal has nothing left to refuse with.**

The order of the verbs is a confession. It is them admitting: *we are people who would only ever truly hear when we could no longer doubt what we were seeing.*

And the heartbreak of the ayah is that Allah designed the *dunyā* precisely so that those who would only believe with sight could never be saved. Because the entire point of the test is that you act on the hearing **before** the sight arrives.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is the architecture of our own demand for proof. How often, in our own hearts, do we say to Allah some version of *just show me, and then I'll change.* Just give me the job, and I'll start praying. Just heal me, and I'll wear the hijab. Just bring him back, and I'll trust You. Just let me see, and I'll believe.

This ayah is whispering: that demand is the criminal's demand. And the only difference between the criminal who says it on that Day and the believer who refuses to say it now is **timing**.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So look at what the linguistic construction has shown us so far. An unfinished conditional that points to a scene too large to contain. A posture that flips the entire orientation of the human being. An order of verbs that confesses the corruption of how they had been listening all along.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Economy of Certainty — Yaqīn Has a Closing Window

I want to start somewhere very modern.

There is a strange feature of human cognition that psychologists have studied for decades. They call it **hindsight bias**. The technical phenomenon is simple: once an outcome has occurred, the human mind retroactively believes it knew that outcome was coming all along. After a stock crashes, people say *I knew it.* After a relationship ends, they say *I saw the signs.* After a election goes one way, half the country says *it was obvious.*

The mind has an almost magnetic pull toward what one researcher called "I-knew-it-all-along-ism." Even when we did not know. Even when we did not see. Even when the evidence we had was not enough to justify the confidence we now feel.

And here is the cruel part: the certainty that arrives **after** the outcome is **useless**. It cannot be acted on. It cannot change anything. It can only torture us with the question of *why didn't I see it sooner?*

This is the psychology of the criminals in this ayah.

[PAUSE]

Now read the closing phrase again. *Innā **mūqinūn**.*

"Indeed, we are now **certain**."

The word **mūqinūn** comes from the root *y-q-n* — *yaqīn*. And *yaqīn* is one of the most loaded words in the entire Islamic vocabulary. It is the highest grade of belief. It is belief without doubt, without trembling, without "but what if." When Ibrahim عليه السلام asked Allah to show him how the dead are raised and was asked *do you not believe?*, his answer was *yes, but so my heart can be at certainty* — *li-yaṭmaʾinna qalbī*. He had belief. He was reaching for *yaqīn*.

*Yaqīn* is the destination of every believer's lifelong journey.

And now look at what the criminals are saying.

They are saying: *we have arrived.* We have what every prophet's heart longed for. We have what every righteous person prayed for. We have **yaqīn**.

[PAUSE]

But there is a feature of the spiritual economy that the criminals have just discovered too late. *Yaqīn* — like every other treasure in this religion — **has a closing window.**

It is valued only when it is **purchased on credit.** When you believe *before* you can see. When you trust *before* you have proof. When you obey *before* the consequences become visible. That is the *yaqīn* that buys Paradise.

The *yaqīn* of the criminals is *yaqīn* purchased after the close of the market.

It is a beautiful currency in a country that no longer accepts it.

[PAUSE]

This is the core insight of the ayah. The Quran is not describing a group of people who *failed to believe*. They believe now. The Quran is describing a group of people who **believed too late** — and discovered that timing was the entire test.

Think about what this changes.

It means that the question is not whether you will eventually accept the truth. **Every human being eventually accepts the truth.** The criminals in this ayah have accepted it. They are uttering the *shahādah* of belief. They are calling Allah *Rabbanā* — "Our Lord." They are confessing they have heard. They are confessing they have seen. They are confessing they are certain.

Every single thing the believer says, the criminal will eventually say. *Eventually.*

The difference is not in the content of the belief.
The difference is in **when** the belief arrives.

[PAUSE]

This should reframe everything about how we think about *imān*. Faith is not, in the deepest sense, a matter of *what is true*. It is a matter of *what we are willing to act on **before** it is forced upon us*. The believer is someone who, with limited evidence, **chooses** to live as though the unseen is real. The denier is someone who, with the same limited evidence, refuses — until the evidence is no longer limited.

But when the evidence is no longer limited, **the choice is no longer available.**

The closing of the window is the closing of the test.

[PAUSE]

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?**

Every act of worship I perform tomorrow — every prayer, every dhikr, every restraint, every charity — is being purchased with the most valuable currency I will ever possess: **the currency of acting before I can see.** Once I can see, the currency will be worthless. So tomorrow's *ṣalāh*, prayed in a state where I cannot prove Allah is listening, is worth infinitely more than the prostrations of the criminals on the Day they could prove He was.

The window is open. The currency is in my pocket. The market is taking it. **That is the only thing that matters about today.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Return Request — And What Allah's Silence Reveals

Now look at what they ask for.

*Rabbanā... fa-rjiʿnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan.* "Our Lord... so return us — we will work righteousness."

I want you to feel the texture of this request.

They are not asking to be let into Paradise. They are not asking for forgiveness. They are not asking for intercession. They are asking for **time**.

*Return us.* Send us back. Reset the clock. Open the window again. Just one more chance. Just one more breath. Just one more month, one more day, one more *ṣalāh*. We see now. We hear now. We are certain now. *Just let us go back and do it.*

[PAUSE]

There is a moment that almost every human being experiences. It is the moment of *if only.*

*If only I had said something to her before she died.*
*If only I had taken the job when it was offered.*
*If only I had walked away from that addiction at the start.*
*If only I had been kinder to my parents while they were here.*

Every person who has lived past the age of thirty knows the bitter taste of *if only.* It is a specific kind of pain. It is the pain of seeing clearly **after** the moment of choice has closed. The pain of having the wisdom you needed yesterday — and having it today, when yesterday is no longer reachable.

Now multiply that pain by infinity.

That is what the criminals are experiencing in this ayah.

Every human "if only" is a tiny rehearsal for the *if only* of that Day. Every regret we feel in this life is a small mirror of the regret that is being described here. And the function of the small regrets, in Allah's mercy, is to **train us** to recognize the structure of the large one before we are caught inside it.

[PAUSE]

But notice something now. Notice an absence in this ayah.

Allah quotes their words. He gives us the full text of their request. *"Our Lord, we have seen and we have heard. So return us — we will work righteousness. We are now certain."*

And then He does not give us His response.

The ayah ends. The criminals' words hang in the air. And Allah moves on to the next ayah — which begins **"And if We had willed..."** — addressing us, not them.

He does not answer them in this ayah.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most devastating uses of silence in the Quran. The criminals are pleading. They are using the most intimate word in the Arabic language for the relationship with God — *Rabbanā*, "Our Lord." They are confessing everything that should have been confessed. They are promising everything that should have been promised. And the response... is nothing.

In other ayāt elsewhere in the Quran, Allah does respond. In Sūrat al-Muʾminūn He says, in response to a nearly identical request, *kallā* — "No." Just one word. In Sūrat Fāṭir He says *did We not give you a lifetime in which whoever wanted to take heed could have taken heed?* (35:37).

But here, in As-Sajdah, He gives them **nothing.** Not even a "no."

What this invites us to consider is something terrifying. The most painful thing that can happen to a soul is not punishment. It is **being addressed by silence by the One whose Speech created you.** To call upon your Lord and receive no reply. To plead with the One who said *udʿūnī astajib lakum* — "Call upon Me, I will respond" — and to discover, on the only day when the response would have mattered most, that the door of *duʿāʾ* has been sealed shut.

The criminals on that Day are not in Hell yet. They are at the threshold. And the silence they are receiving at the threshold is, perhaps, the first taste of what Hell really is. Not flame. **Distance.** The absence of the Voice that, in this *dunyā*, was always reaching out toward them and always being refused.

[PAUSE]

Here is the inverse, and it is the most beautiful inverse in the religion.

**Every *duʿāʾ* you make today is a response to the response Allah is *already giving you.*** The fact that you are calling on Him means He has already opened the door to your call. The fact that you turn to Him in your moments of need means He has already turned to you and pulled your heart in that direction. The criminals on that Day are calling without an open door. You, today, in the *dunyā*, have the door propped open and a voice from the other side saying *keep calling, I am listening, I am listening, I am listening.*

The horror of that ayah is also the gift of this moment.

[PAUSE]

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?**

Every *duʿāʾ* I make tomorrow — even the smallest, even the half-distracted one, even the rushed one as I leave the house — is happening through an **open door that is going to close.** The criminals would trade every breath of their eternity for one *duʿāʾ* made while the door was open. I have that *duʿāʾ* available to me right now. I will have it available tomorrow. I will have it available next week. *Until I don't.*

The window of *duʿāʾ* is wider than I treat it. And it is also narrower than I think.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now look at how this ayah is built. Step back, and look at the whole shape.

It opens with a verb of **sight** (*law tarā* — "if only you could see").
It moves to a posture of **head** (*nākisū ruʾūsihim* — "their heads lowered").
It centers on a relationship — **before their Lord** (*ʿinda Rabbihim*).
It quotes them speaking of **sight and hearing** (*abṣarnā wa samiʿnā*).
It quotes them asking for **return** (*fa-rjiʿnā*).
It ends with their declaration of **certainty** (*innā mūqinūn*).

Notice the symmetry. The ayah opens with **what we would see if we could** — and ends with the criminals declaring **they have finally seen.** Allah's invitation to see, at the beginning of the ayah, is mirrored by the criminals' confession of having seen, at the end. The ayah is one long arc from *the sight we are spared* to *the sight that cannot be unseen.*

And the deepest stroke of the architecture: the ayah is held together by the word **Rabb**.

The ayah opens describing the *mujrimūn* standing **before their Lord** (*ʿinda **Rabbihim***).
And the only word they can begin their plea with is **Rabbanā** — "**Our** Lord."

They never stopped being His. He never stopped being theirs. The relationship was never severed. They simply refused to live as though it were real. And on that Day, the first word out of their mouths is the word they spent their whole lives refusing to say.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **unfinished "if only you could see"** is the same insight as **the closing window of yaqīn.** Allah leaves the sentence unfinished because the scene is too large for words — and the criminals' arrival at certainty is, itself, that unspeakably large scene. The grammar is hollowed out the same way the moment is hollowed out: by the magnitude of what has finally been revealed.

The **flipped posture of *nākisū*** is the same insight as **the closed door of return.** A head can be voluntarily lowered, or involuntarily lowered. A door can be voluntarily entered, or it can be sealed against you. Both images are about a body and a moment caught in a position it cannot escape.

The **reversed order of *abṣarnā* before *samiʿnā*** is the same insight as **the criminal's demand for proof.** Hearing should come first. The whole structure of *imān* depends on hearing-before-seeing. The reversal of those verbs is the autopsy report on a soul that lived its entire life backward.

Three linguistic discoveries. Two themes. **One reality**: the test of this life is whether you will trust before you can verify, bow before you are bent, call before the door closes.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

Five questions. None of them rhetorical. Carry them as you walk away.

1. **Where am I currently demanding sight before I will commit to hearing?** What in my life have I made conditional on Allah "showing me" first — and what would it mean to act before He shows me?

2. **What posture have I refused to take voluntarily that I would not want to be taken for me?** Is there a *sajdah* I have been delaying? A humility I have been postponing? A surrender I have been arguing with?

3. **If my future self could speak to me right now with the *yaqīn* of the criminals in this ayah, what would they beg me to do today?**

4. **The criminals in this ayah will say "Rabbanā." Am I living a life that earns me the right to say it earlier?**

5. **If Allah granted me, tomorrow, the *duʿāʾ* of return that the criminals are denied — what would I actually go back and do differently? And what is stopping me from doing those things right now, while the door is open?**

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The criminals on that Day will say every word the believers say today — but they will say it on the wrong side of the closing door.**

### The Closing Invitation

You walked into this reflection with a head that is not lowered. You walked in with a sentence that has not been finished. You walked in with a door that has not closed.

That is the gift of this ayah. Not the warning. The **gift**.

This ayah is not, in the deepest sense, about the criminals. It is about you. It is about the strange and almost inconceivable mercy that you are reading these words **before** the scene they describe is the scene you are standing in. That window — the window in which you can hear about that scene without yet being inside it — is the only place in the entire arc of your existence where the choice is yours. Everywhere before this window, you did not exist. Everywhere after this window, the choice will have been made for you.

There is no obligation in this reflection. There is only an invitation: **use the window.** Pray one more *rakʿah* than you would have. Make one *duʿāʾ* you have been postponing. Forgive one person whose face has hardened in your memory. Lower your head once, voluntarily, before the world lowers it for you involuntarily.

The criminals would give the entire weight of their eternity for what you have in the next hour.

### A Brief Du'a

*Rabbanā,* we have heard. Open our hearing further before You open our sight. Let us bow now, while bowing is still a choice, so that we never stand among those whose heads are lowered against their will. Keep the door of our *duʿāʾ* open, and keep our tongues turned toward it, until the breath You gave us is returned to You — and let our return to You be the return of those who saw You with the eye of the heart before they saw You with the eye of the body. *Āmīn.*
