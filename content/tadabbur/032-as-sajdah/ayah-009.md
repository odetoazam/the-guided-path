# The Breath, the Hearing, the Sight, the Hearts — And Still So Little Thanks

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
ثُمَّ سَوَّىٰهُ وَنَفَخَ فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِهِۦ ۖ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَـٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ ۚ قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
Thumma sawwāhu wa nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi, wa jaʿala lakumu as-samʿa wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidah — qalīlan mā tashkurūn.

**Translation:**
Then He proportioned him and breathed into him from His [own] spirit, and made for you hearing and sight and hearts — little is the thanks you give.

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

To enter this ayah, you have to feel the size of the surah it lives in.

Surah As-Sajdah is short. It is dense. It opens with three letters — *Alif-Lām-Mīm* — and then a single declaration: this book is truth from the Lord of all worlds. No doubt. No defense. Just a verdict.

And then, before the surah does anything else, Allah does something that should startle us. He **answers the question nobody asked Him out loud.**

The Makkans who first heard this surah were not asking about prayer. They were not asking about charity. They were asking — in their hearts, in their arguments, in the late-night doubts that visit every person who has ever walked under a sky — *what is a human being? Where did we come from? Why do we matter? And why, if we matter, do we feel so small?*

Allah does not lecture them on theology. He walks them through a sequence.

Ayah 4: the heavens and the earth, made in six days.
Ayah 5: time itself — a day that is a thousand years of your counting.
Ayah 6: He knows the unseen and the seen.
Ayah 7: He perfected everything He created.
Ayah 8: He began the creation of man from clay, then made his progeny from a despised fluid.

And then — ayah 9. The one we are sitting with today.

[PAUSE]

Notice where we are. Allah has just reminded us that we were clay. That we came from a fluid the human soul recoils from. He has positioned us low. Very low. Lower than we usually like to position ourselves.

And then, into that low place, He breathes.

Here is the hook. Hold it lightly, because it will only land later. **This ayah gives you four enormous gifts — a body proportioned by Allah's hand, a spirit breathed from His own, hearing, sight, hearts — and then ends with a sentence so short and so devastating that for centuries scholars have stopped at it and could not move.** Five gifts in nine words. And then four words of verdict.

What is the verdict? And why is it placed *here*, of all places, right after the most intimate moment of human creation?

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

There are three things in this ayah we need to open carefully. Not because they are decorative — but because each one carries the entire meaning of the verse on its shoulders.

The first is the word *sawwāhu* — He proportioned him.
The second is the phrase *nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi* — He breathed into him from His spirit.
The third is the ending — *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — little is the thanks you give.

We will move through them slowly.

### Element One: *Sawwāhu* — "He Proportioned Him"

Before we touch this word, I want to teach you a concept.

In any language, there are verbs that mean simply *to make*. And there are verbs that mean *to make something in exact balance*. English has the second kind too — we say "calibrate." We say "tune." We say "level." These verbs do not just mean construction. They mean construction with a target in mind, where every part has to be in correct relationship with every other part.

Now look at the Arabic word: *sawwā*.

The root is **s-w-y**. From this root we get the word *sawāʾ* — which means *equal*, *balanced*, *level*. From this root we get *istawā* — which can mean to rise up, to settle evenly, to come into perfect equilibrium. And from this root we get *sawwā* — to proportion, to balance out, to bring into perfect symmetry.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says *thumma sawwāhu* — "then He proportioned him" — He is not saying *He made him*. He has another word for that. He used it in the previous ayah: *badaʾa khalq al-insān*. "He began the creation of man." That is making.

This is something different. This is **after the making.** This is the calibration.

Let's sit with this for a moment.

Notice how your body works. Your two eyes are positioned so that the images overlap exactly enough to give you depth. If they were a millimeter further apart, you would see the world flatter. Your jaw closes in such a way that the teeth meet — not approximately, but precisely — at a pressure your skull can absorb. Your inner ear has three semicircular canals at perfect right angles to each other so that you know where "up" is even with your eyes closed. Your spine has four curves that distribute the weight of your head across your hips so that you can walk upright for eighty years without collapsing.

None of this is "making." All of this is **calibration**.

The Arabic verb *sawwā* is in **Form II** — a form which, in Arabic grammar, intensifies and concentrates the action of the verb. It does not mean Allah balanced him a little. It means Allah balanced him *deliberately, completely, every part to every other part*.

Here is what is remarkable. The word *sawwā* implies that there was something to balance *against*. You do not "proportion" something unless you have a standard. The standard is built into the word itself.

So already, in three syllables — *thumma sawwāhu* — Allah has told us: you were not assembled. You were tuned.

Now stay with me, because what comes next would be impossible without this first step.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi* — "And Breathed Into Him From His Spirit"

This is one of the most carefully constructed phrases in the entire Quran. Every word in it is doing work. We have to take them one at a time.

Let me teach you something about Arabic prepositions before we begin.

In English, we say "breathed into him a spirit." It is a transaction. Something is given; something is received. Done.

Arabic can say it that way too. But Arabic can also say it a **different** way — one that does not exist with the same precision in English. Arabic has a preposition called *min*. *Min* usually means "from." But classical grammarians spent centuries dividing *min* into categories, because it does not always do the same thing. Sometimes *min* means *from the source of*. Sometimes *min* means *some of*. Sometimes *min* indicates *the kind of thing*.

Look at the ayah again. Allah does not say *nafakha fīhi rūḥahu* — "He breathed into him His spirit." He says *nafakha fīhi **min** rūḥihi* — "He breathed into him **from** His spirit."

That little word — *min* — changes everything.

Because here is the question every classical scholar had to answer: what does it mean for Allah to breathe into the human being "from" His spirit?

Three classical positions sit on this ayah. I want you to receive them not as three competing answers but as **three camera angles on the same reality.**

The first camera angle: *min* here indicates **honor by association**. The way we say "the House of Allah" — not because Allah lives in it, but because He has honored it with attribution to Himself. On this reading, *min rūḥihi* means: a spirit honored by being attributed to Allah. A gift He claims as His own.

The second camera angle: *min* here indicates **the source**. Not that any part of Allah's essence is inside the human being — far above that — but that the *rūḥ* itself originates from a command, a creation, a reality whose source is entirely Allah and nothing else. On this reading, *min rūḥihi* means: from a spirit whose only origin is Him.

The third camera angle: *min* here is the *min* of **partition** in a metaphorical sense — not partitioning the Divine, which is impossible, but partitioning a special class of created thing called *rūḥ*. On this reading, *min rūḥihi* means: from that special spirit that belongs to Him alone to give.

Notice what all three have in common. None of them — *none of them* — say the human being contains a piece of God. All three insist on Allah's transcendence. But all three also agree on one thing:

**Whatever the** *rūḥ* **is, it is too important for Allah to attribute it to anyone or anything other than Himself.**

[PAUSE]

He could have said *nafakha fīhi rūḥan* — "He breathed into him a spirit." That would have been grammatically clean. That would have been theologically safe.

He did not. He said *min rūḥihi* — "from His spirit."

Why?

Here is what this invites us to consider. Every human being walking on this earth has been touched, in the moment of becoming a human being, by a breath that Allah has chosen to call **His**. Not a piece of Him. Not a fragment of His essence. But a gift so precious that He attaches His name to it.

The clay was just clay. After *sawwā*, the clay was perfectly calibrated clay. But it was still — biologically speaking — a magnificently arranged piece of dirt.

Until the breath.

And after the breath, the dirt is something else entirely. It is a being whose interior — whose spirit, whose center, whose presence — has been attributed by Allah Himself to Allah Himself.

Think about what this does to your idea of yourself.

You walk into a room. You feel small. You feel forgotten. You feel that the world has not noticed you. And somewhere in your chest, breathing, is a *rūḥ* that Allah called **His**.

[PAUSE — sit here]

Now, one more thing about this phrase, because the grammar is showing us something else.

The verb *nafakha* is **past tense**. *He breathed*. Done. Completed. It happened.

But the thing that was breathed — the *rūḥ* — is still in you. Right now. Breathing your chest up and down as you read this. The verb is past; the gift is present.

This is what classical Arabic grammarians sometimes called *al-fiʿl al-mādī ʿalā maʿnā al-istimrār* — a past-tense verb whose effect extends into the present. The breath happened once. The breath has never stopped.

### Element Three: The Shift — From "Him" to "You"

Now I want you to notice something that almost every reader misses.

Read the ayah in Arabic again, slowly. *Thumma sawwā**hu** wa nafakha fīhi min rūḥihi — wa jaʿala **lakum** us-samʿa wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidah.*

Do you see it?

"He proportioned **him**. He breathed into **him**. And He made for **you** hearing and sight and hearts."

The pronoun changes.

The first part of the ayah is about **him** — third person, singular, masculine. It is about Adam. About the human being as an idea, a species, a creation event.

And then — without warning, without transition, without even a comma in classical Arabic grammar that would prepare you — Allah turns and addresses **you**.

Arabic rhetoricians have a name for this. They call it *iltifāt* — literally, "turning the face." It is the rhetorical move where the speaker, mid-sentence, rotates from talking *about* someone to talking *to* someone. It is one of the most distinctive features of the Quran's voice.

And it is doing work here.

Stay with this. Because the *iltifāt* is the hinge on which the entire ayah turns.

Allah is teaching us: the creation of Adam was not a museum piece. The breath that entered him entered **you**. The faculties He made for him are the faculties He made **for you**. The verdict that comes at the end of this ayah is not a verdict on Adam. It is a verdict on **you**.

You were not invited into this ayah as a spectator. You were ambushed into it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three Continued: *As-Samʿa wal-Abṣāra wal-Afʾidah* — "The Hearing, the Sights, and the Hearts"

Now we come to the three gifts Allah names. And every word here is precise.

Look at the grammar. *As-samʿ* — hearing — is **singular**. *Al-abṣār* — sights — is **plural**. *Al-afʾidah* — hearts — is also **plural**.

This is not random. The grammarians spent centuries on this asymmetry.

Why is hearing singular while sight and heart are plural?

Here is the classical reading. **Hearing is one because what is heard is one.** Sound enters from every direction; both ears process the same incoming reality. You cannot point your ear at one thing while ignoring another the way you can with your eyes. Hearing is the **passive** sense — you cannot close your ears. Sound comes to you, and it comes as a single stream.

**Sight is plural because what is seen is multiple.** You can look here, then there. You can choose. You can focus. You can turn your face. Sight is the **active** sense — you direct it.

And **hearts are plural because they are individual.** Every person has his own. No two are alike. No two carry the same burden. No two arrive at the same conclusion. *Al-afʾidah* is the plural of *fuʾād* — and *fuʾād* in classical Arabic does not just mean "heart." It means the heart at its most alive, the heart when it is burning, the heart when it is responsive.

The root *f-ʾ-d* is connected to *tafaʾʾud* — to glow, to be lit, to burn. The *fuʾād* is not the muscle in your chest. It is the part of you that catches fire when truth touches it.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has given us. Three faculties. Three doorways.

The first — hearing — is how revelation enters. The Quran is heard before it is read. The first command to the Prophet ﷺ was *iqraʾ* — recite — and the angel was heard, not seen.

The second — sight — is how the signs of creation enter. *In the heavens and the earth are signs for those who reflect.* You see the mountains. You see the stars. You see the faces of the people you love age in front of you.

The third — *fuʾād* — is the inner court where the case is decided. Hearing brings the witness. Sight brings the evidence. And the *fuʾād* is the judge.

And here's what's remarkable. Allah did not name the *ʿaql* — the intellect. He did not name the mind. He named the **glowing heart**. Because the deciding faculty in the human being, in Quranic anthropology, is not the calculating brain. It is the burning heart.

[PAUSE — sit with this]

### The Linguistic Bridge

We have walked through three elements:
- *Sawwā* — the calibration of the body.
- *Nafakha min rūḥihi* — the breath attributed to Allah Himself.
- *As-samʿ, al-abṣār, al-afʾidah* — the three faculties given to you, in the second person, by name.

The construction has shown us something. **You are not an accident.** Your body is calibrated. Your spirit is honored by being called His. Your faculties are not generic equipment — they are differentiated, specified, and given a hierarchy.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what it means to be a human being who has been given all of this and then meets the final four words of the ayah.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Anatomy of Ingratitude

I want to start somewhere that has nothing to do with the Quran. I want to start in your kitchen.

You walk in tomorrow morning. You turn the faucet. Clean water comes out. Hot, if you want it. You drink it without thinking. You brush your teeth without thinking. You wash a cup without thinking.

For most of human history, every person who ever lived on this planet woke up unsure whether they would have water that day. Whether the water they had would kill them. Whether they could spare any of it to brush their teeth. The act you just performed — turning a handle and receiving clean hot water on demand — would have been, to every one of your ancestors before about 1900, indistinguishable from a miracle.

You did not say *al-ḥamdu lillāh*.

You did not even register that something had happened.

[PAUSE]

This is the anatomy of ingratitude. And ingratitude is not — listen carefully — ingratitude is not *malice*. It is not that you sat down and decided, "I will refuse to thank Allah for the water." It is that the water has become so reliable, so present, so *given*, that you do not even see it anymore.

Familiarity is the corrosion of gratitude.

And this is the trap Allah is naming in the final words of the ayah.

*Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.* Little is the thanks you give.

Let me show you what is happening grammatically here, because the Arabic is doing something the English translation cannot capture.

*Qalīlan* — little — is placed at the **front** of the sentence. In Arabic, fronting a word puts emphasis on it. The natural word order would be *tashkurūn qalīlan* — "you give thanks a little." Instead, Allah places it like this: *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — "**little** is what you give of thanks."

Some classical commentators went further. They read *mā* not as a relative particle but as a **negation**. On that reading the phrase means: *"little — rather, you do not give thanks at all."* The *mā* makes the *qalīlan* almost ironic. Even calling your gratitude "little" is being generous.

Whichever reading you take, the effect is the same. Allah did not say *you are ungrateful*. That would have been a direct insult. He said something more devastating. He said: **whatever thanks you do give is so small that it almost does not count.**

And it sits at the end of an ayah whose beginning is overwhelming.

Look at the structure again.

He proportioned him. (Gift.)
He breathed into him from His spirit. (Gift.)
He made for you hearing. (Gift.)
And sight. (Gift.)
And hearts. (Gift.)
**Little is the thanks you give.** (Verdict.)

Five gifts. One verdict. And the verdict is not "you are an enemy." The verdict is: *you barely noticed*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because here is where this becomes personal.

Why is human beings' default state ingratitude?

The answer is hidden in the first part of the ayah. *Thumma sawwāhu* — He calibrated him. The calibration is so perfect that the human being does not feel it. You do not feel your spine holding you up. You do not feel your inner ear telling you where the floor is. You do not feel the trillions of cellular operations keeping you alive while you read this sentence.

**Allah's gifts are so well-designed that they disappear into the background.**

Your hearing only becomes visible to you when you go deaf. Your sight only becomes visible to you when it dims. Your heart only becomes visible to you when it fails. As long as the gift is functioning, the gift is invisible.

So gratitude — *shukr* — is not a feeling. It is not what happens when something *new* arrives. Gratitude is a deliberate act of *seeing what has been here the whole time*.

The grateful person is not the person with more. The grateful person is the person who has trained their attention to notice what the ungrateful person has stopped seeing.

[PAUSE — major pause]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means that the work is not to *acquire* something to be grateful for. The work is to *recover the sight* you already lost. To go back to your kitchen tomorrow morning and — before you turn the faucet — to say *bismillāh*. And after you drink — to say *al-ḥamdu lillāh*. Not as a ritual phrase. As a re-seeing. As a deliberate act of putting back into the visible field a gift that had become invisible.

This ayah is not condemning you. It is diagnosing you. And the diagnosis comes with the cure built into it. *Tashkurūn* — the verb is present tense. It is not too late. The gratitude can still be given.

### Theme Two: The Three Faculties and the Order of the Witness

Now let's turn to the second theme. And to do it, I want you to picture a courtroom.

In a courtroom there are three positions. There is the **witness stand** — where information enters. There is the **evidence table** — where physical signs are examined. And there is the **judge's bench** — where the decision is made.

Every legal proceeding in every culture works in this order. Testimony first. Evidence second. Judgment third.

Now read the ayah again. *Wa jaʿala lakumu as-samʿa wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidah.* He made for you hearing — and sight — and hearts.

**Hearing first. Sight second. Heart third.**

This order is not random. And it is not a one-time order. Allah uses this same order — *samʿ, baṣar, fuʾād* — in multiple places throughout the Quran. It is a deliberate sequence.

Why this order?

Because this is the order of how truth gets into a human being.

[PAUSE]

The first faculty — *samʿ* — is how revelation reaches you. You cannot see Allah. You cannot see the Day of Judgment. You cannot see Paradise. You cannot see the angels. Every single matter of the unseen — *al-ghayb* — enters your life through one channel: someone telling you. The Prophet ﷺ heard from Jibrīl. The Companions heard from the Prophet ﷺ. The next generation heard from the Companions. The chain of transmission of every truth that matters runs through ears.

This is why, in Quranic ethics, hearing is the **first** faculty named. Because the most important things you will ever know cannot be seen — they can only be heard.

The second faculty — *baṣar* — is how the visible signs of Allah confirm what was heard. You heard that Allah is the Creator. Then you walked outside and saw the sky. You heard that Allah is the Sustainer. Then you watched a tree push its way through concrete. The eyes confirm what the ears received.

And the third — *fuʾād* — is where it all gets weighed.

The *fuʾād* is not the receiver. The eyes and ears are receivers. The *fuʾād* is the **decider**. It is where the case closes. It is where you say *yes* or *no* to what your ears and eyes have brought you.

Here is what this means.

[PAUSE]

The reason most people miss truth is not that they lack hearing. The radio is on. The lecture is happening. The verse is being recited.

It is not that they lack sight. The signs are everywhere. The mountains are there. The faces of the dying are there.

It is that the *fuʾād* — the burning heart — has gone cold. The judge has fallen asleep at the bench. The evidence is being presented and there is nobody home to receive it.

And so Allah, in another ayah of the Quran, will say: *they have hearts with which they do not understand, and eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear* (7:179). The faculties are still there. The architecture is intact. But the deciding fire has gone out.

Now look back at where ayah 9 places the *fuʾād*.

It places it **at the end of the list of gifts** and **right before the verdict of ingratitude**. The juxtaposition is doing something. It is saying: *the gift of the burning heart was given to you so that you would respond — and you have not responded.*

The case has been presented. The witness was heard. The evidence was seen. The judge — your *fuʾād* — was given to you. And you returned a verdict of silence.

*Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means that the next time you read a verse, listen to a reminder, or witness a moment of stunning beauty in the world — and feel **nothing** — the diagnosis is not that the message failed. The diagnosis is that the *fuʾād* needs rekindling.

And the practical question becomes: *how do I rekindle the burning heart?*

The Quran's answer, scattered across many surahs, is this: through **dhikr**. Through repeated, deliberate, slow remembrance of Allah by the tongue and by the heart. The way you rebuild a fire that has gone cold is not by demanding it burn — it is by patiently adding small dry kindling and breathing on it until the embers catch.

The faculties Allah gave you in this ayah are not broken. They are dormant. The breath that He breathed into you at creation is still there. The fire that He placed in your *fuʾād* is still there — under the ash.

The work of a believer is to be the keeper of that fire.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now let's look at the whole ayah as a single piece of architecture.

> *Then He proportioned him*
>   *and breathed into him from His spirit,*
>     **and made for you**
>       *hearing*
>       *and sights*
>       *and hearts —*
>   *little is the thanks you give.*

Notice the shape.

The ayah moves from **inside out**. It begins with the body — the calibration. It moves to the spirit — the breath. Then it expands outward to the three faculties that connect the inner human being to the external world.

And then — at the exact moment the human is fully equipped, fully fitted, fully alive — the ayah ends with the verdict.

The architecture is a **funnel**. Allah pours in. Pours in. Pours in. And at the bottom of the funnel, where everything should overflow into gratitude — *qalīlan mā tashkurūn*.

The structure of the ayah is the structure of the human condition.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: *sawwā* showed us calibration, *min rūḥihi* showed us attribution to Allah Himself, and the asymmetry of singular *samʿ* and plural *abṣār* and *afʾidah* showed us that we have been given specific, individual, irreplaceable faculties.

From the thematic side: the architecture of these gifts is so perfect that they go invisible, and the diagnosis of *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* is not about your enmity — it is about your **attention**.

What is the single insight that holds both together?

**You have been formed with more care than you remember, and the recovery of gratitude is the recovery of memory.**

You forgot what you are. You forgot whose breath is in you. You forgot which faculties were given and what they were given for. And the work of every believer, every day, is the slow, deliberate work of remembering.

### Questions to Carry

Five questions. I am leaving them open.

1. **What gift in your life has become so reliable that you have stopped seeing it?** Pick one — water, breath, a person, an ability. Stay with it for a minute.

2. **When was the last time something entered through your hearing, was confirmed by your sight, and was answered by your** *fuʾād*? When was the last time a verse made your heart actually move?

3. **Where in your day is the breath of** *rūḥ* **honored, and where is it ignored?** What activities treat you as a being with a spirit, and what activities treat you as a body alone?

4. **If the verdict on you ended today —** *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* **— what would you most want to add to the small column of your thanks?**

5. **What would it look like to live one full day in this ayah?** To wake up calibrated. To breathe with awareness that the breath is from Him. To hear, see, and feel as gifts received this morning — not equipment owned for decades.

### One-Sentence Distillation

You were calibrated, breathed into, and equipped with three faculties so that you could see Him — and the only thing left for you to do is start.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow morning, when you open your eyes, do not jump up.

Lie still for ten seconds. Notice that your eyes opened — that they did so without you having to command them. Notice that you can hear — that the room is full of sound that your ears are sorting without effort. Notice that your chest is rising and falling — that the breath Allah breathed at the beginning is still in you, still moving.

And then say two words. *Al-ḥamdu lillāh.*

That is it. Not a long supplication. Not a recitation. Two words. Said with your whole attention, in the first ten seconds of being awake.

If you do this every morning for a month, the ayah will have already started to do its work in you.

### Du'a

O Allah, the One who calibrated us when we were clay, who breathed into us from a spirit You call Your own, who gave us hearing and sight and hearts to find You with — wake our attention. Take the small column of our thanks and make it grow. And do not let the verdict of *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* be the last thing said about us when we meet You.

آمين.
