# The Most Wrong: When the Reminder Itself Becomes the Evidence

## Surah 32, Ayah 22

### The Arabic

> وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّن ذُكِّرَ بِـَٔايَـٰتِ رَبِّهِۦ ثُمَّ أَعْرَضَ عَنْهَآ ۚ إِنَّا مِنَ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ مُنتَقِمُونَ

### Transliteration

*Wa man aẓlamu mimman dhukkira bi-āyāti rabbihi thumma aʿraḍa ʿanhā. Innā mina-l-mujrimīna muntaqimūn.*

### Translation

> "And who is more unjust than one who is reminded of the signs of his Lord, then turns away from them? Indeed, from the criminals We will exact retribution."

---

## Introduction: The World Beneath the Question

Let me ask you to imagine something before we open this ayah.

Imagine you've been driving down a highway at night. Someone in the passenger seat — someone who loves you — looks up and says quietly, "You're drifting." You glance, see the rumble strip under your tires, and you correct.

Now imagine the same scene. The same warning. The same voice. But this time, you hear it... and you keep drifting. Not because you didn't hear. Not because you doubt the speaker. You heard. You understood. And you simply turned your face away from the warning and kept your hands where they were.

[PAUSE]

Something just happened in that second scenario that's almost impossible to name. It wasn't ignorance. It wasn't a mistake. It was something else — something the Arabic language has a precise word for, and English barely touches.

This is the world of As-Sajdah's twenty-second ayah.

Surah As-Sajdah is a short surah, but it has an architecture as deliberate as a building. It opens by establishing that this Quran is from the Lord of the worlds. It moves through creation — how Allah fashioned the human being from clay, then from a despised fluid, then breathed of His own spirit. It contrasts two kinds of people: those whose sides leave their beds at night to call upon their Lord (v.16), and those who, in this very ayah we're about to enter, are reminded and walk away.

The original listeners in Makkah were hearing this in a specific climate. They were watching certain Qurayshi elders — people who had heard the Quran recited, who knew exactly what was being claimed — turn their backs in public and act as though nothing had been said. Allah is naming what was happening in front of everyone.

But here's what's stunning, and what we're going to spend the next hour with:

Allah does not ask, "Who is unjust?" He asks, "**Who is MORE unjust?**" He is not opening the door to a comparison among ordinary wrongdoers. He is opening the door to a *superlative* — and what fills the door He opens may not be what we'd expect.

Stay with me. Because by the time we leave this ayah, we may have to ask whether the most dangerous moment in a human life is not the moment of sin — but the moment a sign is delivered to us, and we feel ourselves quietly choose to look elsewhere.

## PART 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Aẓlam* — Not "Wrong," But "More Wrong Than Anyone"

Before we look at the word, let's teach the structure.

In Arabic, when you want to say someone is "the most" of something — the tallest, the wisest, the most generous — you don't add a word like "most." You change the shape of the word itself. You put it into a pattern called the *afʿal* pattern. *Kabīr* (big) becomes *akbar* (bigger/biggest). *Karīm* (generous) becomes *akram* (more generous). The word is bent into a superlative shape.

Now, look at what Allah does here. He doesn't say *ẓālim* — "unjust." He uses *aẓlam* — bent into that superlative shape. **More unjust. Most unjust.**

And He couples it with *man* — "who" — in a construction called *istifhām inkārī*, a rhetorical question whose answer is not requested but assumed. "Who is more unjust than this?" is not a question Allah is asking us to answer with a name. It is a question whose meaning is: *no one. No one is more unjust than this.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment.

In the Quran, this exact phrasing — *wa man aẓlamu mimman...* — appears more than a dozen times. And each time, Allah uses it to mark a kind of injustice so severe that nothing else can stand beside it. He uses it for the one who forges a lie against Allah. He uses it for the one who hides testimony. He uses it for the one who prevents the mosques of Allah from being mentioned.

And He uses it here — for the one who is reminded and turns away.

Notice something the listener of that era would have caught immediately: this construction is *placing* the heedless turner in the same category as the inventor of falsehood, the suppressor of truth, the destroyer of sacred space. The man who simply walked away from a recitation is being shelved next to the most extreme injustices the Quran names.

Why?

Hold that question. We'll come back to it. But first, let's notice the word underneath *aẓlam* — the root *ẓ-l-m*.

In classical Arabic lexicons, *ẓulm* is defined as *waḍʿ al-shayʾ fī ghayri mawḍiʿihi* — "putting something where it does not belong." It is the violation of right placement. To wrong someone is to put them in a position they don't belong in. To wrong yourself is to put your own soul in a place it does not belong.

So when Allah calls this man *aẓlam* — the most misplacing — He is saying: **this man has displaced something so foundational that no other displacement reaches it.**

What did he displace? He displaced a sign from its proper response. A sign from Allah belongs in the place of reception, reflection, surrender. He took it and put it in the place of dismissal. He moved a sacred thing into the bin of the ignored.

That is the wrong here. Not that he didn't believe. Not even that he sinned. That he *moved a sign of his Lord out of its proper place.*

[PAUSE]

One clear statement before we move on: Allah is telling us, by the shape of this word, that the gravest form of injustice is not the loud transgression. It is the quiet displacement of what is sacred into the category of the irrelevant.

### Element Two: *Dhukkira* — The Passive Voice of a Reminder That Was Already Inside You

Now we move to a word that carries more in its grammatical shape than English can hold.

Before the word, the concept. In Arabic, verbs have voices — active and passive. *He reminded* is active. *He was reminded* is passive. The passive voice in Arabic is built by changing the internal vowels of the verb. *Dhakkara* — he reminded. *Dhukkira* — he was reminded.

But there's more. The Arabic verb *dhakkara* — Form II of the root *dh-k-r* — is itself an intensified form. The basic root *dhakara* means simply "to remember" or "to mention." When you push it into Form II — *dhakkara* — it becomes "to *cause* to remember," "to bring back to memory," "to remind something that was already known."

This is critical. Let me say it plainly.

The Arabic word *dhukkira* — the word Allah uses here — does not mean "he was taught something new." It means "**he was made to remember something he already knew.**"

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. In the Quranic worldview, the human soul was not born blank. Allah took a covenant from every soul before this life — *alastu bi-rabbikum* (7:172) — "Am I not your Lord?" And every soul answered, *balā* — "yes, indeed." The knowledge of Allah is not new information delivered to a stranger. It is *recovered memory* delivered to someone who once knew.

This is why the Quran calls itself *al-dhikr* — "the Reminder." Not "the Teaching." Not "the Lesson." The Reminder. Because what it does is wake up something already sleeping inside you.

Now look back at the ayah: *dhukkira bi-āyāti rabbihi* — "he was reminded of the signs of his Lord."

This man was not handed strange new information. He was handed back his own forgotten knowledge. The signs of *his Lord* — *rabbihi*, with the possessive pronoun — were brought to him. The Lord who fashioned him in the womb. The Lord whose breath of spirit is in him. The Lord he once acknowledged in a covenant his conscious mind no longer remembers but his soul still carries.

And he turned away.

You see what just happened? The Arabic grammar has just told us something English cannot easily say. **This was not a man rejecting a stranger's idea. This was a man refusing to recognize his own memory.**

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing in the grammar. The verb is passive — *dhukkira*, "he was reminded." Allah does not name the reminder. Was it the Quran? A friend? A funeral he attended? A near-miss with death? A verse on a billboard? A line in a book? A pang in the chest at three in the morning?

The passive voice opens the door wide. It does not matter who or what delivered the reminder. What matters is that it *arrived.* The grammar makes the reminder universal. Every reminder counts. Every nudge from Allah, whether it came through scripture or through a stranger's offhand sentence or through your own heart at a quiet moment — every single one of them places you in this ayah's window.

This is one of those moments where the absence of a specified agent does enormous work. By not naming the reminder, Allah is telling you: do not look for a specific kind of reminder to apply this ayah to. *Look for every reminder.* Every reminder you have received and quietly set aside.

[PAUSE]

One clear statement: The grammar makes this ayah unavoidable. Anyone who has ever felt the pull of a sign and chosen to look elsewhere — and that is every one of us — has stood, at least for a moment, in the place this ayah describes.

---

### Element Three: *Thumma Aʿraḍa* — The Particle of Distance and the Verb of the Turned Face

Two words now, working together: *thumma aʿraḍa.*

First, *thumma.* In Arabic there are several words that mean "then." There is *fa* — which means "then" in the sense of immediately, right after, in direct sequence. And there is *thumma* — which means "then" with delay, with separation, with a gap.

When Allah says *dhukkira... thumma aʿraḍa* — "he was reminded... *then* he turned away" — He is not describing a man who heard the reminder and immediately, reflexively, looked away. The *thumma* suggests something more chilling: he heard it. He registered it. Time passed. And *then,* with full possession of himself, he turned away.

This was not a flinch. This was a decision.

[PAUSE]

Now the verb itself — *aʿraḍa.* The root is *ʿ-r-ḍ.* In the basic form, the root means "width" or "side." *ʿArḍ* is the width of something. But when the verb is pushed into Form IV — *aʿraḍa* — it takes on a very specific physical meaning: to turn your *side* to someone. To present your shoulder. To literally show the side of your face rather than the front of it.

Imagine someone is speaking to you and you slowly rotate your body until they're addressing your shoulder. Until they have to speak to the side of your head rather than your face. That is *iʿrāḍ.* It is not closing your eyes. It is not plugging your ears. It is showing your *side* — making it physically clear that what is being said no longer belongs in front of you.

This is the word Allah chooses for what this man does to the signs of his Lord.

[PAUSE]

And notice — once again — what Allah does *not* say. He does not say "he denied them." He does not say "he argued against them." He does not say "he forgot them." He says he turned his side to them. *Aʿraḍa ʿanhā.*

There is a category of person we tend to worry about most: the loud denier. The one who argues. The one who writes books against the truth. Allah, in this ayah, is not pointing at that person. He is pointing at someone quieter and far more common: **the one who simply turns the side of his face. Who does not deny. Who does not argue. Who just... declines to face it.**

Stay with this. Because most of us are not in danger of becoming the loud denier. Most of us are in daily danger of becoming the quiet turner. The one who scrolls past the verse. Who closes the tab. Who lets the reminder finish and then moves on as if nothing was said. The one whose body language toward the sacred is the body language of the half-turned shoulder.

[PAUSE]

And the *thumma* tells us this turning was not panic. It was poise. There was a pause between the reminder and the turn. In that pause, the man could have responded. He chose not to. He recovered his composure, gathered himself, and rotated.

One clear statement: This ayah is not warning us primarily against the rejection of Allah's signs. It is warning us against the polite, composed, time-elapsed, fully-conscious sideways turn — the one we perform a dozen times a day without ever feeling that anything dramatic has happened.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

We have three words now: *aẓlam* — the most displacing. *Dhukkira* — was reminded of what he already knew. *Aʿraḍa* — turned his side.

The linguistic picture is complete: the gravest injustice (*aẓlam*) is not the loud denial of a stranger's claim. It is the calm, composed, side-turned refusal (*aʿraḍa*) to acknowledge what your own soul already remembers (*dhukkira*).

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Sacred Geometry of Attention

I want to start somewhere unexpected. Somewhere you already live.

You know the feeling of being in a conversation with someone who is technically present but whose attention is elsewhere. They're nodding. They're looking in your general direction. But you can feel that their interior is turned to the side. Their phone is in their hand. Their eyes flick. Even if they don't physically move, you can feel the *iʿrāḍ* — the inner sidelong turn — and it lands as an injury. We've all been on both sides of it.

Now expand that.

Every relationship in your life has a geometry of attention. Your face is either toward the person, or it is at an angle. And the angle of your face is doing more work than the words coming out of your mouth. A spouse can be sitting at the same dinner table and be three time zones away. A friend can be on the phone with you and be reading their email. A parent can be in the same room as their child and have their face fully turned to a screen. There is a physical body present, but the *attention* has rotated.

[PAUSE]

What Allah is doing in this ayah is taking that intimate, familiar dynamic — the geometry of attention — and applying it at the highest scale.

The signs of Allah are not abstract data. They are communications from your Lord, directed at *you*, in the most personal way. When a reminder arrives — through a verse, through a death in the family, through a stranger's words, through a moment of beauty that cracks you open — Allah is, in that instant, *speaking to you.* Your Lord, *rabbuka*, the one who is shaping your reality second by second, has turned His face toward you with something to say.

And in the instant that He turns His face toward you, *you have a choice about which way your face will be.*

You can face Him.

Or you can show Him the side of your face.

[PAUSE]

Now you understand why this is *aẓlam* — the most displacing. Because there is a sacred geometry here. When the Lord of the worlds directs His attention to a single human being, the right placement of that human's face is *toward Him.* To rotate — to present the shoulder rather than the face to the One who is fashioning your very ability to rotate — is to put a human soul where it does not belong with such severity that no other misplacement quite matches it.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ taught that the highest moment of worship is *sajdah* — when the face, the most central organ of human dignity, is pressed against the ground in the direction of the Lord. The whole prostration is the geometry of perfect facing. The face says: *I am toward You. Nothing of me is turned aside.*

And the surah you are reading is called *As-Sajdah.* The surah of facing.

[PAUSE]

The ayah at verse 15 of this same surah describes the believers: when they hear the signs of their Lord, *kharrū sujjadan* — they fall, in prostration. The geometry of full facing. Verse 22 — seven ayahs later — describes the inverse: reminded of the signs of their Lord, they turn the side. The geometry of partial facing.

The surah is built on this contrast. The two responses to a sign are not "belief and disbelief." They are *the direction of the face when the sign arrives.*

So here is the practical entry. Tomorrow, when a reminder of Allah arrives in your life — and they arrive every single day, multiple times, often quietly — notice the direction of your face. Not the physical face. The inner face. The face of your attention.

Ask: is anything in me at an angle right now? Is my shoulder up? Is my body language toward this sign the body language of someone receiving a beloved letter — or the body language of someone whose ride has just arrived and they're already half-turned toward the door?

[PAUSE]

One specific application: when a verse of the Quran is recited in your presence, even unexpectedly — on a podcast, in a passing car, at a janazah — for the next thirty seconds, *do not multitask.* Do not let your face be at an angle. Stop the scroll. Lower the volume of the world. Let your inner geometry be face-to-face for at least the duration of those words. This is not extra credit. This is the minimum the ayah is asking of you.

### Theme Two: The Mathematics of Heedlessness — Why "Forgetting" Is Not What It Seems

I want to teach you a small, brutal piece of mathematics.

In the human nervous system, there is a phenomenon called *habituation.* The first time you smell a strong scent in a room, your brain registers it intensely. The tenth time, it fades. The fiftieth time, it has become invisible to you. The scent is still there. Your sensors still detect it. But your conscious attention has stopped flagging it. The brain has decided: this is now background.

Habituation is essential for survival — without it, you would be overwhelmed by the persistent stimuli of your own life. But there is one place where habituation becomes catastrophic.

When you habituate to a *warning.*

[PAUSE]

The first time you ignored a reminder of Allah, your soul registered it. There was a flicker. A heaviness. A small interior twist of *I should have responded to that.* The second time, the flicker was smaller. By the hundredth time, the reminder arrives and there is no flicker at all. You hear the verse. You feel nothing. You scroll on. The system has habituated.

The Quran has a name for this end state. It calls it *qaswat al-qalb* — hardness of heart. *Rān ʿalā qulūbihim* — rust upon their hearts (83:14). The Prophet ﷺ described it: every sin is a black spot on the heart; if the person repents, it is polished away; if they continue, the spots grow until the heart is wholly covered (Tirmidhi). The heart does not break suddenly. It hardens slowly, one un-responded-to reminder at a time.

Now hear the ayah again with this in mind.

*Who is more unjust than one who is reminded of the signs of his Lord, then turns away?*

The injustice is not just the single turning. The injustice is what each turning *trains the soul to become.* Every act of *iʿrāḍ* makes the next *iʿrāḍ* easier and the next reminder fainter. You are not merely committing an act. **You are sculpting your future capacity to perceive.**

This is the silent horror of the ayah. The man who turns his side from the signs of his Lord is not just losing this one moment. He is teaching his own heart to feel less the next time. He is renovating his own interior so that, by the time he is old, the things that should crack a person open will pass through him like wind through an empty room.

[PAUSE]

And here is the part that should stop us cold.

He has *chosen* this. Not in a single thunderclap of a choice — those are rare, and people repent from them. He has chosen it in a thousand small, almost-invisible turnings that did not feel like choices at the time. The man in this ayah is not a monster. He is, in every respect, an ordinary man who simply did not face the reminders. And the slow accumulation of his ordinary turnings has made him *aẓlam* — the most displacing of all.

This is why Allah closes the ayah with a sentence that sounds, on first hearing, almost disproportionate.

*Innā mina-l-mujrimīna muntaqimūn.* "Indeed, from the criminals We will exact retribution."

Notice: He calls them *al-mujrimīn* — the criminals. Not "the disbelievers." Not "the wrongdoers." *Mujrimīn.* The root *j-r-m* in classical Arabic carries the meaning of *cutting* — of severing. A *mujrim* is one who has cut themselves off, severed the proper bond. By the time someone has habituated to ignoring Allah's signs, the bond has not just been weakened. It has been *cut.* Not by Allah — by them, by their own thousand small turnings.

And the retribution — *muntaqimūn* — is not Allah's anger arriving from outside as a foreign force. It is, in part, the natural completion of what the person has been doing to themselves all along. They cut the bond. The state of being cut is now made permanent. The interior they were building, brick by brick, in every small *iʿrāḍ* — that interior is now made their final dwelling.

[PAUSE]

This is the mathematics of heedlessness. Every reminder either *opens* the heart a little wider or *closes* it a little tighter. There is no neutral. The reminders that pass through you and leave no mark are not leaving no mark. They are leaving the mark of having passed through without effect, which is itself a re-shaping of the soul.

So here is the specific application. Tonight, before you sleep, ask yourself: what reminder reached me today that I did not respond to? It might have been a verse. It might have been a thought. It might have been the strange tightness in your chest when you walked past someone in need. It might have been a memory that surfaced unbidden of someone you've wronged.

That was not noise. That was a *dhikr* — a reminder. And the ayah is asking you a simple question: did you face it, or did you show it your side?

You do not have to fix everything. You only have to *not turn your face away.* Just face the reminder for sixty seconds before you sleep. Acknowledge it. Sit with it. Let it leave a mark.

That alone is the difference between the geometry of *sajdah* and the geometry of *iʿrāḍ.* That alone is the difference between this ayah being a description of *us* or being a description of *someone else.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of As-Sajdah, Now Visible

Notice what this surah has done. It opened by establishing that this Quran is a revelation from the Lord of the worlds. It moved to the creation of the human being — clay, then fluid, then spirit. Then it described the believers — those whose sides leave their beds at night to call upon their Lord (v.16). The *side* is up. The face is toward Him.

And here, in v.22, the inverse: those who, when reminded, present their side (*aʿraḍa*) to the signs. The body language has flipped. The believers lifted their sides off their beds to face Him. The unjust kept their beds, and instead presented their sides to Him.

The surah is one sustained meditation on the geometry of facing. As-Sajdah — the surah of prostration — is asking, from beginning to end: *which direction is your face turned?*

### Bringing It Together

The three words we entered — *aẓlam, dhukkira, aʿraḍa* — and the two themes — sacred geometry, the mathematics of heedlessness — are not separate insights. They are one insight from different angles.

The most displacing injustice (*aẓlam*) is the displacement of *your face* (the geometry) from the *signs that have arrived to wake your own buried memory* (*dhukkira*). And every small *iʿrāḍ* (the mathematics) calcifies the muscles of the face so that, eventually, facing Him becomes impossible — not because He has hidden, but because you have rotated past the point of being able to turn back.

This is why the ayah is so severe and so quiet at the same time. The catastrophe is not loud. It is the soft, daily, polite turning of the shoulder.

### Questions to Carry

1. When a verse of the Quran enters my hearing unexpectedly, what does my body — and my interior body — do? Do I face it, or do I show it my side?

2. What was the last reminder that genuinely landed on me — and what did I do with it in the days that followed? Did I let it shape me, or did I let it dissolve?

3. Is there an area of my life where I have been *dhukkira* — clearly reminded — many times, and where I have, each time, calmly turned my side? What would facing it actually require of me?

4. The believers in v.15 *fell* when they heard the signs. When was the last time a verse moved me physically — not metaphorically, but actually moved me? If never, what has happened to me?

5. If my pattern of response to reminders continues for the next twenty years unchanged, what kind of heart will I have at the end of it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The gravest injustice a human soul can commit is not loud rebellion but the quiet, composed, time-elapsed sideways turn from a reminder that arrived to wake what the soul already knows.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to do anything dramatic. You only have to stop turning your side.

The next time a reminder reaches you — and one will reach you today, perhaps within the hour — let your face be toward it. Not your physical face. The face of your attention. Stop, even for thirty seconds, and let the reminder land. Do not perform anything. Do not solve anything. Just *face it.*

That is the whole of the difference between this ayah being about you, and this ayah being about someone else entirely.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who fashioned our memory before we were born, and who keep returning to us the reminders we keep losing — do not let our faces be at an angle to Your signs. When You send a reminder, soften the muscles of our turning. Let our first instinct be to face, not to rotate. And before our hearts harden into the kind of soil that no reminder can reach, plant Your *dhikr* in us so deeply that we cannot turn away even if we tried.

Āmīn.
