# The Sigh at the End of Every "Mine"

## Introduction: Two Hands, One Book

Listen first to the Arabic. Let it move through you before you understand a word of it.

> [ayah:69:25] وَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِىَ كِتَـٰبَهُۥ بِشِمَالِهِۦ فَيَقُولُ يَـٰلَيْتَنِى لَمْ أُوتَ كِتَـٰبِيَهْ
>
> *wa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu bi-shimālihi fa-yaqūlu yā-laytanī lam ūta kitābiyah*
>
> "But as for him who is given his record in his left hand, he will say: O, I wish I had never been given my record…"

I want you to picture the scene the surah has been building toward. The whole of *Al-Haqqah* begins by hammering on a single drum. **Al-Haqqah.** The Reality. *Mā al-Haqqah?* What is the Reality? And then, as if the word itself were too large for the tongue: *wa mā adrāka mā al-Haqqah* — and what could possibly make you grasp what the Reality is?

Then come the nations that thought they had grasped reality and were wrong. ʿAad, with their towering bodies and their wind. Thamud, with their mountains carved into homes. The drowned people of Nuh. One by one the surah knocks down everyone who built a life on the assumption that *this world* was the solid thing.

And then the trumpet. The sky splitting like worn cloth. The angels at its torn edges. The Throne carried above. And the verse that should make every one of us go silent: *yawma'idhin tuʿraḍūna lā takhfā minkum khāfiyah* — on that Day you will be exposed, not one hidden thing of you will stay hidden.

And now — the divide. Two people. The same Day, the same exposure, the same book of deeds. But one receives it in the right hand and runs through the crowd shouting *hāʾumu-qraʾū kitābiyah* — "Here! Everyone! Read my book!" — so eager he cannot keep the joy inside his chest.

And the other.

The other receives the same kind of book, in the other hand, and the first words out of his mouth are not a defense, not an excuse, not even a scream. They are a **wish**. A wish so deep it bends the grammar of the language to carry it.

Here is what I want us to find today. **This man is not punished, in his own first words, by fire.** Before any chain touches him, before any blaze, his torment is already complete — and it is made entirely of one sound, repeated, like a bell that will not stop ringing. Stay with me, and you will hear that sound. And once you hear it, you will hear it in your own ordinary regrets, the small ones, today.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Breath That Will Not Leave — *Hāʾ as-Sakt*

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something small about how language carries feeling — because Arabic is about to do something English simply cannot.

When you are exhausted, when something has finally broken in you, what does your body do? It does not form a word. It *exhales*. A long breath out — *haaah*. Grief breathes. Relief breathes. The deepest emotions in a human being do not arrive as sentences; they arrive as breath. We sigh before we speak, and sometimes instead of speaking.

Now — classical Arabic has a feature that scholars of recitation call **هاء السكت**, *hāʾ as-sakt*, the "ha of pause" or the "ha of silence." It is a soft, breathy *h* added to the very end of certain words. Grammatically, it is not part of the word's root. It carries no meaning of its own. Its entire function is to let the voice come to rest — to give the breath somewhere to land. Open *Lisān al-ʿArab* and you find it described as exactly this: a *h* of stillness, of settling, appended at the moment of pause.

It is, in the most literal sense, a built-in sigh.

Now look at what this man says, and watch the ends of his words. I will mark the breath.

> *yā-laytanī lam ūta **kitābiyah***  — I wish I had never been given my **book**-*haah*
>
> *wa lam adri mā **ḥisābiyah***  — and never known what my **account** was-*haah*
>
> *mā aghnā ʿannī **māliyah***  — my **wealth** availed me nothing-*haah*
>
> *halaka ʿannī **sulṭāniyah***  — gone from me is my **authority**-*haah*

[PAUSE]

Four times. *Kitābiyah. Ḥisābiyah. Māliyah. Sulṭāniyah.* Four times the man reaches the end of a word and the word *exhales* for him. Notice — these are not random words. He is naming the four pillars of his existence: **his record, his reckoning, his wealth, his power.** And every single one of them collapses into the same defeated breath. *…iyah. …iyah. …iyah. …iyah.*

The Quran is not merely *telling* you he is in despair. It has *built the despair into the sound of his speech.* You can hear a man sighing even if you do not know one word of Arabic. The reciter cannot recite these verses without breathing out, four times, like someone who has run out of everything.

Here is what is remarkable, and you must not miss it. **The man in the right hand uses this exact same breath.** Go back two verses: *hāʾumu-qraʾū **kitābiyah*** — "Here, read my **book**-*haah*!" Same ending. Same soft *h*. Same exhale.

So the very same sound — *kitābiyah* — comes out of both men. But in one mouth it is the breath of a person who cannot contain his **joy**, and in the other it is the breath of a person who has run out of **hope**. The sound is identical. The soul behind it is the difference between Paradise and the Blaze.

Let that land. **On that Day, you will not be given a different language than your neighbor. You will be given the same sounds, the same book, the same Day — and what will distinguish you is only what is inside the breath.** The same *haah* that is a gasp of delight for one is a sob for the other.

And one detail more, because the Quran wastes nothing. Cling to it. Each of those four words carries the *yāʾ al-mutakallim* — the "ya of *me*, of *mine*." *Kitāb-**ī**-yah.* *Māl-**ī**-yah.* *Sulṭān-**ī**-yah.* My book. My wealth. My power. He spends his last coherent words clutching the word "**mine**" — and every "mine" he grabs dissolves, in the same breath, into a sigh. He is holding sand and naming each grain as it falls.

That is what the *hāʾ as-sakt* reveals here: **a life spent saying "mine" ends as nothing but an exhale.**

## Element Two: The Wish for the Death You Once Fled — *Yā Laytahā Kānat al-Qāḍiyah*

Let me teach the second feature before we open it.

There is a particle in Arabic, **لَيْتَ**, *layta*. We translate it "would that" or "I wish." But *layta* is a very specific kind of wish. The grammarians call it the particle of *tamannī* — and classically, *tamannī* is the wishing for **the impossible**, or for something so out of reach it might as well be impossible. When you say *layta*, you are not making a plan. You are mourning a door that is already locked. *If only.* It is the grammar of a wish that knows, even as it is spoken, that it can never come true.

And notice the small cry that opens it: *yā*. Al-Jalalayn pauses on it to tell us it is here a *yā* of *tanbīh* — a particle of *alerting*, of calling out for attention — and yet there is no one named on the other end of the call. He throws a *yā* into the air, the way a man cries out to a room he already knows is empty. The opening word of his lament is a summons, and the summons reaches no one.

This man uses it twice. The first time gathers up the book and its reckoning in one breath — *yā-laytanī lam ūta kitābiyah wa lam adri mā ḥisābiyah*, "I wish I had never been given my book, and never known what my account was." Hear what the classical commentators catch in that second clause. He does not merely wish the book away; he wishes away the *knowing* itself. Al-Tabari glosses it with almost brutal plainness — *lam adri ayy shayʾin ḥisābiyah*, that he had never learned *what* his reckoning even came to — and al-Muyassar hears the whole lament spoken *nādiman mutaḥassiran*, in raw regret and grief. There is a particular horror folded into that: for this man, even the *knowledge* of where he stands has become unbearable, a thing he would undo if he could. To simply *know* the account is already part of the punishment. And then — the second wish, the one I want to sit in with you.

> [ayah:69:27] يَـٰلَيْتَهَا كَانَتِ ٱلْقَاضِيَةَ
>
> *yā-laytahā kānat al-qāḍiyah*
>
> "O, I wish **it** had been the one that finished everything."

Notice something immediately. **"It."** *Laytahā* — the *hā* is a feminine pronoun, "it / she." But the verse never tells you what "it" is. There is no noun. He just says: I wish *it* had been the finisher.

The commentators are unanimous on the unspoken referent. The *muyassar* commentary fills the silence: he means *al-mawtah* — **the death** he died in the world. *Yā layta al-mawtah allatī muttuhā fī al-dunyā kānat al-qāṭiʿah li-amrī* — "I wish the death I died in the world had been the one that cut my matter off for good, and I had never been raised after it."

But pause on *why the word is left out.* Why does he not simply say "I wish **death** had finished me"? Because the man cannot even bear to name it. It has become too enormous, too charged. He gestures at it — *it, that thing* — the way a person in shock cannot say the name of what they have lost. The grammatical gap *is* the trauma. The missing word is louder than any word he could have said.

Now feel the full weight of what he is wishing. **He is wishing that death had been the end.**

Stop and hold that against everything you know about being human. What is the one thing every nafs runs from? What did ʿAad and Thamud and every tyrant in this surah build their walls and carve their mountains to escape? **Death.** It is the universal terror. We spend fortunes to delay it by months. We cannot look directly at it.

And here is a man who, given one clear look at the Reality, wishes with all that is left of him that death had been *more* final — that it had been a true ending, a door into nothing. The thing he spent his whole life fleeing has become the thing he now begs for. **He is not afraid of annihilation anymore. He is in love with it.** Because non-existence has become better than the existence in front of him.

And then the word he chooses for death: **القاضية**, *al-qāḍiyah*. From the root *q-ḍ-y*, to decree, to settle, to finish a matter completely. *Al-qāḍī* is the judge — the one who brings a dispute to its final, unappealable end. So when he wishes his death had been *al-qāḍiyah*, he is wishing it had been **the verdict that closes the case forever** — no appeal, no resurrection, no after.

Do you see the terrible irony? He stands on the Day of *al-Haqqah*, the Day of the true and final verdict, and what he longs for is a *counterfeit* finality — the false "the end" that death seemed to offer in the world. He wanted the small ending instead of the great accounting. He wanted the curtain instead of the courtroom.

What this invites us to consider is severe and clarifying: **the comfort some people take in believing death is the end is, on that Day, the one thing the condemned will beg for and not receive.** The man wishes the atheist's creed were true. *Layta* — if only there were nothing after. And the grammar of *layta* answers him before he finishes: this is a wish for the impossible.

## Element Three: When God Stops Speaking *To* You — The Great Turning Away

This third feature is not a single word. It is a **shift** — and it is the most frightening thing in the passage, though it is the easiest to read straight past.

Let me first teach the device. Arabic rhetoric has a phenomenon called **التفات**, *iltifāt* — literally "the turning of the face." It is when the speech suddenly pivots: from "you" to "he," from past to present, from addressing someone directly to speaking *about* them. The Quran uses it constantly, and it is never decorative. It is a camera that suddenly cuts to a different angle, and the cut itself means something.

Now watch the passage move. For five verses, the man is a **speaker**. He has a voice. He says "I wish… *I* wish… *my* wealth… *my* authority." Whatever else is happening, he is still a *someone* — a self with a tongue, making his case, even if the case is only grief.

And then, verse 30:

> [ayah:69:30] خُذُوهُ فَغُلُّوهُ
>
> *khudhūhu fa-ghullūhu*
>
> "Seize him, and shackle him."

[PAUSE]

Feel everything that just happened in those two words.

First: **he stops speaking.** Mid-grief, his voice is simply gone. He does not get to finish his thought. He does not get a reply to his wish. The narration moves on as if he were no longer a participant but a *thing being processed.*

Second — and this is the heart of it — **notice who is being addressed, and who is not.** *Khudhū-hu* — "seize *him*." The command is plural: *khudhū*, you-all, take. Spoken to the angels of punishment. And the man? He is now **هُ**, *hu* — "him." An object. A pronoun. Allah does not say to him, "I will seize you." Allah does not address him at all anymore. **He has stopped being someone God speaks *to*, and become someone God speaks *about*.**

Count the *hu*. *Khudhū-**hu*** — seize him. *Ghullū-**hu*** — shackle him. *Ṣallū-**hu*** — drive him in. *Uslukū-**hu*** — thread him. Four times: him, him, him, him. He is now nothing but a grammatical object passed from one command to the next, the way you would hand off a parcel.

This is the deepest poverty in the entire scene, deeper than the fire. In the world, this was a man of *sulṭān* — authority. People turned their faces *toward* him. They waited on his word. And now the only One whose attention is existence itself has **turned His face away.** Allah will not even speak to him directly. The dialogue is over.

Stay with me, because there is a precise contrast hidden here. The believer, in *other* scenes of the Quran, is *addressed*: *yā ayyatuhā an-nafs al-muṭmaʾinnah, irjiʿī ilā rabbiki* — "O soul at peace, return to your Lord." Spoken *to* her. Face turned *toward* her. The intimacy of being called by your Lord, in the second person, *you*.

And this man gets the opposite. The intimacy is withdrawn. He becomes a *case*. **The torment of the fire is the second sentence. The first sentence is: you are no longer worth addressing.**

What the *iltifāt* reveals — and you can build your whole inner life on this — is that the worst thing is not God's anger spoken *to* you. The worst thing is to be spoken *about* and not *to*. To slide from "you" to "him." There is mercy even in being scolded directly, because to be addressed is to be in relationship. This man has fallen out of the conversation entirely.

[PAUSE]

### A bridge before we go deeper

Look at what these three features have shown us, side by side. The *hāʾ as-sakt* showed a man whose every "mine" dissolves into a sigh. *Layta al-qāḍiyah* showed a man begging for the non-existence he once fled. And the *iltifāt* showed a man falling out of being addressed by God at all — from "you" to "him."

Three different windows. One single room. And now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Two Things That Sink a Life — and Why *These* Two

Begin where you actually live. Think of someone — maybe yourself in a hard season — who is, by every visible measure, *fine*. Provided for. Respected. In control of their corner of the world. Wealth in the account, authority in the room. If you asked them "what is your life built on?" and they were honest, they might point to exactly those two things: *what I have* and *what I command.* Security and standing. Resources and respect.

That is precisely the man in our verses. Look at the two pillars he clutches in his last words: **مَالِيَهْ** — *my wealth* — and **سُلْطَانِيَهْ** — *my authority*. These are the two oldest idols of the human heart. They are what ʿAad and Thamud died defending. They are what the modern world, with all its sophistication, still organizes itself around: net worth and influence.

Now — *al-Haqqah* lets him keep those words right up to the edge, and then, when the verdict comes, it tells us what was *actually* on the ledger. And here is the thing I cannot get past. After all his talk of wealth and power, the indictment names **only two things**, and they are not the two he was thinking about:

> **إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ لَا يُؤْمِنُ بِٱللَّهِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ ۝ وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ**
>
> "Indeed, he did not believe in Allah, the Most Great, **nor did he urge the feeding of the poor.**"

Two charges. And notice their shape. The first is **vertical** — his broken relationship with God *above* him: *lā yuʾminu bi-llāhi al-ʿAẓīm*. The second is **horizontal** — his broken relationship with the human *beside* him: *lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīn*. Up and across. The two axes of every human life. The whole of him is summarized in a failure of the vertical and a failure of the horizontal — a man who was connected to neither his Lord nor his neighbor, suspended in his wealth, attached to nothing but himself.

Now look closer at the second charge, because the precision here is shattering. It does not say "he did not *feed* the poor." It says **he did not *urge* the feeding of the poor** — *lā yaḥuḍḍu*. The root *ḥ-ḍ-ḍ* means to *incite, to urge on, to spur.* He is not condemned merely for failing to feed a hungry man. He is condemned for not even **encouraging** that it be done. Not for refusing to give — for refusing to so much as *care that giving happen.*

Sit with how low that bar is. Allah does not, in this charge, even require that he had the money to feed anyone. Encouragement is free. A word costs nothing. To say to others, "there is a hungry person, let us feed them" — this requires no wealth at all. And he would not even do *that.* The hunger of the poor was so far outside his concern that it never once moved his tongue.

Here is the connection the surah is making, and it is the connection your whole spiritual life turns on: **the man who does not truly believe in the Most Great above him will not be moved by the small and hungry beside him.** The vertical and the horizontal are one cord. When you genuinely feel the *ʿAẓīm* — the Most Great — over you, the human being in front of you stops being beneath your notice. And when God shrinks in your heart, the poor disappear from your sight entirely. They are the same wound, seen from two angles.

And now the cruelest, most just irony of the whole passage. He clutched **مَالِيَهْ** — *my wealth.* And what was his crime? That he would not move that wealth toward **طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ** — *the food of the poor.* The very thing he hoarded is named beside the very thing he withheld. The wealth that was supposed to be his security becomes the precise instrument of his indictment. *Mā aghnā ʿannī māliyah* — "my wealth availed me nothing" — he says it himself, not yet knowing that his wealth did worse than fail him: it *testified against* him.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is almost embarrassingly practical. The lowest rung of salvation in this passage is not heroic sacrifice. It is *to care that the hungry be fed* — enough to say so, enough to urge it. Today you can clear that bar with a sentence. *Let us feed someone.* The man in the Blaze is there, in part, because across an entire lifetime he could not be bothered to say those four words.

## Theme Two: The Friend You Cannot Buy, and the Food You Cannot Refuse

Let me start, again, with something human and close.

There is a particular loneliness that comes to people at the very top. The powerful man surrounded by people who need him — and the slow, cold realization that not one of them is *for him.* They are for what he can give. When the giving stops, the room empties. You have seen it: the fallen executive, the disgraced leader, the rich relative who discovers, the moment the money is gone, that he has no friends — only former beneficiaries. Recall *al-Qurtubi's* portrait of this very man: a *ras fī ash-shar*, a *head* in evil, with a crowd of followers behind him, called forward by name and lineage because he led so many. A man, in other words, who was *never alone* in the world.

Now hear his sentence:

> [ayah:69:35] فَلَيْسَ لَهُ ٱلْيَوْمَ هَـٰهُنَا حَمِيمٌ
>
> *fa-laysa lahu al-yawma hāhunā ḥamīm*
>
> "So there is no devoted friend for him here, today."

**حميم**, *ḥamīm.* The word means an intimate, a true friend — but it comes from the root of *heat*, *ḥ-m-m*: the friend whose closeness *warms* you, the one whose loyalty runs hot. The man who, in the world, could summon a thousand followers with a word now has *not one* of them. *Laysa lahu… ḥamīm.* Of all the people who orbited his wealth and power, **zero** are present. The crowd was never his. It was his money's. And his money, by his own admission, has availed him nothing.

And here the classical commentators open a second door onto that lost word *sulṭān*. We have been hearing *halaka ʿannī sulṭāniyah* as "gone is my **power**" — the authority that gathered crowds. But al-Muyassar reads it along a different grain: *dhahabat ʿannī ḥujjatī* — "**my argument** has left me; I have no plea left to plead with." On this reading, *sulṭān* is not the throne but the *case* — the proof a man marshals to defend himself, the word *sulṭān* itself carrying in the lexicon the sense of a decisive **evidence**, a warrant. (The two senses are not rivals so much as one ruin seen twice: the man of power was also a man who always had an answer, an argument, a way to talk himself out.) Hold that against the *iltifāt* we just traced. He is no longer addressed; he has lost the very standing to speak — and now we learn he has also lost the *argument* he might have spoken. The mouth is taken, and the case is taken. He stands not only unanswered but **undefended**, with nothing in his hands and nothing on his tongue.

Look at the three little words placed around his loneliness: *al-yawma* — *today* — and *hāhunā* — *right here.* "There is, **today**, **right here**, no friend for him." The Quran nails him to a coordinate. Not "in general." Not "as a rule." *Here. Now. In this place.* At the one moment a human being most needs someone in his corner, the corner is empty, and the verse will not let him pretend otherwise.

And then — because his sentence is not only emptiness but a precise mirror of his crime — comes the food.

> **وَلَا طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِنْ غِسْلِينٍ ۝ لَّا يَأْكُلُهُۥٓ إِلَّا ٱلْخَـٰطِـُٔونَ**
>
> "Nor any food except *ghislīn* — none eats it but the deliberate sinners."

Hold the two scenes together. In the world, his crime was the food of the poor — *ṭaʿāmi al-miskīn* — which he would not even urge to be given. In the Fire, his sentence is **food** — *ṭaʿām* — the same word — but now it is *ghislīn*, and there is nothing else for him to eat. He withheld food; he is sentenced to food. The punishment is cut from the exact cloth of the crime.

And what is *ghislīn*? This is the only place in the entire Quran the word appears — a true *hapax legomenon*, a word God uses one time and never again, as if it names something that should not exist twice. The lexicographers and commentators describe it as the *washings* of the people of the Fire — the pus, the discharge, the foulness that runs from wounds. The root carries the sense of *washing off*, of what is rinsed away from a body. It is, precisely, the *opposite* of food: not what nourishes a body but what a body expels.

Feel the full arc. The man would not move his clean wealth toward clean food for a hungry human being. His reward is to be given the one "food" that is no food at all — the rinsings of the damned — and to be given it as his *only* option: *wa lā ṭaʿāmun illā min ghislīn*, **no food except** this. The man of abundance, who had every dish he wanted, ends with a single revolting course and no menu.

And who shares this table? *Lā yaʾkuluhu illā al-khāṭiʾūn* — "none eats it but the *khāṭiʾūn*." Note the word carefully. *Khāṭiʾ*, from *kh-ṭ-ʾ*, is not the one who errs by accident — that would be *mukhṭiʾ*. *Khāṭiʾ* is the one who sins **deliberately**, who *intends* the wrong, the one who knew and chose anyway. This is the final precision of his justice: he is not here for an honest mistake. He is here for what he *meant.*

What does this leave us holding? Two things, and they are mirror images. **The friend cannot be bought** — every relationship built on what you provide will evaporate the instant you cannot provide, and on that Day you will stand, *today, right here,* with exactly as many true friends as you loved for the sake of God and no more. And **the food you refuse will be the food you are served** — what you withhold from the hungry is not gone; it waits for you. Generosity is not a charge against your wealth. It is the only part of your wealth you actually keep.

So tomorrow: build at least one bond that survives your bankruptcy — a relationship that owes you nothing and is held together only by God. And let some of your food, your provision, leave your hand for someone who cannot repay you. Those are the two investments this passage says will still be standing *hāhunā*, right here, on the Day the crowd disperses and the wealth falls silent.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: A Life That Rhymes With Its Own Ruin

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole.

The man's own words — his five verses of grief — are stitched together by that sighing *hāʾ as-sakt*: *kitābiyah, ḥisābiyah, māliyah, sulṭāniyah.* Four exhales, each ending a thing he called **"mine."** And then the verdict-verses answer his speech *point for point*, like a key turning in a lock:

- He clutched **مَالِيَهْ** (*my wealth*) → he is charged with withholding **طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ** (*the food of the poor*), and sentenced to **طَعَامٌ مِّنْ غِسْلِينٍ** (*food of foul discharge*).
- He clutched **سُلْطَانِيَهْ** (*my authority* — or, as al-Muyassar reads it, *my argument*, the case I would have pleaded) — the power that gathered crowds and the voice that always had an answer — → and is left with **لَيْسَ لَهُ… حَمِيمٌ** (*not one devoted friend*), unaddressed and undefended.
- He grieved his **كِتَابِيَهْ** and **حِسَابِيَهْ** (*my book, my account*) → and the account, read aloud, names the two failures beneath everything: **no faith above, no mercy across.**

The passage is a perfect chiasm of cause and consequence. **What he held became what indicted him. What he withheld became what he is fed. Whom he ruled became no one who stays.** His ruin is not arbitrary; it *rhymes* with his life. That is the proof built into the architecture: the Day of *al-Haqqah*, the Reality, returns to each person the exact shape of what they truly were.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are one insight seen twice. The *hāʾ as-sakt* let us *hear* a self made entirely of "mine" collapse into breath. The two charges let us *see* why: a man clutching "mine" had severed both cords — to the God above and the poor beside — that are the only things that make a self more than its possessions. *Layta al-qāḍiyah* and the great turning-away — "him, him, him" — are simply what is left when a life of "mine" meets the Day when nothing is yours: a wish to not exist, and a fall out of being addressed by the only One who matters.

He spent a lifetime saying *mine.* He spends eternity saying *if only.*

## Questions to Carry

1. If my record were read aloud today, would the two things named be my faith and my mercy — or my wealth and my standing?
2. Whom do I love for the sake of God alone — a bond that would survive my losing everything I provide? Could I name even one *ḥamīm*?
3. The lowest bar in this passage is to *urge* that the hungry be fed. When did I last even *say the words* — never mind give? What stops my tongue?
4. What am I currently calling "mine" with the most certainty — and what would it sound like with the sigh of *…iyah* at the end?
5. Do I, somewhere quietly, *hope* death is the end — and what would it mean to live as though it certainly is not?

## One-Sentence Distillation

A life built on what is "mine" ends as a sigh and a wish for non-existence, because the only things that survive the Day are the faith we held above us and the mercy we passed across to others.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need to wait for the book to be placed in your hand to know which hand it would land in. The two charges are read out in advance, in this very surah, as a mercy — so that we might still move the cord. There is still a vertical to mend and a horizontal to reach across. Let something leave your hand this week toward someone who cannot repay you. Say the four words the man would not say: *let us feed someone.* And love at least one person for nothing they give you — so that, *hāhunā*, right here on that Day, you are not standing alone.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, *al-ʿAẓīm*, the Most Great — let belief in You so fill my heart that the hungry beside me never fall out of my sight. Do not let my life be a fistful of "mine" that ends in a sigh; make me of those whose record warms their right hand. And on the Day the crowd disperses, do not turn Your face from me — keep me among those You still call *you*. Āmīn.
