# Take Your Adornment, But Do Not Commit Excess

## Surah Al-Araf (7:31-32)

[ayah:7:31] ۞ يَـٰبَنِىٓ ءَادَمَ خُذُوا۟ زِينَتَكُمْ عِندَ كُلِّ مَسْجِدٍ وَكُلُوا۟ وَٱشْرَبُوا۟ وَلَا تُسْرِفُوٓا۟ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُسْرِفِينَ

[ayah:7:32] قُلْ مَنْ حَرَّمَ زِينَةَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَخْرَجَ لِعِبَادِهِۦ وَٱلطَّيِّبَـٰتِ مِنَ ٱلرِّزْقِ ۚ قُلْ هِىَ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا خَالِصَةً يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ ۗ كَذَٰلِكَ نُفَصِّلُ ٱلْـَٔايَـٰتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَعْلَمُونَ

*Ya bani Adama khudhu zinatakum 'inda kulli masjidin wa kulu wa ishrabu wa la tusrifu. Innahu la yuhibbu al-musrifin.*

*Qul man harrama zinata Allahi allati akhraja li-'ibadihi wa al-tayyibati mina al-rizq. Qul hiya lilladhina amanu fi al-hayati al-dunya khalisatan yawma al-qiyamah. Kadhalika nufassilu al-ayati li-qawmin ya'lamun.*

**O children of Adam, take your adornment at every place of prostration, and eat and drink, but do not commit excess. Indeed, He does not love the excessive. Say, "Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah which He has brought forth for His servants and the good things of provision?" Say, "They are for those who believe during the worldly life, exclusively theirs on the Day of Resurrection." Thus do We detail the signs for a people who know.**

## Introduction

Once the Qur'an corrects shameless exposure, it does not swing into a religion of ugliness.

That is one of the passage's beauties. After warning against stripping and indecency, Allah tells the children of Adam to take their adornment at every place of prostration. Then He tells them to eat and drink. The surah is teaching balance. Worship is not meant to be careless and shabby, nor is piety meant to declare war on Allah's wholesome provision.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators do not leave this floating in the abstract. Ibn Kathir reads the ayah as a direct refutation of a practice the idolaters of Arabia had: circling the Sacred House naked. He carries the report through Ibn Abbas - recorded by Muslim, an-Nasa'i and Ibn Jarir - that they used to go around the House unclothed, men and women both, the men by day and the women by night.

At-Tabari describes the same audience, and then adds a second thing about them. These were people who stripped themselves at the House, and who also forbade themselves food that Allah had never forbidden them - and they did it, in his words, as an act of piety in their own estimation, something they imagined brought them nearer to their Lord.

[PAUSE]

That detail changes how the ayah reads. The two halves of 7:31 are not a loose pairing of two unrelated pieces of advice. They are two corrections aimed at one community that had gone wrong in opposite directions at the same time: too little covering in worship, and too many self-invented prohibitions at the table. One group of people, two distortions, one verse.

[PAUSE]

And the balance has a boundary. Eat and drink, yes. Adorn yourselves, yes. Yet do not commit excess. So the Qur'an refuses both distortions at once: false ascetic severity on one side and appetite without measure on the other.

[PAUSE]

The hook of this passage is this: **Allah does not purify His servants by forbidding beauty and provision in themselves, but by teaching them to receive both lawfully, gratefully, and without israf.**

[PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. Adornment Is Taken Into Worship

The ayah begins:

*Khudhu zinatakum 'inda kulli masjid.*

Take your adornment at every place of prostration.

[PAUSE]

The word *zinah* is broad in Arabic. It carries adornment, presentability, what beautifies and dignifies outward appearance. But it is worth noticing how narrowly the early commentators read it here. At-Tabari glosses "take your adornment" as *min al-kisa' wa'l-libas* - of covering and of clothing. Al-Muyassar reads the ayah as saying: be, at the performance of every prayer, in a state of the adornment the Shari'ah asks for - garments that cover what should be covered, along with cleanliness and purity.

[PAUSE]

That is a quieter reading than the word on its own might suggest. Against people who came to worship with nothing on, the first meaning of "adornment" is simply: be dressed, and be clean. The verse is not opening a door to display. It is closing a door on exposure. Anyone who wants to read this ayah as a licence for extravagance has to get past the fact that the two commentators standing closest to the language read *zinah* here as clothes.

[PAUSE]

Still, the wording is generous rather than grudging. Allah does not say "cover yourselves." He says *khudhu zinatakum* - take your adornment. The verb *khudhu* is an imperative of taking, and taking assumes there is something there to be taken. So the ayah teaches that worship is not approached with neglectful indifference to the body. There is an adab to how one appears before Allah, and you are asked to bring it deliberately rather than drift into worship however you happen to be.

[PAUSE]

Then: *'inda kulli masjid* - at every place of prostration. The word *masjid* is built from the root of *sujud*, prostration: the place is named after the act performed in it. And here the commentators lean in slightly different directions, which is worth seeing plainly rather than smoothing over. Ibn Kathir and at-Tabari anchor the phrase to its immediate occasion, the Sacred House and the tawaf performed around it. Al-Muyassar reads it as the time of every prayer - be in this state whenever you stand to pray. The two readings are not at war. The second simply carries the first outward.

What neither reading softens is *kulli* - every. Not the crowded prayer. Not the one people will see. Every one.

### 2. Lawful Enjoyment Is Commanded, But Excess Is Forbidden

Then:

*Wa kulu wa ishrabu wa la tusrifu.*

Eat and drink, but do not commit excess.

[PAUSE]

This is an extraordinary moral proportion. Allah does not say merely, "avoid food," or "fear delight." He commands eating and drinking as part of a sane human life.

[PAUSE]

And it helps to remember who is being addressed. At-Tabari reads these commands against people who had put lawful food off-limits for themselves. Eat, he explains, of the good things I have provided you and made lawful to you; drink of what is lawful to drink. Read that way, "eat and drink" is not a push to consume. It is the lifting of a ban that was never Allah's to begin with. The command restores something that human hands had taken away.

[PAUSE]

But He places a limit in the same breath: *la tusrifu*. Do not cross the bounds - in quantity, attitude, wastefulness, or obsession. The verb is Form IV, *asrafa*, to go past the edge of a thing. Al-Muyassar glosses it as exceeding the limits of moderation, and then adds something easy to skim past: Allah does not love those who go to excess in food and drink *and in other than that*. Israf is not a rule about the table. The table is where this ayah names it, but the trait travels.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, what the commentators do not do. They do not hand you a quantity. There is no threshold here, no count of mouthfuls, no ceiling on what a garment may cost. The ayah gives a direction and leaves the measuring to a conscience that has to stay awake.

Then the reason:

*Innahu la yuhibbu al-musrifin.*

Indeed He does not love the excessive.

The issue has quietly shifted. The prohibition came as a verb - do not commit excess. The reason comes as a noun, *al-musrifin*, an active participle built from the very same root the prohibition was built from. The Qur'an names the act, then names what the act makes of you if you keep at it. A *musrif* is not someone who once overdid it. It is someone who has become the kind of person who spills past the edge, until that is simply how he is.

### 3. The Surah Rebukes False Piety That Forbids Allah's Beauty

Then:

*Qul man harrama zinata Allahi allati akhraja li-'ibadihi wa al-tayyibati mina al-rizq.*

Who has forbidden the adornment of Allah that He brought forth for His servants and the wholesome things from provision?

[PAUSE]

The rhetorical question cuts sharply. If Allah brought it forth, by what right does someone else outlaw it?

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir states the target plainly: the ayah refutes those who prohibit some category of food, drink or clothing on their own reasoning, without relying on what Allah has legislated - prohibitions built, as he puts it, on false opinion and fabrication. At-Tabari addresses it to the same crowd, those who went bare at the House and forbade themselves the good things of provision, and puts the question to them on Allah's behalf: who forbade you the adornment of Allah, which He created for His servants so that they might adorn themselves with it and be dignified by wearing it, and the lawful part of His provision that He provided His creation for their food and their drink?

[PAUSE]

The grammar carries the argument. *Harrama* is Form II, the doubled form - not "he found something forbidden," but "he pronounced it forbidden." He issued a ruling. Set against it is *akhraja*, Form IV: He brought it out, produced it, put it forth. One party produces; the other party bans what was produced. The verse lays the two verbs side by side and lets the imbalance speak.

[PAUSE]

Notice the phrase itself: *zinat Allah*, the adornment of Allah. The beauty is attributed to Him - not because He wears it, but because it belongs to His gift, His creation, His permission. Whoever forbids it is not merely restricting a thing. He is overruling its owner.

Then *li-'ibadihi*, for His servants. The recipients are named by their servanthood, not by their appetite. The gift arrives already framed by worship, which is why enjoying it and worshipping are not set against each other here. Ibn Kathir reads it in exactly that direction: these things were created for those who believe in Allah and worship Him.

[PAUSE]

And the provision is called *tayyibat*, wholesome and good, not morally suspect by default. Here at-Tabari does something honest that is easy to walk past: he records that the scholars of interpretation differed over what precisely *al-tayyibat mina al-rizq* refers to. The principle of the ayah is not in dispute. The exact boundary of what falls under it was discussed among the early commentators themselves, and at-Tabari reports the disagreement rather than flattening it into a single answer.

### 4. The Believers' Relationship to Dunya and Akhirah Is Clarified

Then:

*Hiya lilladhina amanu fi al-hayati al-dunya khalisatan yawma al-qiyamah.*

They are for those who believe in the worldly life, exclusively theirs on the Day of Resurrection.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar unpacks the sentence carefully. What Allah has made lawful of clothing, and of the good things of food and drink, is a right belonging to those who believe in the life of this world - and, he adds, others share in it with them here. On the Day of Resurrection it is theirs alone.

[PAUSE]

That is worth sitting with. The believer is not promised sole use of the world. The sharing is stated as a plain fact, not as a grievance and not as evidence that something has gone wrong. In dunya, believers share the world of provision with others.

*Khalisatan* comes from the root of *khulus*, purity in the sense of being unmixed. So what changes on the Day of Resurrection is not that the gifts get better. It is that they stop being shared, stop being mixed, stop being contested - the divine gift belongs to them without mixture, rivalry, or contamination. The verse teaches that lawful beauty here is not a betrayal of piety. It is a preview handled with restraint.

[PAUSE]

Then the passage closes on its own method: *kadhalika nufassilu al-ayat li-qawmin ya'lamun*. *Nufassilu* is Form II from the root of *fasl*, separating - to lay something out in distinct parts, to draw the lines between things. That is precisely what these two ayahs have just done. They separated adornment from indecency. They separated lawful provision from israf. They separated Allah's prohibitions from man's. The verse names the very thing it has been performing.

And the detailing lands *li-qawmin ya'lamun*, on a people who know. Al-Muyassar glosses them as those who know what He makes clear to them and who understand what He distinguishes for them. Knowledge here is not cleverness. It is the capacity to receive a distinction and keep holding it. Which may be why both errors in this passage are so durable: a flat rule is easier to carry than a line. "All of it is forbidden" and "none of it is forbidden" each save you the work of discernment. Detail is only useful to someone willing to know.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Islam Refuses Both Spiritual Shabbiness and Spiritual Gluttony

These ayahs are profoundly corrective because human beings often lurch toward extremes. Some imagine nearness to Allah requires hostility to beauty, comfort, or taste itself. Others baptize indulgence and call it gratitude. The Qur'an accepts neither.

[PAUSE]

And this is not a theoretical worry. At-Tabari's description of the first audience puts both errors inside one community at once - people who would not clothe themselves for worship, and who would not eat what Allah had made lawful. The extremes are not opposites that repel each other. They can sit comfortably in the same person, because both of them spare him the harder work of measure.

[PAUSE]

Instead the passage teaches a liturgy of proportion: adornment in worship, nourishment in life, and a red line at israf. That is a much more searching ethic than simple denial, because it requires inner measure rather than outward theatrics.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, how the two halves guard each other. "Take your adornment" would read as a licence if it stood alone - so "do not commit excess" is fastened to it inside the same ayah. "Do not commit excess" would harden into a doctrine of deprivation if it stood alone - so the next ayah asks, sharply, who forbade what Allah brought forth. Neither sentence can be lifted out and flown as a banner. The passage appears to have been built so that quoting half of it misquotes it.

### Theme 2: Forbidding What Allah Made Lawful Can Be Its Own Distortion

The rhetorical challenge in ayah 32 should unsettle every tendency to invent holiness through prohibition. Sometimes the ego feels safer with more rules than Allah made, because invented harshness can imitate seriousness. But the Qur'an asks: who forbade it?

[PAUSE]

The commentators are precise about what exactly is wrong here, and the precision matters. At-Tabari does not describe these people as insincere. He says they forbade themselves lawful things seeking righteousness before their Lord - as they reckoned it. They meant it. Ibn Kathir names the actual fault: they prohibited things on their own understanding, without relying on what Allah had legislated.

So the objection is not to restraint. It is to legislating. Someone who chooses to go without a thing for himself has done nothing this ayah condemns; voluntary restraint runs all through the Qur'an. What the ayah confronts is the move from "I will not" to "this is forbidden" - the moment a personal severity gets upgraded into a ruling from Allah that Allah never made.

[PAUSE]

That distinction is also the reason this passage asks to be handled rather than swung. It gets pulled hard in both directions: quoted by people arguing that religion demands austerity, and quoted by people arguing that no restraint applies at all. Neither is what the commentators found here. And where the boundaries actually fall - what counts among the *tayyibat*, what a garment may be, what crosses into israf in a particular life - is the work of fiqh, and at-Tabari himself records that the early scholars differed over the referent of *tayyibat*. Tadabbur is not the place to settle it, and this reflection is not attempting to.

[PAUSE]

What the question does is keep religion honest. Not everything restrained-looking is righteous, and not everything beautiful is suspect. The real issue is whether the beauty is Allah's lawful beauty and whether the one enjoying it remains inside gratitude, modesty, and measure.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs place a believer between two corruptions and teach him to walk between them.

Do not strip worship of beauty. Do not strip life of Allah's lawful provision. But do not cross into israf either. The children of Adam are not healed from shamelessness by becoming enemies of every adornment. They are healed by learning how to take beauty into worship and provision into life without letting either become excess.

[PAUSE]

And the address is worth hearing once more before we leave it. *Ya bani Adam* - children of Adam. It is the same address that opened the earlier ayahs where clothing was first named as a gift and the garment of taqwa was named as the better one. The correction is not aimed only at one tribe circling one House in one century. It is aimed at a human instinct, and it reaches everyone who inherited it.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: in my own habits, do I distort piety more by needless severity or by a quiet normalization of excess? And then a second question, closer to the bone: the last time I called something forbidden, was I reporting Allah's ruling, or promoting my own discomfort into one?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **Allah's religion does not sanctify ugliness or appetite, but teaches the children of Adam to receive adornment and provision as gifts that must remain under worship, gratitude, and measure.**
