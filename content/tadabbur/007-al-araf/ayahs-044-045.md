# A Caller Announced Between Them

## Surah Al-Araf (7:44-45)

[ayah:7:44] وَنَادَىٰٓ أَصْحَـٰبُ ٱلْجَنَّةِ أَصْحَـٰبَ ٱلنَّارِ أَن قَدْ وَجَدْنَا مَا وَعَدَنَا رَبُّنَا حَقًّا فَهَلْ وَجَدتُّم مَّا وَعَدَ رَبُّكُمْ حَقًّا ۖ قَالُوا۟ نَعَمْ ۚ فَأَذَّنَ مُؤَذِّنٌۢ بَيْنَهُمْ أَن لَّعْنَةُ ٱللَّهِ عَلَى ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

[ayah:7:45] ٱلَّذِينَ يَصُدُّونَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ وَيَبْغُونَهَا عِوَجًا وَهُم بِٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ كَـٰفِرُونَ

*Wa nada ashabu al-jannati ashab al-nari an qad wajadna ma wa'adana rabbuna haqqan fa-hal wajadtum ma wa'ada rabbukum haqqan. Qalu na'am. Fa-adhdhana mu'adhdhinun baynahum an la'natu Allahi 'ala al-zalimin.*

*Alladhina yasudduna 'an sabili Allahi wa yabghunaha 'iwajan wa hum bi al-akhirati kafirun.*

**And the companions of Paradise will call to the companions of the Fire, "We have certainly found what our Lord promised us to be true, so have you found what your Lord promised to be true?" They will say, "Yes." Then a caller will announce between them that the curse of Allah is upon the wrongdoers - those who turn people away from the path of Allah and seek to make it crooked while they are disbelievers in the Hereafter.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

Truth in the Qur'an is not only privately realized. Sometimes it is publicly announced.

These ayahs take us to one of those moments. The people of Paradise call across to the people of the Fire, not because they need information, but because reality has now become undeniable enough to be spoken aloud between both assemblies. What Allah promised was true. It was true in mercy and true in warning.

[PAUSE]

The commentators are careful about the timing, and it matters. Al-Tabari says this call happens *after they have entered* - after the people of the Garden are in the Garden and the people of the Fire are in the Fire. Al-Muyassar says the same. So this is not a conversation at a threshold, with both sides still waiting to hear. It is a conversation between two settled homes. Nobody is asking in order to learn.

[PAUSE]

Then a caller rises between them and names the curse of Allah upon the wrongdoers. But the Qur'an does not leave *zulm* undefined here. It specifies its social form: they turned people away from Allah's path, sought to bend that path into crookedness, and denied the Hereafter. So the curse lands not only on private disbelief, but on obstructive religion and corruptive influence.

[PAUSE]

The hook of this passage is this: **when the akhirah arrives, truth is no longer an internal hunch or debated doctrine; it becomes a publicly acknowledged reality, and the gravest wrongdoers are exposed as those who not only missed the path but worked to warp it for others.**

[PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. The People of Paradise Speak Across the Divide

The ayah begins:

*Wa nada ashabu al-jannati ashab al-nar.*

The companions of Paradise called to the companions of the Fire.

[PAUSE]

The scene is striking because the two final destinations are not sealed into muteness. There is an address across the divide. And the content is:

*Qad wajadna ma wa'adana rabbuna haqqan.*

We have found what our Lord promised us to be true.

The verb *wajadna* - we found - gives the sense of reality encountered and verified. Promise has become experience.

[PAUSE]

Slow down on the verb *nada* for a moment, because Al-A'raf is unusually full of it.

The Qur'an opens a sentence with this verb - *wa nada*, "and he called out" - in seven places. Four of them are in this surah. And when you line those four up, the whole shape of Al-A'raf becomes audible.

The first is at the very beginning of the human story: after Adam and his wife taste the tree, *their Lord called out to them* - "Did I not forbid you that tree?" A call that is a question.

The second is our ayah: the people of the Garden call to the people of the Fire - and again, a question.

The third comes four ayahs later, when the men on the heights call out to people they recognise by their marks.

The fourth comes six ayahs later, when the people of the Fire call back.

[PAUSE]

So the surah begins its account of humanity with a voice asking a question that the hearer cannot answer well, and it ends its judgment scene with voices asking questions across a divide. Al-A'raf is a surah that keeps raising its voice.

And there is a small detail that ties every one of these calls together. Look at the particle immediately after each of them: *an*. *Wa nudu an tilkumu al-jannah* - and they were called, *that* this is the Garden. *An qad wajadna* - *that* we have found. *An la'natu Allah* - *that* the curse of Allah. *An salamun 'alaykum* - *that* peace be upon you. *An afidu 'alayna* - *that* pour upon us.

Five calls in eight ayahs, and every single one of them is introduced by the same little word. The surah is not just describing shouting. It is quoting announcements.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing about who speaks first here. In the ayah immediately before ours, the people of the Garden are on the receiving end: *wa nudu* - "and they were called" - a passive verb, no speaker named, telling them *this is the Garden you have been given to inherit for what you used to do.* They are addressed before they address anyone.

Then, in the very next line, the first thing they do with their own voices is turn and speak to someone else. They were called; now they call.

[PAUSE]

And what exactly did they find? Al-Tabari fills in the content precisely: what they found true is what their Lord promised them *in the dunya, on the tongues of His messengers* - reward for believing in Him and in them, and for obeying Him.

That phrase is worth holding: *on the tongues of His messengers*. What is being verified on that Day is not a vague sense that things would work out. It is a specific message, delivered by specific men, in ordinary human speech, in this world. The Day of Judgment does not introduce new information. It confirms delivered information.

### 2. The Reply From the Fire Is Only One Word

Then the question:

*Fa-hal wajadtum ma wa'ada rabbukum haqqan.*

Have you found what your Lord promised to be true?

[PAUSE]

Notice the same verb again - *wajadtum*, "did you find." The Qur'an does not give the two groups different words. It does not say *we found* and *you suffered*. Both sides are described as having *found*. The act is identical. Only the content differs.

Notice also the word *rabbukum* - **your** Lord. He is still their Lord in that sentence. Even here, at the far end of everything, the Qur'an does not strip the relation away. The One who promised the Garden and the One who warned of the Fire is the same Rabb, addressed with the same word to both parties in a single breath.

[PAUSE]

And there is a quiet asymmetry in the grammar that is easy to walk past. The first half says *ma wa'adana rabbuna* - what our Lord promised **us**. The pronoun is there. The second half says *ma wa'ada rabbukum* - what your Lord promised. Flat. No object pronoun at all.

The commentators feel the gap and fill it. Al-Muyassar writes it out as *ma wa'adakum rabbukum* - "what your Lord promised **you**." He supplies the word the ayah leaves out.

The ayah itself does not supply it. It leaves the promise standing without naming who it was aimed at - which is, in a way, the more terrible version. The warning was simply out there, in the open, announced. Nobody has to prove it was personally addressed to them.

[PAUSE]

There is also one word doing double duty across this whole exchange, and it is the hinge of the passage: *wa'ada*, to promise.

Al-Tabari glosses the first half as the promise of *reward* for faith and obedience, and al-Muyassar glosses the second half as the promise of *punishment* for disobedience. Same verb. The Qur'an does not switch to a word for "threat" when it turns to the people of the Fire. Both Garden and Fire are *wa'd* - things Allah said He would do.

Which means the question being asked across the divide is not "were you punished?" It is: "did He keep His word?" And there is only one answer to that.

[PAUSE]

And the answer:

*Qalu na'am.*

They said: yes.

There is terrible force in the brevity. No argument remains. No delay remains. One word is enough.

[PAUSE]

You can actually watch a commentator flinch at how short it is. Al-Muyassar cannot leave it at one syllable; he expands it into a full sentence - "Yes, we have indeed found what our Lord promised us to be true." He completes what the Qur'an refuses to complete.

That instinct is understandable. A sentence feels unfinished. But the ayah's whole power is that it does not finish. In the dunya these were people with speeches, with counter-arguments, with alternative readings, with reasons why the warning did not apply. On that Day the entire apparatus reduces to two letters.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir, commenting on this ayah, points the reader across to a scene in Surat As-Saffat, where the same kind of exchange happens - but personally rather than publicly.

There, the people of the Garden turn to one another asking questions, and one of them says: *I had a companion, who used to say to me, "Are you really one of those who believe? When we have died and become dust and bones, are we really going to be called to account?"* And then, a line later: *so he looked, and saw him in the middle of the Blaze.*

[PAUSE]

Put the two scenes side by side and you see what our ayah is doing differently. In As-Saffat it is one man remembering one friend, one voice he used to hear at work or at home, and the shock is intimate. Here in Al-A'raf there are no individuals at all. It is *ashabu al-jannah* and *ashabu al-nar*, whole assemblies, speaking as bodies.

The Qur'an gives us both, and we need both. One tells you what this looks like from the inside of a friendship. The other tells you what it looks like from the outside, as public record.

### 3. Then a Public Announcement Interprets the Whole Scene

Then:

*Fa-adhdhana mu'adhdhinun baynahum.*

Then a caller announced between them.

[PAUSE]

The announcement is public, between the two assemblies. The meaning of the divide is named out loud:

*An la'natu Allahi 'ala al-zalimin.*

The curse of Allah is upon the wrongdoers.

This is not random doom. It is divine judgment interpreted.

[PAUSE]

The word for the caller carries its own weight. *Mu'adhdhin* comes from the root that gives *udhun*, the ear, and *idhn*, permission, and *adhan*, the call to prayer. At its base the root is about something reaching the ear - making a thing heard, putting it into people where it cannot be missed.

So this is not a verdict written in a ledger. It is a verdict *sounded*. The same family of words that in this world summons people toward the path is used here to announce what happened to those who fought it.

[PAUSE]

And the word is rarer than you would expect. *Mu'adhdhin* - a caller, a herald - occurs in exactly two places in the entire Qur'an.

One is here.

The other is in Surat Yusuf, where the cup has been placed in Binyamin's bag and *a caller called out: "O caravan, you are thieves."*

Two heralds in the whole Book, and both of them are announcing an accusation to a crowd that has to stand there and hear it. That is what this kind of voice is for in the Qur'an: not information, but public naming.

[PAUSE]

Notice too that the caller is left indefinite - *mu'adhdhinun*, "a caller," with no name and no description. Classical commentators offer different suggestions about who he is, and the sources gathered for this passage do not settle it. The Qur'an keeps the announcement and drops the announcer. What matters is not whose voice it is. What matters is that it is said out loud, and that nobody can claim they did not hear.

[PAUSE]

Then there is *baynahum* - between them.

The voice is placed in the space that divides the two groups. It does not come from the Garden's side or the Fire's side. It occupies the gap.

And two ayahs later this surah will tell us what is actually sitting in that gap: *and between them is a partition.* A wall stands there. Yet a voice crosses it. Whatever separates the two assemblies is solid enough to be called a barrier and permeable enough to carry an announcement.

[PAUSE]

One more word: *la'nah*, curse. In Arabic the word carries the sense of being driven off and pushed far away - banishment rather than a magical harm. To be cursed by Allah is to be put at distance from Him.

Read that back against the sentence that opened this passage. The people of the Garden have just said they *found* what was promised. Finding is nearness; you can only find what you have come close to. The announcement names the exact opposite condition: driven away.

[PAUSE]

And now something worth sitting with.

The sentence *la'natu Allahi 'ala al-zalimin* - "the curse of Allah is upon the wrongdoers" - occurs in exactly two places in the Qur'an. One is our ayah. The other is in Surat Hud, where people who invented lies against Allah are brought before their Lord, the witnesses say *these are the ones who lied against their Lord*, and then the same words fall: *behold - the curse of Allah is upon the wrongdoers.*

And then look at what comes next in Hud. The following ayah is our ayah 45, word for word, with one small emphatic pronoun added: *those who turn away from the path of Allah and seek to make it crooked, and they are disbelievers in the Hereafter.*

[PAUSE]

So in both of the only two places where this sentence appears, it arrives with the same definition attached. The Qur'an never lets the word *zalimin* stand alone in this formula. Wherever the curse is announced in these words, a description follows immediately telling you exactly who is meant.

That is a mercy in the structure itself. A bare curse on "the wrongdoers" would leave every anxious heart guessing. The Qur'an refuses to leave it vague. It says the sentence, and then it hands you the definition.

### 4. The Wrongdoers Are Defined by Obstruction and Crookedness

Then the next ayah specifies:

*Alladhina yasudduna 'an sabili Allahi wa yabghunaha 'iwajan.*

Those who turn people away from the path of Allah and seek to make it crooked.

[PAUSE]

This is powerful because the wrong is not only failure to walk the path oneself. It is *sadd* - obstructing others from it. And not only obstructing, but *yabghunaha 'iwajan* - wanting the path itself bent, distorted, re-narrated into crooked form.

Al-Tabari is exact about what *sabil Allah* means here. The path of Allah, he says, *is His religion*. So the obstruction is not blocking a road. It is standing in the way of a whole din.

And on the second phrase he is exact again: they attempted the path of Allah - "that they might change it and alter it away from the straightness Allah gave it."

[PAUSE]

Read that carefully and something clarifies. The crookedness is not *in* the path. Allah gave the path its straightness. The crookedness is an attempt made *on* it - a hand reaching for something upright and trying to bend it.

Al-Muyassar adds the motive, and it is chilling in how ordinary it sounds: they want the path to be crooked *so that no one can make it out clearly.*

That is the goal. Not destruction of the path - obscuring of it. A straight road is visible from far away; you can see where it goes. A road with enough bends in it stops looking like it goes anywhere. You do not have to demolish guidance to disable it. You only have to make it hard to see the line.

[PAUSE]

The verb matters too. *Yabghuna* - from a root meaning to seek, to want, to go after something. The ayah does not say they *made* the path crooked. It says they *seek* it crooked.

And they never succeed. Elsewhere the Qur'an says of the Book that Allah sent down: *and He placed no crookedness in it.* The very thing these people are reaching for is the very thing Allah has declared absent. Their whole project is an attempt on something that cannot be done. What is recorded against them, then, is not damage achieved. It is the wanting.

[PAUSE]

The phrase *yabghunaha 'iwajan* occurs three times in the Qur'an - here, in Surat Hud, and in Surat Ibrahim - and twice more in the second person, aimed at people who were still alive to hear it.

One of those is in Al 'Imran, addressed to the People of the Book: *why do you turn away from the path of Allah those who believe, seeking to make it crooked, while you are witnesses?* That last clause is devastating - people who had actually seen the evidence.

[PAUSE]

And the other one is in this very surah.

Forty ayahs after our passage, Shu'ayb says to his people: *do not sit on every road, threatening, and turning away from the path of Allah those who believe in Him, and seeking to make it crooked.*

Sit with that placement. The same charge that is announced by a herald over the damned in ayah 45 is delivered by a living prophet to a living community in ayah 86 - as a warning, while there was still time. Al-A'raf says it once as a verdict and once as an appeal.

Which means this ayah is not only a description of people whose case is closed. It is the finished form of a warning that used to be addressed to people whose case was open.

[PAUSE]

Then the root disease:

*Wa hum bi al-akhirati kafirun.*

While they are disbelievers in the Hereafter.

If there is no believed final accounting, crookedness becomes much easier to normalize.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari breaks this last phrase into three specific denials rather than one vague attitude. They deny, he says, the coming of the Hour; and the resurrection in the Hereafter; and the reward and punishment in it.

That third one is the practical one. A person can hold a loose belief that something happens after death and still live as though nothing is being weighed. It is the denial of *reward and punishment* - not the denial of an afterlife in the abstract - that frees a hand to start bending the road.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: One Day the Truth Will Be Publicly Obvious

Much of dunya is lived in partial visibility. People debate revelation, argue about justice, reinterpret warnings, and treat certainty as if it belonged to nobody. These ayahs remind us that such ambiguity is temporary.

[PAUSE]

There is a day when both the saved and the ruined alike answer from inside what they have found. At that point truth is not merely believed. It is encountered.

And notice what that Day does *not* do. It does not deliver a new message. Al-Tabari's gloss keeps pointing backward: what was found true is what was promised *on the tongues of the messengers*, in the world, in language people already heard. Nothing arrives on that Day that had not already been said.

[PAUSE]

Which reframes what the akhirah actually is in this passage. It is not the moment the information becomes available. It is the moment the information becomes impossible to argue with.

That should change how we treat disagreement now. If the akhirah were the arrival of new evidence, then holding out until then would be reasonable. But if it is only the confirmation of evidence already delivered, then every day spent waiting for something more convincing is a day spent refusing what has already been given.

[PAUSE]

The single word *na'am* is the picture of that. Everything a person ever said against the message, and everything he said in his own defence, ends up compressed into one syllable of agreement that costs him nothing because it changes nothing.

### Theme 2: Crookedness Becomes Most Dangerous When It Tries to Rebrand the Straight Path

The description of the wrongdoers is especially searching. They do not merely wander off. They work against the path and seek to make it crooked. That means one of the gravest corruptions is not blunt hostility alone, but distortion that still remains close enough to the path to talk about it, reinterpret it, and mislead others concerning it.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar's phrase is the one to keep: they want the path crooked *so that no one can make it out clearly*. That is a description of confusion as a strategy. Not opposition - fog.

And the Qur'an's other uses of *yasudduna 'an sabili Allah* show how ordinary the people who do this can look. In one place they are people who marched out of their homes for show and reputation. In another they are religious scholars and monks who consume people's wealth wrongfully and block the way of Allah. Not one of those descriptions is of an obvious enemy. They are insiders - respected, funded, and in some cases officially religious.

[PAUSE]

This should make us fear any role we play in making Allah's path look smaller, more compromised, or more bent than it really is, especially when that distortion comes wrapped in intelligence, respectability, or inherited custom.

And it should make us careful in a very specific way. Al-Tabari said the crookedness is an attempt to move the path away from *the straightness Allah gave it*. So the test is not whether our version of religion is defensible, or popular, or workable. The test is whether it is still the same shape as what was handed down.

[PAUSE]

There is a gentler side to this too, and it is worth saying. If the crime is making the path hard to see, then the opposite of the crime is making it easy to see. Every time someone explains a matter of the din clearly, without bending it to be more comfortable and without bending it to be more severe, they are doing the exact reverse of what this ayah condemns.

### Theme 3: What You Believe About the Last Day Decides What You Do to the Road

Ayah 45 does not put the denial of the Hereafter first. It puts it last, as the closing clause - as if to say: *and here is what was underneath all of it.*

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an makes that link from the other direction too. In Surat Ibrahim, the same charge appears - turning people from the path of Allah and seeking to make it crooked - but there the sentence opens with the diagnosis instead of closing with it: *those who prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter, and turn away from the path of Allah, and seek to make it crooked.*

One ayah ends with it. The other begins with it. Together they close the circle: preferring dunya to akhirah, and bending the path, are two views of one condition.

[PAUSE]

And this surah says it a third way just a few ayahs after our passage, when the people of the Fire are described as *those who took their religion as amusement and play, and worldly life deluded them.*

Look at that description carefully. It is not "those who had no religion." They had one. They just held it the way you hold a game - something you engage with seriously enough while it is happening, and put down when something real comes up.

[PAUSE]

That is the quiet danger in this theme, and it is much closer to home than obstruction and curses. Very few of us are out on the road bending it for others. But almost all of us know what it is to hold the din as one activity among many, in a life whose actual centre of gravity is here.

The ayah's order is the warning. Denial of the akhirah is not listed as the loudest sin. It is listed as the last one - the thing at the bottom that made the rest possible.

### Theme 4: The Passage Names the Wrongdoers, and Then Models How to Respond

There is an adab worth naming here, because a passage that announces a curse is easy to misuse.

The word *zalimin* in this ayah is announced by a herald, on a Day when every case is closed, over people whose file is finished. It is a verdict, not a label handed to the living for use on each other.

[PAUSE]

And the surah itself shows us what to do when we hear it - not in a separate lesson somewhere else, but three ayahs later, inside this very scene.

When eyes in this scene are turned toward the companions of the Fire, the words that come out are not a list of who deserves it. They are a prayer: *Our Lord, do not place us with the wrongdoing people.*

[PAUSE]

That is the response the passage models. Someone in the middle of the judgment scene hears the curse announced on the *zalimin*, looks over at them, and immediately asks not to be one of them.

Not: *now I know who they are.*

Rather: *keep me out of that.*

[PAUSE]

It is a small thing and it changes everything about how these two ayahs should be read. The definition in ayah 45 was given so that we could examine ourselves against it, not so that we could sort our neighbours with it. The description is detailed and public precisely so that a person can hold it up against his own speech, his own teaching, his own way of representing the din, and ask honestly whether any of it fits.

The living do not have closed cases. That is the whole reason a definition this specific is worth reading.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs stage one of the Qur'an's great public unveilings.

The people of Paradise confirm promise fulfilled. The people of Fire answer with a bare yes. Then a caller announces the meaning of it all: Allah's curse is upon the wrongdoers, especially those who obstructed His path, sought to bend it, and denied the Hereafter that would have exposed them.

[PAUSE]

And the surah is not finished with this exchange. Six ayahs later it happens again, in reverse.

*And the companions of the Fire will call to the companions of Paradise* - the exact same opening, with the two groups swapped - *"Pour upon us some water, or some of what Allah has provided you."*

Put the two calls next to each other and the whole architecture appears. In our ayah, Paradise asks a question, and it costs the Fire nothing to answer it; they can say *yes* freely, because agreeing is all that is left to them. In the later ayah, the Fire asks for something, and the answer is that Allah has forbidden both to those who rejected Him.

[PAUSE]

One direction can still speak and be answered. The other can speak and receive nothing. That is the difference the announcement in between was naming all along.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: am I merely trying not to leave the path myself, or am I also careful not to speak, model, or normalize religion in a way that makes the path look crooked to others?

And a second question, from Al-A'raf's own placement of the same charge in the mouth of Shu'ayb: if that warning were being delivered to a living community now, with time still left, is there anything in how I carry this din that it would be aimed at?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **when the final world arrives, truth becomes publicly undeniable, and among the most condemned are not only those who refused Allah's path, but those who spent life making that path harder to see straight.**
