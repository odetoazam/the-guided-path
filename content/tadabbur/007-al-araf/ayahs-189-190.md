# From One Soul, Then a Shared Prayer

## Surah Al-Araf (7:189-190)

[ayah:7:189] ۞ هُوَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَكُم مِّن نَّفْسٍ وَٰحِدَةٍ وَجَعَلَ مِنْهَا زَوْجَهَا لِيَسْكُنَ إِلَيْهَا ۖ فَلَمَّا تَغَشَّىٰهَا حَمَلَتْ حَمْلًا خَفِيفًا فَمَرَّتْ بِهِۦ ۖ فَلَمَّآ أَثْقَلَت دَّعَوَا ٱللَّهَ رَبَّهُمَا لَئِنْ ءَاتَيْتَنَا صَـٰلِحًا لَّنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ ٱلشَّـٰكِرِينَ

[ayah:7:190] فَلَمَّآ ءَاتَىٰهُمَا صَـٰلِحًا جَعَلَا لَهُۥ شُرَكَآءَ فِيمَآ ءَاتَىٰهُمَا ۚ فَتَعَـٰلَى ٱللَّهُ عَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

## Introduction

These ayahs move through one of the most human scenes in the surah.

Creation from one soul. The making of a mate. Repose between the two. The quiet beginning of pregnancy. Then the burden grows heavier, vulnerability deepens, and both parents suddenly stand before Allah in prayer. Few human moments strip away illusion like that one. When a life entrusted to us is forming beyond our control, tawhid becomes urgent.

[PAUSE]

Then the passage turns with painful sharpness. The gift requested from Allah arrives, yet gratitude is corrupted. The blessing is not denied as a blessing. It is mis-related. Something of shirk enters where shukr had been promised.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. Human Intimacy Begins in Unity and Rest

Ayah 189 opens:

*Huwa alladhi khalaqakum min nafsin wahidah wa ja'ala minha zawjaha li-yaskuna ilayha.*

He is the One who created you from a single soul and made from it its mate so that he might find repose in her.

[PAUSE]

The beginning is theological before it is relational. *Huwa alladhi* - He is the One. Human pairing is introduced as divine making, not self-originating arrangement.

Then: *min nafsin wahidah* - from a single soul. The early authorities read this concretely: al-Tabari says the single soul means Adam, citing a report from Mujahid to that effect, and al-Muyassar adds the other half plainly - the mate made from it is Hawwa. Ibn Kathir frames the whole opening under a single heading: all mankind are the offspring of Adam, and from the two of them people spread across the earth. It is the same tawhid-rooted lineage the Qur'an names elsewhere when it says *"O mankind, We created you from a male and a female"* (49:13). The phrase carries unity at the root of humanity. Before rivalry, before fragmentation, before the later corruptions of desire and idolatry, there is shared origin.

And the purpose of the mate is named with beautiful economy:

*Li-yaskuna ilayha.*

So that he might find repose in her.

The root *s-k-n* gives stillness, settledness, rest. al-Muyassar glosses the phrase as being made so that he might find intimacy and quiet assurance in her - *li-ya'nasa biha wa yatma'inna*. The first note struck here is not conquest or utility. It is sakinah-like repose, a human quieting within lawful companionship.

### 2. Pregnancy Exposes Dependence and Draws Out Du'a

Then the ayah moves into embodied life:

*Fa-lamma taghashshaha hamalat hamlan khafifan fa-marrat bihi.*

When he covered her, she carried a light burden and moved about with it.

[PAUSE]

*Taghashshaha* - a Form V verb from a root that carries the sense of one thing enveloping and covering another - is read by the commentators as the conjugal union between the pair; al-Muyassar renders it plainly as their coming together. And notice, right at this word, how al-Muyassar reads the couple: he pauses mid-sentence to say that what is meant is *the genus of husband and wife among Adam's descendants* - not the first two persons alone. The scene is at once the primal marriage and every marriage that repeats its pattern. Hold onto that gloss; it becomes the key to the hardest question in the next ayah.

The burden begins light. al-Muyassar draws out a physical realism starker than the translation suggests: what she first carries is a light drop of fluid, and she carries it as she stands and sits and goes about her days, until she completes the term and her delivery draws near. Life starts almost hidden. The ayah does not rush the scene. It lets us feel the early stage when the trust is present but not yet crushing in weight.

Then:

*Fa-lamma athqalat da'awa Allaha Rabbahuma.*

Then when it grew heavy, the two called upon Allah, their Lord.

This is one of the most revealing turns in the passage. As heaviness grows, du'a rises. Anxiety clarifies dependence. The two are not shown negotiating power with life. They are shown praying.

And what do they ask?

*La'in ataytana salihan la-nakunanna mina al-shakirin.*

If You give us a sound child, we will surely be among the grateful.

The *salih* they beg for is glossed by al-Muyassar as a child sound and whole in form - *basharan sawiyyan salihan* - a healthy, complete human being; and the promise attached is shukr, that they will be among those who thank Him for the sound child He grants. In moments of need, the fitrah often speaks cleanly. The heart knows where the gift must be traced.

### 3. The Gift Arrives, but the Relationship to the Giver Is Corrupted

Then ayah 190 turns:

*Fa-lamma atahuma salihan ja'ala lahu shuraka'a fima atahuma.*

But when He gave the two a sound child, they made for Him partners regarding what He had given them.

[PAUSE]

The wording is severe because the source of the gift remains explicit. *Atahuma.* He gave the two. al-Tabari begins his comment by tying the answer back to the request - Allah provided them a sound child *exactly as they had asked* - which sharpens the betrayal: this was not an unlooked-for blessing that arrived without context, but the specific thing they had begged for and promised to thank Him for. And al-Muyassar underscores who produced it: the child was one *Allah alone brought into being*, so any partner set up beside Him in that gift is a lie about who actually gave.

Here the commentators disagree, and because the disagreement touches something serious it should be stated plainly rather than smoothed over. al-Tabari, after glossing the opening, says in his own words that the people of interpretation *differed* over what the "partners" were. One transmitted line - reported through Qatada from al-Hasan from Samura ibn Jundub, and raised to a narration from the Prophet ﷺ - understands the shirk as lying "in the name": a name was given to the child that made him a servant of someone other than Allah. al-Muyassar carries the same sense in his gloss of this ayah, that the child whose creation Allah alone accomplished was then, by naming, devoted to other than Him.

[PAUSE]

But that report does not settle *who* the actors were, and a great many scholars declined to build the ayah's meaning on it at all, questioning its transmission. The stronger evidence is in the sentence itself. The verb of associating is dual - *ja'ala*, "the two made" - yet the closing indictment moves to the plural: *'amma yushrikun*, "what *they* associate." The sentence widens before it ends. And al-Muyassar has already told us who the pair in this scene is: back at the pregnancy he read the couple as the genus of husband and wife among Adam's descendants. Read that way the whole unit is coherent. Allah opens with the single origin of humankind, narrates the pattern that repeats in every marriage descended from it, and closes by naming the idolaters among those descendants who receive a sound child from Allah and then attach it to charms, ancestors, false deities, and lucky signs.

This is the reading that deserves the weight, and not only because it fits the grammar. Adam was a prophet - one whom, as the Qur'an says, his Lord chose, turned to in mercy, and guided (20:122). To read this verse as a record of shirk committed by a prophet and his wife is to assert about a chosen servant of Allah something the Qur'an nowhere states and that the plural ending of the verse actively resists. The reports about a name are transmitted material, weighed and contested; they are not the settled sense of the ayah, and nothing in the reflection below depends on them. What is beyond dispute is the moral shape, and it holds either way.

The phrase *fima atahuma* matters. It is in what He gave them. The blessing itself becomes the site of corruption. That is the danger. Gifts from Allah do not only test us through deprivation when they are withheld. They also test us through attribution when they are received.

Then the ayah closes with transcendence:

*Fata'ala Allahu 'amma yushrikun.*

Exalted is Allah above what they associate.

The closing rises abruptly upward, as if to cleanse the air after the ugliness of the act. al-Muyassar reads *ta'ala* as Allah's exaltation and purity above every form of shirk. Allah remains utterly above the partners people invent around His gifts.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Human Need Often Produces Pure Tawhid Before Comfort Complicates It

There is something painfully recognizable here. In difficulty, people often become theologically clear.

When the burden is light, life feels manageable. When it grows heavy, the illusion of control weakens. Then du'a comes alive. Then phrases of dependence become sincere. "If You give us..." "If You save us..." "If You open this..." In those moments the heart often speaks with honesty.

[PAUSE]

But the passage warns that the real test is not only whether we call upon Allah under pressure. It is whether we preserve that clarity after relief arrives. Many people speak the language of tawhid in the delivery room, the hospital corridor, the crisis night, the exam season, the immigration line, the debt spiral, the waiting period. Then when the gift finally comes, the heart begins to attach ultimate significance to means, persons, symbols, systems, or superstitions around the gift itself. That drift is exactly what the sentence enacts when it moves from the dual "the two made" to the plural "what they associate" - a single human scene opening out into the recurring habit of a whole species, which is why the verse ends indicting the many rather than the two.

### Theme 2: Blessing Can Become a Site of Shirk If Gratitude Is Not Guarded

The passage does not present shirk here in abstract theological terms first. It presents it in the atmosphere of blessing.

That is important. People often imagine idolatry as something far away, cold, formal, and obvious. But one of the ways the Qur'an teaches us to recognize its logic is this: whenever a gift from Allah is received and then functionally re-sourced, re-attributed, or emotionally treated as though others now share in Allah's decisive giving, the corruption of gratitude has begun. And the transmitted reading that locates shirk even in a *name* - a single word spoken over a newborn - is sobering whatever one concludes about its chain. It shows how little it takes for a heart to smuggle a partner into a blessing that Allah alone gave.

[PAUSE]

This does not mean denying means. Parents, doctors, provision, medicine, effort, and human care all exist. The Qur'an never asks us to erase secondary causes. It asks us not to let secondary causes rise into rival claimants over the heart. The gift must keep leading us back to the Giver.

## Closing Synthesis

These two ayahs move from shared origin and tenderness to one of the subtlest spiritual dangers: corruption after relief.

Allah creates humanity from a single soul, makes the mate for repose, then lets the scene move into pregnancy, growing heaviness, and a shared cry to Him for a sound child with a promise of gratitude. But when the gift is given - given, al-Tabari notes, precisely as it was asked for - the response is not preserved in purity. Partners are introduced in relation to what Allah Himself gave, and the passage closes by exalting Allah far above every association laid upon His blessings.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: what promises did I make to Allah when I was vulnerable that became less clear once relief arrived? When a blessing enters my life, does it deepen tawhid and shukr, or does it quietly become wrapped in rival attributions, anxieties, and attachments that steal from the Giver's due? And where have I confused gratitude for the means with a heart-level dependence that belongs only to Allah?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the unit lands here: **the Qur'an shows that even the most intimate human blessings are given within tawhid, and that the real danger is not only failing to pray in vulnerability but receiving the answered gift and then allowing gratitude to be corrupted by associations that the Giver never shared.**
