# They Say, "We Will Be Forgiven"

## Surah Al-Araf (7:167-169)

[ayah:7:167] وَإِذْ تَأَذَّنَ رَبُّكَ لَيَبْعَثَنَّ عَلَيْهِمْ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ مَن يَسُومُهُمْ سُوٓءَ ٱلْعَذَابِ ۗ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَسَرِيعُ ٱلْعِقَابِ ۖ وَإِنَّهُۥ لَغَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

## Introduction

Not every punishment ends at the moment of impact. Some become a condition stretched across history.

That is how this passage opens. After the Sabbath-town story, the surah widens the frame and shows a declaration hanging over Banū Isrāʾīl through time: recurring humiliation, recurring testing, recurring opportunities to return. The classical commentators are unanimous on the historical subject here — Ibn Kathīr titles the section "the humiliation placed upon the Children of Israel," and al-Muyassar reads the verse as a promise to send against *the Jews* those who would make them taste the evil of punishment and abasement until the Day of Resurrection, all of it tied to their persistent disobedience. And yet even here the Qur'an refuses moral laziness. It says they are scattered as nations, and among them are righteous people, while others are less than that. So the passage is not only about historical consequence. It is about Allah's exactness. He can judge a people severely without ceasing to know the difference between the upright remnant and the corrupted heir.

[PAUSE]

Then the final ayah brings the danger closer to the heart. The issue is no longer merely what enemies do to a community from outside, but what heirs of the Book do from inside: taking the passing goods of the lower life while soothing themselves with a cheap sentence, *sa-yughfaru lana* - we will be forgiven. The tragedy is not ignorance without scripture. It is self-deception beside inherited revelation.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. The Declaration of Ongoing Humiliation Is Framed Inside Allah's Two-Sided Lordship

Ayah 167 begins:

*Wa idh ta'adhdhana Rabbuka la-yab'athanna 'alayhim...*

When your Lord declared that He would surely send upon them...

[PAUSE]

The verb *ta'adhdhana* carries a forceful announcing, a made-known declaration that will not remain hidden. Al-Tabarī notes that it is built on the pattern of *īdhān* — a proclaiming, a making-known — and even reaches for a line of the old poet al-Aʿshā to show that the root means "to make known" or "announce," so that the sense of the opening is close to *"and remember, O Muhammad, when your Lord announced and made known."* Here the classical scholars part slightly on the exact force: Mujāhid glosses *taʾadhdhana* as simply "declared," while others read it as "ordained" — Ibn Kathīr adds that the phrasing carries the weight of a vow, a binding pledge. Either way the content is fixed: until the Day of Resurrection there will be those who subject them to grievous punishment. This is not a passing political setback. It is a historical condition under divine decree.

But the verse does not end there. It closes with:

*Inna Rabbaka la-sari'u al-'iqab wa innahu la-ghafurun rahim.*

Your Lord is swift in punishment, and He is surely forgiving and merciful.

That pairing matters. Allah is not trapped inside one attribute in our sight. The same Lord who is swift in punishment — al-Muyassar adds, *for whoever deserves it by his disbelief and disobedience* — is still *ghafur rahim*, and al-Muyassar directs that mercy pointedly: forgiving of the sins of *those who repent*, merciful toward them. The forgiveness is not a vague amnesty hovering over the wrongdoing; it is the open door held ready for the one who turns back. The passage refuses the simplification that a history of chastisement means the doors of mercy have been erased. Punishment is real, but mercy remains a live truth for whoever returns.

### 2. Scattering Does Not Mean Moral Sameness

Ayah 168 says:

*Wa qatta'nahum fi al-ardi umaman.*

We broke them up in the earth as nations.

[PAUSE]

This is dispersal, fragmentation, loss of unified settlement — al-Tabarī glosses *umaman* as "scattered groups, separated communities," and reports from Ibn ʿAbbās the vivid picture that "into every land some group of them enters." Ibn Kathīr connects this scattering to the divine word in Sūrat al-Isrāʾ, *"Dwell in the land, and when the promise of the Hereafter comes We shall bring you as a mixed crowd"* (17:104) — the dispersal itself is folded inside a longer decree. But immediately the ayah qualifies the picture:

*Minhumu al-salihuna wa minhum duna dhalik.*

Among them are the righteous, and among them are other than that.

The Qur'an will not let us flatten a scattered people into one single moral summary. Some are *salihun* — al-Muyassar defines them as those who uphold both the rights of Allah and the rights of His servants — while others are *duna dhalik*, below that: the negligent who wrong their own souls. Then the ayah names the method of Allah with them:

*Wa balawnahum bi-al-hasanati wa al-sayyi'ati la'allahum yarji'un.*

We tested them with good things and bad things so that perhaps they might return.

This is a profound line. Al-Muyassar unfolds both sides of the test: We tried them with ease of living and abundance of provision, and We tried them also with hardship of living and with calamities and afflictions — the whole design aimed at one hope, *that they might return to the obedience of their Lord and repent from disobeying Him.* Blessings test. Hardships test. Ease is not proof of approval, and pain is not proof of abandonment. Both can be forms of *bala'* meant to produce *ruju'*, a returning back to Allah.

### 3. The Most Dangerous Sentence in the Passage Is "We Will Be Forgiven"

Ayah 169 describes later heirs:

*Fa-khalafa min ba'dihim khalfun warithu al-kitab...*

After them came successors who inherited the Book.

[PAUSE]

The tragedy begins with privilege. They inherited the Book. They are not outside revelation. And the very word chosen for them, *khalf*, carries a shadow: the classical usage distinguishes *khalaf* (an upright successor) from *khalf* (a blameworthy one), and it is the latter, weighted form the ayah selects for these heirs.

Then the ayah exposes them:

*Ya'khudhuna 'arada hadha al-adna wa yaquluna sa-yughfaru lana.*

They take the fleeting goods of this lower world and say: we will be forgiven.

The word *'arad* is telling. It is passing stuff, a transient gain, something that does not endure — from a root that means what merely *presents itself* and then is gone. And *al-adna* lowers the whole scene further: at once "the nearer" and "the lower" life, the meaner of two options. So they trade the inheritance of the Book for what is brief and low, then cover the trade with presumptive hope in forgiveness.

The Qur'an then tears away the excuse:

*Wa in ya'tihim 'aradun mithluhu ya'khudhuhu.*

If a gain like it comes to them, they take it again.

[PAUSE]

That repetition exposes the lie. This is not a stumble followed by remorse. It is a pattern accompanied by religious self-soothing.

Then the ayah asks:

*A-lam yu'khadh 'alayhim mithaqu al-kitabi an la yaqulu 'ala Allahi illa al-haqq?*

Was not the covenant of the Book taken from them that they would say about Allah nothing but the truth?

And yet they had already *darasu ma fihi* - studied what was in it. Their problem is not lack of access. It is betrayal beside knowledge. The covenant they broke was specifically about *speech about Allah* — that they say concerning Him nothing but the truth — which makes their glib *sa-yughfaru lana* not merely wishful but a violation of the very pledge they had memorized: they invent about their Lord a forgiveness He never guaranteed for the unrepentant.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: One of the Most Dangerous Forms of Sin Is to Turn Divine Mercy Into Cover for Repeating the Same Worldliness

The sentence *sa-yughfaru lana* can sound pious on the surface. It mentions forgiveness. It remembers mercy. But in this ayah it is the speech of delusion, because it is attached to a repeated willingness to seize the same lower gain again and again. It is worth setting it beside 167's promise that Allah is forgiving and merciful *to those who repent*: the same passage that keeps the door of mercy open also shows what it looks like to stand at that door and refuse to walk through it — to hold the language of forgiveness while withholding the turning-back that forgiveness is for.

[PAUSE]

This is a terrifying spiritual pattern. A person does not deny Allah's mercy. He abuses it. He does not reject revelation openly. He keeps it nearby while treating it as insurance. He sins, consoles himself with future forgiveness, and when the same opportunity returns, he takes it again without real rupture in the soul.

That can happen to anyone with religious literacy. We can know the texts, inherit the language, study the covenant, and still use sacred knowledge to make ourselves emotionally comfortable inside repeated compromise. At that point mercy is no longer being received as a door into tawbah. It is being weaponized against tawbah.

### Theme 2: Allah Tests Communities Through Both History and Immediate Gain

This passage moves between civilizational scale and intimate scale. On one level there is scattering, humiliation, historical vulnerability, and long arcs of testing across peoples and generations — the *hasanat* and *sayyi'at* that al-Muyassar reads as prosperity and abundance on one hand, hardship and calamity on the other. On another level there is one immediate temptation: a piece of worldly gain in front of the hand.

[PAUSE]

That means the crisis of a community is not only what happens to it geopolitically. It is also what its heirs do when the lower life offers itself cheaply. A people may speak endlessly about historical suffering while remaining unable to refuse immediate advantage purchased by betrayal of truth. The Qur'an will not let us separate those levels. And it is sobering that the same test of *hasanat* — good things, ease — that was meant to draw them back to their Lord becomes, in the hands of the later *khalf*, the very *'arad* they clutch and cannot release. The gift meant to produce return became the bait that produced betrayal.

And the passage ends by lifting the eye again:

*Wa al-daru al-akhiratu khayrun lilladhina yattaqun.*

The home of the Hereafter is better for those who have taqwa.

The cure for low bargains is not merely stronger willpower. It is a restored scale of value. If the akhirah becomes real again, the lower goods shrink back to their proper size.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs trace a painful movement from historical chastisement to internalized self-deception.

Allah declares a long condition of humiliation over Banū Isrāʾīl, yet the same verse keeps mercy alive by naming Him swift in punishment and still forgiving and merciful to those who turn back. They are scattered across the earth into communities in every land, but the Qur'an still distinguishes the righteous among them — those who keep the rights of Allah and of His servants — from those below that, and it explains that both blessings and afflictions were sent as tests so they might return. Then the deepest wound appears: heirs of the Book who know the covenant, study the text, repeatedly grab the passing goods of the lower world, and pacify themselves with the phrase, "We will be forgiven."

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this unit is to ask: where have I turned hope in Allah's forgiveness into a cushion for repeating the same compromise? Do I still feel that worldly gain is small because the akhirah is greater, or has the passing *'arad* of this life quietly become heavy in my hand again?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the unit lands here: **a people can suffer long consequences in history and still be tested anew in every generation, but one of the gravest failures of the heirs of revelation is to know the covenant, prefer the lower gain, and keep using mercy-language to make repeated betrayal feel safe.**
