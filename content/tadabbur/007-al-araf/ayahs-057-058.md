# Thus We Bring Forth the Dead

## Surah Al-Araf (7:57-58)

[ayah:7:57] وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُرْسِلُ ٱلرِّيَـٰحَ بُشْرًۢا بَيْنَ يَدَىْ رَحْمَتِهِۦ ۖ حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَقَلَّتْ سَحَابًا ثِقَالًا سُقْنَـٰهُ لِبَلَدٍ مَّيِّتٍ فَأَنزَلْنَا بِهِ ٱلْمَآءَ فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِۦ مِن كُلِّ ٱلثَّمَرَٰتِ ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نُخْرِجُ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَذَكَّرُونَ

[ayah:7:58] وَٱلْبَلَدُ ٱلطَّيِّبُ يَخْرُجُ نَبَاتُهُۥ بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِۦ ۖ وَٱلَّذِى خَبُثَ لَا يَخْرُجُ إِلَّا نَكِدًا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نُصَرِّفُ ٱلْـَٔايَـٰتِ لِقَوْمٍ يَشْكُرُونَ

*Wa huwa alladhi yursilu al-riyaha bushran bayna yaday rahmatih. Hatta idha aqallat sahaban thiqalan suqnahu li-baladin mayyitin fa-anzalna bihi al-ma'a fa-akhrajna bihi min kulli al-thamarat. Kadhalika nukhriju al-mawta la'allakum tadhakkarun.*

*Wa al-baladu al-tayyibu yakhruju nabatuhu bi-idhni rabbih. Wa alladhi khabutha la yakhruju illa nakidan. Kadhalika nusarrifu al-ayati li-qawmin yashkurun.*

**And He is the One who sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy until, when they have carried heavy clouds, We drive them to a dead land, then We send down water upon it and bring forth thereby every kind of fruit. Thus do We bring forth the dead, so that you may remember. And the good land - its vegetation emerges by permission of its Lord; but that which is bad does not emerge except meagerly. Thus do We diversify the signs for a people who are grateful.**

## Introduction

These ayahs are among the Qur'an's most gentle lessons in resurrection.

Nothing here begins with terror. It begins with winds carrying glad tidings before mercy, clouds gathering, and a dead land receiving water until fruit emerges from what looked finished. Then the surah says plainly: thus We bring forth the dead.

[PAUSE]

Something small is worth noticing before anything else. In the whole of ayah 57, the ordinary Arabic word for rain is never used. What comes down is called two things: *rahmah*, His mercy, and *al-ma'*, the water. The event that any farmer would have called rain is named once by its meaning and once by its substance, and never by its everyday name. Before a single point is argued, the vocabulary has already reframed the sky.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir notes where this sits in the surah. It follows the passage in which Allah has established that He created the heavens and the earth, that the affair belongs to Him, that He has made things serve mankind, and that He is to be called upon because He is able to do all things. So the rain is not introduced as a nature scene. It arrives as evidence in an argument that was already running.

[PAUSE]

But the passage does not stop at bodily resurrection. It moves into moral agriculture. Good land brings out its growth by the permission of its Lord. Corrupted land yields poorly. So the same rain that proves resurrection also becomes a parable of revelation and the heart. The question is not only whether water came down. The question is what kind of earth received it.

[PAUSE]

The hook of this passage is this: **Allah teaches resurrection through the revival of dead land and teaches spiritual receptivity through the differing responses of good and spoiled soil, so that we learn both how the dead will rise and how hearts respond to mercy now.**

[PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. Mercy Arrives First as a Sign Before the Downpour

The ayah begins:

*Wa huwa alladhi yursilu al-riyaha bushran bayna yaday rahmatih.*

He is the One who sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy.

[PAUSE]

The wording is beautiful. The rain is not the first thing named. Before mercy fully arrives, there are signs that announce it. *Bushran* - glad tidings. The believer is being taught to read the approach of mercy before its fullness lands.

[PAUSE]

Look at the phrase that carries it: *bayna yaday rahmatih*. Literally, *between the hands of His mercy*. It is the ordinary Arabic way of saying "in front of," but the picture underneath the idiom is a body: the winds go where the hands go, out ahead of the one arriving. The wind is not the mercy. The wind is what runs in front of it.

[PAUSE]

And the winds are plural - *al-riyah*, not one wind. Al-Muyassar describes them as the good, gentle winds, bearing news of the rain that they themselves stir up by Allah's permission, so that creation begins to rejoice in Allah's mercy before a drop has fallen. That is a precise observation about how mercy is experienced. There is a stage before the gift where you only feel the air change, and that stage is already part of the gift.

[PAUSE]

There is a well-known difference among the reciters here. Al-Tabari, when he restates the ayah in his own words, gives the word as *nushuran* - scattered, spread out - and then pauses to discuss the form of the word and how the Arabs used it. Other readings give *bushran*, glad tidings, which is the reading in front of us. The two are not in conflict so much as layered: winds that scatter and spread, and winds that announce. Either way the wind's job is the same. It goes first, and it goes wide.

### 2. The Wind Lifts What Is Heavy As If It Were Light

Then:

*Hatta idha aqallat sahaban thiqalan.*

Until, when they have carried heavy clouds.

[PAUSE]

The verb is *aqallat*, and its root is *q-l-l* - the root of *qalil*, little, few, light. In the fourth form the verb means to lift something up, to carry it, in the sense of finding it light enough to bear. So the literal sense of the sentence is something like: *until the winds found them easy to lift.*

And what is the object of that verb? *Sahaban thiqala* - heavy clouds. The word for heavy sits directly beside a verb built out of the word for light.

[PAUSE]

That is not an accident of vocabulary. It is the whole point in miniature. A rain cloud carries a weight of water no one could lift, and the air carries it without strain. What is impossible by one measure is effortless by another. Hold that thought, because in a few words the passage is going to ask you about raising the dead.

### 3. Dead Land Is Chosen as the Site of Revival

Then:

*Suqnahu li-baladin mayyit.*

We drive it to a dead land.

[PAUSE]

The land is called *mayyit* - dead. Not merely dry, but dead. Then water descends and fruit emerges.

This is why the analogy works with such force. Allah does not ask us to imagine life from slightly weakened matter. He asks us to watch life return to what looked finished.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar fills in what that death looked like: a land whose ground had gone barren, whose trees and crops had dried out. That detail matters. This is not empty desert that never held anything. It is land that had lived, and stopped. And when the rain comes, al-Muyassar says its trees *returned* laden with kinds of fruit. Returned. The vocabulary of the revival is the vocabulary of coming back to something that was there before.

[PAUSE]

Notice also the verb that moves the cloud. *Suqnahu* - We drove it. The root *s-w-q* is the ordinary word for driving, the word you would use for a herdsman moving animals along a road toward somewhere in particular. The cloud is not drifting. It is being taken somewhere. Al-Muyassar says it plainly: Allah drove it there in order to give life to that land.

[PAUSE]

And watch who is speaking. The ayah opens in the third person - *He* is the One who sends the winds. Then, exactly at the moment the cloud is driven, the voice changes: *We* drove it, *We* sent down the water, *We* brought forth the fruit, *We* bring forth the dead. The description was about Him; the action is spoken by Him. The shift happens at the precise word where the scene stops being weather and starts being a decision.

[PAUSE]

The destination is also left indefinite - *li-baladin mayyit*, to *a* dead land, some dead land, any dead land. It is not one famous valley. It is the pattern everywhere, every season.

### 4. The Ayah Itself Names the Resurrection Argument

Then comes the interpretive key:

*Kadhalika nukhriju al-mawta.*

Thus We bring forth the dead.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an does not leave the parable implicit. The revival of land is itself an argument, a demonstration, a training of imagination so that bodily resurrection stops sounding strange.

[PAUSE]

But the argument is carried by something even tighter than the word *kadhalika*, "thus." It is carried by a repeated verb.

A few words earlier, of the fruit: *fa-akhrajna bihi min kulli al-thamarat* - **We brought out** by it every kind of fruit. And now, of the dead: *nukhriju al-mawta* - **We bring out** the dead. Same root, *kh-r-j*. Same verb form. Same subject: We.

[PAUSE]

The sentence does not say the resurrection will be *like* the harvest. It uses one verb for both and simply changes the object. Fruit, then the dead. The Qur'an is not reaching for a comparison; it is reporting the same act done to two different things. Al-Muyassar reads it exactly this way: as We give life to this dead land with rain, We bring the dead out of their graves alive after they have crumbled away.

[PAUSE]

And the root of "dead" ties the two halves together the same way. The land is *mayyit*. Those who will be raised are *al-mawta*. One root, *m-w-t*, sits at both ends of the ayah - once on soil, once on people.

[PAUSE]

Then the purpose clause: *la'allakum tadhakkarun*. So that you may remember.

Not "so that you may learn." *Tadhakkur* is the word for calling something back to mind, recovering what you already had. Resurrection is not being introduced to you as news. It is being handed back to you as something you were always in a position to know, because you have watched it happen to the ground under your feet every year of your life.

[PAUSE]

Notice too who is addressed: *la'allakum* - you, all of you, plural, without qualification. The sign of the reviving earth is put in front of everybody. Keep that in mind, because the next ayah ends differently.

### 5. The Good Land and the Corrupted Land Do Not Respond the Same Way

Then the next ayah deepens everything:

*Wa al-baladu al-tayyibu yakhruju nabatuhu bi-idhni rabbih.*

The good land - its growth comes forth by the permission of its Lord.

[PAUSE]

Two things in that short sentence repay slowing down.

First, the land is not the one doing the work. In Arabic, the subject of the verb is *nabatuhu* - its vegetation. The plant comes out; the land does not push it out. Grammatically the good land is a place, not an agent. Its whole excellence is that something else can rise through it.

[PAUSE]

Second, even that is qualified: *bi-idhni rabbih*, by the permission of its Lord. The best soil in the world does not produce on its own credit. And the ayah says *its* Lord - the land has a Lord, a possessive attached to it. The good ground is not independent; it is owned, and its yield is licensed.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari describes the good land as one whose soil is good and whose watering places are sweet, and says its produce comes out in its own time and season. Two conditions, not one: what the ground is made of, and what runs through it.

[PAUSE]

Then the contrast:

*Wa alladhi khabutha la yakhruju illa nakida.*

That which is corrupted does not come forth except meagerly.

The same world, the same rain, different yield. The difference now lies in receptivity.

[PAUSE]

Now compare how the two halves are built, because the grammar is not symmetrical and the asymmetry is the mercy in the ayah.

The first is *al-baladu al-tayyib* - a noun with an adjective. The good land, described.

The second is not an adjective at all. It is *alladhi khabutha* - "the one that **went** bad." A relative pronoun and a verb in the past tense. The Qur'an does not say "the bad land." It says the land that became bad.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari's gloss follows the same grammar. He does not describe a category of ground; he describes a change of state: its soil turned poor, its watering places turned salty. Something happened to it. Salinity is not how ground is born. It is what ground comes to after a long history of what has run through it.

[PAUSE]

That is a guard the ayah builds into itself, and it is worth holding onto, because this is the point where the parable is most often misused. Nothing here says that some people were made of good dirt and others of bad. One side is a description; the other is a verb, with a past tense behind it. And on the good side, the yield is not credited to the soil either - it comes *by permission of its Lord*. Neither half leaves room for anyone to look at another person and file them under a soil type.

[PAUSE]

Then the last word of the contrast: *nakid*.

Al-Tabari glosses it as *'asiran fi shiddah* - coming out with difficulty, under strain, grudgingly - and cites a line of old Arabic poetry in which the word describes a person: someone who does not deliver when he promises, and whose giving, when it comes at all, comes hard.

So *nakid* is not mainly a measurement of quantity. It is a description of manner. The problem with the spoiled ground is not only that little grows. It is that everything that does grow is extracted grudgingly, late, and without sweetness.

[PAUSE]

And look at what the sentence does not say. It does not say nothing comes out. It says *illa nakida* - nothing comes out **except** grudgingly. Something still comes. Even the salted ground yields. That single word keeps the door open, and it is the reason this parable is a diagnosis and not a sentence.

### 6. The Signs Are Turned So They Can Be Seen From Every Side

The passage closes:

*Kadhalika nusarrifu al-ayati li-qawmin yashkurun.*

Thus do We turn the signs about, for a people who are grateful.

[PAUSE]

*Nusarrifu*, from the root *s-r-f*, is the verb for turning something this way and that, over and over. Al-Muyassar explains it as varying the proofs and the evidence - the same truth presented from one face, then another, then another, until it can be recognised. That is exactly what the two ayahs have just done: the same claim delivered first as weather, then as farming, then as the grave, then as the state of a heart.

[PAUSE]

And then the ending narrows. Ayah 57 ended with *la'allakum tadhakkarun* - so that **you** may remember, addressed to everyone. Ayah 58 ends with *li-qawmin yashkurun* - for a people who are **grateful**.

[PAUSE]

The sign is shown to all. It lands with the thankful. Al-Muyassar reads *yashkurun* as those who are grateful for Allah's blessings and who obey Him - gratitude that has consequences in behaviour, not just in feeling. Which is the only ending that fits a parable about soil: gratitude is what receiving looks like from the inside.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Resurrection Is Meant to Become Imaginable Through the World Around Us

One of the mercies of the Qur'an is that it does not only assert resurrection propositionally. It trains the eye to see its pattern in the world already. Dead land lives again. Fruit emerges from what had no sign of life. The earth itself becomes a tutor in akhirah.

[PAUSE]

This matters because many denials of resurrection are failures of imagination before they become failures of creed. The ayah rehabilitates imagination by making the world a rehearsal for what will come.

[PAUSE]

It is worth noticing what kind of argument this is. There is no premise and conclusion here, no "if you accept this, you must accept that." There is a scene, and then a sentence that names the scene's meaning. The persuasion is done by watching. You are not being asked to follow a chain of reasoning; you are being asked to look at a field in spring and admit you have already seen the thing you say cannot happen.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar draws two conclusions out of the ayah, not one: that you may take heed, and from it come to know both Allah's oneness and His power to raise the dead. The same rainfall answers two different questions. Who is behind this? And can He do it again, to me?

[PAUSE]

There is also a quiet hinge in the middle of ayah 57 that the next ayah will pick up. One water comes down, and what comes up is *min kulli al-thamarat* - every kind of fruit. A single rain, an enormous variety of outcomes. In ayah 57 that variety is evidence of power. In ayah 58 the same fact - one rain, different results - turns and becomes a question about the ground. The passage uses the same observation twice, first to prove something about Allah, then to ask something about us.

### Theme 2: Revelation Falls Widely, But Hearts Do Not Yield Equally

The land image in ayah 58 is spiritually severe. It suggests that the issue is not always whether revelation, reminder, or mercy reached a person. Sometimes the issue is what kind of inner soil received it.

[PAUSE]

The tayyib land produces by the permission of its Lord. The corrupted land yields only poorly. So the believer should not only ask whether he heard the signs. He should ask whether his heart is being made good soil for them.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators read the parable in exactly this direction. Al-Muyassar puts it directly: clean ground, when rain falls on it, brings out good growth easily by Allah's permission and will - and likewise the believer, when Allah's ayat come down on him, benefits from them, and they bear fruit in him as a sound life. The salt-flat ground brings out only what is grudging and useless - and likewise the one who rejects gains nothing from Allah's signs.

[PAUSE]

But hold that alongside the grammar of the ayah itself, or the parable turns into something it does not say.

The ayah does not divide humanity into two kinds of dirt. Read it again: one side is described - *the good land* - and the other side is narrated - *the one that went bad*. A verb, with a history behind it. And the yield on the good side is not the soil's achievement; it happens *by permission of its Lord*. Between those two details, the ayah closes off both of the readings a person would most like to have: that the failing ground was doomed from the start, and that the fruitful ground did it by itself.

[PAUSE]

So the honest use of this parable is diagnostic and self-directed. It is a question you put to yourself about a condition you may be in, not a label you attach to somebody else. Ground turns salty slowly, by what keeps running through it. That is a description of a process, and processes can be interrupted.

[PAUSE]

And remember the word *illa*. Even the spoiled ground puts something out. The ayah refuses to describe any land as producing nothing at all.

That also explains the closing to a people who are grateful. Gratitude is one of the signs of healthy soil. The grateful heart receives, yields, and returns goodness instead of resisting what descended upon it.

### Theme 3: Mercy Sends Word Ahead of Itself

There is a whole spiritual discipline folded into the first line of ayah 57, and it is easy to walk past.

Mercy does not arrive without warning. Something goes in front of it - *bayna yaday rahmatih* - and that something is not the gift, only the announcement of the gift. The wind is not the water. Al-Muyassar's phrasing is that people begin to rejoice at Allah's mercy at the stage when all they have is moving air and gathering cloud.

[PAUSE]

The believer is being trained to read that stage. Not to wait for the rain before being glad, but to recognise the signs that run ahead of it.

[PAUSE]

This also reframes waiting. The dry season and the wind are not two different periods, one bad and one good. The wind arrives while the land is still dead. Relief is already in motion before anything has visibly changed on the ground - and the person standing in that field has to decide whether to read the sky.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs turn the natural world into revelation about both the future and the self.

The winds announce mercy. The clouds are driven. Dead land receives water and life returns. Thus the dead will be brought out. Then the soil itself becomes a mirror: good land yields by Allah's permission, corrupted land yields with difficulty. The signs are diversified for a people who are grateful.

[PAUSE]

Follow one root and the whole passage lines up. *Kh-r-j* - to come out - appears four times in two ayahs. Twice Allah is the one bringing out: the fruit, and then the dead. Twice something simply emerges: the plant from the good ground, and the grudging growth from the ruined ground. The same act, seen from the giving end and from the receiving end. That is the passage in a single root.

[PAUSE]

One way to sit with this passage is to ask: when mercy falls on my life in the form of Qur'an, reminders, openings, and warnings, am I more like land ready to yield or land that lets almost nothing come forth?

[PAUSE]

And two smaller questions to carry alongside it, both of them taken straight from the wording. If the wind comes before the rain, what have I been treating as an empty season that was actually an announcement? And if the ground goes salty by what runs through it rather than by how it was made, what has been running through mine?

[PAUSE]

The architecture of the passage lands here: **Allah revives dead earth so we may believe in resurrection, and He contrasts good and corrupted land so we may understand that the real question under every rainfall of mercy is what kind of heart receives it.**
