# The Gates of Heaven Will Not Be Opened for Them

## Surah Al-Araf (7:40-41)

[ayah:7:40] إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِنَا وَٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ عَنْهَا لَا تُفَتَّحُ لَهُمْ أَبْوَٰبُ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَلَا يَدْخُلُونَ ٱلْجَنَّةَ حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ ٱلْجَمَلُ فِى سَمِّ ٱلْخِيَاطِ ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ

[ayah:7:41] لَهُم مِّن جَهَنَّمَ مِهَادٌ وَمِن فَوْقِهِمْ غَوَاشٍ ۚ وَكَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

*Inna alladhina kadhdhabu bi-ayatina wa istakbaru 'anha la tufattahu lahum abwabu al-sama'i wa la yadkhuluna al-jannata hatta yalija al-jamalu fi sammi al-khiyat. Wa kadhalika najzi al-mujrimin.*

*Lahum min Jahannama mihadun wa min fawqihim ghawash. Wa kadhalika najzi al-zalimin.*

**Indeed, those who deny Our signs and are arrogant toward them - the gates of heaven will not be opened for them, nor will they enter Paradise until the camel passes through the eye of the needle. And thus do We recompense the criminals. For them from Hell is a bed, and above them are coverings. And thus do We recompense the wrongdoers.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

Some passages of the Qur'an warn by argument. Others warn by image. These ayahs do both, but their force comes especially from the imagery.

The people described here did two things together: they denied Allah's signs and they were arrogant toward them. So the punishment answers both. What they would not open themselves to is now answered by what will not open for them. What they stood above in arrogance is now answered by an enclosure from below and above.

[PAUSE]

The gates of heaven will not be opened for them. They will not enter the Garden until the camel passes through the eye of the needle. Then, as if impossibility were not enough, the next ayah makes punishment tactile: Hell as bedding below and coverings above. The whole image is one of closure.

[PAUSE]

Something else is worth noticing before we begin. This is the second time in a few lines that Al-A'raf has described these people in exactly these words. Four ayahs earlier the surah said the same thing - *those who denied Our signs and were arrogant toward them* - and then gave a plain verdict: they are the companions of the Fire, remaining in it. Here the surah says it again, word for word, and this time it does not give a verdict. It gives pictures. The description is repeated; the treatment is deepened.

That repeat is not a coincidence of vocabulary. In the entire Qur'an, the phrase *wa istakbaru 'anha* - "and they were arrogant toward them" - occurs in only two places, and both of them are in this passage of Al-A'raf. The surah is not scattering the idea. It is circling one specific posture and going at it twice.

[PAUSE]

The hook of this passage is this: **the soul that repeatedly seals itself against Allah's signs can arrive at a final recompense marked by divine closure - barred ascent, impossible entry, and punishment enclosing it from every side.**

[PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. Denial and Arrogance Are Paired as One Moral Posture

The ayah opens:

*Inna alladhina kadhdhabu bi-ayatina wa istakbaru 'anha.*

Those who denied Our signs and acted arrogantly toward them.

[PAUSE]

This pairing matters. Some people do not reject truth because they lack exposure, but because bowing before it feels like a diminishment of self. So *takdhib* and *istikbar* become allies. Falsehood and ego reinforce each other.

Al-Tabari draws out both halves carefully. On the first he says: they disbelieved Our proofs and Our evidences, so they did not accept them as true, and they did not follow Our messengers. Notice what he does with *ayat* there. He reads them not as vague wonders but as *proofs* - things that argue, that carry a case. To deny them is not to miss a beautiful sight. It is to be handed an argument and refuse the conclusion.

[PAUSE]

On the second half he is even more precise. *Wa istakbaru 'anha*, he says, means they were too proud to accept them as true, and they disdained to follow them and to submit to them. Three refusals stacked in one word: refusing to affirm, refusing to follow, refusing to submit.

That last one is the root of it. The Arabic verb here is not simply "they were arrogant." It is *istakbaru 'an* - they made themselves big *away from* something. The preposition matters. Arrogance in this ayah is not an attitude floating in the air. It has a direction. It is arrogance measured against a specific thing that was placed in front of them, and it moves away from it.

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet warning in that. A person can be perfectly humble in every other room of his life and still carry this precise form of pride in one room only - the room where a particular truth is waiting for him, the one he has decided not to walk into.

### 2. The Gates of Heaven Are Not Opened

Then:

*La tufattahu lahum abwabu al-sama'.*

The gates of heaven will not be opened for them.

[PAUSE]

The image reaches above. The heavens are not pictured as welcoming, but shut. The denial in the world is met by refusal of opening in the unseen.

The verb itself is worth slowing down on. Arabic can say "to open" with a light verb, *fataha*. Here the middle letter is doubled - *tufattahu* - which in Arabic usually carries the sense of intensity, or of doing something again and again, or of doing it to many things at once. It fits: these are *gates*, plural, and the ayah is not describing one door staying shut on one occasion. It is describing a general condition. Nothing gets flung open, ever, anywhere along that wall.

[PAUSE]

And what exactly is it that fails to get through? The commentators give two answers, and they hold both together rather than choosing.

Ibn Kathir reports the first from Mujahid, from Sa'id bin Jubayr, and from Ibn 'Abbas: what does not ascend is their good deeds and their supplications. The gates are not primarily an exit for them; they are the passage their worship would have taken upward, and that passage is closed.

Al-Tabari reports the second alongside it: the gates are not opened for their souls when those souls leave their bodies. So the closure covers both the living and the dying. In life, nothing of theirs rises. At death, nothing of them rises either.

[PAUSE]

Then al-Tabari gives the reason, and it is the key to the whole image. Nothing of theirs ascends, he says, because their deeds are foul - and only the good word and the righteous deed are raised. He is pointing at another ayah of the Qur'an to explain this one: *ilayhi yas'adu al-kalimu al-tayyibu wa al-'amalu al-salihu yarfa'uh* - "to Him ascends the good word, and the righteous deed raises it."

That reframes everything. The gates of heaven are not arbitrary. They are not a security checkpoint that refuses certain names. They are a filter with a very simple rule: what is good goes up. If nothing goes up, it is because nothing that was sent had the quality that rises.

[PAUSE]

The closure, then, is not a wall built against them. It is the shape of what they were sending.

It is worth pausing over how strange this image would have sounded to the first listeners. The Qur'an elsewhere does speak of the gates of the sky opening - and not always as mercy. In the story of Nuh, *We opened the gates of the sky with pouring water*, and that opening was the flood. And in another place Allah says that even if He opened a door from the sky to these people and they kept climbing up through it, they would still say their eyes have been dazzled.

So the closed gates here are not the Qur'an's only picture of a sealed heaven. They are the end point of a person who was already sealed. A door was never really the problem.

[PAUSE]

### 3. Paradise Is Named as Impossible Through a Deliberately Impossible Image

Then:

*Wa la yadkhuluna al-jannata hatta yalija al-jamalu fi sammi al-khiyat.*

They will not enter Paradise until the camel passes through the eye of the needle.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an could simply have said "never." Instead it makes you see impossibility. A massive creature and an impossibly narrow opening. The image is not there for ornament. It is there to make the sealed nature of the matter unforgettable.

Al-Muyassar glosses it with disarming flatness: they cannot enter Paradise unless the camel enters the hole of the needle - *and this is impossible*. The commentator does not elaborate the picture. He just says the word. That flatness is itself instructive: the ayah has already done all the work.

[PAUSE]

Look at the words the Qur'an chose to build it with, because each one is unusual.

*Yalija* - the verb - comes from a root that means to slip into something narrow, to thread through a tight passage. The root appears fourteen times in the Qur'an, and almost every other time it is Allah who is the one doing the threading: *He makes the night slip into the day, and makes the day slip into the night*. That is the root's home in the Qur'an. It belongs to the effortless divine act of passing one thing through another with no seam and no strain.

Here, for once, something is asked to thread through - and it is a camel, and the opening is a needle.

[PAUSE]

*Sammi* - the opening - is even rarer. The word appears here only. Its root shows up three other times in the whole Qur'an, and in all three it is *samum*: the scorching wind, the heat that gets in through the pores. The old lexicographers connect the two senses through the idea of a tiny passage - a *samm* is a fine aperture, and *samum* is what penetrates through fine apertures. Which means that the needle's eye in this ayah shares its root with a word the Qur'an uses for the punishment of the Fire.

*Khiyat* - the needle - comes from a root that appears in only one other place: the fasting ayah, where you eat and drink until the white thread becomes distinguishable to you from the black thread of dawn. That is a root about threads and the threading of them. The needle is simply the tool that puts a thread through a hole.

[PAUSE]

So the sentence is built entirely out of the vocabulary of narrow passage: a verb for slipping through, a noun for the aperture, a noun for the threading instrument. And then a camel is placed at the front of it.

[PAUSE]

One honest note on the word *al-jamal*. The reading in the mushaf, and the one this reflection follows, is *al-jamal* - the camel. But the commentators do record a second reading of the consonants, *al-jummal*, meaning a thick twisted rope of the kind used for ships. Both readings are transmitted; the sources gathered for this passage do not adjudicate between them, and there is genuine disagreement among the classical scholars here.

What is worth seeing is that the disagreement does not move the meaning at all. Whether you picture a camel or a ship's cable, you are picturing exactly the same thing: bulk that will never pass through a thread's opening. The ayah is so well constructed that even a difference in reading cannot damage it.

[PAUSE]

The construction *hatta* - "until" - deserves one more look. Arabic uses it to name a condition that ends a state. *Wait until he comes.* But a condition can be named precisely in order to show it will never arrive. This is the Qur'an setting a term that the listener himself can calculate will never be met. It is a "never" that arrives by way of the listener's own arithmetic, which is why it lands harder than the word "never" ever could.

[PAUSE]

And there is one last thing about the phrase *yadkhuluna al-jannata*, "they enter the Garden." It occurs four times in the Qur'an. In three of them it is a promise: *whoever does righteous deeds, male or female, being a believer - those will enter the Garden and will not be wronged in the slightest*; *except whoever repents and believes and does righteous work - those will enter the Garden and will not be wronged at all*; *whoever does righteousness, male or female, being a believer - those will enter the Garden, provided for in it without measure*.

Three promises. And here, once, the same phrase in the negative.

Read those together and you notice what the three promises have in common. Every one of them is built on a door being open now: work righteousness, believe, repent. The one negation in this set describes people who have finished refusing all three.

### 4. The First Refrain

Then the ayah closes with a formula:

*Wa kadhalika najzi al-mujrimin.*

And thus do We recompense the criminals.

[PAUSE]

*Mujrim* comes from a root whose old sense is to cut or to sever - and from there, to earn something by cutting it off, and from there, to commit a crime. The one who is called *mujrim* in the Qur'an is not simply a lawbreaker in a legal sense. He is someone who has cut himself away.

Which is worth sitting with in an ayah about closed gates. Before anything was closed against him, he did the cutting.

[PAUSE]

### 5. The Punishment Is Enclosure

Then the next ayah:

*Lahum min Jahannama mihad.*

For them from Hell is a bed.

[PAUSE]

*Mihad* is what is spread beneath one for lying down. A bed is usually associated with rest and ease. Here the language is turned into horror.

Al-Tabari's gloss is almost domestic in its detail. A *mihad*, he explains, is what people spread out for themselves to sit and to lie down on - like a *firash*, a mattress that gets laid out, like a *bisat*, a rug that gets unrolled. He is not reaching for a fearsome word. He is describing furniture.

[PAUSE]

Notice also the small preposition the ayah puts in front of it: *min Jahannam* - "from Hell." The bedding is not simply located in Hell. It is made out of Hell. The Fire is not the room the bed sits in. The Fire is the fabric.

And the word order carries something too. In Arabic the sentence runs: *for them - from Hell - a bedding.* The two prepositions come first, and the noun that names the object arrives last, undefined, with no article: *a* bedding. Not the famous bed you have heard about. Something unnamed and unmeasured.

[PAUSE]

There is a second thing hidden in this word, and it is easy to miss.

*Mihad* is used of Hell in six places in the Qur'an. In five of those six it comes attached to a cry of condemnation - *and wretched is the resting place*, *and how wretched a resting place*. The formula is so consistent that a listener who knew the Qur'an would expect it here automatically.

Here it does not come. Our ayah names the bedding and then, instead of exclaiming over it, simply adds what is above. This is the one place in the Qur'an where Hell's *mihad* is laid down without the accompanying cry.

[PAUSE]

The effect is a kind of terrible calm. The other passages step back and react. This one does not step back. It stays in the room and keeps describing.

And there is one more layer under the word. The same root gives *mahd*, the cradle - the word the Qur'an uses three times for the infant 'Isa speaking from it. It also gives the earth: *Did We not make the earth a mihad?* and *the One who made the earth for you a mahd*. The root's natural home in the Qur'an is mercy: the cradle a baby is laid in, the earth spread out under our feet so that we can live on it.

Hell in this ayah is given that same word. It is described as a cradle.

### 6. And Above Them, Coverings

Then:

*Wa min fawqihim ghawash.*

And above them are coverings.

The punishment encloses from below and above. There is no breathable margin.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari fills in the picture with the same domestic vocabulary he used a line earlier. *Ghawash*, he says, is the plural of *ghashiya* - what covers a thing over from above. And then he sums up the whole ayah in one sentence: for them from Hell is a bedding beneath them, spread out like mattresses, and from above them, also from it, blankets - and they are between the two.

*And they are between the two.* That is the sentence. Not a fire around them, which the imagination can hold at some distance. A mattress and a blanket, and a person in between.

[PAUSE]

The word *ghawash* is itself a small marvel. Its root appears twenty-nine times across the Qur'an - it is a well-used root - and yet this plural form, *ghawash*, occurs here and nowhere else. The Qur'an reached for a shape of the word it never uses again.

It is also indefinite, and the ending carries that unfinished sound - *ghawashin*, coverings, some number of them, unspecified. The ayah refuses to count them or name them. Whatever is layered on top of these people is not given a limit.

[PAUSE]

And now the connection that makes this ayah a mirror rather than merely a scene.

The same root gives *ghishawa* - a covering laid over a person's *eyes*. The Qur'an uses it twice, and both uses describe exactly the state these ayahs opened with. Near the very start of the Qur'an: *Allah has sealed their hearts and their hearing, and over their sight is a covering.* And later, of the man who takes his own desire as his god: *Allah has sealed his hearing and his heart and placed over his sight a covering.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that pairing. In this world the covering was over the eyes - a thin film that let a person go on living normally while simply not seeing. In the next world the covering is over the whole person, from above, with a bed of Fire beneath.

The same root. The same act. Only the scale has changed.

Which is the deepest thing this passage does. It does not invent a punishment and attach it to a crime. It takes the crime, and it enlarges it until it becomes the punishment. A film over the eyes becomes a blanket over the body. A refusal to open becomes gates that do not open. A self made big becomes a self with no room to move.

### 7. The Second Refrain

Then the formula returns:

*Wa kadhalika najzi al-zalimin.*

And thus do We recompense the wrongdoers.

[PAUSE]

This is the same refrain as the previous ayah, with one word swapped: *mujrimin* becomes *zalimin*.

That doubling is unusual. The phrase *wa kadhalika najzi* - "and thus do We recompense" - appears in a number of places in the Qur'an. But this is the only place where it lands twice in a row, at the end of one ayah and again at the end of the very next.

[PAUSE]

Two labels, then, for the same people. *Mujrim*, from the root of cutting - the one who severed himself. *Zalim*, from a root that means to put a thing somewhere it does not belong, and which carries the sense of darkness. To wrong is to misplace: to give a thing less than its right, or to give yourself more than yours.

Read that back onto the opening of the passage. Someone who is handed proofs from his Lord and treats them as less than they are has misplaced them. Someone who stands above those proofs has misplaced himself. Both of the words in this refrain are diagnoses of position - one about cutting away, one about being in the wrong place.

And they are the last words spoken over these two ayahs, one after the other, like a verdict read twice so that no one can claim they misheard it.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Repeated Inner Closure Can End in Final Outer Closure

These ayahs are deeply moral in how the punishment mirrors the crime. The arrogant denier keeps the truth outside himself. He refuses opening, refuses softening, refuses to bend. Then the recompense arrives in images of not being opened for and not entering into.

[PAUSE]

That should make us fear not only explicit disbelief but every habit of inward sealing, every repetition of hearing truth without allowing it to pierce. The great closures of akhirah are often preceded by many smaller closures of heart.

But there is something in al-Tabari's reading of these gates that turns the fear in a more useful direction.

He does not say the gates are locked against these people. He says nothing of theirs ascends, because what they were sending up was not of the kind that rises - and only the good word and the righteous deed are raised. Which means the ayah is not only a threat. It is also, read from the other side, a description of how the traffic upward actually works.

[PAUSE]

Every day a person is sending something up or sending nothing. A word said in truth. A prayer offered without an audience. A deed done because it is right and not because it will be seen. These are the things the Qur'an says rise.

And if that is the mechanism, then the horror of this ayah is not that a gate slammed. It is that a lifetime of upward traffic came to nothing, because nothing that was sent had the weight to lift.

[PAUSE]

That reframing changes what we should do with the ayah. It is not asking us to check whether we are on the wrong side of a locked door. It is asking a simpler question, and one we can actually answer today: what did I send up this week that was of a kind that rises?

### Theme 2: Hell Is Pictured Here as the Opposite of Shelter

The bedding and coverings image is especially arresting because these are usually images of domestic comfort. A bed beneath and coverings above are what make a human being feel protected. But in this passage every symbol of shelter is inverted.

[PAUSE]

That inversion matters spiritually. The people here sought safety in denial and self-exaltation. What they receive is a counterfeit shelter that is really suffocation. The very shape of refuge remains, but every mercy has been removed from it.

And the vocabulary confirms it precisely. The word for the bedding, *mihad*, is the word the Qur'an uses for the earth spread out beneath us as a gift - and it shares its root with *mahd*, the cradle, the safest place a human being ever lies down in. The word for the coverings shares its root with the film that lay over these people's eyes in this world.

Every element of the picture is a borrowed mercy with the mercy taken out.

[PAUSE]

There is a warning in that which reaches beyond disbelief. A person can build a whole life that has the correct shape of a good life - the routines, the reputation, the comfort, the sense of being covered and settled - while the thing that made those forms good was quietly removed. The shape survives longer than the substance. That is exactly what makes it dangerous. You can lie down in it and feel protected right up until the moment you cannot breathe.

### Theme 3: Al-A'raf Is a Surah About Thresholds

Read these two ayahs inside their surah and something opens up.

Al-A'raf is named after a barrier. A few ayahs after our passage the surah says: *and between them is a partition, and on the heights are men who recognise everyone by their marks* - people standing at a threshold, calling out to the people of the Garden, *who have not entered it though they long to*.

[PAUSE]

Now put the sequence together. In 7:36 the surah names those who denied the signs and were arrogant toward them, and gives the verdict. In 7:40 it names them again in the same words, and this time shows us gates that do not open and a Garden that will not be entered. Then at 7:46 there is a wall, with people on top of it who have not entered and still hope. And a few lines later, at 7:49, the same people are told: *enter the Garden. No fear upon you, nor shall you grieve.*

The verb "to enter" runs through the whole stretch. Refused at 7:40. Longed for at 7:46. Granted at 7:49.

[PAUSE]

This is a surah that keeps putting people at doorways and asking who gets through. And that is worth noticing precisely because it stops our passage from being read as a standalone thundercloud. It is one position on a map - the far end of a spectrum the surah itself lays out, with hoping and entering plotted right beside it.

### Theme 4: What This Passage Says, and What It Does Not

There is an adab worth naming here, because passages like this one are easy to misuse.

These ayahs describe a *completed* condition. The verbs are done: they denied, they were arrogant. The surah is speaking about people whose case is closed, from the vantage point of the Day when all cases are closed. It is not handing anyone a test to apply to their neighbour, or to a stranger on the internet, or to a family member who is currently struggling to believe. Nobody among the living has a closed case.

[PAUSE]

And the Qur'an itself guards this. Of the four places where the phrase "they enter the Garden" appears, one begins: *except whoever repents and believes and does righteous work.* The exception clause is right there in the Qur'an's own handling of this vocabulary. Repentance is precisely the thing that reopens what has closed.

Look also at what the surah does immediately after our passage. It does not linger. The very next ayah says: *and those who believed and did righteous deeds - We do not burden any soul beyond its capacity - those are the companions of the Garden, abiding in it forever.*

[PAUSE]

That is a remarkable placement. Directly after the most airless image in the surah comes a statement about how *light* the requirement actually is. No soul is burdened beyond what it can carry. The passage that shows a camel and a needle turns around, in the space of one line, to say that the entry it just called impossible was never a matter of impossible demands.

And the ayah after that gives us the words of the people who made it: *praise be to Allah, who guided us to this. We would never have been guided had Allah not guided us.*

[PAUSE]

Which is where a reader should be left. Not congratulating himself on being outside the description, but noticing that the ones who got in did not congratulate themselves either.

## Closing Synthesis

These ayahs compress a whole theology of consequences into a few unforgettable images.

Denied signs and arrogant distance lead to closed gates, impossible entry, and a punishment that spreads beneath and above. The surah is not merely telling us that there is punishment. It is showing us what spiritual closure looks like when it hardens into destiny.

[PAUSE]

And it shows it through a vocabulary that is, at every point, borrowed from mercy. A gate is what opens. A needle is what threads. A bed is what rests you. A covering is what warms you. The Qur'an takes four words of comfort and turns each of them inside out - not by inventing new horrors, but by removing the mercy from things we already know.
