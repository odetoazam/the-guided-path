# The Strength That Was Never Ours

### Surah Adh-Dhariyat, Ayah 45

> [ayah:51:45] فَمَا ٱسْتَطَـٰعُوا۟ مِن قِيَامٍ وَمَا كَانُوا۟ مُنتَصِرِينَ

**Famā-staṭāʿū min qiyāmin wamā kānū muntaṣirīn**

*"And they were not able to rise, nor could they defend themselves."*

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Stillness After

Let me ask you to do something strange before we begin.

The next time you stand up — from a chair, from your bed, from your prayer — pay attention to the thing you have never once paid attention to in your life. The decision to stand, and the standing, arrive at the same moment. You think *up*, and the body is already obeying. There is no gap. There is no negotiation. The legs do not ask for terms. The will and the muscle move as one, so seamlessly that you have lived your entire life never noticing that they are two separate things at all.

We are about to meet a people who discovered, in a single instant, that they were two separate things. And by the time they discovered it, it was already too late.

To understand this verse, you have to stand where they stood.

Picture a civilization carved into stone. The people of Thamud were not a wandering tribe. They were masons of mountains. The Qur'an tells us elsewhere they *"carved homes out of the mountains, feeling secure"* (15:82). Imagine the labor in that — the confidence in that. To look at a sheer cliff of solid rock and decide it would become your living room, your hall, your fortress. These were people whose entire relationship with the world was: *we shape it. It yields to us. We are strong enough.*

A prophet came to them — Salih, peace be upon him — with a single, gentle sign: a she-camel, and a command to share the water and leave her unharmed. And the masons of mountains, the men who bent stone to their will, looked at this sign of their Lord and **hamstrung the camel** in arrogant defiance.

And then this surah, in its breathless final third, tells us what happened. *"They were insolent toward the command of their Lord, so the thunderbolt seized them while they were looking on"* (51:44).

**While they were looking on.** Hold onto that. They *saw* it coming. The men of strength watched the moment of their undoing arrive — and then comes our verse, which describes not the blow itself, but the silence after it.

*Famā-staṭāʿū min qiyāmin wamā kānū muntaṣirīn.*

Here is the hook. The Qur'an could have told us they were destroyed in a thousand dramatic ways. Instead, Allah chooses to describe their end through two things they *could not do.* Two failures. And the first failure is so small, so humble, so almost-embarrassing, that we miss its terror entirely.

They could not stand up.

That's it. The mountain-carvers could not perform the single act a toddler masters before its first birthday. And in *why* they couldn't — in the exact word Allah chose for "able" — there is a truth about you, about your strength, about every capacity you think you own, that I want us to spend the next hour uncovering.

Let's begin.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word for "Able" — and What Ability Really Is

Before we open the verse, let me teach you something about a family of Arabic words, because this verse turns on it.

In Arabic, you can take a simple verb and reshape it into different "forms" — patterns that bend the meaning. One of these patterns is called **Form X**, and it has a flavor all its own. It often means *to seek something, to ask for it, to try to obtain it.* From the root meaning "forgiveness," Form X gives you *istaghfara* — to *seek* forgiveness. From the root meaning "to be ready," it gives you *istaʿadda* — to *make oneself* ready.

Now hold that idea — *Form X is the form of seeking* — and look at the verb in our verse.

> ٱسْتَطَـٰعُوا۟ — **istaṭāʿū** — "they were able"

This is Form X. The morphology is confirmed: a Form X verb, perfect tense, third person, masculine, plural. We translate it flatly as "they were able." But sit with me, because the root underneath it changes everything.

The root is **ط و ع (Ṭ-w-ʿ)**. And do you know what that root means, all on its own, before any forms are added to it?

It means **willing obedience. Pliancy. To yield without being forced.**

The lexicons are clear on this. *Ṭawʿ* is the opposite of *karh* — the opposite of compulsion. When something is *ṭāʾiʿ*, it bends to you willingly. A horse that comes when called. A door that opens at a touch. Reality that *cooperates.*

[PAUSE]

Now put the pieces together. *Istaṭāʿa* — "to be able" — is Form X (the seeking form) built on the root of *willing yielding.*

So what does "ability" literally mean in Arabic?

**To be able is to seek, and find, that the world yields to you.**

Think about what that quietly claims. Your power to lift a cup is not a property *inside* you. It is a *relationship* — a relationship in which the cup, the muscle, the nerve, the joint, the air, the floor beneath you, all *consent* to cooperate with your will. Every act of "being able" is a moment in which a thousand things you do not control choose, in that instant, to obey.

We think strength is something we *possess.* The Arabic says strength is something the world *grants* us, moment by moment, by yielding.

[PAUSE — let this land]

Now read the verse with new eyes:

> فَمَا ٱسْتَطَـٰعُوا۟ — *Famā-staṭāʿū* — "And they were not able..."

The masons of mountains — the men whose whole identity was that *stone itself yielded to them* — reached for the world, and for the first time in their lives, **nothing yielded.** Not the rock. Not the air. Not their own legs. The relationship of "ability" — the silent, lifelong consent of creation to cooperate with their will — was simply switched off.

They had spent their lives believing the yielding came *from them.* In one instant they learned it had always been *given* to them. And what is given can be withdrawn.

This is what the word reveals: **ability is borrowed pliancy.** You do not own your strength. You are lent the cooperation of a world that answers to its Maker, not to you.

### Element Two: "Standing" — the Smallest Verb in the World, and Its Double Meaning

Look at what they were unable to do:

> مِن قِيَامٍۢ — **min qiyām** — "to rise / any standing"

The word *qiyām* is from the root **ق و م (Q-w-m)** — to stand, to rise, to get up. It is among the most ordinary acts a body performs. And notice the small word in front of it: **مِن (min).**

In Arabic grammar, when a negation is followed by *min* attached to an indefinite noun like this, the *min* is what grammarians call *zāʾidah* — "extra," but not pointless. It is there for **emphasis**, and it makes the negation **total.** It is the difference between "they could not stand" and **"they could not manage so much as a single act of rising — not the slightest, not at all."**

Allah does not say *they did not stand.* He says *they could not find within themselves even the faintest trace of standing.* The door to that simple act was sealed completely.

But there is a second layer here, and the classical scholars caught it.

Al-Qurṭubī notes that *qiyām* here carries two meanings at once. The first is the obvious one: **nuhūḍ** — rising up, getting to your feet. Al-Baghawī agrees and cites Qatādah: *"they did not get up from that collapse."*

But al-Qurṭubī gives a second meaning, and it is striking. He says *qiyām* can mean **to stand up to something — to withstand it, to bear it, to repel it.** He gives the Arabic idiom: *lā aqūmu li-hādhā al-amr* — "I cannot *stand* to this matter," meaning *I cannot withstand it, I haven't the strength to meet it.* The way we say in English, "I couldn't stand against it."

So the single word *qiyām* holds both a body that cannot rise from the ground **and** a will that cannot stand up to what has come for it. The physical collapse and the total inability to resist are named in one breath.

[PAUSE]

And then al-Qurṭubī preserves something almost unbearable from Ibn ʿAbbās. He says of these people: *"dhahabat ajsāmuhum wa baqiyat arwāḥuhum fī-l-ʿadhāb"* — **"their bodies were gone, and their souls remained in the punishment."**

Sit with that for a moment. The body — the proud, mountain-carving body — undone, and yet the *self* still present, still aware, still inside the consequence. The very thing they trusted, their physical might, is the first thing taken; and what is left is the part of them that cannot be carved from stone, exposed and unable to rise.

This is the genius of choosing such a *small* word. Allah does not describe a spectacular death. He describes the failure of the most ordinary motion in the human repertoire. Because the terror is not in the size of what they lost. The terror is in this: **the thing you never thought to be grateful for is the thing that goes first.**

When was the last time you thanked Allah that you *could stand up?*

### Element Three: The Second Failure — When Self-Rescue and the Rescuer Both Vanish

The verse does not stop at standing. It adds a second clause, and the addition is doing precise work.

> وَمَا كَانُوا۟ مُنتَصِرِينَ — *wamā kānū muntaṣirīn* — "nor could they defend themselves"

Let me first teach you this word, because it is richer than the translation lets on.

The root is **ن ص ر (N-ṣ-r)**, and it means **to help, to aid, to grant victory.** It is one of the great roots of the Qur'an — *naṣr*, divine victory; *anṣār*, the helpers.

But the word here is **muntaṣir** — a Form VIII active participle (confirmed in the morphology). And Form VIII bends the meaning *inward, toward the self.* Where *naṣara* means "to help someone," the Form VIII *intaṣara* means **to help *oneself*, to defend *oneself*, to take one's own victory, to avenge oneself.** It is the reflexive, self-directed version of being helped.

So *muntaṣirīn* means **"ones who could secure their own rescue."**

Now watch how the two clauses of this verse map a complete collapse, with no exit left.

The first clause — *they could not rise* — is about their **own bodies, their own capacity.** Self-power, gone.

The second clause — *they could not defend themselves* — is about **rescue, victory, deliverance** from any direction. And the scholars open this out beautifully. Al-Muyassar says it means *muntaṣirīn li-anfusihim* — they could not secure victory *for themselves.* And al-Qurṭubī adds the other half: *mumtaniʿīn min al-ʿadhāb... mā kāna lahum nāṣir* — they could not **fend off** the punishment, **and there was no helper for them at all.**

Do you see the architecture? The verse closes off rescue from *both* possible directions:

- **From within** — they could not rise, could not muster their own strength. *Self-rescue: sealed.*
- **From without** — they had no defender, no ally, no power that could be marshaled on their behalf. *Outside-rescue: sealed.*

[PAUSE]

And there is one more touch. Allah does not say *wamā intaṣarū* — "and they did not defend themselves." He says *wamā **kānū** muntaṣirīn* — "and they **were** not ones-who-defend-themselves." The verb *kānū* (from the root **ك و ن**, the root of *being*) frames it as a **state, a condition of their very being.** It was not that they tried and failed in the moment. It is that, when the truth was revealed, **defending themselves was never something within their nature at all.** The capacity they assumed was part of who they were turned out never to have been theirs.

Two negations, *famā... wamā...* — bracketing the whole verse like a closing door. No way up. No way out. No one coming.

This is what the three words reveal together: when Allah withdraws His leave, the human being is left with **neither the floor beneath his own feet nor a single hand reaching down from above.**

### Bridge: From the Words to the World

Look back at the path the words have walked us down.

*Istaṭāʿū* taught us that ability is borrowed — the world *yields* to us only by leave.

*Qiyām* taught us that the first thing withdrawn is the most ordinary thing — the standing we never thank Him for — and that it means both the body that cannot rise and the will that cannot withstand.

*Muntaṣirīn* taught us that when leave is withdrawn, *both* doors of rescue close at once: no power from within, no helper from without.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about an ancient people and the words that buried them — but about reality itself. About *your* reality, this morning, when you stood up without thinking.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Illusion You Live Inside

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a mental model of your life, and at the center of it is a quiet, unexamined assumption: *I can.* You assume that tomorrow you will wake and your eyes will open. You assume that when you decide to walk to the kitchen, the walking will happen. You assume that the income will come, the body will hold, the mind will produce the next thought. You don't *believe* these things — believing implies you considered the alternative. You simply build on them, the way you build on the floor being there.

Psychologists have a name for a piece of this: the **illusion of control.** We systematically overestimate how much of our outcomes we author. We feel we *make* our health, *earn* our security, *generate* our own capability. The sense of agency is so total that we cannot feel the seams.

Now here is what this verse does. It does not argue with that illusion. It does something far more devastating: **it shows you the illusion from the other side of the seam.**

The people of Thamud lived inside the maximum version of *I can.* They could bend mountains. If anyone had earned the right to feel self-authored, it was them. And the verse catches them at the precise instant the seam tears — the instant when the will says *up* and the body, for the first time in a lifetime, **does not answer.**

*Famā-staṭāʿū min qiyām.* The world stopped yielding.

[PAUSE]

I want you to feel why this is the most merciful possible warning, and not a cruel one. Because there is a way to learn this lesson gently, and a way to learn it the way Thamud did.

Every one of us *will* meet the moment when the body stops yielding. We call it many things — illness, age, the sudden diagnosis, the limb that won't move after the stroke, and finally, death itself. There will be a morning when you think *up* and nothing rises. This is not a threat. It is a certainty. It is written into the loan agreement of being a created thing.

The question this verse puts to you is not *whether* you will meet that moment. It is: **will you meet it as a stranger or as someone who saw it coming?**

Thamud met it as strangers. *They were looking on* (51:44) — they watched it arrive — and still it found them unprepared, because they had spent their lives believing the yielding came from them. To live thinking your strength is *yours* is to guarantee that its withdrawal will feel like a betrayal, a horror, an injustice.

But there is another way to live. You can take the standing you did this morning and recognize it for what it is: not your possession, but His daily gift. *Ṭawʿ* — the willing cooperation of a thousand things that answer to Him. You can let every successful, ordinary motion of your body become a small, silent *Alhamdulillah* — not because you are sick, but precisely because you are well, and you know Who is lending you the wellness.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Build into your day one deliberate noticing. When you rise for Fajr and your legs carry you to the prayer — pause, even for a breath, and register that He let the world yield to you one more time. The person who thanks Allah for standing while he *can* stand will not be ambushed on the day he cannot. He has already made his peace with the truth of the loan. The standing was never his. So its return to the Lender is not a robbery — it is the natural close of a gift gratefully held.

### Theme Two: The Two Empty Hands — and the One That Is Never Empty

Now let me take you to the second movement of the verse, and to the deepest place it points.

When something goes truly wrong in a human life — not an inconvenience, but a crisis that reaches the floor of who you are — watch what the mind does. It does this in a fixed order, and you have done it yourself.

First, you reach **inward.** *I can handle this. I just need to be stronger, smarter, more disciplined. I'll figure it out.* This is the muscle of self-reliance, and our entire culture trains it like a religion: resilience, grit, "you've got this," self-made, self-sufficient.

And when the inward reach fails — when you discover the problem is bigger than your strength — you reach **outward.** *Who can help me? Who has the power, the money, the connection, the influence to pull me out of this?* You scan the horizon for a rescuer.

The verse names exactly these two reaches. *Famā-staṭāʿū min qiyām* — the inward reach: their own power. *Wamā kānū muntaṣirīn* — the outward reach: a rescue, a defender, a helper from anywhere.

And the verse seals both. **Both hands come back empty.**

[PAUSE]

Now, why would Allah show us a scene of *total* abandonment — no self, no savior? Is this just to frighten us about a long-dead nation?

No. The Qur'an never shows you a darkness without lighting a single, specific lamp behind it. And to see the lamp, you have to read this verse in its home.

This entire surah, Adh-Dhariyat, is building toward one sentence. Just thirteen verses after our ayah, the surah delivers its crown:

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلرَّزَّاقُ ذُو ٱلْقُوَّةِ ٱلْمَتِينُ
> *"Indeed, it is Allah who is the Provider, the Possessor of Power, the Firm."* (51:58)

**Dhū al-Quwwah al-Matīn** — the Possessor of Power, the Utterly Firm. *Al-Quwwah* — power, strength, capacity. The very thing Thamud thought they had. *Al-Matīn* — firm, solid, immovable, the way they thought their mountain-homes were solid.

Do you see the conversation the surah is having with itself? Thamud reached inward for *quwwah* and found their own was hollow. They reached outward for a *nāṣir* and found the horizon empty. **They had been reaching for the wrong attributes in the wrong place the entire time.** Real *quwwah* was never in them, and never in any helper they could buy. It belonged — wholly, exclusively — to *Dhū al-Quwwah al-Matīn.*

This is the lamp. The verse empties both human hands not to leave you in despair, but to **break the addiction to the two false reaches** — so that, when you finally turn, you turn to the only Hand that is never empty.

[PAUSE]

Here is the part that should reorder how you carry your troubles.

Most of us treat Allah as the **third** reach. We try ourselves first. We try people second. And then, exhausted, when the self has failed and the helpers have shrugged, *then* we raise our hands and say *yā Rabb.* He becomes the last resort, the emergency line, the one we call when the real options run out.

Thamud shows you the end of that road. On the day the *staṭāʿa* fails and the *nāṣir* vanishes, you discover that the One you treated as the backup was always the *source* — the One who was lending the strength to your "self-reliance" and the One who was sending every helper who ever helped you. The self and the savior were never two independent powers. They were both *His giving,* wearing two disguises.

The believer's revolution is simply to reverse the order. To make Him the **first** reach instead of the last. To say, before I lift the cup, before I rise for the prayer, before I face the hard conversation: *bismillah* — in the name of the One whose leave makes all of this possible. To work the means with full effort, yes — Salih's people were not punished for being weak, they were punished for being arrogant — but to lean the whole weight of the heart on the Lender, not on the loan.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Notice your *first* instinct in the next difficulty that finds you. When the bad news comes, where does your heart run *first* — to your own cleverness, to a person, or to Him? You cannot fake this; the first reach is the honest one. So train it. Before you problem-solve, before you call anyone, take three seconds and turn to Him *first* — even a sentence: *Yā Allah, this is from You and I turn to You with it.* Not as the last resort when the hands are empty, but as the first move while the hands still seem full. Because the one truth Thamud learned too late is the one you can learn today, on your feet, in good health: **there was only ever one Power in the room.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this single short verse are really one truth seen from two angles.

The **linguistic journey** showed us that *ability itself* — *istaṭāʿa* — is the world *yielding* to us by Allah's leave, and that the first thing withdrawn is the humblest thing, the standing we never thanked Him for. The **thematic journey** showed us that this is the illusion we all live inside: *I can*, built on a borrowed floor.

The linguistic journey showed us that *muntaṣir* — self-rescue — was sealed alongside any outside helper. The thematic journey showed us our two false reaches, inward and outward, and named the only Hand that is never empty: *Dhū al-Quwwah al-Matīn.*

And here is the architecture of the whole thing. The verse is built as **two closed doors** — *famā... wamā...* — *not this, and not that.* The body cannot rise; the self cannot be saved. Allah walls off every exit a human might imagine. And He does it precisely so that, a few verses later, He can fling open the only true door: *Indeed it is Allah who is the Provider, the Possessor of Power, the Firm.* The emptying of the human hand is the surah's way of pointing at the Hand that fills.

Thamud carved their security into mountains and could not, in the end, perform the act of a child rising to its feet. We were shown their silence so that we would never mistake the loan for our own wealth.

### Questions to Carry

1. When did I last thank Allah specifically for an ability I have never once noticed — that I can stand, walk, see, breathe without effort? What would change in me if I noticed it daily?
2. In my last real crisis, where did my heart run *first* — to my own strength, to a person, or to Allah? What does the honest answer tell me about where my trust actually lives?
3. I work hard and rely on means — but do I lean my heart's weight on the means, or on the One who makes the means yield? Can I tell the difference in myself?
4. If I knew that the floor of "I can" was on loan and could be recalled at any moment, what about how I'm living would I change *this week*?
5. Am I treating Allah as my first reach, or my last resort?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The strength you call your own is the world yielding to you by His leave — and the day He withdraws that leave, you cannot rise on your own and no rescuer comes, because all power was always His alone.

---

### The Closing Invitation

You will stand up many times today. I invite you to let just one of those standings be different — to let one ordinary rising become a moment of recognition: *He let the world yield to me again.* You don't have to wait for weakness to learn what Thamud learned. You can learn it on your feet, in your strength, with gratitude instead of grief. That is the whole mercy of being warned by someone else's silence: it offers you the lesson without the loss.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, Possessor of Power, the Firm — every time I rise, it is by Your leave, and I had forgotten to thank You. Make You my first turning and not my last resort. And on the day my strength is taken and no helper remains, do not leave me without the only Help that was ever real — Your help. Āmīn.*
