# The Ones Who Gave the Night and Still Asked Forgiveness

## Adh-Dhariyat 51:17-18

> كَانُوا۟ قَلِيلًۭا مِّنَ ٱلَّيْلِ مَا يَهْجَعُونَ ﴿١٧﴾ وَبِٱلْأَسْحَارِ هُمْ يَسْتَغْفِرُونَ ﴿١٨﴾

> *Kānū qalīlan mina-l-layli mā yahjaʿūn. Wa bi-l-asḥāri hum yastaghfirūn.*

> "They used to sleep but little of the night, and in the hours before dawn they would seek forgiveness."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question Nobody Asked Out Loud

Let me put you somewhere first.

You are sitting among the first listeners of this surah. The opening has just swept over you like a storm — Allah swearing by the scattering winds, by the clouds heavy with rain, by the ships running on the sea, by the angels distributing the command. *Wadh-dhāriyāti dharwā.* Oath after oath, building pressure, and then the verdict lands: **what you are promised is true, and the reckoning is going to happen.** No escaping it.

And then, almost suddenly, the storm clears. The surah opens a window onto a garden.

*Inna-l-muttaqīna fī jannātin wa ʿuyūn* — "The people of God-consciousness are in Gardens and springs." *Ākhidhīna mā ātāhum rabbuhum* — "receiving what their Lord has given them." We see them already arrived. Already at rest. Already drinking from springs that don't run dry.

[PAUSE]

And here is where a question forms in the heart of every honest listener. A question we are usually too polite to ask out loud.

*What did they do?*

Not in a cynical way. In a hungry way. Because if there are people who end up like **that** — settled, secure, receiving — then I want to know what kind of life produces that ending. Show me the road. Show me their days. Show me what they were like when they were still here, still struggling, still us.

And Allah answers. But notice **where** He starts His answer.

He does not start with their bravery in battle. He does not start with the size of their charity — that comes one verse later. He does not start with anything a crowd could see.

He starts with their **nights.**

He starts with the one part of a human life that has no audience. The hours when the performance ends, when the reputation goes to sleep, when there is no one left to impress and nothing left to prove. He reaches past everything public and puts His finger on the most private room in the house of a person's life — and He says: *here. Look here. This is what they were made of.*

[PAUSE]

There is something almost stunning hidden in just nine Arabic words. Because by the time these two verses finish, they will have shown you two things that should not fit together — and the fact that they fit together at all is the whole secret.

These people did **the most.** And they claimed the **least.**

Stay with me. Let's open this slowly.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Word That Refuses to Sit Still — *Qalīlan*

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds a sentence, because it will let you see what's actually happening here.

In English, word order is rigid. "The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" mean completely different things — position **is** meaning. Arabic is different. Arabic can move words around the sentence and hold them in place with grammar instead of position. This gives a single sentence the ability to **shimmer** — to hold more than one valid reading at once, not by accident, but by design. The classical scholars didn't treat this as a flaw to be cleaned up. They treated it as **richness** — when a verse can carry two meanings without contradiction, you don't pick one and throw the other away. You receive both.

Hold that thought.

Now look at the first verse:

> [ayah:51:17] كَانُوا۟ قَلِيلًا مِّنَ ٱلَّيْلِ مَا يَهْجَعُونَ

The pivot word is **قَلِيلًۭا — *qalīlan*** — "little," "a small amount." And around it sits a tiny word, **مَا — *mā*** — which the grammarians tell us here is *zā'ida*: an "extra" particle, one that adds no separate meaning of its own but changes the music and the weight of the line. *Al-Jalālayn* says it plainly: *mā* here is added, and the sleeping is what the verb *kānū* is reporting. *Al-Baghawī* says the same — *mā* is *ṣila*, connective filler.

Now — what does the sentence actually say? Let's sit with this, because the classical commentators sat with it too, and they did not all land in the same place.

**The first reading**, and the most direct: *they used to sleep only a little of the night.* The night was long, and they gave most of it to standing before Allah, and only a small sliver of it to sleep. *Al-Muyassar* draws the picture: they slept little, they prayed to their Lord in devotion, and at the night's end they sought forgiveness. This is the portrait of *qiyām al-layl* — the night-standing — as a settled way of life.

**The second reading** comes from Ibn ʿAbbās, reported through Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, and *al-Baghawī* preserves it: here *qalīlan* — "few" — attaches not to the **hours** but to the **nights** themselves. The meaning becomes: *few were the nights that passed over them in which they did not pray* — rarely did a whole night go by without them rising for some portion of it, at its beginning or its middle. So "little" is not "a little sleep each night" but "a few rare nights of full sleep across a whole life."

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference? The first reading zooms into a single night and shows you a person who sleeps briefly. The second reading pulls back across an entire life and shows you that the nights they slept straight through were the exceptions — the anomalies — the ones you could count.

And there is even a **third** angle. *Al-Baghawī* records Anas ibn Mālik saying these were people who prayed between Maghrib and ʿIshā', and Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī saying — in the strongest expression — that they *would not sleep* in the way the heedless sleep at all.

Now, here is what I want you to understand about these differences. This is not a flaw in the verse. This is the verse doing exactly what good Arabic does — **shimmering.** Each scholar is standing at a slightly different angle to the same light. One sees the short hours of a single night. One sees the rare full-sleep nights across a lifetime. One sees the early-evening prayers. And when you let all of them sit together, you don't get confusion. You get a **person** — someone whose entire relationship with the dark hours was reorganized around Allah, in whatever form that took.

The word *qalīlan* refuses to sit still. And that restlessness is the point. **However you measure it, the night belonged to Allah, and sleep got the leftovers.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Word They Chose for Sleep — *Yahjaʿūn*

Here is something easy to miss, and it rewards the noticing.

Arabic has more than one word for sleep. There is *nawm* — the ordinary, general word, the one in *Āyat al-Kursī* (*lā ta'khudhuhu sinatun wa lā nawm*). There is *ruqād*, the sleep of the People of the Cave. There is *subāt*, sleep as a kind of cutting-off, used in Surah An-Naba'.

But the word here is none of those. The word here is from the root **ه-ج-ع — *hajaʿa.***

Let's go to the lexicon, because *al-Qurṭubī* takes us there carefully. *Al-hujūʿ*, he says, is specifically **sleep at night.** Not daytime napping — night sleep. And then he gives us something finer: *al-tahjāʿ* — a related form — means **the light sleep, the brief doze.** He even quotes the old poet Abū Qays ibn al-Aslat:

*"The helmet has worn the hair from my head, so I taste no sleep but a light doze."*

A **light doze.** *Tahjāʿ.* The thin, shallow, easily-broken sleep of someone who is half-listening even as they rest.

Now feel what the verse has done. It did not say *they slept little* using the heavy, total word for sleep. It reached for the **root that already carries lightness inside it** — the root of the brief night-doze. So even the small portion they gave to rest is described with a word that means *a sleep that barely closes the eyes.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it's beautiful and it's exact.

It's as if the verse is saying: not only did they sleep **little** — even that little was **light.** They were like someone resting with one ear open. The heart had not fully let go of its Beloved even in the dark. There is a kind of sleep that is really just a pause in the conversation — and that is the sleep being described.

You know this feeling from one place in your own life. The night before something you love and long for — a journey you've waited years for, the arrival of someone precious. You "sleep," but you don't really sleep. You doze. You surface every hour. The heart is already awake because the heart is already **there.**

That is *hujūʿ.* That is the sleep of a heart that is already where it most wants to be — already standing before its Lord — and only reluctantly, lightly, lets the body rest.

The choice of this single root tells you their sleep was not an escape from their day. It was a thin seam **inside** a night that otherwise belonged to Allah.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Word Pushed to the Front — *Bi-l-asḥār*

Now the second verse. And here Allah does something with **word order** that I need you to see.

> [ayah:51:18] وَبِٱلْأَسْحَارِ هُمْ يَسْتَغْفِرُونَ

Let me teach the device first. In Arabic, the normal, default flow of a verbal sentence is verb-then-the-rest. When you pull a word **forward**, out of its expected place, and plant it at the front of the sentence, the grammarians call this *taqdīm* — "fronting" — and it almost always means one of two things: **emphasis** or **specialization.** You are spotlighting that word. You are saying: *this, specifically this.*

The expected order here would be "and they seek forgiveness in the pre-dawn hours." Instead, Allah fronts the time: **"and in the pre-dawn hours — *they* — seek forgiveness."** The when is pulled to the front and spotlit. And then, on top of that, the separate pronoun **هُمْ — *hum*, "they"** — is inserted even though the verb already contains "they." That extra, unnecessary pronoun is a second spotlight. Arabic doesn't waste words; when it gives you a pronoun it didn't grammatically need, it is pressing down hard: **these are the ones. These specific people. At this specific hour.**

So what is this hour that gets the spotlight?

**Al-asḥār** — plural of *saḥar.* The lexicon is precise: the *saḥar* is the **last portion of the night, just before dawn** — that thin band of darkness right before the sky begins to gray. It is the deepest, coldest, most silent stretch. It is also — and this is the whole point — the moment when the body is **screaming** for sleep. The portion they could most easily have claimed for themselves. The reward at the end of the long night-vigil.

[PAUSE]

Now watch what they do with it.

They have spent the night standing. *Qalīlan mā yahjaʿūn* — they have barely slept. Dawn is almost here. Every fiber of the body is saying: *you've earned this — lie down now, even for an hour.*

And in that exact window — the *saḥar* — *al-Muyassar* tells us, and the verse declares — **they turn to seeking forgiveness.**

Look at the verb: **يَسْتَغْفِرُونَ — *yastaghfirūn*.** It comes from the root **غ-ف-ر — *ghafara*** — whose core meaning is *to cover, to veil, to shield.* But the verb here is in a specific form — Form X, the *istafʿala* pattern — and that pattern in Arabic carries a precise flavor: **seeking, requesting, actively asking for.** *Istaghfara* is not "to be forgiven." It is **to go and ask to be covered.** It is the soul reaching out its hands and saying: *cover me, shield me, forgive me.*

[PAUSE — let this land]

Here is the thing I cannot get past, and I don't want you to get past it either.

**What do they have to ask forgiveness for?**

They just gave the night its full worth. They stood when the world was unconscious. They chose Allah over their own pillows, over their own exhausted bodies. If anyone in creation had earned the right to feel **good** about their night — to lie down at *saḥar* with a quiet little glow of satisfaction — it was them.

And instead, at the very moment the spiritual books should show a credit, **they ask to be forgiven.**

The people who did the most. Asking as if they did nothing.

This is where the linguistic journey has been carrying us the whole time. Let's now ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Who You Are at 3 a.m.

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a version of you that the world sees. It is real, but it is curated. It answers emails in a certain tone. It says the right things at the dinner table. It knows when it's being watched, and — we are all human — it performs, a little, even when we don't mean to. This is not hypocrisy. This is just being a social creature.

And then there is another version of you. The one that exists at 3 a.m. The one that is awake when the phone is finally face-down and there is no one to be anything **for.** Psychologists sometimes talk about a person's "default mode" — what the mind drifts toward the moment external demands fall away. And here is the uncomfortable truth that every honest person eventually meets: **the 3 a.m. self is the truer self.** Not because the daytime self is fake — but because the night self is the one with nothing to gain from pretending.

What do you reach for when no one would ever know? Where does the heart go when it's off the leash?

[PAUSE]

This is the room Allah walked straight into.

Of all the things He could have praised the righteous for, He reached past the public square and into the one place where character has no witness and no payoff. The night. *Qalīlan mina-l-layli mā yahjaʿūn.* Because the night cannot be faked. There is no audience to perform the vigil for. No one is going to congratulate you for waking at *saḥar.* No one even has to **know.** Which means the person who is found standing there, in the cold and the silence, is standing there for one reason only: **because of Who they are standing before.**

This is the deepest test of sincerity that exists, and Allah named it as the **first** mark of the people of the Gardens. Not "they were generous in public" — that's the next verse, and it matters, but it comes second. First: *what were they like when the lights were out and the world was asleep and there was nothing in it for them?*

[PAUSE]

And notice what the verse is **not** asking of you.

It is not asking you to be superhuman. Look again — *qalīlan.* A **little.** It is not the language of impossible, hours-long marathons of worship. Ibn ʿAbbās's reading was that rarely did a night pass with **nothing** in it — even a little, at the start or the middle. The door this verse opens is not "give the whole night or you've failed." The door is: **let the night know your name.** Let there be some seam in the dark — even a small one, even a few minutes at *saḥar* — that belongs to Allah and to no one else and that no one else will ever see.

So here is what this could change about tomorrow. Not a grand resolution you'll break by Thursday. Something smaller and more durable: **one appointment with Allah that has no human witness.** A few minutes before *Fajr.* A short prayer in the dark that you tell no one about — and specifically *because* you tell no one about it, it becomes a private wealth that the daytime self can't spend and the crowd can't touch. The night self needs somewhere to go that isn't just scrolling. Give it Allah.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: The Inverted Ladder — Why the Closest Ask the Most

Now to the part that turns everything upside down.

Here's how the human mind expects the world to work. Achievement should produce confidence. The more you accomplish, the more secure you should feel. Climb the ladder, and at each rung you should feel **more** entitled to look down and rest. We assume a straight line: *did more → owe less → feel better about myself.*

The believers in this verse walk the ladder **backwards.**

They did the most — gave the night, barely slept, stood while the world lay unconscious. And at the top of that ladder, at *saḥar*, at the exact moment they could have stood tall and looked down — they fell to their knees and asked to be **forgiven.** *Wa bi-l-asḥāri hum yastaghfirūn.*

[PAUSE]

Why? Let's sit with this, because it's the heart of the whole thing.

There is a phenomenon you may have felt and never had a word for. The people who are best at something are often the most aware of how far they fall short. The master musician hears every flaw in their own performance that the audience never catches. The deeper the expertise, the higher the standard, and the higher the standard, the more visible the gap. The amateur is delighted; the master is humbled — **by the same performance.**

Now lift that into the realm of the soul. These people spent the night **close to Allah.** And nearness does something. When you are far from a great light, you don't notice your own dust. Step close, and the light reveals every particle. **The closer they drew, the more they saw of His majesty — and the more they saw of His majesty, the smaller their own worship looked in their own eyes.** They didn't ask forgiveness *despite* their night of worship. They asked forgiveness *because* their night of worship had brought them near enough to see Whom they were dealing with.

[PAUSE]

Feel the full shape of it now.

A heedless person sleeps through the night and wakes up feeling fine — owes nothing, asks nothing, because they never came close enough to the Light to see their own dust. The believer stands the whole night and rises at dawn feeling they have barely begun — because they came so close that "barely begun" is the only honest thing to say.

This is the inverted ladder. **In the world, the more you do, the more you're owed. With Allah, the more you do, the more you realize you owe.** And — here is the mercy folded inside it — that very realization, that *istighfār* at *saḥar*, is itself counted as one of their highest deeds. Allah praises them not only for the standing but for the **asking.** He loves that, having done so much, they claimed so little. The humility is not a footnote to the worship. The humility **is** the worship's crown.

And this dismantles something poisonous that lives in many sincere hearts: the quiet voice that says, *once I'm finally good enough, finally consistent enough, I'll stop feeling like I'm falling short — I'll arrive.* This verse says: the most beloved people **never** felt they arrived. They lived their whole lives in holy poverty before Allah, hands always out, always asking to be covered. That feeling you have of never being quite enough before Him — that is not a sign you're failing. Handled rightly, it is the **exact** posture of the people of the Gardens. They felt it too. They just turned it into *istighfār* instead of despair.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change tomorrow? One concrete thing: **end your best deeds with *istighfār*, not with satisfaction.** Finished a good prayer? *Astaghfirullāh.* The Prophet ﷺ himself, after the most complete acts, would seek forgiveness. Gave in charity, helped someone, fasted a hard day? Seal it by asking Allah to forgive the shortfall in it — the distraction, the trace of showing-off, the gap between what He deserves and what you managed. This single habit does two things at once: it guards your best deeds from the pride that eats them, and it keeps your hands in the exact open position that this verse calls beloved.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: A Portrait in Two Directions

Now step back and see how these two short verses are built — and then see where they sit.

Verse 17 is the night and the body: *what they did when no one watched, and how little they kept for themselves.* Verse 18 is the dawn and the heart: *what they reached for at the very edge, when they could finally have rested.* The body gives; the heart, having given, still asks. Together they form the **vertical** axis of the righteous — the secret bond between a servant and their Lord, conducted in the dark, witnessed by no one.

And then look at what comes **next**, verse 19: *wa fī amwālihim ḥaqqun lis-sā'ili wa-l-maḥrūm* — "and in their wealth was a right for the one who asks and the one deprived." That is the **horizontal** axis — the visible, daytime bond with other people, the open hand toward the poor.

Do you see the design? Allah builds the portrait of those in the Gardens from the **inside out.** First the hidden vertical: the night, the worship no one sees, the *istighfār* at dawn. **Then** the public horizontal: the charity people can see. The secret life with Allah comes first — because that is the **root**, and the visible generosity is the **fruit.** A person whose nights belong to Allah is a person whose wealth will end up belonging to the poor. The hidden worship is not separate from the public goodness. It is what **produces** it.

## Two Roads, One Destination

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey have been the same insight approached from two directions.

The words showed us: *qalīlan* that won't sit still — the night reorganized around Allah however you measure it. *Yahjaʿūn* — a sleep so light the heart never fully let go. *Bi-l-asḥār*, fronted and spotlit — the precious final hour given not to rest but to asking.

The themes showed us: the self at 3 a.m. is the true self, and these people gave that self to Allah. And the closer they drew, the less they claimed — the inverted ladder, where the most beloved are those who, having done the most, ask as though they did nothing.

Both roads arrive at the same place. **These were people whose love was greater than their need for credit.** That is the whole portrait. They wanted Allah more than they wanted to feel good about wanting Allah.

## Questions to Carry

1. Is there any seam in my night — even a small one — that belongs to Allah and to no one who could ever know about it?
2. When I do something good, where does my heart go next — toward quiet satisfaction, or toward *istighfār*?
3. Do I secretly believe that one day I'll be "good enough" to stop feeling that I fall short before Allah — and is that belief keeping me from the very humility He loves?
4. Which self is truer: the one the daytime sees, or the one that exists at 3 a.m.? And which one am I actually building?
5. If Allah were to summarize my life the way He summarized theirs — starting with my nights — what would the first line say?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The people of the Gardens are those who gave the night its worship and still, at the edge of dawn, asked to be forgiven — the nearer they drew, the less they claimed.**

## The Closing Invitation

You don't have to become someone else tonight. You only have to let the dark hours know your name — even a little, even once, even badly. And whatever you manage, seal it not with a sense of having arrived, but with an open hand: *astaghfirullāh.* That posture — giving much, claiming little — is not a burden laid on you. It is the door these verses are holding open. Walk through it the way they did: lightly, in the dark, asking.

## Du'a

*O Allah, let there be a portion of my night that belongs to You alone — unseen, unspoken, untraded. Wake my heart at the hours before dawn the way You woke theirs, and when I have given You my best, teach me to ask Your forgiveness for it, knowing how small it is before Your majesty. Cover me, O Most Forgiving, and let my love for You outgrow my need to feel worthy of it.*

*Āmīn.*
