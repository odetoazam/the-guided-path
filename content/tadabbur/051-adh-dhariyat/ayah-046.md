# Before All of Them: The First Departure and the Name They Chose

---

## THE AYAH

**Arabic:**
> وَقَوْمَ نُوحٍ مِّن قَبْلُ‌ۖ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُواْ قَوْمًا فَـٰسِقِينَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa-qawma Nūḥin min qablu — innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn*

**Translation:**
And the people of Nuh — before all of them — indeed, they were a people who had broken out of bounds.

---

## INTRODUCTION: BUILDING THE WORLD

### The Gallery

By the time you reach this ayah, you have witnessed three catastrophes.

Surah Adh-Dhariyat — the surah of the scattering winds — has been building since verse 38. After the luminous scene of Ibrahim's angelic guests and the promise of a son, after the sudden destruction of Lut's town, the surah turns to a gallery. Civilization after civilization that stood within reach of truth and chose otherwise.

First: Pharaoh and his armies. The phrasing is swift and physical — *fa-akhadhāhu wa-junūdahu fa-nabadhnahum fī al-yamm* — "We seized him and his armies and threw them into the sea." The word *nabadhnahum* — we threw them — carries the texture of discarding. Like something picked up and released.

Then ʿĀd. Allah sent upon them *al-rīḥ al-ʿaqīm*, the sterile wind — a wind described as infertile, life-destroying. Not even a wind that kills dramatically; a wind so fundamentally opposed to creation that it leaves ruins where it passes.

Then Thamūd. *Fa-akhadhathum al-ṣāʿiqatu wa-hum yanẓurūn* — "The thunderbolt seized them while they were watching." They saw it coming. They could not move. The detail of their watching is one of the most devastating phrases in the Quran.

Three civilizations. Three distinct punishments. Each vivid, each physical, each complete.

And then — verse 46.

*Wa-qawma Nūḥin min qablu.*

No punishment described. No image of the flood. No waters rising, no ark, no wailing. Just: the people of Nuh, before all of them.

And a single diagnosis: *innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn.* Indeed, they were a people who had broken out of bounds.

### The Question the Text Places Before You

Something happens in that compression that I want you to feel before we examine it.

After three vivid, elaborated destructions — this one arrives in nine words. The oldest catastrophe, the first time an entire civilization was erased from the earth, the story that contains more Quranic chapters dedicated to it than almost any other prophetic narrative — and here it receives less space than either ʿĀd or Thamud.

Why does Allah mention Nuh's people *last*, after chronologically they came first? The flood preceded Pharaoh by millennia, preceded ʿĀd and Thamud. They are the original. Yet they appear at the end of this list, marked with that quiet temporal note: *min qablu* — before all of them.

And why — after three scenes that each give you something to picture — does this one offer nothing to picture at all? Just a name, a temporal marker, and one word of diagnosis?

Something is being said in that brevity. Something is being said in that order.

Stay with me.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### I. Two Readings of One Word — The Sign That Contains Its Own Destruction

Before anything else in this ayah can be properly heard, we need to pause at its very first syllable — because the classical scholars of Quranic recitation disagreed about how to read it. And that disagreement is itself illuminating.

Let me teach you something about Arabic grammar first.

Arabic nouns carry grammatical case — their endings shift depending on their role in a sentence. When a noun is the direct object of a verb (the thing acted upon), it takes the accusative case. When it is in a possessive or prepositional chain, it takes the genitive case. These two endings are phonetically distinct. And in the tradition of Quranic recitation, different chains of transmission reaching back to the Prophet ﷺ preserved different authorized readings.

The first word here — *qawma*, "the people of" — is contested between two canonical readings.

The majority of reciters read: **وَقَوْمَ نُوحٍ** — with a *fatḥa*, the accusative ending. Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both document this reading. *Wa-qawma Nūḥin.* The noun is the object of an implied verb carried forward from the preceding verses — *We destroyed, We seized, We took* — the verb doesn't need to be restated because the pattern has already been established. This reading says: *something happened to the people of Nuh.* The punishment is assumed.

But al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both record that Hamza, al-Kisa'i, and Abu Amr recited: **وَقَوْمِ نُوحٍ** — with a *kasra*, the genitive ending. This reading connects the phrase backward in the surah to the word *āya* — sign — which appeared in verse 37 about Lut's town: *"And We left in it a sign for those who fear the painful punishment."* In this reading, the people of Nuh belong to that same chain of signs. Not just: something happened to them. But: *in them — in their story, in their existence, in the fact of what they were — there is a sign.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with both readings for a moment.

In the accusative reading, the people of Nuh are **recipients**. Something was done to them. The grammatical role is passive — they receive the action of divine response.

In the genitive reading, the people of Nuh are **themselves a sign**. They are something to be read, contemplated, understood. The grammatical role is referential — their existence teaches.

Two authorized transmissions. Two grammatical functions. And together, they say something neither says alone.

**The people of Nuh ARE the sign OF their own destruction.**

Their story is not merely a warning from outside — a historical event we observe. Their existence as a community that chose the way it chose is itself the most profound lesson available to us. They did not just receive a punishment. They became a text.

The Quran carries both truths simultaneously, and it did not have to choose between them. The word works both ways. The grammar opens in both directions. This is one of those places where the dual transmission of recitation does theological work that a single reading could not.

### II. مِّن قَبْلُ — The Word That Hangs in Deep Time

Now look at the phrase that follows: *min qablu*.

"From before." "Before."

In Arabic grammar, the word *qablu* is a relational term — it normally connects to something, takes a defined referent. "Before Pharaoh." "Before them." "Before this moment." It belongs to the class of words that take a *muḍāf ilayhi*, a completing noun that gives it direction.

But here, *qablu* stands without that completion. Classical grammarians call this *mabnī ʿalā al-ḍamm* — the word is "sealed," cut off from its referent, carrying only its ending ḍamma without a defined object to complete it. It doesn't say *before Pharaoh.* It doesn't say *before ʿĀd.* It just says: **before.**

Before all of them.
Before the list that was just given.
Before everything in this sequence.

[PAUSE]

That sealed, unanchored *qablu* reaches into deep time in a way that a specified referent cannot. The moment you give it a defined object — "before Pharaoh," "before ʿĀd" — you tie it to something. You locate it. You limit it. But left absolute, *min qablu* hovers. It points backward without terminus. It occupies a position in history that is simply: *before.*

The original. The prototype. The first.

Here is what that does to the architecture of the passage. The surah has been moving backward through history — Pharaoh, then ʿĀd, then Thamūd. Each story takes us earlier. And now it arrives at Nuh's people, marked only with *min qablu* — before all of these, before the list, before the sequence, at the origin point. The movement backward through time reaches its source and stops.

And there, at the source, it delivers not an elaborate scene but a single diagnosis.

**The oldest catastrophe requires the fewest words.** Not because it is less important — but because it is most foundational. Every destroyed civilization that appears before it in the list is, in some sense, an instance of the same pattern. And the pattern was established here, before everything.

### III. The Two Names — Identity Offered, Identity Chosen

Now I want to show you something in the Arabic that the translation cannot fully carry.

The root of the word *qawm* — "people" — appears twice in this single verse.

*Wa-**qawma** Nūḥin* — "the people of Nuh."
*Kānū **qawman** fāsiqīn* — "they were a people who had broken out."

Same root. Same word. Two different grammatical positions. Two different identities.

Let me stay with the root first. The root *q-w-m* in Arabic carries the image of *standing*, of rising. *Qāma* — to stand up, to rise. A *qawm* is not merely a crowd. It is a community — those who stand together, who have a shared orientation, a shared posture toward the world. The word carries dignity, solidarity, and a kind of collective moral uprightness.

Now notice what Allah gives them in the first naming.

**قَوْمَ نُوحٍ** — "the people of Nuh." They are identified by their prophet. Their identity is given in relationship to him — *Nuh's people.* This is not a neutral designation. To be called *Nuh's people* is to be the people he prayed for, the people he labored among for decades upon decades, the people he wept for and pleaded for and built an ark for. To be *Nuh's people* is to have been counted among the recipients of one of the most sustained prophetic missions in all of revelation.

That is the first name. The name of what was offered to them.

And then — *innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn.* They were **a people of fisq.** Now they are defined not by their prophet but by their behavior. Not by what was given to them, but by what they did with it.

[PAUSE]

Two names. Two identities. And the distance between them is the entire tragedy.

They were given the first name by grace. They chose the second name by action.

And here is the quietly shattering detail this verse holds: the word *qawm* appears in both names. They are still a *qawm* in the second naming — still a people who stand together. The root of collective dignity is still there. But what they stand together in is now *fisq.* Their solidarity, their shared standing, is oriented toward departure rather than toward what they were given.

They stood together — in the wrong direction.

[PAUSE]

Surah al-ʿAnkabūt tells us that Nuh lived among them for nine hundred and fifty years (29:14). Surah Nuh records him describing the length and the intensity: he called them *laylān wa-nahārā* — night and day (71:5). And his calling, he says, only increased them in *firār* — in flight, in running away (71:6).

This is the texture of their fisq. Not ignorance. Not never having heard. They heard night and day for the better part of a millennium — and they increased in their running. Their choice was not a failure of information. It was a failure of will. They knew the boundary, and they exited it.

[PAUSE — major]

### IV. فَـٰسِقِينَ — The Date That Breaks From Its Skin

This final word is where the entire ayah arrives. And it carries an image from ancient Arabic that I want to restore to you, because when you see it, everything that came before it becomes complete.

The root *f-s-q* in its earliest Arabic usage meant: a date coming out of its husk. Classical lexicographers, including the material preserved in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, record this as the root's physical origin — the image of a date fruit that has ripened to the point where it bursts through its outer casing. The casing was not a prison. It was the date's protection, its completion, its natural form. When the date breaks free of its skin, it doesn't become more itself — it begins to decay. The exit from the husk is not liberation. It is the beginning of ruin.

From this physical image came the Quranic usage. A *fāsiq* is someone who exits the bounds that were meant to complete and protect them. The word does not describe someone who never knew the boundary. It describes someone who was *inside* it — who had a covenant, a framework, a structure that gave them meaning and protection — and who chose to come out.

[PAUSE]

This is why *fāsiq* is not the same as *kāfir* in this context, not the same as *jāhil*, not the same as one who was simply uninformed. The *fāsiq* knows. The *fāsiq* was there. The fisq happens from within, not from without.

The people of Nuh were inside the covenant — they had Nuh among them, they had the call arriving night and day, they knew what was required of them. Their fisq was not a failure of proximity to truth. It was departure *from* proximity. They were dates ripe enough to burst their own skin.

Notice too how the word ends: *fāsiq**īn***. The final syllable closes on the *qāf* — the hard, deep consonant that cuts off at the back of the throat. There is nothing soft about this word's ending. It does not trail away. It stops, hard and clean, like a door closing.

**The sound of the word performs its meaning.** The exit is not a drift; it is a cut.

[PAUSE]

And so the full diagnosis — *innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn* — arrives with a kind of terrible clarity. Allah doesn't need to describe the flood here. He doesn't need to describe the ark, the rising waters, the wailing at what could not be undone. One word is sufficient. They exited. They broke from their husk. And what followed is simply the consequence of having left what was meant to hold them.

When you exit what was completing you, you have already done the most essential thing. The water is, in a sense, secondary.

[PAUSE — long]

### The Bridge

The linguistic construction of this verse has shown us four things that converge on a single insight.

Two authorized readings reveal that the people of Nuh are simultaneously the object of divine response and a sign for those who come after — they are what they teach.

An absolute, unsealed *before* reveals that they are not one instance in the list but the origin point of the list — the source toward which the surah's backward movement through history was always heading.

A double naming reveals the gap between the identity offered to them by grace and the identity they chose by action — *Nuh's people* and *a people of fisq* sharing the same root, oriented in opposite directions.

And a root image — the date exiting its husk — reveals that fisq is not the movement of ignorance but the movement of departure: chosen, decisive, from within.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme I: The Compression Principle — Why the Oldest Story Needs the Fewest Words

Here is something true about how deep expertise works.

When a physician who has practiced for thirty years walks into a room, they need far less diagnostic information than a resident in their first year. The resident needs every data point — the vitals, the labs, the imaging, the detailed history. The experienced physician looks once and names what they see. Not because they're being careless. Because the pattern has been fully internalized. The essential form is so completely understood that elaboration would be redundant. One look is enough.

The knowledge hasn't decreased. It has deepened to the point of compression.

[PAUSE]

Hold that and come back to the structure of this passage.

Pharaoh and his armies: three verses (38–40). The scene is vivid — seized, thrown into the sea, the armies following. There is dramatic movement in the narration.

ʿĀd: two verses (41–42). The sterile wind that left nothing of what it came upon except ruins — *illā jaʿalathu ka-al-rimīm.*

Thamūd: two verses (43–45). The thunderbolt that struck them while they watched — that detail of their helplessness, of seeing it coming, is some of the most devastating Arabic in the Quran.

The people of Nuh: one verse (46). No punishment described. No image of the waters. Just: they existed, they were *qawman fāsiqīn*, and the word *min qablu* tells us they came first.

The chain compresses as it moves backward through time. Three verses, two verses, two verses, one verse. As if the surah is distilling — finding the essential form, reducing toward the original.

[PAUSE]

The Meccan listener who first received this surah knew the story of Nuh in detail. It is spread across multiple chapters — Surah Hud, Surah al-Aʿrāf, Surah al-Anbiyāʾ, and most extensively the surah named for him. They had heard the ark, the mockers, the century of building, the son who refused, the woman who betrayed, the animals entering two by two, the springs of the earth erupting and the gates of heaven opening. They knew all of it.

So when this single verse arrives — after Pharaoh's vivid drowning, after ʿĀd's devastating wind, after Thamūd's watching eyes — the compression does not indicate lesser importance. It indicates the *opposite*. The story is so thoroughly known, so fully internalized, that one verse contains everything. *Fāsiqīn.* The whole story is in that word. The diagnosis is complete. Nothing needs to be added.

The experienced physician. One look. The essential form.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this compression does to the listener who hears the surah not as history but as address.

The original Meccan audience was not receiving a catalogue of ancient disasters. They were standing inside one. They had a prophet in their city. They had the revelation arriving in their language, addressing their questions, responding to their objections. The destruction gallery of verses 38–46 was not a lecture about the past — it was a mirror held up to the present.

And the mirror's deepest reflection was the people of Nuh. Not because of what was done to them, but because of what was true of them: *innahum kānū qawman fāsiqīn.* They were people who had the truth within reach, who heard the call, who lived inside the invitation — and who chose departure.

**The pattern reaches all the way back.** It is the oldest human tendency in the recorded Quranic account. It is *min qablu* — before everything else. Which means: what the people of Mecca faced when the Prophet ﷺ arrived was not a new situation. It was the recurrence of the original situation. The oldest test, newly administered.

And what we face when the Quran arrives in our hands — when we read an ayah and feel it move something in us and then close the book and return to the familiar — is not a new situation either.

It is the oldest one.

### Theme II: Fisq — The Danger Specific to Those Who Already Know

Let me take you to a texture most of us recognize, even if we don't have a precise name for it.

There is a particular quality of struggle that belongs not to the person who is uninformed but to the person who is fully informed and still choosing wrongly. The person who knows exactly what is happening to their health and still makes the same choice at the end of the day. The person who knows what the right word to their spouse would be and lets the moment pass. The person who has read this passage before, who has felt something open in them before, who understood something clearly before — and who returns to the same pattern the next morning.

This is not ignorance. It is something harder to name and, in many ways, more dangerous.

The Arabic tradition gives it a precise name. It gives it a root image. And it places that image at the bottom of history — not to terrify, but to name.

*Fisq.*

[PAUSE]

The people of Nuh did not suffer from proximity failure. They were as close to prophetic truth as a community could be. By the timeline preserved in Surah al-ʿAnkabūt (29:14), Nuh remained among them for nine hundred and fifty years. Surah Nuh records him describing his own mission in terms of total dedication: he called them at night and he called them by day. He called them in public and he called them privately. He tried every mode of address available to a prophet (71:8–9). He held nothing back.

And his calling — *lam yazid-hum duʿāʾī illā firāran* — only increased them in *firār*: in flight, in running away (71:6).

Sit with that word, *firār.* It is active. It is not passive rejection; it is active fleeing. They ran from what they had every opportunity to understand. The more clearly the call arrived, the more energetically they departed from it.

That is fisq in its most concentrated form. Not the failure of one who never heard. The failure of one who has been hearing for so long that hearing itself has become the trigger for departure.

[PAUSE]

This is what the root image of the date unlocks. The date inside its husk is not imprisoned. The husk is its completion — the form it was made to inhabit while it becomes what it is meant to become. The date that bursts free doesn't become more date. It becomes date that has left what it needed, and begins to change in ways that are not the direction it was made for.

The people of Nuh had a husk. The covenant with Allah, the call of a prophet, the framework of a truth that was asking them to become something — to rise to the standing that the word *qawm* implies. That framework was not a cage. It was their completion. And their fisq — their choosing of departure — was not liberation from an oppressive constraint. It was the exit from the very structure that would have made them what they were made to be.

And after that exit: the consequence follows not as punishment imposed from outside but as the natural result of having left what was holding you.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this presses directly on us.

The most spiritually dangerous moment is not the moment of genuine unknowing. It is the moment *after* knowledge. The moment when something is understood clearly — an ayah lands, a truth is felt, a direction is seen — and the choice is made to return to the familiar rather than follow the understanding.

That is not ignorance. That is *firār.* That is the active turning away from what has been clearly received.

The people of Nuh are not a distant story about a people we don't resemble. They are the archetype of a pattern that runs through human history and runs through the interior of every person who has ever heard the call and, in some small or large way, run from it.

*Min qablu* — before everything else — this was already happening. It is the oldest pattern. It is not the exception to human behavior in the presence of truth. It is, Allah shows us through the compression of this single verse, close to the default.

[PAUSE]

Which means: the choice to *stay* — to resist the fisq, to remain inside what is completing you rather than breaking free from it — is always the harder choice. It requires something the departure does not require. Departure has gravity on its side. The date bursts its husk because that is what pressure produces without resistance.

Remaining requires an active counter-pressure. An orientation maintained against the direction of ease.

[PAUSE]

But the surah does not end with the destruction gallery.

After verse 46 — after the chain of nations that chose departure — the surah pivots. Verse 56 arrives: *Wa-mā khalaqtu al-jinna wa-al-insa illā li-yaʿbudūn* — "I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me."

The purpose was never hidden. It was not complex. It did not require nine hundred and fifty years to understand. The people of Nuh knew what they were made for. Their fisq was not the failure to understand their purpose. It was the refusal of it — the choosing of departure from what the purpose asked of them.

The surah places these two things — the destruction gallery and the purpose declaration — directly next to each other. The gallery shows what happens when the purpose is refused. The declaration shows what the purpose is. And between them stands verse 46: the most ancient refusal, compressed to a single diagnosis, before all the rest.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now I want to show you the shape of the whole passage you have been inside, because the architecture carries its own meaning.

Surah Adh-Dhariyat opens with the scattering winds. *Al-dhāriyāt dhurwan* — "by those that scatter with a scattering." The surah's opening oaths move through cosmic phenomena — winds, laden clouds, ships, beings that execute commands — each described in terms of its function, its doing of what it was made for. The winds scatter. The clouds carry burden. The ships cleave the water. Creation doing what creation does.

Then the surah turns to human history. And in verses 38–46, it runs a gallery of civilizations that did *not* do what they were made for. The movement is explicitly backward through time: Pharaoh → ʿĀd → Thamūd → Nuh. And as it moves backward, it compresses, until it arrives at the most ancient story with the fewest words: *min qablu. Qawman fāsiqīn.*

And then the surah turns forward again, past the destruction gallery, toward the positive declaration: *I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the structure?

The opening oaths show creation fulfilling its purpose — wordlessly, without deliberation, without choice.

The destruction gallery shows human communities refusing theirs — the fisq that exits the covenant, the firār that runs from the call.

And verse 56 names what the purpose always was — what was always available, what each of these communities had within reach.

The gallery is the negative image. The declaration is the positive. And verse 46 — the people of Nuh, before all of them — is the point where the negative image reaches its origin. The deepest layer of the gallery. The original refusal, which all the subsequent ones repeat.

**The winds know what they are for.** They scatter because that is what they are made to do, and they do not deviate. The question the surah places before the human listener — after all of this, at the end of the gallery, facing the purpose declaration — is simply: *will you?*

[PAUSE — major]

### The Two Journeys Are One

The linguistic journey showed us:

Two readings carrying two truths simultaneously — the people of Nuh as recipients of divine response *and* as a sign for those who contemplate them. They are what they teach.

An absolute *before* that places them at the origin point of the pattern — not one instance but the prototype.

A double naming that maps the gap between the identity offered by relationship with a prophet and the identity chosen through behavior — the same word, the same root, pointed in opposite directions.

And a root image — the date exiting its husk — that names fisq not as ignorance but as departure from what was completing you.

The thematic journey showed us:

History compresses toward the essential diagnosis. The oldest story needs the fewest words because the pattern is most fully established there. Brevity here is not dismissal — it is the highest form of emphasis.

And the most dangerous spiritual state is not unknowing but chosen exit — the fisq of those who heard Nuh night and day and chose *firār*, active flight from what they understood.

These are the same insight, approached from two directions.

**The people of Nuh are not a lesson about distant people. They are the compressed prototype of the only human failure that matters: knowing the boundary of the covenant and choosing, clearly and freely, to exit it.**

### The Closing Invitation

There is one shift this ayah invites.

Not a dramatic overhaul. Not the resolution of every struggle. Just: the noticing.

To become the kind of person who notices — in the small, unremarkable moments of a single day — when fisq is available. When the exit is right there. When the call has been heard clearly and the *firār* is beginning. And to name it for what it is: not just a personal failing, not just a moment of weakness, but the oldest pattern in recorded human history, playing out again, in miniature, in this moment.

Nuh called his people night and day for nine hundred and fifty years. He received almost nothing in return except departure. And he called again.

The call is still arriving.

The pattern is known.

The choice — the choice that makes the pattern or refuses it — is always present.

That is what *min qablu* means when it is turned toward us rather than away from us: before everything else, before all the history, before all the subsequent stories — this moment of choosing is *the* moment. The original test. Newly available.

### Du'a

*Yā Allah — You showed us the people who heard the most patient call in history and still ran. We ask You: do not let our knowledge become our *firār.* Do not let understanding become the thing we flee from. Protect us from the fisq that does not announce itself — the small exiting, the quiet departure from what was meant to complete us. Make us of those who, when called night and day, do not increase in flight but in return. And when we have broken from our husk, as we will, bring us back — before the water rises, before what was protecting us is lost. Āmīn.*
