# I Want Nothing From You

### Surah Adh-Dhariyat, Verses 57–58

> [ayah:51:57] مَآ أُرِيدُ مِنْهُم مِّن رِّزْقٍ وَمَآ أُرِيدُ أَن يُطْعِمُونِ
>
> *Mā urīdu minhum min rizqin wa mā urīdu an yuṭʿimūn.*
>
> **"I do not want any provision from them, nor do I want them to feed Me."**
>
> [ayah:51:58] إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلرَّزَّاقُ ذُو ٱلْقُوَّةِ ٱلْمَتِينُ
>
> *Inna-llāha huwa-r-Razzāqu dhu-l-quwwati-l-matīn.*
>
> **"Indeed, it is Allah who is the Provider — the firm Possessor of strength."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Hungry Gods

Let me take you somewhere old. Older than the Kaʿba's rebuilding. Older than Makkah's marketplaces.

Walk into almost any temple of the ancient world — Mesopotamia, Egypt, the city-states along the great rivers — and you would find, at the center, a statue. And you would find, twice a day, every day, priests bringing that statue **food.** Bread, meat, beer, fruit. They laid it before the carved face. They believed the god ate. Not metaphorically — they believed the deity drew its strength from the offering, and that if the offerings stopped, the god would weaken, withdraw, perhaps die.

This was not a fringe idea. This was the **operating assumption of religion** across most of human history. The relationship between a human and a god was understood as a kind of feeding. You sustain the god; the god, fed and satisfied, sustains you. A trade. A contract. Sacrifice was the currency.

And even where there were no statues — among the Arabs at their idols, around the Kaʿba choked with three hundred sixty stone faces — the logic was the same. You brought the deity something. You slaughtered for it. You poured for it. You fed it, in one form or another, because a god that received nothing was a god that gave nothing.

Now hold that whole world in your mind — that ancient, universal instinct that **worship is a transaction, and the deity is, at some level, in need.**

[PAUSE]

Because in the verse just before ours, Allah has said something staggering: *"I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me"* (51:56). The whole purpose of your existence, stated in nine words: worship.

And the moment that lands, a question detonates in the heart of anyone raised on the old assumption: *Then He needs it. He created us because He wants something from us. We are here to feed the God.*

And before that thought can even finish forming —

before you can complete the sentence —

Allah answers it.

> *"I do not want any provision from them, nor do I want them to feed Me."*

Listen to what just happened. The oldest assumption in the history of religion — that the gods are hungry, that worship is the meal we owe — Allah reached into it and pulled it out by the root. *I want no provision from you. I do not want you to feed Me.*

So here is the question I want us to carry for the next hour, and I promise you it will go deeper than it first appears:

**If God wants nothing from your worship — then what is your worship for? And what kind of God is it who commands you to come to Him, and asks for nothing in return?**

Stay with me. The answer is hidden in two words, and in one gap between them.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Urīdu* — "I Want." The Verb of the Divine Will.

Before we open the word, let me put something simple in your hands.

In every language, there is a difference between *needing* and *wanting*. But they bleed into each other. When a starving person says "I want food," the wanting is really a need — the body is collapsing without it. When a billionaire says "I want that painting," the wanting is pure desire, sitting on top of a life that is already complete. Same verb. Two utterly different realities underneath it.

Now hold that distinction, and look at the word Allah chooses — twice — in this verse.

> مَآ **أُرِيدُ** ... وَمَآ **أُرِيدُ**
>
> *Mā **urīdu** ... wa mā **urīdu*** — "I do not **want** ... and I do not **want**."

The verb is **أُرِيدُ — *urīdu*.** Its root is *r-w-d* (ر و د). And here is the first thing worth sitting with. The root in its origin, the lexicographers tell us, carries the image of a *rāʾid* — the scout, the one sent ahead of a tribe to **seek** out pasture and water, going gently back and forth across the land, searching. *Rāda* — to seek, to go looking for what you lack.

And the form Allah uses, *urīdu*, is what Arabic grammarians call **Form IV** — the *afʿala* pattern. Over centuries this Form IV settled into *the* verb of willing, of wanting, of intending: *arāda, yurīdu.* It became the word for the will itself.

So at the most literal layer, *urīdu* descends from a root that means **to seek what you do not have.**

And what does Allah do with it?

[PAUSE]

He **negates** it. *Mā urīdu.* "I do not seek. I do not want."

Do you feel the precision of that? Of all the verbs available, the one chosen carries, in its very bones, the idea of *a seeker reaching for something absent* — and Allah places a flat *mā*, a "not," in front of it. He is not saying "I have enough provision." He is not saying "I am satisfied with little." He is reaching into the very verb of seeking-what-you-lack and saying: **this does not describe Me. There is no reaching here. There is no lack here to reach from.**

Notice something else, and this one is easy to slide past. The verb is in the **first person.** Not "Allah does not want." *I* do not want. أُرِيدُ — *urīdu*. This is Allah speaking, directly, in His own voice, about His own will. There is no narrator standing between you and the speaker. The Lord of the worlds turns to you and says, in the first person, with His own "I": *I want nothing from you.*

When a king sends a message through a herald, you hear the king's words secondhand. When the king himself leans toward you and speaks — *I* — the distance collapses. That is what this verb does. The God of the ancient temples spoke, if at all, through hungry priests. This God speaks Himself, and the first thing He says about His own will toward you is a **denial of need.**

Here is what this single word reveals: **The independence of Allah is not a calculation He arrived at. It is the texture of His very will.** He does not *decide* to need nothing. There is nothing in Him that seeks. *Mā urīdu.*

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *An Yuṭʿimūn* — "That They Feed Me." The Most Intimate Need, Denied.

Now watch the second half of the verse, because Allah does something deliberate. He does not simply repeat Himself. He narrows.

The first clause denied wanting **provision** — *rizq*, a wide word, covering everything that sustains a life. But the second clause zooms in to one specific, intimate act:

> وَمَآ أُرِيدُ **أَن يُطْعِمُونِ**
>
> *wa mā urīdu **an yuṭʿimūn*** — "nor do I want **that they feed Me.**"

The word is **يُطْعِمُونِ — *yuṭʿimūn*.** Root *ṭ-ʿ-m* (ط ع م) — the root of *ṭaʿām*, food, and *ṭaʿima*, to taste, to eat.

And here the grammar does something the first verb did not. *Yuṭʿimūn* is **Form IV** of the eating-root — and Form IV here is genuinely **causative.** *Ṭaʿima* means "to eat." Put it in Form IV — *aṭʿama* — and it becomes "to **make** eat," that is, "to **feed.**" To cause another to eat.

So *an yuṭʿimūn* is, at the most literal level: *that they cause Me to eat. That they put food into Me.*

Let that image stand for a moment in its full, almost shocking concreteness — because Allah chose it on purpose. He did not say "nor do I want their offerings." He said: **I do not want them to feed Me.** The exact gesture of those ancient priests, bread in hand, raised to the carved mouth of the idol. The most basic, bodily, dependent act in all of creation — *eating* — Allah names it and refuses it. *I do not want to be fed.*

And do you see the **progression** from the first clause to the second? From the general to the most specific. From *rizq* — all sustenance — down to the single most intimate form of dependence a living thing knows. We can imagine a powerful being who needs no *wealth*. But every living thing we have ever met needs to **eat.** Hunger is the universal mark of the contingent, the created, the dependent. To breathe is to need air; to live is to need food. It is the most inescapable neediness there is.

And precisely *there* — at the deepest, most undeniable point of creaturely dependence — Allah plants His denial. **Not even this. I do not even want to be fed.**

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you the gap that the grammar leaves open — because this is where the classical scholars lingered, and where the verse opens into something tender.

*An yuṭʿimūn.* "That they feed Me." But there is a question buried in it: **feed whom, exactly?** The pronoun says "Me." Yet the mufassirūn noticed that the act of feeding, when commanded, always has a *recipient on the ground* — a hungry mouth somewhere in creation.

Listen to how the classical commentators read it. They give us not competing guesses but layers of one truth — camera angles on the same scene:

- **Al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar** read the plain surface: *I want no provision from them and I do not want them to feed Me — for I am the Provider, the Giver. I am free of all need of the creation; rather, they are the ones poor toward Me, in every state.* But al-Jalālayn push the scope further still, reading the first denial as: *"I do not want from them any provision — for Myself, for themselves, or for others; nor do I want them to feed Me — nor themselves, nor others."* Even the provision creatures imagine they generate for their own sustenance is not theirs to generate. There is no autonomous chain of supply in creation. Trace any meal far enough and you find a single originating hand.

- **Al-Qurṭubī** records the plain reading, and then adds a view carried from **Ibn ʿAbbās and Abū al-Jawzāʾ**: *that they should provide for themselves, or feed themselves* — meaning, do not imagine you even sustain your own selves. And a further reading: *that they feed My servants.*

- And then **al-Baghawī** opens the door all the way. He explains *"that they feed Me"* to mean *that they feed someone of My creation* — and then he says the thing that will stay with us for the rest of this reflection. **He attributed the feeding to Himself** — *"that they feed Me"* — *"because the creation are Allah's dependents (ʿiyāl Allāh), and whoever feeds the dependents of someone has fed him."*

And he cites the ḥadīth qudsī — narrated by Abū Hurayra in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* — in which Allah will say on the Day of Resurrection:

> *"O son of Adam, I asked you for food and you did not feed Me."* The servant will say: *"My Lord, how could I feed You, when You are the Lord of the worlds?"* And Allah will say: *"Did you not know that My servant so-and-so asked you for food, and you did not feed him? Had you fed him, you would have found that with Me."*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened to the verse?

In *51:57*, Allah says: *I do not want you to feed Me.* He needs nothing.

And in the ḥadīth, Allah says: *You did not feed Me* — meaning, *you did not feed My hungry servant.*

These are not in tension. They are the **two faces of the same God.** The God who is utterly beyond your bread — who would not be diminished if every fire of sacrifice on earth went cold forever — is the *same* God who so binds Himself to the hungry of His creation that He calls *their* hunger **His own.** He needs nothing for Himself. And He takes the need of the weakest creature and writes it into His own name.

So when the verse says *"I do not want them to feed Me"* — it is not a cold statement of a remote, self-enclosed deity. It is the opening half of a sentence the ḥadīth completes: *I do not need your food for Myself — so when I ask you for food, know that I am asking on behalf of the one in front of you whom I have made My own.*

What this word reveals: **Allah severed worship from His own need — and re-attached it to the need of the weak.** The feeding He refuses for Himself, He commands toward His creatures, and then claims as if it reached Him.

## Element Three: *Al-Razzāq, Dhu-l-Quwwati-l-Matīn* — The Reversal Made Explicit.

We have heard two denials. Now comes the affirmation — and the architecture of the passage clicks into place.

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلرَّزَّاقُ ذُو ٱلْقُوَّةِ ٱلْمَتِينُ
>
> *Inna-llāha huwa-r-Razzāqu dhu-l-quwwati-l-matīn.*

Look at the scaffolding of emphasis Arabic stacks here, because every piece is load-bearing.

First, **إِنَّ — *inna*** — "Indeed." The particle of certainty and emphasis that opens the clause like a struck bell. *Truly, certainly, without doubt.*

Then **هُوَ — *huwa*** — "He." Now, grammatically, this pronoun is not strictly needed; the sentence would parse without it. When Arabic inserts a free-standing pronoun like this between the subject and its description, the grammarians call it **ḍamīr al-faṣl — the pronoun of separation** — and its function is **restriction.** It means: *He, and He alone.* Not "Allah is a provider, one among providers" — but **"Allah — He is *the* Provider, exclusively, to the exclusion of all else."**

And then the name itself: **ٱلرَّزَّاقُ — *ar-Razzāq*.** Notice it is not *ar-Rāziq* — "the one who provides." It is *ar-Razzāq*, built on the intensive pattern *faʿʿāl*. This is the form Arabic uses for the one who does the action **constantly, abundantly, overwhelmingly, to all** — the way you would not call a man who cooked once a *cook*, but a man whose whole life is feeding others, ceaselessly, earns the intensive name. *Ar-Razzāq:* the One whose provision pours without pause, to every creature, in every moment, without exception and without end.

Now — do you see the mirror?

> *Mā urīdu minhum min* **rizq** *...* — *"I do not want any* ***provision*** *..."*

The root r-z-q appears in verse 57 as something Allah refuses to receive. Then watch what verse 58 does:

> **ٱلرَّزَّاقُ** — *ar-***Razzāq.**

The same three letters come back — *crowned.* What He disclaims as something creation could give Him, He claims as what defines Him. The very concept of provision — sustenance, livelihood, supply, everything that keeps a living thing alive — it belongs to Him, flows from Him, and cannot flow *to* Him.

**This is the architecture.** Allah did not merely deny needing your provision. He revealed that He *is* the provision. The denial and the declaration are the same statement seen from two directions. *I want no rizq from you* — *because I am ar-Razzāq.* What He disclaims receiving, He claims as the source of giving.

And al-Jalālayn's expanded reading of verse 57 now illuminates verse 58 with a brighter light. If even the provision creatures seem to generate for their own selves ultimately flows from Allah — if there is no autonomous chain of sustenance anywhere in creation — then when verse 58 declares *huwa-r-Razzāq*, it is not a modest claim. It is a total one. Al-Ṭabarī captures this when he describes Allah as *al-mutakaffil bi-aqwātihim* — the One Who **guarantees** their sustenance, the One Who has taken upon Himself the responsibility of every creature's provision. Not merely the Source but the Guarantor.

[PAUSE]

Then comes the title of what that hand holds:

> ذُو ٱلْقُوَّةِ — *Dhu-l-Quwwah* — "The Possessor of strength."

*Quwwah* is the word for ability made actual, power made real — not potential capacity but enacted strength. The God who provides for every creature in every moment, from the bacterium converting light to the archangel sustaining the heavens, requires not just will but *quwwah*: a strength that never thins from being drawn upon, never depletes as the demands upon it multiply.

And then the word that seals the verse, and this one deserves the slowest attention:

> ٱلْمَتِينُ — *al-Matīn.*

Classical lexicographers traced *matīn* to the *matn* — the dense cord of the back, the part of the body that, when strong, makes everything else possible, and when weak, makes everything else collapse. *Matīn* means: firm, dense, resistant to wearing-out, impossible to cut through. Its opposite is *wahin* — thin, frail, easily severed.

Al-Ṭabarī, surveying the variant readings across the major cities of early Islam, notes a quiet but instructive difference among the reciters. **The majority** — across virtually every major center of Quranic recitation — read the final word as *al-matīnu*, in the nominative, making it a description not of *al-quwwah* (the strength) but of *Dhu* itself — that is, of Allah: *"The Possessor of strength — He, the Firm One."* Al-matīn, in this reading, is another divine epithet. A minority, including Yahyā ibn Wathāb and al-Aʿmash, read it in the genitive, *al-matīni*, describing *al-quwwah* directly: *"The Possessor of firm strength."* Al-Ṭabarī notes that both readings arrive at the same shore. Whether al-matīn describes Allah's person or His attribute, firmness clings to this God at every grammatical angle you approach from.

And al-Muyassar makes explicit what the word means at the edge of its range: *lā yuqharu wa lā yughālabu.* **He cannot be overcome. He cannot be overpowered.** Not merely steady, as a wall is steady until a greater force arrives. But the firmness of something that cannot encounter a superior force — because no superior force exists. The provision that flows from al-Matīn flows from an inexhaustible source that nothing in creation can interrupt, diminish, or redirect.

[PAUSE]

Hold the whole architecture of verse 58 now:

*Inna* — certainty strikes its note.
*Allāh* — the name that contains all the rest.
*Huwa* — He alone. The pronoun of restriction. Not one provider among many.
*ar-Razzāq* — provision pours from Him to every creature, ceaselessly, in every instant.
*Dhu-l-Quwwah* — the strength that makes this outpouring real.
*al-Matīn* — and that strength is unconquerable, undiminished, unending.

Five beats. And together they answer not just the surface concern — *who feeds whom* — but the deepest anxiety underneath it: what if the God who needs nothing also has nothing to give? What if divine self-sufficiency is simply another name for divine absence?

*Inna-llāha huwa-r-Razzāqu dhu-l-quwwati-l-matīn.*

Allah is not self-sufficient *instead of* being generous. He is self-sufficient *as the very ground of* His generosity. The one who needs to receive can only give out of what remains; he gives carefully, guardedly, knowing his stores are finite. The one who receives nothing from creation can pour into creation *without end.* His giving does not diminish Him. His provision does not come from somewhere He then loses. The ar-Razzāq pours because that is what He is — not what He does when He has surplus.

This is what the two verses taken together reveal: **the God who wants nothing from you is also the God who withholds nothing from you.** The same divine independence that empties your worship of any transactional weight is the same divine strength that fills the earth, and fills you, and fills the sparrow and the whale and the microbe and the angel — all without diminishment, all without remainder.

# PART TWO: THE THEOLOGICAL JOURNEY

## The Gap That Changed Everything

There are two verses here. And between them, there is a gap of one breath — a pause between *57* and *58* — that may be the most important structural feature in this entire passage.

Verse 57 ends on a negative. *I want nothing from them. I do not want them to feed Me.* If the Quran stopped there, you might walk away with a kind of cold portrait: the God who is above need, remote, requiring nothing, beyond reach. The perfect but untouchable sovereign.

And verse 58 begins: *Inna-llāha huwa-r-Razzāq.* **Indeed, Allah — He is the Provider.**

The denial of receiving *becomes* the declaration of giving. The God who says *I take nothing from you* immediately reveals Himself as the one from whom everything flows to you.

In the Quran, this pattern — divine independence followed by divine giving — appears again and again. *"O humanity, you are the ones in need of Allah; and Allah — He is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy"* (35:15). The divine transcendence is never an end-station. It always opens into outpouring.

And this is the theology that the two verses of 51:57-58 enact in miniature: **self-sufficiency is not a wall between Allah and creation. It is the source of endless generosity.** Only the one who is free of all need can give without limit.

[PAUSE]

## What Worship Becomes

Go back to the question I asked at the beginning. *If God wants nothing from your worship — then what is your worship for?*

We can answer it now.

Verse 56: Allah created jinn and mankind to worship Him.
Verse 57: He wants nothing back from that worship. No provision, no feeding.
Verse 58: He is the Provider, the Firm Possessor of strength.

Here is what this triangle reveals: **worship does not give Allah anything He lacked. Worship gives you something you would otherwise lack.**

The classical commentators pressed into this. Al-Ṭabarī records an early disagreement on the meaning of verse 56 itself — *"I created jinn and mankind except to worship Me"* — noting that some among the early scholars read it as referring specifically to the guided (*suʿadāʾ*) among creation, those for whom worship is the actualized purpose, while the misguided were created for disobedience. Others held the statement universal: the purpose is worship for all, whether or not all fulfill it. The debate is real and the ikhtilāf is old. But on verses 57 and 58 the scholars converged without dispute: the worship is entirely for the benefit of the worshippers, not for any benefit to Allah. He is the Self-Sufficient. They are the ones poor toward Him, in every state.

So what is worship? It is not a payment. Not a tribute. Not a transaction you perform so that an account somewhere gets credited and the God starts paying attention.

Worship is the posture of a creature whose entire existence flows from a source it did not make and cannot replace. Worship is what it looks like when the contingent thing — the thing that could have not existed, the thing that will one day return to not existing — *turns toward* the Necessary Being and says: I know what I am. And I know what You are. And I know the direction that all provision flows.

The ancient temples got it almost completely backwards. They understood that something flowed between deity and creature. They understood that the relationship required an orientation. But they had the direction wrong. They thought the creature fed the god. The Quran comes and says: **it only ever goes one way. And you are on the receiving end.** Always.

[PAUSE]

## The God Who Takes on Hunger

There is one more thing to say, and it returns us to the ḥadīth qudsī.

The verse says: *I do not want you to feed Me.*

And then — in the ḥadīth — Allah says on the Day of Judgment: *O son of Adam, I was hungry and you did not feed Me.*

We looked at this earlier through al-Baghawī's lens: the creation are Allah's dependents, and whoever feeds them feeds Him. But let us linger here for one more moment, because the ḥadīth and the verse are doing something together that neither can do alone.

The verse establishes the absolute: **Allah needs nothing.** He is ar-Razzāq. He is al-Matīn. He is beyond hunger, beyond need, beyond the diminishment that needing implies. This is not poetry. This is metaphysics. There is no hunger in Allah.

And the ḥadīth descends from that absolute into the most particular, most concrete, most urgent moment of human encounter: the hungry person standing in front of you. And Allah says, from the height of absolute self-sufficiency: **that hunger is Mine.**

He is not contradicting the verse. He is completing it. *I don't need your food.* And: *When My creature needs your food, I am there in the need.* He took His own complete freedom from need and used it — not to stand apart from the needy creation, but to *bind Himself to the neediness of the weakest creature.* The feeding He refused for Himself, He claimed for His creation. The provision He wants nothing of from you — He asks you to direct toward the hungry, and then *writes it as if you gave it to Him.*

This is one of the most extraordinary things the Quran and ḥadīth together describe: a God who is beyond every human need, and yet who finds the hunger of the least of His creation *intolerable enough to make it His own.*

**He who needs nothing chose to identify Himself with those who need everything.**

# CLOSING: Return to the Ancient Temples

Take yourself back to where we started. The temple. The priests. The daily offering. The bread raised to the stone mouth.

They were not entirely wrong. There *is* a relationship. Something does flow between the creature and the Creator, something that the human heart has always, in every culture, in every century, felt the pull of. They were right about the orientation — the sense that existence requires a gesture toward something greater than itself.

But they had the direction reversed. They thought the human sustained the god. The Quran comes and turns the whole apparatus around. **The god does not receive from the creature. The god sustains the creature.** Not just sometimes, not just in response to the correct offering, but always, without exception, by the very nature of who He is: ar-Razzāq, the Unceasing Provider; al-Matīn, the Unconquerable; dhu-l-quwwah, whose strength makes every moment of sustenance possible for every creature that has ever drawn breath.

And the worship He commands? It is not your payment into the temple treasury. It is your *recognition.* Your turning toward the source of all you have ever received. Your *wuqūf* — standing — before the One who stood before creation itself.

Worship is not what you bring to God. It is what you *become* when you understand what God brings to you — without stopping, without diminishment, without asking anything in return.

*Mā urīdu minhum min rizqin wa mā urīdu an yuṭʿimūn.*

*I want nothing from them. I do not want them to feed Me.*

The God who says this is not a king who has grown bored with tribute. He is the God whose very existence is provisionment — whose nature is to give what He does not need to receive. He commands your worship not to fill a divine lack, but because in turning toward Him, you finally see clearly what has always been true: **everything you have ever been given has come from the same hand, and that hand has never once needed yours in return.**

And the verse that follows the denial?

*Inna-llāha huwa-r-Razzāqu dhu-l-quwwati-l-matīn.*

Indeed, Allah — He alone — is the Provider, the Possessor of strength, the Unconquerable.

Five words naming what the ancient world was reaching for and never found: a God who gives without needing. A God who pours without being filled. A God whose strength, being inexhaustible, can be inexhaustibly generous.

You were not created to feed this God.

You were created to be fed by Him — and to worship the hand that does the feeding.
