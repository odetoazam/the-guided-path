# The Pillar That Drowned Him

## Adh-Dhariyat 51:38–40

> <!-- ayah:51:38 --> وَفِى مُوسَىٰٓ إِذْ أَرْسَلْنَـٰهُ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ بِسُلْطَـٰنٍ مُّبِينٍ

> <!-- ayah:51:39 --> فَتَوَلَّىٰ بِرُكْنِهِۦ وَقَالَ سَـٰحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ

> <!-- ayah:51:40 --> فَأَخَذْنَـٰهُ وَجُنُودَهُۥ فَنَبَذْنَـٰهُمْ فِى ٱلْيَمِّ وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ

*Wa fī Mūsā idh arsalnāhu ilā Fir'awna bi-sulṭānin mubīn.*
*Fa-tawallā bi-ruknihi wa qāla sāḥirun aw majnūn.*
*Fa-akhadhnāhu wa junūdahu fa-nabadhnāhum fī l-yammi wa huwa mulīm.*

**And in Moses [too was a sign], when We sent him to Pharaoh with a clear authority. But he turned away with his pillar [of power] and said, "A magician or a madman." So We seized him and his hosts and flung them into the sea, and he was blameworthy.**

## INTRODUCTION

### The world these words fell into

To feel what is happening in these three verses, you have to hear where they sit in the surah — because they are not the opening of a story. They are the last figure in a procession.

Adh-Dhariyat begins with wind. Allah swears by *adh-dhāriyāt* — the scattering winds — and by the clouds that carry, and the ships that run, and the angels that distribute. Four oaths, all of them things that *move*, things that carry something from one place and set it down in another. And then the point of the oaths lands: *innamā tū'adūna la-ṣādiq, wa inna d-dīna la-wāqi'* — what you are promised is true, and the reckoning is going to happen. It is going to fall on you the way rain falls when the clouds have carried it far enough.

Then Allah begins pointing. *And in the earth are signs for those who have certainty.* *And in yourselves — do you not see?* Sign after sign, each one introduced with the same small preposition: *wa fī* — "and in." And in the sky is your provision. And in the earth. And in yourselves.

Then the pointing turns to history. And in the story of Ibrahim's guests. And in the people of Lut, whom We destroyed and left as a sign for those who fear the painful punishment. And in 'Ad, when We sent against them the barren wind. And in Thamud. And in the people of Nuh before them.

Do you hear the drumbeat? *Wa fī… wa fī… wa fī.* Allah is walking the listener down a corridor, and on every wall hangs a sign — a cosmos here, a soul there, and then a series of ruined civilizations, each one a warning nailed to the wall.

And then, at the end of the corridor, the last portrait:  **وَفِي مُوسَىٰ** — *and in Moses.*

This is the climax of the procession. Every earlier nation was a people, a crowd, a tribe. Now Allah brings out the single most powerful individual the ancient world could imagine — a man who ruled the mightiest state on earth, a man who would later stand on the bank of a river and say to a slave-born prophet, *"Am I not better than this contemptible one?"* Pharaoh. The one who said *"I am your lord, most high."* The absolute ceiling of human power.

The people who first heard these verses in Mecca were not emperors. They were a small, mocked, squeezed community, being called liars, being called *exactly* what Pharaoh calls Moses in verse 39. Sit with that. The Prophet ﷺ was being called a magician. He was being called a madman. Those two accusations were not ancient history to the first audience — they were *this week's insults*, thrown at them in the marketplace.

So when Allah tells them the story of Moses and Pharaoh, He is not giving them a history lesson. He is holding up a mirror to the man throwing the insults — and quietly showing the mockers how his story ends.

### The hook

Here is the strange thing, and I want you to hold the question rather than answer it yet.

The most powerful man on earth. An army. A throne. A kingdom that had lasted for centuries. And when the truth arrived at his door, the Qur'an does not say he *stood* against it. It does not say he *fought* it. It uses one small word to describe what he did — and then tells you what he leaned on when he did it.

Whatever he leaned on — that is exactly what Allah used to bury him.

Stay with me. Let's find out what it was.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### One / Two men, two prepositions: *bi-sulṭānin mubīn* and *bi-ruknihi*

Let me teach you something small about Arabic first, because the whole first movement of these verses turns on it, and if I don't hand you the tool now you'll walk past the treasure.

In Arabic there is a tiny particle, a single letter — *bi-* (بِ) — attached to the front of a word. It's one of the most ordinary things in the language. It can mean "with," "by means of," "leaning on," "by the strength of." When you do something *bi-* something, you are doing it *carried by that thing*, *equipped with it*, *relying on it*. If I say I crossed the river *bi-* a boat, the boat is what carried me. The *bi-* tells you what a person is depending on to act.

Now hold that, and watch these two men.

Moses arrives. How?  **بِسُلْطَانٍ مُّبِينٍ** — *bi-sulṭānin mubīn.* He comes carried by a *sulṭān* — and the word *sulṭān* comes from the root س-ل-ط, which is the root of dominance, of overpowering authority, the root behind the word *sulṭān*, "sovereign." <!-- morphology:51:38:11 root=سلط pos=N --> But notice: Moses himself owns nothing. He is a fugitive who left Egypt with a price on his head, a shepherd, a man with a speech difficulty, holding a stick. The *sulṭān* he carries is not his own power. It is authority *loaned* to him — clear, luminous proof from Allah. And it is *mubīn* — from the root ب-ي-ن, "to be clear, to make plain, to separate truth from falsehood." <!-- morphology:51:38:12 root=بين pos=N --> The word *mubīn* is itself a form that means not just "clear" but "clarifying" — it makes *other* things clear. Moses walks in with borrowed light that exposes everything it touches.

Now the other man. Pharaoh. How does he meet this?  **فَتَوَلَّىٰ بِرُكْنِهِ** — *fa-tawallā bi-ruknihi.*

Same particle. *Bi-.* He too leans on something. But look at what.

*Rukn.* From the root ر-ك-ن. <!-- morphology:51:39:4 root=ركن pos=N --> Say it out loud: *rukn.* Feel how the word closes. It ends in that hard, blocked *k-n* — the tongue seals shut. It *sounds* like what it means. A *rukn* is a corner. It is the pillar of a building. It is the load-bearing part, the strongest section, the part you brace against when everything shakes. When Allah describes the mountains being firm, when Lut in his distress cries out that he wishes he could take refuge *in a strong support*, he says *rukn shadīd* — a mighty pillar. The *rukn* is the thing you run to when the earth moves under you. It is your fortress corner. It is what feels *unshakeable*.

Now put the two verses side by side and let them speak to each other, because this is the design.

Moses comes **bi-sulṭānin mubīn** — leaning on clear, borrowed, divine authority.
Pharaoh turns away **bi-ruknihi** — leaning on his own pillar.

The classical scholars looked at "his pillar" and each of them saw something real, and I don't want you to choose between them — I want you to stack them. Some said *ruknihi* means his army, his hosts, the massed military that made him untouchable. Some said it means his ministers and his court, the men who held up his rule. Some said it means simply his own might, his power, the strength he trusted. Layer them together and you get the full portrait: **the pillar was everything in his life that was not Allah.** The whole architecture of his security — the soldiers, the advisors, the throne, the reputation, the sheer weight of him — all of it gathered into one word. *His corner.* The thing he braced against when the truth came knocking.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does. Two men, same crisis, same little preposition — and the entire difference between them is *what comes after the "bi-."* One leaned on light that wasn't his. One leaned on a pillar that was. And the whole surah has been about signs that *point away from themselves* toward Allah — and here is a man whose signpost points only back at his own strength.

What you lean on is the whole story. **Everything downstream is just the consequence of your *bi-.***

[PAUSE]

### Two / *Sāḥirun aw majnūn* — the "or" that gives him away

He turned away. And then  **وَقَالَ سَاحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ** — *and he said: a magician, or a madman.*

I want to slow down on one word here, and it is the smallest word in the sentence. Not *sāḥir*. Not *majnūn.* The word between them. **أَوْ** — *aw.* "Or."

Here is why it matters, and let me build it for you.

When a person has actually examined something and reached a verdict, they name it precisely. They say: *this is X.* One thing. The judgment is settled, so the word is single. But when a person has *not* examined anything — when they only want the thing gone, when they are reaching in the dark for any label that will make it go away — they throw out options. *It's either this or that. It's a magician… or, I don't know, a madman.*

Feel the difference. *Sāḥir* — a magician <!-- morphology:51:39:8 root=سحر pos=N --> — is somebody in total *control*. Calculated, skilled, deliberate, manipulating reality with precision. *Majnūn* — a madman, from the root ج-ن-ن which means "to be covered, hidden, veiled," the same root as *jinn*, the hidden ones — <!-- morphology:51:39:10 root=جنن pos=N --> is somebody who has *lost* all control, whose reason is veiled from him, who doesn't know what he's doing.

**You cannot be both.** A master of calculation and a man who has lost his mind are opposite diagnoses. The magician is too much in command; the madman is not in command at all. And Pharaoh throws *both* at Moses in a single breath, joined by that lazy little *aw*.

That "or" is the sound of a mind that isn't analyzing. It's the sound of a mind *evading.* He is not telling you what Moses is. He is telling you that he needs Moses to be *anything* — anything with a label, because a labeled thing is a filed-away thing, a dismissed thing, a thing you no longer have to look at. The content of the accusation is irrelevant. Its *function* is to close the door.

[PAUSE]

And here is the quiet horror underneath it, given where he's standing. He has just watched a staff become a serpent. He has watched a hand come out glowing. And his response is not *what is this?* — the honest question of a man confronting something real. His response is *quick, give it a name so I don't have to deal with it.* Magician. Or. Madman. **The label is a wall he builds so he never has to walk through the door.**

One word — *aw* — and Allah has shown you the entire interior mechanism of a man refusing the truth.

[PAUSE]

### Three / *Nabadhnāhum* and *mulīm* — flung like refuse, blamed by his own hand

Now the response. And I need you to hear two words at the end, because the whole justice of these verses lives in them.

 **فَأَخَذْنَاهُ وَجُنُودَهُ فَنَبَذْنَاهُمْ فِي الْيَمِّ** — *So We seized him and his hosts and flung them into the sea.*

First the verb *akhadha* —  "We seized him." It is a firm, total word, the word for a grip that closes and does not open. But it's the next verb I want to put in your hand.

**نَبَذَ** — *nabadha.* <!-- morphology:51:40:9 root=نبذ pos=V --> Let me teach you the word before I let you feel it land. *Nabadha* does not mean simply "to throw." Arabic has other words for throwing. *Nabadha* is the specific verb for casting something aside *because it is worthless.* It's what you do with a peel, with scraps, with something used up and beneath your notice. It's the word the Qur'an uses elsewhere for how the People of the Book *nabadhū* the Book of Allah *behind their backs* — tossed it away like trash. *Nabadha* carries contempt in its very body. And listen to the sound of it — *na-badh* — that flick at the end, the tongue tossing the *dh* away. The word itself performs the discarding.

Now hold the two halves of the verse together and feel the collision.

On one side: **وَجُنُودَهُ** — *and his hosts.* <!-- morphology:51:40:6 root=جند pos=N --> Armies. Chariots. The massed military of the ancient superpower. The very *rukn* he leaned on in verse 39 — remember, one reading of his "pillar" *was* his army — is now named explicitly and dragged in with him.

And on the other side: they are *nabadha'd.* Flung. Discarded. Tossed away like something you don't even look at as it leaves your hand.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done? The man gathered the greatest concentration of power on earth — and before Allah, all of it together weighed exactly as much as a peel you flick off your fingers. He needed an *army* to feel safe. Allah did not need an army to end him. He *nabadha'd* them. Grandeur on one side of the verse; garbage-disposal on the other side of the same verse. And the sea — *al-yamm*, <!-- morphology:51:40:14 root=يمم pos=N --> the deep water — simply closed over the top and went quiet, as if nothing of importance had ever been there.

And then the last word. The verdict. And this is the most precise word in all three verses, so let me teach you the grammar, because everything turns on a single form.

 **وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ** — *and he was mulīm.*

You will often see this translated "and he was blameworthy," or "worthy of blame." That's close. But the *form* of the word carries something the translation drops.

The root is ل-و-م, "to blame." <!-- morphology:51:40:17 root=لوم pos=N --> Now, Arabic could have said *malūm* — that's the ordinary passive participle, and it means simply "one who is blamed," someone on whom blame has fallen from outside. If the verse said *malūm*, you'd picture a man being pointed at, condemned by others.

But it does not say *malūm.* It says *mulīm.* And *mulīm* is not the passive participle — it is the **active** participle, from a different, heavier form of the verb (form IV, *alāma*). *Alāma* means "to do the very thing that earns blame." So *mulīm* does not mean "one who was blamed." It means **"one who did what makes a person deserve blame" — the one who *earned* it, the one who *brought it on himself.*** The blame is not a label pinned on him from outside. It is the *product of his own hand.* He is not the object of blame. He is its *author.*

[PAUSE]

Feel the perfect justice of the word choice. Allah does not say "and We blamed him." That would leave the tiniest crack for the objection: *maybe the judge was harsh.* Allah says he was *mulīm* — **he manufactured his own condemnation.** Every drop of it traces back to a choice he made. He turned away. He labeled the truth to avoid it. He leaned on his pillar. Nobody did this to him. He did it, and the word *mulīm* seals it: the blame carries no name but his own.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

So look at what these words, taken together, have shown us. A man leaned on the wrong *bi-* — his pillar instead of his Lord. He reached for a label — *magician, or madman* — to avoid the thing he didn't want to face. And he ended flung like refuse into the water, holding, at the very end, a blame stamped with his own signature.

But this is not really a story about a dead king. Allah did not hang this portrait in the corridor so we could shake our heads at ancient Egypt. **He hung it so we would recognize ourselves in it.** So let's ask what these words reveal not just about a pharaoh — but about the pillar every one of us is leaning on right now.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme one / What is your *rukn*?

Let me start somewhere very ordinary, in your life, not in Egypt.

There is a particular feeling most of us know. It's late, and you're lying awake, and some fear moves through you — a health scare, a job that might disappear, a relationship fraying, a child you're worried about. And notice what your mind *does* in that moment. It doesn't sit in the fear. It goes looking for the ledge to grab. *It's okay — I have savings.* *It's okay — I know people, I have connections.* *It's okay — I'm smart, I'll figure it out.* *It's okay — I have him, I have her, I have them.*

That reflex — the thing your mind reaches for at 2 a.m. to make the fear survivable — **that is your *rukn.*** That is your pillar. That's the corner you brace against when the ground moves. And here is the uncomfortable, clarifying question this verse puts to you: *what is it, actually?* Not what you'd *say* it is if someone asked you at the mosque. What does your mind actually reach for when it's frightened and honest and alone?

Because Pharaoh's tragedy was not that he *had* a pillar. Every human being leans on something. His tragedy was *what* he chose. He had, genuinely, the strongest pillar any human being had ever assembled — the largest army, the deepest treasury, the most absolute authority on the planet. If a pillar could save a man, his would have. And when the crisis came, he *turned away with it.* He leaned his full weight on it.

And that pillar — I want you to really see this — **that pillar did not fail to save him. It did something worse. It became the thing that killed him.** The army he trusted didn't stand aside while he drowned. The army *drowned with him.* The very hosts that were his source of safety in verse 39 are the *junūd* flung into the sea in verse 40. His pillar didn't just prove useless. It became his anchor. It pulled him down.

That is the thing about a false *rukn* that we never quite believe until we're in the water. It is not neutral. When you make anything other than Allah your source of ultimate security, you don't just risk it letting you go. You risk it *taking you with it.* The wealth you leaned on becomes the thing you sold your prayers for. The reputation you leaned on becomes the cage you can't tell the truth from. The person you leaned on becomes the reason your faith rises and falls with a text message. **The pillar becomes the weight.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is where I don't want to be glib, because the honest struggle is real. Allah is not telling you to have no savings, no career, no plans, no people you love. Moses came *bi-sulṭān* — he too leaned on something. Having means is not the sin. The question is one of *architecture*: is the thing you lean on holding *you* up, or are *you* holding *it* up as a god? Moses's *sulṭān* was borrowed light that pointed past itself to Allah. Pharaoh's *rukn* was a fortress he mistook for the ground itself. The savings are fine. The savings *as your peace* — that is the *rukn.* The plan is fine. The plan *as your certainty* — that is the *rukn.*

There's a simple, brutal test for it, and you can run it on yourself tonight. Take the thing your mind reaches for at 2 a.m. Now imagine Allah removes it. Just take it away in your mind — the money gone, the person gone, the reputation gone, the plan collapsed. And watch what happens *inside you.* If the answer is *"then I have nothing"* — you've found your *rukn*, and it isn't Allah. Because a person whose pillar is Allah, when you take everything else away, still has everything. And a person whose pillar is the everything-else, when you take it away, discovers he was standing on water the whole time.

What would actually change tomorrow if you internalized this? One concrete thing: the next time the fear comes and your mind sprints for the ledge, *before* you let it grab the savings or the connection or the plan, make it stop for the length of one breath and say *ḥasbunallāh* — Allah is enough for me. Not instead of the plan. *Before* the plan. Reorder the architecture. Let Allah be the ground, and let everything else be what it actually is — a tool in His hand, not a pillar under your feet.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme two / The verdict with your name on it

Let me take you into the most crowded room in modern life: the room where nothing is anyone's fault.

We are fluent, now, in the language of the un-responsible. It wasn't me — it was my upbringing. It was my trauma. It was the system. It was the way I was raised, the people around me, the pressure I was under, my mental health that day, the fact that they provoked me first. And some of that is real — genuinely, some of it is real, and Islam is not cruel about the burdens people carry. But somewhere in the fluency, a lot of us have quietly built a whole self out of it. A self whose defining move, whenever the mirror gets too close, is to *find the address to send the blame to* — anywhere, as long as it's not here, not me.

And notice — this is exactly Pharaoh's move in verse 39, dressed in modern clothes. He doesn't examine Moses; he *labels* him. *Magician, or madman.* The label isn't a conclusion; it's an *exit.* And that is precisely what "it's my trauma, it's the system, it's them" so often becomes — not an explanation we sit inside and grow from, but an exit we bolt through so we never have to stand in front of the mirror. He labeled to avoid the truth about *Moses.* We label to avoid the truth about *ourselves.* Same mechanism. Same door slammed so we never have to walk through it.

Now hear the last word of verse 40 again, into that room.  **وَهُوَ مُلِيمٌ.** He was *mulīm.* Not *malūm* — not "one who was blamed by others." *Mulīm* — *one who authored his own blame.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how that word walks straight into the room where nothing is anyone's fault and quietly closes every exit? Allah does not say *the circumstances condemned him.* Allah does not say *history judged him harshly.* Allah does not even say *We blamed him* — which would at least let a person imagine appealing the verdict. Allah says he was *mulīm.* **The blame was his own product. His own manufacture. His own signature at the bottom of the page.**

And I want to say something that sounds harsh but is actually the most dignifying thing in these verses. **To be told the blame is yours is to be told you were free.** *Mulīm* is a word that can only be spoken over someone who *had a real choice.* You cannot author blame if you were never an author of anything. The whole modern instinct to move the blame off ourselves — we think it's protecting us, but it's actually erasing us. It's saying *I wasn't really there, I wasn't really choosing, things just happened through me.* And the Qur'an, with one word, hands your life back to you: **you were there. You were choosing. It has your name on it.** That is terrifying. It is also the only ground on which a human being can actually change, because you cannot repair what you refuse to own.

This is what makes the Muslim's relationship with accountability the exact opposite of despair. The believer runs *toward* the ownership — *rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā*, "our Lord, we have wronged our own selves," the words of Adam, the first *taking* of the blame in human history. Not "the serpent did it." Not "she gave it to me." *We wronged ourselves.* And because Adam authored his blame, Adam could be forgiven — Allah turned to him. Pharaoh, standing in the same position, authored his blame too, but spent his last breath still labeling, still leaning, still *mulīm* with no *rabbanā ẓalamnā* on his lips. Same ownership. Opposite endings. The difference was not whether the blame was theirs. The difference was whether they *picked it up and brought it to Allah.*

[PAUSE]

So here is the concrete thing, and it's small enough to do this week. The next time you feel the reflex fire — the instant, automatic *it wasn't me, it was them, it was the situation* — catch it. Don't suppress the real factors; name them honestly. But then ask the one question the ego never volunteers: *and what was my part?* Find the sliver that is genuinely yours, however small. And then do the thing Pharaoh never did with his ownership: **take it to Allah.** Not to beat yourself with it — to hand it over. *Rabbī, this part was mine. I own it. Forgive it.* That single sentence is the whole distance between Adam and Pharaoh. Both were *mulīm.* Only one came home.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture — who is holding the pen

Now that we've done the work, stand back and look at the shape of these three verses, because the design itself is the final proof.

Watch the *subjects* of the sentences. Watch whose hand is moving.

Verse 38: **We** sent him — *arsalnāhu.* <!-- morphology:51:38:5 root=رسل pos=V --> Allah acts.
Verse 39: **He** turned away — *tawallā.* <!-- morphology:51:39:2 root=ولي pos=V --> Pharaoh acts.
Verse 40: **We** seized, **We** flung — *akhadhnāhu, nabadhnāhum.* Allah acts.

We… he… We. Do you see it? Pharaoh's entire rebellion — all his turning-away, all his labeling, all his leaning on his pillar — is a single verb, *tawallā*, and it sits **completely surrounded** by the action of Allah. His defiance is enclosed on both sides. It is bracketed. The most powerful man on earth gets exactly one verb of his own, and it is boxed in between *We sent* and *We seized.* **The pillar he leaned on could not even get him out of the middle of the sentence.** Grammatically, he is trapped between two acts of God before the sea ever touches him. The structure *is* the theology: human defiance is always, only, a small thing enclosed by divine will.

And there is a second architecture, finer than the first. Listen to the last words that fall on each man's story:

He called Moses a *majnūn* — a madman. That was Pharaoh's verdict, the label he spent to make the truth go away.
And Allah's final word over Pharaoh is *mulīm* — blameworthy by his own hand.

The one who *handed out* the verdict *received* one. The mouth that labeled was, in the end, labeled — but there is all the difference in the world between the two labels. *Majnūn* was a lie thrown to escape reality. *Mulīm* was the truth that reality threw back. Pharaoh spent a false name on Moses; Allah rendered the true name over Pharaoh. **He got to *say* one word about another man. Allah got to say the *last* word about him.** That is the whole account of every soul that ever set itself against the truth: you may spend your breath naming, dismissing, labeling — but the final word is never yours.

### Bringing it together

And now see that our two journeys — the words and the themes — were always the same insight walking toward you from two directions.

The linguistic journey showed you a man defined by his *bi-*: what he leaned on. The first theme asked you what *you* lean on. Same truth. The linguistic journey showed you *mulīm*: blame with a single author. The second theme put you in front of your own mirror. Same truth. Pharaoh's *rukn* and Pharaoh's *mulīm* are not two facts about him — they are one fact seen twice. **The pillar you lean on instead of Allah is the very thing that authors your blame**, because the moment you make your safety something other than Him, every choice that follows is bent toward protecting *it* instead of obeying *Him* — and that bend, repeated a thousand times, is exactly what a life spends manufacturing when it earns the word *mulīm.*

### Questions to carry

Take these with you. Don't answer them quickly.

1. When fear comes to you at night, what is the first thing your mind reaches for to feel safe? Name it. Is it Allah — or is it your pillar?
2. If Allah removed the thing you lean on most, would you discover you still had everything, or that you had nothing?
3. Where in your life are you currently reaching for a label — *"that person is just…," "this is just…"* — to avoid walking through a door you know you should walk through?
4. Think of the last thing that went wrong that you told yourself wasn't your fault. Was there a sliver that was yours? Have you picked it up and brought it to Allah — or is it still sitting in the room where nothing is anyone's fault?
5. When your final word is written over your story, what would make it *mulīm* — and what, by Allah's mercy, would make it something else?

### The one sentence to carry

**Every pillar you lean on instead of Allah is a pillar He can fling into the sea — and when it sinks, the verdict will carry your name, not His.**

### The invitation

So this is the quiet invitation these three verses leave on the table. Not fear — reordering. Go and find your *rukn*, the real one, the 2 a.m. one. And don't necessarily tear it down — just move it. Take it off the throne where you've been treating it as the ground you stand on, and put it back in your hand as a tool, and let Allah be the ground. Let your *bi-* become *bihi* — "by Him." And where you find blame you've been mailing to other addresses, gently bring it home, own your sliver, and hand it to the One who turns toward those who turn to Him. Adam did it and was forgiven. The door Pharaoh refused to walk through is still open for you.

### A closing du'a

*O Allah, You who sent Moses with clear light and flung the tyrant's pillar into the sea — do not let us lean on anything but You. Make You our rukn, our corner, the ground beneath our feet, so that when everything else is taken we find we still have everything. And when we have wronged our own souls, give us the tongue of Adam and not the silence of Pharaoh — let us say "rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā" before the water closes over us, and turn to us, for You are the Ever-Relenting, the Merciful. Āmīn.*
