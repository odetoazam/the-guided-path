# The Barren Wind: When the Instruments of Life Are Sent to Take It

> وَفِى عَادٍ إِذْ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلرِّيحَ ٱلْعَقِيمَ
> مَا تَذَرُ مِن شَىْءٍ أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ إِلَّا جَعَلَتْهُ كَٱلرَّمِيمِ

> *Wa fī ʿĀdin idh arsalnā ʿalayhimu r-rīḥa l-ʿaqīm. Mā tadharu min shayʾin atat ʿalayhi illā jaʿalat-hu ka-r-ramīm.*

> **"And in 'Aad [was a sign], when We sent against them the barren wind. It left nothing it came upon but reduced it to crumbled ruin."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Text

I want you to do something before we open these words. I want you to stop thinking of wind as weather.

For the people who first heard this surah, wind was not a number on a forecast. Wind was survival. Wind was the messenger that arrived before the rain — you felt it on your skin, you smelled the moisture in it, and you knew the herds would drink and the crops would come. Wind carried the pollen from one date palm to another so that fruit would set. Wind filled the sails. Wind cooled the body at the end of a furnace-hot day. In the desert, **wind was the breath of life arriving from somewhere you could not see.**

And the Quran knows this. Listen to how Allah usually speaks about the winds:

*"And it is He who sends the winds as glad tidings before His mercy..."* (7:57)

*"And among His signs is that He sends the winds bringing good tidings..."* (30:46)

Glad tidings. Mercy. Good news you can feel on your face. That is what wind *is*, in the imagination of the listener.

Now hold that. Hold the wind as the carrier of life. Because this surah — Surah adh-Dhariyat, "The Scattering Winds" — opens its very first oath with wind:

*"By the [winds] that scatter, scattering..."* (51:1)

The surah is *named* after wind. It begins by swearing an oath upon the wind. And then, near its end, after walking us past nation after nation, Allah returns to the wind one last time. The same creation. The same breath of the unseen.

Except this time, He does not call it the bringer of good news.

He calls it **barren.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what should unsettle you before we even begin. Allah did not send 'Aad a strange new weapon. He did not send fire from a foreign sky or a beast from the sea. He sent them **wind** — the most ordinary mercy in their world, the thing that had blessed their fathers and filled their lungs every single day. He sent them the gift. And He emptied the gift of everything that made it a gift.

What does it mean when the very thing designed to give you life is sent, unchanged in form, to take it?

That is the question hiding inside one word. Let us go and find it.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: ٱلْعَقِيمَ — *al-ʿaqīm*, "The Barren"

### First, let us learn the word away from this ayah

Before we touch the wind, I want you to meet this word somewhere else — somewhere more intimate, where it lives most naturally in the Arabic language.

The root is ع-ق-م (*ʿ-q-m*). And its home, its first and most human meaning, is the body of a woman or a man who cannot have a child. *Imraʾatun ʿaqīm* — **a barren woman, one who does not conceive and does not bear.** Al-Qurtubi says it plainly in his commentary on this very verse: *"and from it, a barren woman — she does not carry and does not give birth."*

Sit with what that word carries. Barrenness is not ugliness. It is not weakness. A barren person can be beautiful, strong, whole in every visible way. The barrenness is *invisible*. It is a closing-off at the deepest level — the level of generation itself, the level where new life is supposed to begin. Everything looks capable. Nothing comes forth.

That is *ʿaqīm*.

### Now, place that word on the wind

ٱلرِّيحَ ٱلْعَقِيمَ — *al-rīḥa l-ʿaqīm* — **"the barren wind."**

Allah did not say "the destroying wind." He did not say "the violent wind" or "the burning wind," though it was all of those things. He reached past every word for force and chose the word for a womb that does not bear.

Why?

Look at what the scholars say a wind is *for*, and watch the word do its work. Al-Qurtubi: this wind *"does not pollinate clouds and does not pollinate trees, and there is no mercy in it, no blessing, no benefit."* Al-Baghawi: *"it does not pollinate a tree and does not carry rain."* Al-Jalalayn: *"it is the one in which there is no good, for it carries no rain and pollinates no tree."* And al-Jalalayn goes one step further — it names this wind. Of all the winds the Arabs knew and classified, this one is *al-Dabūr*, identified not merely by direction but by character: the wind that carried none of the cross-pollinating, rain-bearing generativity that made other winds the breath of life. The Arabs tracked their winds carefully, because knowing which wind was coming told you what the land would receive. To name this wind as al-Dabūr was to say: this is the wind that arrives with nothing in its hands.

Do you hear what they are all circling? **Every function of a healthy wind is a function of giving life.** A wind that *laqaḥa* — that pollinates — is a wind that makes the trees bear fruit. A wind that carries rain is a wind that makes the earth bear crops. The wind, in its very nature, is a *fertilizing* thing. It is the desert's instrument of conception.

And Allah took that exact instrument — the one designed to make things bear, to make things conceive, to make life begin — and He made *it* **barren.** He reached into the wind and closed off the place where life comes from.

What the scholars describe as the absence of *khayr* — no good — al-Muyassar names with a word that cuts to the root of it: no *barakah*. Not merely "no benefit," but no *blessing*. And *barakah* is not the same as usefulness. *Barakah* is the quality by which a created thing participates in divine abundance — the way a blessed rain does more than water can, the way a blessed word lands more deeply than its syllables should reach. When *barakah* is present, creation exceeds itself. When *barakah* is withdrawn, the instrument remains perfectly intact and yet stops generating the excess that made it a gift. The *ʿaqīm* wind still moves. It still arrives. It simply no longer carries the current of divine generativity that it had always been channeling without anyone noticing.

[PAUSE]

Notice something, and let it land slowly. This is not merely a wind that *kills*. Many things kill. This is a wind whose entire identity — its reason for existing — has been **reversed at the root.** The mother becomes the executioner. The bringer of conception becomes the thing that can conceive nothing, give nothing, leave nothing alive behind it. It is sterile in the most total sense: it does not even leave the *seeds* of a future. It generates only absence.

Here is what is remarkable. There is a precision in choosing *ʿaqīm* that no other word could reach. A "violent wind" tells you about *force*. A "barren wind" tells you about *consequence* — that after it passes, **nothing will ever grow here again from what it touched.** It is not the wind of an afternoon's destruction. It is the wind that forecloses the future.

### What this single word reveals

When Allah withdraws His mercy, He does not always need to send something new and terrible. Sometimes He simply takes the most familiar mercy in your life — and empties it of its fertility. The gift remains in your hand. It has just stopped being able to give.

## Element Two: مَا تَذَرُ مِن شَىْءٍ ... إِلَّا — The Architecture of "Nothing... Except"

### First, the grammar — learned simply

There is a structure in Arabic, and in your own language too, that is the most absolute way a human being can speak. It is the structure of **total negation followed by a single exception.**

"*Nobody* came to the meeting *except* one man."
"*Nothing* survived the fire *except* the stone wall."

Think about what that shape does to your mind. First it sweeps the entire field clean — *nothing, nobody, not one thing*. It empties the whole space. And then, into that swept-empty space, it admits exactly one reality through the narrow door of *except*. Everything is denied; one thing is affirmed. It is the most airtight sentence a language can build. There is no room left in it.

In Arabic this is the structure مَا ... إِلَّا — *mā... illā* — "not... except." And Allah builds the second ayah on exactly this frame.

### Now watch Him build it

مَا تَذَرُ مِن شَىْءٍ أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ إِلَّا جَعَلَتْهُ كَٱلرَّمِيمِ

Let us walk it word by word, slowly, because every piece is load-bearing.

**مَا تَذَرُ** — *mā tadharu* — "it does not leave." The verb *tadhar* (root و-ذ-ر) means *to leave behind, to let remain.* The wind leaves nothing remaining.

**مِن شَىْءٍ** — *min shayʾin* — "of any *thing*." Now stay with me, because this is the heart of it. The word *shayʾ* is the single most general, most all-encompassing noun in the entire Arabic language. It is the word for *anything that exists* — a person, a stone, a tent, an animal, a wall, a blade of grass, a memory carved in wood. When you want to say "a thing, any thing at all, with no limit," you say *shayʾ*.

And notice — Allah leaves it **completely unspecified.** He does not say "it left no *person*." He does not say "it destroyed their *houses*." He refuses to name the object. Read that absence carefully: the *unnamed* object is the entire point. To name a category — people, homes, herds — would be to imply other categories survived. By naming *nothing in particular*, Allah includes *everything without exception.*

Classical commentators felt this boundlessness and wanted to name what it enclosed. Al-Jalalayn, parsing the verse, offers two words where the Quran offered one: *nafs aw māl* — "soul or wealth." Two categories: the person, and what the person possesses. Everything a human life *is* and everything it *has*. Not merely a poetic pairing — these are the two pillars upon which the entirety of human civilization rests. The wind did not distinguish between them. The *nafs* of the father and the *māl* of the household went to *ramīm* with equal finality. The Quran deliberately withholds these names, because it wants you to feel the totality before you can organize it into categories. But the scholars' instinct reveals what the wordless *shayʾ* implies: nothing with a name, and nothing without one, was spared.

**أَتَتْ عَلَيْهِ** — *atat ʿalayhi* — "that it came upon." And here is a quiet, chilling detail. The wind does not *seek*. It does not hunt or target or choose. It simply *comes upon* things — *atat ʿalayhi*, "it passed over it." Whatever happened to lie in its path. There is no escaping a thing that does not aim, because there is no way to be the one it is *not* looking for. It looks for no one. It comes upon everything.

**إِلَّا جَعَلَتْهُ** — *illā jaʿalat-hu* — "except that it made it." Here is the single exception, the one reality admitted through the narrow door. After sweeping the entire field to *nothing*, only one outcome is allowed to exist.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what this structure has done? Allah has built a sentence with **no exits.** *It leaves nothing... that it comes upon... except.* Three times the door is sealed: *nothing* (total), *anything* (unlimited), *whatever it came upon* (inescapable). And then a single outcome is let in. The grammar itself is airtight — and that is the point. The structure of the sentence *is* the experience of the punishment. There was nowhere to stand. There was no thing that was spared. The sentence has no opening in it, because the event had no opening in it.

### What this structure reveals

When divine judgment becomes total, language stops offering alternatives — because reality stopped offering them. The *mā... illā* is not a poetic flourish. It is a closed door rendered in grammar. And we were *shown* the closing, so that we would understand: there are states of affairs from which there is exactly one exit, and Allah is the One who decides whether the door is mercy or *illā*.

## Element Three: ٱلْعَقِيمَ → ٱلرَّمِيمِ — The Sound That Seals the Fate

### A short lesson on listening

The Quran is not only meant to be parsed. It is meant to be *heard*. And sometimes the meaning is carried not by the dictionary but by the **sound** — by the way two words rhyme across a distance and lock together in your ear like a sentence closing on itself.

I want you to listen to two words. Not study them yet. Just hear them.

The wind is *al-ʿaqī**m**.*
What it leaves behind is *al-ramī**m**.*

*ʿaqīm... ramīm.*

Say them out loud. Hear it? **The two words rhyme.** Long *ee*, then *m*. *-īm... -īm.* They are sonic twins. They bookend the entire event — the wind at the start of the description, the result at the end, and they *chime against each other across the two ayahs.*

### Now let the sound carry the meaning

This is not an accident of Arabic. This is design you can *hear*.

The wind is **ʿaqīm** — barren, the one that gives birth to nothing.
And what does the barren thing produce? It produces **ramīm.**

What is *ramīm*? The root is ر-م-م (*r-m-m*), and it means **that which has crumbled and decayed — old bones gone to powder, dry plant-stalks that disintegrate the moment you touch them, the chaff and dust of something that was once whole.** *Ramīm* is what is left when a thing has been so thoroughly broken down that it no longer holds its shape — it crumbles in the hand. Al-Jalalayn glosses it: *al-bālī al-mutafatit* — the decayed and disintegrated, broken past the point of fragments, into the form of what was once a thing but can no longer be named as one. The same root appears in Surah Ya-Sin, when the denier holds up a rotted bone and sneers, *"Who will give life to the bones when they are *ramīm* — crumbled to dust?"* (36:78).

So follow the line the sound draws for you:

The **barren** wind — *ʿaqīm* — that can generate no life...
...leaves behind only **crumbled dust** — *ramīm* — that holds no shape.

[PAUSE]

The sterile produces the pulverized. The womb that bears nothing leaves behind only powder. And your *ear* knew it before your mind did — because the two words *sound the same.* The rhyme is the argument. *ʿaqīm* and *ramīm* are bound together by their sound precisely because they are bound together by their reality: **emptiness of life on one end, emptiness of form on the other.** The wind that could conceive nothing left behind nothing that could stand.

This is what it means to say the Quran's sound is not decoration. The fate of 'Aad is sealed inside a rhyme.

## Bridge: From Words to Reality Itself

We have found three things, and they are converging.

We found a wind made **barren** — the instrument of life emptied of its power to give.
We found a sentence with **no exits** — *nothing... except* — judgment rendered total in grammar.
We found a **rhyme that binds** the barren cause to the crumbled effect — *ʿaqīm* to *ramīm*.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because Allah did not describe a windstorm in the ancient desert to teach us meteorology. He described it so that we would *see something we cannot otherwise see.* Let us go to the unseen reality these stones are pointing toward.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: When the Things Meant to Nourish You Go Barren

### Begin where you live

Think of the blessings in your life that function like wind. Not the dramatic gifts — the ones you notice and thank God for on the day they arrive. I mean the *ordinary* ones. The ones that have been arriving so regularly, so quietly, for so long that you have stopped counting them as gifts at all.

The ability to think clearly in the morning. The health that lets you stand up without pain. The relationships that have been there so long you have begun to treat them like furniture. The steady supply of enough — enough food, enough shelter, enough time between each crisis to breathe. These are wind-gifts: ambient, constant, uncounted. You feel them only when they stop.

Al-Muyassar frames these two ayahs with words worth sitting with: *"and in the matter of 'Aad and their destruction are signs and lessons for those who contemplate."* For those who *contemplate* — who read the ruins slowly, who do not rush past what the ruins are pointing at. The word the scholars use, *man taʾammala*, comes from the same family of deep, patient looking that tadabbur asks of us. This sign is not for those who read quickly. It is for those who stop and ask: *what was that wind to them, before it came?*

It was everything the wind always is. It was the same mercy they had breathed their whole lives.

And then al-Muyassar names what changed: the wind had *no barakah in it and brought no good*. Not "no power" — the wind was immense. Not "no presence" — it arrived, filled the horizon, was unmistakable. The Quran tells us elsewhere (69:6-7) that Allah subjected this wind to 'Aad for seven nights and eight days in succession, until they fell as though they were hollow trunks of palm trees toppled to the ground. The wind came. It simply had no *barakah* in it anymore.

What is *barakah*, at its root? It is the divine quality that allows created things to give beyond their visible measure — to participate in an abundance that is not their own. A blessed harvest feeds more than its acres should produce. A blessed word reaches deeper than its syllables. A blessed relationship gives more than its two parties contain. *Barakah* is creation's channel for divine generativity — the current of divine abundance flowing through ordinary instruments.

And when *barakah* is removed — when the wind is made *ʿaqīm* — the instrument remains entirely intact. It still functions in every external sense. It still moves. It still arrives. It simply no longer carries the excess. The channel has been closed at its source.

[PAUSE]

Here is what makes this the most unsettling kind of sign. Not a plague from nowhere, not a fire from an empty sky — those you would recognize immediately as punishment. But the withdrawal of *barakah* from what is already in your hands? That you might not notice for a long time. The wind feels like wind. The relationship is still there. The health still functions. The mind still works. Until you look up and realize that something has been happening quietly — that nothing has been growing, that nothing has been conceiving, that you have been breathing a barren air for longer than you knew.

This is what 'Aad experienced, and this is what the sign asks you to turn and look at in your own life: **are the ordinary gifts still being held in gratitude?** Not just noticed — *held*. Acknowledged as loan, not possession. Received from a Giver who can receive them back.

## Theme Two: The Nation That Measured Its Strength by Itself

There is a reason 'Aad was destroyed by *air*.

The Quran is not careless about which punishment arrives for which crime. Elsewhere, when it introduces this same people, it gives us their signature: *"They said: 'Who is greater than us in strength?'"* (41:15). That was their posture — the confidence of the most powerful civilization that had ever existed in their region, the builders, the strong-bodied, the ones who looked at the world around them and saw it as a record of their own greatness rather than as evidence of the Greatness that made them possible.

Allah's answer to that question is what we are reading. He did not send them a greater army. He did not require a power proportionate to their strength. He sent them *air* — the most constant companion of their lives, the mercy so universal that the greatest among them and the least among them breathed the same of it every moment. And He made it *ʿaqīm*. And it was enough.

Look at the procession in this surah. Pharaoh, drowned in the sea. 'Aad, undone by the air around them. Thamud, destroyed by a single cry they could not withstand. The instruments differ; the principle holds. Each civilization was unmade by the very fabric of the creation they had forgotten was gift and not possession. Each was made *ramīm* by something so ordinary that its power had long since become invisible to them.

[PAUSE]

This is the specific irony of 'Aad's end that the classical commentators heard beneath the text: the *strongest* people, brought low by the *most ordinary* thing. Not by a weapon proportionate to their power. By breath. By the thing their nostrils had been drawing in since the moment they were born. The mightiest civilization of their era, and what finished them was a wind they could not name as their enemy until it had already made them *ramīm* — because it still *looked* like wind.

What they mistook was this: they had spent their lives inside a mercy so large and so constant — the fertile winds, the generous land, the great physical strength — that they had renamed it *their own nature*. Not gift. Nature. Not loan. Inheritance. They forgot the word *gift* so completely that no one among them was in the practice of saying thank you for the air they breathed.

And so the air breathed them out instead.

## Theme Three: The Sign Left for Those Who Contemplate

We are not standing in the ruins of 'Aad. We are reading about them.

That distinction matters enormously. The nations that received the punishment did not first receive the *sign* — or if they did, they did not look at it slowly enough. But Allah has given us something different: the story of what happened to them, preserved and transmitted and arranged into the very surah that swore by wind in its opening breath, so that we could hold both at once — the wind as mercy and the wind as judgment — and understand that the difference between them is not about the wind.

Al-Muyassar says it plainly: *āyāt wa ʿibar li-man taʾammala* — signs and lessons for the one who contemplates. The word for *lesson* here, *ʿibrah*, comes from a root that means to *cross over* — as in crossing from one shore to the understanding of what the shore means. A *ʿibrah* is what happens when you look at something that occurred to someone else and something in you crosses over into the understanding that reaches through time.

What is the *ʿibrah* of 'Aad for us?

Not: *be afraid of wind.* The wind in your lungs tonight is still a mercy. Nothing in these ayahs changes that.

The *ʿibrah* is more precise and more personal: **ordinary gifts are not ordinary.** They are instruments of divine generativity, carrying *barakah* they did not produce. They arrive from a Giver whose generosity can be closed. And the ones who are kept safe from the *ʿaqīm* wind are not the strongest or the most deserving — they are the ones who remained in the practice of receiving the gift as gift, of not having renamed the loan as nature.

[PAUSE]

This surah is named for the winds. It opened with an oath upon the winds that scatter, that carry burdens, that glide with ease, that apportion the command. Those winds of 51:1-4 are winds of divine mercy and order — the same divine will that disperses the rain also disperses the revelation. And the wind of 51:41 is the same wind, sent by the same Sender, no longer carrying the rain. The surah holds both possibilities within the same word because that is the truth of the wind: it is not its own. It is *arsalnā* — *We sent it* — in verse 41, just as it was sent in the surah's opening oath. Both winds are dispatched. Both are instruments. The difference is what the Sender placed inside the sending.

And the surah ends, a few verses after these two, with a command addressed directly to us: *"So flee to Allah. Indeed, I am to you from Him a clear warner."* (51:50)

To *flee to Allah* — *ففروا إلى الله* — is the only direction that has no *ʿaqīm* wind. It is the one movement that crosses toward the Source of all *barakah*, rather than away from it. Everything else — the strength, the civilization, the accumulated greatness — can be made *ʿaqīm* in a single night. The Source cannot.

## Closing: What the Ruins Remember

*Ramīm* — crumbled to powder.

Al-Jalalayn calls it *al-bālī al-mutafatit*: the decayed and disintegrated, broken past the point where fragments retain a name. What was once a wall is not broken stone — it is dust. What was once a person is not a body — it is something the desert absorbed long ago. The nafs and the māl, which between them constituted the entire architecture of a life, both gone.

But the *sign* is not *ramīm*. The sign was left standing. These two ayahs exist because Allah preserved the account — arranged it in the surah that swore by wind, placed it in a procession of destroyed nations, transmitted it across centuries to arrive in front of you, reader, who is still breathing.

You are reading because the sign was left for you.

The barren wind left nothing standing in the land of 'Aad. The story of it has stood ever since. And it stands so that you would know — before the wind arrives, while the air is still sweet and the ordinary gifts are still ordinary — what you are holding in your hands, and Whose hands are holding it.

*He sent the wind. He could send it barren. Today it is not barren. That is not nothing.*

*"And in 'Aad [was a sign]..."*

The sign is still being read. That means we are still in the time of signs. And the time of signs is the time of return — while there is yet a moment to cross over.
