# Enjoy Yourselves for a Time: When Permission Becomes a Sentence

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the words in front of you.

> **وَفِى ثَمُودَ إِذْ قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَمَتَّعُوا۟ حَتَّىٰ حِينٍ**
>
> *Wa fī Thamūda idh qīla lahum tamattaʿū ḥattā ḥīn.*
>
> "And in Thamud [was a sign], when it was said to them: 'Enjoy yourselves for a time.'"

Eight words. One sentence inside a sentence. And inside that inner sentence — the one spoken *to* Thamud — there is a single command that I want us to walk all the way around before we are done tonight: **Enjoy yourselves.**

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this verse, you have to know where it stands.

Surah Adh-Dhariyat opens with winds. Scattering winds, then clouds heavy with burden, then ships running easy on the sea, then angels distributing a divine command. Oaths sworn on things in motion. And then, after the oaths, after the warning of a Day that is coming, the surah does something deliberate. It lines up nations. Like figures in a gallery, each one mounted and labeled. The guests of Ibrahim. The people of Lut. Pharaoh and his hosts. And then — almost in a rhythm now, a drumbeat — **ʿĀd. Thamud. The people of Nuh.**

Each one is introduced with the same quiet refrain: *here is a sign.* "And in ʿĀd... and in Thamud... and in Musa..." The Arabic word is **آية** — a sign, a token, something left behind on purpose so that those who come after can read it. The surah is not telling you history for its own sake. It is holding up exhibits. *Look at this one. Now look at this one.*

And when the camera lands on Thamud, watch what it chooses to show you. It does not show you the mountains they carved their homes into — and they were master carvers, a people of stone and confidence. It does not show you the she-camel, the miracle sent through their prophet Ṣāliḥ, drinking on its appointed day. It does not even show you the moment they hamstrung her, the act of defiance that sealed everything.

The Quran skips all of it.

It shows you one thing: a sentence spoken to them *afterward*. After the camel was slaughtered. After the line was crossed. **"It was said to them: enjoy yourselves for a time."**

In the fuller account in Surah Hud, we hear the same moment with three days attached to it: *"Enjoy yourselves in your homes for three days — that is a promise not to be belied."* (11:65). Three days. The respite of Thamud. The clock had already started.

### The hook

So here is what I want you to sit with as we begin.

The word spoken to a people about to be destroyed is not "Tremble." It is not "Repent" — that window had closed. It is not even "Beware."

It is **"Enjoy."**

The kindest-sounding word in the language, handed to them at the cruelest moment in their history. A permission that is actually a verdict. A gift that is actually a clock.

How can the same word — *enjoy* — be a mercy in one mouth and a sentence in another? And who, exactly, is the one saying it? The verse is strangely careful not to tell us. [PAUSE]

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: قِيلَ — "It was said." The speaker who steps out of frame.

Let me teach you something about language before we touch the word itself.

In any language, there are two ways to report a statement. You can name the speaker — *"The judge said: you are sentenced."* Or you can hide the speaker — *"It was said: he is sentenced."* English does this with the passive voice. Arabic does it too, and it has a special, compact way of doing it: it changes the *vowels inside the verb itself.*

The active form would be **قَالَ** — *qāla*, "he said." But our verse does not say *qāla.* It says **قِيلَ** — *qīla.* Same three letters, same root, **q-w-l**. But the internal vowel has shifted from an *a* to an *i*, and that small shift does something large: it surgically removes the speaker from the sentence. This is what Arabic grammarians call the **fiʿl majhūl** — the verb whose doer is, literally, *made unknown.* The very name of the construction means "the unknown."

So the Quran does not say "Ṣāliḥ said to them, enjoy." It does not say "We said to them, enjoy." It does not say "the angel said." It says: **قِيلَ لَهُمْ** — *it was said to them.* The agent has stepped out of the frame.

Now — let's sit with this. Why would the Quran do that?

Notice something first. The Quran is perfectly capable of naming Allah as the speaker when it wants the weight of His voice to land. All through the Book: *"We said... I am... your Lord says."* The naming is a choice. And so the *not-naming* is also a choice. The veil over the speaker here is deliberate.

Here is what that veil accomplishes. When you are still in the conversation with someone — still being addressed, still being argued with, still being warned face to face — there is a *you* and a *me* in the room. Relationship is alive. But when the verdict comes down in the passive voice — *it was said* — the warmth of direct address is gone. The accused is no longer being spoken *with*. A sentence is simply being *pronounced over* them.

Think of the difference between a doctor who sits with you, holds the conversation, says *"I want us to try something"* — and the cold line on a chart: *"Patient to be discharged."* The second one isn't crueler in its words. It's crueler in its *grammar.* The person has become a case. The dialogue has ended. [PAUSE]

That is what قِيلَ does to Thamud. Earlier in their story there was conversation — a prophet among them, pleading, reasoning, a she-camel as a living sign, back-and-forth, *"O my people, worship Allah."* Direct address. Living relationship. By the time we reach *qīla lahum,* all of that is over. The named speaker has withdrawn. What remains is a verdict in the air, spoken by no one they can argue with anymore.

The hidden speaker is not a gap in information. **It is the sound of a door that has already closed.**

And there is a second thing the passive does, quieter still. By refusing to name who said it, the verse makes the command feel like it is coming from *everywhere and nowhere* — from the structure of reality itself. Not one voice they could resist or bargain with, but the simple, settled fact of their fate, now spoken into the world. *It was said.* As if the universe itself had been informed.

What does this single vowel-shift reveal? That the most frightening thing is not being shouted at by God. **It is being talked about instead of talked to.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: تَمَتَّعُوا — "Enjoy yourselves." The shape of the word betrays the gift.

Now to the command itself. And here I need you to look not at the meaning first, but at the *shape* of the word — because in Arabic, the shape of a verb carries meaning the way the cut of a key carries the lock it will open.

Let me teach the concept. Arabic verbs come in patterns, called *forms* — ten of them in common use. The plain, basic form is Form I. But you can take a root and *re-mold* it into heavier forms that add meaning: intensity, causation, mutual action, and — the one we need — **reflexivity**, where the action curves back onto the one doing it.

The root here is **m-t-ʿ** (متع). In its noun, **matāʿ**, it means provision, goods, the stuff of enjoyment — but with a specific flavor the lexicons are unanimous about: it is *provision that runs out.* Temporary supply. The food you take for a journey, precisely because the journey ends. The Quran uses this very word elsewhere to define the entire dunya: *"the enjoyment of this worldly life is little"* (مَتَاعُ الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا قَلِيلٌ, 4:77), and *"the life of this world is nothing but the matāʿ of delusion"* (3:185). The root itself, before any sentence is built, already whispers: *this does not last.*

Now watch what form the verse puts it in. Not the plain form. It is **تَمَتَّعُوا** — *tamattaʿū* — **Form V**, the *tafaʿʿala* pattern, with the doubled middle letter and the *ta-* prefix curling the action back onto the doers. Form V is the reflexive, the self-involved form. It does not mean merely "have enjoyment." It means **"take your enjoyment for yourselves," "immerse yourselves in pleasure," "go on and indulge."**

Stay with what that combination is doing. The root says *temporary.* The form says *throw yourselves into it.* Put them together and the command is: **"Plunge, completely, into the thing that is about to run out."**

That is not a description. That is a portrait of every soul that has ever chosen the immediate over the eternal. [PAUSE]

And here is the part that should stop us. Grammatically, تَمَتَّعُوا is an **imperative** — a command. *Enjoy!* In the language of commands, an imperative is normally permission, invitation, even generosity. *Eat. Drink. Be welcome.* When Allah tells the people of Paradise *"Eat and drink in satisfaction"* (kulū wa-shrabū, 69:24), the imperative is pure honor.

So why does the *same grammatical mood* — a command to enjoy — feel like ice water here?

Because an imperative only carries warmth when the relationship behind it is warm. The command to "enjoy" in Paradise comes from a Lord who has *just welcomed* His servant in. The command to "enjoy" given to Thamud comes wrapped in the passive voice we just examined — *it was said* — after the conversation has ended. **Same form. Opposite meaning. The difference is entirely in the relationship the words sit inside.**

This is what the classical scholars are circling when they comment on this verse. Look at how they read it, each adding a layer:

- Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī are spare and devastating: *tamattaʿū ḥattā ḥīn* means "until your appointed terms run out" — and al-Baghawī ties it directly to Hud: after they hamstrung the camel, *"it was said to them: enjoy for three days."* The enjoyment *is* the countdown.
- Al-Muyassar fills in the silence that follows the command: "*they disobeyed the order of their Lord, so the thunderbolt of punishment seized them while they looked on at their own punishment with their own eyes.*" The respite was spent in defiance, not return.
- Al-Qurṭubī adds a second camera angle worth holding. The mainstream reading is "live, enjoying the world, *until the moment of destruction* — three days." But he preserves a minority reading: that *tamattaʿū* could mean *"accept Islam and enjoy until your terms naturally end."* In other words, even classical scholars heard, faintly, a version where this word could have been a doorway and not only a sentence.

Hold those together — not as a multiple-choice question, but as one composite truth. **The word "enjoy" was a fork in the road that had already been walked past.** It *could* have been an invitation to a life of submission. By the time it was spoken to Thamud, it had collapsed into a clock. And what determined which one it was? Not the word. The word was identical. What determined it was whether the door of the relationship was still open — and they had slammed it shut with their own hands on the body of the camel.

What does تَمَتَّعُوا reveal? That **the gift of pleasure is morally neutral until you see what it is attached to.** Attached to a living relationship with the Giver, "enjoy" is honor. Attached to a closed door, the exact same "enjoy" is the most terrible sentence a soul can receive: *go ahead — take your fill of the thing that ends.* [PAUSE]

### Element Three: حِينٍ — "a time." The terror hidden in the missing number.

We come to the last word, and it is the smallest, and I think it is the sharpest.

**حَتَّىٰ حِينٍ** — *ḥattā ḥīn* — "until a time," "for a time."

Let me teach the grammatical point, because everything turns on it. In Arabic, a noun can be *definite* — "**the** time," al-ḥīn, a specific, pointed-at moment — or it can be **indefinite**, marked by that little *-in* ending you hear at the end: *ḥīn-in.* That tanwīn, that *-in,* makes the noun *vague.* Not "the appointed hour." Just — **"a time." Some time. A while.**

Now. We *know,* from Surah Hud, that the actual term was three days. Precise. Countable. *"Three days — a promise not to be belied"* (11:65). The number existed. Allah knew it. Ṣāliḥ announced it to them with that very precision.

So ask the question the grammar forces on us: **here, in Adh-Dhariyat, why is the number removed?** Why does this verse say only "*until a time*" — an unspecified, indefinite, blurred-out *some-while* — when the Quran elsewhere gives the exact count?

Read the absence carefully. Because this is the verse where Allah is showing us Thamud as a **sign for us** — *and in Thamud was a sign.* And the lesson is not really about three specific days in a vanished Arabian valley. The lesson is about **every soul that has ever been told "enjoy, for a time" without being told how long the time is.** [PAUSE]

That is *us.* That is every one of us, right now.

You and I have been told, in effect, *tamattaʿū ḥattā ḥīn* — enjoy, for a time. And the time is **ḥīn-in.** Indefinite. Unmarked. No number. We do not know if our *ḥīn* is fifty more years or the space between this breath and the next. The blurring of the number is not vagueness for its own sake. **The blurring is the point.** It transfers Thamud's situation directly onto the listener. Thamud had three days they could count. We have a *ḥīn* we cannot count — which is, if you really feel it, *more* frightening, not less. They at least could number their respite. Ours is hidden from us entirely.

And notice the cruel grace folded into that. The very fact that you do not know the length of your *ḥīn* is the only reason repentance is still on the table for you. Thamud, once the three days were *named,* were past the point of return — the naming of the term was the sealing of the verdict. Your term is *unnamed.* Which means, for you, the door that closed on Thamud is — *right now* — still open. The indefinite *ḥīn* that should terrify you is the same indefinite *ḥīn* that gives you room to turn back. [PAUSE]

One more thing about this little word. *Ḥattā* means "until." It points to an endpoint. Every "enjoy" in this verse is fenced. There is no *tamattaʿū* in the Quran's account of the destroyed nations that runs forever. The pleasure always has *ḥattā* stitched to its hem — *until.* The enjoyment is real, but it is *bounded,* and the boundary is named in the same breath as the gift. To be told "enjoy *until*" is to be handed a cup and told, in the same sentence, that it has a bottom.

What does حِينٍ reveal? That **the most honest thing the Quran can tell you about your pleasures is that they have an "until" attached — and that the kindest cruelty is to hide the exact hour, so that you live every hour as if it might be the boundary.** [PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

Step back and see what these three words have done together.

**قِيلَ** showed us a speaker who has stepped out of the frame — the chilling silence of being talked *about* instead of talked *to.*

**تَمَتَّعُوا** showed us a command shaped like a gift and weighted like a sentence — "plunge into the thing that ends" — neutral until you see whether the door behind it is open or shut.

**حِينٍ** showed us a deliberately blurred clock — a respite with the number rubbed out, so that Thamud's three days could land squarely on our own unmeasured lives.

Three words. One mechanism. **The grammar of respite.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange mercy of being given *time.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The two faces of being left alone

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of silence we all know. You've done something wrong — said the cruel thing, broken the promise, crossed a line with someone who loves you. And at first they argue. They push back. They cry, they raise their voice, they *engage.* That stage, painful as it is, is the warm stage. The relationship is still being fought for.

And then one day — they go quiet. Not the explosive quiet of a held breath. The other quiet. The settled one. They stop correcting you. They stop expecting anything. They become *polite.* And some part of you goes cold, because you understand, without being told, that the engagement has ended. They are no longer trying to change you. They have simply... let you be.

Every human being knows that the second silence is worse than the first. **Being argued with is a form of love. Being left alone can be a form of judgment.** [PAUSE]

This is precisely what the Quran names, in its own vocabulary, in the verses surrounding this one and across the Book. There is a *respite* that is mercy — Allah gives time so the servant can turn back, and the Quran is full of this: *"Your Lord is full of forgiveness... He gives respite."* And there is a *respite* that is something else entirely. The Quran calls it **istidrāj** — being drawn out, lured forward by degrees, *through the very gifts you mistake for approval.* "We will draw them in by degrees from where they do not know. And I will give them respite — indeed, My plan is firm" (7:182-183). And: *"Let not those who disbelieve think that the respite We give them is good for their souls. We only give them time so they may increase in sin"* (3:178).

Do you hear it? *The respite given to Thamud is istidrāj in its purest, most compressed form.* "Enjoy yourselves for a time" is the *theology of being left alone,* spoken out loud. It is what the second silence sounds like when God says it.

And here is the movement I want you to make with me, because it transforms everything. The terrifying part of istidrāj is that **from the inside, it feels exactly like mercy.** Thamud's three days did not feel like a sentence. They felt like a reprieve. The sun still rose. The food was still sweet. The homes still stood, carved into the mountains, magnificent. Nothing in the *experience* of those three days announced what they were. The enjoyment was real. Only the door behind it had changed.

So the question that should rise in you — the one the whole verse is engineered to provoke — is this: **how do I know which respite I am living in?** When my life is going well, when the provision flows and the days are pleasant and nothing pushes back — is that the warm silence of a Lord pleased with me, or the cold silence of being left to my *ḥīn*?

The Quran's answer is not a feeling. It is a *direction.* The respite of mercy and the respite of istidrāj feel identical from inside the pleasure. They differ only in *which way you are facing while you enjoy it.* Thamud spent their three days, in al-Muyassar's words, *disobeying the command of their Lord* — facing away. The believer is handed the same indefinite *ḥīn,* the same daily pleasures, the same unmarked clock — and spends it *facing back toward the Giver.* Same gift. Same "enjoy." Same "until." The entire difference is the orientation of the heart that receives it. [PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Stop reading your comfort as a verdict. A pleasant life is not evidence that Allah is pleased with you, and a hard life is not evidence that He is not. Thamud had three beautiful days. The only thing that ever told you which respite you are in is not how good it feels — it is *which direction you turn while it lasts.* So tomorrow, take one ordinary pleasure — your coffee, your child's laugh, the ease of your own breathing — and consciously turn it back toward the Giver with a single "alhamdulillah" that *means* it. That one act is the difference, in miniature, between the two silences.

### Theme Two: Time itself is the test, and you are inside the three days

Now let me take you into the deepest water.

We tend to think the test of life is made of *events* — the big trials, the moral choices, the moments of crisis. But this verse quietly proposes something more radical. It suggests that **time itself is the substance of the test.** Not what happens *in* the time. The time.

Look again at what Thamud were actually given. Not a trial of events — the events were over, the camel already slaughtered, the verdict already reached. What they were given was *raw duration.* Three days of pure, undirected time, with a single instruction: *enjoy.* No new prophet. No new sign. No fresh argument. Just — time, and what they would do with it.

And here is the thing about being handed pure time: **it reveals you, because there is nothing else in it but you.** A crisis can make a person rise to an occasion they don't normally inhabit. But empty, granted, unstructured time? That shows what a person *is* when nothing is forcing their hand. Thamud, given three days and told to enjoy, used them to keep facing away. The time didn't change them. The time *exposed* them.

Now feel the weight of the missing number again. You — right now — have been handed exactly this. Not three days. A *ḥīn.* An indefinite span, unmarked, running. And the instruction over your life, in the most literal sense, is the same: you have been permitted to enjoy. The provision is laid out. The dunya is *matāʿ,* genuine if temporary supply. **You are, at this very moment, inside your three days.** You simply cannot see the number on them. [PAUSE]

Let me push one layer further, into the paradox, and I'm going to let it sit unresolved because it is meant to.

The same fact — *you don't know your ḥīn* — is at once the most frightening thing about your life and the most merciful. Frightening, because it could end mid-sentence, mid-plan, mid-breath, with no warning and no posted count. Merciful, because *that very not-knowing is what keeps your door open.* Thamud's tragedy was sealed at the moment the number was *spoken* — three days, named, fixed. The naming was the closing. You have *not* been given your number. Which means — and let this land — **the kindest thing Allah has done for you is to hide from you the length of your respite.** If you knew you had forty years, you would squander thirty-nine of them. If you knew you had a week, you would break under it. The hidden *ḥīn* is the precise condition under which a soul can keep turning back, every single day, because every single day might be the boundary and might not be. The not-knowing *is* the mercy. [PAUSE]

So the difference between Thamud and you is not the situation. The situation is identical: enjoy, for a time, the provision that ends. The difference is one of *grammar,* and we already found it in Part One. Thamud were spoken about — *qīla lahum,* it was said to them, the door shut, the speaker withdrawn. You are still being spoken *to.* This verse, right now, in your ears, is the proof. The fact that these words are *reaching* you — that you are still being addressed, still being warned, still inside the conversation — is itself the evidence that your respite is not yet istidrāj. **A soul that is still being warned has not yet been left alone.** The most reassuring thing in this entire terrifying verse is that you are *hearing it.* Thamud are not the warning to fear. Thamud are the warning you have been *graciously given* — which is the one thing they were not. [PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Treat your time as the test, not just your deeds. Tonight, before sleep, do the small brutal arithmetic that Thamud could not avoid and that we spend our lives avoiding: *I do not know the number on my days.* Then do one thing with the next unit of time — the next hour, the next morning — that you would do if you knew it were near the boundary. Not dramatic. Maybe a prayer you've been delaying, a forgiveness you've been withholding, a name you've been meaning to call. Let the unmarked *ḥīn* press you toward *one* turning, today, while the door — *because you are still hearing this* — is open.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now that we have done the work, let me show you how this verse is built — because the structure *is* the argument.

Stand back and look at the eight words as a single mechanism:

**وَفِى ثَمُودَ** — *the exhibit.* "And in Thamud [a sign]." A people held up for us to read.

**إِذْ قِيلَ لَهُمْ** — *the closed door.* "When it was said to them." The passive voice withdraws the speaker. The conversation has ended. They are now talked *about.*

**تَمَتَّعُوا** — *the gift that is a sentence.* "Enjoy yourselves." A command shaped like generosity, weighted like a verdict, made of a root that means *the thing that runs out.*

**حَتَّىٰ حِينٍ** — *the blurred clock.* "For a time." The number rubbed out, so the respite lands on every listener who does not know the length of their own.

Do you see the shape? It moves from a *sign held up for us,* through a *door closing on them,* to a *gift that conceals a clock* — and then it stops, mid-air, on an *unmarked time* that is deliberately left hanging. The verse does not tell you what happened next. It does not narrate the thunderbolt. **It ends on the open-ended word** — *ḥīn* — because the verse wants to leave *you* standing inside the respite, not watching their destruction from a safe distance. The architecture refuses you the comfort of the ending. It leaves you in the three days. [PAUSE]

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The *grammar* showed us a speaker who has withdrawn (*qīla*), a gift that is neutral until you see the door behind it (*tamattaʿū*), and a clock with the number erased (*ḥīn*). The *themes* showed us that being left alone has two faces, that comfort is no verdict, that time itself is the test, and that you are — at this moment — inside your own unmarked respite.

They meet in one place: **whether "enjoy, for a time" is a mercy or a sentence is decided not by the words, which are identical for the saved and the doomed, but by whether the door of relationship is open while you receive them — and the proof that yours is still open is that you are still being spoken to.**

### Questions to carry

1. When my life is comfortable, do I read it as Allah's pleasure — and when it is hard, as His displeasure? What would it change to stop reading my circumstances as verdicts at all?
2. If I knew the number on my *ḥīn* — if I knew the count of my days — what is the *one* thing I would stop doing tonight, and the one thing I would start? And why am I not doing them, given that I *don't* know the number?
3. There is a silence I have mistaken for peace in my life. Is it the warm silence of being at rest with Allah, or the cold silence of being left to my pleasures? How would I tell the difference — and am I willing to look?
4. What is my *matāʿ* — the temporary provision I have plunged into as though it would last? What would it mean to enjoy it *facing the Giver* instead of facing away?
5. The fact that this verse reached me is evidence I am still being warned, still being addressed. Do I receive that as the mercy it is — or do I keep treating the warning as something for other people, for Thamud, for the destroyed and the distant?

### One-sentence distillation

*To be told "enjoy yourselves for a time" is either the warmest gift or the coldest sentence — and the only difference is whether you are still facing the One who said it, which, since you are still hearing these words, you still can be.*

### The closing invitation

I am not going to ask you to be afraid. Fear is what Thamud felt for three measured days, and it did not save them. I am going to ask you for something quieter and harder: to notice that you are *inside* the respite right now, that the number on your days is mercifully hidden, and that the door — *because these words are still reaching you* — has not closed. Let that not be information. Let it be a turning. One turning, today, toward the Giver, while the *ḥīn* is still unmarked and the conversation is still open.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, You who hid from us the number of our days as a mercy and not a cruelty — do not let our ease deceive us, and do not leave us alone with our pleasures. Keep us inside the conversation with You; keep speaking to us, and keep us turning back. And let the "enjoy, for a time" that You have written over our lives be, for us, a doorway and not a verdict — until we meet You with our faces turned toward You, and not away.* Āmīn.
