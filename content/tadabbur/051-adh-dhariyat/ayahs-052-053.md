# Did They Pass Down a Script?

## The Universal Grammar of Rejection

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the two verses first, and then I want to take you somewhere before we ever open them.

> **كَذَٰلِكَ مَآ أَتَى ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِم مِّن رَّسُولٍ إِلَّا قَالُوا۟ سَاحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ**
>
> *Kadhālika mā atā alladhīna min qablihim min rasūlin illā qālū sāḥirun aw majnūn*
>
> "Likewise, no messenger came to those before them but they said: 'A magician, or a madman.'"

> **أَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِهِۦ ۚ بَلْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ طَاغُونَ**
>
> *Atawāṣaw bihi — bal hum qawmun ṭāghūn*
>
> "Did they hand this down to one another? Rather, they are a people who have overflowed every bound."

Now close the book for a moment and come with me.

### Rebuilding the world

Imagine a man in Makkah. He has spent years saying one thing — *there is no god but Allah* — and for that one sentence he has lost his standing in his own city. The people who used to call him *al-Amīn*, the trustworthy, the one they left their valuables with, now cross the street when they see him. They have a name for him now. Two names, actually. In the morning, in the marketplace, someone leans toward a visiting trader and says, *don't listen to him — he's a sorcerer, he splits families with his words.* And by the afternoon, a different voice in a different corner says, *don't trouble yourself — the man's lost his mind.*

He carries this home. Not the bruises — the **labels**. Because a bruise heals and a label follows you into the room before you arrive. And there is a particular loneliness in being misnamed by people who once knew exactly who you were.

Now widen the lens. Step back through time. Stand at the door of Nūḥ, who called his people for nearly a thousand years. Listen at the threshold of Mūsā before Pharaoh's court. Walk the ruins of ʿĀd and Thamūd, whose stories Allah has just finished telling in this very surah. And listen — *really* listen — to what each people said when their messenger arrived.

**Sorcerer. Or madman.**

The same two words. In different languages. Across centuries that never touched. By peoples who never met, never corresponded, never sat in the same room. Egyptians and the people of Hūd and the Quraysh of Makkah — separated by oceans of time — reaching, every single time, for the *identical* two accusations.

### The hook

And here is the question this surah is about to put to you. A question so sharp it should stop you where you sit.

**If these peoples never met — who taught them all to say the same thing?**

[PAUSE]

Did they leave each other a will? Did one generation of deniers write it down and pass it forward — *here, son, when the next one comes claiming revelation, here are the two words to use*? Is there a hidden manuscript, handed across the centuries, that explains why the rejection of truth has always had the *exact same grammar*?

Allah is about to ask that question out loud. And then He is going to answer it. And the answer is going to tell you something about your own heart that you did not come here expecting to hear.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Kadhālika* — the word that turns a wound into a lineage

Let me teach you a small thing about Arabic before we touch the verse.

There is a word, **كَذَٰلِكَ** — *kadhālika* — and we usually translate it flatly as "thus" or "likewise" or "in the same way." It is built from the particle of resemblance, the *kāf* of comparison — the same little tool you use when you say *this is like that.* When you put it at the front of a sentence, you are reaching backward and forward at once: you are saying *what is about to happen here matches something already known.*

Now notice where Allah places it. **It is the very first word.** Before the messengers are mentioned. Before the accusation is quoted. The first thing out is: *Likewise.* *Just like this.*

Like *what*?

Here is what the classical commentators saw, and they are in agreement on it. Al-Qurṭubī says plainly: this is **تسلية للنبي** — *tasliya li-n-Nabī*, a consolation for the Prophet ﷺ. Al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn say the same: *just as your people called you a sorcerer and a madman, so did the peoples before them call their messengers.* The "likewise" reaches back over the head of the man in Makkah and connects his wound to a chain.

And al-Tabari is precise about which peoples, exactly, the "those before them" refers to — not an abstract archive of history but the very nations whose stories this surah just finished narrating: Nūḥ's people, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh and his court. The surah walked the Prophet ﷺ, and us, through their rubble just verses earlier. The "likewise" does not reach into some distant, unnamed past. It reaches back to the procession the Quran itself just conducted — the ruins still visible on the page.

Sit with what that does.

The Prophet ﷺ is being insulted. And the *first word* Allah gives him is not "don't worry" and not "they will be punished" — though that comes. The first word is **kadhālika**: *this is familiar.* *This belongs to a pattern.* *You are not the exception; you are the latest link.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. When you are suffering, the cruelest feeling is not the pain — it is the suspicion that the pain is *yours alone*. That something is uniquely wrong with you. That if you were only a little better, a little more eloquent, a little more likeable, this would not be happening. Isolation is what makes suffering unbearable. The mind whispers: *everyone else manages to be accepted — why can't you?*

And *kadhālika* reaches directly into that whisper and silences it. It does not remove the rejection. It **re-files** it. It takes the experience out of the drawer marked "your personal failure" and puts it in the drawer marked "the cost every truth-bearer has paid." The insult is the same insult Nūḥ heard. The same insult Mūsā heard. You did not earn it by being deficient. **You earned it by standing where the messengers stood.**

Here is what is remarkable. With one word — the *first* word — Allah turns a wound into a **lineage**. The very thing that made the Prophet ﷺ feel alone becomes the proof he is in the noblest company that ever walked the earth.

The insult, it turns out, is a credential.

[PAUSE — let that land]

### Element Two: *Mā ... illā* — the lock that leaves no exception

Now look at the architecture of the sentence itself, because Allah has built it with a very particular tool, and the tool is the message.

Let me teach it to you first in plain terms. In Arabic — and in English too — there is a way of speaking that works by **shutting a door and then opening one tiny window.** You say: *Nobody came to the party… except Zayd.* What have you done? You have first denied *everyone* — slammed the door on the whole world — and then carved out a single exception. Grammarians call this *al-istithnāʾ al-mufarragh*, the "emptied-out exception." And it is the **strongest** way Arabic has of saying *this and nothing but this.* Because you didn't just say "Zayd came." You said "the entire universe of people is excluded — and into that total emptiness, only Zayd." It is exclusivity with the force of a vault closing.

Now hear the verse with that in your ear:

**مَآ أَتَى ... مِن رَّسُولٍ إِلَّا قَالُوا**

*"No messenger came… except they said."*

Do you feel the shape of it? Allah does not say "most peoples rejected their messengers." He does not say "many said sorcerer or madman." He builds the vault: **mā** — *no* — slams the door on every messenger who ever came. And then the only window He opens is: **illā qālū** — *except that they said it.*

Not one exception. Not one people in all of history who received their messenger and said, *finally, the truth, welcome.* Every single time, the door of rejection.

[PAUSE]

And look — there is one more thing hidden here, a single letter most translations cannot carry. The verse says **min rasūlin** — *min* before "messenger." In Arabic that little *min* is not needed for the grammar; it is an **extra** *min*, what the grammarians call *min zāʾida*, and its entire job is to drive the negation deeper. It takes "no messenger" and turns it into "not so much as a *single, solitary* messenger — not one of any kind whatsoever." It is the difference between "no one came" and "not a soul came." It seals the vault from the inside.

So what is the unspoken thing this construction reveals?

Here is what it invites us to consider. The uniformity is **total**. And total uniformity is itself a clue. When something happens *occasionally*, you can explain it by circumstance — this people was harsh, that prophet was unlucky. But when something happens *every single time, without one exception, across every culture and century* — circumstance cannot explain it anymore. A pattern that admits no exception is not produced by the *outside*. It is produced by something constant on the **inside**. The construction is already pointing past the messengers, past the cultures, toward a single shared cause that Allah has not yet named.

The grammar has set the trap. Now the next verse springs it.

### Element Three: *Atawāṣaw* — did they leave each other a will?

This is the hinge of the whole passage. One word. Let me walk into it slowly.

First the concept. Arabic verbs come in patterns, and there is one pattern — the grammarians call it **Form VI**, the *tafāʿala* shape — whose specific job is **mutual, reciprocal action**. Two or more parties doing something *to and with each other.* When you take the root for "fighting" and put it in Form VI, you don't get "he fought" — you get *they fought one another.* When you take "helping," you get *they helped each other.* The shape itself means *back-and-forth between parties.*

Now the root here is **و ص ي** — *w-ṣ-y* — and on its own it means to *enjoin, to counsel, to leave as a binding instruction.* It is the root of *waṣiyya* — a **last will and testament.** The thing a dying person hands forward to those who come after: *this is what I leave you. Remember it. Pass it on.*

Put the root into Form VI and prefix the questioning *hamza*, and you get this astonishing word:

**أَتَوَاصَوْا**

*Atawāṣaw.*

"Did they **bequeath it to one another**? Did they leave each other a *will* about it? Did one generation sit the next one down and say — *here is the inheritance; when the messenger comes, these are the two words you use*?"

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why the introduction stood you in front of all those scattered peoples? This is the question made into a single verb. Allah is voicing the *exact wonder* a thinking person feels: peoples who never met, who could not have coordinated, who were separated by the width of the world and the depth of time — and yet the script is **identical.** Sorcerer. Or madman. Every time. How?

The question is what the grammarians call *istifhām inkārī* — a question that is not seeking information but **denying a premise.** It carries amazement and rebuke folded together. It is the tone of a judge holding up two confessions written in different cities, in different decades, by people who never met — and the wording is *word for word the same* — and asking the courtroom: *"What, did they conspire? Did they pass this down?"* And everyone in the room already knows: **they couldn't have.** There was no manuscript. There was no council. There was no will handed across the centuries.

And that is the genius of the question. By asking *"did they coordinate?"* and making the impossibility of coordination obvious, Allah forces your mind to the only door left. If it wasn't passed *between* them from the outside… then it must rise *within* them from a common source on the inside.

The question has emptied every external explanation. And the something on the inside, as the next verse will name it, has a word.

---

*The grammar has done its work. The vault that admits no exception. The will that was never written. We have been shown a pattern too perfect to be coincidence and too uncoordinated to be conspiracy. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: *Saaḥir aw majnūn* — the two doors we run to when the truth indicts us

Begin where you live.

Picture someone you love sitting you down to tell you a hard truth about yourself. Maybe it's about how you've been treating people. Maybe it's about something you keep avoiding. They are calm, they are clear, and what they are saying is *true* — some part of you knows it's true before they finish the sentence.

Now watch what your mind does in the next half-second. Watch closely, because it happens faster than thought.

Your mind reaches for one of two handles. Either: *"What's their angle? Why are they really saying this? They're manipulating me, they want something, this is a guilt trip."* Or: *"They're overreacting. They're being dramatic. They don't know what they're talking about."*

Notice those are the **only two doors.** Door one: *the messenger is too clever — he has an agenda, he's playing me.* Door two: *the messenger is not credible — he's irrational, ignore him.* One door attacks the speaker's **integrity.** The other attacks the speaker's **sanity.** And through whichever door you run, you arrive at the same destination: **you never have to deal with what they actually said.**

Now look back at the verse.

**سَاحِرٌ أَوْ مَجْنُونٌ.** Sorcerer. Or madman.

[PAUSE]

A *sāḥir*, a sorcerer, in that world, was someone with *too much* power over minds — dangerously persuasive, manipulating hidden forces, bending people with his words. *"Don't listen to him — he's working you."* The accusation says: the problem isn't his message, it's his *method*. He's reaching into places ordinary speech doesn't reach. He's deploying something you can't name and can't defend against. And notice — this accusation is self-sealing. Once you believe someone is a sorcerer, every compelling word becomes further proof of his sorcery. The more powerfully the truth lands, the more evidence it is that he is manipulating you. The accusation cannot be falsified by the very thing that would falsify it.

And a *majnūn* — the word built on the root *jnn*, the root of what is hidden and veiled, the root from which *jinn* also comes — is one whose reason has been overtaken, whose rational faculty has been eclipsed. Not lying, not strategizing, just *gone*. You don't argue with a *majnūn*. You don't bring his words to the family table for serious consideration. You don't feel guilty for not engaging him; he cannot be held fully responsible for what comes out of a mind that has broken. The *majnūn* accusation releases you not just from engagement but from the *obligation* to engage.

[PAUSE]

See the architecture together. One label makes the messenger *too much* — too powerful, too clever, too dangerous, accessing forces beyond the ordinary. The other makes him *too little* — not even reliably rational, not in full command of his own faculties. Between those two extremes, there is no space left for the messenger to be what he actually is: a sane human being carrying a true thing that calls you to account.

That is the point. The two labels do not need to be consistent with each other. In the morning he is a cunning sorcerer; by the afternoon he is a broken madman. They don't even need to be simultaneously believed. They are not descriptions — they are **exits.** And they are complete. Between them they cover every possible route by which you might feel obligated to take his message seriously, and they close every one.

Al-Tabari records that the Quraysh of Makkah deployed a third label alongside these two — they also called the Prophet ﷺ a *shāʿir*, a poet: a man of beautiful fictions rather than binding truth, someone who creates moving arrangements of words without claiming that those words come from beyond himself. But the Quran, in these two ayahs, reaches past that third accusation. "Poet" was culturally specific — it had a particular resonance in a world that prized and feared the *sāʿir* as almost supernaturally gifted. "Sorcerer or madman" needed no cultural translation. They were the accusations every people in every age reached for regardless of what they thought of poets or storytellers. They are, as the verse's very architecture has already shown us, the *universal* grammar of dismissal — the two labels that transcend language, time, and culture, because they address something deeper than culture: the two ways a human mind can explain away a truth-bearer without engaging what he brought.

[PAUSE]

Two labels. Two exits. The vault shut every time. In every language, across every century, among every people that ever received a messenger from Allah.

Now the verse has shown you the pattern. The next verse will ask you the only honest question that pattern raises. And then it will answer it.

### Theme Two: *Bal hum qawmun ṭāghūn* — the answer that was inside the question all along

Here is how the Quran handles the most astonishing mystery in these two verses.

It doesn't explain it slowly. It doesn't build toward it with qualifications. It asks the question — short, sharp, impossibly concise:

**أَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِهِ**

*"Did they bequeath it to one another?"*

And then, before you have finished blinking, it answers:

**بَلْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ طَاغُونَ**

*"Rather, they are a people who have overflowed every bound."*

[PAUSE]

The particle *bal* — **بل** — is what the grammarians call an *iḍrāb*, an Arabic rhetorical pivot. It doesn't merely introduce the answer; it sweeps the previous hypothesis off the table entirely. Not "no, that's wrong, and the actual reason is..." — that formulation is too slow for *bal*. *Bal* works like a hand clearing a chessboard: *that whole frame is not the frame. Here is the frame.* The question of whether there was a will is not only answered — it is *dismissed as the wrong question.* Stop looking outside. Stop looking between the generations. Stop looking for a manuscript. Turn and look within.

And what *bal* turns us toward is a single word: **طَاغُونَ** — *ṭāghūn*.

Let me give you the root. It comes from *ṭ-gh-y* — **ط غ ي** — and the image embedded in the classical lexicons is water that has overflowed its banks. The Nile at flood is described with this root: it has exceeded its natural limit, broken through every boundary designed to contain it, and gone past every marker set to hold it in its place. Applied to a human being, it describes someone who has overflowed every limit that God, reason, conscience, and covenant have placed on a person — a transgressor who has exceeded his bounds in every direction, and who can no longer be contained by truth because he has already broken the vessel that would be needed to receive it.

[PAUSE — let that image settle]

Now here is what classical commentators saw in the mechanism behind this word. Al-Muyassar states it with notable precision: *"Did the earlier and later generations bequeath rejection to one another when they all said this? Rather, they are transgressors — their hearts and their deeds grew alike in kufr and in tughyan. And so the later ones said what the earlier ones said, not because the earlier ones left them instructions, but because they reached the same inner state."*

*Tashābahat qulūbuhum.* **Their hearts grew alike.**

[PAUSE]

This is the answer the whole passage has been building toward, and it is breathtaking in its simplicity.

The peoples who called their messengers sorcerers and madmen were not connected by a manuscript. They were not connected by a conspiracy. They were connected by a **condition.** When a human heart fills past the boundary — when transgression becomes the defining fact of what a person is — a specific thing happens to that person's relationship to truth. He can no longer receive it on its own terms. He can only defend against it. And defense, when the heart is in that state, always reaches for the same two handles. Either the messenger is dangerously more than human (sāḥir) or pitiably less than human (majnūn). Both explanations route around the message. Both protect the self from having to answer what was actually said.

And because tughyan — this overflow, this exceeding of every boundary — produces the same inner architecture in every soul that travels far enough down that road, the reaction it generates is identical across every century and culture. Not because anyone taught it. Not because it was passed down. But because when hearts grow alike in transgression, they reach for the same words.

[PAUSE]

So the question *atawāṣaw* — *did they leave each other a will?* — is answered not with "no, it was merely coincidence" but with something far more sobering: it was *convergence.* Not the convergence of information passing between people, but the convergence of a spiritual state developing within them. The same overflow, in the same direction, past the same limits — producing, in every vessel it fills, the same reaction to the same truth.

This is why the *mā ... min rasūlin illā qālū* vault admits no exception. The vault is total because tughyan, when it reaches a certain depth, generates a gravitational pull toward these two exits — and every people that traveled far enough down the road of transgression arrived at the same door. There is no external explanation for the pattern. There is only the internal explanation: the same soil, in every era, in every tongue, growing the same fruit.

**The manuscript doesn't exist. The coordination never happened. The grammar of rejection is not passed down — it grows up.**

[PAUSE — let that settle fully before continuing]

### Theme Three: What the *tasliya* actually gives, and what it asks in return

Now watch what the consolation does once you understand the full mechanism.

It would have been enough for Allah to say: *"Don't be sad — other prophets were rejected too."* That is comfort of a kind. But it is a blunt comfort. It addresses the pain without explaining the pain. It says *you're not alone* without telling you *why* you're not alone, or what the accusations of the rejectors reveal about *them* — or about any of us.

What these two ayahs offer is something more precise. Something that functions not just as comfort but as **re-framing at the level of understanding.**

First: *kadhālika* tells the Prophet ﷺ this experience is familiar — he is the latest link in a chain, not an isolated case.

Second: *mā ... min rasūlin illā qālū* tells him the pattern is total — not one exception across all of history, which means this is not about his particular delivery, or his specific community's character, or anything he could have adjusted.

Third: *atawāṣaw* disposes of the only external hypothesis — maybe they coordinated, maybe they learned from each other — and clears it from the room.

And fourth: *bal hum qawmun ṭāghūn* names the cause.

And do you see what the cause is *not*?

The cause is not: *you were insufficiently clear.* The cause is not: *you chose the wrong words.* The cause is not: *if only you had been more gentle, more measured, more like them in manner, they would have heard you.* The cause is a spiritual state on their side — an overflow, a transgression that has gone past the limit where truth can land — and you cannot speak someone out of tughyan. There is no argument so well-constructed that it bypasses a heart that has already exceeded the capacity to receive. The locked door is not a puzzle to be solved with a better key. It is a condition to be named, and understood, and accepted.

[PAUSE]

This matters for anyone who has ever carried something true into a conversation that didn't want it.

There is a kind of suffering that comes from bearing truth and being rejected for it — and not knowing why. And the worst part of that suffering is the question your own mind keeps asking in the hours after: *What did I do wrong? What should I have said differently? Was I too direct? Not direct enough? Did I pick the wrong moment, the wrong words? If I were just a little better at this, would they have heard me?*

And when the mind cannot find a satisfying external cause, it turns inward: *the problem must be me. My inadequacy. My failure to make the truth palatable. My deficiency as a communicator, as a person, as someone worth listening to.*

Allah reaches into that exact whisper and names what is actually happening. The door closes not because of what you brought to it. It closes because of the condition of the room behind it. And being given that clarity — not as accusation, not as vindication, but as a precise explanation — is one of the most merciful things you can receive when you're standing outside a closed door wondering what you did wrong.

[PAUSE — take that in]

But there is one more turn. Because this *tasliya* is not only comfort for someone trying to speak truth to others. It also holds a mirror — quietly, without announcing it — up to every one of us.

The Quran has just described, in anatomical detail, what tughyan produces in a heart. It produces the two labels. It produces the vault that admits no exception. It produces a condition in which the soul cannot receive truth without immediately reaching to discredit the one who brought it.

And now the question turns around.

When *you* encounter a truth you find uncomfortable — when something someone says, or something you read, or something the Quran is placing in front of you right now indicts a part of how you have been living — what does your hand reach for?

Do you reach for "that's not from a reliable source" (the modern *sāḥir*) or "that person doesn't understand the full picture" (the modern *majnūn*)? In the half-second you barely notice — before conscious thought has had time to form — do you reach for a way to make the message not-binding without having to engage what it actually said?

[PAUSE]

The pattern of rejection is universal because the architecture that produces it — tughyan, overflow, the heart that has gone past its limits — is available to every human being. Not only to the people of Nūḥ. Not only to Pharaoh's court. Not only to the Quraysh of Makkah. To you. To me. Right now.

The *tasliya* at its deepest is also a mirror. Not a shaming one. A clarifying one. One that says: *here is the mechanism — recognize it when it stirs in you.* Because the most important question these two ayahs ultimately put to the reader is not what you think about the historical messengers. It is what happens in your own chest when a truth arrives that would cost you something to receive.

## CONCLUSION

Let me return to where we began.

A man in Makkah carries two labels the city has given him. A sorcerer who bends minds with hidden forces. Or a madman whose reason has gone. The same labels Nūḥ carried for nearly a thousand years. The same labels Mūsā heard in Pharaoh's court. The same labels pinned to the messenger of ʿĀd, and to the messenger of Thamūd — whose ruins this very surah just walked through, just verses before these two.

Now you know why there is no manuscript.

There was never a will passed down through the centuries. There was never a council of deniers who agreed on the language. The Quraysh did not consult the archives of Pharaoh's court. The people of Hūd did not compare notes with the people of Lūṭ. No coordination. No inheritance. No script handed across the generations.

Only hearts that, when they overflow their limits, *grow alike* — their hearts and deeds converging in kufr and tughyan — so that the later ones said what the earlier ones said, not because they were taught it, but because they reached the same inner place.

[PAUSE]

The grammar of rejection does not come from *between* people. It rises *within* them. From the same soil, in every age, in every tongue, the same bitter fruit. Sorcerer. Or madman.

And now — in the quiet after all of this — the surah will give the Prophet ﷺ his instruction. *"So turn away from them — you are not blameworthy."* Not defeat. Not despair. Just clarity. The locked door is not your failure. You have done what a messenger does. Now walk.

And for you, standing here centuries later, reading these two ayahs: the question is not whether the pattern was real. You have seen that it is structural. That it admits no exception. That it was not coordinated but grown, from within, in every era.
