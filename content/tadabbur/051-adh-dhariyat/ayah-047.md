# The Finished Building That Is Still Expanding

### Surah Adh-Dhariyat, Ayah 47

> وَٱلسَّمَآءَ بَنَيْنَٰهَا بِأَيْي۟دٍ وَإِنَّا لَمُوسِعُونَ

**Wa-s-samāʾa banaynāhā bi-aydin wa-innā la-mūsiʿūn**

*And the heaven — We built it with strength, and indeed We are ever expanding [it].*

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sudden Look Upward

Let me put you where the first listeners were standing.

For the last forty verses, Adh-Dhariyat has been walking you through a graveyard of nations. The people of Nuh, drowned. ʿAd, taken by a barren, screaming wind. Thamud, struck while they watched. The house of Pharaoh, seized. The surah moves from ruin to ruin, and the refrain underneath each one is the same: this is what power does when it is denied. These were not small peoples. They built. They carved homes into mountains. They raised monuments that outlasted them. And one by one, the surah lays them in the ground.

You are meant to feel small by verse 46. You are meant to feel the fragility of everything human beings construct. Every empire is a sandcastle at low tide.

And then — without warning — the surah lifts your chin and makes you look **up**.

*And the heaven — We built it.*

[PAUSE]

Notice the violence of the contrast. You have just watched the strongest civilizations on earth crumble like dried clay. And in the very next breath, Allah points to the one structure that has never cracked, never sagged, never needed a single repair. The roof over the world. The thing every one of those dead nations lived their whole lives beneath and never once saw waver.

He is making an argument. He is saying: *you saw what My strength did to them. Now look at what My strength has been holding over your head this entire time, silently, without a pillar, without a flaw.*

But that is only the doorway. Because hidden inside these six words is something that should stop you completely. Allah uses two different kinds of words for two different kinds of time. He says the heaven was **built** — finished, done, past tense, complete. And then He says something about Himself that is not past, not finished, not complete at all. He calls Himself, right now, in this moment, *something that is still happening.*

The building is done. And He is still building.

How can both be true at once? Stay with me — because the answer changes what you believe about a God you may have quietly assumed was finished with you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Gets Pulled to the Front

Let me teach you something small about how language carries emphasis, and then I'll show you Allah doing it.

In ordinary speech, word order is lazy. We say "I closed the door." Subject, verb, object — in that comfortable order. But imagine you walk into a room and you are furious about one specific thing, and you say: "*The door* — I closed it." You pulled the door to the front. You yanked it out of its normal slot and put it first, before the verb, and then — notice — you still mentioned it again with the word "it." *The door, I closed it.* That repetition isn't clumsy. It's the sound of something mattering. You front the thing your whole sentence is really about.

Arabic does this deliberately and with precision. The grammarians have a name for it — a construction where a noun is thrown to the front of the sentence, and then a pronoun later in the sentence points back to it. The fronting is emphasis. It says: *this. Look at this first.*

Now look at how the ayah opens.

> وَٱلسَّمَآءَ بَنَيْنَٰهَا

**Wa-s-samāʾa** — *and the heaven* — comes first. And grammatically it sits in the accusative case, the case of the object. Which is strange, because the verb hasn't arrived yet. The object is standing before its own verb, out of order, pulled forward. And then the verb comes — **banaynā**, *We built* — and trailing off the end of it is a tiny pronoun, **hā**, *it.*

*The heaven — We built it.*

[PAUSE]

It is the exact structure of the furious person and the door. Allah has reached into the sentence and pulled the heaven to the very front, ahead of even the act of building it. He is not narrating. He is **pointing.** Out of everything He could have led with after the parade of dead nations, He leads with the one object He wants your eyes locked onto.

And here is what's remarkable about *which* thing He fronts. He has spent forty verses pointing downward — at cities, at peoples, at things on the ground that fell. The first word out of His mouth when He turns to His own work is the thing that is **highest.** The eye that has been forced to look down at ruins is dragged up to the ceiling of creation. The fronting isn't just grammar. It is the camera tilting from the rubble to the sky.

What this single word-order reveals: *Allah is redirecting your gaze from what collapses to what holds.* And He does it before He even tells you what He did with it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: Strength — and a Word Spelled to Be Unmistakable

Now look at *how* He says it was built.

> بَنَيْنَٰهَا بِأَيْي۟دٍ

**bi-aydin** — *with strength.*

Every one of the classical commentators is in agreement here, and they barely vary. Al-Jalalayn: *bi-quwwah* — with power. Al-Baghawi: *bi-quwwah wa-qudrah* — with strength and capability. Al-Qurtubi reports it from Ibn ʿAbbas and others: *bi-quwwah wa-qudrah.* The word **ayd** means raw power, might, capacity.

But I want to show you something in the spelling that the reciters and the scribes have preserved for fourteen centuries, because it is doing quiet work.

In the muṣḥaf, this word is written with an *extra* letter — an additional yāʾ — so that the rasm reads بِأَيْي۟دٍ rather than the simpler form you might expect. Now, why would that matter? Because there is another Arabic word that *sounds* and *looks* dangerously close: **aydin**, the plural of *yad* — **hands.** *Bi-aydin* could, to a careless ear, drift toward "with hands."

And so the word for **strength** is written in a way that sets it apart, marks it, keeps it from being mistaken for **hands.**

Sit with what that protects. Allah is describing how He raised the heaven, and the orthography itself leans away from any image of physical hands lifting, straining, hauling something into place. This is not a laborer heaving a beam onto a wall. There is no exertion here, no body, no toil. **Ayd** is pure capacity — power without effort, might without strain. The same root the surah will return to at its very end when it names Allah *dhū-l-quwwah*, the Possessor of strength.

[PAUSE]

Let me say clearly where the ground is firm and where it softens. That the word means strength — that is documented, lexically attested, agreed upon by all four commentators. The reading of the *spelling* as a deliberate guard against confusion with "hands" is the kind of thing the scholars of rasm note as a marker of meaning; take it as an invitation the orthography offers, not a verse of its own.

But the core stands without any of that: the heaven was built with sheer, effortless might. And here is the turn. **The very same power** you watched bury four civilizations is the power named here. In their case, that power *unmade.* Here, that identical power *builds* — and not just builds, but builds the most stable thing in your experience. The strength is one strength. What changes is what it is turned toward.

What this word reveals: *the power that destroys the deniers and the power that holds up your sky are not two powers. They are one — and most of the time, it is building, not breaking.*

### Element Three: The Building Is Finished. The Builder Is Not.

This is the heart. Stay very close to me here.

Arabic, like every language, has tenses — but it also has something subtler than tense. It has a difference between a **verb that reports a completed act** and a **noun-like form that describes an ongoing, living quality.**

Think of the difference in English between saying *"she sang"* and saying *"she is a singer."* "She sang" is an event. It happened; it ended. "She is a singer" is not an event at all — it is a state, a standing reality, something true of her right now and continuously. Arabic marks this distinction with great care. The completed act is the perfect verb. The ongoing, living quality is the **active participle** — *ism al-fāʿil* — the form that turns a doing into a permanent description of the doer.

Now watch what Allah does across this one short ayah.

For the heaven, He uses a **perfect verb**: **banaynāhā** — *We built it.* Completed. Done. Past. The construction is finished; the roof is up; there is nothing left to add to the heaven as a built thing.

And then, for Himself, He does *not* use a verb at all. He shifts to a **participle**:

> وَإِنَّا لَمُوسِعُونَ

**la-mūsiʿūn** — *[We are] ones who expand.* Ever-expanding. Continuously making vast.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the seam? The building is reported as **over.** The Builder is described as **ongoing.** *banaynā* closes; *mūsiʿūn* never closes. One word looks back at a finished act; the next word describes a quality that is happening *now,* with no end built into it. A participle does not stop. It is not "We expanded" and it is not even "We will expand." It is nearer to *We are, by Our very nature, expanders* — this is simply what We are doing and what We are.

And there is the emphatic **lām** fused to the front of it — **la-mūsiʿūn** — the particle of oath-like stress, the linguistic equivalent of underlining. *And indeed, most certainly, We are ever-expanding.*

Now here is the second blade of this word, and it is the sharpest thing in the ayah.

**Expanding *what*?**

The participle has no stated object. Read it again — *and indeed We are ever-expanders* — and notice that Allah never tells you the noun. Expanders of *what?* The sentence simply… opens, and does not close the door.

This is not an accident, and it is not a gap to be embarrassed by. Look at how the classical commentators, faced with that open door, walked through it in different directions — and notice that **none of them could narrow it to one thing, because the grammar refuses to be narrowed:**

- Ibn ʿAbbas, as al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi report: **la-qādirūn** — *We are fully able, We have the power.* The vastness is the vastness of capability.
- Another reading from Ibn ʿAbbas: **mūsiʿūn ar-rizq** — *We expand the provision* upon Our creation. The vastness is the vastness of sustenance.
- Al-Muyassar: *We are ever-widening its expanses and its regions* — the vastness of the heaven's own reaches.
- Another, as al-Qurtubi gives it: **dhū saʿah** — *Possessors of boundless capacity* — that the making of this heaven and everything else costs Us nothing, constrains Us in nothing.
- Al-Daḥḥāk: **aghniyāʾ** — *We are rich, without need,* pointing to the verse "upon the one of abundance, his capacity" (2:236).
- And al-Ḥasan: **muṭīqūn** — *We are entirely capable* of it.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what has happened? Six great readers reach into the open door of this participle and each pulls out something true — power, provision, the heaven's own reaches, infinite capacity, freedom from need, total capability — and **they do not contradict each other.** They are six camera angles on a single reality: a God whose making is never strained, never finished in the sense of *exhausted,* never out of room. The object was left unspoken precisely so that *all* of them could be true at once.

And here — carefully, honestly — is where the modern ear hears one more thing. The root of *mūsiʿūn* is the root of *widening, of making spacious.* When a contemporary reader who knows that the cosmos itself is measurably stretching, that the distance between galaxies is growing, encounters *"and indeed We are ever-expanding it,"* something resonates. I will not tell you the classical scholars meant the physical expansion of the universe — they did not have that frame, and they read it as power, provision, and capacity. And I will not tell you this verse is a proof to win an argument. What I will say is this: the grammar left the door open, and the door has never been forced shut. A word that means *to keep making vast,* attached to a Builder described as *never ceasing,* spoken of the heaven — that word has room in it. It always did.

What this word reveals: *the construction of the cosmos is complete, but the Constructor is not a retired Builder. He is, in this very moment, by His own nature, still making room.*

---

### Bridge

So look at what these six words have given us. A heaven **pulled to the front** of your vision, away from the ruins. Built with a strength so effortless its very spelling refuses the image of straining hands. And a Builder who finished the building yet never finished **being a Builder** — who left the object of His expanding deliberately unspoken so that power, and provision, and capacity, and the reaches of the sky itself could all pour through the same open word.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the God who chose them, and about the life you are living underneath that roof.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Believe You Are a Finished Building

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of despair that does not feel like despair. It feels like *realism.* It is the quiet conviction that your life has taken its shape. The walls are up. The rooms are where they're going to be. You are thirty-four, or forty-seven, or nineteen and already certain — and you have decided, somewhere below the level of words, that the construction is *over.* This is who I am now. This is what my marriage is. This is the ceiling of what I'll earn, what I'll heal, what I'll become. The building is built. There's nothing left but to live in it and maintain it until it comes down.

We don't say it out loud. We call it being mature. Managing expectations. But underneath, it is a belief about God as much as about ourselves — a belief that **He, too, is finished with us.** That whatever expanding He was going to do in our lives, He did, and the verdict is in, and the dimensions are fixed.

[PAUSE]

Now bring that exact feeling to this ayah.

Allah looks at the single most *finished* thing in all of creation — the heaven, the completed roof of the universe, *banaynāhā,* built, done, past tense, sealed — and even **there,** even over the most completed structure imaginable, He refuses to describe Himself as finished. He says: *and indeed We are ever-expanding.* Over the done thing, the Builder is still building.

If He will not call Himself finished even with the heaven *completed* — on what grounds did you decide He is finished with **you?**

Here is the Arabic gift underneath this, and it is precise, not sentimental. The root of *mūsiʿūn* — w-s-ʿ — is the native opposite of a word you know in your body even if you don't know its name: **ḍayq.** Constriction. Tightness. The chest that won't fully fill. The Qur'an uses *ḍayq* for exactly the feeling we've been describing — *yaḍīqu ṣadruh,* the breast that constricts. And *saʿah,* this root, is its cure: width, room, spaciousness, the breath that finally goes all the way down.

So when Allah names Himself, after the heaven is built, as the One who is *ever-making-vast* — He is not making an astronomy claim to you in your worst hour. He is telling you the **direction** in which He works. His default motion is **outward.** Toward more room. Away from the tightness. The God over your head is, by the grammar of His own self-description, *anti-constriction.* He expands. It is what He is doing right now, to things you cannot see, with a power that costs Him nothing.

[PAUSE]

And remember the unspoken object. He did not say *We expand the heaven* and stop. He left it open — *We are ever-expanders* — and the commentators poured *provision,* *capacity,* *ability,* *freedom from need* into that opening. Which means the verse does not restrict His expanding to the sky. The same open word that can hold the widening cosmos can hold the widening of a constricted life. *Mūsiʿūn ar-rizq,* Ibn ʿAbbas said — *We expand the provision.* The room He makes is not only in the heavens. It is in circumstances. In hearts. In what was closed and is suddenly, against all your realism, not closed.

What this changes about tomorrow: when you catch yourself describing your life as a *built thing* — fixed walls, final rooms, settled verdict — stop and notice that you are claiming to know something about Allah that Allah refuses to say about Himself even over a finished universe. The honest posture is not "this is who I am now." It is: *the One over my head has not stopped expanding, and He left the object of that expansion unspoken — which means it could be me.* Tonight, name one wall in your life you have treated as load-bearing and permanent. Then say, with the verse: *la-mūsiʿūn.* He is still building. Ask Him for the room.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Power Over Your Head Is the Hand That Feeds You

Let me bring you to the second thing, through the door of a fear you may not admit you have.

Many people carry a God of consequences. When they think of divine *power* — strength, might, *quwwah* — what rises in them is the parade of dead nations this very surah just marched past. Power means the wind that took ʿAd. Power means being seized. For a certain kind of believer, especially one who grew up being warned more than wooed, *the strength of God is a threat held over the head.* Something to stay on the right side of. Something that, if it ever turns toward you, ends you.

Now look at where Allah aims that strength in this ayah.

*We built the heaven **bi-aydin** — with strength.* The might that buried the deniers is, in the very same surah, the might that **raised the roof over every living thing.** The wind that destroyed and the heaven that shelters come from one power. And what does that roof *do,* once it's up? The surah already told you, twenty-five verses earlier: **wa fī-s-samāʾi rizqukum** — *and in the heaven is your provision* (51:22). The thing built with strength is the thing your sustenance comes down from. Power, here, is not pointed at you. It is pointed *over* you, and it is **feeding** you.

[PAUSE]

Watch how the whole surah closes the circle, because this is the proof and it is breathtaking.

This ayah hands you two things: **strength** — *ayd* — and **expansion** — *mūsiʿūn,* which Ibn ʿAbbas read as the expanding of **provision.** Strength and provision. Hold those two words.

Now travel eleven verses to the very end of the surah, where Allah states, flat and final, who He is:

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلرَّزَّاقُ ذُو ٱلْقُوَّةِ ٱلْمَتِينُ
>
> *Indeed Allah — He is the Provider, the Possessor of strength, the Firm.* (51:58)

[PAUSE]

Look at what He named Himself. **Ar-Razzāq** — the Provider, the ceaseless Giver of provision. *Dhū-l-quwwah* — the Possessor of **strength.** The two qualities He *demonstrated* in verse 47 — building the heaven *with strength,* expanding *provision* — are the two qualities He *names Himself by* in verse 58. The verse showed you the strength and the provision in action over your head; the surah's conclusion tells you those are not things He does, they are things He **is.** The heaven was the evidence. *Ar-Razzāq Dhū-l-Quwwah* is the verdict.

So the strength you were afraid of and the sustenance you depend on are *the same attribute, seen from two sides.* He is not strong *and also,* reluctantly, a provider. His strength *is* the engine of His provision. The might is in the service of the feeding. *Dhū-l-quwwah* and *ar-Razzāq* sit in the same breath of the same verse, describing the same One.

[PAUSE]

Then comes the line that the entire passage was built to land — verse 50, right after the heaven and the earth and the pairs:

> فَفِرُّوٓا۟ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ
>
> *So flee to Allah.*

*Flee* — the word for running from danger. But run **to** the One whose power you feared. The surah takes the strength that frightened you and tells you it is your *shelter.* You don't flee *from* this power. You flee *into* it. The roof built *bi-aydin,* with that strength, is the thing you run under when the storm comes — and the storm, often, is the very fear of that power. He is telling you: the might is not your predator. It is your refuge. Run *toward* the strength.

What this changes about tomorrow: the next time the size of God frightens you — the power, the accountability, the might that ends nations — let the verse correct the picture. That same strength is, at this exact moment, holding up the sky that drops your rizq. It built you a roof before it ever became a threat. *Dhū-l-quwwah* is *ar-Razzāq.* So practice fleeing *to* it. When fear of God curdles into dread and distance, that is the moment the surah says *firrū ilā-llāh* — run *closer,* not further. Make your du'a not to a power you are dodging, but to the Strength that has been feeding you under an unbroken roof your entire life.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that the work is done, stand back and see the whole shape — because this verse was never alone. It is the middle term of an argument that spans the surah.

At verse **22**, Allah said: *in the heaven is your provision.* He planted the promise.

At verse **47** — our verse — He built the heaven *with strength,* and named Himself the one *ever-expanding* it, and left the object open so *provision* could pour through. He laid the evidence.

At verse **50**, He said: *so flee to Allah.* He gave the command the evidence demands.

And at verse **58**, He closed it: *Indeed Allah is the Provider, the Possessor of strength, the Firm.* He signed the verdict — naming Himself by the exact two attributes verse 47 had shown in action.

Provision promised. Strength and provision demonstrated. Flight commanded. Strength and provision declared as His very Names. The heaven in verse 47 is the hinge the whole structure turns on — the visible, undeniable, unbroken roof that proves every other claim in the chain.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes turn out to be one insight approached from different gates.

The grammar showed us a **finished building and an unfinished Builder** — *banaynā* sealed, *mūsiʿūn* ongoing. The first theme heard, in that same seam, the answer to our despair: *you are not a closed structure, because He is not a retired Builder.*

The grammar showed us a **strength so effortless the spelling refuses straining hands**, and an **expanding whose object was left open** for provision to flow through. The second theme heard, in those same two words, the answer to our fear: *the power you dread is the power that feeds you — His strength is the engine of His provision.*

One verse. Two wounds — the despair of feeling finished, the dread of feeling threatened. And the same six words heal both, because behind both is the same God: still building, never straining, ever making room, and pointing the whole of His strength toward holding up the roof your sustenance falls from.

### Questions to Carry

1. What wall in my life have I treated as load-bearing and permanent — and did I ever ask the One who is *still expanding* to move it?
2. When I imagine the *power* of God, does my body brace, or does it relax under a roof? Which one does verse 58 say is true?
3. The object of *mūsiʿūn* was left unspoken so anything could fill it. What would I dare to put there, in my own life, if I believed He meant it?
4. *Firrū ilā-llāh* — flee *to* Allah. Is there a fear of Him I have been fleeing *from,* when the command was to run *closer?*
5. I live under an unbroken roof every day and never see it waver. What else of His steadiness have I stopped noticing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The same effortless strength that raised the heaven without a single flaw never stopped being a Builder — and the God who is still, in this moment, making the cosmos vast has not finished making room in your life, or in your provision, or in you.

### The Closing Invitation

You walked in, perhaps, carrying a quiet certainty that your shape was set and that the power above you was something to keep your distance from. Let this verse loosen both. Tonight, before sleep, go outside if you can and look up — at the one structure that has held over every ruined nation and every settled life, including yours. Say the verse. And let it land that the One who built it is *not done,* that His strength has been a roof and not a threat, and that the room He is still making could, by the open word He chose, be made for you. You do not have to flee far. You have to flee *toward.*

### A Du'a From These Words

*O Allah, You raised the heaven with a strength that never strained, and You are still — at this very moment — expanding what You have made. I have called myself finished; You never called Yourself finished, even over a completed sky. So make room where my life has gone tight. Let the strength I feared be the roof I shelter under. You are Ar-Razzāq, Dhū-l-Quwwah — the Provider, the Possessor of strength — so I flee to You, and to no one beside You. Widen me.*

*Āmīn.*
