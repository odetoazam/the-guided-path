# Do Not Set Anything Beside Him

## A Tadabbur of Surah Adh-Dhariyat, Ayah 51

> وَلَا تَجْعَلُوا۟ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَـٰهًا ءَاخَرَ ۖ إِنِّى لَكُم مِّنْهُ نَذِيرٌ مُّبِينٌ
>
> *Wa lā tajʿalū maʿa-llāhi ilāhan ākhar — innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn.*
>
> **And do not set up with Allah another god. Indeed, I am to you, from Him, a clear warner.**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me put you somewhere before we open a single word.

You are sitting in Makkah, sometime in the years when this surah was revealed. You believe in Allah. Understand this clearly — you are not an atheist. The Quraysh were not atheists. If you stopped a man in the street and asked him, "Who created the heavens and the earth?" he would answer without hesitation: *Allah.* The Quran itself tells us this — when you ask them who created them, they will say "Allah" (43:87). Belief in a Creator was not the problem.

So what was the problem?

The problem was the word **with**.

These were people who said: *Yes, Allah. Of course, Allah. The Most High, the Creator.* And then — beside Him, around Him, underneath Him — a whole architecture of other things. Intercessors. Idols at the Kaʿba. Daughters of God. Angels imagined as deities. They did not want to remove Allah. They wanted to *add* to Him. They kept Allah at the top and built a scaffolding of partners beneath.

And into this world — a world that already says "Allah," a world that already believes in a Creator — comes a single short command that does not argue about the *existence* of God at all. It assumes you already believe in Him. It goes straight for the scaffolding.

*Do not set up — with Allah — another god.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold this, because it changes everything about how we hear this ayah. We tend to read it as if it were aimed at someone far away — the pagan, the polytheist, the person who has never heard of tawhid. But it was revealed to people who already affirmed God. Which means its first audience is not the person who *denies* Allah. Its first audience is the person who *believes in* Allah — and quietly keeps something *beside* Him.

That person might be sitting in seventh-century Makkah.

That person might be reading this today.

**Here is the hook.** This ayah does not say "do not *worship* another god." It uses a different, stranger, more revealing verb. And it does not say "I am a warner *to* you." It adds two small words — *from Him* — that, once you see them, will reframe the entire relationship between mercy and warning, between the One who threatens and the one who is sent. By the end, I want you to see that the warning you fear and the One you fear it from are not two things at all.

Let's begin where the ayah begins — with the act of making.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: تَجْعَلُوا — "Do Not *Make* a God"

Before we touch the Arabic, let me give you an everyday distinction in English.

There is a difference between *finding* something and *making* something. If I find a stone, the stone was already there; I merely came upon it. But if I *make* a chair, then before my act there was no chair — there was only wood. The chair exists because I positioned things, assembled them, set them up. The chair is, in a real sense, *my doing.*

Hold that distinction. Now look at the verb Allah chose.

The ayah says **وَلَا تَجْعَلُوا** — *wa lā tajʿalū.* The verb is from the root **jīm-ʿayn-lām (jʿl)** — *jaʿala.* And here is the thing you must feel: *jaʿala* does not primarily mean "to worship." There is a perfectly good Arabic word for worship — *ʿabada* — and the Quran uses it constantly. Allah could have said *wa lā taʿbudū maʿa-llāhi ilāhan ākhar* — "do not *worship* another god with Allah."

He did not. He said *do not **make**.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with this, because it is doing quiet, devastating work.

*Jaʿala* is the verb of *placing, setting, fashioning, rendering, appointing.* It is what a craftsman does. It is an act of construction. And what it tells us is this: **the other god has no independent existence. You make it.** The idol does not arrive from somewhere. The rival is not discovered lying in the world like a stone. It is *manufactured.* It is set up. It is positioned by a human hand and a human heart.

Notice something. This is almost more humbling than a command about worship. A command about worship says: *direct your devotion correctly.* But a command about *making* says something deeper — it says: **you are the factory.** The false god you carry is not an external power that overwhelms you. It is something you yourself assembled, plank by plank, and then bowed to. Allah is not warning you about an invader. He is warning you about your own workshop.

This is why the Quran elsewhere has Ibrahim ask his people the unanswerable question — *do you worship what you yourselves carve?* (37:95). You carved it. You *jaʿala*-ed it. The thing has no being except the being you lent it.

And here is what that means for you, sitting here, who owns no carved idol of wood and stone. The verb has not expired. *Jaʿala* is still happening — in every heart that takes something real and finite, and *sets it up,* and lends it a weight that belongs to Allah alone. You make the god. Which means — and stay with this — **you can also unmake it.** A thing you constructed, you have the authority to dismantle. The verb that condemns you also frees you.

That is what one word — *tajʿalū* — opens. Now let us look at the word standing right beside it, because the two were designed to be heard together.

---

## Element Two: مَعَ — The Theology Hidden in "With"

There is a word in this ayah so small that you could read past it a thousand times: **مَعَ** — *maʿa* — "with."

*Do not set up — **with** Allah — another god.*

Let me teach you the device first, plainly. In any language, the difference between *instead of* and *alongside* is the difference between subtraction and addition. "I'll take tea *instead of* coffee" removes the coffee. "I'll take tea *with* coffee" keeps both. One is replacement. The other is accompaniment.

Now watch what Allah chose, and — just as importantly — what He did *not* choose.

He did **not** say *do not set up another god* and stop there, as if the danger were simply *having* a false god in a vacuum. He did **not** say *do not set up a god instead of Allah,* as if the danger were *replacing* Him. He said *do not set up another god* — **maʿa-llāh** — *with Allah.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the precision of that?

The sin being named is not the *removal* of Allah. It is the *addition* of a partner. **It is the word "with" that is forbidden.**

And this, brothers and sisters, is the entire psychology of the Makkans — and very nearly the entire psychology of us. They were not trying to erase God. They were trying to *keep* God *and.* God and the idols. God and the intercessors. God and the inherited customs. The whole spiritual disease lives in the conjunction. It is the "and" that destroys tawhid.

Al-Muyassar captures the simplicity exactly: *do not make, with Allah, another object of worship.* The crime is *withness.* The crime is that there is a second chair in a room that was only ever meant to hold one throne.

And here is the part that should make us all go quiet. Total atheism is actually rare and difficult; it takes a kind of effort to deny the Creator entirely. But *withness* is easy. *Withness* is the default setting of the unexamined heart. We almost never say, "I will replace Allah." We say, "Allah — *and.*" Allah and my career. Allah and what people think of me. Allah and my own control over outcomes. Allah and the number in the account. We don't dethrone Him. We just keep slipping a second chair in beside His throne, and telling ourselves the throne is still His because, after all, we never *removed* Him.

This ayah closes that loophole forever. Tawhid is not "Allah at the top." Tawhid is **Allah with nothing beside Him.** Not first among many. Not greatest among partners. *Alone.* The forbidden word is *with.*

[PAUSE — let this sit]

So the first half of the ayah, in two words, has told us: you *make* false gods (*tajʿalū*), and the way you make them is by *addition* (*maʿa*). Now the ayah turns, and a voice speaks. Let us listen to who it is and where it comes from.

---

## Element Three: مِنْهُ ... مُبِينٌ — "From Him, a Clear Warner"

The second half of the ayah is a single sentence: **إِنِّى لَكُم مِّنْهُ نَذِيرٌ مُّبِينٌ** — *innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn* — "Indeed I am, to you, from Him, a clear warner."

I want to focus on two things: the small word **مِنْهُ** — *minhu*, "from Him" — and the last word, **مُبِينٌ** — *mubīn*, "clear."

**First: "from Him."**

Look at the chain of relationships packed into this short clause. *I* (the warner) — *to you* (the people) — *from Him* (Allah). The warner stands in the middle. He is not the source of the warning; he is the conveyor of it. *From Him.* The warning originates with Allah and arrives through the messenger.

Now here is what is striking, and the classical commentators noticed it. *Minhu* — from whom? The grammar offers a beautiful tension. Al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, and al-Baghawī read it the obvious way: *from Allah* — the warner is sent *from God.* But al-Qurṭubī preserves a second reading, attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās: the warning is *from* the Prophet ﷺ — "I warn you of his might and his sword if you associate partners with Me." Both readings are present in the tradition, and rather than forcing us to choose, they layer into something deeper, like two camera angles on one scene.

Hold the dominant reading — *from Allah* — and feel its weight. [PAUSE]

**The warning comes from the very One you are being warned about.**

Think about what that means. In the ordinary world, a warning *about* a powerful party usually comes from a *third* party. A friend warns you about a dangerous person. A stranger warns you about an oncoming storm. The warner and the threat are different parties. But here — the One whose displeasure is the danger is *the very One who sent the warner to spare you from it.* The threat and the rescue share a single source. The hand that could strike is the hand that sent the messenger to say: *don't make Me strike.*

This is the secret heart of *minhu.* The warning is not the opposite of mercy. **The warning is a form of mercy.** A God who did not care would let you walk off the cliff in silence. A warner sent *from Him* is proof that the One you fear would rather warn you than punish you. That is why, just one ayah earlier, the command was *flee to Allah* (51:50) — flee *to* the very One you might think you should flee *from.* The refuge and the danger are the same Lord. There is nowhere else to run, and — wondrously — there is nowhere else you *need* to run.

**Second: "clear" — مُبِينٌ.**

The final word is *mubīn,* from the root **bā-yā-nūn (byn)**, which carries the meanings of *separation, distinction, clarity, making-plain.* And *mubīn* is built on Form IV of this root — the form *abāna.* I want to be careful and precise here, the way the grammar demands. Form IV does many things in Arabic; we should not flatten it. With this particular root, *abāna* / *mubīn* carries a rich double sense: it can mean *being clear in oneself* — manifest, unmistakable — and it can mean *making other things clear* — distinguishing, clarifying, separating truth from falsehood.

So *nadhīrun mubīn* is not merely "a clear warner" in the sense of "a warner you can see." It is a warner who is himself unmistakable *and* who **makes the matter plain** — who separates, like the root suggests, the path of safety from the path of ruin so sharply that no one can later say, *I could not tell which was which.*

[PAUSE]

Feel why that word lands exactly here, at the end. The whole ayah has been about a confusion — the confusion of *withness,* of keeping Allah *and.* That confusion thrives in fog. As long as the lines are blurry, the heart can tell itself comforting stories: *surely a little alongside is fine; surely the second chair doesn't really count.* And then comes the last word — *mubīn* — and the fog is forbidden. The warner makes it **plain.** There is no blur left to hide in. One throne. No "with." This is clear. You have been told clearly.

And do you hear what *mubīn* does to your future self? It closes the door on the oldest excuse in the human heart: *I didn't know. It wasn't clear.* A *clear* warner is sent precisely so that, on the Day when accounts are opened, no soul can say it was never made plain. *Mubīn* is mercy and it is finality at once — the clarity that saves you if you heed it, and that leaves you no defense if you don't.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Three words have carried us a long way. *Tajʿalū* — you *make* your false gods; they have no being but the being you lend them. *Maʿa* — the sin is not replacing Allah but adding *beside* Him; the disease lives in "with." *Minhu... mubīn* — the warning comes *from the very One warned about,* and it comes *clear,* so that no fog and no excuse remain.

Now let us ask what all this reveals not just about Arabic words — but about the architecture of a human heart, and about how it lives tomorrow morning.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Tyranny of "And" — Shirk as Crowding

Let me start nowhere near religion. Let me start with your phone.

You know the feeling of *divided attention.* You sit down to be present with someone you love — and the phone is face-up on the table. You haven't replaced the person. You're still there. You're nodding. But there is a *second* presence in the room now, glowing, waiting, and a part of you is *with* it. You have not chosen the phone *instead of* the person. You have chosen the person *and.* And everyone who has ever been on the other side of that "and" knows that it is a kind of betrayal that wears the mask of presence.

That is the psychology this ayah is diagnosing. [PAUSE]

The deepest human disloyalties are almost never *replacements.* They are *additions.* The spouse who strays does not usually announce, "I am leaving you." They keep the marriage — *and.* The friend who betrays you rarely declares open enmity. They stay your friend — *and.* The heart is a master of the hedge. It learns early that you can keep almost anything if you simply refuse to *let go of the alternative.* Keep God — *and* keep the escape route. Keep the throne occupied — *and* keep a second chair, just in case.

Now hear the ayah again with this in your chest: *wa lā tajʿalū maʿa-llāhi ilāhan ākhar.* Do not keep a second chair. Not beneath Him. Not beside Him. Not "just in case."

Why is Allah so absolute about the *with*? Why is "Allah-and" forbidden when "Allah" is affirmed?

Because the *with* is not honesty about your divided heart. The *with* is the **refusal to trust Him fully.** Every partner we set beside Allah is a hedge against the possibility that He is not enough. Allah *and* the savings, in case He doesn't provide. Allah *and* the approval of people, in case His approval doesn't satisfy. Allah *and* my own grip on the outcome, in case He doesn't come through. The second chair is fear wearing the costume of piety. It whispers: *worship Him, yes — but don't put all of yourself in His hands.*

And this is why the disease is so much harder to see than open denial. The atheist's heart, at least, is undivided in its error. But the heart of *withness* tells itself it is *believing* — while quietly keeping one hand on the rail. It is the most respectable form of distrust. It can pray five times a day and still be crowding the throne.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you the paradox, and let it sit without rushing to soften it. **The thing you keep *beside* Allah to feel safe is the very thing that makes you unsafe.** The hedge is the wound. As long as there is an *and,* there is a part of you that has not arrived, not surrendered, not rested. The whole exhausting project of the divided heart is the attempt to be loyal to two thrones — and two thrones is a war that never ends, fought inside your own chest, every day, forever.

What does the ayah offer instead? Not "less." *More.* It offers the unbearable relief of a single chair. The peace of a heart with one and only one ultimate concern. This is why removing the *with* is not deprivation — it is **rest.** You were never built to serve two masters. You were built to flee to One.

**So what changes tomorrow morning?** Here is one specific thing. Sometime tomorrow, you will feel the familiar tug — the reach for the second chair. The flash of fear that says, *secure the backup; don't fully trust; keep the hedge.* When you feel it, name it. Say to yourself, plainly: *this is the "with." This is me sliding a second chair beside the throne.* You do not have to win the whole war by sunset. You only have to *see* the chair for what it is — because, remember the first word, *you made it.* And what you made, by His help, you can carry back out of the room.

## Theme Two: The Mercy That Wears the Face of a Warning

Let me ask you to remember a moment you probably resented at the time.

Someone who loved you told you something you did not want to hear. A parent, a teacher, a friend who refused to flatter you. They warned you — about a road you were on, a person you trusted, a habit that was quietly eating you. And in the moment, the warning felt like an attack. It felt like coldness. Only later — sometimes years later — did you understand that the warning was the *most loving thing they did.* The people who only ever told you what you wanted to hear were not kinder. They simply cared less.

Now hold that, and come back to *minhu — from Him.* [PAUSE]

We have a deep instinct to split the universe in two. There is the God of mercy, soft and warm, the one we run *to.* And there is the God of warning, stern and frightening, the one we run *from.* We imagine them almost as different faces — and we much prefer the first. We would like a religion of comfort with the warnings edited out.

This ayah forbids that splitting. *Innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn* — I am to you, *from Him,* a clear warner. The warning and the mercy come *from the same source.* They are not two Gods. They are not even two moods. **The warning is what the mercy looks like when you are walking toward a cliff.**

Think it all the way through. If Allah were indifferent to you, what would He do? *Nothing.* He would let you set up your second chair in silence. He would let you make your little gods and bow to them undisturbed, and let the consequences arrive on their own, unannounced. Silence is what indifference looks like. **A warner is what love looks like.** The very existence of *nadhīr* — a warner sent *from Him* — is evidence that the One you might be tempted to flee from is bending toward you, has gone to the trouble of sending someone, has said, in effect: *I would rather warn you than lose you.*

And remember 51:50, the ayah right before this one: *fa-firrū ila-llāh* — **flee to Allah.** Not *from.* **To.** This is the resolution of the paradox the whole surah has been building. Where do you flee when the danger is the displeasure of the Almighty? You flee *toward* Him. Because the One who warns is the only one who can shelter you from what He warns about. There is no third party, no neutral ground, no other refuge. The hand that holds the consequence is the only hand open to receive you. *Flee to Allah* — and then, *do not set up anything beside Him.* The fleeing and the un-crowding are one act.

[PAUSE]

And now the second word does its final work — *mubīn.* The warning is *clear.* Why does that matter so much for mercy? Because a vague warning is a cruelty disguised as kindness — it lets you tell yourself you didn't really understand. But a *clear* warner removes the fog precisely so that you have a real chance to turn. *Mubīn* is the clarity of someone who loves you enough to not let you hide in ambiguity. It is the friend who says the hard thing *plainly,* so you cannot pretend you misheard.

**So what changes tomorrow?** This: the next time the Quran warns you — the next time you hit an ayah of fear, of consequence, of the Fire, and your heart flinches and wants to skip ahead to the gentle one — stop. Read it again and say: *this is from Him. This is the warning of the One who is also my refuge. This is not Him turning away from me; this is Him refusing to let me walk off the cliff in silence.* Let the warning soften you instead of scaring you off — because the One it comes *from* is the very One you are fleeing *to.*

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the two ayahs together, 50 and 51, because the structure is the proof of everything we have said.

**51:50** — *Fa-firrū ila-llāh* — "So flee **to** Allah" — *innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn.*

**51:51** — *Wa lā tajʿalū maʿa-llāhi ilāhan ākhar* — "And do not set up **with** Allah another god" — *innī lakum minhu nadhīrun mubīn.*

Look at the symmetry. The *same* warner-clause closes both — *I am to you, from Him, a clear warner* — repeated word for word, like a refrain, like a heartbeat under two commands. And between them sit the two halves of tawhid:

- **51:50 is the positive motion:** flee *to* Allah. *Toward.* The single throne, approached.
- **51:51 is the negative motion:** set up nothing *with* Allah. *Beside.* The single throne, kept clear.

[PAUSE]

This is tawhid given to you as a complete act — both directions at once. It is not enough to run *toward* Allah if you are still dragging a second chair behind you. And it is not enough to clear the room of rivals if you never actually run to the One who remains. The two ayahs are the two hands of a single embrace. *Come to Me — and bring no one with you.* And the warner stands at the door of both, saying the same clear thing twice, *from Him,* so you cannot miss it.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same insight approached from two sides.

The words said: you *make* (*tajʿalū*) your false gods by *addition* (*maʿa*), and the correction comes *from Him* (*minhu*), made *plain* (*mubīn*).

The themes said: the human heart's deepest disloyalty is the hedge — "Allah *and*" — and the warning that calls us out of it is not God's coldness but His love, clear enough that we cannot pretend we never heard.

Construction and crowding. Warning and mercy. One throne, approached and kept clear. It is all one ayah, and it is all one call.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where, honestly, is the *and* in my life right now — the second chair I keep beside the throne "just in case"?
2. What am I actually afraid will happen if I let go of that hedge and trust Him with the outcome?
3. When the Quran warns me, do I receive it as Him turning *toward* me — or do I flinch as though He were turning away?
4. The false gods I serve — did I *find* them, or did I *make* them? And if I made them, what is stopping me from unmaking them?
5. If the warning is *mubīn* — truly clear — what is the excuse I have been quietly rehearsing for the Day it is read back to me?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Tawhid is not choosing Allah over rivals but refusing to set anything *beside* Him — and the call to do so comes, unmistakably clear, *from the very One* whose displeasure is being warned of, which means the warning itself is His mercy reaching toward you.

## The Closing Invitation

You will not dismantle every second chair tonight. That is not the invitation. The invitation is smaller and more honest: to walk through tomorrow able, for the first time, to *see* the chairs — to recognize the "and" when it slips into the room, and to know that the One asking you to clear the throne is not your adversary but your refuge, calling you *toward* Him with a warning that is, underneath, the most loving thing He could have sent.

Flee to Him. And bring no one with you.

## Du'a

*O Allah, You who warned us because You would not let us walk in silence toward ruin — clear our hearts of every second chair, every hedge, every "and" we have set beside Your throne. Let the One we flee to be the only One we flee to, and make Your warnings sweet to us as the sign of Your nearness. You alone — with nothing beside You. Āmīn.*
