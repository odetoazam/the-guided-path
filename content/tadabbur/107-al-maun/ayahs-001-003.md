# Have You Seen Him?

## The Creed Hidden in How You Treat the One Who Cannot Repay You

> أَرَءَيْتَ ٱلَّذِى يُكَذِّبُ بِٱلدِّينِ ۝ فَذَٰلِكَ ٱلَّذِى يَدُعُّ ٱلْيَتِيمَ ۝ وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ
>
> *Ara'ayta alladhī yukadhdhibu bid-dīn. Fa-dhālika alladhī yaduʿʿu al-yatīm. Wa-lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi al-miskīn.*
>
> **"Have you seen the one who denies the Recompense? That, then, is the one who shoves the orphan away, and does not even urge the feeding of the poor."**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

Let me take you to Makkah, before the migration.

This is a small surah. Seven short ayahs. We're going to sit with only the first three. And to feel what they're doing, you have to understand the world they dropped into.

Makkah was a society organized around something very simple and very brutal: **what can you do for me?** It was a city of trade, of tribe, of alliance. Your worth was your network. Your protection was your bloodline. If you had a strong tribe behind you, you were safe — your debts were honored, your insults were avenged, your name carried weight in the marketplace.

And if you didn't?

Then you were nothing. The two people in that society with the least leverage — the absolute bottom of the ladder of *what can you do for me* — were **the orphan** and **the destitute.** The orphan: a child whose father has died, which in that world meant a child with no protector, no one to enforce his claim, no one to avenge a wrong done to him. You could take his inheritance and he could do nothing. The destitute: a person so emptied of resources he cannot even feed himself, and crucially, **cannot pay you back** for anything you give him.

These are the people who can do nothing *for* you.

Now — keep that world in your mind, because here is the turn. Into this society of leverage and reciprocity, a Book begins to descend that talks constantly about something nobody can see, touch, or trade: **a Day of Reckoning.** A day when the accounts are settled by Someone other than the tribe. A day when the orphan has a Protector and the destitute has a Claimant.

And the people of Makkah laughed at this. *Yawm al-dīn* — the Day of Recompense — was the great joke, the great absurdity. *Resurrection? Judgment? After we are dust and bones?* The Qur'an records their mockery again and again.

So here is the situation our three ayahs walk into. You have a man. He stands in the marketplace. He denies the Day of Judgment — openly, repeatedly, as a matter of sophisticated opinion. And the Qur'an is about to do something startling. It is about to tell us that you don't need to hear his theological argument to know what he believes.

**You just need to watch his hands.**

### The Hook

The surah opens with a question — *"Have you seen?"* — as if pointing at someone across the street. And then it does something almost unsettling. It takes a man's *belief* — an invisible thing, locked inside his chest — and it says: *here, I'll show it to you. Look at what he does to a child.*

How do you *see* a disbelief? How does Allah take the most hidden thing in a human being — what he truly holds to be real — and make it visible to your eyes?

That is what these three ayahs do. Stay with me, and let's watch it happen.

# PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Ara'ayta* — "Have You Seen?"

Before we touch the word, let me give you something from ordinary life.

Imagine a friend is describing someone to you, and instead of saying *"there's a person who behaves badly,"* he grabs your shoulder, turns you physically toward the street, and says: *"You see that guy? Watch him."* Something changes, doesn't it? You are no longer being **told about** someone. You are being made to **witness** him. The abstraction collapses into a person standing in front of you.

That is the work this first word is doing.

The ayah opens: **أَرَءَيْتَ** — *Ara'ayta.* Look at how it's built. There is a little hamza at the front — *a-* — which in Arabic is the particle of a question. And then *ra'ayta*, from the root **rā'-yā** (ر أ ي), the verb *to see.* Literally, word for word: **"Have you seen?"**

Now here is the first thing to notice, and it's easy to walk past. The verb of *seeing* is in the **perfect tense** — *ra'ayta*, "you have seen," something completed. Allah does not ask, *"Will you ever see?"* or *"Can you imagine?"* He speaks as though the sight is **already available.** As though the man is already standing there in plain view, and all that remains is for you to turn your eyes.

Let's sit with this, because there's something profound in the choice of verb.

The man in this ayah is defined by his relationship to something he **cannot see** — the Day of Recompense, the unseen reckoning. He treats the invisible as unreal. *I'll believe it when I see it,* is the entire posture of his soul.

And how does Allah begin? **"Have you *seen*..."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the quiet irony? The man's whole philosophy is *seeing is believing.* So Allah says, in effect: *Fine. You want to deal only in what the eyes can see? Then let Me show you something you can see. Let Me make his inner disbelief — the very thing he thinks is hidden and private — into something as visible as a man shoving a child in the street.*

The scholars note something further about *ara'ayta* in this form. It is not only asking *have your eyes seen* — it carries the sense of *have you come to know? have you considered? have you truly grasped the reality of this person?* It is *seeing* that ripens into *understanding.* The English "Have you seen?" actually captures this perfectly — when we say "Oh, I see," we don't mean with our eyes.

So the word is an **invitation to witness** — and a challenge. It hands you the man and says: look closely. Don't take My word for what he believes. I'm going to show you the evidence, and you can read it yourself.

What this single opening word reveals: **Allah is about to make an invisible creed visible — and He invites you to be the witness who confirms the verdict with your own eyes.**

## Element Two: *Yukadhdhibu* — The Verb That Won't Sit Still

Now we arrive at the heart of the diagnosis. The one who does *what?*

**يُكَذِّبُ** — *yukadhdhibu.* "Denies." Our translations say *denies the Recompense.* But that flat English word is hiding two pieces of Arabic machinery, and once you see them, this man comes alive.

First, let me teach you a feature of Arabic before we use it.

In Arabic, many verbs have a "base" form and then an "intensified" form, built by **doubling the middle letter** of the root. The grammarians call this **Form II.** Think of it like the difference between a single tap and a hammering. The root *k-dh-b* (ك ذ ب) at its base means *to lie, to speak falsely.* But double that middle letter — *kadhdhaba* — and the meaning shifts. It no longer means simply *to tell a lie.* It means **to actively, repeatedly, emphatically declare something to be a lie. To brand it false. To wage a campaign of denial against it.**

Here's an everyday way to feel the difference. There's a difference between someone who quietly doubts a news story, and someone who goes on the air every single day to denounce it as a hoax, to call its reporters frauds, to make the *debunking of it* his identity. The first is *kadhaba* — he holds something false. The second is *kadhdhaba* — he **cries it down**, repeatedly, as his project.

The man in our ayah is the second man. **Yukadhdhibu.** He doesn't merely fail to believe in the Recompense. He **rejects it with energy.** He invests in its denial. It is a posture, a stance, almost a hobby.

And there's a second piece. The verb is in the **imperfect tense** — *yukadhdhibu*, not *kadhdhaba.* In Arabic, the imperfect carries the sense of **ongoing, continuous, present action.** It is not "he denied once, long ago." It is **"he keeps on denying."** Right now. Continuously. As a way of life.

Put the two together. Form II intensity, layered onto the ongoing imperfect tense, and you get a man whose denial of the Day of Judgment is not a one-time conclusion he reached. It is a **living, breathing, repeated rejection** — something he does the way you and I breathe. Every day, in how he thinks and speaks and acts, he is *crying down* the reckoning.

Now — what is he denying? **بِٱلدِّينِ** — *bid-dīn.*

This word *dīn* (root د ي ن) is one of the richest in the Qur'an, and here it matters enormously. The four classical commentators are unanimous on its meaning in this ayah: al-Jalālayn says *dīn* here means **al-jazāʾ wa'l-ḥisāb** — *the Recompense and the Reckoning.* Al-Muyassar reads it as **al-baʿth wa'l-jazāʾ** — *the Resurrection and the Recompense.* Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both confirm: *al-jazāʾ wa'l-ḥisāb fi'l-ākhira* — *the Recompense and Reckoning in the Hereafter.* This is *Yawm al-Dīn,* the Day of Judgment, the line we say in every Fātiḥa: *Māliki Yawmi-d-Dīn.*

But hold the wider field of this root in your mind, because it deepens everything. The root *d-y-n* also gives the Arabic word for **debt** — *dayn.* And it gives the word for **religion** — *dīn.* One root, three meanings: a debt owed, a reckoning of accounts, and the whole structure of submission to God.

Do you see how they're the same idea seen from three angles? **A debt is something that must be repaid. A reckoning is the day the debts are called in. And religion is living as though that day is real.**

So when this man *yukadhdhibu bid-dīn* — when he wages his ongoing campaign against *al-dīn* — he is denying that any account will ever be called in. He is declaring, with energy, that **no debt is ever truly owed. No deed is ever truly weighed. The books will never be opened.**

And now we are perfectly positioned for what comes next. Because if you genuinely believed that — that nothing is ever weighed, that no one will ever call you to account — *what would you do to the person who cannot make you pay?*

[PAUSE]

What this verb reveals: **his disbelief is not a passive gap where faith should be. It is an active, daily, energetic insistence that no one is keeping the books — and a man who insists on that will live by it.**

## Element Three: *Yaduʿʿu* and *Lā Yaḥuḍḍu* — The Sound of a Shove and the Silence of Neglect

Here is where the ayah turns from diagnosis to evidence. And here is where I want you to **listen** — not just read.

Watch the hinge between ayah one and ayah two. It is a single letter: the **فَ** — *fa.* **Fa-dhālika** — "**so** that one," "**then** that is the one." In Arabic this little *fa* is the particle of **consequence and result.** It means: *what follows is the proof, the outworking, the necessary consequence of what came before.*

So the structure is: *the one who keeps crying down the Reckoning — **so THAT is the very one who** shoves the orphan.* The *fa* welds the creed to the conduct. It says: you want to identify the man who denies Judgment? **Here is how you recognize him.** Not by his words. By his hands.

And now the hands. **يَدُعُّ ٱلْيَتِيمَ** — *yaduʿʿu al-yatīm.*

Say it out loud. *Yaduʿʿu.* Feel what your mouth does on that doubled *ʿayn* — *duʿʿ* — it's a guttural, percussive, choking sound, and it ends in a kind of shove. The word itself is a push. The root *d-ʿ-ʿ* (د ع ع) does not mean *to neglect* the orphan or *to ignore* him. It means **to drive away with violence, to thrust, to shove, to repel harshly.** This is not a man who forgets the orphan. This is a man who, when the orphan comes near — for a scrap, for shelter, for his own rightful due — **physically thrusts him away.**

And notice: it's in the same ongoing imperfect tense. *Yaduʿʿu* — he **keeps** shoving him. It's a habit. A reflex.

Now feel the sound of it next to the *yukadhdhibu* from before, and you start to hear what the Qur'an is doing acoustically. The denial — *kadhdhaba* — and the shove — *yaduʿʿu* — both have this hard, doubled, percussive texture. **The sound enacts the violence.** A heart that hammers *no* at the Reckoning is a hand that hammers the child away.

Then comes ayah three, and here you must read an **absence** as carefully as you read the shove.

[ayah:107:3] وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ

Listen to this verb too — *yaḥuḍḍu*, root *ḥ-ḍ-ḍ* (ح ض ض), another doubled, percussive sound. It means **to urge, to incite, to actively encourage** someone toward something. And it is **negated**: *lā yaḥuḍḍu* — he does *not* urge it.

Now here is the asymmetry I need you to see, because it is the most piercing thing in the whole passage.

With the orphan, the sin is an **action**: he *shoves.* He does something cruel.

But with the poor man, look at what the ayah measures. It does not say *"he does not feed the poor."* Read it again. It says **"he does not even *urge* the feeding of the poor."** *Yaḥuḍḍu* is not feeding. It is the much, much smaller thing — merely *encouraging others* to feed. Lifting a finger. Saying a word. Nudging your neighbor: *that family is hungry, someone should help.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see how low the bar has been set, and how he still fails to clear it?

The Qur'an is not even asking this man to open his own purse. It is measuring whether he would so much as **speak a word** to move others to feed the hungry. And the verdict is: not even that. Not even the cheapest, most effortless act of compassion — a sentence, an encouragement — survives in this heart.

This is a devastating piece of rhetorical precision. By setting the standard at *yaḥuḍḍu* — mere urging — rather than at *yuṭʿimu* — actual feeding — the ayah exposes the **total** evaporation of mercy. A man might fail to feed the poor out of poverty himself. But to fail to even *encourage* it costs nothing. It requires no wealth. It is pure heart, or its absence. And here, there is nothing there.

So watch the two symptoms together:

- **Active cruelty** — *yaduʿʿu al-yatīm* — he shoves the orphan. The sin of commission.
- **Total absence of compassion** — *lā yaḥuḍḍu* — he won't even urge feeding the poor. The sin of omission, measured at its lowest possible threshold.

Between them, they map the **complete collapse of mercy** — both the doing of harm and the refusal of the smallest good. And both are presented by that little *fa* as the **direct, visible consequence** of one invisible thing: *yukadhdhibu bid-dīn.* He denies the Reckoning.

What these final words reveal: **a creed you cannot see has become a cruelty and a coldness you cannot miss — and the Qur'an has lowered the bar so far that the man's failure to clear it leaves no doubt about what truly lives in his chest.**

### Bridge

So look at what these twelve words have built. *Ara'ayta* turned us into witnesses. *Yukadhdhibu* showed us a denial that is active, daily, energetic — a man hammering *no* at the idea of any reckoning. And then the *fa* of consequence handed us the proof: the shove of the orphan, and the silence where even the cheapest mercy should have spoken.

The grammar has shown us how a hidden belief becomes a visible deed.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About you and me, and what *we* actually believe, measured the only way it can truly be measured.

# PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: What You Do to Those Who Cannot Repay You

Let me start where you already live.

Think, honestly, about how differently you treat two kinds of people. There is the person who can do something for you — your boss, a client, someone influential, someone whose good opinion opens doors. And there is the person who can do **nothing** for you — the exhausted worker cleaning the office at midnight, the beggar at the intersection, the elderly relative who will never again be useful to anyone, the child who interrupts your important work.

We all feel the gravitational pull. Around the first kind of person, we stand a little straighter. We are patient, attentive, warm. Around the second, the warmth costs us something, because there is no return on it. No reciprocity. Nothing comes back.

And so a quiet truth emerges: **your real character is not how you treat people who can repay you. It is how you treat people who cannot.** Anyone is kind to those who hold leverage over them — that's not kindness, that's strategy. The treatment of the powerless is the only place your true self has nowhere to hide.

Now look again at *who* the Qur'an chose for these ayahs.

Not just any vulnerable person. **The orphan** and **the destitute.** Of all the people in that society — and in ours — these two are the purest case of *cannot repay you.* The orphan is a child with no father to protect his interests, no power to enforce a claim, no ability to avenge a wrong, and no future capacity, for years, to be of any use to you. The *miskīn*, the destitute, is by definition the one with nothing — the one who, if you feed him, can never feed you back.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an did not pick these two by accident. It picked the **two human beings with the lowest possible leverage** — the two people who fall completely outside the entire Makkan economy of *what can you do for me.* And it said: **here. Right here, at the very bottom of the ladder of usefulness, is where your true creed is written.**

Because think about what a man's behavior toward the orphan actually reveals. If you genuinely believed that **no one is keeping accounts** — that there is no Day when this child's tears will be weighed against your comfort — then the orphan is simply a weak obstacle. He's in your way and he can't fight back. Shove him. Why not? Who's watching? Who will ever make you pay?

But if you believed — really believed, in your bones — that there is a Reckoning, that this child has a **Protector** who sees, that every shove will one day be set on a scale before the King of the Day of Recompense... could your hand even rise against him?

This is the genius of the surah's logic. **It locates your theology in your hands.** It says your belief about the unseen Judgment is not really proven by your tongue in the mosque. It is proven at the exact moment when you are alone with someone who cannot repay you and cannot stop you — and you decide what to do.

The orphan is a **test instrument.** The destitute is a **test instrument.** They are placed in your path precisely *because* they can offer you nothing, so that whatever you give them can only be explained one way: **you believe Someone is keeping the books.**

[PAUSE]

So here is the question this theme leaves on your tomorrow. Not "do you believe in the Day of Judgment?" — everyone reading this would say yes. The real question is sharper: **where, in your daily life, is the person who can do nothing for you — and how do you actually treat them?** The relative no one visits. The worker no one thanks. The child you're too busy for. The stranger at the door. *That* interaction — the one with no return on it — is the truest reading of your creed you will get all day.

The practical application is almost frighteningly simple: **find the orphan in your life.** Not necessarily a literal orphan — find the person who falls outside your economy of usefulness, who can repay you with nothing — and do one good thing for them this week with no possibility of return. That single act, done for no reason an accountant could explain, is *tasdīq* — it is your hand confirming what your tongue claims to believe.

## Theme Two: Why Losing the Reckoning Kills Compassion

Let me start with a question modern people ask constantly, often without realizing the Qur'an answered it in three lines.

We tend to assume that being kind and being a believer are two separate things. Plenty of people who deny any judgment, any God, any afterlife are perfectly decent. And plenty of religious people are cruel. So we conclude: *morality floats free of belief. Compassion has nothing to do with theology.*

The Qur'an looks at this and says: **look more closely at the foundation under your feet.**

Watch the architecture of these three ayahs one more time, but now as a piece of moral reasoning. Ayah one: a man denies the Reckoning. Ayah two, joined by that *fa* of consequence: **so** he shoves the orphan. The surah is making a claim about cause and effect. It is saying that something about denying the final accounting **loosens the grip of mercy** on the human heart.

Why would that be? Let me build it slowly.

What is it that makes the weak *matter* — really matter, with a claim on you that you cannot brush aside? In a world of pure leverage, the weak don't matter, by definition. The orphan has no power, so he has no claim. The beggar has no wealth, so he has no voice. The whole logic of *what can you do for me* says the powerless are simply... irrelevant. Surplus. Easy to push out of the way.

The **only** thing that can give the powerless a claim — a claim that overrides their uselessness to you — is the conviction that there is a **Higher Court** in which their case is heard. That above the marketplace of human leverage, there sits a Judge who counts the orphan's tear and the hungry man's night, and who will one day call you to answer for both.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it now? **The Day of Judgment is not an abstract doctrine about the future. It is the foundation under the value of every powerless human being on earth.** Take away the Reckoning, and you have not just removed a belief about the afterlife. You have pulled the floor out from under the orphan. You have removed the only reason — when no one is watching and the weak cannot fight back — that you should not simply shove them aside.

This is why the surah moves so directly from *yukadhdhibu bid-dīn* to *yaduʿʿu al-yatīm.* It is not saying every disbeliever is cruel in every moment. It is exposing a **logic** — a gravitational slope. When the books are closed forever, the weak slide, slowly, from *persons with a claim* to *obstacles without one.* The compassion may linger for a while out of habit or temperament. But its root has been cut. And a heart with its root cut will, sooner or later, in the unwatched moment, do what this man does.

And now turn it around, because this is the hopeful half. If denying the Reckoning loosens mercy, then **living in the awareness of the Reckoning is the great engine of mercy.** The believer who truly carries *Māliki Yawmi-d-Dīn* in his chest looks at the powerless person — the one who can do nothing for him — and sees not an irrelevance but a **trust.** A claim. A case being recorded in the Higher Court. The Day of Judgment does not make the believer harsh; it makes him tender, *especially* toward the ones the world has written off, because he knows their file is open before the King.

[PAUSE]

The paradox to sit with — and not rush past — is this: the most "useless" person in your day, the one the world says doesn't matter, is in the eyes of the Reckoning the one whose treatment matters *most*, because their helplessness means whatever you do to them is done purely for the sake of the One who is watching. The world's least leveraged person is heaven's most sensitive scale.

So the practical application for tomorrow is an inner one: **when you next encounter someone who can do nothing for you, deliberately remember the Reckoning in that exact moment.** Not as fear, but as the thing that hands that person back their full worth. Let the awareness that *Someone is keeping the books* be the very thing that softens your face, your tone, your hand. That is *Yawm al-Dīn* doing in your heart precisely what its denial failed to do in his.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we've done the work, step back and see how these three ayahs are built. They are a single diagnostic arc, descending from the invisible to the unmistakable:

- **Ayah 1 — the hidden cause.** *Yukadhdhibu bid-dīn.* The disease itself, locked in the chest, invisible: an active, ongoing denial of any reckoning.
- **Ayah 2 — the visible symptom of commission.** *Yaduʿʿu al-yatīm.* The first thing the disease does with its hands: it *shoves.* Active cruelty toward the most protected-less child.
- **Ayah 3 — the visible symptom of omission, at its lowest measure.** *Lā yaḥuḍḍu.* The second thing the disease does: nothing — not even the cheapest word of encouragement to feed the hungry.

And the whole structure pivots on two tiny words. **Ara'ayta** at the front — *have you seen?* — promising to make the invisible visible. And the **fa** in the middle — *so, therefore* — delivering on that promise by welding the unseen creed to the seen deed.

That is the proof of the surah's claim. It opened by promising to show you a man's hidden disbelief, and it kept the promise — not by reporting his words, but by turning your eyes to his hands. *Have you seen the one who denies the Reckoning? Here — watch him shove a child. Now you've seen his disbelief.*

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes turn out to be the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed us a *fa* of consequence binding belief to behavior. The themes showed us **why** that binding is real: because the powerless have value *only* under a Reckoning, your treatment of the powerless **is** your belief in the Reckoning, made visible. The *fa* isn't a rhetorical flourish. It's a law of the human heart. What you hold about the unseen Judgment will, inevitably, show up in your hands when you meet someone who cannot repay you.

*Ara'ayta* — have you seen? The deepest thing the Qur'an wants you to see in this surah is not that man across the street.

It's your own hands.

## Questions to Carry

1. Who is the "orphan" in my life — the person who can do nothing for me — and what do my hands actually do when I'm alone with them and no one is watching?
2. Is there a place where I withhold not just help, but even the *encouragement* of help — the cheapest mercy, the word that costs nothing — *lā yaḥuḍḍu*?
3. When I treat someone well, is it strategy (they can repay me) or creed (Someone is keeping the books)? How would I know the difference?
4. Do I experience the Day of Judgment as a distant doctrine, or as the living thing that hands the powerless person in front of me their full worth?
5. If a stranger could read my belief in the Reckoning only from how I treated the most useless person in my day — what verdict would they reach?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**What you do to the one who can never repay you is the truest reading of whether you believe anyone is keeping the books.**

## The Closing Invitation

This surah does not ask you to argue better about the Day of Judgment. It asks you to let that Day descend out of your theology and into your hands — into your tone with the relative no one visits, your patience with the child who interrupts you, your willingness to so much as *speak a word* for the hungry. Let one act this week be done for someone who can give you nothing back, for no reason a watching world could explain. That act is your own *ara'ayta* — your own seeing made real. It is the small, quiet hand that confirms what the tongue has always claimed.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, Master of the Day of Recompense — let the certainty of Your reckoning live in my hands and not only on my tongue. Soften me toward the ones who can give me nothing: the orphan, the destitute, the forgotten. And let me never be the one who shoves the weak away, nor the one too cold to speak even a word for the hungry. Make the awareness of standing before You the very thing that makes me gentle. Āmīn.*

---

*A note on this surah's revelation:* The classical commentators preserve **several** traditions about whom these ayahs first described — al-Baghawī records that Muqātil named al-ʿĀṣ ibn Wāʾil al-Sahmī; al-Suddī, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, and Ibn Kaysān named al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra; al-Ḍaḥḥāk named ʿAmr ibn ʿĀʾidh al-Makhzūmī; and ʿAṭāʾ, from Ibn ʿAbbās, said it concerned a hypocrite. The scholars likewise differed on whether the surah is Meccan (ʿAṭāʾ, Jābir, and one report from Ibn ʿAbbās) or Medinan (Qatāda). This multiplicity is itself instructive: the Qur'an names a *type*, not merely a man — which is why the portrait still finds us, in any age, the moment we meet someone who can do nothing for us.
