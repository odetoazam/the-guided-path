# Woe to the Ones Who Pray

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by reading you the ayahs, and then I want us to do something uncomfortable. I want us to feel the shock the first listeners felt — because if we don't feel it, we will read these words the way we read a warning label on a product we never intend to misuse. "That's about *other* people." And the entire force of this surah is built to take that escape route away from us.

Here are the words:

> [ayah:107:4] فَوَيْلٌ لِّلْمُصَلِّينَ
> *Fa-waylun lil-muṣallīn*
> **So woe to those who pray —**
>
> [ayah:107:5] ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَن صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ
> *alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn*
> **those who are heedless of their prayer,**
>
> [ayah:107:6] ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ يُرَآءُونَ
> *alladhīna hum yurāʾūn*
> **those who make a show of it,**
>
> [ayah:107:7] وَيَمْنَعُونَ ٱلْمَاعُونَ
> *wa yamnaʿūna l-māʿūn*
> **and withhold even the smallest kindness.**

[PAUSE]

Now. Sit with the first three words. *Woe to those who pray.*

Not woe to the disbelievers. Not woe to the ones who mock the Quran. Not woe to those who never bow their heads. **Woe to those who pray.**

### Rebuilding the world

Picture Makkah, early in the revelation. The community of believers is small, hunted, mocked. And one of the most visible things that marked a Muslim — one of the things that could get you noticed, beaten, boycotted — was the prayer. To pray was to declare your side. The prayer was the *flag*. It was the costliest, bravest, most identity-defining act a person could perform.

So imagine the listener — a believer, or someone hovering at the edge of belief — hearing a surah begin. It opens by attacking the man who shoves the orphan aside and feels no urgency to feed the hungry (ayahs 1-3). The listener nods. *Yes. That cruel man. Woe to him.* He has positioned himself, in his own mind, on the right side of the line.

And then the surah pivots. And the next word of warning — *woe* — is not aimed at the cruel disbeliever. It swings around and lands on **the one who prays.**

[PAUSE]

That pivot is the entire genius of this surah. The listener thought the danger was *out there*, in the man who denies the Day of Judgment. And the Quran says: the same disease can wear a prayer mat. The same denial of the Day can live inside the man who bows five times.

### The hook

So here is the question I cannot shake, and I want you to carry it with me through everything that follows:

**How does a person pray — and earn woe** *for* **the prayer?**

Not despite the prayer. Not alongside the prayer. The grammar will show us that the prayer itself is named as the site of the failure. There is a way to perform the most sacred act in Islam and have it become the very thing held against you.

What is that way? And — the question underneath the question — *how do I know I'm not doing it?*

Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that should have been a comfort — *al-muṣallīn*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In Arabic, there is a difference between describing what someone *does* and naming what someone *is*. If I say "a man who is walking," I'm describing an action in motion. But if I take that verb and shape it into what's called an *active participle* — an *ism fāʿil* — I'm no longer just describing an action. I'm naming a person by it. I'm turning the act into an identity. "The walker." "The teacher." "The worshipper."

Now look at the word Allah chooses:

> **لِّلْمُصَلِّينَ** — *lil-muṣallīn*

This is from the root **Ṣ-L-W**, the root of *ṣalāh*, the prayer. And it is in **Form II** — the intensive, dedicated form. Form I would be a simple act. Form II, *ṣallā*, is the deliberate, established, ritually-shaped prayer. And then it's cast as an active participle, made into an identity, and made plural: **al-muṣallīn — "the ones whose defining act is prayer."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what's happening? This is not "people who happened to pray once." This is not the careless. This word names the regulars. The committed. The ones for whom prayer is *who they are*. The word itself is a badge of honor. In any other sentence in the Quran, to be called one of *al-muṣallīn* would be praise.

And Allah attaches it to **woe**.

Let's sit with this. The Quran could have said *woe to the hypocrites*. It could have said *woe to the negligent*. Instead it uses the most honorable available label for a religious person — and detonates it. **The title that should have been your safety becomes the address to which the warning is delivered.**

Here is what that reveals, and I want to say it plainly: *being one of the people of prayer is not, by itself, the finish line.* The label is not the reality. You can earn the title and miss the thing the title was supposed to point to. The badge can be real and the heart can be absent.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: One letter that changes everything — *ʿan* and not *fī*

Now we come to what may be the most important two-letter word in the entire surah. And to feel it, you need to understand a distinction that Arabic makes with surgical precision.

Teach yourself this first with an everyday image. There is a difference between being distracted **in** the middle of a conversation — your mind wanders for a second, you snap back — and being distant **from** the conversation altogether, present in body but checked out, the conversation a thing happening *over there* while you are somewhere else entirely.

Arabic marks exactly this difference with two prepositions. **فِي (fī)** means *in, within, inside*. **عَن (ʿan)** means *away from, off, at a distance from*.

Now read the ayah again, and watch which one Allah uses:

> **ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَن صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ**
> *alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn*
> "those who are heedless — **ʿan** — away from — their prayer."

Allah did **not** say *fī ṣalātihim* — heedless *within* their prayer. He said *ʿan ṣalātihim* — heedless *about*, *away from*, *at a distance from* their prayer.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter so much? Because *fī* — heedlessness *within* the prayer — is something that happens to every single one of us. Your mind drifts during the rakʿah. You suddenly can't remember if you prayed two or three. This is so universal that there is a built-in remedy for it — the prostration of forgetfulness, *sujūd al-sahw*. The Prophet ﷺ himself experienced lapses within the prayer. *Fī* is the human condition. *Fī* is not what earns woe.

But *ʿan* — that is something else entirely. *ʿan* is not a wandering mind inside the prayer. *ʿan* is a heart that holds the whole prayer at arm's length. The prayer is something *over there* — a box to be checked, an item on a list, a thing to be gotten through. The classical commentary makes this concrete: as preserved in al-Muyassar, these are the ones who **do not establish the prayer as it should be, and do not pray it at its proper time** — *lā yuqīmūnahā ʿalā wajhihā, wa lā yuʾaddūnahā fī waqtihā.* Al-Jalalayn reaches for a single, piercing synonym to unlock the word *sāhūn* itself — *ghāfilūn*, "oblivious" — and then names the symptom exactly: *yuʾakhkhirūnahā ʿan waqtihā*, "they keep pushing the prayer back past its time." Notice what that specifies. The failure is not that they abandon the prayer. It is that they let it *wait* — that it is always the thing that can be done later, after everything else that actually matters to them is done first.

This is why one of the great Companions, when asked about this very ayah, is reported to have pointed not to the daydreamers *inside* prayer but to those who let its time slip away — who pray it late, carelessly, as an afterthought. The heedlessness *ʿan* the prayer shows up in your *relationship* to the prayer: where it ranks, whether it commands your day or gets squeezed into its margins.

[PAUSE]

So notice what one letter has done. It has told us *which* heedlessness is fatal. Not the lapse of attention every worshipper knows. The heedlessness of *distance* — when the prayer has become external to you, a performance you visit rather than a reality you inhabit.

And that word — *external, performance, visited* — is exactly where the next ayah takes us.

### Element Three: The form of the verb that means "to be seen" — *yurāʾūn*

We have one more piece of grammar to uncover, and it is breathtaking once you see it.

In Arabic, there is a verb-form — **Form III** — whose entire signature is *directedness toward another party*. It carries within it an extra letter, a long *alif*, that grammarians call the *alif of mutuality* — the *alif al-mushāraka*. Form III takes an action and aims it *at* someone. *Kātaba* — not just to write, but to write *to* and *with* a correspondent. The form means: *this act has an audience built into it.*

Now the root here is **R-ʾ-Y** — the root of *seeing*, of *raʾā*, "to see." The plainest verb from it would just mean *to see*. But Allah does not use the plain form. He uses **Form III**:

> **ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ يُرَآءُونَ**
> *alladhīna hum yurāʾūn*

And Form III of "to see" does not mean "they see." It means **"they cause themselves to be seen"** — they perform their deed *at* an audience, *for* the eyes of others. This is the precise morphological root of the word *riyāʾ* — showing-off, performing piety for human approval. The grammar itself carries the audience inside it.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the hinge of the whole passage. The deepest problem with the heedless worshipper is not laziness. It's *redirection*. The prayer was supposed to be aimed at One — *iyyāka naʿbudu*, "You alone we worship." But the Form III verb tells us the deed has quietly been re-aimed. It is no longer pointed at Allah. It is pointed at *people*. The *alif of mutuality* has crept into the act of worship, and now there is a hidden audience the prayer is really performing for.

And notice something about the *tense*. Go back and feel the shift. The heedlessness in ayah 5 was given as *sāhūn* — an active participle, a *state of being*, settled, stable, who-they-are. But ayah 6 and 7 give us *verbs* — *yurāʾūn*, *yamnaʿūn* — the present-tense, ongoing, repeated *mudāriʿ*. 

Here is what that contrast reveals: **their distance from the prayer is a fixed condition; their showing-off and their withholding are the live, recurring symptoms of it.** The heedlessness is the disease that sits still inside them. The performing and the withholding are what that disease *does*, over and over, out in the world. The grammar has just handed us a diagnosis: a hollow relationship with Allah (the state) inevitably leaks out as performance toward people and stinginess toward people (the actions).

[PAUSE — let this land]

### The bridge

Let me gather what these three pieces have shown us.

The **participle** *al-muṣallīn* told us this is about the committed, the regular, the people of prayer — not the careless outsider.

The single letter ***ʿan*** told us the failure is not a wandering mind inside the prayer but a heart that holds the whole prayer at a distance.

And the **Form III verb** *yurāʾūn* told us where that distant heart re-aims the prayer: at people, for show.

Three grammatical features. One unified portrait: a person who has perfected the *form* of worship and lost its *direction*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way our own hearts actually work.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The performed self

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You have, on some device within reach right now, a version of yourself that you have curated for an audience. We all do. The photo chosen from among forty. The caption edited five times to sound effortless. The good deed that somehow, naturally, ended up documented. There is a self we *are*, and there is a self we *present* — and the gap between them is the most quietly exhausting feature of modern life.

We have a word for the engine behind it: the performance of an identity for an audience whose approval we crave. We do good — and we check who saw. We help — and we feel the help is somehow incomplete until it's witnessed.

Now hold that familiar exhaustion up against the ayah. *Alladhīna hum yurāʾūn.* The Form III verb. The audience built into the act.

Here is what the Quran is telling us that the modern world cannot: **this disease can infect even your prayer.** The most private, most vertical, most God-directed act you perform — even *that* can be quietly redirected toward the human gaze. You can stand in the night and feel your heart reach not for Allah but for the imagined impression you are making. You can lengthen the rukūʿ because someone is watching. You can soften your recitation because the row behind you can hear.

Let's go one level deeper. Why does this happen? Because *riyāʾ* is not really about wanting to be seen. It's about *where you have located your worth*. The one who performs his prayer for people has, underneath, decided that the verdict that matters is *theirs*. He is standing before Allah while his heart stands before the crowd. He has two qiblas, and the second one has quietly won.

[PAUSE]

And here is the paradox the surah refuses to soften. This man *is praying.* He has not stopped. The shell is intact, beautiful, regular. *Al-muṣallīn.* The tragedy is not the absence of the act. It's the absence of the One the act was for. He has built a magnificent house and forgotten to invite the Owner inside.

I'm not going to let us turn this only on others. The question the ayah forces is inward: *When I do something good — do I feel it is finished when Allah has seen it? Or does some part of me wait, restless, until a person sees it too?* That restlessness is the *alif of mutuality* whispering inside the deed.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Find one act of worship — one — and deliberately hide it. Give a charity no one will know about. Pray a unit of prayer in a room where no one can see. Not because hidden worship is holier in form, but because hiding it is how you *test the direction of your own heart.* If hiding it makes the deed feel pointless, you've just discovered who your audience really was. And that discovery is a mercy — because now you can begin to turn the deed back toward its rightful Owner.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: *Al-Māʿūn* — the woe hidden in the smallest things

Now we arrive at the last word of the surah, and it is the strangest, most unexpected place for a surah about prayer and judgment to land.

> **وَيَمْنَعُونَ ٱلْمَاعُونَ**
> *wa yamnaʿūna l-māʿūn*
> "and they withhold *al-māʿūn*."

What is *al-māʿūn*? Here the classical commentary gives us a composite picture, and every angle deepens the same point. The lexicographers and the early commentators describe it as the *small things*, the everyday necessities people lend one another without a second thought: the bucket, the cooking pot, the bowl, salt, water, a needle, the loan of a tool you'll get back by evening. Some read it more broadly as *zakāh* or any due assistance. But the dominant, most arresting sense is this: **al-māʿūn is the kindness so small that refusing it is almost more revealing than the kindness itself.**

Think about what it takes to withhold these things. Nobody becomes poor by lending a bucket. Nobody is impoverished by sharing a pinch of salt. To say *no* to *al-māʿūn* is not stinginess of the wallet. It is **stinginess of the soul.** It is a heart so contracted, so curved in on itself, that it cannot release even what costs it nothing.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the architecture of the whole surah closing around us. The surah began with a man who *pushes away the orphan* and *does not urge feeding the poor* (ayahs 2-3). It ends with worshippers who *withhold the smallest kindness* (ayah 7). The disbeliever at the start and the heedless worshipper at the end **commit the same crime.** Both have hearts that will not open toward the vulnerable. The prayer in the middle was supposed to be the very thing that pried the heart open — and for these people, it didn't.

This is the surah's verdict, and it's devastating in its precision: **a prayer that does not soften you toward the bucket and the orphan is a prayer that has failed at its one job.** The whole point of standing before the Most Merciful five times a day is to become, even slightly, more merciful. If you can bow before *ar-Raḥmān* at dawn and refuse your neighbor a pot at noon, the bowing did not reach your heart. It stayed *ʿan* — at a distance — exactly as ayah 5 warned.

Let's sit with why Allah chose the *smallest* things to be the final measure. He could have ended with "they withhold great wealth." He didn't. He chose the bucket. Because the great gifts can be given for show — *that's* the *yurāʾūn* of ayah 6. But the tiny, unwitnessed kindness — the thing no one will praise you for, the favor too small to post about — *that* is where the true state of the heart is exposed. **The smallest kindness is the most honest test, precisely because there's no audience to perform for.** Ayah 6 and ayah 7 are two sides of one coin: the man who performs the big visible deeds is the same man who withholds the small invisible ones — because for him, the whole thing was always about being seen.

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Watch for the *māʿūn*-sized moments. The text you could answer but don't feel like it. The small favor asked when you're tired. The minor inconvenience of someone else's need. These are not negligible. The Quran has just told you they are *load-bearing* — they carry the weight of the whole surah's verdict. Tomorrow, say yes to one *māʿūn*-sized request you would normally let slide. It costs you a bucket. It tells you, and tells Allah, that the prayer is reaching the heart.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we've done the work, stand back and see the building whole.

Surah Al-Maʿun has two halves, and they are mirror images:

**First half (1-3):** A man who *denies the Day of Judgment*. Al-Tabari fixes the meaning with precision: to deny *al-dīn* here is to deny *thawāb Allāh wa ʿiqābahu* — Allah's reward and His punishment — and so to feel no pull to obey His command or heed His prohibition. And the proof of that denial is social: he repels the orphan, he doesn't urge feeding the poor. If no reckoning is coming, the weak have no claim on him. *Vertical denial → horizontal cruelty.*

**Second half (4-7):** Worshippers who *outwardly affirm* religion — they pray! — but the prayer is held at a distance (*ʿan*), redirected to people (*yurāʾūn*), and severed from mercy (*they withhold al-māʿūn*). *Hollow vertical → horizontal cruelty.*

Do you see it? The disbeliever and the heedless worshipper **arrive at the same destination from opposite directions.** One never claimed faith; one performs it constantly. But both end with a closed fist toward the weak. The surah's hidden thesis is this: **the true measure of your relationship with Allah is what it does to your relationship with the vulnerable.** Severed from mercy, denial and prayer become indistinguishable.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two sides.

The grammar showed us a worshipper whose prayer is held at a *distance* (*ʿan*), re-aimed at an *audience* (Form III *yurāʾūn*), as a settled *state* that *acts out* as performance and stinginess (participle vs. verb).

The themes showed us the human cost of that grammar: a performed self that worships for the gaze, and a contracted heart that cannot release even a bucket.

They meet in one place: **a prayer that has lost its direction loses its effect.** It no longer points up, so it no longer reaches out.

### Questions to carry

1. When I pray, *where* is my heart standing — before Allah, or before the people who might be watching?
2. Is my prayer something I *inhabit*, or something I *visit* and leave?
3. When a small, unwitnessed kindness is asked of me, what does my first instinct reveal about what the prayer has — or hasn't — done to my heart?
4. If someone measured my faith not by my prayers but by my mercy toward the weakest person in my life, what would they conclude?
5. What is one deed I would lose all motivation to do if I knew no human would ever see it — and what does that tell me?

### One-sentence distillation

**A prayer that holds Allah at a distance, performs for people, and leaves the heart too closed to lend a bucket is not a prayer in your favor — it is a prayer counted against you.**

### The closing invitation

I'm not inviting you to fear your prayer. I'm inviting you to *reclaim* it. The same act that can earn woe can, with the heart turned rightly, become your salvation. The difference is not in the bowing — it's in the direction. Tomorrow, before you say *Allāhu akbar*, take one breath and ask only: *Who am I doing this for?* And then let the answer flow out of the prayer mat and into the smallest kindness the day asks of you. That is how you know the prayer arrived.

### A brief duʿa

O Allah, do not let our prayers stand at a distance from us. Draw our hearts *into* them, and turn them toward You alone. Soften us by every prostration until even the smallest kindness flows from us easily — and never let the badge of worship become the evidence against us.

*Āmīn.*
