# The Honor That Cannot Be Owned

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic, in sound, in plain English — and then let me build the world they fell into.

> **يَقُولُونَ لَئِن رَّجَعْنَآ إِلَى ٱلْمَدِينَةِ لَيُخْرِجَنَّ ٱلْأَعَزُّ مِنْهَا ٱلْأَذَلَّ ۚ وَلِلَّهِ ٱلْعِزَّةُ وَلِرَسُولِهِۦ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱلْمُنَـٰفِقِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ**

*Yaqūlūna la-in rajaʿnā ilā l-madīnati la-yukhrijanna l-aʿazzu minhā l-adhall. Wa lillāhi l-ʿizzatu wa li-rasūlihi wa lil-muʾminīn, wa lākinna l-munāfiqīna lā yaʿlamūn.*

"They say, 'If we return to Medina, the more honorable will surely drive out the more lowly from it.' But honor belongs to Allah, and to His Messenger, and to the believers — yet the hypocrites do not know."

### Rebuild the world

Picture the scene. The Muslims are coming home from a campaign against the tribe of Banu al-Mustaliq. The fighting is over. The danger you'd expect has passed. And it is precisely here — on the road home, in the safety of victory — that the real fracture appears. Not from the enemy outside. From inside the camp.

A quarrel breaks out at a watering hole between a man of the Emigrants and a man of the Helpers. Small thing. The kind of thing that flares and dies. But there is a man watching for exactly this kind of spark — **ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy**, the man the community of Madina had nearly crowned king before the Prophet ﷺ arrived and the whole arrangement dissolved. He has been carrying a wound ever since. And now, in front of his circle, he says it. The major narrations preserved by al-Qurtubī, al-Baghawī, and recorded in al-Jalālayn all place these words in his mouth: *when we get back to Madina, the mighty will throw out the worthless.* He means himself by "the mighty." He means the Prophet ﷺ and the believers by "the worthless."

[PAUSE]

Now hold that. A man stands in the desert and divides the world into two columns. Above the line: me and my people, *the honored*. Below the line: them, *the disgraced*. He is so sure of the arrangement that he swears an oath about what will happen when they reach the city.

And Allah hears it.

These were words spoken in a private circle, on a road, away from the Prophet ﷺ. The young companion who overheard them — Zayd ibn Arqam — carried them back, and was nearly disbelieved. Ibn Ubayy denied everything. And then this surah descended, with the words inside it, exact, eternal, recited until the end of time.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to sit with before we even open the words. This man built his entire identity on a single arrangement: *I am the honored one; they are the lowly ones.* His whole sense of self rested on owning a thing called **ʿizzah** — honor, might, the quality of being un-humiliatable.

The ayah is going to do something to that arrangement. It is not going to argue with him about *who* is on top. It is going to reach underneath the entire structure and remove the floor. And it does it with so few words that you can miss the demolition if you're not listening.

Let's listen.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The two words that fight each other: *al-aʿazz* and *al-adhall*

Before we touch the ayah, I want to teach you one small feature of Arabic, because the whole verse turns on it.

Arabic has a special form for comparison and superlative — grammarians call it the **elative**, the *afʿal* pattern. It's the shape behind words like *akbar* (greater / greatest) and *aṣghar* (smaller / smallest). One word carries both "more" and "most" — context decides. The important thing about the elative is this: **it is inherently relational.** It cannot exist alone. *Greater* demands a *than*. *Most honored* demands a crowd to be most honored *among*. You cannot be "the greater" in an empty room. The word only works if there is a ranking, a ladder, a hierarchy already assumed.

Hold that feature in your hand. Now look at what Ibn Ubayy says:

> **لَيُخْرِجَنَّ ٱلْأَعَزُّ مِنْهَا ٱلْأَذَلَّ**
> *la-yukhrijanna l-aʿazzu minhā l-adhall*
> "the **more honored** will surely drive out from it the **more lowly**."

Two elatives, back to back. *Al-aʿazz*, from the root ʿ-z-z, the most honored, the mightiest. *Al-adhall*, from the root dh-l-l, the most lowly, the most humiliated. Listen to them next to each other — *al-aʿazz... al-adhall* — they almost rhyme, they mirror each other in shape, two opposite poles cast in the same mold. The sound itself builds the ladder he's imagining: top rung, bottom rung, locked together.

Let's sit with what he has done linguistically. By using elatives, he has not merely insulted the believers. He has asserted that honor and lowliness are **fixed positions on a permanent scale**. *The* honored. *The* lowly. Definite. Settled. As if these were ranks stamped into the nature of things, and he simply happens to occupy the high one.

Now notice something — and this is the first place the ground shifts.

**He never says who is who.**

Read it again. "The more honored will drive out the more lowly." The Arabic gives you no names. No subject is specified beyond the bare label. Grammatically, the sentence is wide open. Ibn Ubayy *intends* himself as *al-aʿazz* — the classical commentators are unanimous that in his mind, he is the mighty and the believers are the lowly. But the words he chose don't fasten those labels to anyone. He left the slots empty and assumed everyone would fill them the way he did.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Allah preserves the sentence *exactly* — and then, three words later, fills the empty slots Himself. And He fills them in the reverse of what Ibn Ubayy assumed. The man left a gap thinking it was obvious who stood where. Allah walked into that gap and inverted it. *You said the honored will expel the lowly? Yes. That will happen. But you have the names backwards.*

This is the genius of leaving it unnamed. If Ibn Ubayy had said "I will drive out Muhammad," the lie would be specific and small. By speaking in absolute categories — *the* honored, *the* lowly — he made a claim about the structure of reality itself. And a claim about the structure of reality is exactly the kind of claim Allah answers. He didn't reach down to correct one man's boast. He reached down to correct one man's entire map of the universe.

What this single pair of words reveals: **the hypocrite's worldview is a fixed hierarchy with himself permanently at the top — and he was so certain of the arrangement he didn't even bother to name the players.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — The oath he swears, and the word that buries it

Now look at the verb. He doesn't say "the honored will drive out the lowly." He says **لَيُخْرِجَنَّ** — *la-yukhrijanna.*

Let me teach you what's loaded into that one word, because Arabic packs an extraordinary amount of certainty into it.

There are two intensifiers stacked here. The first is the **lām** at the front — the *lām* of the oath. It's the grammatical residue of swearing. It signals: *I solemnly affirm that...* The second is the **nūn** doubled and stressed at the end — what grammarians call *nūn al-tawkīd*, the heavy nūn of emphasis. Arabic uses it to nail a future action down as absolutely, unquestionably going to happen. Put them together — *la-yukhrijanna* — and you have one of the most emphatic constructions the language can produce. It's not "will expel." It's closer to: *I swear it will most certainly, beyond any doubt, expel.*

The verb itself, *yukhrijanna*, is from kh-r-j, to go out — but in this form (Form IV) it's causative: not "to go out" but "to make go out," to **drive out, expel, eject.** He is swearing, with the full emphatic machinery of the language, that he will physically eject the Prophet ﷺ and his people from their own city.

Feel the confidence in it. This is a man at the absolute peak of his certainty. Every grammatical resource of emphasis, deployed. The oath-lām, the doubled nūn, the causative verb of forcible expulsion. There is no hedge anywhere in this sentence. He is *sure*.

Now stay with me, because the ayah is about to do something devastating, and it does it quietly.

Follow the verse to its very last word:

> **وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱلْمُنَـٰفِقِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ**
> *wa lākinna l-munāfiqīna lā yaʿlamūn*
> "but the hypocrites **do not know.**"

*Lā yaʿlamūn.* They do not know.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened across the span of one verse? It opens with the most emphatic *certainty* the language can express — *la-yukhrijanna*, I absolutely swear it will happen — and it closes with the flat verdict that the man who swore it **does not even know.** The sentence is an arc from total confidence to total ignorance. The louder he swore, the less he understood.

This is one of the most piercing pairings in the Qur'an. We tend to assume that certainty and knowledge travel together — that the more sure someone is, the more they must know. The ayah severs that assumption with a blade. Here is a man at maximum certainty and minimum knowledge *at the same time*. His confidence is not evidence of his knowledge. His confidence is a symptom of his ignorance. *Lā yaʿlamūn* — they do not know — is placed exactly where, in his own mind, he was most sure.

And notice: Allah does not call him a liar here. He doesn't say *the hypocrites are lying.* He says *the hypocrites do not know.* That's almost gentler and far more crushing. A liar at least knows the truth he's concealing. This man doesn't even have that. He has built an oath, a worldview, an entire sense of his own greatness — on a thing he has fundamentally misunderstood. He is not a clever villain who knows the truth and fights it. He is a man stumbling in the dark, swearing oaths about a room he cannot see.

What this reveals: **the volume of our certainty is no measure of the truth of our claim. The most emphatic human swearing can sit directly on top of the deepest ignorance — and often does.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — Where Allah places the word "honor"

Now the center of the whole thing. The pivot. Watch the word order, because in Arabic, *order is meaning.*

When Allah answers, He could have said it in the ordinary arrangement: *al-ʿizzatu lillāh* — "honor belongs to Allah." Subject, then owner. Normal. Instead He says:

> **وَلِلَّهِ ٱلْعِزَّةُ**
> *wa lillāhi l-ʿizzah*
> Literally: "And **to Allah** belongs the honor."

He puts the owner *first.* "To Allah" comes before "the honor." Let me teach you why this matters, because it's a precise rhetorical device.

In Arabic, when you take a phrase that would normally come later and move it to the front — grammarians call this **taqdīm**, "fronting" — you are doing one specific thing: you are creating **exclusivity, restriction, specialness.** Fronting the thing that should come second means: *this, and only this. This belongs here and nowhere else.*

We do a faint version of this in English with stress. "*To Allah* belongs the honor" — say it that way and you hear it: not to you, not to your tribe, not to anyone you were counting on. The fronting builds a wall of exclusivity around the word ʿizzah.

So when Ibn Ubayy says "I am the *aʿazz*, the possessor of *ʿizz*" — Allah responds not by arguing about *who* has more honor, but by relocating the entire category. *You think honor is a thing on your ladder, a quantity you possess more of than them? No. Honor — the whole of it — belongs to Allah. It was never on your ladder at all. It was never yours to own.*

[PAUSE]

And now feel the structure of the giving. *Wa lillāhi l-ʿizzah* — to Allah belongs honor — *wa li-rasūlihi* — and to His Messenger — *wa lil-muʾminīn* — and to the believers.

Trace that chain. Honor originates in Allah. It flows to His Messenger. It flows to the believers. It is not generated by tribe, or numbers, or wealth, or the throne of Madina that Ibn Ubayy lost. It is *lent downward* from its single Source. The believers have ʿizzah — real ʿizzah — but notice they have it *third in line, as recipients,* never as owners. Their honor is connected to its origin like a lamp connected to a current. Unplug it from Allah and it goes dark.

The classical commentators draw out what each ʿizzah means in this chain. Al-Baghawī explains it beautifully: the ʿizzah of Allah is His overpowering dominance over all that is beneath Him; the ʿizzah of His Messenger is the raising of his religion over every other way; the ʿizzah of the believers is Allah's aid given to them against their enemies. Three honors — but one Source. The Messenger's honor and the believers' honor are *branches* of Allah's. They have no independent root.

And here is the quiet completion of the demolition. Ibn Ubayy located his ʿizzah in himself — *al-aʿazz*, a fixed rank he owned. Allah's answer says: honor is not a rank anyone owns; it is a gift that flows from one Owner. The man was clutching something he was never holding. He thought ʿizzah was a possession. It was always a loan — and he had borrowed it from the wrong lender.

What this reveals: **honor is not a property of persons. It is a property of Allah, lent to those who attach themselves to Him. The moment you imagine you own it, you have already lost it — because you have unplugged it from its only Source.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

So look at what these three small features have shown us, standing together.

The two elatives showed us a man imagining honor as a *fixed rank on a ladder he tops.* The emphatic oath, collapsing into *they do not know*, showed us that his towering certainty sat on an empty foundation. And the fronted *lillāhi l-ʿizzah* showed us where honor actually lives — not on any human ladder at all, but as a possession of Allah, lent downward, never owned.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about one man's words — but about something running through every one of us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — Borrowed honor: where we plug in our worth

Let me start where you live, not where Ibn Ubayy lived.

There is a quiet calculation running underneath most of our days. It asks: *Where do I stand? Am I above or below? Did that interaction raise me or lower me?* We're often not even aware of it, but it's there — at the dinner with the more successful friend, in the comment section, when the promotion goes to someone else, when we walk into a room and instantly, automatically, locate ourselves on the ladder. *Am I the aʿazz here, or the adhall?*

We are all running Ibn Ubayy's calculation. The only difference is the currency. His was tribe and political throne. Ours might be income, followers, the school our kids got into, the body in the mirror, the title in the email signature. But the structure is identical: we locate a thing called *worth* somewhere in the world, and then we anxiously check, all day, whether we still have enough of it.

And here is the thing about every one of those currencies. **They are all borrowed, and the lender can call the loan at any time.**

Think about it honestly. The honor you get from your job — what happens to it when you retire, or get laid off, or the industry changes? The honor you get from your body — what happens to it across thirty years? The honor from your wealth, your network, your reputation — each one is a lamp plugged into a wall socket that is not yours, that someone else controls, that can be switched off without your permission. You don't own the current. You're renting the glow.

This is the trap the ayah exposes. Not that Ibn Ubayy wanted honor — wanting honor is human, even healthy. The trap is *where he plugged the cord in.* He attached his ʿizzah to a socket called "my tribe, my status, my numbers" — and assumed the current was his own, permanent, un-switch-off-able. He said *al-aʿazz*, the honored one, as though it were carved into him like a name.

And the ayah's answer is not "honor is bad." The answer is: *you plugged into the wrong source.* **Wa lillāhi l-ʿizzah.** There is exactly one socket where the current never fails, one Source that no one else controls, one Lender who does not call the loan out of cruelty or whim. Plug your worth into Allah, and your honor becomes as stable as its Source. Plug it into anything else, and you have built your entire sense of self on a wall you don't own, in a house that will be demolished.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this sharper, because there's a subtle thing here. The believers in this ayah *do* have ʿizzah. Honor is not taken away from human beings — it's *relocated*. So this isn't a teaching about becoming nobody, about having no dignity, no standing, no honor. It's a teaching about the *direction the cord runs.* The believer can walk into any room — the room of the wealthier, the more powerful, the more credentialed — and feel no tremor on the ladder, because his worth was never on that ladder. It's connected to something none of those people control. That's not arrogance. It's the opposite of arrogance. It's the deep, quiet steadiness of a man who knows his honor is *given*, not *won* — and therefore cannot be *taken* by anyone except the One who gave it.

The arrogant man is fragile, always, because borrowed honor must be defended. Ibn Ubayy *had* to threaten, had to swear the oath, had to draw the ladder — because honor you think you own is honor you are terrified of losing. The believer with ʿizzah from Allah has nothing to defend. You cannot rob a man of a thing he never claimed to own.

**What changes tomorrow:** the next time you feel the ladder-calculation start — the comparison, the flush of being above or the sting of being below — catch it and ask one question. *Which socket is this honor plugged into?* If it's plugged into a person, a number, a possession, a reputation — you've found a piece of your worth that someone else can switch off, and that's worth knowing about, because it's a piece you'll spend your life anxiously defending. The practice is to keep, quietly and deliberately, moving the cord. *Wa lillāhi l-ʿizzah.* To Allah belongs the honor. Plug back in there. Every time.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two — The certainty of the man who cannot see

Now the harder one. Because Theme One we can accept about *other* people. This one is about us.

Remember the arc of the verse: maximum certainty — *la-yukhrijanna*, I swear it absolutely — collapsing into *lā yaʿlamūn*, they do not know. The most confident sentence and the most ignorant man were the same man.

We have a deep, wired assumption that confidence is a signal of truth. We trust the certain voice in the room. We trust our *own* certainty most of all — when something *feels* obviously true, we rarely interrogate it. And the ayah is telling us something disturbing: **the feeling of certainty and the fact of knowledge are not the same thing, and they can come completely apart.**

Think about how Ibn Ubayy could be so sure and so wrong at once. It wasn't a calculation error. He didn't add the numbers up wrong. He was *blind to an entire dimension of reality* — the dimension where honor belongs to Allah, where the unseen current runs, where the ladder he was standing on did not actually exist. Within his flat, two-dimensional map of tribe and status, his conclusion was perfectly logical. *We have the numbers, we have the standing, therefore we are the aʿazz.* The logic was fine. The map was missing a dimension.

That's what *lā yaʿlamūn* names. Not stupidity. Not a failure of reasoning. A failure of *map.* He was reasoning flawlessly about a reality that was not the real one.

[PAUSE]

Now turn it on yourself, gently. Because here is the uncomfortable application: *every confident wrong conclusion feels, from the inside, exactly like a confident right one.* Ibn Ubayy did not experience himself as deluded. He experienced himself as *obviously correct.* The blindness was invisible to the blind man. That's the nature of blindness.

So how many of our own certainties are reasoned flawlessly on a map that's missing a dimension? The certainty that this loss is the end of us. The certainty that that person is beneath us, or that we are beneath them. The certainty that our worth depends on the thing we're terrified of losing. The certainty that we know how our life *should* go, who is honored and who is lowly, what counts and what doesn't. We swear our private oaths — *la-yukhrijanna* — about all of it, with the full emphatic confidence of people who cannot see the dimension where Allah is arranging things entirely differently.

And the answer the ayah models is not "be less confident about everything" — that's just a different paralysis. The answer is to **build the missing dimension into the map.** The believer's humility is not "I know nothing." It's: *the most decisive-seeming facts of my life — who's up, who's down, what's honor, what's loss — are being arranged by One who sees the dimension I'm blind to, and I will hold my certainties loosely enough to let Him be right.*

There's a mercy hidden in this, too. If your most confident reading of your own situation — *this is humiliation, this is the end, I am the adhall here* — can be as wrong as Ibn Ubayy's confident reading was, then your darkest certainty about yourself is also subject to *lā yaʿlamūn.* You do not actually know that your life is the failure you're so sure it is. You're reading a flat map. The One who sees the full dimension may have you written down, that very moment, among *al-muʾminīn* to whom honor belongs.

**What changes tomorrow:** when you next notice yourself *gripping* a certainty — especially a certainty that someone is honored and someone is lowly, that something is a final humiliation or a final triumph — pause and say to yourself the diagnosis the ayah gives: *maybe this is a flat map.* Not to become paralyzed. To make a small, deliberate space — a held breath of room — for Allah to be arranging a dimension you cannot see. The companions who heard *lā yaʿlamūn* learned to distrust the loudness of their own certainty. That distrust is not weakness. It is the beginning of actually knowing.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bring it together

Look at how the two halves of this verse are really one motion.

The first half is a man's entire inner world, spoken aloud: *I locate honor in myself and my tribe; I am certain of the arrangement; I will defend my position by force.* Borrowed honor and blind certainty — and they go together, always. They are the same disease seen from two angles. **You can only be that certain about a ladder if you cannot see that the ladder isn't real.** His confidence (Theme Two) was the direct product of where he'd plugged his worth (Theme One). Misplace your honor, and you will be blindly certain about a world that isn't there. The two themes are one.

And the linguistic discoveries are the same insight from the inside of the words. The two elatives *built* the false ladder. The collapsing oath *exposed* the blind certainty. And the fronted *lillāhi l-ʿizzah* *relocated* the honor to its only true Source — pulling the cord out of the wall Ibn Ubayy thought he owned and showing where the current actually originates. Grammar and theme are saying the identical thing: *honor is not yours to own, and your certainty that it is, is itself the proof you cannot see.*

### Questions to carry

- Where, exactly, is my sense of worth plugged in right now — and who controls that socket?
- What is one certainty I am gripping so tightly that I've stopped asking whether my map is flat?
- When I walk into a room, do I locate myself on a ladder? Whose ladder is it — and did Allah build it, or did I?
- Is there a place in my life where I feel like *al-adhall*, the lowly one — and have I considered that this is a flat reading of a dimension I cannot see?
- What would I stop defending, if I truly believed my honor was given by Allah and could not be taken by anyone else?

### One-sentence distillation

The hypocrite swears, with all the certainty language can hold, that he owns honor and can withhold it — and Allah answers, in three fronted words, that honor was never his to own, only ever lent from the one Source he was too blind to see.

### The closing invitation

This is not a call to want no honor. The believers in this very ayah are *given* honor. It is a call to move the cord — to stop spending your one life anxiously defending a worth plugged into walls you don't own, and to plug it, deliberately and daily, into the one Source that no person, no loss, no humiliation, and no death can ever switch off. And it is an invitation to hold your certainties — about who is up and who is down, including about yourself — loosely enough to let the One who sees every dimension be right.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, to You alone belongs all honor — pull our hearts out of every borrowed socket and plug them into You, where no one can ever switch off the light. Where we are certain, show us the dimension we cannot see; and where we feel ourselves to be the lowliest in the room, write us, by Your mercy, among the believers to whom You have lent Your ʿizzah. Save us from the certainty of those who do not know.
