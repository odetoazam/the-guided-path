# Timber Propped Against a Wall: The Magnificent Hollow

---

## INTRODUCTION

> [ayah:63:4] ۞ وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ ۖ وَإِن يَقُولُوا۟ تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ ۖ كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُّسَنَّدَةٌ ۖ يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ هُمُ ٱلْعَدُوُّ فَٱحْذَرْهُمْ ۚ قَـٰتَلَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ ۖ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

*Wa idhā raʾaytahum tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum, wa in yaqūlū tasmaʿ li-qawlihim, kaʾannahum khushubun musannada, yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim, humu-l-ʿaduwwu faḥdharhum, qātalahumu-llāhu annā yuʾfakūn.*

"And when you see them, their bodies impress you; and if they speak, you listen to their speech. They are as if they were timber propped up. They reckon every shout to be against them. They are the enemy, so beware of them. May Allah destroy them — how are they deluded?"

---

### Rebuilding the World

Step into Madinah, a few years after the Hijra.

The Prophet ﷺ is building a community out of almost nothing. And inside that community is a problem that has no clean edges. There is a group of men who pray in the same rows. Who give in the same collections. Who march out — sometimes — in the same direction as the army. They say the *shahada* with their mouths. By every visible measure, they are part of the family.

And they are not.

These are the *munafiqun* — the hypocrites. And you have to understand something about how they appeared, because the whole verse turns on it. They were not marginal figures skulking at the edges. **They were impressive.** Some of them were the most striking men in the assembly. Tall. Well-built. Beautifully dressed. And when they spoke — they spoke *well*. Eloquent. Measured. The kind of voice that makes a room go quiet to listen.

Ibn Abbas reported that the figure most associated with this verse, Abdullah ibn Ubayy, was *jasīm* — large, imposing in frame — *faṣīḥ* — eloquent — *dhalq al-lisān* — fluent and sharp-tongued. When he spoke, even the Prophet ﷺ would turn and listen to what he was saying.

So picture the scene the original listeners lived inside. You are in the *masjid*. The most magnificent-looking man in the room rises to speak. His presence fills the space. His words are polished. Everything your eyes and ears can measure says: *this is a man of weight, a man of substance, a leader.*

[PAUSE]

And then Allah speaks one sentence that takes that entire magnificent surface — and reveals what is behind it.

---

### The Hook

There is a word in this verse — one image — that does something almost cruel in its precision. Allah does not say these men are weak. He does not say they are ugly or stupid or small.

He grants them everything the eye can see. He grants them everything the ear can hear.

And then, with a single comparison, He empties all of it out — and shows you that the most impressive surface you have ever stood in front of can be standing over nothing at all.

What is the comparison? And why did Allah choose *this* object, out of everything in creation, to describe a human being?

Let's go and see.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ — The Two Channels of Impression

Before we touch the verse, let me name something about how human beings actually judge each other.

When you meet someone new, you do not have access to their soul. You cannot see their *taqwa*. You cannot weigh their sincerity on a scale. So what do you actually use? You use two channels, and only two, in the first moments: **what they look like, and what they say.** The eye and the ear. Presence and speech. Everything else — character, honesty, what they are like at 3am when no one is watching — all of that is *inferred* later, from these two surfaces.

Now look at how Allah builds this verse.

> وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ
> "And when you see them, their bodies impress you."

> وَإِن يَقُولُوا۟ تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ
> "And if they speak, you listen to their speech."

Notice the architecture. Allah has named **exactly the two channels** — sight and sound, body and word — and nothing else. This is deliberate. He is mapping the entire surface through which one human impresses another, and He is conceding the whole thing to them. *Yes,* says Allah. *On the eye: they win. On the ear: they win.*

Let's open the first word. **تُعْجِبُكَ** — *tuʿjibuka*. The root is ʿayn-jīm-bā, the root of *ʿajab*, wonder, amazement. It is in Form IV here, which makes it transitive — it does not mean "they wonder," it means **they cause wonder in you.** Their bodies *act upon* you. The grammar puts you, the viewer, on the receiving end. You are not choosing to be impressed. The impression is being *done to you*. Their *ajsām* — their physiques, their frames — reach out and seize your admiration before you have decided anything.

And then **تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ** — "you listen to their speech." Notice it does not say *tasmaʿuhum*, "you hear them." It says *tasmaʿ **li**-qawlihim* — you *listen to* their speech, you incline your ear toward it, you lean in. There is a willingness in it. The kind of listening you give to something worth your attention.

[PAUSE]

So stay with what Allah has done. In two short clauses He has granted these men a total victory on the surface. Magnificent to see. Compelling to hear. If impression were the whole story, they would be the best of the community.

And here is the thing I want you to feel before we move on: **Allah is not being sarcastic.** He is not saying "you *think* they look good." He is confirming it. *Tuʿjibuka* — it genuinely impresses you. The surface is real. The frame really is imposing. The eloquence really is eloquent.

Which makes what comes next so much heavier. Because Allah is about to tell you that all of this — every bit of which is *true* — is also completely, structurally hollow. He is not going to deny the surface. **He is going to walk you behind it.**

---

### Element Two: كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُّسَنَّدَةٌ — Dead Timber Leaned on a Wall

Here is the sentence the whole verse was building toward.

> كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُّسَنَّدَةٌ
> "As if they were timber propped up."

Let's teach the comparison before we feel it, because Allah chose every part of this with terrifying care.

First word: **خُشُب** — *khushub*. We translate it "wood" or "timber," but Arabic has a choice here that English flattens. The Arabic word for a living, growing tree is *shajar*. The word *khashab / khushub* means **cut wood. Dead wood. Lumber.** A tree that was once alive, rooted, drinking water, producing fruit — and is now a beam. The life is gone. What remains is the shape.

Hear the word itself: *khu-shub.* There is something hard and dry in it, two heavy consonants with no softness, the sound of something knocked against stone. The word sounds like what it describes — dead, dry, hollow timber.

Now the second word: **مُّسَنَّدَة** — *musannada*. The root is sīn-nūn-dāl, *to lean, to prop, to support against something.* It is a passive participle in Form II — the intensive form — meaning it has been *firmly propped, leaned up* against something. al-Baghawi explains it plainly: *mumāla ilā jidār* — **leaned against a wall.**

So now assemble the full image. Not a forest. Not even a single standing tree. **Beams of dead lumber, leaned up against a wall.**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because there are at least three things inside this image, and they open one after another.

The first thing your eye notices: **a propped beam looks like a support.** When you see timber leaned against a wall, your mind says "structure." It has the *form* of something that holds weight. It looks load-bearing. This is the *ajsām* from the first half of the verse — the magnificent frame. From across the room, propped timber and a load-bearing pillar look identical.

But notice the second thing. **Which one is holding up which?** A real pillar holds up the wall. Propped timber — the wall is holding up *it*. Reverse the relationship completely. These men *appear* to be supporting the community. In truth, the community is the only thing keeping them standing. Remove the wall, and the beam simply falls. They are carried by the very thing they look like they are carrying.

And now the third thing, the deepest. al-Baghawi gives us the phrase that should stay with you: he says they are *ashbāḥ bilā arwāḥ, wa ajsām bilā aḥlām* — **figures without souls, and bodies without minds.** Cut wood is what is left when the life has left. The tree's whole interior life — the rising sap, the reaching roots, the fruit — is *gone*, and only the external shape is preserved. That is the precise relationship between the *munafiq's* magnificent surface and his hollow interior. The body remained. **The life left.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why Allah granted them the surface so generously in the first half? *Tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum* — their bodies impress you. Of course they do. **A beam of fine timber is genuinely impressive lumber.** The grain, the size, the polish — all real. The verse never denied it. It simply walked you around to the back and showed you there are no roots. Nothing is growing. Nothing is alive in there. The magnificence is the magnificence of a thing that used to be alive and now only holds its shape.

And one more grammatical note, because it sharpens everything. al-Jalalayn and al-Baghawi both record a documented variant reading: most reciters read *khushub* with a vowel on the *shīn*; Abu Amr and al-Kisa'i read it *khushb*, with the *shīn* silenced, sukūn. al-Jalalayn notes the silenced reading evokes their *tark al-tafahhum* — their failure to comprehend, the stillness of minds that have stopped working. Even the sound of the word can go quiet — like the thing it describes.

That is what this single image reveals: **you can keep the entire shape of a living thing long after the life inside it has died.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ — They Reckon Every Shout Is Against Them

You would expect that after the image of dead, propped timber, Allah would describe these men as *calm*. Wood does not feel. Wood does not fear. A propped beam just sits there.

Instead Allah says something that breaks the image open from the inside:

> يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ
> "They reckon every shout to be against them."

Let's teach the verb. **يَحْسَبُون** — *yaḥsabūn* — from the root ḥā-sīn-bā, the root of *ḥisāb*, calculation, accounting. To *ḥasiba* is not just to think — it is to **reckon, to compute, to assume after running the numbers.** It is the mind doing arithmetic on incomplete information and landing on a conclusion. And notice: *yaḥsabūn* is the present-tense, ongoing form. Not "they reckoned once." **They are reckoning, continuously, all the time.**

And what is the sum their minds keep computing? **كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ** — that *every* shout is *against them*.

*Ṣayḥa* — a shout, a cry, a raised voice. al-Jalalayn gives examples: a call in the camp, someone shouting for a lost animal, any sudden loud sound. And *kulla* — **every** one. Not some shouts. Not threatening shouts. *Every single raised voice.* Someone calls out across the market — *that's about us.* A commotion at the edge of the camp — *they've come for us.* A voice rises in the *masjid* — *we've been exposed.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel what Allah has just done to the image.

On the outside: dead, still timber — no feeling at all. On the inside: **a mind that never stops running, computing threat out of every sound in the air.** The most magnificent, composed, eloquent surface in the room — and behind it, a man flinching at every shout, certain the whole world is closing in on him.

Why? al-Muyassar gives the reason with devastating economy: *li-ʿilmihim bi-ḥaqīqati ḥālihim* — **because they know the truth of their own state.** That is the engine of the fear. The hypocrite's terror does not come from outside. It comes from the one fact he can never escape: *he knows what he really is.* He knows the surface is a surface. And a man who knows his foundation is hollow lives in permanent dread that the next shout will be the one that knocks it over.

This is the genius of placing the fear *right after* the timber. The propped beam looks immovable. But it knows — in whatever way a thing can know — that it is only leaning. That one shove, one shout, one shift of the wall, and it falls. **The grander the surface, the more there is to lose, and the more terrified the thing behind it becomes.**

And notice the cruel symmetry with the opening of the verse. In the first half, *they* impress *you* — the surface reaches out and acts on the world. In this half, *the world* terrifies *them* — every sound reaches in and acts on the surface. They project total command outward. They absorb total panic inward. **The more impressive the projection, the more fragile the thing projecting it.**

[PAUSE]

This single clause reveals what the image alone could not: that the hollow self is not at peace. **It is the most frightened thing in the room.**

---

### The Bridge

Look at what these three pieces of language have shown us.

Allah granted them the entire surface — *tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum* — body and word, the two channels of impression, fully conceded. Then with one image — *khushubun musannada* — He walked us behind the surface and showed us dead timber leaned on a wall, the shape of life with the life gone out. And then He told us the secret of the interior — *yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim* — that the hollow shape is not calm but quietly, constantly terrified, because it knows what it is.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because this is not only a verse about seventh-century Madinah and one eloquent man. It is a verse about a way of being human that every one of us knows the pull of.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Magnificent Hollow — When the Surface Outgrows the Soul

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a phone in your pocket, and on that phone is a version of you. A curated version. The good angle. The right words. The moments worth posting. You have spent real effort on that surface — and there is nothing strange about that. Every human being manages an exterior. We dress for the interview. We compose ourselves before we walk in.

But you also know the specific, private weariness of a particular gap: **the gap between how good the surface looks and how thin the thing behind it has become.** The version of you that everyone admires, and the version of you that lies awake knowing it is mostly a version. The polished sentence in the group chat sitting on top of a faith you have not actually fed in months. The presence you perform on the outside, supported by — what, exactly, on the inside?

That gap is the entire subject of this verse.

[PAUSE]

Here is what Allah is doing, and it is more merciful than it first appears. He is not condemning the *ajsām*. He is not against the surface. The tragedy of the *munafiq* is not that he looked impressive — it is that **the surface was all there was.** He poured everything into the part the eye can see and the ear can hear, and starved the part only Allah can see.

Think about what makes timber *khashab* and not *shajar*. The difference is not the shape. From the outside, a beam of fine cedar and a living cedar can look nearly the same. The difference is *roots*. The living tree is connected to something beneath the surface that feeds it — water, depth, an unseen source. The beam is connected to nothing. It is sustained by nothing. It is just leaned against a wall, borrowing the wall's stability because it has none of its own.

So here is the question this verse puts to you, gently but without flinching: **what are you rooted in?** Not what do you look like. Not what do you sound like. What is *underneath*, feeding the surface? Is there a private life with Allah — a *salah* no one sees, a Quran no one knows you read, a fear and a hope no camera captures — that is actually holding you up? Or have you become structurally dependent on the wall — on other people's approval, on the group's momentum, on the borrowed stability of looking like you belong?

[PAUSE]

Because here is the test the timber fails, and it is a test that comes to all of us eventually. **Remove the wall, and what happens?** Take away the audience. Take away the approval. Put the propped beam alone in an empty room. The living tree stands on its own roots. The propped timber falls.

The *munafiq* could function as long as the community held him up. The moment of testing — the battle, the crisis, the night alone — was the moment the wall was pulled away. And there is nothing underneath.

What does this change about tomorrow? It moves your attention from the surface to the roots. It asks you to spend, this week, even a little of the effort you spend on the visible self on the *invisible* one. One unit of *salah* prayed slowly when no one knows. One page of Quran with no post about it. One quiet act of obedience whose only witness is Allah. **You are growing roots.** You are becoming *shajar* and not *khashab* — a thing that can stand when the wall is gone, because it is fed from a source the wall never provided.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Fear Underneath — Why the Hollow Cannot Rest

Now let's go behind the surface into the place the verse refuses to leave alone: *yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim* — they reckon every shout is against them.

You know this feeling, even if you have never lived a double life on the scale of Abdullah ibn Ubayy. Have you ever done something you were ashamed of and then walked into a room and felt *certain* everyone could tell? Two people stop talking as you approach — *they were talking about me.* Your name comes up and your stomach drops before you even know the context. A message arrives and your heart races before you've read it. That is *yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim* in miniature. **The guilty conscience hears its own verdict in every sound.**

Now scale it up to a whole way of living, and you have the inner life of the hypocrite. al-Muyassar named the engine: *li-ʿilmihim bi-ḥaqīqati ḥālihim* — because they know the truth of their own state. The fear is not paranoia in the sense of being baseless. It is paranoia in the sense of being *misdirected* — they are right to fear, but they fear the wrong thing. They fear the shout in the camp. The real exposure they should fear stands above all camps.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to feel. We usually assume that the person with the most impressive surface has the most peace. The composed one. The one who always says the right thing. We look at them and think: *they have it together.* This verse demolishes that assumption. It says the opposite is true. **The more elaborate the surface, the more frightened the interior** — because there is more to protect, more distance between the seen and the real, more that a single shout could bring down.

The believer and the hypocrite can look identical from the outside. Both pray. Both are present. The difference is what each one does with a sudden noise. The one with roots, the one whose private life with Allah is real, hears a shout and is not destabilized — because his standing was never borrowed from the room. There is nothing in the dark for the shout to expose. But the hollow one cannot afford a single unexamined sound, because any of them might be the one that reaches the gap between his surface and his truth.

And feel the mercy hiding even here. *Why* does Allah tell us about their fear at all? He could have left them looking magnificent and serene. Instead He shows us the trembling underneath — and in doing so He tells the rest of us something liberating: **the performance is not worth it.** Whatever the surface buys you, it cannot buy you rest. The eloquent, imposing, admired hollow man is, in the one place that matters, the most frightened man in the room. The whole project of impressing the world, when it is built on nothing, does not even deliver the peace it promised. It delivers *this* — flinching at every shout for the rest of your life.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change tomorrow? It reframes what you are actually chasing when you polish the surface. Ask honestly: *am I building a self that can rest, or a self that has to keep flinching?* The cure for *yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim* is not a better surface — it is *closing the gap.* It is making the seen and the unseen self into one thing, so that no shout can expose a hidden interior, because there is no hidden interior. That is *ikhlāṣ* — sincerity — the integration of the private and the public self around Allah. The sincere person fears Allah and therefore fears almost nothing else. The hollow person fears everything else and has forgotten to fear Allah. **One real fear, rightly placed, cures a thousand false ones.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see how the whole verse is built, because the structure itself is the argument.

Allah moves in a deliberate sequence, from the outermost layer to the innermost, and then renders judgment:

- **The surface seen:** *tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum* — the impressive body.
- **The surface heard:** *tasmaʿ li-qawlihim* — the impressive speech.
- **Behind the surface:** *kaʾannahum khushubun musannada* — dead timber, the shape of life with no life.
- **Inside the hollow:** *yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim* — the trembling, computing fear.
- **The verdict on the whole:** *humu-l-ʿaduwwu faḥdharhum* — these are the enemy, beware.

Allah peels the human being like layers — body, then speech, then the cavity behind both, then the fear in the cavity. Each layer takes you further from what the eye admires and closer to what is actually there. By the end you are standing in the most interior room of these men, and it is empty, and it is afraid.

And then the final two strokes. **قَاتَلَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ** — *qātalahumu-llāh.* The root is qāf-tā-lām, to fight or kill, here in Form III. As an expression it is an imprecation — al-Jalalayn glosses it *ahlakahum*, "may Allah destroy them." The One whose shout truly matters has spoken — and notice the irony: they spent their lives flinching at *every* shout in the camp, while the only voice that could actually undo them was the one they never feared.

And the last word: **أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ** — *annā yuʾfakūn* — "how are they deluded?" The verb is from the root hamza-fā-kāf, the root of *ifk* — to be turned away from the truth, twisted from reality. It is passive: they are *being turned.* Something turns them. After all the eloquence and all the magnificence, the closing word is *bewilderment* — how does a being with eyes and ears and a tongue this gifted end up turned this completely away from what is real?

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The language showed us *khushub musannada* — dead timber propped on a wall, magnificent surface over hollow interior. The themes showed us that this is not an ancient curiosity but a live human possibility: the curated self that outgrows the soul, and the fear that lives inside every gap between what we show and what we are.

It is one truth: **A surface can be perfected to the point of magnificence and still be standing over nothing — and the being who lives that way will spend its life flinching at every shout, because it knows.** The cure is not a better surface. It is roots. It is closing the gap. It is becoming one self, fed from a source beneath the surface that no wall provides and no shout can shake.

### Questions to Carry

1. If the wall were pulled away tomorrow — the approval, the audience, the community's momentum — would I stand on my own roots, or would I fall?
2. How much of my effort goes into the part of me the eye and ear can measure, and how much goes into the part only Allah sees?
3. When a sudden "shout" comes — a name, a message, a silence — what does my reaction reveal about the gap between my surface and my truth?
4. What is actually feeding me beneath the surface? Can I name the private acts of worship that root me, or have I become *khashab* — shape without sap?
5. Of all the things I fear, is the one fear that would cure the rest — the fear of Allah — actually anywhere on the list?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You can keep the entire magnificent shape of a living thing long after the life has left it — and the hollow self, however impressive, lives in permanent fear because it alone knows it is only leaning on a wall.

---

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not asking you to tear down your surface. It is inviting you to *fill it* — to make the seen self and the unseen self one integrated thing, rooted in a real and private life with Allah. Not so that you will impress people. So that when the wall is gone and the shouts come, you will be standing on something that was never borrowed. Grow roots this week, quietly, where no one is watching. Become *shajar*, not *khashab*.

### Du'a

O Allah, do not let us be figures without souls, or frames without inner life. Root us in a private worship of You that no eye sees and no shout can shake, until our seen self and our hidden self are one, integrated in sincerity to You. And place in our hearts the one true fear of You that quiets every false fear, so that we may stand when the wall is taken away. *Ameen.*
