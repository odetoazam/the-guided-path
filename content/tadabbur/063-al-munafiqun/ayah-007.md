# The Tunnel and the Treasuries

### *Surah al-Munafiqun, Ayah 7*

> هُمُ ٱلَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ لَا تُنفِقُوا۟ عَلَىٰ مَنْ عِندَ رَسُولِ ٱللَّهِ حَتَّىٰ يَنفَضُّوا۟ ۗ وَلِلَّهِ خَزَآئِنُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَٰكِنَّ ٱلْمُنَٰفِقِينَ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

*Humu lladhīna yaqūlūna lā tunfiqū ʿalā man ʿinda rasūli llāhi ḥattā yanfaḍḍū — wa lillāhi khazā'inu s-samāwāti wa l-arḍi wa lākinna l-munāfiqīna lā yafqahūn.*

**"They are the ones who say, 'Do not spend on those who are with the Messenger of Allah until they disband.' And to Allah belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth — but the hypocrites do not understand."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me put you somewhere before we read a single word.

It is the sixth year after the Hijra. The Muslim army is returning from the campaign against Banū al-Muṣṭaliq, and they have stopped at a well. At the water, a fight breaks out between two men — one of the Emigrants, the Muhājirūn, who left everything behind in Mecca to follow the Prophet ﷺ, and one of the local Helpers, the Anṣār, the people of Medina on whose land and generosity the Emigrants now lived.

It is a small thing. A scuffle at a well. But a man is watching, and in that small thing he sees an opening.

His name is ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy. Before the Prophet ﷺ came to Medina, this man was on the verge of being crowned its king. The arrival of Islam dissolved his kingdom before it was ever placed on his head. He never forgave it. And now, watching this quarrel, he turns to the people around him and he says — this is preserved in the authentic narration of Zayd ibn Arqam, recorded by al-Bukhārī — he says something like: *"They have done this to us. They have crowded us out in our own land. By Allah, when we return to Medina, the mightier will surely expel the weaker."*

And then he reaches for a weapon. Not a sword. **Money.**

He turns to his people — the people of Medina, who held the wealth, the farms, the supply lines — and he gives them a strategy. *Stop feeding them. Close your hands. These Emigrants have nothing of their own; they live on what you give. Cut the supply, and watch them scatter back to wherever they came from. You don't need to fight Muhammad. You just need to starve the people around him.*

[PAUSE]

Now — here is what I want you to feel before we open the ayah.

This was not stupid. This was a *good plan.* It was the kind of plan that works. Economists have a name for it now; we call it leverage. We call it an embargo, a boycott, sanctions, a supply chain. It is the oldest move in the book of power: you do not need to defeat people who depend on you. You only need to remind them that they depend on you. Control the resource, and you control the people.

ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy looked at the believers and he saw a *dependency.* He saw a pile of people held together by nothing but bread, and he reached for the bread.

And the Qur'ān quotes him. Word for word, almost. It lifts his strategy out of his private council and pins it to the page forever — *"Do not spend on those who are with the Messenger of Allah until they disband."*

Here is the hook. Here is what should keep you in your seat:

The Qur'ān does not respond to this strategy with an argument. It does not say "but Allah will protect the believers" or "this is wicked of you." It responds with **one clause about geography.** About warehouses. About *where things are kept.* And in that single clause, it does not just answer the boycott — it reveals that the man who designed it did not understand the most basic fact about the universe he was living in.

He thought he was holding the supply.

He was not even close to the supply.

Let's find out why.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: One Root, Two Men — *The People of Nifāq Who Forbade Infāq*

I need to teach you something about how Arabic words are built before we touch this ayah, because if you don't see it, the whole verse stays flat. If you do see it, it will never close again.

In Arabic, almost every word grows from a **root** — usually three consonants — like a seed. From that one seed, you can grow a whole orchard of words by changing the vowels and adding letters around it. The root carries a core *meaning,* and every word grown from it carries some scent of that core, no matter how far it travels.

Think of it in English the way "spec" gives you *inspect, spectacle, spectator, suspect, perspective* — all of them have something to do with *looking,* even when the words feel unrelated. Arabic does this far more tightly and far more deliberately. And the Qur'ān, more than any text, plays the orchard against itself. It will plant two words from the same root in the same breath and let the meaning crackle between them.

Watch this one.

The root here is **ن-ف-ق (n-f-q).**

Now look at the ayah. Look at the very first command the hypocrites give:

> **لَا تُنفِقُوا**
> *lā tunfiqū* — "Do not spend."

That word, *tunfiqū,* is from the root **n-f-q.** It is Form IV of the verb — *anfaqa* — and it means to spend, to give out, to send your wealth outward into the world to benefit others. From this same root comes the great Qur'ānic word **infāq** — the spending in the path of Allah that the Book praises on nearly every page.

So the hypocrites' command is, literally: *"Do not do infāq."* Do not give.

Now keep that root in your mind — **n-f-q** — and travel to the end of the ayah. To the word that names *them:*

> **ٱلْمُنَٰفِقِينَ**
> *al-munāfiqīn* — "the hypocrites."

[PAUSE]

Listen to it. *Mu-NĀ-fi-qīn.* That word is also from **n-f-q.** The very same seed.

Do you feel what just happened? The Qur'ān calls them by a name built from the *exact root* of the action they are forbidding. The **munāfiqūn** — the people of **nifāq** — are the ones standing in the street shouting *"lā tunfiqū!"* — "no infāq!"

The people of *n-f-q* are forbidding *n-f-q.*

But it goes deeper than wordplay, because the two words pull the root in opposite directions, and that is the whole point.

Let me show you where the word *munāfiq* actually comes from, because the classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* — trace it to a startling image. The root *n-f-q* gives the Arabs a word, **nāfiqā'** (and **nafaq**), which is the **burrow of a small desert rodent**, the jerboa. And here is what makes the jerboa's burrow special: it is a tunnel with **two openings.** The animal digs into the earth and prepares a second exit, hidden, just beneath the surface. When a predator comes at the front door, the jerboa bursts out the back. It is never trapped, because it is never fully committed to one way in.

That. *That* is the **munāfiq.** A creature with two exits. A heart with a hidden back door. He stands with the believers at the front — he prays, he marches, he says the words — but he has dug a second tunnel out the back, ready to bolt the moment the predator of difficulty or loss appears. He is never *in.* He is never trapped by his own commitment, because he never truly made one.

Now hold both meanings of the root at once.

**Infāq** is the most outward-facing act a human can do: you take what is yours and you push it *out* into the open, into other people's lives, for the sake of Allah. It is the soul with its doors flung open.

**Nifāq** is the most inward-hiding act a human can do: you take your true self and you tunnel it *down,* underground, behind a second exit, hidden from view. It is the soul with a trapdoor.

[PAUSE]

And so the ayah's structure is not an accident of vocabulary. It is a diagnosis. **The reason these men forbid infāq is because they are men of nifāq.** A soul that has dug itself a hidden tunnel cannot bear to send its wealth out into the open. The hand closes because the heart already closed. The man who is hiding himself will hide his money. The architecture of the hypocrite — two exits, nothing fully given — *produces* the command "do not spend."

You cannot give outwardly when your whole self is built to hold back.

Here is what this single root reveals, and then we'll sit with it: **stinginess is not first a problem of the hand. It is a problem of the architecture of the heart.** The closed fist is downstream of the hidden tunnel.

## Element Two: *Yanfaḍḍū* — The Sound of Something Coming Apart

Let me teach you one more piece of Arabic machinery, because the hypocrites' plan hides inside a single verb, and that verb tells you exactly how they see the believers.

In Arabic, you can take a simple action-verb and put it into what's called **Form VII** by adding a prefix — *in-.* And Form VII does something specific: it makes the verb **happen to itself.** It takes an action and turns it into something that *comes about on its own,* without a named doer. If the simple verb means "to break," Form VII means "to break apart, to shatter, to come undone" — not *someone* broke it, but *it broke,* it fell to pieces of its own accord.

Now hear the word the hypocrites chose:

> **حَتَّىٰ يَنفَضُّوا۟**
> *ḥattā yanfaḍḍū* — "until they disband / scatter / fall apart."

This is from the root **ف-ض-ض (f-ḍ-ḍ),** whose core meaning is to break something up, to scatter it, to disperse a gathering into loose, separate pieces. The Arabs used it for breaking the seal on a letter, for scattering a crowd, for a string of pearls when the thread snaps and they go rolling in every direction across the floor.

And the hypocrites put it in **Form VII** — *yanfaḍḍū* — *they will scatter on their own.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with the word-picture inside their mouths.

When ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy looks at the community gathered around the Prophet ﷺ — the Emigrants and the Helpers, the believers who left their homes for Allah — what does his verb tell us he sees?

He sees a **string of loose beads.** He sees a pile of separate people held together by one single thread, and the thread is *money.* In his mind, these people are not bound by faith, not bound by love, not bound by a covenant with Allah — they are bound by bread. By the next meal. By the supply. Pull out the thread of provision, he thinks, and the whole necklace will *yanfaḍḍū* — scatter across the floor like pearls when the string snaps. They will roll away in every direction and be gone.

His entire strategy lives in that one verb. He has mistaken the *thread.* He thinks he holds it. He thinks the thing binding these people is the resource he controls.

And notice — this is the precision of the Qur'ān — it lets *him* supply the verb. The Book does not describe the believers as a fragile string of beads. It puts that image only in the *quoted speech of the hypocrite,* so that we understand: **this is how nifāq sees the believing community. It cannot perceive any bond except a material one.** The man who has only ever been held together by his own self-interest cannot imagine that anyone else is held by something stronger. He projects his own emptiness outward and calls it analysis.

He looks at people bound by *lā ilāha illā Allāh* and sees people bound by lunch.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this verb reveals: **the hypocrite always underestimates the believer, because he is measuring with the wrong instrument.** He weighs the community on a scale of provisions and concludes it is light. He never thinks to weigh it on the scale of faith — because faith is a weight he has never carried, and you cannot measure with a unit you do not possess.

He reached for the thread of money to make them scatter.

But the thread was never money. And the One who held the real thread is named in the very next clause.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: *Wa lillāhi Khazā'in* — The Word That Relocates the Universe

Now we arrive at the answer. And I want you to notice *how* the Qur'ān answers, because the grammar of the response is itself the response.

After quoting the boycott — *"do not spend on them until they scatter"* — the ayah turns, and the turn begins with a single small letter that changes everything:

> **وَلِلَّهِ خَزَآئِنُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ**
> *wa **li**llāhi khazā'inu s-samāwāti wa l-arḍ* — "And **to Allah** belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth."

That little **li-** prefix — *lillāhi* — is the Arabic of belonging, of possession, of *this is whose it is.* And in Arabic, when you want to stress ownership above everything else, you move the owner to the front of the sentence. The natural order would be "the treasuries of the heavens and earth belong to Allah." But the ayah front-loads it: *lillāhi* — *to Allah* — first. **To Allah** belong the storehouses. The emphasis lands like a gavel: *whose* before *what.*

And then the word at the center of it all: **خَزَآئِن** — *khazā'in,* from the root **خ-ز-ن (kh-z-n),** to store, to hoard, to lock away in a vault. A **khizāna** is a treasury, a storehouse, the locked room where provisions and wealth are kept in reserve. And here it is **plural** — *khazā'in* — not one treasury but *all* the treasuries, the vaults upon vaults. And not the vaults of Medina, not the granaries of one tribe — **the treasuries of the heavens and the earth.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened to ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy's strategy?

His entire plan depended on one belief: *I control the supply.* The food, the wealth, the provision of these Emigrants — it flows through *my* people's hands. I am standing at the storehouse door. Close my hand, and the supply closes.

And the Qur'ān, in seven Arabic words, **relocates the storehouse.**

It says: you think you are standing at the treasury? The treasuries are not in Medina. They are not in your granaries. They are not yours to open or close. **The treasuries — all of them, the vaults of the sky and the earth — belong to Allah.** Rain is in His vault. The harvest is in His vault. The unseen channels by which a man finds his next meal from a direction he never expected — that is in His vault. You imagined you held the tap. You were never even in the building.

This is what the scholars draw out of this clause. Al-Baghawī says it plainly: *no one gives anyone anything except by His permission, and no one withholds except by His will.* Al-Qurtubī tells the story of a man who asked the ascetic Ḥātim al-Aṣamm, "Where do you eat from? Who feeds you?" And Ḥātim answered with nothing but this clause: *"wa lillāhi khazā'inu s-samāwāti wa l-arḍ"* — "**To Allah belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth.**" That was the whole answer. There is no other supply. There never was.

[PAUSE]

So watch the full architecture of the hypocrite's error.

He commanded: **do not spend** (*lā tunfiqū*) — believing the spending was *his.*
He predicted: **they will scatter** (*yanfaḍḍū*) — believing the thread was *his.*
And Allah replies: **the treasuries are Mine** (*lillāhi khazā'in*) — the spending was never yours, the supply was never yours, the thread was never yours.

He was rationing water at a well, while standing at the foot of the One who owns the rain.

---

### The Bridge

We have watched three pieces of language do their work.

We saw a single **root** — *n-f-q* — split into two men: the people of the hidden tunnel forbidding the act of open giving, and we learned that the closed hand is born from the closed heart.

We saw a single **verb** — *yanfaḍḍū* — reveal that the hypocrite can only ever see a community as a fragile string of beads, because money is the only thread he has ever known.

And we saw a single **clause** — *lillāhi khazā'in* — pick up the entire universe and move the storehouse out of human hands and into the hands of the One who owns the heavens and the earth.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way provision and trust actually move through your life.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Scarcity Mind and the Owner of the Vaults

Let me start where you actually live, far from seventh-century Medina.

There is a particular feeling that lives in the modern chest, and psychologists have a name for it: the **scarcity mindset.** It is the quiet, constant background hum that there is *not enough* — not enough money, not enough time, not enough opportunity — and that the only way to be safe is to *hold,* to *clutch,* to keep your hand closed around what you have. Researchers who study it find something striking: scarcity doesn't just make you cautious. It actually *narrows your mind.* It captures your attention so completely that you lose the ability to think well about anything else. The fear of not-enough makes you, measurably, less wise.

Now look again at ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, because he is the scarcity mind enthroned.

His entire worldview is a closed system. There is a fixed amount of provision, it flows through human hands, and therefore power means *controlling the flow.* "Do not spend on them." Cut the supply. In his universe, generosity is a leak, giving is a loss, and the way to win is to make others depend on what you refuse to release. He is clutching the well.

And against this, the ayah does not offer a feeling. It offers a *fact about reality:* **wa lillāhi khazā'inu s-samāwāti wa l-arḍ.** The vaults are not finite, are not in human hands, and are not a closed system. They are the treasuries of the heavens and the earth, owned by One whose generosity does not deplete Him.

Here is the movement I want you to make with me, because it transforms everything.

The believer is not asked to *pretend* there is enough. The believer is asked to relocate the storehouse. The question "will there be enough?" is the wrong question, asked of the wrong owner. The right question is *whose vaults are these?* — and the answer reorganizes your entire relationship to giving.

Because watch what happens to *infāq* once you believe *lillāhi khazā'in.* If the treasuries are finite and yours, then every act of giving is subtraction. You give, you have less, the pile shrinks — of course you clutch. But if the treasuries belong to the One who owns the heavens and the earth, and you are merely a hand that money passes *through,* then giving is not subtraction at all. It is **circulation.** You are not the vault; you are a channel. And a channel does not become poorer by letting water flow through it. A channel becomes *blocked* by refusing to.

[PAUSE]

This is why the very same Allah who says *the treasuries are Mine* commands, throughout the Qur'ān, *spend* — *anfiqū.* He is not asking you to give from a closed system and trust that the math somehow works. He is telling you the math was never closed. *"Whatever you spend, He will replace it"* (34:39). *"Shayṭān threatens you with poverty and commands you to indecency, while Allah promises you forgiveness from Him and abundance"* (2:268). Read that last verse again with this ayah beside it: the voice that whispers *don't spend, you'll run out* — the Qur'ān names its source, and it is not the One who owns the vaults.

So here is the thing this changes about tomorrow.

The next time your hand tightens — over money, over time, over credit, over knowledge you could share, over an apology you could give — notice the voice doing the tightening. It is the voice of ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy: *hold, or you'll have nothing.* And then make the correction the ayah makes. Say it the way Ḥātim al-Aṣamm said it: *wa lillāhi khazā'inu s-samāwāti wa l-arḍ.* The vault is not in my hand. I am the channel, not the source. Then **open the hand** — not because you are reckless, but because you finally understand who owns the supply.

The scarcity mind clutches the well. The believing mind looks up at the One who owns the rain.

## Theme Two: Why They "Do Not Understand" — Nifāq as a Failure of *Fiqh*

The ayah ends on a word that should stop us, because of what it does *not* say.

> **وَلَٰكِنَّ ٱلْمُنَٰفِقِينَ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ**
> *wa lākinna l-munāfiqīna lā **yafqahūn*** — "but the hypocrites do not **understand**."

The verb is from **ف-ق-ه (f-q-h)** — *fiqh.* And here is what you must feel about this word: it is *not* the ordinary word for knowing. Arabic has *ʿilm* for information, for knowing-that, for facts you can hold. **Fiqh is something else.** Fiqh is the deep comprehension that grasps *the thing beneath the thing* — the underlying reality, the connection, the *why.* It is the difference between knowing the words of a sentence and understanding what it means. It is why the entire science of deriving rulings from their sources is called *fiqh,* not *ʿilm* — because it requires not just data, but penetration into the meaning underneath.

So when Allah says the hypocrites *lā yafqahūn,* He is making a precise diagnosis. He is **not** saying they are uninformed. ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy was, by every worldly measure, a *brilliant* man. He understood politics, leverage, supply chains, group psychology. He could read a quarrel at a well and see a strategy in it. He was *clever.* His *ʿilm* of how power works in this world was excellent.

But he had **no fiqh.** Because fiqh would have grasped *the thing beneath the thing* — and the thing beneath the bread, beneath the supply, beneath the whole economy he thought he controlled, was the simple, total fact that **the treasuries belong to Allah.** He could see the surface of provision with perfect clarity and was completely blind to its source. He counted every coin and missed the Owner.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how unsettling this is, because it is meant to unsettle *us,* not just him.

It is entirely possible to be *intelligent* and have no *fiqh.* To understand markets, systems, leverage, and people — to be the smartest strategist in the room — and to be, at the level that finally matters, *blind.* The hypocrite's failure is not a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of *depth.* He understood everything except the one thing underneath everything.

And now connect this back to where we began, to the tunnel with two exits. Why does the *munāfiq* fail to understand? It is not random that *this* verb closes a verse about *these* men. Look at what nifāq *is:* a soul that has refused to fully commit, that keeps a hidden back door, that will not let itself be trapped by faith. And **a soul that holds itself back from faith holds itself back from the comprehension that faith opens.** You cannot grasp *the thing beneath the thing* while you are standing with one foot already in the escape tunnel. Fiqh requires you to be *in.* It requires the kind of surrender that the hypocrite, by definition, refuses. His two exits cost him his depth.

This is the quiet terror and the quiet mercy of this word. The terror: cleverness will not save you if you have no fiqh of the One beneath it all. And the mercy: fiqh is not reserved for the brilliant. It is opened to the *committed.* The simplest believer who has truly grasped *lillāhi khazā'in* — who knows in his bones that the vaults are Allah's — has a deeper fiqh than the most sophisticated strategist who never looked up.

So here is what this changes about tomorrow.

When you face a difficulty — a loss of income, a closing door, someone with power over your supply seeming to hold your fate in their hand — there are two ways to see it, and they are the two ways in this ayah. You can see it the way the *munāfiq* sees: surface only. *This person controls my provision; this circumstance determines my future.* That is *ʿilm* without *fiqh.* Or you can see *the thing beneath the thing:* this person, this circumstance, is a hand that provision passes through — and the vault belongs to Allah. The boycott was real. The hunger was a real threat. And it changed *nothing* about who owned the treasuries.

Ask Allah for *fiqh.* Not just to know — to **understand.** To see beneath the surface of your own life to the Owner of the storehouses standing behind every closed and open door.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Step back and look at the whole verse now, because the linguistic discoveries and the two themes were never separate. They were one insight approached from different sides.

The hypocrites are named **munāfiqūn** — people of the hidden tunnel, the souls with two exits. *Because* they hold themselves back from full commitment (**nifāq**), they cannot bear to send their wealth outward (they forbid **infāq**). *Because* they have only ever been held together by self-interest, they imagine the believers are a fragile string of beads that money can scatter (**yanfaḍḍū**). And *because* they keep one foot in the escape tunnel, they have no depth — they understand the surface of provision and are blind to its Owner (**lā yafqahūn**).

And standing against all of it, like a mountain in the middle of the verse, is one clause that the whole surah is built to make you feel: **wa lillāhi khazā'inu s-samāwāti wa l-arḍ.** To Allah belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth.

The man closed his hand around a well, and Allah revealed Himself as the Owner of the rain.

It is the same insight twice. The disease is a closed system — a closed heart, a closed hand, a closed and surface-level understanding. The cure is a single relocation: the storehouse is not yours, was never yours, belongs entirely to the One above the heavens and the earth. And the moment that relocation happens in you, the hand opens, the heart opens, and the eyes finally see beneath the surface of things.

1. When my hand tightens — over money, time, knowledge, forgiveness — whose voice is doing the tightening, and do I believe its claim that I'll run out?
2. Where in my life am I behaving as if *I* am the vault, rather than a channel the provision passes through?
3. Am I, like ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, *clever* about the surface of my circumstances but blind to the Owner beneath them?
4. Have I dug a "second exit" — a place where I hold myself back from full surrender, just in case? And what is that hidden tunnel costing my ability to *understand*?
5. Do I measure the strength of what binds me to Allah and to other believers by something material — or by something the hypocrite could never weigh?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The hypocrites tried to scatter the believers by closing the supply — never comprehending that the One who owns the treasuries of the heavens and the earth was the only supply there ever was.

## The Closing Invitation

You are invited, today, to relocate one storehouse. To take one thing your hand is clutching out of fear of not-enough, and to open the hand over it while saying the truth out loud: *the treasuries belong to Allah, and I am only a channel.* Not as a leap of recklessness — as an act of *fiqh,* of finally understanding who owns the rain. Let one closed fist become a channel, and watch whether you are diminished or freed.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, owner of the treasuries of the heavens and the earth — unclench my hand from what I fear losing, and let me understand that I was never the source of anything. Grant me not just knowledge, but fiqh: to see beneath the surface of every closed door to You standing behind it. And save me from the hidden tunnel — make me fully Yours, with no second exit.*

*Āmīn.*
