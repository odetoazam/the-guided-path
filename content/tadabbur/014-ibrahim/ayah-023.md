# They Are Admitted to Gardens by Their Lord's Permission, and Their Greeting There Is Peace

## Surah Ibrahim (14:23)

[ayah:14:23] وَأُدْخِلَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ جَنَّـٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ خَـٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا بِإِذْنِ رَبِّهِمْ ۖ تَحِيَّتُهُمْ فِيهَا سَلَـٰمٌ

*Wa udkhila alladhina amanu wa 'amilu al-salihat jannatin tajri min tahtiha al-anharu khalidina fiha bi-idhni rabbihim. Tahiyyatuhum fiha salam.*

**And those who believed and did righteous deeds will be admitted into gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding there by permission of their Lord. Their greeting therein is: Peace.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

This ayah arrives like mercy after suffocation.

The previous hereafter scenes are full of exposure, helplessness, accusation, and failed alliances. Then the surah turns, and without noise or spectacle it gives the believers something all the previous scenes were starving for: entry, permanence, and peace.

Notice what the ayah does not do.

It does not argue back. It does not answer Shaytan's speech, correct him, or settle the score. After an entire scene of people talking at each other and getting nowhere, this ayah simply moves to a different kind of world and describes what is spoken there.

[PAUSE]

The hook of the ayah is this: **Jannah is not presented here merely as reward after pain, but as a world whose deepest texture is secure admission by Allah and a settled atmosphere in which the language itself has become peace.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Wa udkhila alladhina amanu**: Entry Itself Is a Gift Done to Them

The ayah begins with a passive verb:

**wa udkhila**

they are admitted, made to enter.

[PAUSE]

That matters. The believers do not storm Jannah, seize it, or earn entry in a self-sufficient way. Entrance is granted to them. It is done for them.

The passive also leaves the doer unnamed - and unmistakable. Nobody has to be told who admits them.

Look at what happens to the other group in the same passage. Two ayahs earlier they are described with the verb *barazu*: they come out into the open before Allah, all of them together (14:21). The root carries the sense of bare, exposed ground - stepping out where nothing stands between you and what is looking at you.

And the word for what the believers are placed into, **jannat**, is built on a root that means to cover and to screen. It is the same root behind *junnah*, a shield, and *jinn*, the hidden ones. A garden earns the name because its growth closes over the ground beneath it.

One group is brought out into the open.

The other is brought under cover.

[PAUSE]

Then the believers are described by the two things the Quran repeatedly joins:

**alladhina amanu wa 'amilu al-salihat**

those who believed and did righteous deeds.

Al-Tabari unpacks the first half carefully: these are the ones who took Allah and His Messenger as true, who affirmed that Allah is one, that His messengers were genuinely sent, and that what they brought is truth from Allah. Belief in this reading is not a warm feeling. It is a verdict about what is real, made and then stood on.

And the second half he glosses just as concretely: they acted in obedience to Allah, keeping to His command and His prohibition. Both halves of that. Righteous action is not only what you do; it is also what you stop yourself from doing. A life is shaped as much by its restraints as by its output.

Faith is not left abstract, and deeds are not left rootless. The pair arrives together because the Garden belongs to the people whose inward trust and outward action learned to belong together in dunya.

[PAUSE]

Then the place itself:

**jannatin tajri min tahtiha al-anhar**

gardens beneath which rivers flow.

Al-Tabari renders *jannat* plainly as orchards - cultivated, planted, tended ground, not wilderness. Al-Muyassar fills in the picture further: the rivers run beneath their trees and beneath their dwellings. Water underneath the whole arrangement, always moving, never asked for.

This is a place that has already been prepared, planted, and watered before anyone arrives.

### 2. **Khalidina fiha bi-idhni rabbihim**: Permanence Still Rests on Divine Permission

Then the ayah gives one of the most beloved descriptions:

**khalidina fiha**

abiding there, remaining there permanently.

[PAUSE]

After so much human instability, the word **khalidina** lands with enormous tenderness. No eviction, no collapse, no reversal, no expiration.

Al-Tabari's gloss is one word longer than you expect, and the extra word does the work: remaining there *forever*. Al-Muyassar turns it around and says the same thing from the other side - they never come out of it, ever. It is worth sitting with that second phrasing. Much of the fear in a human life is not fear of never arriving somewhere good. It is fear of arriving and then being made to leave.

But then the ayah adds something beautiful:

**bi-idhni rabbihim**

by permission of their Lord.

Even in Paradise, the believer is kept inside adab. His permanence is not independence. His safety is still under the Lord who brought him there. Jannah is security, but not autonomy from Allah. It is security *with* Him, from Him, by His permission.

[PAUSE]

There is an honest difference among the classical commentators about which word the permission attaches to, and it is worth naming rather than smoothing over.

Al-Tabari reads it with the entering: they were admitted into those gardens by their Lord's own command to them to enter. On that reading, *idhn* is not a passive allowance but an order - the door opens because He says the word.

Al-Muyassar reads it with the abiding: they never leave, by their Lord's permission and by His might and His power. On that reading, the permission is what holds them there, and the strength that keeps them is His, not theirs.

The Arabic allows both because the phrase sits between the two. And the two readings do not fight. One says you got in by His word. The other says you stay in by His strength. Neither leaves any room for a believer to stand in Jannah and think he arranged this.

[PAUSE]

There is something else worth noticing about this exact phrase. **Bi-idhni rabbihim** is not new here. It is how Surah Ibrahim opened.

The very first ayah of the surah describes the Book sent down "to bring people out of the darknesses into the light, *bi-idhni rabbihim*" - by the permission of their Lord (14:1).

Word for word, the same phrase.

So the first movement of the surah and this one are bracketed by the same three words. Being brought out of darkness is by His permission. Being brought into the Garden is by His permission. The exit and the entry are the same kind of event, done by the same authority, separated by a lifetime.

And the phrase does not stop here. Two ayahs later, the good word is compared to a good tree that "gives its fruit every season, *bi-idhni rabbiha*" - by the permission of its Lord (14:25). Even a tree does not produce on its own terms. And earlier in the surah, the messengers themselves tell their people that they cannot bring a single proof except *bi-idhni-llah*, by Allah's permission (14:11).

Guidance, prophecy, fruit, and Paradise.

Nothing in this surah happens without His permission. The believers in this ayah are simply the ones who stopped fighting that fact.

### 3. **Tahiyyatuhum fiha salam**: The Speech of That World Has Been Healed

Then the final line:

**tahiyyatuhum fiha salam**

their greeting there is peace.

[PAUSE]

The word **tahiyyah** is more than a passing hello. It is the form of reception, recognition, and address that meets a person in that world.

And it carries something in its root. *Tahiyyah* is built on the root of *hayah* - life. Classical lexicographers connect the word to the old Arabic greeting that wished a person long life. To greet someone was to wish life over them.

But in that world life is no longer the open question. Nobody there needs to be wished more of it. So the greeting shifts to what is actually being given.

And what is that form?

**salam**.

Not negotiation.

Not fear.

Not hidden rivalry.

Not accusation.

Not one more speech of blame.

Peace.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar adds a detail the ayah leaves compressed, and it opens the line considerably: they are greeted there with peace *from Allah, from His angels, and from the believers*.

So this is not only the sound of neighbours being kind to each other. The greeting comes from three directions at once, and one of those directions is above all the others.

The Quran shows each of these elsewhere. The angels enter upon them from every gate saying, *salamun 'alaykum bima sabartum* - peace be upon you for what you patiently bore (13:24). And from Allah Himself: *salamun qawlan min rabbin rahim* - "Peace," a word from a Merciful Lord (36:58). Elsewhere the very phrase of our ayah is repeated of the people of the Garden: *wa tahiyyatuhum fiha salam* (10:10), and again of the believers on the day they meet Him (33:44).

It is worth remembering what that word also is. *As-Salam* is one of the names of Allah. Their home is called *dar as-salam*, the abode of peace, and the Quran places it "with their Lord" (6:127).

So the greeting of that world is not merely a pleasant custom. It is a Name being spoken over people, endlessly, by the One who owns it.

[PAUSE]

After all the broken language of dunya and all the devastating speech scenes of the Hereafter - followers pleading, leaders failing, Shaytan disowning - the language of Jannah is healed down to its greeting.

[PAUSE]

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: One of the Deep Human Longings Is Not Just for Survival, but for Safe Arrival

Many people live as though life is one long attempt merely to keep going.

Get through the week.

Manage the next burden.

Stay afloat in relationships, work, fear, regret, and inner noise.

But beneath that is a deeper longing: not just to survive movement, but to arrive somewhere finally safe.

[PAUSE]

This ayah answers that longing with the verb of admission. The believers are not left wandering in endless transition. They are brought in.

That is spiritually profound because much of dunya feels like exposure without arrival. The soul is always bracing. Jannah here is not described first by luxury, but by entry and abiding. You are in. And you remain.

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet mercy in where this ayah sits, too.

The previous ayah opens with a time marker: Shaytan makes his speech "when the matter had been decided" (14:22). Ibn Kathir, commenting on that verse, describes the scene the same way - Iblis stands and speaks only after the judgement between the servants is finished, once the believers have already been sent to the Gardens and the others to the Fire. His speech is aimed at the ones left standing with him, and its purpose, Ibn Kathir says bluntly, is to pile grief on top of their grief.

Which means the believers never hear it.

That whole terrible speech - the confession, the mockery, the disowning, the "do not blame me, blame yourselves" - happens somewhere they are no longer standing. They are already inside. They are already under cover. The last argument of their old enemy takes place out of their hearing, and the first thing spoken over them instead is peace.

### Theme 2: Peace in the Quran Is Not Merely the Absence of War, but the Healing of the Whole Atmosphere

The final word of the ayah is **salam**, and that is not accidental.

Human life is full of distorted speech. We greet each other while hiding envy. We reassure while carrying resentment. We negotiate, posture, flatter, threaten, and defend. Even our words can feel unsafe.

[PAUSE]

The ayah says the atmosphere of Jannah is so healed that even the greeting has become peace.

That means no one is guarding themselves there.

No one is scanning for subtext.

No one is waiting for betrayal in the next sentence.

No one is trying to secure their place through language.

What the heart has wanted for so long - to be received without fear - becomes the native speech of that world.

[PAUSE]

And once you notice that this ayah is about speech, the whole passage around it reorganises itself. This entire stretch of Surah Ibrahim is about what people say.

The weak say to the arrogant: can you take any of this punishment off us? And the answer is a shrug: it makes no difference now whether we panic or endure, there is no escape (14:21). Speech that asks and gets nothing.

Then Shaytan speaks: Allah promised you truly, I promised you and broke it, I had no power over you except that I called and you answered, so do not blame me, blame yourselves - I cannot rescue you and you cannot rescue me (14:22). Speech that seduced, then abandoned.

Then this ayah: **tahiyyatuhum fiha salam**. Speech that has been made safe.

And then, immediately after, the surah turns and gives two parables - about *words*. A good word is like a good tree, its root firm and its branches in the sky, giving its fruit every season by its Lord's permission (14:24-25). A foul word is like a foul tree, torn up from the surface of the earth, with no ground under it at all (14:26). Then: Allah keeps firm those who believe with the firm word, in this life and in the Hereafter (14:27).

[PAUSE]

Read in that order, the passage is a study of language under pressure.

Words that beg and fail. Words that lure and desert. Words that have no root and get pulled out of the ground. And, set against all of them, a word that is rooted, a word that keeps a person standing, and a greeting that has become peace.

The contrast even reaches down to the vocabulary of settledness. The foul tree has *no qarar* - no stable place to rest (14:26). Those who traded Allah's favour for ingratitude are led into the home of ruin, and it is said of it: *wretched is the qarar* (14:28-29). The believers, meanwhile, are **khalidina fiha** - the ones who stay.

Your speech, in the end, is a report on what you are rooted in. That is why the Garden's greeting can be trusted. The people there are not being polite. They are simply saying out loud what has become true of them.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

This ayah is short, but it contains an entire emotional world.

The believers are admitted, not self-installed.

They abide, not temporarily rest.

Their permanence remains under their Lord's permission, so security never becomes independence - by His command they entered, and by His strength they remain.

And the language of that place is peace, spoken over them by Allah, by His angels, and by one another.

Set beside the surrounding scenes of accusation, helplessness, and disowning, the contrast is overwhelming. The people of false reliance find no escape. The people of iman and righteous action are brought into a world whose first spoken texture is salam.

The same three words that opened this surah - *by the permission of their Lord* - are the words that carry them through the door.

### Questions to Carry

- What forms of false security are you still clinging to instead of asking Allah for real admission into His peace?
- How much of your religious life is aimed merely at surviving, and how much is aimed at arriving with Allah?
- Al-Tabari reads righteous action as keeping to both His command and His prohibition. Which of those two halves do you quietly neglect?
- If Jannah's greeting is peace, what in your speech now needs to be purified so it begins to resemble the world you are asking for?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**This ayah presents Jannah as secure admission by Allah into a lasting world where even the basic language of relationship has been healed into peace.**

### Du'a

*Allahumma, admit us among those who believe and do righteous deeds, keep us by Your permission among the people of abiding nearness, and begin teaching our tongues the language of salam before the day we hope to hear it forever.*
