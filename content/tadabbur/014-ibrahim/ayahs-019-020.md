# He Created the Heavens and the Earth in Truth, and Replacing You Is Not Difficult for Him

## Surah Ibrahim (14:19-20)

[ayah:14:19] أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ بِٱلْحَقِّ ۚ إِن يَشَأْ يُذْهِبْكُمْ وَيَأْتِ بِخَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ

[ayah:14:20] وَمَا ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ بِعَزِيزٍ

*A-lam tara anna Allaha khalaqa al-samawati wa al-arda bi-l-haqq. In yasha' yudhhibkum wa ya'ti bi-khalqin jadid.*

*Wa ma dhalika 'ala Allahi bi-'aziz.*

**Have you not seen that Allah created the heavens and the earth in truth? If He wills, He can remove you and bring a new creation. And that is not difficult for Allah.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

After showing the unbeliever's deeds as ash in violent wind, the surah does something bracing: it expands outward until the entire sky and earth are in view.

Why?

Because the human being overestimates his indispensability. We imagine our rebellion is a dramatic force, our presence is a fixed necessity, and the world somehow needs us to continue as we are. These ayahs correct that delusion by placing us inside a creation made in truth by Allah, then stating with complete ease that He can remove us and bring another creation.

Watch the camera move across three ayahs. In 14:18 we are looking at a handful of ash blowing away in a storm — the deeds of those who rejected their Lord. In 14:19-20 we pull back until the whole sky and the whole earth fill the frame, and the people holding those deeds turn out to be just as removable as the ash. Then in 14:21 the camera closes back in, and everyone is standing exposed before Allah, arguing about who led whom. Small, then vast, then small again — but the second "small" is a courtroom.

The hook of the passage is this: **once the heavens and earth are seen as created in truth, the human being's imagined centrality collapses, and what remains is the sobering realization that Allah is under no strain at all in replacing a creation that refuses its Lord.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Khalaqa al-samawati wa al-arda bi-l-haqq**: Creation Is Not Random Material, but a Truth-Bearing Act

The ayah begins with:

**a-lam tara**

have you not seen?

[PAUSE]

This is not only physical sight. It is an invitation to notice what should already be evident.

Look at the shape of the phrase. It is a question mark and a negation stacked on top of each other: the questioning *a-* in front of *lam tara*, "you did not see." In Arabic, that combination almost never expresses doubt. It expresses the opposite. It is the tone of someone saying *surely you know this* — pressing you to admit what you have already been shown rather than asking for new information.

Al-Tabari makes the kind of seeing explicit. He explains the address as: have you not seen, O Muhammad, **with the eye of your heart** — and so come to know. Sight here passes through the eyes and lands in the understanding. Al-Muyassar goes a step further and simply paraphrases the verb as *have you not known*. Two commentators, working independently, move the word from the retina to the mind.

There is a small difference among them worth noticing. Al-Tabari reads the address as directed to the Prophet ﷺ himself. Al-Muyassar reads it as directed at whoever is being addressed, and adds that what is meant is people in general. This is not a conflict so much as two ends of one line: the Prophet ﷺ is addressed first, and through him everyone who hears the ayah is addressed too. Either way, the verse is not asking you to acquire a fact. It is asking you to stop overlooking one.

Then:

**khalaqa al-samawati wa al-arda bi-l-haqq**

He created the heavens and the earth **in truth**.

Al-Tabari adds a word to this that changes its weight. Allah brought the heavens and the earth into being **alone** — with no backer behind Him and no helper beside Him. That detail is not decoration. It is the hinge on which the rest of the passage turns. The One who needed no assistance to raise the heavens will need no assistance to take you away. If nobody helped Him build it, nobody is in a position to stop Him from rearranging it.

Al-Tabari also records something about the verb itself. Reciters of Madinah and Basra, along with some of Kufa, read it as the past-tense verb **khalaqa** — "He created." The reciters of Kufa generally read it as **khaliqu** — "Creator of," a title rather than a finished act. Al-Tabari notes that both readings are well-established among the leading reciters, that their meanings are close, and that whoever recites with either one is correct. The text in front of us carries the verb. But it is worth pausing on how little separates them: one reading says *He did this*, the other says *He is the One who does this*. A completed act and a permanent identity, describing the same reality from two angles.

The phrase **bi-l-haqq** matters immensely. Creation is not described as accidental, decorative, or morally empty. It is bound up with truth: right purpose, real meaning, just order, non-futility.

Al-Muyassar unpacks that single word into three claims, and it is worth taking them one at a time. First, Allah brought the heavens and earth into being **in the right way** — a way that points back to His wisdom. Second, He did **not** create them as *'abath*, idle play, something done for no reason. Third — and this is the part we usually miss — He made them so that people would **reason from them** to His oneness and the completeness of His power, and then worship Him alone and associate nothing with Him.

So *bi-l-haqq* is not only a statement about the quality of the workmanship. It tells you what creation is **for**. The sky and the earth are evidence. They were built to be read. A person who looks at the world and concludes nothing is not merely uncurious; he is walking past an argument that was assembled for him.

Notice also the small preposition. It does not say Allah created them *and they conform to* the truth, as though truth were a standard applied from outside. It says He created them **bi-** — with truth, in truth, truth built into them. The haqq is not a label stuck on the surface of creation. It is in the material.

That means the world is not neutral ground on which disbelief and obedience are equally at home. The world itself was made in a way that testifies to reality. To rebel against Allah inside such a creation is not simply to break a rule. It is to live against the grain of what the world was made to mean.

### 2. **In yasha' yudhhibkum wa ya'ti bi-khalqin jadid**: The Human Being Is Not the Fixed Point

Then comes the sentence that cuts pride down to size:

**in yasha' yudhhibkum wa ya'ti bi-khalqin jadid**

If He wills, He can remove you and bring a new creation.

[PAUSE]

Look first at how short the condition is. **In yasha'** — *if He wills*. That is the whole mechanism. No process is described, no cause is named, no instrument is mentioned. Between your continued existence and your disappearance there stands exactly one thing, and it is not a law of nature or a chain of events. It is a will.

The verb **yudhhibkum** carries the force of taking away, making disappear, causing to go. Your continued presence is not self-secured. Its root simply means *to go*; the form used here means *to make something go*. You are not the one walking; you are the one being walked off the stage.

Al-Tabari does not soften this. He glosses "remove you" with a second verb: **He causes you to pass away** — He makes you cease. This is not relocation and it is not exile. It is the ending of a people.

Then:

**wa ya'ti**

and He brings.

The removal is followed immediately by replacement. The universe does not stall when a people are taken away. Allah is not left with an empty plan because a generation refused Him.

Then the phrase:

**bi-khalqin jadid**

a new creation.

Notice that it is indefinite. Not *the* new creation, not a named successor — just *a* creation, *some* creation. The ayah does not even bother to specify who takes your place. That vagueness is part of the sting. You are not being threatened with a rival; you are being told that the slot you occupy is generic.

Al-Tabari fills in the sense: another creation besides you, **in your place**, whose making Allah renews. Al-Muyassar adds one word that changes the temperature of the whole sentence. The replacements are **a people who obey Allah**.

Sit with that. The new creation is not described as stronger than you, or smarter, or more numerous. It is described by one quality only: it obeys. Which tells you what the current creation is actually being measured on, and what it is failing at. The threat is not "someone better will outcompete you." It is "someone will simply do the one thing you were made for."

This is part of what makes the verse so humbling. Not only can you be removed; you can be replaced. The moral universe is not built around your permanence.

There is an older argument buried in these words, and Ibn Kathir brings it to the surface. He reads the ayah as **proof that the resurrection happens** — Allah pointing to the fact that He created the heavens and the earth, which are a mightier construction than the making of a human being. The reasoning is the plainest kind: the One who did the greater thing is not going to be stopped by the lesser one. The Quran states that comparison directly elsewhere — *la-khalqu al-samawati wa al-ardi akbaru min khalqi al-nas*, "the creation of the heavens and the earth is greater than the creation of mankind, but most people do not know" (40:57).

So there are two readings on the table, and they are worth holding together rather than choosing between. Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar read *a new creation* as a replacement **in this world**: this people goes, another people comes. Ibn Kathir reads the same words as a demonstration of Allah's power to **raise the dead**. Both readings run on the identical engine — the One who built the sky is not strained by remaking you — and the surah itself refuses to separate them. Two ayahs later, in 14:21, everyone is already standing before Allah, arguing about who misled whom. The replacement in this world and the standing in the next are being shown as the same power, exercised twice.

### 3. **Wa ma dhalika 'ala Allahi bi-'aziz**: What Feels Immense to Us Is Not Hard for Him

Then the second ayah closes the thought:

**wa ma dhalika 'ala Allahi bi-'aziz**

and that is not difficult, not inaccessible, not beyond Him at all.

[PAUSE]

The word **'aziz** can carry senses of might, strength, and also something hard to attain or overcome. Here the negation tells you that replacing a people, overturning a creation-order, or bringing forth new creation is not some immense burden for Allah.

Al-Tabari puts two words on it. Wiping you out and putting another creation in your place is not, upon Allah, something **out of reach** and not something **hard to get at** — and he gives the reason in a single clause: because He is the One with power over whatever He wills. Al-Muyassar refuses to leave it at a denial and flips it into a positive: it is not beyond Allah, *rather it is easy and simple for Him*. The verse says "not hard." The commentator hears "effortless."

Three small features of the sentence carry that.

First, **dhalika** — *that*. It is the far demonstrative, and it reaches back to grab the entire operation just described: removing a whole people, producing a fresh creation in their place. All of it gets bundled into one small pointing word, as if to say: this thing that took your breath away to hear — that. One item.

Second, the word order. **'ala Allahi**, "upon Allah," is pushed forward, ahead of the thing being denied. The stress lands there. It may well be beyond you to imagine; the question of whether it is heavy **upon Allah** is a different question entirely, and the answer is no.

Third, the little **bi-** on *'aziz*. In Arabic, when you deny something with *ma*, you can attach an extra *bi-* to the word you are denying. It adds nothing to that word's meaning; it hardens the denial. The sentence is not saying "it is not particularly difficult." It is saying "difficult is not a word that touches this."

There is a quiet echo here that a reader of the whole surah will catch. Surah Ibrahim opens by naming Allah **al-'Aziz** — twice in its first four ayahs, once as *al-'Aziz al-Hamid* (14:1) and once as *al-'Aziz al-Hakim* (14:4). *Al-'Aziz* is the One nothing is out of reach for. Now, at 14:20, the very same word appears again — this time denied, and denied of the task. Nothing is *'aziz* upon the One who is *al-'Aziz*. The name and the negation are two sides of one truth, sitting sixteen ayahs apart.

To us, replacing worlds feels unimaginable.

To Allah, it is not hard.

That is the scale correction these ayahs impose.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Ego Lives as Though Allah's Project Depends on It

This can show up in arrogant or subtle ways.

Sometimes a civilization lives as though its power is permanent.

Sometimes a sinner lives as though tomorrow is guaranteed.

Sometimes even a believer serves as though religion itself would be poorer without his contribution.

[PAUSE]

These ayahs sweep all of that away.

Allah created the heavens and the earth in truth. He was not lonely before you. He is not trapped by you now. And if He wills, He can remove you and bring another creation.

And this is exactly where al-Tabari's small phrase does its work. Allah made the heavens and earth **with no backer and no helper**. If He did not require partners to build the universe, the idea that He requires you to maintain it does not survive contact with the first half of the ayah. The passage is arranged so that the second half is unarguable once you have accepted the first.

The Quran says this same thing elsewhere in almost the same breath, and attaches a name to it. *Wa rabbuka al-ghaniyyu dhu al-rahmah, in yasha' yudhhibkum* — "And your Lord is the Self-Sufficient, the Owner of mercy. If He wills, He can remove you" (6:133). That is the reason underneath the threat. He can replace us because He never needed us — and the very same ayah calls Him the Owner of mercy in the same line. Self-sufficiency and mercy are not in tension. The One who has no need of you is the One still giving you time.

This is not meant to produce despair. It is meant to produce sobriety, humility, and relief from self-importance. You are invited into Allah's truth. You are not holding it up.

There is an unexpected comfort in that. If Allah's plan rested on your shoulders, every one of your failures would be a crack in the structure of reality. It does not. Which means your mistakes are survivable, your absence is bearable, and your work — real as it is — is a privilege extended to you rather than a load you were conscripted to carry. Being non-essential is not the same as being unwanted. It is what makes the invitation an invitation.

### Theme 2: Seeing Creation in Truth Changes How We Read Our Own Lives

If the heavens and earth are made **bi-l-haqq**, then your life is not floating in meaninglessness.

Your choices matter.

Your rebellion matters.

Your repentance matters.

Your response to Allah matters because you are living inside a creation already stamped with purpose and truth.

[PAUSE]

And that means the worst delusion is not merely sin. It is trivialization. Treating life as though it were casual, self-authored, and disposable while standing inside a world made with haqq.

Al-Muyassar's reading sharpens the charge. He says the heavens and earth were not made as idle play; they were made so that people would reason from them to Allah's oneness and His complete power, and worship Him alone. If that is true, then treating the world as scenery is not a neutral attitude. It is a misreading of a text that was written to be understood. The evidence was laid out at the scale of galaxies, and the response was a shrug.

Notice too what the replacement people are said to do. They **obey**. Not "they build more," not "they last longer" — they obey. So when the ayah measures a creation, obedience is the measure it uses. That is a very simple question to put to your own life, and a hard one to dodge: on the one axis this ayah actually cares about, where am I?

These ayahs wake the servant up. They say: you are not in a toy world, and you are not its center. You are in Allah's truthful creation. So return to the truth before you learn your replaceability too late.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

The surah has just shown deeds scattered like ash. Now it shows the doer himself in true scale.

The heavens and the earth were made in truth.

You were placed inside that truth, not above it.

And the One who made all this can remove you and bring another creation without difficulty at all.

These two ayahs are repeated word for word later in the Quran. In Surah Fatir the same two sentences appear again in sequence — *in yasha' yudhhibkum wa ya'ti bi-khalqin jadid* (35:16), then *wa ma dhalika 'ala Allahi bi-'aziz* (35:17). Not a paraphrase, not an echo: the identical pair, dropped into a different surah. When the Quran repeats itself this exactly, it is worth reading as a fixed statement being restated rather than a new point being made — the same verdict on human indispensability, entered twice into the record.

And notice which two things the ayahs place side by side. On one side, the heavens and the earth, made in truth, alone, by a Lord with no helper. On the other side, us — and the single sentence it takes to remove us. The passage does not argue that we are worthless. It argues that we are not load-bearing.

That is not only a threat. It is a liberation from delusion. We do not need to inflate ourselves, secure ourselves, or imagine ourselves indispensable. We need to align ourselves with the haqq in which we were placed.

And there is one more thing hidden in the wording. The ayah says *if He wills* — and He has not willed it. Every morning you are still here is a will that went the other way. The replacements have not been sent for. The door al-Muyassar describes is still open: obey, and be counted among the people who do.

### Questions to Carry

- Where do you still live as though your continued presence is guaranteed rather than given?
- What would change if you felt, deeply, that Allah's religion and world do not depend on your ego?
- In what part of your life are you resisting the truth while standing inside a creation already made by it and for it?
- The replacement creation is described by one quality: it obeys. On that measure alone, what would an honest account of this week look like?
- If Allah built the heavens and earth as evidence to be reasoned from, when did you last actually look at them and let them argue with you?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**These ayahs humble the servant by placing him inside a world created in truth and reminding him that the Lord who made the heavens and the earth can replace a refusing creation without effort at all.**

### Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let us live with the arrogance of imagined permanence. Show us the truth of Your creation, make us small in the right way before Your greatness, and keep us among those who align with the haqq before we are removed from the chance to do so.*
