# Their Deeds Are Like Ashes Struck by Wind on a Stormy Day

## Surah Ibrahim (14:18)

[ayah:14:18] مَّثَلُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ بِرَبِّهِمْ ۖ أَعْمَـٰلُهُمْ كَرَمَادٍ ٱشْتَدَّتْ بِهِ ٱلرِّيحُ فِى يَوْمٍ عَاصِفٍ ۖ لَّا يَقْدِرُونَ مِمَّا كَسَبُوا۟ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلضَّلَـٰلُ ٱلْبَعِيدُ

*Mathalu alladhina kafaru bi-rabbihim a'maluhum ka-ramadin ishtaddat bihi al-rihu fi yawmin 'asif. La yaqdiruna mimma kasabu 'ala shay'. Dhalika huwa al-dalalu al-ba'id.*

**The example of those who disbelieved in their Lord is that their deeds are like ashes upon which the wind blows fiercely on a stormy day. They have no power over anything of what they earned. That is the far error.**

[PAUSE]

## Introduction

This ayah is about wasted effort.

Not laziness.

Not absence of action.

But a whole life of doing, building, achieving, gathering, earning, and producing that reaches the Day of Allah only to discover that what seemed substantial has the density of ash in a storm.

The hook of the ayah is this: **the tragedy of disbelief is not only punishment for evil, but the exposure of a life's apparent substance as spiritually unholdable once it is severed from the Lord for whom deeds were meant to stand.**

Notice where this parable is placed. The verses just before it describe a body in torment — foul water forced down in sips, death arriving from every direction while the person still does not die. The reader braces for more of the same. Instead the surah changes the subject entirely. It stops describing what will be *done to* these people and starts describing what will happen to what they *did*.

That shift is the point. Punishment is something that arrives from outside you. This is something that happens to the inside of your own life's work.

[PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **A'maluhum ka-ramad**: The Ayah Chooses Ash, Not Stone, Dust, or Leaves

Before the parable names anything at all, it names *people*:

**mathalu alladhina kafaru bi-rabbihim**

the likeness of those who disbelieved in their Lord.

Only then does it mention deeds. And this small ordering caught the attention of the earliest Arabic grammarians. Al-Tabari records that they differed over it. Some of the Basran grammarians read the sentence as beginning a fresh statement — *and among the things We relate to you is the likeness of those who disbelieved* — with the rest of the verse then explaining what that likeness is, the same construction as *mathal al-jannah*, "the likeness of the Garden," in Surah Ar-Ra'd (13:35). Others, from the Kufan school, said the comparison really belongs to the *deeds*, and that Arabic simply puts the people first because people are the better-known thing, then brings the statement about the deeds along with their owner. On that reading the sense is: *the likeness of the deeds of those who disbelieved in their Lord is like ashes.*

[PAUSE]

Both readings arrive at the same meaning. But notice what the word order preserves either way. The verse will not let you look at the deeds by themselves. The person comes first and the deeds arrive attached to him, because the thing that made them ash was not a flaw inside the deeds. It was something about the doer.

And the ayah is precise about what that something is. Not *kafaru bi-llah* — they disbelieved in God — but **bi-rabbihim**, in their **Rabb**. Their Lord. The one who owns them, sustains them, raises them, supplies them. Every deed they ever performed used strength He gave, in a body He formed, with time He allotted. To reject the *Rabb* while spending His provision is not one belief among many. It is a contradiction running underneath the whole life.

Then the comparison itself. The parable does not compare their deeds to something merely broken.

It compares them to:

**ramad**

ash.

[PAUSE]

Ash is what remains after something has already burned. It suggests prior activity, prior energy, prior material. There was once form here. There was once weight. There was once something that looked combustible, useful, or warm.

But now what remains is the faint residue after consumption.

This matters because the ayah is not talking about people who never acted at all. It is talking about deeds that looked like deeds, labor that looked productive, effort that could be pointed to, perhaps admired. Yet when separated from true belief in the Lord, the final remainder is ash.

The classical commentators are strikingly specific about what kind of deeds are meant here — and it is not what most readers expect. Al-Muyassar glosses "the deeds of the disbelievers in this world" with two examples: *al-birr*, plain goodness and decency toward others, and *silat al-arham*, keeping the bonds of family alive.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that.

The parable is not describing a life of obvious wickedness turning to nothing. It is describing kindness turning to nothing. The man who visited his aging relatives. The woman who gave and gave. Real good, actually done, actually helping people — and still, on that Day, ash.

That is what makes the image so severe. Ash is not the leftover of garbage. Ash is the leftover of something that genuinely burned, something that gave real heat while it lasted. The deeds were not fake. Their benefit in this world was real. What they lacked was the one thing that would let them survive the crossing into the next.

Ibn Kathir names that missing thing directly: these were people who worshipped others besides Allah and rejected His Messengers, and so they built their acts, in his phrase, on no foundation at all. Al-Muyassar uses the same word at the end of his comment — *striving and working upon no foundation*. Not defective work. Unfounded work. The distinction matters, because a foundation is precisely the part of a building nobody sees once the building is finished.

### 2. **Ishtaddat bihi al-rih fi yawmin 'asif**: The Image Is Not Mild Wind, but Violent Exposure

Then the ash meets:

**al-rih**

the wind,

and not in an ordinary scene, but

**fi yawmin 'asif**

on a storming, violently windy day.

[PAUSE]

The verb **ishtaddat** intensifies the force. This is not a breeze passing over a quiet surface. The parable is built for removal. Ash is chosen because it cannot withstand such a day.

Look closely at the little word attached to that verb: **ishtaddat bihi** — the wind grew violent *with it*. Not merely *over* the ash or *against* the ash. The ash is swept up into the wind's own violence and carried inside it. There is no moment where the ash resists and then loses. The intensifying of the storm and the disappearance of the ash are one event.

[PAUSE]

And notice which word carries the description **'asif**. Grammatically it attaches not to the wind but to the **yawm**, the day. A *stormy day*. Al-Muyassar unpacks the phrase as "a day possessed of violent wind" — the wind has become the day's whole character, the way we say a bitter winter rather than a bitter hour. This is not a gust that passes and lets the afternoon return to normal. There is no sheltered hour on that day, no calm stretch to gather anything back in.

Al-Muyassar then adds four words that finish the picture: the wind **left no trace of it**. Not scattered but recoverable. Not diminished. No trace.

That gives the image its spiritual force. Some things appear stable only because the testing atmosphere has not yet arrived. They keep their shape in calm weather. But when the true wind comes, their reality is revealed.

The Day of Judgment is not only a courtroom. It is also a climate in which false substance cannot remain gathered.

But there is a further turn in the parable that is easy to miss, and al-Muyassar puts his finger on it: *disbelief did away with their deeds just as the wind did away with the ash.* Read that carefully. The wind is not the thing that destroyed the deeds. **Kufr** is the thing that destroyed the deeds. The wind only completes what fire had already done.

[PAUSE]

Because that is the honest sequence in the parable. The ash was already ash before the storm ever arrived. The wind did not burn anything. It simply removed what was already weightless and showed everyone what it had been all along.

Which means the Day is not when the loss happens. The Day is when the loss becomes visible.

### 3. **La yaqdiruna mimma kasabu 'ala shay'**: The Real Disaster Is Powerlessness Over What They Earned

The ayah then interprets its own parable:

**la yaqdiruna mimma kasabu 'ala shay'**

they have no power over anything of what they earned.

[PAUSE]

This is devastating. The deeds are described as something they **kasabu** - acquired, earned, worked for. There was exertion. Intention at some level. Ownership in the worldly sense.

But on that Day, they no longer possess any ability over what they once thought belonged to them. The labor has slipped out of their hands.

The issue is not merely that the deeds are few. It is that they are unusable. Unretrievable. Unholdable.

The verb the ayah chooses is worth pausing on. **Yaqdirun** comes from the root *q-d-r*, the root of **qudrah**, power, capability, the very word used for Allah's own power over all things. What is denied to them is not possession. It is *capacity*. They are not told their deeds were confiscated. They are told they can do nothing with them.

[PAUSE]

And the ayah blocks every exit. **Mimma kasabu** carries the partitive *min*: not one portion of what they earned. **'Ala shay'** — over any *thing*, and in Arabic a negated *shay'* is the smallest unit the language has, the last crumb. Between those two words there is no remainder left to claim. Nothing of it. Not any of it.

Al-Muyassar draws out precisely what is lost: they will not find among their deeds anything that *benefits* them with Allah. That is a careful phrase. It does not say the deeds never happened. The visit to the relative happened. The generosity happened. What is gone is any power to cash it in — the benefit, the claim, the weight.

Ibn Kathir adds the detail that makes it unbearable: their deeds vanished from them *at the moment they most needed the reward*. Not lost early, while there was still time to rebuild. Lost at the exact instant of maximum need, with the account open and nothing in it.

Then the ayah closes:

**dhalika huwa al-dalalu al-ba'id**

that is the far error.

Far, because the person did not merely miss the road by one step. He built an entire economy of effort away from its Lord.

[PAUSE]

Two small things in that closing phrase repay attention.

First, Arabic slips the pronoun **huwa** — *that, that is* — between the subject and the predicate. The sentence would work without it. It is there to shut the door: *this*, precisely this, and nothing else you might have been calling by that name, is the far straying.

Second, ask what **dhalika** is pointing back at. Al-Muyassar answers it plainly: *that striving, that working on no foundation — that is the far straying, far from the straight path.* The word does not point at a creed they recited. It points at the whole enterprise. The effort itself was the misguidance. A man can be walking with great energy, and walking is exactly the problem.

And "far" has a measure. Al-Muyassar reads *ba'id* as distance from the **sirat al-mustaqim**, the straight path. Distance is not the same as wickedness. You can drift far without ever once feeling that you rebelled. That is what makes this straying the *far* kind — nothing dramatic marked the moment it became irreversible.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Many People Fear Failure, but the Ayah Warns About Futility

Worldly people often fear visible collapse.

We fear embarrassment, loss, humiliation, public failure, not being recognized, not finishing what we started. But this ayah warns of something deeper than failure: futility.

Failure still assumes the thing had weight and simply did not reach its target. Futility is worse. Futility means the thing never had the kind of substance you thought it did.

[PAUSE]

A person may pour years into work, identity, reputation, generosity, architecture, family legacy, ideological struggle, or self-made significance. From the ground, it may look impressive. But if it is severed from right relation to the Lord, the Day can reveal it not as a collapsed building, but as ash caught in wind.

This does not make action meaningless. It makes belief and orientation decisive.

And here the commentators' specificity does something uncomfortable. If the parable were about cruelty, most of us could read it from a safe distance. But when the examples given are decency and kindness to family, the safe distance disappears. These are the deeds people are *proudest* of. These are the ones a person reaches for when he asks himself whether his life was good.

[PAUSE]

Which means the ayah is not warning about the worst part of a wasted life. It is warning about the best part.

That is a harder thing to hear, and it needs to be heard carefully. The Quran is not saying kindness is worthless. It is saying that kindness, on its own, is not a *foundation* — and a house does not stand on its finest room.

There is also a mercy hidden inside the severity. The verse identifies the missing thing precisely, and the missing thing is not a quantity of good works. It is a relationship with the Rabb. That is a gap that can be closed in a moment. The tragedy described here is not that the requirement was too high. It is that the requirement was never taken seriously.

### Theme 2: The Soul Needs More Than Activity; It Needs Weight Before Allah

The ayah does not attack action itself. It attacks action cut off from its Rabb.

That should sober believers too. We can become fascinated by visible output while neglecting the hidden matter that gives deeds weight before Allah: belief, sincerity, submission, truthfulness, and orientation to Him.

[PAUSE]

Return to the word the commentators kept reaching for: **asas**, foundation. Ibn Kathir says these people built on none; al-Muyassar says their striving stood on none. It is a builder's word, and builders know something the rest of us forget. The foundation is the only part of a building that nobody admires, because by the time there is anything to admire, the foundation is buried.

So a life can be assessed entirely by its visible floors. The career, the reputation, the good name, the service to family — all of it measurable, all of it praised, and none of it able to report on what is underneath.

The wind in this parable is, among other things, an inspection. It does not test the floors. It tests the footing.

There is a version of religious life that also chases ash: doing what looks substantial in public while neglecting the inner root that would let the deed stand in divine weather.

So the question is not only: what are you doing?

It is: what will remain of what you are doing when the wind rises?

What in your life has the density to stay?

What would still matter if applause vanished, biography vanished, memory vanished, and only Allah's judgment remained?

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

The parable is simple and mercilessly clear.

There are deeds.

They look like something.

But their true composition is ash.

Then comes the day built for disclosure: fierce wind, storm atmosphere, no sheltering calm. And the people have no power left over anything they thought they earned.

But the parable has already told us the deeds were ash *before* the wind arrived. Kufr did that, not the storm. The Day does not perform the loss. It publishes it.

And the ayah names the people before it names the deeds, because that is where the answer lies too. The fix is not more output. It is the *bi-rabbihim* — the Lord who was denied, and who can be turned to at any hour a person is still breathing.

That is the far error, because it is not only moral rebellion. It is existential misinvestment. A whole life can be spent manufacturing what will not remain.

### Questions to Carry

- Which parts of your life are built for appearance in calm weather but would not survive the wind of truth?
- Are you more concerned with doing much, or with doing what will actually remain before Allah?
- What current effort in your life most needs renewed sincerity before it becomes ash in a storm?
- The commentators say the deeds in this parable were things like plain decency and keeping family ties. Which of your own good deeds are you quietly counting on — and is it standing on a foundation, or only on its own goodness?
- If the loss happens long before the Day reveals it, what would it take to check the footing of your life now rather than then?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**This ayah warns that disbelief does not merely invite punishment; it can turn a lifetime of apparent achievement into ash that the first real wind of the Hereafter scatters beyond recovery.**

### Du'a

*Allahumma, do not let our deeds become ash. Give our actions truth, sincerity, and weight before You, and protect us from spending our lives earning what we will have no power to hold on the Day it matters most.*
