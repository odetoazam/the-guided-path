## INTRODUCTION

I want you to picture a specific scene before we open the ayahs.

It is somewhere in seventh-century Madinah. A small house. The family inside has been fasting all day. There is not much food in the house — there never is, this is the early years of the community in Madinah, food is scarce, and what is there has been carefully prepared and is being looked at by everyone in the household with the particular kind of focus that only a person who is genuinely hungry can give to a piece of bread. The sun has set. The fast is about to be broken. The food is on the small low table in the middle of the room. The children are sitting around it. The parents have just finished the maghrib prayer.

And there is a knock at the door.

At the door is a poor man. He has not eaten today either. He says: *I am hungry. Do you have anything to share?*

Now I want you to ask yourself a very honest question. **What would you do?**

The honest answer for almost everyone alive — including for me — is that *we would give him something*, but with a particular small interior calculation running underneath the giving. We would give him *some* of the food. We would not give him *most* of it. We would not give him *the part we ourselves had been most looking forward to*. We would give him what is sometimes called the *charitable margin* — the part of our food we could most easily afford to lose. The bread is for him. The meat is for us. The water is for him. The dates are for us. We would give in a way that protected our own portion of the meal, and we would feel good about the giving, and we would close the door, and we would sit back down and break our fast on what was left, *which would be most of it*.

The ayahs we are about to read are about a different kind of giving.

They are about a family that did not protect its own portion. They are about a family that gave away the food they themselves had been waiting all day to eat. And they are about *what they wanted back for it* — which is the part the Quran preserves, in a single quoted sentence, that almost no English audience has ever been told they are reading.

These are three ayahs from Surat al-Insan — Surah 76, the surah whose name means *the human being*. And the surah is, in a deep sense, asking a single question across its entire length: *what kind of human being is the human being supposed to be?* And the answer the surah gives — not in a list of doctrines, not in a set of rules, but in a few specific scenes from the inside of an actual believing household — is going to land harder than almost any abstract description of charity ever could.

There is also, in the middle of these three ayahs, **one small attached Arabic pronoun** — three letters on the end of one word — that decides what kind of giving this is. The classical commentators are, in fact, largely agreed on what it refers to; and what they agree on is *harder* than the softer reading that English audiences are usually offered. We will get to the pronoun, and we will let the commentators say what they actually said.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### 1. يُطْعِمُونَ الطَّعَامَ — The Sentence That Holds the Food and the Hungry in the Same Grip

The first thing that happens in 76:8 is a small syntactic choice that the English translation completely flattens.

The ayah says: *wa-**yuṭʿimūna-ṭ-ṭaʿāma***. *And they feed the food*.

In English, this sounds redundant. Of course feeding involves food. Why didn't Allah just say *they feed the poor and the orphan and the captive*, the way every charity ad does in English? Why name *the food* separately at all, when the verb *yuṭʿimūn* already contains *feeding* by definition?

Here is the actual grammar, and it is worth getting right. The verb *aṭʿama* is **doubly transitive** — it takes *two* direct objects: the one you feed, and the thing you feed them with. *Aṭʿamahu-ṭ-ṭaʿām* — *he fed him the food*. Arabic permits you to name either object and drop the other. You can say *they feed the poor* and leave the food unnamed. You can say *they feed the food* and leave the recipients unnamed.

**76:8 names both.** *Yuṭʿimūna-ṭ-ṭaʿāma* — the food — *…miskīnan wa-yatīman wa-asīrā* — the poor, the orphan, the captive. The sentence holds *the thing given* and *the ones given to* in the same grip, on the same verb, and it puts the food **first**. Before you are told who received it, you are told *what left the house*.

(A note for precision, because it matters: *al-ṭaʿām* here is a plain direct object — *mafʿūl bihi*. It is the thing fed, not a verbal noun. It is not what the grammarians call a *mafʿūl muṭlaq*, the cognate object; that construction would require the verbal noun of the verb itself — *yuṭʿimūna iṭʿāman*, *they feed a feeding*. That is not what the ayah says, and the meaning here does not need it.)

What the ayah *does* say is quieter and, I think, heavier — and it sits in one syllable: the definite article.

Not *some food*. Not *a portion of food*. **ٱلطَّعَام — *the* food.** The food that was in the house. The meal they were sitting in front of. And Arabic had an obvious, ordinary way to say the other thing: *yuṭʿimūna min ṭaʿāmihim* — *they feed **from** their food* — with the partitive *min* that means "some of, a portion of." Arabic uses that construction constantly. It is not used here. There is no *min*. There is no partition. There is *the food*, definite, whole, named as one thing, going out the door.

In English, we say *they fed the poor*, and the sentence leaves us free to imagine it was extra food — leftovers, scraps, what was beyond their needs, what they could spare. English *naturally permits the charitable margin*: the assumption that the giver gave from what was *extra*, not from what was *load-bearing*.

And this is exactly where the commentators refuse to let us off. **al-Ṭabarī** glosses the phrase: *kāna hāʾulāʾi-l-abrāru yuṭʿimūna-ṭ-ṭaʿāma ʿalā ḥubbihim iyyāhu, wa-shahwatihim lahu* — "these righteous ones would feed the food **while loving it themselves, and craving it**." He transmits Mujāhid on the same words in three words: ***wa-hum yashtahūnahu*** — "**while they wanted it**." And **al-Muyassar** adds the detail that closes the door completely: they fed the food *maʿa ḥubbihim lahu **wa-ḥājatihim ilayh*** — "along with their love for it **and their need of it**."

*Their need of it.* Not their surplus. Not their margin. The food was *load-bearing* — they needed it — and it went out the door anyway.

This is the grammar and the tafsir pointing at the same thing: *no inner reservation*. *Not protecting your own portion*. *The meal you had been looking at all day going out the door with the visitor while you sit down to nothing*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment. Because the modern relationship to charity is structured almost entirely around the *charitable margin*. We calculate. We figure out what we can spare. We earmark a percentage of our income for giving — a percentage that is, by definition, *the part we would not miss*. We give from what is *extra*. And the giving feels good, and the giving is real, and the giving is not nothing — but it is a different kind of giving from what 76:8 is naming. It is *min ṭaʿāminā* — *from* our food, a slice off the edge. The ayah does not say *min*. And the two commentators do not let the word *ṭaʿām* float free into abstraction: they pin it to a household that **wanted it and needed it**.

The believers being praised in 76:8 are not giving from their *extra*. They are giving from their *plate*. **They are giving the food.**

Now hear the next phrase — because it contains a three-letter pronoun that decides what the whole act costs.

### 2. عَلَىٰ حُبِّهِ — The Pronoun That Prices the Gift

After Allah names what they fed, He says three words: *ʿalā ḥubbihi*. **Despite love for it.**

The phrase is a preposition plus a noun plus a pronoun. *ʿalā* (upon, despite, in spite of, on the basis of) + *ḥubbi* (love, the noun, in genitive because of the preposition) + *-hi* (the attached pronoun *his/its*, third person masculine singular).

Everything turns on three letters: **what does *-hi* refer to?**

The nearest available referent is *al-ṭaʿām* — *the food*, grammatically masculine, sitting two words back. And this is not merely the grammatically natural reading; it is what the commentators in front of us actually say, and they say it without hedging.

**al-Ṭabarī** takes the phrase and renders it directly: they fed the food *ʿalā ḥubbihim iyyāhu, wa-shahwatihim lahu* — "**upon their love for it, and their craving for it**." He puts the pronoun on the food explicitly, by replacing it with an unambiguous pronoun of his own (*iyyāhu*, "it"), and then he doubles it with a word that removes all doubt about what kind of love this is: *shahwah*, **appetite**. This is not affection for a concept. This is *wanting to eat it*. And he supports the reading with Mujāhid, whose entire comment on the phrase is three words: ***wa-hum yashtahūnahu*** — "**while they were craving it**."

**al-Muyassar** reads it the same way, and even resolves the preposition for the reader: *maʿa ḥubbihim lahu wa-ḥājatihim ilayh* — "**along with their love for it and their need of it**." Note what he has done: he glosses *ʿalā* as *maʿa*, "along with" — the giving happened *with the wanting still running*, not after the wanting had been extinguished.

So the reading of the two commentaries in front of us is settled, and it is the harder one. The phrase names **the price of the gift**. They wanted this food. They craved it. They needed it. *And they gave it anyway.* The love was not absent from the giving; the love was *what the giving cost*.

[PAUSE]

There is a **minority reading**, and it should be named as the minority it is, not dressed up as a consensus.

Some have proposed that the pronoun refers not to the food but to **Allah** — *ʿalā ḥubbi Allāh*, "**out of love for Him**" — reading the pronoun as pointing forward to the One whose face is named in the very next ayah. It is a defensible construction, and it produces a beautiful meaning: that the love which propelled the food out the door was love for *Allah*, not love for the recipient.

But it is not what al-Ṭabarī says, and it is not what al-Muyassar says, and I am not going to tell you the commentators split evenly when they did not. Both of the tafsīrs consulted here commit to **the food**, and al-Ṭabarī reinforces it with a transmitted report from Mujāhid. Where a reading is the majority position, say so; where it is a minority, say that too. **The food is the majority reading, and it is the one I will build on.**

And here is the thing worth noticing: *we do not need the minority reading to get the love of Allah into this passage.* We do not have to squeeze it out of a contested pronoun — **because the very next ayah says it outright.** *Li-wajhi-llāh.* **For the face of Allah.** The motivation is not hidden in an ambiguity. It is stated, in plain words, one sentence later.

So the two things are both here, but they arrive in the order the ayahs give them, not smuggled into a single pronoun:

- ***ʿalā ḥubbihi*** (76:8) names **the cost** — they loved the food, craved it, needed it, and released it.
- ***li-wajhi-llāh*** (76:9) names **the direction** — the whole act was pointed at One face.

I want you to feel why the pairing matters.

Because almost every modern conversation about charity gets *one or the other*, never both. Either people talk about the *cost* of the giving — *I gave even though I needed it, I gave even though it hurt* — and the conversation becomes about *the heroism of the giver*, with the giver as the central figure. Or people talk about the *motivation* — *I gave because I loved God, I gave for the right reason* — and the conversation becomes about *the purity of the intention*, with the inner state of the giver as the central figure.

The Quran gives you both, in sequence, in two consecutive sentences, and it will not let you keep one and drop the other. **The cost is real *and* the direction is real** — the food is loved *and* the One it is given for is loved more; the giver wanted to keep it *and* the wanting was placed on a scale that had something heavier on the other side. **Neither alone is enough.** A giving that has cost but no Beloved is just self-mortification — suffering for nothing larger than the suffering. A giving that has a Beloved but no cost is easy generosity from the margin, where the love is never actually tested. Put 76:8 and 76:9 back to back and you get the only combination that holds: *the love of the food is exactly what proves the love of Allah is greater*. The cost is the evidence that the love is real. The Beloved is the reason the cost is bearable.

[PAUSE]

Hold the cost and the direction in mind, because the third anchor — the most radical move in the entire passage — is hiding in the third recipient on the list, and most listeners walk past it without noticing what it is doing.

### 3. وَأَسِيرًا — The Captive on the List Is Probably an Enemy

After the doubled-pronoun phrase, Allah names the recipients of the food. Three categories. *Miskīnan wa-yatīman wa-asīrā*. **A poor person, and an orphan, and a captive.**

The first two are relatively easy to receive. *Miskīn* is the poor person — etymologically from *sukūn* (stillness, settled-ness), the *settled-poor*, the person whose poverty is not a passing crisis but a permanent condition of their life. The poor have been the obvious object of charity in every religious tradition that has ever existed. Feeding the poor is the universal first move of religious giving. Nothing surprising here.

*Yatīm* is the orphan — etymologically from a root meaning *to be alone*, *to be isolated*, *to be without one of the companions one was supposed to have*. The yatīm is the child whose parents are gone, whose advocates are gone, whose protective relational network has been removed by death. Feeding the orphan is also a universally recognized religious obligation, named directly in many places in the Quran (89:17, 90:15, 93:9, 107:2). The orphan is the *legally vulnerable child*, the one whom the believing community has a structural duty to absorb.

So far, two ordinary categories. Two recipients you would expect any religious tradition to name.

And then, third on the list — *wa-asīrā*. **And a captive.**

I need you to feel how unexpected this is, because the English translation makes it look ordinary. *Captive* in modern English just means *prisoner*, and we have no real intuition for what that meant in seventh-century Madinah.

The commentary does not leave this to our imagination. **al-Muyassar** glosses the word directly: *wa-asīran **usira fi-l-ḥarbi mina-l-mushrikīna wa-ghayrihim*** — "**and a captive, taken prisoner in war, from the polytheists and others**." That is the plain sense: a **prisoner of war**, taken in battle from the opposing side, currently held in custody by the Muslim community. The asīr is a member of the army that was *just* trying to kill the believers. The asīr was, twenty-four hours ago, on the battlefield with a sword in his hand, attempting to cut down the same Muslims whose household is now feeding him.

(And note the reach of *wa-ghayrihim*, "**and others**" — the category is not narrowed to one enemy. Whoever is in your custody, whatever brought them there, they are on the list.)

And Allah is putting him on the list of recipients of the food.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment, because almost no English audience has ever been told to.

The believing family in 76:8 has just been described as feeding *the food* — definite, whole, no partitive *min*, no charitable margin. They are doing it *while still craving it and needing it* (*ʿalā ḥubbihi*, as al-Ṭabarī and al-Muyassar read it). And the third category of person they are feeding it to is *the man who was trying to kill them yesterday*. Not in a metaphor. Not in a poetic embellishment. *In the actual seventh-century historical context of the surah*, the *asīr* is the captured enemy combatant being held in the city, and Allah is naming him — alongside the poor and the orphan — as a person the believing household is *expected to feed from their own plate, with their own food, despite their own hunger, out of their own love for Allah*.

This is one of the most radical statements about the boundaries of mercy in any religious text in human history.

Most religious traditions have a *circle* of who counts as a legitimate recipient of charity. The circle starts at the family, expands to the tribe, expands to the religion, expands to the *fellow believer in distress*, and stops there. The enemy is *outside the circle*. The enemy is, by definition, not a person you owe anything to, because the relationship is defined by mutual hostility. **The asīr in 76:8 is on the inside of the circle.** He is the enemy, and he is on the list, and he is on the list *between the poor and the orphan*, in the middle of the most basic religious obligations a believer has, named with the same word *yuṭʿimūna* (they feed) as the other two categories. He is not an exception. He is not a special case. **He is the third item on a three-item list of who you feed.**

And remember — the food they are feeding him with is *the food*. The food they themselves craved. The food they had been waiting all day to eat. **They are giving the food they wanted to eat themselves to the man who was trying to kill them yesterday, for the face of Allah, while they themselves go hungry — and they are telling him, to his face, that they want nothing back for it.** (We will get to that part in the next anchor.)

Why? Why does the Quran do this?

Because — and this is the deepest theological move of the entire passage — the believers in this ayah are not feeding the asīr *because the asīr deserves food*. They are feeding the asīr *because Allah is the One whose face the food is being given for*, and Allah's face is not contingent on whether the recipient deserves the food. The recipient is not the reason for the giving. The recipient is just the *conduit through which the giving reaches the One it is actually for*. And if the One it is actually for is *Allah*, then it does not matter who the conduit is. The conduit can be the poor person you have known all your life. The conduit can be the orphan child of your neighbor. **The conduit can be the man who was trying to kill you yesterday.** The food is going *through* the recipient *to* the Beloved, and the Beloved sees the food arrive regardless of which conduit it traveled through.

This is what it means to give *li-wajhi-llāh* — **for the face of Allah** — in the operational sense the next ayah is going to spell out. *Li-wajhi-llāh* is the only motivation for giving that *can* include the captive enemy on the list, because every other motivation has the recipient embedded somewhere in it as a partial reason. *I give because I love them. I give because they need it. I give because they deserve it. I give because we share a faith.* All of these reasons would *exclude the asīr*, because the asīr does not love you back, and his needing it is irrelevant if he was trying to kill you, and he certainly does not deserve it from your perspective, and you do not share a faith. **Only the motivation of giving for Allah's face survives the inclusion of the enemy on the list.** And the Quran, by putting the asīr on the list, is *forcing the listener to upgrade their motivation for charity to the only motivation that can hold the asīr*.

If your charity excludes the captive enemy, your charity is not yet for *Allah's face*. It is still for some smaller reason that has the recipient embedded inside it. The asīr on the list is the *test* of whether you have actually arrived at *li-wajhi-llāh*, or whether you are still operating from a lesser motivation that has not yet been forced to its full extent.

[PAUSE]

This is the Quran's surgery. *It puts the most impossible recipient on the list, and waits to see whether your charity can include him, and if it cannot, your charity has not yet become what it is supposed to be.*

Now hear the last move — because in the next ayah, Allah does something almost no listener notices the strangeness of: *He puts a sentence in the givers' mouths*, in direct quotation, and the sentence does something to the recipient that the food alone could not do.

### Part 1 Continued: إِنَّمَا نُطْعِمُكُمْ لِوَجْهِ ٱللَّهِ — The Sentence That Cancels the Debt

In 76:9, Allah quotes the believers as saying:

***Innamā nuṭʿimukum li-wajhi-llāhi lā nurīdu minkum jazā'an wa-lā shukūrā.***

**We feed you only for the face of Allah; we want from you no reward and no thanks.**

Notice first **who they are addressing**. The pronouns are second-person plural — *nuṭʿimukum*, *we feed YOU*; *minkum*, *from YOU*. The sentence is aimed at the people they have just handed the food to: the poor, the orphan, and the captive.

Now — was this sentence actually *spoken* to them, or was it said inwardly? Here the commentators genuinely differ, and it is worth being straight about the difference rather than picking the prettier option.

**al-Ṭabarī reads it as spoken.** His gloss is explicit: ***yaqūlūna li-lladhīna yuṭʿimūnahum dhālika-l-ṭaʿām*** — "**they say to those whom they feed that food**: we do not want from you, O people, for our feeding you, any recompense or thanks." He repeats it on the next ayah too — *yaqūlūna li-man aṭʿamūhu min ahli-l-fāqati wa-l-ḥājah*, "**they say to those they fed, from the destitute and the needy**." For al-Ṭabarī this is real, audible, addressed speech: the words come out of the mouth, in the room, to the face of the one holding the food.

**al-Muyassar reads it inwardly.** His gloss: ***wa-yaqūlūna fī anfusihim***, "**and they say within themselves**: we are only being good to you seeking the pleasure of Allah."

Two readings, both from the commentary in front of us. Neither is a fringe view. And rather than collapse them, I want to sit with what al-Ṭabarī's reading — the more explicit one — actually accomplishes, because it is more radical than the inward reading, and it is the one modern readers are least likely to have been offered.

[PAUSE]

Think about what it costs to *receive* food when you are hungry.

If you have never had to accept charity, this may not be obvious. But every culture that has ever handed food from one hand to another knows that **the food arrives carrying a debt**. The recipient eats, and along with the eating comes an obligation: *now I owe you*. Now I must thank you. Now I must remember. Now, when you pass me in the market, I must lower my eyes a little. Now there is a permanent asymmetry between us, and the food I ate put it there. In a face-to-face society built on honour, this weight is not imaginary — it is the reason a proud hungry man will sometimes refuse a meal.

And this is *especially* true of the third recipient. The **asīr** — the captured enemy — is being fed by the very people he tried to kill. Imagine the weight of *that* meal. Every bite is a humiliation compounded: I am eating from the hand of my enemy, who has me in custody, who now has one more claim on me.

**So the believers speak.** They say: *innamā nuṭʿimukum li-wajhi-llāh* — this food is not from us to you, it is *for a face that is not yours and not ours*. And then, the operative clause: ***lā nurīdu minkum jazā'an wa-lā shukūrā*** — **we want from you no recompense and no thanks.**

That sentence is not a boast. **It is a release.** It reaches over and lifts the debt off the recipient's back before he has even finished the meal. It says: *you owe me nothing. Not repayment. Not gratitude. Not deference. Not a changed way of looking at me tomorrow. Eat, and walk out of here owing me nothing at all, because you were never the one I was giving to.*

This is the part that should genuinely stop a modern reader. We tend to assume that the highest charity is the *anonymous* one — give and vanish, never let them know it was you. And there is real merit in that. But 76:9 as al-Ṭabarī reads it is doing something anonymity *cannot* do. The anonymous giver protects his own sincerity but leaves the recipient holding an unpayable debt to an unknown creditor. The giver in 76:9 stays in the room, looks the recipient in the face, **and cancels the debt out loud.** He accepts the awkwardness of being known as the giver, in order to spare the other man the weight of being known as the indebted.

*He takes the discomfort onto himself so the hungry man can eat in peace.*

And notice: this is only *sayable* because of *li-wajhi-llāh*. If the food were from *them*, they could not cancel the debt — a gift from a person creates an obligation to that person, and no amount of "don't mention it" dissolves it. But if the food is **for the face of Allah**, then the giver is not a creditor at all. He is a hand the food passed through. There is no debt to cancel because there was never a creditor in the room. The sentence is true, and that is exactly why it works.

On al-Muyassar's reading, the same words name the same orientation held silently — the intention fixed on Allah's pleasure whether or not the recipient ever hears it. Both readings converge on the one thing the ayah will not let go of: **the giving is pointed at one face, and the recipient is not it.** Whether that fact was announced or held in the chest, Allah heard it, and Allah recorded it in the Book you are reading fourteen hundred years later.

[PAUSE]

And then, in 76:10, the believers' quoted words continue with the *reason* underneath the giving:

***Innā nakhāfu min rabbinā yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīrā.***

**Indeed we fear, from our Lord, a Day frowning and severe.**

I want to pause one more moment on a single word in this sentence, because it is one of the rarest and most physically loaded words in the entire Quran. The word is **qamṭarīrā**.

The root is **ق-م-ط-ر** — *qāf-mīm-ṭā'-rā'*. **Four consonants.** Like *taqshaʿirru* in 39:23, this is a *quadriliteral* root. We sat with quadriliterals in the previous reflection: the language goes to four letters when three are not enough, when the meaning is too physically embodied for the normal three-letter shape, when the word needs to *touch the physical world directly*. *Iqshaʿarra* was the verb of goose-flesh. *Qamṭarīr* is in the same rare category.

What does it mean? *Qamṭarīr* is the adjective for **a face that has gathered itself into a frown so severe that the brow is contracted, the eyes are narrowed, the entire face has folded in on itself in displeasure**. It is the verb of *the face becoming a frown*, where the frown is so total that the face has *become* the displeasure rather than just *expressing* it. The classical lexicons say it is the look of a man so distressed that *his whole face is collected together* into a single grim contraction. *Qamṭarīr* is the four-letter quadriliteral word for the most severe possible facial expression a human being can wear.

And Allah uses it to describe **a Day**. *Yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīrā* — *a frowning, qamṭarīr Day*. The Day itself has a face. The Day itself is wearing a facial expression. And the facial expression is the most contracted, most severe, most physically embodied frown that exists in the Arabic vocabulary. **The Day of Judgment is being named as a face that has folded itself into a frown so severe the language had to invent a four-letter word to hold the frown.**

This is the fear underneath the giving. *We feed you because we are afraid of a Day that has a face, and the face is qamṭarīr.* The believers are not feeding the food out of generic religious obligation. They are feeding the food because *the face of a specific Day is approaching*, and the face is contracted, and they want to do the only thing they can do in the dunya cradle — *give the food, while it is still possible, with the right interior orientation, before the Day arrives wearing the frown the language can barely contain in four consonants*.

The chain is now visible:
- They feed ***the*** *food* — definite, no partitive *min*, no charitable margin
- They feed it *while still craving and needing it* (*ʿalā ḥubbihi* — the cost of the giving)
- They feed it *to the captive enemy as well as the poor and orphan* (the radical inclusion that forces motivation to upgrade)
- They say *only for the face of Allah, and we want nothing back* — spoken to the recipients' faces on al-Ṭabarī's reading, held within on al-Muyassar's (the sentence that cancels the debt)
- And the reason underneath all of it is that *they fear a frowning, qamṭarīr Day* (the four-letter word for the most severe face possible)

**Five moves in three ayahs.** Each one is doing surgery on a different dimension of how human beings actually give. And the five moves together form a portrait of charity that no other text I know of has ever assembled in this density.

Let me now build a bridge to what this asks of you, today, the next time you have food on a table and a knock at the door.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1: The Sentence That Wants Nothing Back

I want to start with the part that I think is the most quietly transformative move in the entire passage, because it is the part most people walk past.

The sentence in 76:9 has two halves, and English readers usually only feel the first. The first half names the direction: *we feed you only for the face of Allah*. The second half names **what has been switched off**: ***lā nurīdu minkum jazā'an wa-lā shukūrā*** — *we want from you no recompense and no thanks*.

That second half is a list of *renounced returns*, and it is worth counting them. **Jazāʾ** is the material return — repayment, recompense, the favour repaid later, the debt honoured. **Shukūr** is the immaterial return — thanks, gratitude, acknowledgement, being spoken well of. Between them they cover essentially everything a human being can want back from a person they have helped. And al-Ṭabarī notes that *shukūr* here can be read either as a plural of *shukr* — *thanks, in all its forms* — or as a verbal noun, *the thanking itself*. Either way, the whole category is closed.

So here is the question the ayah puts to us. Not *did you give?* — but: **when you gave, what did you want back?**

Now I want to ask you to do something almost impossibly hard.

Be honest with yourself for sixty seconds. Recall the last time you gave something — money to a homeless person on the street, food to a hungry friend, time to someone who needed it, attention to a child who was asking for it. *Recall the giving*. And then *recall the sentence that was running quietly underneath it*. Not the words you said out loud. The words you were saying *to yourself*, in the back of your head, *about* the giving, *about* the recipient, *about* yourself for being the giver.

For most of us — and I am including myself in this very specifically — that quiet sentence is *not* the sentence in 76:9. It is something more like:
- *I hope they appreciate this.*
- *I hope they remember this.*
- *I hope someone else sees me doing this.*
- *I hope they don't ask again next week.*
- *I hope they realize how much this is costing me.*
- *I am the kind of person who does this — that means I am a good person.*
- *This is going to count on the Day.*
- *I will remember this the next time I am criticized.*
- *Why don't they thank me more?*
- *Why am I always the one giving?*

Every single one of these is **a fishing for some kind of return** — return-as-appreciation, return-as-being-seen, return-as-self-image, return-as-leverage. Each one names the *direction the giving is actually pointed*, and almost all of ours are pointed *back at ourselves* in some way. Every one of them would fall under *jazāʾ* or *shukūr*, and 76:9 has closed both.

Now — I want to be precise about what the believers renounce, because it is easy to overstate this, and overstating it makes the ayah *less* true rather than more.

They do **not** renounce all hope of reward whatsoever. Look at the grammar: *lā nurīdu **minkum*** — "we want nothing **from you**." The renunciation is aimed at *the recipients*. And al-Ṭabarī makes the rest explicit in his gloss on the very next ayah: *wa-lākinnā nuṭʿimukum **rajāʾan minnā an yuʾminanā rabbunā min ʿuqūbatih*** — "**but we feed you out of our hope that our Lord will secure us from His punishment**" on a Day of severe terror.

So the picture is not a giver who wants nothing at all. It is a giver who has **cut off every channel of return except one**. Not the recipient's gratitude. Not the recipient's prayer for them. Not the social capital of being known as generous. Not a favour banked for later. All of it, closed. And what remains open is a single channel pointed at *Allah* — hoping for His face, fearing His Day. The entire act collapses into an arrow with one head and one direction.

That is the shape of *ikhlāṣ* — sincerity, the purification of motive. And notice that the ayah does not describe it as a feeling or a temperament. **It describes it as a renunciation you can actually state**: *here is what I am not asking for.* The work of becoming the kind of person 76:9 describes is not the work of *feeling generous*. It is the work of **noticing what you are still hoping to get back, and letting go of it one channel at a time**.

Practical: the next time you give something — anything, even something tiny, even just holding a door for a stranger — *notice what you are hoping for in return*. Don't try to change it. Don't try to manufacture the 76:9 posture. *Just notice*. The noticing is the first move. Most of us have been giving for decades without ever once auditing what we were quietly expecting back, and the noticing alone — *for one act of giving, just once* — is the beginning of the work. And once you have noticed, then the next time, you can *try*, gently, to close one channel. *Allāhumma ijʿal hādhā li-wajhik. Ya Allah, make this for Your face.* And if the moment allows it — if saying so would lift a weight off the person rather than put one on them — you can do the fuller thing the believers in the ayah did, and **say the release out loud**: *you owe me nothing for this.* That is the entire practice. And it is available to you today, the next time you have anything to give to anyone for any reason.

### Theme 2: The Captive on the List Is the Test

I want to come back to the third recipient — the *asīr*, the captured enemy combatant — because I do not think most modern readers have allowed themselves to feel how radical this inclusion is, and the radicality is the entire point.

Let me try to make this concrete in a modern frame.

Imagine that there is a person in the world right now whom you regard as your enemy. Not a vague enemy. *A specific person*. Someone who has hurt you, or hurt your family, or hurt people you care about. Someone whose name produces a small clench in your chest the moment you think it. Someone who, if you saw them on the street tomorrow, you would cross to the other side to avoid. *Picture them now*. Hold the image. You will not say their name out loud. You will not write it down. *You just need to know who you are picturing*.

Now I want to tell you something that 76:8 is very directly saying about that person.

**Allah is saying: that person is on the list of people you are supposed to feed — with the food you yourself wanted, for the face of the One the giving is actually for, and wanting nothing back from them for it.**

Not metaphorically.

Not as a vague principle of universal kindness.

*Specifically, in the historical context of the surah*, the asīr was the man who had been on the battlefield with a sword in his hand twenty-four hours ago, trying to kill the very Muslims whose household was now feeding him. The hostility was real. The harm-intention was real. The danger was current. And Allah named him in the same breath as the poor and the orphan, using the same verb *yuṭʿimūn*, with the same *food*, and the same released claim: *we feed you only for the face of Allah; we want nothing back from you*.

Most religious frameworks have a *charity-circle* that gets bigger as the believer matures. You start by being kind to your family. You expand to your neighbors. You expand further to your fellow believers. You expand further to *strangers in distress*. And you stop somewhere just short of your *enemies* — because that is the natural outer limit of what charity can include without breaking down. *The enemies are the place where the circle ends*, in almost every religious system that has ever existed.

76:8 *includes the enemy inside the circle*. Not at the outer edge. *In the middle of the list*, between the poor and the orphan, with no special framing, no qualifications, no exceptions. The asīr is just *there*, named as a recipient, with the same word as the other two recipients, in a single ayah.

This is — and I am being completely serious about this — one of the most theologically radical things in the Quran, and almost no English translation makes it possible to feel the radicality.

Why does the Quran do this?

Because of the move I named in Part 1: *only the motivation of giving for Allah's face survives the inclusion of the enemy on the list*. Every other motivation collapses when the recipient is the asīr, because every other motivation has *the recipient* embedded somewhere in it as a partial reason. *I give because I love them, I give because they need it, I give because they deserve it, I give because we share a tribe, I give because they will reciprocate, I give because the community is watching*. None of these survive the asīr. The asīr does not love me, his need is real but my care for his need is not the load-bearing reason, he does not deserve it from my perspective, we do not share a tribe, he will not reciprocate, the community is not necessarily watching me feed him. **All the lesser motivations get stripped away when the recipient is the captive enemy.** And what is left, after the stripping, is **only the One the food is actually for**.

**The asīr is the test of whether you have actually arrived at *li-wajhi-llāh***. The asīr is the place where every counterfeit motivation falls off and only the real motivation is left standing.

And here is the harder thing — the thing the surah is actually asking of the modern reader.

We do not have *military prisoners* in our households. The historical situation of the original ayah is not the modern situation. But the *function* of the asīr in the ayah is *the person whom no other motivation could have made you give to*, and **everyone has at least one of these in their life**, in some form. The estranged sibling. The parent who hurt you. The ex-spouse. The colleague who undermined you. The neighbor who wronged you. The stranger whose entire identity-group you have been trained to regard with suspicion. *Whoever your asīr is, they exist*, and the surah is saying that **the giving they make possible is the only giving that proves your charity is for Allah's face**.

Because when you give to the people you naturally love, the giving could have been propelled by the loving. *That is fine, that is good, but it is not yet 76:8.* When you give to the people who naturally deserve it, the giving could have been propelled by the deserving. *Also fine, also good, also not yet 76:8.* When you give to *the person whom you would never give to except for the One you are giving for* — the person whose face you do not want to see, whose name you do not want to speak, whose existence you have organized your life around avoiding — *that* giving is the giving that has nothing left in it except *li-wajhi-llāh*. **And that giving is the entire test of whether the rest of your charity is real.**

I am not going to tell you to do anything heroic. I am going to ask you to do something very small, and to do it once.

The next time you find yourself with a small bit of *resource* — money, time, food, attention, a kind word, a prayer — pause for one beat before you decide who to give it to. And ask yourself: *who is the person in my life whom I would NEVER think of giving this to?* The person whose name produced the small clench in your chest. The asīr-figure of your specific 2026 life. *And then give the small thing to them*. Not in a grand way. Not in a way that restarts the whole relationship. *Just one small unilateral gift*, with the posture fixed before your hand moves: *innamā uʿṭīka li-wajhi-llāh — I give you this only for the face of Allah*. *Lā urīdu minka jazā'an wa-lā shukūrā — I want from you no recompense and no thanks*. *Innī akhāfu min rabbī yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīrā — I fear from my Lord a frowning, severe Day*.

And decide, honestly, which version of the second sentence the situation calls for. Sometimes the right thing is to let it stay unspoken, the way al-Muyassar reads it — *fī anfusihim*, held in the chest, the recipient never knowing. But sometimes — and this is the harder, braver option, and it is the one al-Ṭabarī puts in the believers' mouths — the right thing is to *say it to them*: **you owe me nothing for this.** Not as a performance of your own humility. As a **release**, so that the thing you handed over does not sit on their shoulders as a debt. With an estranged person especially, the unpayable debt is often the exact thing that keeps the estrangement locked. Cancelling it out loud can be the whole gift.

If you can do this once — once in your entire life, with one specific asīr, in one specific small act — *you will know what 76:8-10 was talking about*, in your own body, in the way no commentary can convey. The act will produce a particular interior sensation that is unmistakable. I have done this maybe three times in my life — small things, very small things — and each time, *the interior of the chest goes very still in a way I cannot describe except by saying it is the stillness of the food going through the recipient to the One it was actually for, with no obstruction*. The stillness is the proof that the motivation has finally arrived at the place 76:9 names.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes have been the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammar said: *the believers in 76:8-10 feed **the** food — definite, whole, with no partitive* min *and therefore no charitable margin — while still craving it and needing it (*ʿalā ḥubbihi*, which both al-Ṭabarī and al-Muyassar read as love for the food), to a list that includes the captive enemy as an ordinary middle item between the poor and the orphan, declaring that they want neither recompense nor thanks in return, and motivated by the fear of a Day named with a four-letter quadriliteral word for the most severe possible facial expression*.

The themes said: *what you are quietly hoping to get back is the actual content of your giving, and the work of becoming a 76:9 giver is the work of closing those channels of return one at a time until only the One remains — and the test of whether you have arrived is whether your charity can include the asīr-figure of your specific life, the one person you would never give to except for the One you are giving for*.

The portrait of charity in 76:8-10 is the portrait of **giving that has been stripped of every counterfeit motivation until only one direction remains**. The food is real. The cost is real. The Beloved is real. The fear of the Day is real. And the recipient walks away **owing nothing** — because the food went from the hand *through* the recipient to the One whose face was the actual destination, and a person who was never a creditor has no debt to collect.

This is what *ikhlāṣ* looks like in operational form. Not as a feeling. Not as a virtue you have either acquired or not. *As a claim you have stopped making on the person you helped.* And the practice of becoming this kind of giver is available to you today, with the next small act, with the next hope-of-return you notice and quietly let go.

*You do not have to start with the asīr.* You can start with anyone. Just notice what you are hoping for back. Just close one channel. Do it again tomorrow. Do it again the day after. **The 76:9 posture is what your giving eventually becomes when enough of those channels have been closed that it forgets how to point in any other direction.** And the 76:8 act — the food given to the captive enemy with no claim attached — is what naturally happens *to* the body of a person who has been doing this long enough, because by then the lesser motivations have been worn so thin that the asīr is just another item on the list, and the giving happens without strain.

### Questions to Carry

1. *Yuṭʿimūna-**ṭ**-ṭaʿāma* — *they feed **the** food*, definite and whole, with none of the partitive *min* that would have made it "some of their food." Where in your life have you been giving from the *margin* — from what you would not miss — and calling it generosity? What would change if you started giving from *the plate*, even once?

2. *ʿAlā ḥubbihi* — al-Ṭabarī glosses it *wa-shahwatihim lahu*, "and their craving for it"; al-Muyassar adds *wa-ḥājatihim ilayh*, "and their need of it." The cost is named in 76:8; the direction is named in 76:9 (*li-wajhi-llāh*). **Where in your charity is the cost real and the direction unclear? Or the direction real and the cost zero?** What would it look like for both to be present at once?

3. The asīr is the captive enemy on the list of recipients — al-Muyassar: *usira fi-l-ḥarbi mina-l-mushrikīna wa-ghayrihim*, "taken prisoner in war, from the polytheists and others." **Who is the asīr-figure of your specific life right now?** Name them silently. Not to write down. Just so you know who you are picturing. And then ask: what tiny thing could you do for them, today, *li-wajhi-llāh*, with no claim attached to it?

4. The commentators differ on whether 76:9 was *spoken to the recipients* (al-Ṭabarī: *yaqūlūna li-lladhīna yuṭʿimūnahum*) or *said within* (al-Muyassar: *fī anfusihim*) — and both readings land on the same renunciation: *lā nurīdu minkum jazā'an wa-lā shukūrā*. **What are you still quietly hoping to get back from the people you have helped?** Spend sixty seconds tonight on the most recent thing you gave, and name the return you were hoping for. Don't judge it. Just name it. The naming is the first move.

5. The Day in 76:10 is named as *qamṭarīr* — a four-letter quadriliteral for the most severely contracted facial expression in the Arabic vocabulary. The believers' giving in 76:8-9 is *because they fear that face*. Where in your spiritual life has the *dunya complacency* been the absence of an honest reckoning with that face? What would change if you let yourself feel, for sixty seconds, that the Day with the *qamṭarīr* face is real?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The believers in 76:8 feed *the* food — the food they were still craving and still needed — to the captive enemy as well as the poor and the orphan, and then release the recipient from every claim it could have created: *we feed you only for the face of Allah; we want from you no recompense and no thanks* — and the entire test of whether your charity has arrived at the place this ayah names is whether you have closed every channel of return except the One, so that the person you helped walks away owing you nothing at all.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

You did not come into this reflection needing more theology about charity. You came into it carrying a body that has been giving things to people for years, with quiet expectations attached to the giving that you have never quite let yourself look at. The work of the next twenty-four hours is just *the looking*. The next time you give anything to anyone for any reason, notice what you are hoping will come back. Notice the small returns you are fishing for. *Don't judge yourself for finding what you find.* Just notice. And then, *the next time*, close one of them — and, where it would lift a weight rather than add one, say the release out loud the way al-Ṭabarī has the believers say it: *you owe me nothing for this*. That single closed channel, repeated thousands of times across a lifetime, is what eventually produces the kind of giver Allah preserved in 76:8-10. And the day will come when enough has been closed that you find yourself, without strain, doing a small thing for the asīr of your specific life, and no part of you will need to be congratulated for it, because by then the only audience that mattered will already have been the only audience the entire time.

That is what 76:8-10 is for.
