# The Master Who Thanks the Servant

### Surah Al-Insan, Ayah 22

> إِنَّ هَٰذَا كَانَ لَكُمْ جَزَآءً وَكَانَ سَعْيُكُم مَّشْكُورًا
>
> *Inna hādhā kāna lakum jazā'an, wa kāna saʿyukum mashkūrā.*
>
> **"Indeed, this is for you a reward, and your striving has been thanked."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Voice That Finally Speaks

I want you to walk into this surah the way its first listeners did — slowly, and a little overwhelmed.

Surah Al-Insan begins with a question that empties you out. *"Has there not come upon the human a span of time when he was not a thing even mentioned?"* You did not exist. Not as a name, not as a whisper, not as an item on any list. Then Allah tells us how He made us — *min nuṭfatin amshāj* — from a drop, mingled, mixed. And then He says the thing that this entire surah is built around: *innā hadaynāhu as-sabīl, immā shākiran wa immā kafūrā* — "We guided him to the path; either grateful, or ungrateful."

Hold onto that word. **Grateful.** *Shākir.* The surah opens by saying: here is the whole human test, reduced to a single fork in the road. You will either be one who gives thanks, or one who covers it over. Everything else is commentary on that.

And then the surah does something remarkable. It stops arguing. It stops warning. And it simply begins to *describe.* For verse after verse — fifteen of them — Allah paints. Springs of water mixed with camphor. A fountain called Salsabīl. Cups of silver that gleam like glass, and glass that is somehow made of silver. Green garments of fine silk and rich brocade. Bracelets of silver. A drink, pure, *sharāban ṭahūrā.* Youths who never age moving among them like scattered pearls. It is the most sensuous, the most luxurious passage of Paradise in the entire Qur'an, and it pours out in near-total silence. No one in the scene speaks. You are just shown it. You are walked through the rooms.

[PAUSE]

And then — verse 22 — for the first time in the whole passage, **a Voice speaks.**

Someone addresses the people of the Garden directly. After all that silent splendor, words arrive. And here is the question I need you to carry, because it will pull us all the way through tonight:

When God Himself finally breaks the silence of Paradise to say one thing to the people who made it there — *what does He say?*

He does not say, "Look what I have given you." He does not say, "Was I not generous?" He does not say, "Now you see what your obedience earned." [PAUSE]

He says: **thank you.**

He thanks *them.* The Master thanks the servant. The One who owns the springs and the silk and the youths and the very breath those servants once used to worship Him — turns to them and *thanks them for their effort.*

Tonight we are going to try to understand how that is even possible. Stay with me.

---

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: كَانَ — The Reward That "Already Was"

Let me start with the smallest, quietest word in the verse. The one your eye slides right past. **كَانَ — *kāna.*** "Was."

It appears twice. *Hādhā **kāna** lakum jazā'an* — "this **was** for you a reward." *Wa **kāna** saʿyukum mashkūrā* — "and your striving **was** thanked."

Now, before we touch the verse, let me teach you the thing the verse is doing. In Arabic, *kāna* is the past-tense verb of being — "he was," "it was." That seems simple. But notice something strange about where it's sitting. These words are being spoken **in Paradise, to people who are already there, about a reward they are currently enjoying.** The silk is on them. The fountain is in front of them. The reward is *now.*

So why does Allah use the **past** tense? Why "this *was* a reward" and not "this *is* your reward"?

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

When you describe a present pleasure in the past tense, you are saying something very specific: *this was already settled before you got here.* This was not improvised. This was not a decision made at the gate. The past tense reaches backward, behind the moment of arrival, all the way to a decree that was written **before you ever struggled.**

Think about what that does to the believer's whole life in reverse. Every night you dragged yourself to prayer when sleep was sweeter. Every time you held your tongue when the cruel reply was right there. Every coin you gave when you wanted to keep it. In those moments it felt uncertain — *is any of this landing? Does any of it count?* And here, *kāna* answers from the far side of death: **it was always going to land. It was already written as yours. The "was" was true even while you doubted it.**

The classical commentators read this past tense as pointing to permanence and establishment — that this reward is a *fixed, settled* fact, not a fragile gift that could be withdrawn. Al-Jalālayn glosses it plainly: *kāna lakum jazā'an* — this established, this confirmed, is for you a reward. The *kāna* is the grammar of a promise that was kept.

And notice — Allah says it twice. *Kāna... wa kāna.* Two beats, like a seal pressed down and pressed again. The reward *was.* The thanks *was.* Both already true. Both already written. **You were running toward something that was already waiting with your name on it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: سَعْيُكُم — He Thanks the *Running*, Not the Arriving

Now look at *what,* exactly, is being honored. *Wa kāna **saʿyukum** mashkūrā* — "and your **saʿy** was thanked."

The word is **سَعْي — *saʿy.*** And I want to teach you this word carefully, because everything turns on it.

*Saʿy* does not mean "your deeds." It does not mean "your accomplishments." It does not mean "your results." The root *sīn-ʿayn-yā* means **to walk with effort, to move briskly, to run, to strive.** It is motion with exertion in it. It is the word the Qur'an uses for the *running* between Ṣafā and Marwa in the rites of pilgrimage — *the back-and-forth, the searching, the breathless effort of Hājar looking for water.* It is the word in Sūrat al-Najm: *wa an laysa lil-insāni illā mā **saʿā*** — "the human has nothing except what he strove for."

Feel even the *sound* of it. *Saʿy.* That guttural *ʿayn* in the middle — you have to push it up from your throat. The word for effort is, quite literally, **effortful to pronounce.** The mouth has to work to say it.

So here is what stops me. Allah does not say *"your achievements were thanked."* He does not say *"your perfect record was thanked."* He says **your striving** was thanked. **Your running. Your trying.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it changes everything about how you are being measured.

Think of the parent watching a small child take its first steps. The child stands, wobbles, falls. Stands, takes one step, falls. By any "results" standard, the child has *failed* — it did not cross the room. But what does the parent do? The parent erupts in joy. The parent claps. Because the parent was never measuring the *crossing.* The parent was watching the *trying.* The effort itself was the thing worth celebrating.

That is the word Allah chose. Not your outcome — your *saʿy.* Your wobbling, falling, standing-back-up *striving.*

And this is not a one-off. Hear its twin in Sūrat al-Isrā': *wa man arāda al-ākhirata wa **saʿā** lahā saʿyahā wa huwa mu'min, fa-ulā'ika kāna **saʿyuhum mashkūrā*** — "whoever wants the Hereafter, and **strives** for it the striving it deserves, while being a believer — *their striving is thanked.*" Almost the exact same phrase. Twice in the Qur'an, Allah pairs the same two words: *saʿy* and *mashkūr.* **The striving — and the thanks for it.**

Why does this matter for you, tonight, in a body that gets tired and a faith that wobbles? Because you have probably been grading yourself on the wrong exam. You have been measuring whether you *arrived* — whether your worship was perfect, whether your heart was fully present, whether you finally became the person you keep failing to become. And Allah is telling you: **I am not grading the arrival. I am thanking the run.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: مَّشْكُورًا — The Unnamed Thanker

And now we reach the word the whole verse has been walking us toward. The word the Voice in Paradise actually came to say. **مَّشْكُورًا — *mashkūrā.* "Thanked." "Appreciated."**

Let me teach you its shape first. *Mashkūr* is, in Arabic, what we call an *ism mafʿūl* — a **passive participle.** It does not name the one *doing* the action. It names the one *receiving* it. "Broken." "Written." "Loved." "Thanked." The doer is left off the page.

So the verse says *saʿyukum mashkūrā* — "your striving is **thanked**" — and grammatically, **it never names who is doing the thanking.** The Thanker is hidden behind the passive voice.

Why would Allah hide Himself here?

[PAUSE]

Let's go slowly, because this is the heart of it.

First, ask the obvious question that the passive voice forces: *thanked by whom?* The commentators are unanimous, and the surah leaves no doubt. Al-Qurṭubī states it directly: *mashkūrā* — "thanked, **from Allah Himself**." And then he does something tender. He asks the question we are all silently asking — *what could it possibly mean for the Creator to thank a creature?* — and he answers it: **Allah's thanking of the servant is His accepting the servant's obedience, His praising the servant, and His rewarding him.** Al-Baghawī reports from ʿAṭā': *"you gave thanks, so I will reward you with the most excellent reward."* Al-Qurṭubī also preserves Qatāda's reading — *"He forgave them their sins and thanked them for their good"* — and Mujāhid's — *mashkūrā,* meaning *"accepted."*

So pull it together: when Allah *accepts* your deed, He is said to *thank* it. And when He thanks it, He rewards it beyond all proportion — *for He is the Possessor of immense bounty.*

Now — *why the passive? Why hide the Thanker behind the grammar?*

Because of what it does to the *scale* of the moment. If the verse had said, "I thanked your striving," the focus would rest on the act of speaking. But by saying "your striving **was thanked**" — and leaving the Doer unspoken — Allah lets the *thanks itself* fill the whole frame. The striving is lifted up and held in the light, and the Hand holding it up is so obviously, so overwhelmingly *His* that it does not need to be named. It is the difference between a king announcing "I hereby honor you" — and a whole kingdom falling silent because everyone already knows whose honor it is.

[PAUSE]

But here is the thing I cannot get past. **Look at the direction this word runs.**

This is not a divine name we are used to. We are taught from childhood to thank Allah. *Al-ḥamdu lillāh.* We are the thankers; He is the Thanked. That is the natural order of the universe — the gift flows down from Him, and the gratitude flows up from us.

And in this verse, **the entire direction reverses.** The gratitude flows *down.* He thanks *us.*

Is this an accident of one verse? No. Allah named Himself with this. Among His names: **ash-Shakūr** — "the Ever-Thankful," "the One who multiplies His servant's small good into vast reward." *Wa-llāhu Shakūrun Ḥalīm.* *Innahu Ghafūrun Shakūr.* And in Sūrat al-Baqarah, right after describing the *saʿy* — the running — between Ṣafā and Marwa, Allah says: *wa man taṭawwaʿa khayran fa-inna-llāha **Shākirun** ʿalīm* — "whoever volunteers good, then indeed Allah is **Thankful,** Knowing." There it is again, in the very same breath as the *running.* The striving — and the God who thanks it.

[PAUSE]

So understand what kind of God you are dealing with.

He is not a master who tallies your debts. He is not an employer who pays the agreed wage and turns away. He is *ash-Shakūr* — the One who, when you give Him a drop, treats it as though you had given Him an ocean, and thanks you for the ocean. The One whose acceptance is so generous that the language can only call it *Him thanking you.*

And that — *that* — is what the Voice came into the silence of Paradise to say.

---

### Bridge

So three words have shown us three things. *Kāna* — that your reward was *already written,* settled before you doubted. *Saʿy* — that what Allah honors is your *striving,* not your arriving. And *mashkūr* — that the One who needs nothing turns to you and *thanks* you, reversing the whole direction of gratitude in the universe.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the ache at the center of being human.

---

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Unseen Labor and the God Who Sees It

Let me take you out of the Garden for a moment, and into an ordinary, unbearable human experience.

There is a particular kind of pain that has no dramatic name, but everyone here has felt it. It is the pain of **effort that no one saw.**

The mother who was up four times in the night, and in the morning no one knows. The worker who stayed late, fixed the thing quietly, prevented the disaster that — precisely because it was prevented — no one will ever notice or thank her for. The student who studied and still failed, and now carries the failure with none of the credit for the trying. The person who, for years, *did not* relapse, *did not* lash out, *did not* give up — and got no medal, because the world only counts what happens, never what was heroically *prevented.*

There is an ache wired into us to **be seen.** Not flattered. *Seen.* To have someone know what it cost. We can survive almost any amount of hardship if one person looks at us and says, *I know what you carried. I saw.* And much of human despair is simply the suspicion that no one ever did, and no one ever will.

[PAUSE]

Now bring that ache back to the verse.

Your worship is the most *unseen* labor of your life. By its very nature. The night you prayed when no one was awake to witness it — that was the point; you hid it. The anger you swallowed for the sake of Allah — no one could see the swallowing, only the silence. The charity the Qur'an praises most is the charity given *in secret,* so secret that "the left hand does not know what the right hand gave." Your entire spiritual life is, deliberately, a vast archive of effort that **no human being saw.**

And the world's grammar tells you that unseen effort is worth nothing. If it didn't get noticed, did it even happen?

[PAUSE]

Into that, Allah says: *saʿyukum mashkūrā.* **Your striving was seen. Every step of the run. And not only seen — *thanked.***

Do you understand the reversal? The very thing that made your worship feel like it disappeared — its hiddenness — is the thing that made it most precious to the One who was the only Witness. *Nothing was lost.* The night prayer no one knew about was logged. The swallowed anger was logged. The secret charity was logged. And on the Day when the accounts are opened, the Master of the worlds will not merely confirm them — He will turn to you and *thank you* for them.

There is a narration the scholars love to mention here. Al-Qurṭubī, in his commentary on this very verse, begins to relate a story of a man — and the tradition carries through the sources — of the dweller of Paradise who is so overwhelmed by what he has been given that he assumes he must already have done something to deserve being released from owing more. The point the commentators draw out is this: in Paradise, the soul finally grasps that the relationship was never a cold transaction. It was a Master who *delighted* in the servant's trying.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes *who you are performing for.* Tomorrow, when you do the small, invisible, unthanked good — the patience no one will praise, the prayer no one will see, the kindness that goes unreturned — you can stop waiting for the human eye that is never going to turn your way. You were never working for that eye. You were running, the whole time, toward a Witness who misses nothing, forgets nothing, and is preparing — even now — to say *thank you.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The God Who Grades the Trying

Now let me name the other wound this verse reaches, because it may be the more common one in our age.

We live inside a culture of **results.** You are your output. You are your metrics, your grades, your follower count, your salary, the visible deliverable. Effort is invisible and therefore worthless; only the *result* is real. We have trained an entire generation to feel like a failure the moment the *outcome* falls short — regardless of how hard they fought for it.

And we drag this poison straight into our relationship with Allah.

So the believer thinks: *My prayer was distracted again. My fast was hungry and irritable, not serene. I tried to read Qur'an and my mind wandered the whole time. I keep intending to change and I keep failing. What is any of this even worth? Look at the* result *— I am still the same flawed person. I arrived nowhere.*

[PAUSE]

Listen to me very carefully, because the verse is speaking directly into this.

Allah did not say He thanks your *results.* He said *saʿyukum mashkūrā* — He thanks your **striving.** The *running.* And we established what that word holds: it is the word for Hājar's desperate back-and-forth between two hills, searching for water for her child — running, finding nothing, running again, finding nothing — *seven times.* By a "results" standard, Hājar's running *failed* every single lap. She did not find water by running. The water — Zamzam — burst out somewhere else entirely, by the feet of her baby, by the mercy of Allah.

And yet Allah took her *saʿy,* her failed-by-results running, and **wrote it into the religion forever.** Every pilgrim who comes to His House until the end of time *re-runs Hājar's steps.* He did not immortalize her *success.* He immortalized her *trying.*

[PAUSE]

That is the God you worship. *He grades the trying.*

So here is the reframe, and I want you to actually take it home: **Your distracted prayer that you fought to finish is not a failed result. It is a successful *saʿy.*** Your hungry, cranky fast that you held onto anyway — that is *saʿy.* The Qur'an you read while your mind kept wandering and you kept dragging it back — every drag-back was a *step* in the run. You think you arrived nowhere. Allah says: *I wasn't measuring the arriving. I was watching you run, and I am going to thank you for it.*

Now hear the *justice* and the *grace* held together in this one verse, because this is the masterstroke. Allah uses **two** words for what you receive. *Jazā'an* — "a **reward.**" And *mashkūrā* — "**thanked.**"

*Jazā'* is the language of *justice.* A recompense. A wage. It means: **you earned this. This was owed to you fairly.** That alone would be staggering generosity. But Allah does not stop at justice. He adds *mashkūr* — *thanks* — which is the language of *grace,* of something **beyond** what is owed. Al-Qurṭubī catches it exactly: when Allah *accepts* the deed, He *thanks* it; and when He thanks it, He rewards it *beyond all proportion,* for He is *Dhū al-Faḍl al-ʿAẓīm* — the Possessor of immense bounty.

So the structure is: **justice is the floor. Thankfulness is the overflow.** He gives you what you earned — and then, on top of it, He *thanks you,* as though you had done Him a favor. As though the One who gave you the very limbs you worshipped with, the very breath you praised Him with, the very guidance that put you on the path at all — owed *you* gratitude.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It frees you to *try without the paralysis of perfectionism.* The lie that says "if I can't do it perfectly, why bother" dies here. Allah is not waiting at the finish line with a stopwatch, comparing your result to a standard you'll never hit. He is walking beside you on the path, counting your steps, and the steps *themselves* are what He treasures. So pray the imperfect prayer. Give the small charity. Take the wobbling step toward Him. **The trying is the worship.** And the One watching is already reaching for the words *thank you.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: The Ring of Gratitude

Now let me show you something about this surah that we could not have seen at the beginning, but can see now.

Go back to where Al-Insan started. Verse 3: *innā hadaynāhu as-sabīl, immā **shākiran** wa immā kafūrā* — "We guided him to the path; either **grateful**, or ungrateful." The whole human test, set up as a choice about *shukr.* Will you be a *shākir* — one who gives thanks?

And now look at where the Paradise scene ends. Verse 22: *wa kāna saʿyukum **mashkūrā*** — "your striving was **thanked.**"

Do you see it? **The surah opens with the human being asked to be the one who *gives* thanks — *shākir.* And it closes with the human being the one who *receives* thanks — *mashkūr.*** Same root. *Sh-k-r.* The beginning and the end of the journey, bound together by a single word turned inside out.

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest architecture of the verse. **The one who *chose* gratitude becomes the one who is *given* gratitude.** You spent your life as a *shākir* — thanking Him in the dark, thanking Him through hardship, thanking Him when no one saw. And at the end of the road, the verb comes back to land on you: you are now *mashkūr* — the *thanked one.* **You become what you gave.** The gratitude you poured upward your whole life returns, pouring back down, from the only Source whose thanks could ever be enough.

### Bringing It Together

This is why the linguistic discoveries and the human themes were the same insight all along.

The grammar said the Thanker is *hidden in the passive voice* — and the theme of unseen labor said your whole worship was *hidden from human eyes.* Same hiddenness. The hidden God thanks the hidden good.

The grammar said Allah honors the *saʿy,* the *striving* — and the theme of a results-culture said you keep grading yourself on the *arriving.* The God who grades the trying frees the one drowning in outcomes.

And the grammar said *kāna* — it *was,* already written — which means the thanks you will one day receive *was already true* while you were still running and still doubting whether any of it counted. **It always counted. It was already *mashkūr* before you ever reached the Garden.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose eye have I been working for — and what would change in my worship if I truly believed the only Witness who matters never once looked away?
2. Where in my life have I abandoned the *trying* because I was ashamed of the *result* — and what would I begin again, if I knew Allah grades the striving?
3. Am I a *shākir* — one who gives thanks even in the dark, even when unthanked? Because that is the one who is promised to become *mashkūr.*
4. What is the small, invisible, unglamorous good I could do tomorrow — the one no human will ever notice — knowing that the One who misses nothing is preparing to thank me for it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The One who owes you nothing, and to whom you owe everything, will look at your hidden, imperfect striving — and *thank you for it.*

### The Closing Invitation

So I will leave you with an invitation, not a command. Tonight, somewhere private, do one small act of worship that no one will ever know about. A prayer. A whispered remembrance. A secret charity. And as you do it, let go — *completely* — of needing anyone to see. Not your community, not your family, not your own ego keeping score. Let it be a step in the *saʿy,* a stride in the run toward Him. And carry this with you as you do it: somewhere beyond the veil, in a place already prepared, with your name already written on it, the Lord of the worlds is getting ready to say to you the words He came into the silence of Paradise to say —

*Thank you.*

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, *ash-Shakūr,* who multiplies the little we give and forgets none of it — make us among those who chose gratitude in the dark, that we may be among those whose striving You thank in the light. Accept the imperfect run, the wobbling steps, the worship no one saw, and let our *saʿy* be *mashkūr* with You. And when the Voice speaks in Paradise, let us be among those it speaks to.

*Āmīn.*
