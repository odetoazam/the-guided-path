# Whoever Wills: The Door That Is Both Yours and His

> **إِنَّ هَٰذِهِۦ تَذْكِرَةٌ ۖ فَمَن شَآءَ ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِۦ سَبِيلًا • وَمَا تَشَآءُونَ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا**
>
> *Inna hādhihi tadhkirah. Fa-man shā'a-ttakhadha ilā rabbihi sabīlā. Wa-mā tashā'ūna illā an yashā'a-llāh. Inna-llāha kāna ʿalīman ḥakīmā.*
>
> "Indeed, this is a reminder. So whoever wills may take a way to his Lord. And you do not will except that Allah wills. Indeed, Allah is ever All-Knowing, All-Wise."

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me set the scene before we open a single word.

Surah Al-Insan — the Surah of the Human Being — does not begin with a command. It begins with a question that feels almost tender:

*"Has there come upon the human a span of time in which he was nothing worth mentioning?"* (76:1)

Sit in that for a moment. Before you had a name, before anyone wept at your birth, before there was a "you" at all — there was a long stretch of time in which you simply were not. Not forgotten. Not lost. **Just nothing worth mentioning.**

And then the surah moves with extraordinary gentleness. Allah describes how He made the human from *nuṭfatin amshāj* — a drop, a mingling of fluids. How He gave him hearing and sight. And then comes the line that this entire surah is quietly built around:

*"Indeed, We guided him to the way — whether grateful or ungrateful."* (76:3)
**إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ السَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا**

We *showed* him the way. We *guided* him to it. Notice — Allah does not say "We forced him down the way." He shows it. He guides to it. And then He hands the human something heavy and trembling and luminous: **a choice.** Grateful, or ungrateful.

The surah then unfolds two worlds. One: chains, shackles, a blazing fire — for the *kafūr*, the deeply ungrateful. The other: a Paradise so specific it almost overwhelms you. Springs with names — *Kāfūr*, *Salsabīl*. Silver vessels that are themselves like crystal. Youths who do not age, moving among the righteous like scattered pearls. Garments of fine green silk. A drink that is *pure* — *ṭahūrā*.

And after pages of this — after the chains and the pearls, after the fire and the silk — the surah suddenly turns and looks straight at *you*, the one listening:

*"Indeed, this is a reminder."*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. I want you to feel the strangeness of what happens in these two short ayahs.

In one breath, Allah says: **whoever wills** — *fa-man shā'a* — let him take a way to his Lord. Your move. Your will. The door is unlocked; walk through it.

And in the very next breath, before you can even straighten your back with the pride of "I chose this," He says: **and you do not will except that Allah wills.**

Two sentences. They sit side by side. And on the surface they seem to *contradict* each other. One hands you the steering wheel. The other says the wheel was never only in your hands.

What is going on here? Why would Allah place these two statements skin to skin, with no buffer between them? Is your choice real, or isn't it?

Stay with me. Because the answer is not a compromise between the two. The answer is something far more beautiful — and far more freeing — than either one alone. And it is hidden inside the grammar of a single repeated verb.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tadhkira* — The Word That Says "You Already Know This"

Let me start with a small experiment, before we touch the Arabic.

Think of the difference between **learning** something and **remembering** something.

When you learn, something enters you from the outside. A new fact, a phone number, a stranger's face. It wasn't in you; now it is.

But remembering is different. When you remember, nothing new arrives. Something that was *already inside you* — buried, dusty, covered over — rises back to the surface. You don't say "oh, interesting" when you remember. You say **"oh — of course. I knew that."** There's a click of recognition. A homecoming.

Hold onto that distinction. Now look at the word Allah chose.

He does **not** say *inna hādhihi taʿlīm* — "this is a teaching," an instruction, new information. He says:

**إِنَّ هَٰذِهِۦ تَذْكِرَةٌ** — *inna hādhihi tadhkirah* — "Indeed, this is a **reminder**."

The word *tadhkira* comes from the root **dh-k-r** (ذ ك ر), which carries the meanings of *memory, mention, remembrance*. And the specific form it takes here is built on the second verbal pattern — *dhakkara* — which intensifies and directs the action: not merely *to remember*, but **to cause someone else to remember**. To jog. To stir what is already sleeping inside a person.

[PAUSE]

Let this land.

Allah did not say the Quran brings you knowledge you never possessed. He said it **stirs awake** something you already carry. The surah just spent its whole length describing your origin from a drop, your destiny in fire or in silk — and then it tells you what it has been doing the whole time: *reminding* you.

Reminding you of **what**?

Here is where the classical scholars open the door. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both gloss *tadhkira* as *mawʿiẓa* — an admonition, a piercing reminder that reaches the heart and not just the head. Al-Jalālayn calls it *ʿiẓa lil-khalq* — an admonition *for the creation*, for all of us.

But here is what the word itself insists on: you cannot *remind* someone of something they never knew. The very choice of *tadhkira* over *taʿlīm* is a quiet, staggering claim about you — **that the truth of your Lord is not foreign to you.** It is native. It was placed in you before you were a person worth mentioning. The Quran is not installing software. It is wiping the dust off a window that was always there.

And this is why the message can be so brief and still be enough. A teacher delivering wholly new material needs volumes. But a friend reminding you of what you already love needs only a word. *Inna hādhihi tadhkirah.* This is a reminder. That's all it has to be.

One clear statement, then, of what this single word reveals: **The Quran does not argue you into a stranger's God. It reminds you of your own Lord — the One your soul met before your memory began.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Ittakhadha* — To Take a Way *For Yourself*

Now watch what the willing human is invited to do.

*Fa-man shā'a* — "so whoever wills" — **ittakhadha** ilā rabbihi sabīlā. Most translations render it flatly: "let him take a way to his Lord." But the verb Allah selected is doing something the English flattens.

Let me teach the structure first. Arabic verbs grow on patterns, and each pattern bends the meaning in a particular direction. The plain verb here would be *akhadha* (أَخَذَ) — simply "to take," to grasp, to seize. But Allah does not use the plain form. He uses the **eighth pattern** — *ittakhadha* (ٱتَّخَذَ).

What does the eighth pattern characteristically do? Among its core functions, it folds the action **back toward the doer** — it carries the sense of doing something *for one's own self, to one's own benefit, as one's own possession*. The eighth form turns "to take" into "to take-up-for-yourself, to adopt, to make-your-own."

Think of the everyday difference between *picking up* a tool that's lying around and **adopting** it as *your* tool — the one you reach for, the one that becomes part of how you work. The first is incidental. The second is a claim of ownership, an act of self-investment.

That is the verb here.

[PAUSE]

So the ayah is not saying, vaguely, "let a path exist between you and God." It says: *fa-man shā'a* — whoever wills — let him **make the way to his Lord his own**. Let him adopt it. Let him take it up as a personal possession, something he reaches for, something that becomes part of who he is.

Notice what this does to the whole idea of guidance. Back in 76:3, Allah said *We* showed *him* the way — that was Allah's act. The path was laid down by the Lord. But here in 76:29, the *taking* of that path is framed as the human's own act of acquisition — *ittakhadha*, he takes it for himself.

So there are two motions, and they belong to two different agents. **Allah lays the road. You adopt it as yours.** The road is a gift; the walking is a claim. He provides the way; you make it your way.

And do you feel the dignity in that? Allah could have said "We will drag whoever We please down the road." Instead He builds the road, points to it, and then steps back and says: *now — if you want it — take it up as your own.* The eighth form preserves your ownership of your own journey. The way to God is not something done *to* you. It is something you *adopt*.

What this single verb reveals: **Salvation is offered as a gift but received as a possession. Allah builds the path; the believer claims it with his own hands.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Sabīlā* — Indefinite, Open, Unspecified

And now read the very last word of ayah 29. There is something hiding in plain sight.

**سَبِيلًا** — *sabīlā* — "a way."

Not *al-sabīl*, "**the** way." Not *sabīla rabbihi*, "the way **of** his Lord," fully specified and bounded. Allah leaves it **indefinite**: *sabīlan* — *a* way. An open word, a word with the article stripped off, a word left deliberately wide.

Let me teach why this matters. In Arabic, as in English, putting "the" in front of a noun closes it down to one known, defined thing — *the* door, *the* road. Leaving it indefinite — *a* door, *a* road — leaves it open, plural in possibility, unbounded. Grammarians call this *tankīr*, and it can be made to carry the meaning of *breadth, multiplicity, openness*.

So why, when describing the way to the Lord, does Allah leave it open rather than fix it to one narrow definition?

Here the classical commentators do something beautiful: instead of forcing one meaning, they offer a cluster — and they *tell us* the meanings converge. Al-Qurṭubī lays them side by side: *sabīl* is **a path leading to His obedience and the seeking of His pleasure**; or *sabīl* means **wasīla**, a *means*, a connecting cord to God; or it is **a road to the Garden**. And then he says, gently, *wa-l-maʿnā wāḥid* — **"and the meaning is one."** Al-Baghawī echoes it: *wasīla bi-l-ṭāʿa*, a means by way of obedience. Al-Muyassar widens it further: a way reached **through faith and God-consciousness**, leading to forgiveness and pleasure.

Do you see what the indefinite *sabīlan* has done? It has held the door open for *every* sincere approach. The one who comes through patient obedience. The one who comes through love. The one who comes through fear of the Fire. The one who comes through gratitude for the springs of *Salsabīl*. The scholar's way is *a* way. The broken-hearted's way is *a* way. The convert's way, the returner's way, the lifelong-struggler's way — each is *a* way to the Lord.

[PAUSE]

And notice one more thing — *to* **whom** the way leads. Not *ilā Allāh*, "to Allah" — the name of overwhelming majesty. But **ilā rabbihi** — "to **his Lord**." *Rabb* is the name of nearness: the Nurturer, the Sustainer, the One who raised you stage by stage from a drop. And it is *rabbihi* — **his** Lord, with the personal pronoun. *Your* Lord. The way is not a cold road to a distant deity. It is the path home to the One who has been raising you, intimately, your entire existence.

What this final word reveals: **The way to God is one in destination and many in entrance. However you come, if you come to your Lord, you are on a way.**

---

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have built between them.

*Tadhkira* — this is not new information; it is the dusting-off of what your soul already knows.

*Ittakhadha* — the way is offered as gift but taken as your own possession.

*Sabīlā* — and the door left open, indefinite, wide enough for every sincere approach to your own Lord.

Three words, and already a complete theology of the human journey: *you already know the way; the way is yours to claim; and the way is wider than your fear of being excluded.*

Now let us ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because the surah is about to do something that has unsettled hearts for fourteen centuries. It is about to take this whole language of *your* will — *whoever wills, let him take, let him claim* — and place it inside a larger will entirely.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Knot of the Two Wills — Are You Free or Not?

Let me begin where you actually live, not in a textbook.

You have had the experience — everyone has — of making a decision and feeling, with total certainty, *I am choosing this. This is me, deciding.* You chose to read this. You chose, this morning, to get up or to lie there a few minutes more. The sense of authorship is one of the most undeniable feelings a human being has.

And yet — you have *also* had the other experience. The experience of looking back at your life and realizing how little of it you actually arranged. You did not choose the country you were born in, the language that shaped your thoughts, the parents whose wounds you inherited, the decade you happened to live through, the chemistry of your own brain. The deepest currents of your life were running long before you "decided" anything.

So which is it? Are you the author of your life, or a leaf on a river?

Modern people feel this as genuine anguish. The neuroscientist says your choices are just neurons firing, determined by prior causes — free will is an illusion. The philosopher of freedom insists you are radically, terrifyingly free and fully responsible. And the ordinary person, caught in between, oscillates: *taking credit when things go well, and pleading "I couldn't help it" when they don't.*

Now read what Allah does. He does not pick a side in this debate. **He says both — in two sentences, touching.**

*"So whoever wills may take a way to his Lord."* — Your will is **real**. The verb *shā'a* is yours. The choice is genuinely placed in your hands. The whole surah hinges on it: *grateful or ungrateful*, you decide.

*"And you do not will except that Allah wills."* — And your will, real as it is, **exists inside** a greater will. You did not generate yourself. You do not even generate your own wanting from nothing. The very capacity that you exercise — the *power to will* — is itself given, sustained, and encompassed by the One who made you.

[PAUSE]

Here is the key that unlocks it, and it lives in the grammar we already uncovered. Look at the **verb**. It is the same root — *sh-y-'* — **in both ayahs**. *Fa-man **shā'a*** — whoever wills. *Wa-mā **tashā'ūna** illā an **yashā'a***-llāh — you do not will except that Allah wills. The same verb of willing, attributed first to the human, then to Allah, then bounded.

Allah did not use *two different words* to say "your fake willing" versus "My real willing." He used the **same word**. Your willing is real willing — the very same verb. **And** it is enclosed.

This is the position the mainstream of the scholars articulated, and al-Muyassar states it plainly: *wa-mā turīdūna amran min al-umūr illā bi-taqdīr Allāh wa-mashī'atih* — "you do not intend any matter except by Allah's decree and will." Your choice is not a fiction. But it is not a sovereign, self-originating power either. It is a **real capacity, given and held** by your Lord.

Al-Ṭabarī sharpens this on the very phrase in front of us. He reads *wa-mā tashā'ūna* not as some vague, general willing but as *the willing of the very thing ayah 29 just invited* — *ittikhādh al-sabīl ilā Rabbikum*, your taking of the way to your Lord. *That* willing, he says, you do not have *illā an yashā'a-llāh* — except that Allah wills it for you — *li-anna l-amra ilayhi lā ilaykum*: **"because the matter belongs to Him, not to you."** Not that your reaching for the road is unreal, but that the road, and the strength to reach for it, were never quarried from your own ground. And here the early tradition preserves a detail worth knowing: al-Ṭabarī records that in the reading of Ibn Masʿūd the verse runs *wa-mā tashā'ūna illā **mā** shā'a-llāh* — "you do not will except **what** Allah wills" — where the common reading has *illā **an** yashā'a-llāh*, "except **that** Allah wills." The two readings lean in slightly different directions — one toward the *content* of what you will, the other toward the very *occurrence* of your willing — and the commentators hold them together rather than choosing between them. Your willing is bounded both in *that* it happens at all and in *what* it can finally reach. Both leashes are held by the same loving Hand.

Let me give you the analogy that does the real work here.

Imagine a child learning to write, and a parent's hand gently closed around the child's hand, holding the pen. Now — *who is writing?* If you say only the child, you are blind to the strength and the steadiness flowing in from the larger hand. If you say only the parent, you erase the child, who is truly gripping the pen, truly forming the letters, truly *learning*. **The truth is that both are writing, and there is no contradiction in it at all.** The child's effort is completely real. And it is completely held.

[PAUSE]

That is *fa-man shā'a* inside *wa-mā tashā'ūna illā an yashā'a-llāh.* Your hand is truly on the pen. And the larger Hand has never let go.

And now feel what this *frees* you from. The terror of pure free will is the crushing weight of total self-authorship — every outcome on your shoulders alone, no help, no net. The despair of pure determinism is the deadness of "nothing I do matters; I'm just a machine running its program." The Quranic answer dissolves **both** terrors at once. Your striving genuinely matters — *take a way to your Lord, it's real, it counts.* And you are not carrying it alone — *your very willing is held by the One who is ʿAlīm, Ḥakīm,* All-Knowing of exactly what you're facing, All-Wise in exactly how He's holding you.

This is why ayah 30 closes the way it does: *inna-llāha kāna ʿalīman ḥakīmā.* The encompassing will is not a blind force or an indifferent fate. It is **Knowing** — it knows you completely. It is **Wise** — it places you, and tests you, and holds you with perfect purpose. The hand around your hand is not just strong. **It is loving and it knows what it is doing.**

Al-Ṭabarī ties these two names straight back to the verse's claim about willing. *Because* Allah is *ʿAlīm*, he writes, *fa-lan yaʿdū minkum aḥadun mā sabaqa lahu fī ʿilmihi bi-tadbīrikum* — **"not one of you will overstep what has already gone ahead of him in [Allah's] knowledge, in His ordering of you."** Sit with the weight of that. The will that encloses yours encloses it not as a cage but as a *knowing* — your every turning was already seen, already woven into a wise disposing, before you turned. Your willing does not surprise Him; it unfolds inside a knowledge that preceded it and a wisdom that is arranging it. And the surah refuses to leave that as cold abstraction: its very next breath turns the encompassing will toward rescue — *yudkhilu man yashā'u fī raḥmatih*, "He admits whom He wills into His mercy" (76:31). And al-Muyassar names who these are — *wa-hum al-mu'minūn*, "and they are the believers" — the very ones who, back in 76:29, *willed* and *took up* the way; mercy's door turns out to open precisely onto the road they claimed. Al-Ṭabarī unfolds the admission tenderly — He *turns the person back in repentance until he dies repentant of his straying, forgives him his sins, and enters him into His Garden.* The only ones left outside, in al-Ṭabarī's words, are *those who wronged themselves and died upon their shirk* — not the ones who stumbled and turned back, but the ones who never turned at all. So the two destinies the surah opened with — chains for the *kafūr*, silk for the grateful — close here as two ends of the same encompassing will: one reached through a willing that turned home, the other through a willing that refused to. The same will that holds your willing is the will that reaches in to pull you home.

What this changes about tomorrow: When you do good, thank Allah before you congratulate yourself — *He willed the willing.* And when you struggle to do good, do not collapse into "I just can't" — *the door said whoever wills, and the willing itself is a gift you can ask Him for.* This is the secret of *du'ā*: you are asking the Owner of the larger Hand to strengthen the grip of the smaller one. **You ask the Source of your will to will your willing toward Him.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: A Reminder for the One Who *Wants* — The Mercy in "Whoever Wills"

Now I want to return to two small words we have not yet pressed: *fa-man shā'a* — "so whoever wills" — and let them do something to your heart.

Begin again where you live. Think about how human beings issue commands to each other. A parent at the end of their patience: *"You will clean this room."* A boss: *"This will be done by Friday."* A command leaves no room. It seizes you. It does not ask whether you *want* to.

Allah is the Sovereign of the heavens and the earth. He has every right — *every* right — to command in exactly that seizing way. He has just spent the whole surah describing a Fire with chains and shackles for the ungrateful. He could now say: *"So submit, or else."* The force of everything before it would justify the threat.

But that is not what He says. He says:

**فَمَن شَآءَ** — *fa-man shā'a* — **"so whoever wills."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the restraint in that. Feel the *honoring* of you in that.

After the chains, after the fire, after the pearls and the silk — when the moment comes to call you to the way, the All-Powerful lowers His voice and makes it an **invitation**, not a seizure. *Whoever wills.* The door is open. No one is being dragged through it. No one is being shoved. You are being *invited*, as a being with dignity, to walk in on your own feet.

This is the same divine pattern you find elsewhere: *"So whoever wills, let him believe; and whoever wills, let him disbelieve"* (18:29). Not because Allah is indifferent to which you choose — the surah has made the stakes terrifyingly clear. But because **a love that is coerced is not love, and a return that is forced is not a return.** Allah wants you to come to Him *wanting* Him. He has made the wanting itself the threshold.

And now connect it back to the first word we studied — *tadhkira*, the reminder. Why does a reminder pair so perfectly with "whoever wills"? Because **you cannot force someone to remember.** You can force a body through a door. You cannot force a heart to recognize what it had forgotten. Recognition only happens from the inside, when the person *leans toward it.* So Allah reminds — and then He waits for your wanting. *This is a reminder; so whoever wills...* The reminder knocks. The wanting opens.

[PAUSE]

Here is the human door I want you to walk through. Many of us carry a quiet, corrosive belief: *that we are too far gone for the door to be open to us.* That the way to God is for the already-good, the lifelong-pious, the ones who never strayed. That somewhere a list was made and our name is not on it.

Read the words again. *Fa-man shā'a.* **Whoever wills.** There is no qualifier. No "whoever is already righteous." No "whoever has never fallen." No "whoever prayed every prayer of their life." Just — **whoever wills.** The only entry requirement at this door is *that you want to walk through it.* And the want, however faint, however buried under years — that want is enough to put your hand on the latch.

And remember what *sabīlan* taught us: the way is *indefinite*, wide, *a* way and not one narrow gate. So the invitation "whoever wills" opens onto a road broad enough to receive whatever you bring to it — your obedience, your tears, your fear, your gratitude, your late and stumbling return.

What this changes about tomorrow: Stop waiting until you are "good enough" to turn toward Allah. The ayah does not ask for your goodness. It asks for your *wanting*. So tonight, even if your record is a ruin, you can do the one thing the door requires: **want Him.** Whisper *yā Rabb, I want the way.* That whisper is *fa-man shā'a*. You have just put your hand on a door that the All-Powerful left open on purpose — and then, lest you think the wanting was ever yours alone, ask Him in the same breath to *make* you want Him more. That is ayah 30 turned into a prayer.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Circle That Closes

Now let me show you something the surah was doing the whole time — something you can only see now that we have done the work.

Go back to the beginning, to 76:3:

*"Indeed, We guided him to **the way** — whether grateful or ungrateful."*
**إِنَّا هَدَيْنَاهُ ٱلسَّبِيلَ**

There it is — *al-sabīl*, **the** way. At the **start** of the surah, Allah lays the road down. *We* did it. *We* guided. The road exists because He built it, and it is fully defined: *al-sabīl*, the way, with the article, the known road.

And now, at the **end**, in 76:29:

*"So whoever wills may take to his Lord **a way**."*
**ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِۦ سَبِيلًا**

*Sabīlan* — **a** way. Indefinite now. And the verb has flipped: at the start, *He* guided; at the end, *the human takes-for-himself*. **The same road — opened by God at the beginning, claimed by the human at the end.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see the circle? The surah opens with Allah's *hidāya* — His showing of the road. It closes with the human's *ittikhādh* — his adopting of the road. And the road is the same road, *sabīl* to *sabīl*. The whole surah is the space between God laying down the way and the human picking it up.

And then — only then, after the human reaches out to claim the road — comes the final, steadying word: *and you do not will except that Allah wills.* So that the human, at the very moment of claiming the road as **his**, is reminded that even his reaching was held in the Hand that built the road in the first place. The circle does not just close. **It closes back into the One it started with.**

### Bringing It Together

Now watch the two halves of our journey become one insight.

The **linguistic** discoveries told us: this is a *reminder* of what you already know; the way is yours to *take up* as your own; and it is left *open* for whoever comes.

The **thematic** depths told us: your will is *real and enclosed* in a wiser Will; and the door is an *invitation*, not a seizure — *whoever wills.*

These are the same truth seen from two directions. The way to God is genuinely **yours to take** — and the very wanting that takes it is genuinely **His to give.** Your hand is on the pen. The larger Hand has never let go. And the words on the page are real words, written by a real you, held in a love that is *ʿAlīm* and *Ḥakīm* — that knows you, and knows exactly what it is doing.

### Questions to Carry

1. When something good happens in my walk toward Allah, do I instinctively credit myself — or do I remember that even my willing was His gift first?
2. Where in my life am I waiting to be "good enough" before I turn toward Him, when the only thing the door asks is that I *want* to?
3. The Quran says it is *reminding* me of my Lord. What is it in me that already knows Him — and what has been gathering dust over it?
4. If my will is real but held, what would change if I started asking the Owner of the larger Hand to strengthen the grip of my smaller one?
5. The way was left *indefinite* — wide. Which entrance is mine right now: obedience, gratitude, fear, love, or a late return? And am I willing to simply *start*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The way to your Lord is real enough to be yours to claim, and held enough that even your wanting to claim it is His gift — so want Him, and ask Him to want you toward Himself.**

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in these two ayahs obligates you tonight. That is the whole point — *fa-man shā'a*, whoever wills. So this is not a command landing on you; it is a door standing open in front of you. What might shift is small and enormous at once: that you stop treating the way to Allah as a thing you must earn your way *up* to, and start treating it as a thing you simply *want* your way *into* — and that you let the wanting itself become your first prayer. The reminder has knocked. The willing is yours. And the willing, too, is a gift you are allowed to ask for.

### A Brief Du'a

*Yā Rabb — You who guided us to the way and then left the door open for whoever wills: we want the way. Make our wanting real, and then hold it; for You have told us we cannot even will it except that You will. O ʿAlīm who knows exactly where we stand, O Ḥakīm who knows exactly how to bring us home — will us toward You, and let us take, with our own hands, the way that leads to You.* Āmīn.
