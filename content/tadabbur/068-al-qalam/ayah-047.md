# Who Gave Them the Pen?
## On Ghayb, Certainty, and the Audacity of Self-Written Verdicts

---

### The Ayah

[ayah:68:47] أَمْ عِندَهُمُ ٱلْغَيْبُ فَهُمْ يَكْتُبُونَ

*Am ʿindahumu l-ghaybu fahum yaktubūn*

**Or is the unseen with them, so they write?**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before This Moment

Imagine Makkah in the early years of revelation. The Quraysh are not confused. They are not uncertain. They are not troubled by doubt.

They are *confident*.

These are the aristocrats of the Hijaz — the custodians of the Kaʿbah, the men who commanded the trade routes and held the social order in their hands. When this new message came — this voice saying that all of that counts for nothing before Allah, that the freed slave Bilāl and the wealthy merchant Walīd stand on equal ground in the divine accounting — they did not hesitate.

They declared.

With the ease of men whose certainty has never been tested, they made pronouncements about divine reality. *We are the ones who will be honored. We are the ones who are saved. If there is any reckoning, it will favor us. And this man ﷺ who calls himself a prophet? He is the one who is lost.*

They spoke about their standing before Allah as if they had a memo from heaven. As if someone had handed them an official document confirming their status. As if the divine decree was something they had personally inspected.

And Allah asks — with a quiet that is more devastating than any raised voice:

**Or is the unseen with them… so they write?**

[PAUSE]

Five words. And in those five words, the entire architecture of human arrogance is dismantled.

This is the ayah we are sitting with today. Not the longest. Not the most famous. But one of the most precise instruments in the Quran — a question that, once it finds its way into the chest, refuses to leave.

Because here is what I want you to feel before we go anywhere else: **this question is not only for the Quraysh of Makkah.**

It is for every human being who has ever felt certain — genuinely, comfortably certain — about where they stand with Allah.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَمْ — The Final Door in a Cascade of Closed Doors

Before we step into this ayah, let me teach you something about Arabic that will change the way you hear it.

The word *am* (أَمْ) appears in Arabic in two very different grammatical roles, and the difference between them changes everything about what a sentence is doing.

The first role — grammarians call it *am muttaṣila*, the "connected am" — appears in a sentence that already began as a question. "Is this a letter or a book?" The *am* connects two halves of a single question, asking you to choose between options. Ordinary. Functional.

But the second role — *am munqaṭiʿa*, the "disconnected am" — does something entirely different. It breaks from what came before. It introduces a new, independent challenge. The Arab grammarians say it carries the force of *bal* — "nay, rather" — combined with a question. It is not offering alternatives. It is saying: *Stop. Before we go further. Answer me this.*

The *am* that opens this ayah is the disconnected kind. It is a challenge, issued alone.

And here is what makes it extraordinary: **this is not the first such challenge in Surah Al-Qalam.** It is the last in a long and deliberate series.

Go back to verse 35. Allah opens this sequence with a question that leaves no room: *"Shall We make those who submit equal to those who are criminals?"* Then verse 36: *"What is wrong with you? How do you judge?"* And then the cascade begins:

Verse 37: **أَمْ لَكُمْ كِتَابٌ فِيهِ تَدْرُسُونَ** — *"Or do you have a Book in which you study — that in it is whatever you choose for yourselves?"*

Verse 39: **أَمْ لَكُمْ أَيْمَانٌ عَلَيْنَا بَالِغَةٌ** — *"Or do you have binding oaths upon Us?"*

Verse 41: **أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَاءُ** — *"Or do they have associates who will vouch for them?"*

Then — after a turn toward the Day of Judgment, and a reminder that Allah will deal with the deniers Himself — we arrive here.

Verse 47: **أَمْ عِندَهُمُ ٱلْغَيْبُ فَهُمْ يَكْتُبُونَ** — *"Or is the unseen with them, so they write?"*

[PAUSE]

Count what has been done. Each *am* is a door swung open to reveal nothing behind it. Each challenge exposes another empty claim.

Do you have a divine Book granting you special entitlement? *No.*
Do you have binding oaths from Allah? *No.*
Do you have partners in the divine realm? *No.*

And now, after all of that, Allah arrives at what is perhaps the deepest possible challenge: **Fine. You have no book, no oath, no partner. But — do you have the Ghayb itself? Do you possess the unseen? Because that is the only thing left that could possibly explain the confidence with which you speak about your own salvation.**

The *am* here is not merely rhetorical. It is **exhaustive**. It is the final door in a structure that has closed every other exit. And it ends in the same silence as all the others.

[PAUSE]

Hold that image for a moment — not the image of the Quraysh specifically, but the image of any person whose confidence about their standing before Allah is being walked through this sequence with patient, devastating calm.

What do you have? A book granting you special status? Binding promises from Allah? Someone who guarantees your safety? Or — do you have the Ghayb itself?

The question reaches every one of us.

---

### Element Two: ٱلْغَيْبُ — Not Some Hidden Thing, But THE Unseen

Now watch what a single definite article does to the weight of a word.

The ayah does not say *ʿindahum ghayb* — "is a hidden thing with them." It says *ʿindahumu **l-ghaybu*** — "is **the** unseen with them."

That *al* — that tiny prefix — changes everything.

The Arabic root is غ-ي-ب. And if you know anything about Arabic roots, you know that this root carries the image of something that has *set* — the way the sun sets below the horizon. It has disappeared from view. Gone beyond the threshold of perception. The *ghayb* is not merely what is currently unknown to us. It is **what is, by its very nature, beyond the reach of created perception.**

The classical lexicographers define it this way: the ghayb is everything hidden from the senses and from rational deduction — the realm that only Allah's knowledge can penetrate.

And the Quran is very precise about what belongs to it. In Surah Luqmān (31:34), Allah names five things that belong to the Ghayb exclusively — the knowledge of the Hour, the sending of rain, what is in the wombs, what a soul will earn tomorrow, where a soul will die. These are not things humans simply have not figured out yet. They are things that, by divine design, belong to Allah alone.

[PAUSE]

Now: when al-Jalālayn explain this ayah, they connect **الغيب** here specifically to **al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ** — the Preserved Tablet, the source of divine decrees. And Ibn ʿAbbas, as reported by al-Qurṭubī, understood it the same way: the ghayb being referenced is that divine record in which everything has been inscribed.

Sit with what that means. The Preserved Tablet is not merely a list of future events. It is the record of who you are before Allah. It is the record of how your account stands — what has been written for you, what has been decreed. It is, in a real sense, the divine verdict on every created being.

The question then becomes unbearably precise: **is the Preserved Tablet in their possession?**

Because that is exactly what they are claiming. Not in those words. Not explicitly. But when a human being says — with confidence — *I am saved, I am better before Allah than these believers, I will not be held accountable* — what they are claiming is access to the divine record. They are claiming to know what is written about them in the Ghayb.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah does not say: *"are they sure?"* Or *"do they think they know?"* He says: **is the Ghayb with them?** Do they *have* it? Do they *possess* it?

Because that is the only way such certainty would make sense. The only way a human being could speak with genuine, warranted confidence about their standing before Allah is if they had the divine record in their hands. If they could point to it. If they could read from it.

And the answer is silence.

[PAUSE — genuine pause]

### Element Three: يَكْتُبُونَ — They Write

Here is where the ayah's precision becomes almost stunning.

Allah could have said: *do they know the Ghayb?* Or: *do they perceive the Ghayb?* Or: *are they informed of the Ghayb?*

He says: **فَهُمْ يَكْتُبُونَ** — *so they write.*

The verb كَتَبَ in Arabic is not casual. It is the verb of **official inscription**. Of binding record. Of permanent documentation. Its sonic texture — the sharp *kāf*, the stopped *tāʾ*, the open *bāʾ* with the plural suffix flowing outward — carries something methodical, deliberate, authoritative.

In the Quran, Allah uses this same verb — *kataba* — when He *decrees*. **كَتَبَ اللَّهُ** — "Allah has written/decreed." What Allah writes is what is established, permanent, inviolable. The Preserved Tablet itself is called the *kitāb*, the written record. When the Quran says *kataba ʿalā nafsih al-raḥma* — "He has decreed upon Himself mercy" — that *kataba* is the most authoritative inscription possible.

And now Allah asks: **are they the ones doing the writing?**

[PAUSE]

Think about what writing requires. To write something, you must first *know* it. You must have access to it. You must have the *authority* to set it down as fact. When a court writes a verdict, it has the authority to make that verdict binding. When a scribe copies a decree, they do so under the authority of the sovereign — they are transcribing what someone with power has established.

What authority do these people have to write about their own standing before Allah? On whose behalf are they inscribing? From what source are they copying?

Al-Qurṭubī gathers the scholarly readings here: some said *yaktubūn* means they write from the Preserved Tablet what they contend with; others said it means they are *pronouncing judgment* — establishing as settled fact what they want to be true; a third reading is that they "write" in the sense of *prescribing for themselves what they desire*, as if composing their own divine verdict by hand.

All of these readings converge on one point: **they are claiming an authority they do not have.**

[PAUSE]

And then notice something extraordinary. The verb *yaktubūn* is left without an object.

In Arabic grammar, when a transitive verb — a verb that normally takes an object — appears without one, the absence is deliberate. You are left asking: they write… *what*?

Their own salvation? Their own superiority? Their own exemption from accountability? Their guaranteed position above the believers?

The unspecified object catches everyone. Because the question changes with the reader. The text does not fill in the blank — because every person who has ever privately composed a divine verdict for themselves must fill it in themselves.

*What have you been writing?*

The sentence hangs. And in that hanging, every reader must account for what they have inscribed.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### The Bridge

Let me name what the linguistic structure has revealed.

The particle *am* told us: this is the culminating challenge in a cascade that has closed every door — the final question after all the external props have been removed. The definite *al* in *al-ghayb* told us: what is being challenged is not some vague claim to general knowledge, but the claim to possess the divine record itself — the Preserved Tablet, the total unseen that belongs only to Allah. And *yaktubūn* — left without an object, hanging in the air — confronted every reader with a question that has no comfortable answer: what verdict have you been inscribing, without authorization, about your own standing before the One who actually knows?

Three elements. Three angles. One devastating reality.

**They are writing verdicts about the unseen — and they have no pen, no tablet, and no authority.**

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about something every one of us carries.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Epistemology of Spiritual Self-Certainty

Let me start somewhere you might recognize.

You are at a gathering, and someone says — casually, with the ease of complete certainty — *"I'm a good person. Allah knows my heart."*

Or perhaps it shows up differently. A successful person who takes their success as a sign of divine favor. A knowledgeable person who takes their learning as proof of proximity to Allah. A worshipper who takes the volume of their ʿibādah as a guarantee of acceptance. A community that takes its group membership as a kind of cosmic insurance.

And notice: we rarely hear these things as audacious claims. They feel natural. They feel *earned*. Because from inside the certainty, the certainty doesn't feel like arrogance. It feels like honesty.

[PAUSE]

This is not a Quraysh problem. It is a *human* problem. And it may be the most dangerous spiritual condition there is — precisely because it is the one least likely to recognize itself.

The psychologists have a name for a piece of this: the self-serving bias. We consistently evaluate ourselves more favorably than we would evaluate anyone else in the same situation. We explain our successes as evidence of our virtue, and our failures as accidents of circumstance. We see ourselves through a lens that is constitutionally tilted in our favor.

But the Quran takes this further than any psychological framework does.

It is not simply that we evaluate ourselves too generously. It is that we *speak with certainty about things we cannot know.* We make pronouncements about our standing before Allah — where we are in His sight, how our account stands, what our fate is — as if we have read the divine record. As if we have been briefed on our own verdict.

[PAUSE]

The Quraysh were doing this explicitly. When they insisted on their superiority to the believers, when they declared that they would not be punished — they were not expressing hopes. They were not sharing aspirations. They were making *declarations about unseen reality*. They were, in the precise language of this ayah, *writing*.

And Allah asks: on what basis?

Not: on what basis do you hope? Hope is permitted. Hope is good. But on what basis do you *know*? On what basis do you *write*?

[PAUSE]

Here is what makes this ayah remarkable: it does not challenge the Quraysh's theology first. It challenges their *epistemology* — their right to know. Before asking "is what you believe true?" it asks: "how could you possibly know it?"

And the same question reaches across fourteen centuries and finds us.

When you feel certain about where you stand before Allah — certain enough to stop worrying, certain enough to feel at ease, certain enough to quietly compare yourself favorably to another — what is the source of that certainty? The feeling is real. But what grounds it? What divine document have you read? What angelic briefing have you received?

[PAUSE — let this sit]

The classical scholars of *tazkiyah* wrote about a condition called *ghurur* — often translated as spiritual self-delusion or false security. Imām al-Ghazālī gave it extended attention: ghurur is when a person's self-assessment becomes untethered from any real accounting — when the *feeling* of goodness replaces the *work* of goodness, when the *assumption* of acceptance replaces the continuous labor of seeking it.

And the gateway to ghurur is precisely this: claiming to know what you cannot know. Writing verdicts about unseen reality as if you had the authority.

This ayah is the antidote. Not because it tells you that you are not good, or that you are condemned. But because it asks — with devastating gentleness — **how do you know?**

---

### Theme Two: The Ghayb as Protective Boundary

Now let me offer you something unexpected. Because there is another way to read this ayah — not only as a challenge, but as a mercy.

Stay with me.

The fact that the Ghayb is hidden — that human beings do not have access to the divine record, do not know where they stand, do not know what has been written for them — is not a punishment. It is a gift.

Think about what would happen if you knew.

If you knew with certainty that you were saved — that the verdict was final and favorable — what would that do to you? Some would remain the same, perhaps. But for most of us, the certainty of acceptance would loosen the grip of striving. Why continue seeking what you already have? Why remain in the posture of humility and return when the outcome is settled?

And if you knew with certainty that you were not — that the verdict was against you, that hope was closed — what would that do? Many would despair entirely. Why strive when striving changes nothing?

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars spoke of a pairing at the heart of the believer's relationship with Allah: **خَوْف وَرَجَاء** — fear and hope. Not one without the other. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah described the believer as a bird: hope is one wing, fear is the other, and love of Allah is the head. Cut either wing, and the bird cannot fly.

And what sustains this pairing — what makes it possible — is precisely the hiddenness of the divine verdict.

Because you do not know, you continue to hope. Because you do not know, you continue to fear. Because you do not know, you remain in motion — returning, seeking, striving, asking. **The uncertainty is the engine.**

[PAUSE]

This is why the ayah's question is not only a correction of arrogance. It is also a reminder of design. Allah fashioned reality this way: the Ghayb is His. The unseen is His. The divine record is in His hands, not ours.

And that design is merciful.

When the Quraysh tried to "write" their own verdicts, they were not only being arrogant — they were trying to short-circuit a divine structure built for their own benefit. The uncertainty they were trying to escape was the very thing that could have kept them in motion, kept the door open, kept the possibility of return alive.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar's reading of this ayah captures something of this: the challenge is not just *"do they have the ghayb?"* but — by implication — *"are they writing what they wish to be true?"* They are not scribes who discovered what the divine record says. They are forgers composing what they wish it said.

And we can do this too. Not in the explicit way of the Quraysh. Subtly. When we decide — without genuine accounting, without trembling — that we are fine. When we stop *muḥāsaba* — the honest examination of the soul — because we have already privately written the verdict. When the soul settles into comfort about its standing before Allah without any actual evidence from the only One who holds the evidence.

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ said — in a narration in both Bukhārī and Muslim — *"None of you enters paradise by his deeds."* The companions asked: *"Not even you, O Messenger of Allah?"* He said: *"Not even me — unless Allah envelops me in His mercy."*

The one who is most certain he is saved is not necessarily the most correct. The one who trembles most at the question of where they stand may be the closest to an honest answer.

Because the only appropriate response to the Ghayb — the only epistemically honest response to the fact that the divine record is beyond your reach — is the posture the Quran calls *rajāʾ* combined with *khawf*: leaning toward Allah with genuine hope, while knowing your account has not yet been settled.

[PAUSE — major pause]

What would it look like to stop writing your own verdict?

It would look like waking each morning with the question still genuinely open. Not the despair of uncertainty — but the *aliveness* of it. Knowing that the judgment is not yet in. That the door is not yet closed. That Allah's mercy is still moving and available and actively seeking you out.

It would look like *muḥāsaba* taken seriously rather than performed. Because if the verdict is already decided in your mind, accounting is theater. But if the verdict is genuinely open, the accounting matters.

It would look like a different relationship with others. You can no longer speak with confidence about who stands higher in divine sight — not the Quraysh's version of dismissing the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, and not the subtler versions we carry: the quiet sense that our religiosity is more genuine, our sincerity more pure, our standing more secure than others we observe.

The Ghayb dissolves all of that. Not into despair. Into humility. Into the freedom of not having to know.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now let us stand back and see the shape of what Allah has built across these verses.

From verse 35 to verse 47, there are cascading challenges, each beginning with *am*. And look at what they share: every single one is a question about *source*.

Where did you get this confidence? What is the source? Do you have a book? Oaths? Associates? The Ghayb?

Allah is not arguing with their conclusion first. He is asking them to show their work. **What is your source? What authority are you appealing to when you make declarations about divine reality?**

And the answer to every question is the same silence. There is no book. No oath. No partner. No access to the Ghayb.

This is not merely rhetorical technique. It is a profound epistemic structure. Allah is teaching the disbelievers — and through them, all of us — the most basic discipline of the heart: **you cannot make authoritative claims about what you do not know. And about your standing before Allah, you do not know what only He knows.**

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic elements and the themes have been pointing at the same reality from two directions.

The *am* of this ayah is the culminating challenge in a structure designed to show that the disbelievers had no foundation under them. The definite *al* in *al-ghayb* tells us that what they claimed access to was not some vague hidden knowledge, but the total, specific divine record — the Preserved Tablet itself. And the verb *yaktubūn* — left without an object, hanging in the air — confronts every reader with the question that will not go away: **what have you been writing? What verdict have you inscribed for yourself without divine authorization?**

The answer is meant to stop us. To return us to the posture of the one who genuinely does not know. To open the account back up. To put down the pen.

---

### Questions to Carry

These are not rhetorical. Sit with each one honestly.

1. In what areas of my relationship with Allah have I stopped genuinely accounting for myself — because I have privately decided the verdict is already favorable?

2. Where am I speaking about divine reality — my own standing, others' standing — with a certainty that has no source I can actually name?

3. What would change in my ʿibādah if I genuinely did not know where my account stood — if the verdict was truly, honestly still open?

4. Is my current relationship with hope and fear balanced? Am I leaning so far into hope that I have ceased to fear, or so far into fear that I have lost the capacity to hope?

5. What have I been writing for myself — what verdicts, what assurances — that only the One who holds the Ghayb has the right to write?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The ground beneath every claim of confident self-certainty before Allah collapses under a single question: where is the Ghayb, and who gave you access to it?**

---

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah does not leave us in despair. It leaves us in a more *honest* place.

Put down the pen. Not because you are condemned — but because the pen was never yours to hold. The account is open. The mercy is vast. The door has not been closed.

Allah — the One who actually holds the Ghayb, who actually knows what is written for you — describes Himself as *Ghaffār*, the One who covers and forgives abundantly, and *Tawwāb*, the One who turns toward you in return before you have even finished turning toward Him. Surah Al-Qalam itself, just verses after this challenge, will say: *"And indeed, for those who are God-fearing is a great reward."* The challenge and the promise are in the same breath.

The invitation is to live in the openness. To let the genuine uncertainty of your standing before Allah be not a source of dread, but a source of motion — of return, of striving, of hope that is not arrogance but trust.

Stop writing verdicts about unseen reality. And start seeking the One who holds it.

### Du'a

يَا مَنْ عِنْدَهُ مَفَاتِحُ الغَيْبِ — O You in whose hands are the keys of the unseen: we confess that we do not know what is written for us, and we ask You to write for us what is good.

O Allah, protect us from the ghurur that composes verdicts in Your name without Your authority — the quiet arrogance of those who have settled their account before You have settled it.

And keep us in the posture of the one who genuinely does not know — hoping in Your mercy, aware of Your justice, and moving always in Your direction.
