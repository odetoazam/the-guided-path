# Something Circled in the Dark

### Surah Al-Qalam, Ayat 19–20

> **فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَآئِفٌ مِّن رَّبِّكَ وَهُمْ نَآئِمُونَ ۝ فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَٱلصَّرِيمِ**

*Fa-ṭāfa ʿalayhā ṭāʾifun min rabbika wa hum nāʾimūn. Fa-aṣbaḥat ka-l-ṣarīm.*

**"So there circled upon it a visitation from your Lord while they were asleep. And by morning it was as though already reaped — black and barren."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Night Before the Harvest

Let me set the scene, because everything in these two ayat depends on the scene.

There is a garden. Not a small one. The narrations preserved in the commentaries describe it as a flourishing estate — date palms, vines, standing crops — somewhere in the lands of the south, inherited from a father. And here is the detail that matters most: **the father was a righteous man.** When the harvest came in, he would take what his family needed, and he would set aside a portion — deliberately, openly — for the poor. The destitute knew this garden. They came to it the way you come to a place that has never turned you away.

Then the father died, and the garden passed to his sons.

And the sons did the arithmetic that grief and greed do so quickly. They looked at the portion that went to the poor every year and they saw not a blessing but a leak. A loss. Money walking out the gate. So they made a plan. They swore an oath — the surah tells us they *swore* — that they would go out at the very first light of morning and harvest the entire garden. Early. Quietly. Before the poor could wake and gather at the edges. They would strip it clean and leave nothing to give.

[PAUSE]

That is the world you must stand inside before you read these two verses. Picture it. The night before the harvest. The plan is set. The oath is sworn. The brothers are confident — so confident that the surah notes they swore their oath **without exception**, without the small breath of humility that says *in sha Allah*, if God wills. They have decided. Tomorrow belongs to them.

And then night falls. And they go to sleep.

Now — here is the hook. Here is the thing I want you to hunger for as we go.

The Qur'an does not say what happened. Not directly. It does not say "and God destroyed it." It does not name a fire, a flood, a wind. It uses a strange, soft, almost gentle word — a word that, in another context, describes the most beloved act of worship a Muslim can perform. And it tells you *when* it happened in three words that should make the hair on your arms stand up.

**Something moved that night. It moved while they slept. And the word the Qur'an chooses for it is doing something extraordinary.**

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — طَآئِفٌ: The Thing That Circled

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small piece of Arabic, because the whole verse turns on it.

In Arabic, there is a root — **ط-و-ف (Ṭ-W-F)** — and its core meaning is *to go around something, to circle it, to encompass it by moving around its edges.* You already know a word from this root, even if you have never studied Arabic a day in your life. **Ṭawāf.** The circling of the Kaaba. When the pilgrim walks around the House of Allah, around and around, that is *ṭawāf* — from this exact root. To circle. To encompass. To go round and round a thing until you have surrounded it completely.

Hold that image. The pilgrim circling the House. Now let's open the ayah.

The word Allah uses for what came to the garden is **طَآئِفٌ — ṭāʾif.** From that same root, Ṭ-W-F. It is, grammatically, an *active participle* — the form that names the one *doing* the action. So *ṭāʾif* does not simply mean "a punishment." Literally, structurally, it means **"a circler." "One that goes around." "A thing that encompasses by moving around the edges."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment. The destroyer of the garden is not described as something that fell, or struck, or burst in. It is described as something that **circled.** That went around it. That encompassed it the way the pilgrim encompasses the Kaaba.

Notice something. The same motion — circling — is the motion of the most loving act of devotion and the motion of the most total destruction. The pilgrim circles in love. The *ṭāʾif* circles in decree. The shape of the movement is identical. What changes is whose command it carries.

Now go deeper. The classical grammarian **al-Farrāʾ** made a precise observation, recorded by al-Qurṭubī: *a ṭāʾif, by its very nature, only comes at night.* It is a night-visitor. The word itself carries darkness in it. You do not get a *ṭāʾif* at noon. It is the thing that goes around the house **when you cannot see it coming.** When the lamps are out. When the watch is asleep.

And here is where the grammar quietly tightens the rope. The word is **indefinite** — *ṭāʾif*, with the *tanwīn*, the "-un" ending: not *the* visitation, but **"a** visitation." "A certain something." Arabic uses this indefiniteness to create dread. It withholds the name. It is the difference between hearing "the dog is at the door" and hearing "*something* is at the door." The unnamed is always more terrifying than the named, because the mind cannot put walls around it.

And did the scholars name it? Listen to how they answer — and listen as a chorus, not a debate:

- **Ibn ʿAbbās** said the *ṭāʾif* was *a command from your Lord* — *amrun min rabbik.*
- **Qatāda** said it was *a punishment from your Lord* — *ʿadhābun min rabbik.*
- **Ibn Jurayj** described it vividly as *a neck of fire that came out from the valley of Hell* and went around the garden.
- And it was said it was *the angel Jibrīl* himself.

Do you see what each of these is? Not four competing theories fighting for the one correct answer. They are **four camera angles on a thing that came in the dark and could not be fully seen.** One saw the command behind it. One saw the punishment in it. One saw the fire of it. One saw the messenger carrying it. The Qur'an left it indefinite *on purpose* — and the scholars, faithful to that, circled the mystery the way the *ṭāʾif* circled the garden. They never pinned it to one face, because the verse never pinned it either.

**What this single word reveals:** the end did not come crashing. It came *circling* — quiet, encompassing, unnamed, in the dark, while the very motion of its arrival echoed the motion of worship. The decree of Allah does not always announce itself. Sometimes it walks softly around the edges of your certainty.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — مِّن رَّبِّكَ: From Your *Lord*

Now I want to show you a word that should stop you cold — not because it is rare, but because of *which* word it is, in *this* sentence.

The visitation came **مِّن رَّبِّكَ — min rabbika — "from your Lord."**

Let me teach the concept first. In the Qur'an, Allah is named in many ways, and the names are never interchangeable. They are chosen. The name **Allah** carries majesty, divinity, the totality of the Godhead. The name **al-Qahhār** carries overwhelming subjugation. The name **al-Muntaqim** carries retribution. If you were writing a sentence about a garden being annihilated by night, and you reached for a divine name, which would you reach for? The Subduer. The Avenger. The name of wrath.

Allah does not reach for any of those.

He says **Rabb.**

And what is *Rabb*? The root is **ر-ب-ب (R-B-B)**, and its meaning is not "Lord" in the cold, throne-room sense. *Rabb* is **the one who nurtures a thing from its first moment to its completion.** The one who owns, yes — but who owns the way a gardener owns a seedling: by tending it, watering it, raising it stage by stage from nothing into fullness. *Rabb* is the **Nurturer. The Cultivator. The Sustainer who grows things.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel the weight of the word landing exactly where it lands.

**Who grew this garden?** The Rabb. The Cultivator. Every leaf on those vines, every date on those palms, the water in the soil, the sun on the fruit — all of it was the work of the *Rabb*. He is the reason the garden existed at all. The brothers thought the garden was *theirs* to strip. But the One who raised it from seed was the Rabb.

And from whom did the visitation come, the thing that unmade it in a night?

**Min rabbika. From the very same Rabb.**

Do you see it? The hand that grew the garden is the hand that took it. The Cultivator who built it up is the Cultivator who brought it down. This is not the wrath of a distant tyrant. This is the *Rabb* doing what a *Rabb* does — and a gardener who plants is also a gardener who **prunes.** Who clears. Who uproots what has gone wrong in the soil. The destruction is not the opposite of nurture. In the logic of this one word, **the destruction is an act of the Nurturer.** That is far more sobering than wrath would have been. Wrath you can see as alien, external, "not really about me." But the discipline of the One who loves and raises you — that is intimate. That is *for* you.

And there is one more thread here, easy to miss. The word is *rabbi-**ka** —* "**your** Lord." Whose? The pronoun is *ka* — masculine singular — and in the flow of the surah it is addressed to **the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ.** Step back and notice what the verse is doing. It is telling the story of *those* people, but it turns mid-sentence to address *you*, O Muḥammad: this came from **your** Lord. The same Lord who is *with you*, who is consoling you in this very surah against those who called you mad — *that* Lord is the one who handles the arrogant. The destruction of their garden is, quietly, a word of comfort to the believer. **The Rabb who disciplines them is the Rabb who keeps you.**

**What this word reveals:** the ruin came not from a stranger but from the Source — from the very Nurturer who had given the garden in the first place, and who remains, in the same breath, *your* Lord, the one tending *you* even as He prunes them.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — وَهُمْ نَآئِمُونَ: While They Slept

Three words. *Wa hum nāʾimūn.* "While they were asleep." This is the most quietly devastating phrase in the verse, and I want to slow all the way down for it.

First, the grammar — because the grammar *is* the meaning here. This phrase is what Arabic calls a **ḥāl** — a *circumstantial clause.* Its job is to freeze the background, to tell you the *state of affairs* that surrounded the main event. The main event was the *ṭāʾif* circling. And the circumstance — the frozen backdrop against which it happened — was: *and they, asleep.*

Notice the shape of it. It is a **nominal sentence**: *hum* (they) + *nāʾimūn* (sleeping ones). Arabic nominal sentences describe *states* — settled, stable, ongoing conditions. This is not "they fell asleep" (an action). It is "they *were* — in the condition of — asleep." A deep, comfortable, untroubled stillness. The root is **ن-و-م (N-W-M)** — plain sleep. The sleep of people with a plan. The sleep of people who believe tomorrow is already theirs.

[PAUSE]

Now hold the two halves of the verse next to each other and feel the temperature difference between them.

On one side: **something is moving.** Circling. Encompassing. Awake. Active. Coming from the Lord of all the worlds.

On the other side: **they are still.** Horizontal. Eyes closed. Unaware. *Asleep.*

The decree was **awake** while they were **asleep.**

This is the whole human condition in a single image. We sleep on our certainties. We lie down at night inside our plans as if the plans were walls. And the verse says: while you sleep on what you are sure of, *something is moving that you did not account for.* Not necessarily punishment — for them it was, but the principle is larger. The future moves at night. The decree does not need your permission, your awareness, or your wakefulness to unfold. It does not wait for you to open your eyes.

And here is the cruelest tenderness of it: **their sleep was deepest at the moment they were most exposed.** They had never been in more danger than they were that night, and they had never felt safer. The feeling of safety and the fact of safety had come completely apart. Their confidence was at its peak precisely when their position was at its weakest.

This phrase — *wa hum nāʾimūn* — is not an accident of this one story. The Qur'an uses almost exactly this image elsewhere as a standing warning. In Surah al-Aʿrāf, Allah asks: *"Did the people of the towns feel secure that Our punishment would not come to them by night — bayātan — while they slept?"* (7:97) — *wa hum nāʾimūn,* the very same words. And then, even more terrifying, in the next verse: while they are at play in broad daylight (7:98). The Qur'an is teaching a single, repeated lesson: **the decree comes to the sleeping and to the playing alike — to all who have mistaken the calm for safety.**

**What this phrase reveals:** the most dangerous place a human being can stand is inside a certainty he is sleeping on. Awareness is not the same as safety, and the feeling of security is not the same as being secure.

---

### A Bridge

So look at what these three words have built. *Ṭāʾif* — a thing that circled, unnamed, in the dark. *Min rabbik* — from the very Nurturer who grew it. *Wa hum nāʾimūn* — while they slept on a future they thought they owned.

The construction has shown us something about *how* the end arrives: softly, from the source, in the dark, to the sleeping. **Now let's ask what this reveals not just about an ancient garden — but about reality itself, and about the night you and I will sleep tonight.**

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Future You Sleep On

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a calendar. On that calendar are things you have decided. A meeting next Tuesday. A trip next month. A plan for the harvest — your harvest, whatever it is: the deal closing, the degree finishing, the house, the wedding, the launch. And when you write those things down, something in you treats them as *done.* Not hoped-for. Not pending the will of God. **Owned.** You have spent the future before it arrived. You sleep on it.

This is not a flaw in those particular brothers. This is the default setting of the human heart. We are tense-confused creatures: we live in the present but we *bank* in the future, and we forget, every single day, that the future is the one thing we have never been given the keys to.

The brothers did one specific thing that the surah underlines. They swore their oath — *we will harvest at dawn* — and they did it, in the words of the surah, **without saying "in sha Allah."** Without exception. *Wa lā yastathnūn.* They left out the small phrase. The breath of humility. The four syllables that acknowledge: *this is my plan, but the future is not my property.*

And here is what I want you to see — that little phrase, *in sha Allah*, is not a verbal tic. It is not a politeness we tack on. **It is the entire correct relationship between a human being and time, compressed into two words.** It is the difference between sleeping on a future you imagine you own and sleeping in the hands of the One who actually holds it.

The Qur'an cares about this so much that elsewhere it corrected even the Prophet ﷺ on exactly this point: *"And never say of anything, 'Indeed I will do that tomorrow,' except [adding], 'If Allah wills.'"* (18:23–24). Tomorrow is not a possession. It is a loan that may not be renewed.

[PAUSE]

Now go one layer deeper, because there is a hard and beautiful point buried here that al-Qurṭubī does not let us walk past.

He notes — raising it as a point of legal and spiritual reflection — that in this story, **the brothers were destroyed for what they had not yet done.** They had not harvested the garden. They had not yet turned a single poor person away. They had only *resolved* to. They had only made the firm, settled intention — the *ʿazm* — to deprive the needy. And the visitation came before dawn, before the deed. Al-Qurṭubī draws from this that a settled, determined resolve toward wrong is itself something a person can be held to account for. (And we hold this with care — the scholars of *uṣūl* discussed at length the line between a passing thought, which is forgiven, and a hardened resolve, which is weighed; this is the deeper end of that discussion, not a simple ruling.)

But sit with the spiritual core of it, whatever the precise legal frame: **their hearts had already turned the corner before their hands did.** In the eyes of the Rabb who sees the heart, the garden was already lost the moment they decided to strip the poor of their share. The night-visitation was not the beginning of their fall. It was the *visible* arrival of a fall that had already happened, silently, in the dark of their intentions.

**What does this change about how you live tomorrow?** Tonight, before you sleep on your plans, do the thing the brothers refused to do. Take whatever you are most certain of — the harvest you have already counted — and place it, deliberately, in the hands of its Owner. Say *in sha Allah* and *mean* it: not as a hedge, but as a return of the keys. And look honestly at the resolves you are sleeping on. Not just your deeds — your *intentions.* Because the Rabb who saw a garden's fate in a sleeping man's plan sees yours too.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two — The Share You Cannot Cut Off

Here is something every human being quietly believes, no matter how generous they think they are: **that what comes through my hands is mine.** My income. My harvest. My yield. I *earned* it. I may *choose* to give some away — and if I do, I deserve a little applause for my kindness. The poor person at the edge of my garden is a recipient of my generosity, not a holder of a right.

This is exactly the belief the brothers acted on. Their father had given to the poor for years — and the sons looked at that giving and saw it as *their father's optional charity,* now ended with his death. A leak they could finally close. They never once imagined that the portion going to the poor was not a gift they were free to withhold, but **a right embedded in the harvest itself** — placed there by the Rabb who grew the harvest.

And so they planned to *cut*. To harvest early and cut the poor off. To sever the share.

Now watch what the Rabb does — and watch the words He does it with, because here the language delivers a verdict more precise than any sermon.

The surah tells us, just before our verses, that the brothers swore *"la-ya-**ṣrim**unnahā muṣbiḥīn"* — "they would surely **cut/harvest** it in the morning" (68:17). The root is **ص-ر-م (Ṣ-R-M)**: to cut, to sever, to reap by cutting. They were going to be the **ṣārimūn** — *the cutters.* That was their identity, their oath, their plan. To cut.

And now read what they woke to, in our verse 20:

[ayah:68:20] فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَٱلصَّرِيمِ

[PAUSE]

The garden became **ka-l-ṣarīm** — from the *same root they had sworn by.* They wanted to be the cutters; **the garden became the cut.** They reached for the knife of *ṣ-r-m;* the knife turned in their hands and *ṣ-r-m* fell on the garden instead. The very word of their ambition became the word of their ruin.

And the word *ṣarīm* itself carries a haunting double meaning that the lexicographers preserve. *Ṣarīm* means **"the reaped, the stripped-bare"** — a field cut down to black stubble, nothing standing. *And ṣarīm also means "the black night"* — the night so cut off from light that it is total darkness. So *ka-l-ṣarīm* says both at once: the green, living garden became **like a field reaped to nothing,** and **like the black of a starless night.** Color gone. Life gone. Light gone. They went out at dawn expecting a sea of ripe fruit and found a black, cut, barren ground — as if night had been harvested instead of dates.

Feel the precision of the justice. They tried to cut off the poor's share *quietly, in the morning.* So the cutting came *quietly, in the night,* and took the whole. They wanted to *deny a portion;* they were *denied the entirety.* The Rabb did not send them a lecture about generosity. He let the consequence speak in the exact vocabulary of their crime.

This is the deep law underneath the story: **the share of the poor is not yours to cut.** It is woven into the rizq before the rizq reaches you. When you withhold it, you are not saving a portion — you are severing the very thing that made the rest blessed. The brothers thought the poor's share was the *cost* of the harvest. It was actually the *protection* of the harvest. Remove it, and you have not kept more; you have exposed everything.

**What does this change about how you live tomorrow?** Look again at what passes through your hands and stop calling all of it yours. There is a *ṣarīm* in it — a share that was never yours to cut. The Qur'an elsewhere names it plainly: *"And in their wealth was a known right for the beggar and the deprived"* (51:19). Not a gift. A *right* — *ḥaqq maʿlūm.* Give it before you are tempted to harvest at dawn. Give it as the thing that *guards* your harvest, not the thing that diminishes it. Because the hand that closes the gate on the poor is the hand that, all unknowing, invites the night-visitor in.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Word Turns in Their Hands

Now that we have done the work, let me show you the structure of the whole thing — the proof that this was no accident of phrasing.

The parable is built on a single root that runs through it like a blade: **Ṣ-R-M, the cutting.**

- **68:17** — they swore *"la-ya-**ṣrim**unnahā"* — *we will surely **cut** it.* (Their oath.)
- **68:22** — they urged each other at dawn: *"go out to your field if you would be **ṣārimīn**"* — *if you would be the **cutters**.* (Their plan, in motion.)
- **68:20** — *"fa-aṣbaḥat ka-l-**ṣarīm**"* — *it became like **the cut**, the black-barren.* (Their judgment.)

The word they chose to describe their power over the poor became the word that described their powerlessness before the Rabb. **The reapers were reaped.** They sharpened a verb against the destitute, and the Rabb returned it to them — same root, same dawn — turned around.

And one more architectural thread, just as deliberate. They swore to do it *muṣbiḥīn* — "in the morning" (68:17). And our verse says *fa-**aṣbaḥat*** — "and in the morning it became..." (68:20), the same root **Ṣ-B-Ḥ**, the becoming-of-morning. (Note the grammar: *aṣbaḥat* is Form IV here in its sense of *entering upon the morning, becoming-by-dawn* — not a causative.) They had marked a morning on their calendar as the morning of their harvest. The Rabb kept the appointment — and made it the morning of their ruin. **The very dawn they had reserved for cutting others off was the dawn they found themselves cut.**

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same truth approached from two sides.

The **circling** *ṭāʾif* and the theme of **the future you sleep on** are one insight: the decree is *awake and moving* around the edges of your certainty while you lie still inside it. The **Rabb** who both grew and ungrew the garden, and the theme of **the share you cannot cut**, are one insight: everything in your hand is on loan from a Nurturer, including the portion that belongs to someone poorer than you — and the way you treat that loan is the way the loan treats you.

Put simply: **they slept on a future that was not theirs, holding a wealth that was not entirely theirs, and the Owner of both came in the night to collect what they had refused to acknowledge He owned.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is on my calendar right now that I have already counted as *done* — the harvest I am sleeping on? Have I returned its keys to the One who holds it, or am I still gripping them?
2. When I say "in sha Allah," am I returning the future to its Owner, or just clearing my throat?
3. Is there a *ṣarīm* in my wealth — a share that belongs to someone with less — that I have quietly redefined as *optional generosity* so I can keep it?
4. What have I already *resolved* in my heart that has not yet reached my hands? If the Rabb who sees intentions visited tonight, what would He find already decided in the dark?
5. Where in my life have I mistaken the *feeling* of safety for the *fact* of it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Something circled in the dark from the very Lord who had grown the garden, and the morning the brothers had reserved for cutting the poor off became the morning they themselves were cut — because the future they slept on so soundly, and the wealth they gripped so tightly, were never theirs to own.

### The Closing Invitation

You will sleep tonight. That is not in question. The only question this surah puts to you is *how* you will sleep — gripping a future you have declared your own, or resting in the hands of the One who actually holds it. Before you lie down, do the small, enormous thing the brothers refused: name your plan, then hand it back. *In sha Allah.* And before the next dawn you have circled on your calendar, give the share that was never yours to keep — not as a loss, but as the thing that guards everything else. Let the *ṭāʾif* find you already awake to who owns the garden.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Rabb — the Nurturer who grew every good thing I have ever held — I return tomorrow to You before I sleep on it; let me never say "I will" without "if You will." Make the share of the poor sacred in my hands, and let me give it before I am tempted to harvest in the dark. And let the dawn You bring me be a dawn of mercy, not the dawn of the cut. Āmīn.*
