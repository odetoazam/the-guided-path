# The Middle Voice: The Word They Withheld Until the Garden Was Ash

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to these two short verses. Say them slowly.

> [ayah:68:28] قَالَ أَوْسَطُهُمْ أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ
> [ayah:68:29] قَالُوا۟ سُبْحَـٰنَ رَبِّنَآ إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَـٰلِمِينَ

*Qāla awsaṭuhum alam aqul lakum lawlā tusabbiḥūn. Qālū subḥāna rabbinā innā kunnā ẓālimīn.*

*"The most balanced of them said: Did I not say to you — why do you not glorify Allah? They said: Glory be to our Lord; indeed, it was we who were the wrongdoers."*

Before we open a single word, let us stand where these people are standing. Because if we do not feel the ground under their feet, the verses will pass over us like a story about strangers.

### Rebuild the world

There was a man with a garden — an orchard heavy with fruit, the kind of land that in a desert is not a luxury but a lifeline. He died. And in the older, gentler way of things, that orchard had always paid a tax that no government collected: when the harvest came in, the poor came too. They were allowed to glean. The fallen fruit, the corners of the field, a share for the one with nothing — this was simply how a believing man held wealth. He held it with an open hand.

His sons inherited the trees. But they did not inherit the open hand.

They looked at the orchard and they did their arithmetic. They thought: *our father gave too much away. We have families now. We cannot afford his generosity.* And so they made a plan — a clever plan, a quiet plan. They swore to one another that they would go out **at dawn**, before the town was awake, and strip the trees bare in the dark, so that by the time the poor arrived there would be nothing left to give. [PAUSE]

Notice what kind of sin this is. It is not loud. There is no violence in it. It is the sin of the spreadsheet. It is the sin of the person who has decided, very reasonably, very calmly, that mercy is a cost he can no longer carry.

And the Qur'an records the exact words of their oath, a few verses earlier: *layaṣrimunnahā muṣbiḥīn* — "**We will absolutely, certainly harvest it in the morning.**" In Arabic that sentence is built on an oath and an emphatic ending — the grammar of a man who is *completely sure of tomorrow*. They spoke about the next sunrise as if the next sunrise were theirs to spend.

They did not say one word. They did not say *in shā' Allāh* — if Allah wills.

That night, while they slept on their certainty, a visitation passed over the garden from their Lord, and by morning it was as black and bare as a field already harvested and burned. *Ka'ṣ-ṣarīm.* Ash. [PAUSE]

They wake. They set out whispering their plan so the poor won't hear. They arrive. And they cannot even recognize their own land — it is so ruined they first say, "*we must have lost our way.*" Then the horror lands: no, this *is* the place. This is what is left.

**And it is exactly here, in the smoke, that our two verses begin.** One voice rises out of the group. And then the whole group says a single short sentence that, had they said it the day before, would have changed everything.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to hold as we go in. There is a word in this story that the brothers refused to say while they still had their garden — and the moment it was gone, it was the first word out of their mouths. The very same word. **What was it about that word that they could not afford while they were rich, and could not stop saying once they were ruined?**

And there is a second mystery sitting right beside it. Of all the things the Qur'an could call the one wise man among them, it calls him *the middle one.* Not the smartest. Not the oldest. **The middle one.** Why would the place where you stand in a group be the thing that lets you see what everyone else is missing?

Stay with me. Both of these are doors. Let's walk through them slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — أَوْسَطُهُمْ (*awsaṭuhum*): the man in the middle

Let's begin with a small experiment that has nothing to do with the Qur'an — yet.

Imagine a seesaw, the kind from a playground. Picture a heavy child slam down on one end and the other end fly up. Now picture where the *least* movement happens on that whole plank. It is not either end. The ends are violent — they crash down, they shoot up. The one point that barely moves, the still point from which the whole balance is measured, is **the middle.** The fulcrum.

Hold that image. We'll need it.

Now the word. Allah says **قَالَ أَوْسَطُهُمْ** — *qāla awsaṭuhum.* "The *awsaṭ* of them said."

The word *awsaṭ* comes from the root **و س ط** (w-s-ṭ), and its core, literal meaning is simply **the middle, the center.** *Wasaṭ* is the middle of a thing. The form here, *awsaṭ*, is the *afʿal* pattern — the same shape Arabic uses for "biggest" (*akbar*), "smallest" (*aṣghar*). It is the form of *the most*. So at the plain level this says: "the most-middle of them spoke."

But here is the first thing worth sitting with. In Arabic, **the middle of something is also its best part.** This is not a stretch the commentators invented; it lives in the root itself. The lexicographers note that *wasaṭ ash-shay'* — the middle of a thing — means *its choicest, its most protected, its finest.* Think about it physically: the middle of the necklace holds the central pearl. The heart is in the middle of the body. The yolk is in the middle of the egg. The thing you most want to protect, you put in the **center** and you build everything else around it. The middle is not the average. The middle is the treasure.

So when the commentators turn to this word, watch how they don't disagree so much as they each point a different camera at one reality:

- Al-Qurṭubī says *awsaṭuhum* means *amthaluhum wa aʿdaluhum wa aʿqaluhum* — **the most exemplary, the most just, the most intelligent** of them.
- Al-Baghawī says *aʿdaluhum wa aʿqaluhum wa afḍaluhum* — **the most just, the most intelligent, the most virtuous.**
- Al-Jalālayn says simply *khayruhum* — **the best of them.**
- And al-Muyassar renders it *aʿdaluhum* — **the most just, the most balanced.**

Notice they keep circling the same three jewels: **balanced, just, wise.** [PAUSE]

Now here is what is remarkable, and here is where our seesaw comes back. Look at what the *other* brothers were. They were the **extremes.** They had swung all the way to one end of the plank — total certainty, total grip, "we will *absolutely* harvest at dawn," no room for God, no room for the poor, no room for doubt. They were the end of the seesaw that slams down hard.

And the one who could still see clearly was — the Qur'an tells us in a single word — *the one in the middle.* The fulcrum. The point that didn't get swung to the extreme. [PAUSE]

Do you feel what the language is doing? In Arabic, the very word for "just" — *ʿadl* — means *equilibrium, balance, the state where two sides are equal.* Justice and the middle are the same idea. To be just is to refuse to be swung. And so the one man who refused to be swept all the way to greed — the one who stayed balanced — was, *by that very fact*, the one who could still see the truth. **His position is his perception.** He could see what they couldn't, because he was standing where the view is clear.

And this is the same word — the *same root* — that Allah uses for this entire community in another place: *wa kadhālika jaʿalnākum ummatan wasaṭan* — "and thus We made you a *middle* community" (2:143). A community of the fulcrum. A community that does not slam down to one extreme or shoot up to the other. The wise man in this garden is a tiny portrait of what the whole *ummah* is supposed to be.

So what does this one word reveal? **That the clearest sight in any group belongs not to the loudest or the cleverest, but to the one who has refused to be swung to the extreme.** The balanced one is the one who can still see. [PAUSE — major]

### Element Two — لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ (*lawlā tusabbiḥūn*): the grammar of "why on earth didn't you?"

Now to the heart of what this balanced man actually *said*. **أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ** — *alam aqul lakum lawlā tusabbiḥūn.*

Let me first teach you one small piece of Arabic, because it changes the entire emotional temperature of the sentence.

You know the word *lawlā* — many of us have met it as "if it were not for." *Lawlā* in *"were it not for the mercy of your Lord..."* But Arabic has a second, completely different use of this same word, and the grammarians have a name for it: **taḥḍīḍ** — *urging, exhortation, prodding.* When *lawlā* is followed by a **verb** (not a noun), it stops meaning "if not for" and starts meaning **"why don't you?! / why didn't you?! / come on — do it!"**

You already have this exact tone in English. Picture a parent who watches their child make a mess that was completely avoidable, and says: *"Why don't you ever listen?"* That sentence is not really a question. It is grief and exasperation wearing the costume of a question. It means: *you should have, and you knew you should have, and now look.*

That is precisely the force of *lawlā* here, followed by the verb *tusabbiḥūn*. Every commentator we have glosses it the same way: *lawlā = hallā* — "**why don't you?**" Al-Qurṭubī: *hallā*. Al-Baghawī: *hallā*. Al-Jalālayn: *hallā*. They are unanimous on the tone. This is not a calm theological question. This is a man standing in the ashes saying: *"Why — why wouldn't you just glorify Him?"*

And notice — he prefaces it with **أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ** — "**Did I not say to you?**" So this is not new advice. This is a reminder of *old, ignored* advice. There is a whole hidden history compressed into those three words. Al-Qurṭubī catches it: *"this proves that this middle one had commanded them to make the exception, and they did not obey him."* [PAUSE]

So before the garden burned, there was already a voice in the room saying *say the word.* And they had already said *no.*

Now — what is the word he kept urging? *tusabbiḥūn.*

### Element Three — تُسَبِّحُونَ → سُبْحَٰنَ: the verb they wouldn't do and the noun they couldn't stop

Here is where the language does something so precise it should make us sit up.

The root is **س ب ح** (s-b-ḥ), and *tasbīḥ* — its core meaning, as al-Naḥḥās notes inside Qurṭubī's commentary — is **tanzīh**: declaring Allah utterly *above*, *beyond*, *free of any deficiency.* To "glorify" in this sense is to say, in effect: *He is far above my little plans; nothing happens unless He wills it; I am not the author of tomorrow.*

The verb the wise man used — *tusabbiḥūn* — is what Arabic calls **Form II**, the *faʿʿala* pattern (*sabbaḥa*). This form takes a simple root and turns it into a *declared, uttered, actively-pronounced* act. *Tasbīḥ* is not a feeling you have privately. It is a thing you **say.** It is *making* the declaration with your tongue and your stance.

Now watch the commentators open up what this glorifying actually was — and watch them, again, point many cameras at one thing:

- Mujāhid (in Qurṭubī and Baghawī): their *tasbīḥ* was the **istithnā'** — saying *in shā' Allāh*, "if Allah wills." The missing exception clause on their oath.
- Abū Ṣāliḥ: their *tasbīḥ* was literally saying **subḥān Allāh** — "Glory be to Allah."
- Another reading in al-Baghawī: it means **giving thanks** — *tashkurūnahu ʿalā mā aʿṭākum*, thanking Him for what He gave you.
- And another: it means **seeking forgiveness** — *hallā tastaghfirūnahu*, why don't you ask His pardon?

These are not four contradictions. They are four faces of one act. Whether you say *in shā' Allāh*, or *subḥān Allāh*, or *alḥamdulillāh*, or *astaghfirullāh* — every one of them is the same single posture of the heart: **I am not in charge. He is.** Al-Baghawī names the thread exactly: the *istithnā'* was called *tasbīḥ* because it is *taʿẓīm* of Allah — *an acknowledgment that no one is able to do anything except by His will.* That is the reality under all four words.

Now hold your breath for the next verse, because the Qur'an does something here that no human author plotting a story would have the restraint to do.

The wise man begs them, in the past, to **say** *subḥān*. They refused. The garden burns. And in verse 29, the *very first words* out of their mouths are:

**قَالُوا سُبْحَٰنَ رَبِّنَآ** — *qālū subḥāna rabbinā.* "They said: **Subḥān** — Glory be — to our Lord." [PAUSE]

Do you see it? The word they would not say is now the only word they can say. He had urged the **verb** — *tusabbiḥūn,* "**do** the glorifying," while there was still a garden to protect. They wouldn't. And now they pour out the **noun** — *subḥān,* "glory," — over a field of ash.

And notice one more thing about that noun. *Subḥān* here is what the grammarians call an absolute verbal noun — it is *tasbīḥ* in its purest, most concentrated form, the act crystallized into a single word. The same root, stripped down to its essence. It is as if the language is saying: *the thing they were asked for, they finally produced — but in the form of a cry, not a choice.* [PAUSE]

So here is what these three linked words reveal, and it is almost unbearable: **The very breath that would have saved the garden is the breath they spent mourning it.** The word cost them nothing the night before. It cost them everything the morning after.

### The bridge

Look back at what the language has shown us. A man defined by his **balance** could see what the unbalanced could not. He kept urging a single small **utterance** — a five-letter recognition that they were not the owners of outcomes. They refused it as long as they felt powerful, and produced it instantly once they were broken.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about us. About the way the human heart actually works. Because you and I have a garden too, and we have a word we are refusing to say.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The middle voice, and why the room needs it

Let me start somewhere very modern.

In 1961, a Yale psychologist named Stanley Milgram ran an experiment that has haunted us ever since. Ordinary people were told by a calm man in a lab coat to deliver electric shocks to a stranger. Most of them did it — past the point of screaming, past the point of silence — simply because an authority told them to and everyone in the room was acting as though it were normal. Decades of research since then have a name for what was happening in those rooms: **groupthink.** The phenomenon where a group, moving together, generates a kind of gravity. The certainty becomes a current. And to stand against the current — to be the one person who says *"wait, this is wrong"* — turns out to be one of the hardest things a human being can do.

We have all been in that room. The group chat where everyone is piling on someone and you say nothing. The family where a quiet cruelty has become normal and no one names it. The workplace where a plan is clearly heading off a cliff and everyone can feel it but the meeting just... continues. The current is strong. It is so much easier to be an *end* of the seesaw, slammed down with the weight of everyone, than to be the still point in the middle.

Now go back to our garden. **The brothers were a group with a current.** Watch how the Qur'an describes them moving: they *swore together*, they set out *together*, they whispered their plan to each other on the road — *"let no poor man enter upon you today."* They were a unit, and the unit had a momentum, and the momentum was greed dressed up as good sense. Every one of them, alone, in his bed, might have hesitated. Together, they were unstoppable.

Except for one. *Awsaṭuhum.* The middle one. [PAUSE]

And here is the thing the Qur'an wants us to feel: **he failed to stop them.** Read his words again — *"Did I not say to you?"* He had spoken. He had warned. *And they had ignored him.* The balanced voice was *present* in that room the whole time, and it was not enough. The current was stronger than the conscience.

So let this land in two directions at once.

First: **be the middle voice anyway.** Even when it doesn't work. The man in the parable did not save the garden — but Allah immortalized *him.* When the smoke cleared, his was the only name with any dignity left, the only one the Qur'an honors with *awsaṭ.* Your job is not to guarantee the outcome. Your job is to be the fulcrum — to refuse to be swung — and to *say the word*, whether or not the room listens. The dissenting voice that is ignored is not a failed voice. It is a voice that has kept its own soul clean.

Second — and this is harder: **ask which one you are in the rooms you are actually in.** Because we like to imagine we are the middle one. Everyone reading this imagines they are the wise dissenter. But the brothers also surely thought they were reasonable men making a sensible financial decision. The current never *feels* like a current from the inside. It feels like *common sense.* It feels like *everyone agrees because it's obviously right.* The only protection — the only way to know whether you are the fulcrum or the slamming end of the seesaw — is to keep checking yourself against something outside the group. Against the *wasaṭ*. Against the revealed balance.

What does this change about tomorrow? Concretely: the next time you feel a group moving as one toward something, and a small voice in you hesitates — **treat that hesitation as sacred.** Do not let the current silence it. That small voice may be the only *awsaṭ* in the room. Say the word, gently, once. You will not always be heard. The man in the garden wasn't. But you will be the one whom Allah remembers as balanced. [PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two — The word we save for the ashes

Now the deeper wound. The one this whole parable is really about.

Let me ask you something. When was the last time you said *in shā' Allāh* and actually *meant* it — not as a verbal tic, not as a polite filler we sprinkle on sentences, but as a genuine, felt surrender: *I do not own this outcome; my Lord does?*

We say it constantly and feel it rarely. And there is a deep human reason for that, and the parable diagnoses it with surgical precision.

**We reach for God in proportion to how out of control we feel.** Think honestly about your own prayer life. When is it most desperate, most present, most real? The night before the diagnosis comes back. The hour the plane hits turbulence. The moment the phone rings at 3 a.m. *Then* we remember. *Then subḥān Allāh* pours out of us without effort, exactly as it poured out of those brothers over their ruined field. The Qur'an itself names this reflex elsewhere: when the wave towers over the ship, everyone on board cries out to Allah alone — and the moment they reach the shore, they forget.

The brothers had a garden. A *full* garden. And a full garden is the most dangerous thing a heart can hold, because **abundance whispers that you are the author.** Look at their oath again: *"we will absolutely harvest it at dawn."* No *in shā' Allāh.* Why would they add it? The fruit was on the trees. The plan was sound. The morning was, as far as they could see, already theirs. The missing word wasn't an accident of speech. It was an accurate readout of their hearts: *they genuinely believed tomorrow belonged to them.*

This is exactly the disease Allah corrects elsewhere with a direct command to the Prophet ﷺ himself: *"And never say of anything, 'I will surely do that tomorrow,' except [adding], 'if Allah wills'"* (18:23-24). The *istithnā'*, the exception clause — *in shā' Allāh* — is not a magic phrase that bribes God into cooperating. It is a *confession of reality.* It is the tongue catching up to the truth that the heart keeps forgetting: **you make intentions; He makes outcomes.** You hold the seed; He holds the harvest.

And now feel the full weight of the timing in our verse. The word *subḥān* is **the same word** at both ends of the catastrophe. Before: *"why don't you glorify"* — refused. After: *"Glory be to our Lord"* — gushing. [PAUSE]

The tragedy is not that they never said it. **The tragedy is that they only said it once it was free to say** — once there was nothing left to surrender. Surrender is only worth something while you still have something in your hands. *In shā' Allāh* over a garden you might lose is faith. *Subḥān Allāh* over a garden already lost is just grief. The brothers gave Allah the recognition He deserved — but they waited until it cost them nothing, because by then they had nothing. [PAUSE]

But — and here is where the parable turns from despair toward mercy — look at what they say *next*: **إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ** — *"indeed, it was we who were the wrongdoers."*

This is enormous. Notice they do not say "*we have been wronged*" — they do not blame the night, or fate, or bad luck, or even Allah for being harsh. The most natural human reaction to losing everything is to feel *victimized.* They do the opposite. They turn the finger around and point it at themselves: *we* were the *ẓālimūn,* the ones who put things in the wrong place, who wronged the poor and wronged their own souls. Al-Muyassar captures it beautifully: this is them *"returning to their right minds."* This is the sound of repentance — real repentance, the kind that takes full ownership.

So the parable holds two truths in tension, and we must not collapse them into one. **The garden does not come back.** Some consequences are real and they stay. *And yet* — the confession is real too, and Allah preserves it forever in His Book, honoring it, holding it up. Their *late subḥān* did not regrow the orchard. But it may have saved something far more valuable than an orchard.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical heart of it. **Move the word to the front of the sentence.** Say *in shā' Allāh* — and mean it — while your garden is still standing. When the deal looks certain, when the plan is sound, when the fruit is on the trees and tomorrow looks like it belongs to you — *that* is the exact moment to whisper *subḥāna rabbī, this is not mine, it was never mine.* Do not save your surrender for the ashes. Surrender while you still have a harvest, and the harvest itself becomes worship. [PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the shape of these two verses together, because the structure is itself the lesson.

Verse 28 is **one voice** speaking to **the many**: *qāla awsaṭuhum* — the single balanced man, urging them toward *subḥān.*

Verse 29 is **the many** finally answering as **one**: *qālū subḥāna rabbinā* — the whole group, at last, saying the word.

The story is a journey of a single word — *subḥān* — traveling from **one mouth that was ignored** to **every mouth, too late.** The wise man said it first, alone, and was refused. The crowd said it last, together, and the garden was already gone. The entire parable is built around the agonizing gap between *when the word was offered* and *when the word was finally received.* [PAUSE]

### Bringing it together

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the lived themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The grammar told us *awsaṭ* — the middle — is where balance and sight live. The theme told us the one balanced voice in any room is the one that can still see when the current is dragging everyone else under.

The grammar told us *tasbīḥ* is a *declaration*, an active uttering of *He is above my plans, I am not the author* — and that the word they refused as a **verb** they produced as a **cry**. The theme told us we save our surrender for the ashes, when the whole art of faith is to surrender while the garden still stands.

One word. *Subḥān.* It is the fulcrum of the whole story — and the fulcrum, as we learned, is the middle, the *awsaṭ,* the place of balance. **The balanced soul is simply the soul that says *subḥān Allāh* while it still has something to lose.**

### Questions to carry

1. In the rooms I move through — my family, my friends, my work — am I honestly the *awsaṭ*, the balanced one who can still see? Or have I been quietly swept to an end of the seesaw and mistaken the current for my own good sense?

2. When a small voice in me hesitates against what the group is doing, do I treat that hesitation as sacred — or do I let the room talk me out of it?

3. Where, right now, is my "full garden" — the area of my life where things are going so well that I have stopped saying *in shā' Allāh* and started speaking about tomorrow as if I own it?

4. Do I reach for *subḥān Allāh* mostly in turbulence — and forget it on the shore? What would it mean to glorify Him from a place of abundance instead of only from a place of fear?

5. If something I love were taken tonight, would my first words be *"I have been wronged"* — or *"indeed, it was I who wronged"?*

### One-sentence distillation

The one balanced voice had begged them to glorify their Lord while the garden still stood, and they only said the word over its ashes — so that we might learn to surrender our tomorrows to Allah *before* we lose them, not after.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to wait for your own garden to burn. That is the entire mercy of being given this parable: Allah lets you watch *their* loss so that you do not have to live *your own.* The word that they could not afford while they were rich, and could not stop saying once they were ruined — that word is available to you tonight, for free, while everything you love is still in your hands. Move it to the front of the sentence. Say it, and mean it, before the dawn. Let your *subḥān* be a choice, not a cry.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, You are far above our plans and our certainties — subḥānak, we are not the authors of a single tomorrow. Make us of the balanced ones who can still see when the current is strong, and let the words "if You will" and "Glory be to You" rise from our hearts while our gardens still stand — not only when they have turned to ash. And when we wrong ourselves, give us the grace of those who turned the finger inward and said: indeed, it was we who were the wrongdoers.* Āmīn.
