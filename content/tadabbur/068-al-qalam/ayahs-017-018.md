# The Two Words They Forgot

## Introduction: A Garden, a Sunrise, and a Sentence Left Unfinished

Let me give you the words first, and then let me take them away from you again — because the whole secret of these two verses lives in something that is *missing*, and you will only feel its absence if you first feel its weight.

> **إِنَّا بَلَوْنَٰهُمْ كَمَا بَلَوْنَآ أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا۟ لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ • وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ**

> *Innā balawnāhum kamā balawnā aṣḥāba-l-jannati idh aqsamū la-yaṣrimunnahā muṣbiḥīn • wa lā yastathnūn.*

> **"Indeed, We have tried them just as We tried the owners of the garden — when they swore that they would surely harvest its fruit in the early morning. And they made no exception."**

That last line — *and they made no exception* — sounds almost like a footnote. A grammatical aside. You could read past it in half a second. By the end of our time together, I want it to land on you like the closing of a door.

**Let me rebuild the world this came down into.**

Surah Al-Qalam is one of the earliest revelations. The Prophet ﷺ is standing in Mecca, and the city's most powerful men are circling him with one accusation above all others: *majnūn*. He's mad. His reason is veiled. Something has taken hold of his mind. The surah's second verse is Allah's own answer, sworn upon the pen and what it writes: *"You are not, by the grace of your Lord, one whose mind is veiled."* Hold onto that word — *veiled* — because we are going to find it hiding inside this garden.

Now, who are the men hurling that accusation? Not beggars. Not the desperate. They are the *prosperous* of Mecca. Men of trade, of caravans, of standing. Men for whom life has gone *well*. And it is precisely their comfort that has made them deaf. They look at this Prophet — poor, surrounded by slaves and the weak — and they cannot imagine that God would speak through *him* rather than through *them*. Their wealth has become their proof of their own righteousness.

So Allah does something to them. He tells us, in this verse, *"Indeed, We have tried them."* The classical commentators — al-Qurtubī, al-Baghawī, the author of al-Jalālayn — are agreed on what the trial was: **famine and hunger.** The years of drought that struck Mecca. The very prosperity they trusted began to dry up in their hands.

And then Allah holds up a mirror. He says: *you are being tried exactly the way another group was tried once — the owners of a garden.* [PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Why does Allah, speaking to hard, arrogant, prosperous men, choose to reach for a story about a *garden*? Not a battle. Not a punished nation. A garden, a sunrise, and a quiet family decision made at dawn. Why does He freeze the whole tragedy on the image of men who *almost* finished a sentence and didn't?

By the end, I want you to see that these two verses are a trap that has already closed — and that the same trap is open, right now, in the way most of us speak about tomorrow.

Let's begin where the listener of that time would have begun: with a single, devastating verb.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Balā* — The Word for a Test That Wears You Down

Before we touch the verse, let me put a word in your hand that you already know in your body, even if you've never named it.

Think of a coat you have owned for ten years. A good coat. When it was new, you couldn't see what it was really made of — the stitching, the lining, the strength of the seams. But ten winters later, you know that coat completely. The places that frayed. The seam that held. The button that was always loose. *Time wore it down, and in wearing it down, it revealed what the coat truly was.*

That image — a garment worn down until its true nature shows — is the literal, root meaning of the Arabic word Allah uses here. The verb is **بَلَا** (*balā*), root *b-l-w*. In the classical lexicons, the primary sense of this root is **to wear out, to wear down, to make old and threadbare** — exactly what time does to a garment. From that physical image, the Arabs derived its second meaning: **to test, to try.**

Sit with why those two meanings live in one word. [PAUSE]

A test, in the Quranic vision, is not a quiz. It is not information God needs — He already knows. **A test is the process by which what is *inside* you is worn down to the surface where it can be seen.** Pressure applied until the lining shows. The famine didn't *create* the arrogance of Mecca's elite — it *revealed* it. The hardship didn't *make* them ungrateful — it wore away the comfortable surface and exposed the ingratitude that was always underneath.

Now look at how Allah opens the verse:

> **إِنَّا بَلَوْنَٰهُمْ** — *"Indeed, We have tried them."*

Notice the very first thing your ear should catch: **the object is hidden.** *Them.* Who is *them*?

This is the first of our absences. Arabic could have named them. The verse could have said "We have tried the people of Mecca," "We have tried the deniers." Instead it withholds the name and leaves a bare pronoun hanging in the air — *hum*, them. The commentators fill it in for us: al-Qurtubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Jalālayn all say it refers to **the people of Mecca.** But the verse itself doesn't say it. [PAUSE]

Why leave the door open like that?

Here's what that open door does. Because the name is missing, the verse becomes a *frame* with no fixed picture in it. *Them* is whoever is being tested. *Them* was Mecca. And *them*, the moment you read it with an honest heart, becomes **you.** The grammar leaves a seat empty at the table, and every reader who has ever been handed a blessing and then watched it strain quietly takes that seat.

And then comes the hinge of the whole verse — one small word:

> **كَمَا** — *kamā*, "**just as**."

This is a comparison particle. *We tried them — just as We tried the owners of the garden.* And a comparison is a claim. Allah is not telling two separate stories. He is laying one over the other like two transparencies until the lines match up. **He is saying to the proud men of Mecca: your story has already been told. You are a re-run. Sit down and watch yourselves.**

What does this single verb and its hidden object reveal? That the test is not the famine and not the lost garden. **The test is the wearing-down that shows God what was always inside — and shows you, too, if you are brave enough to look.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Aqsamū* and *Muṣbiḥīn* — An Oath at Dawn, and the Time They Chose to Hide

Now we move inside the parable, to the moment Allah freezes for us. He doesn't show us the garden in bloom. He doesn't show us the harvest. He shows us a *decision*, made at a specific hour, and he chooses two words to capture it that, placed side by side, contain the entire moral failure.

The first word:

> **أَقْسَمُوا۟** — *aqsamū*, "**they swore an oath**."

Root *q-s-m*, in **Form IV**. Let me be precise here, because it matters: Form IV in Arabic is often called "causative," but that is a lazy default and it is wrong here. The function of *aqsama* is **denominative** — it is built from the noun *qasam*, an oath, and it means simply *to make an oath, to swear*. These men didn't merely *decide* to harvest their fruit. They **swore** to it. They bound themselves to tomorrow with the weight of an oath.

Teach yourself the everyday version of this first. There is a difference between saying "I'll probably go to the gym tomorrow" and saying "*I swear* I'm going to the gym tomorrow at 5 a.m." The oath does something. It hardens intention into certainty. It plants a flag in a day that has not yet arrived and says: *this day is mine. I have it. I command it.*

That is the posture Allah is photographing. Not a casual plan — a *sworn* plan. They took an oath over the unborn morning. [PAUSE]

Now hold that, and look at the second word, the one that tells us *when* they swore to do it:

> **مُصْبِحِينَ** — *muṣbiḥīn*, "**in the early morning / as they entered the dawn**."

Root *ṣ-b-ḥ*, the root of *ṣubḥ*, the dawn. This is an active participle from **Form IV**, *aṣbaḥa* — and here Form IV carries its "entering-a-time" sense: *aṣbaḥa* means *to enter into the morning, to become a morning-person in that instant*. It functions in the verse as a *ḥāl*, a circumstantial description: they swore to harvest *while being in the dawn*, at first light.

Now — why dawn? The verse doesn't explain. This is another absence, and the commentators step in to fill it, and what they say is chilling in its quietness.

Al-Jalālayn: they chose the early morning *so that the poor would not notice them* — **كي لا يشعر بهم المساكين** — so the needy would not feel their presence, and so they would not have to give them anything. Al-Muyassar says the same: they swore to harvest early *so that no needy person would eat from it*. The fuller story, which al-Baghawī narrates on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās, is this: the garden had belonged to a righteous man in Yemen who, at every harvest, gave the poor their share. He died. His sons inherited the garden — and the wealth — and they resented the old custom of charity. So they made a plan: harvest *at dawn*, quietly, before the poor of the town could gather, and keep it all. [PAUSE]

Do you feel what the word *muṣbiḥīn* is doing now?

The dawn is, in the Quran, almost always a word of *opening*. Of light breaking. Of the world becoming visible. The Prophet ﷺ loved the early morning; barakah was sought in it. It is the most *exposed*, most *honest* hour — the hour when things are seen.

And these men chose **the hour of light as their cover for darkness.** They reached for the most open time of day precisely *because* they wanted to be hidden in it — hidden from the eyes of the poor. [PAUSE]

There is the irony Allah has built into a single participle. You can choose the dawn to hide your stinginess from human eyes. But the One who *split* the dawn — *fāliq al-iṣbāḥ* — was awake before you, and the very light you used as a curtain was His. They thought they were being shrewd about *timing*. They forgot Who owns time.

What do these two words reveal, placed together? **An oath sworn upon the morning — by men who thought they could schedule their cleverness into an hour God could not see.** The grammar of certainty (*they swore*) collides with the grammar of concealment (*at dawn*), and in that collision is the whole disease: confident control of a tomorrow they did not own, in the service of withholding from those God had sent to their gate.

### Element Three: *Wa Lā Yastathnūn* — The Tense That Will Not Stay in the Past, and the Sentence They Never Finished

Now we arrive at it. The footnote that is not a footnote. Verse eighteen, two words:

> [ayah:68:18] وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ

I need you to notice two things about this little phrase, and the first is something you can hear without knowing any Arabic at all.

**Listen to the tense of the verbs as the passage moves.**

*Balawnā* — We tried. Past.
*Aqsamū* — they swore. Past.
*La-yaṣrimunnahā* — they will surely harvest. (A vow about the future, but spoken in the past moment.)

Everything is sealed in the past. It happened. It's over. The oath was sworn, the morning came, the story is told.

And then — *yastathnūn*. **Present tense. Continuous.** Not "they did not make an exception." The grammar literally reads: **"and they keep on not making an exception."** [PAUSE]

Why does Allah break the tense here? Why slide, for two words only, out of the sealed past and into the open, ongoing present?

Because this is not a thing they did *once*. This is who they *are*. The past tense would have made it an event — a single forgetting. The present tense makes it a *condition*. A standing posture of the heart. The arrogance about tomorrow isn't a moment in their story; it is the *air they breathe*, the continuous, habitual reflex of a soul that has stopped reckoning with God. The grammar freezes them mid-breath, forever in the act of forgetting. [PAUSE]

Now the second thing — the meaning of the verb itself.

**يَسْتَثْنُونَ** — *yastathnūn*, from the root *th-n-y*, in **Form X**. The noun from this is *istithnāʾ*, which in Arabic grammar literally means **"to make an exception,"** to set something aside from a general statement. (You will recognize the everyday sense: to *except*, to *exclude a case*.)

So what is the "exception" they failed to make? Here every commentator we have speaks with one voice. Al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubī, al-Baghawī — all of them say the same thing. *Wa lā yastathnūn* means:

> **They did not say, "in shā' Allah" — "if Allah wills."**

That is the missing exception. That is the sentence they never finished.

Stop and feel the architecture of this. [PAUSE]

They built an entire, confident sentence: *"We swear we will harvest this fruit at dawn."* A complete, sealed, sworn statement about tomorrow. And the *istithnāʾ* — the exception — is the clause that would have re-opened the sealed sentence to God: *"...if Allah wills."* Two words in Arabic. *In shā' Allah.* The clause that takes your grip off of tomorrow and places it back in the hands that actually hold it.

**They left it off.** And the verse does not even tell us they "forgot" it warmly, or "neglected" it. It uses the continuous tense to tell us they were the *kind of people* who never say it — to whom it would not even occur. Their certainty about the morning had no seam in it for God to enter.

And here is the absence that crowns all the others. Go back through everything we've read. The hidden *them*. The unnamed reason for the dawn. And now, the climax: **the deepest meaning of these two verses is located not in a word that is present, but in a word that is absent.** The entire tragedy of the garden is *the two words that were not said.* [PAUSE]

This is the most precise reading of "absence" the Quran offers anywhere. Allah does not punish them, in this verse, for what they said. He marks them by what they *left out*. The missing *in shā' Allah* is the open window through which everything that follows — the fire, the ash, the ruined garden of verses 19 onward — comes pouring in.

What does this final element reveal? **That a single sealed sentence about tomorrow, with no room left in it for God, is itself the sin — before a single fruit is even harvested.** They were ruined the night they spoke, not the morning they woke.

### The Bridge

Look back at what these three words have built together.

*Balā* taught us that a test is a wearing-down that reveals — and that the famine of Mecca, like the loss of the garden, was never about the loss. It was about the showing.

*Aqsamū* and *muṣbiḥīn* taught us that these men met their test with an oath of confident control, scheduled into the one hour they thought God could not see.

And *yastathnūn* — in its restless present tense and its missing two words — taught us that the wound was already open before dawn ever broke: a sentence about tomorrow with no seam left for God.

But there is one more thing the *letters themselves* are doing, and I held it back until now because you needed all three pieces first.

Remember how the surah opened — Allah defending His Prophet against the charge of being **majnūn**, a man whose *mind is veiled*. The root of *majnūn* is *j-n-n*: **to conceal, to cover, to veil.** It is the root of the *jinn* (hidden beings), of the *janīn* (the fetus, hidden in the womb), of *junnah* (a shield that covers).

And it is the root of the word at the center of this verse: **al-jannah.** The garden. *A garden is called a jannah because its foliage conceals the ground beneath it.* [PAUSE]

Do you see the thread Allah has woven through His own surah? The deniers accused the Prophet of having a *veiled* mind — *majnūn*. And Allah answers them with a parable about men whose *garden* — *jannah*, from the very same root, the very same idea of *concealment* — became the instrument of their ruin, because they used it to try to *conceal* their stinginess from the poor at dawn. **The accusers said the believer's mind was veiled. Allah shows that it was the deniers' hearts that were veiled** — covered over, *majnūn* in the truest sense — by a green and beautiful garden.

The one they called veiled saw clearly. The ones who could see their garden perfectly were blind. And the same three letters — *j-n-n* — hold both the slander and its answer.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Test You Never See Coming — Because It Arrives Wrapped as a Gift

Let me start where you live.

Picture the thing you are praying for right now. The promotion. The marriage. The house. The child. The visa. The recovery. Picture it granted. Picture yourself, six months after it arrives, having received exactly what you begged God for.

Now I'll ask you the question almost no one asks while they're making the duʿāʾ: **what if the thing you're asking for is the test, and not the reward?**

We carry, most of us, a quiet and unexamined theology: **hardship is the test, and blessing is the prize.** We brace for the test when the diagnosis comes, when the money runs out, when the relationship breaks. And when the good thing arrives — we *exhale*. We think the testing is over. We think we *passed*, and this is the trophy.

These two verses dismantle that completely. [PAUSE]

Look again at the comparison Allah draws. The people of Mecca were tested with *famine* — that part fits our theology, hardship as test. But the group they're compared to, the *aṣḥāb al-jannah*, were not tested with a famine. **They were tested with a garden.** A lush, inherited, fruit-heavy garden. Their test was *the blessing itself.* The wealth that fell into their hands the day their father died was the exam paper. And they failed it before sunrise.

Al-Qurtubī states this with beautiful directness. He says of the people of Mecca: *Allah gave them wealth so that they would be grateful, not so that they would grow insolent* — **ليشكروا لا ليبطروا** — *and when they grew insolent and turned against Muhammad ﷺ, He tested them.* The blessing was the test. The wealth *was* the wearing-down garment of *balā* we spoke of. It was given to *reveal* them. [PAUSE]

Here is why this is so easy to miss, and so dangerous. A hardship *announces itself* as a test. It hurts; you instinctively turn to God; you make duʿāʾ; you brace your soul. The hardship comes with its own alarm bell. **But a blessing arrives silently.** It comes wrapped in joy and relief and the congratulations of everyone around you. Nothing in it rings the bell that says: *careful — this is an exam, and the clock is running.*

So we relax. And in relaxing, we fail. The garden owners didn't lie awake plotting evil. They simply *received* a good thing and, in the comfort of receiving it, their grip on God loosened so quietly they didn't feel it go. By dawn they were swearing oaths over a tomorrow they didn't own and scheming to starve the poor — and I promise you, in their own minds, they were just being *sensible* about the family business.

That is how the test inside a blessing works. It doesn't make you a villain overnight. It makes you *comfortable*, and comfort slowly rewrites what you think you're entitled to. [PAUSE]

Stay with the modern shape of this. The Quran says elsewhere, *"If only the people of the towns had believed and been mindful, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth"* (7:96) — and warns of the town that *"was secure and content, its provision coming to it in plenty from every place, and then it denied the favors of Allah, so Allah made it taste the garment of hunger and fear"* (16:112). Notice the word in that last verse: *libās* — a **garment** of hunger. The same image. Hardship and ease are both *garments* you wear, and both wear you down to reveal what you are.

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow? [PAUSE]

**One specific practice: the night you receive a blessing, treat it like the night before an exam.** When the good news comes — the offer, the yes, the recovery — instead of only celebrating, ask yourself the garden owners' question in reverse: *Whose share is in this? Who did Allah send to my gate along with this gift?* Every blessing arrives with a poor person attached to it — a right, a *ḥaqq*, owed to someone. The garden owners failed the instant they decided the harvest was *theirs alone*. The fastest way to pass the test hidden inside a gift is to find, immediately, the one whose portion is in it — and give it before dawn, in the light, where it can be seen.

### Theme Two: The Sentence With No Room for God — Why We've Never Been More Certain About Tomorrow, or More Afraid

Now let me take you to the missing two words. *In shā' Allah.* And let me first say what it has become, so we can recover what it is.

For many of us, *"in shā' Allah"* has decayed into a soft no. *"Will you come to the event?" "In shā' Allah."* (Meaning: probably not.) It has become a verbal shrug, a way of being non-committal while sounding pious. We've sanded off its edges until it means almost nothing.

These two verses hand it back to us with its full, terrifying weight. [PAUSE]

Look at what *istithnāʾ* — the exception — actually *does* inside a sentence. The garden owners said: *"We will surely harvest at dawn."* A closed loop. A sealed claim of ownership over the next twelve hours. To add *"in shā' Allah"* is not to add politeness. It is to **break the seal.** It is to take a sentence that says *I control tomorrow* and re-open it to say *tomorrow belongs to Someone, and that Someone is not me.* It is a structural admission, built right into your grammar, that you do not own the next breath.

Now consider the most extraordinary fact about these verses. Allah does not, in verse 18, punish them for harvesting the fruit. He doesn't even punish them, here, for wanting to cheat the poor. He marks them — He *freezes the camera on them* — at the precise instant they sealed a sentence about tomorrow with no room for God in it. **The leaving-out of "in shā' Allah" is treated as the rupture itself.** Not the consequence of the sin. The sin. [PAUSE]

This is so important I want to slow all the way down. Allah is teaching us that there is a sin that happens *entirely inside your speech about the future* — before you lift a finger. It is the sin of *certainty without surrender*. The garden was destroyed that night (as the verses that follow describe), but the rupture with God happened *the moment they spoke as though they owned the morning.* They were spiritually ruined at sunset, not at sunrise.

And there is a direct cross-reference Allah Himself seems to gesture toward. In Surah al-Kahf (18:23–24), Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ directly:

> *"And never say of anything, 'Indeed, I will do that tomorrow,' except [adding], 'if Allah wills.'"* — **إِلَّا أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ**

That command — *illā an yashāʾ Allah*, "except if Allah wills" — *is* the *istithnāʾ*. It is the exception the garden owners refused to make. So these two passages are in conversation: Al-Qalam shows you the men who *didn't*, and al-Kahf gives you the command so you *will*. [PAUSE]

Now bring it into your own week. Here is the strange paradox of our age.

We have never in human history been more *certain* about tomorrow. Your calendar is full of appointments you have not yet kept. Your phone has reminders for events months away. You have plans, deadlines, deliveries scheduled to the minute, a whole architecture of confidence about a future that has not arrived. We speak of next year's vacation and next decade's retirement as though they were already in our pockets.

And we have also never been more *anxious.* [PAUSE]

Sit with that pairing, because it's not a coincidence. The garden owners' certainty and the modern person's anxiety are the *same condition*. When you take full ownership of a tomorrow you cannot control, you take on a burden you were never built to carry. You become the sole guarantor of an outcome that depends on a thousand things outside your hands. *Of course* you are anxious — you have appointed yourself god of your own calendar, and you are not equal to the job. The grip that *feels* like control is the exact same grip that produces the dread.

**"In shā' Allah," said with the full weight these verses give it, is not a religious decoration on your plans. It is the release of a burden you were never meant to hold.** It is you saying: *I will do everything in my power — I will harvest at dawn, I will show up, I will work — and the outcome I hand back to the One who owns the morning.* It is effort *plus* surrender. It is the seam in the sentence through which both humility and peace enter at once.

The garden owners had no seam. Sealed certainty, sealed hearts, and a sealed fate by morning.

So what does this change about tomorrow — literally, the actual tomorrow you are planning right now? [PAUSE]

**One specific practice: restore the weight of the phrase by *meaning* it once a day.** Pick the single most certain item on tomorrow's calendar — the thing you are *sure* will happen — and when you think of it or speak of it, add *in shā' Allah* and actually *feel* the seam open. Let it be the place where you admit, on purpose, that this most-certain thing is not yours to guarantee. Not as a shrug. As a release. Do it with the appointment you'd never dream of missing, and you will have done precisely what the garden owners refused to do, in precisely the spot they refused to do it: at the point of maximum certainty.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

We came in through three words and went out through two whole lives — yours and the garden owners' — and I want you to see now that the linguistic discoveries and the human themes were never two things. They were one insight approached from two directions.

*Balā*, the test that wears you down to reveal you, *is* the truth that blessing is a test — because a garden, a gift, an inheritance, is just another garment in which God wears you down to see what you are.

*Aqsamū* and *muṣbiḥīn*, the sworn oath scheduled into the dawn, *is* the human reach for control — the calendar full of certainties, the conviction that we can time our cleverness into an hour God won't notice.

And *wa lā yastathnūn*, the restless present-tense verb and its two missing words, *is* the sentence with no room for God — the sealed tomorrow that is itself the rupture, before a single fruit is picked.

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see how Allah built these two verses, because the architecture is the proof. [PAUSE]

He opens with **a hidden subject** — *We have tried* ***them*** — leaving a seat empty for you. He bridges with **a single word of comparison** — *just as* — laying your story over theirs. He photographs the failure in **two words of confident concealment** — *they swore... at dawn*. And then he closes the entire trap on **a word that isn't there at all** — the missing *in shā' Allah*.

The verse moves from a hidden *them*, to a sworn *we will*, to an absent *if God wills*. From concealment, through false certainty, to the one omission that doomed them. And it is framed — top and bottom — by the root *j-n-n*: the *majnūn* they called the Prophet, and the *jannah* that veiled their own hearts. **The whole structure is about veiling: who is really blind, and what they tried to hide, and the seam they sealed shut against the only One who sees.** The architecture says what the words say: *you cannot conceal the morning from the One who splits the dawn.*

### Questions to Carry

Let these stay open. Don't answer them now; carry them.

1. What blessing in my life have I quietly stopped treating as a test — because it arrived wrapped as a reward?
2. Who is the poor person attached to my last gift from Allah — whose share I have been harvesting at dawn, hoping not to notice them?
3. When I say "in shā' Allah," is there a seam in my sentence, or have I sealed it shut and only added the words as decoration?
4. Where is my certainty about tomorrow actually the source of my anxiety about tomorrow?
5. Which is more veiled — the heart I think is clear, or the garden I can see perfectly?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah tries us most through the blessings we trust most, and the garden is lost not at the harvest but the night before — in any sentence about tomorrow that leaves no room for the One who owns it.**

### The Closing Invitation

You don't need to fear your blessings. That is not the invitation. The invitation is gentler and harder than that: to *receive* them with the seam left open. To work for tomorrow with everything you have — to plan, to swear if you must, to set the alarm for dawn — and then, at the height of your certainty, to let two words break the seal and hand the morning back. The garden owners are not a warning against ambition. They are a warning against ownership. Harvest at dawn — but in the light, with the poor present, and with *in shā' Allah* meant from the heart.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You split the dawn and You own the morning we presume upon. Do not let our blessings become the garment in which we are worn down and found wanting; and do not let us seal a single sentence about tomorrow without leaving the seam open to Your will. Unveil our hearts before they harden, and make us of those who give the poor their share in the light — in shā' Allah.* Āmīn.
