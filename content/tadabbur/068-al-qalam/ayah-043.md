# While You Were Sound

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first, and then let me ask you to forget, for a little while, that you have heard them before.

> خَاشِعَةً أَبْصَارُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ ۖ وَقَدْ كَانُوا يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلسُّجُودِ وَهُمْ سَـٰلِمُونَ

*Khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum, tarhaquhum dhillah — wa qad kānū yudʿawna ilā s-sujūdi wa hum sālimūn.*

"Their eyes lowered, humiliation overwhelming them — and they had indeed been called to prostrate while they were sound."

### Rebuilding the world

To stand inside this ayah, you have to walk into it from the verse before it.

In the verse before this one, something happens that the Quran describes with a phrase that has occupied scholars for fourteen centuries: *yawma yukshafu ʿan sāq* — "the Day the shin is laid bare." The classical commentators differ on exactly what that uncovering refers to, and we will not resolve that here, nor do we need to. What everyone agrees on is the atmosphere it creates: this is the moment of ultimate severity. The veils are gone. Reality stands exposed.

And in that moment, the Quran says, *they are called to prostrate — and they cannot.* Their backs will not bend. Their bodies, which carried them so easily through a lifetime of standing tall, have locked. The one motion that could have saved them is now physically impossible.

That is verse 42.

Now the camera moves. Verse 43 turns to look at their faces — and then it does something that I want you to brace for, because it is one of the most quietly devastating turns in the entire Quran. Having shown you that they *cannot* prostrate now, it tells you: *they used to be called to this very thing — and back then, they could.*

So hold this scene. A crowd, on the worst day, with their eyes pinned to the ground. Something is settling over them like a fog. And a sentence arrives — not as a punishment, but almost as a memory. *You were invited to do this. Again and again. When your back still worked.*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we begin, and then sit with for the next hour.

The word the Quran uses for the lowered, humbled state of their eyes on that Day — *khāshiʿah* — is the **exact same word** Allah uses to praise the believers. It is the word for the most beautiful thing a worshipper can bring into a prayer.

So how can the very same posture be the crown of the believer in one place and the mark of the condemned in another?

[PAUSE]

The answer to that question is the whole ayah. Let's earn it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Khāshiʿah* — the humility that comes for everyone

Let me teach you a small thing about Arabic before we touch the word, because it changes how the verse opens.

In English, when we describe someone, we usually lead with the person: "Their eyes were lowered." Subject first, description second. Arabic can do that — but here it does the opposite. The verse opens with the *description*, fronted, before it tells you whose eyes: **khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum** — literally, "lowered, their eyes." The state comes first. The humbling arrives before the people do.

Grammatically this fronted word is a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial description, telling you the condition they are in. Both al-Qurtubi and the grammarians note its accusative case marks it precisely this way: this is not who they *are*, it is the state that has *come over* them. Keep that distinction. It will matter enormously.

Now the word itself.

**خَاشِعَةً** — from the root *kh-sh-ʿ* (خ ش ع). This is the root of *khushūʿ*. And if that word sounds familiar, it should. It is the word at the summit of one of the most beloved passages in the Quran:

> *Qad aflaḥa l-muʾminūn. Alladhīna hum fī ṣalātihim khāshiʿūn.*
> "The believers have succeeded — those who are humbly submissive in their prayer." (23:1–2)

That is the goal. That is the thing every one of us reaches for and so often cannot find — the lowered, softened, present heart in salah. *Khushūʿ* is the crown jewel of worship. We are taught that a prayer without it is a body without a soul.

And here, in Surah al-Qalam, the very same root is used to describe the condemned on the Day of Judgment.

Let's sit with this, because it is not an accident of vocabulary. It is the entire argument of the verse delivered in a single word.

Notice what *khushūʿ* actually is at its root. The lexicographers describe it as a lowering, a stillness, a yielding — the eyes drop, the voice softens, the body settles. It is the posture of someone standing before something far greater than themselves. Here is the thing: **that posture is coming for every single human being.** There is no version of the Last Day where anyone stands before Allah with a raised chin. Every gaze will lower. Every neck will bend. The khushūʿ is not optional.

So the question was never *whether* you will be humbled before Allah.

The only question is *when* — and therefore *how*.

The believer in Surah al-Muʾminūn brings the khushūʿ **forward**, into the prayer, by choice, while standing on a prayer mat in the quiet of the dunya. The people in our verse have the **exact same khushūʿ** extracted from them — but later, on the open plain, by force, when choosing is no longer a thing that exists.

Same lowering. Same word. Opposite worlds.

Al-Jalālayn captures the second sense precisely: here *khāshiʿah* carries the meaning of *dhalīlah* — abject, brought low. This is not the sweet humility of the worshipper. This is humiliation. And yet — and this is what should make you tremble — Allah chose to name it with the worshipper's word.

[PAUSE]

It is as if the verse is saying: *the lowering you refused to give Me freely, I will receive from you regardless. The only thing you got to decide was whether it would be worship or whether it would be shame.*

What does this single word reveal? **Humility before Allah is not a question you get to answer "no" to. It is only a question of timing. And timing is the difference between the crown and the chains.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Tarhaquhum* — the thing that overtakes you

Now look at what happens to their faces after the eyes drop.

> **تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ**
> *tarhaquhum dhillah* — "humiliation overwhelming them."

Two words. Let's take the verb first.

**تَرْهَقُهُمْ** comes from the root *r-h-q* (ر ه ق). And this is a word worth slowing down for, because the gentle English "covers them" does not carry its weight. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, in his *Mufradāt*, describes *rahaq* as a *covering by force* — *ghishyān bi-shiddah*, an overtaking, an overburdening that comes upon you against your will. It is the same root family as *murāhaqah*. The lexicons connect it to being caught, overtaken, weighed down by something heavier than you can bear or escape.

So this is not humiliation that they *feel* — the way you or I might feel a passing embarrassment and then shake it off. This is humiliation that **catches them and covers them over**, like a wave closing over a swimmer, like a debt no one can pay coming due all at once. They do not wear it. It overtakes them.

And notice the grammar of the verb: it is in the **present-tense form** — *tarhaqu*, not *rahiqat*. Arabic uses this ongoing form to paint a scene as live, unfolding, happening before your eyes. The humiliation isn't reported as a past event. It is *descending on them right now*, continuously, as you watch. The verse holds the moment open.

Now the second word:

**ذِلَّةٌ** — *dhillah*, from the root *dh-l-l* (ذ ل ل). Abasement. Lowness. The opposite of *ʿizzah*, honor.

And here read the absence as carefully as the presence. The word is **indefinite** — *dhillatun*, "a humiliation," not "the humiliation." In Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite in a context like this often signals something the tongue cannot fully measure — a humiliation of a kind, of a magnitude, that is left unnamed precisely because naming it would shrink it. Al-Muyassar reaches for the gap and fills it the only way language can: *dhillatun shadīdah* — a *severe* abasement, from the punishment of Allah. The indefiniteness is doing the work that an adjective can only approximate.

Put the two words back together: **a force you cannot resist, covering you over, in a measure too great to name.**

And here is the quiet horror of it, when you hold it against the root *dh-l-l*. Because *dhillah*, abasement, is the natural and honest condition of a servant before his Master. The believer *chooses* a kind of lowliness before Allah — we call it *dhull*, the willing smallness of the worshipper who knows his place. The scholars of the heart say the sweetest station of the slave is to stand before Allah in *iftiqār*, in admitted need and lowliness.

So once again — exactly as with *khushūʿ* — we meet a reality that is **coming either way**. You will be brought low before Allah. The believer brings himself low, gently, in sujūd, and Allah raises him. These people refused the small, dignified lowering of the servant — and so the great, crushing lowering of the condemned overtook them instead.

What does this reveal? **There is a lowliness you can choose, and it lifts you. And there is a lowliness that comes to find you, and it buries you. The same root. The same direction. The world of difference is in whether you walked there or were dragged.**

### Element Three: *Wa hum sālimūn* — while they were sound

Now we arrive at the phrase I asked you to brace for. After the eyes, after the humiliation, the verse pivots — *wa qad*, "and indeed" — into the past. Into the dunya. Into your life and mine.

> **وَقَدْ كَانُوا يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلسُّجُودِ وَهُمْ سَـٰلِمُونَ**
> "and they had indeed been called to prostrate, while they were sound."

There are three things buried here, and each one opens.

**First — *kānū yudʿawna*: "they used to be called."**

The construction *kāna* followed by the present-tense verb *yudʿawna* is Arabic's way of saying *habitual, repeated, ongoing past action*. Not "they were called once." **They used to be called — over and over, as a way of life.** Every day. Day after day after day.

And the verb is **passive** — *yudʿawna*, "they were called," not "someone called them." The caller is left unnamed. Why? Because the call came from everywhere. The commentators here recall the tābiʿī Ibrāhīm al-Taymī, reported by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī, who said the call was *the adhān and the iqāmah* — the call to the obligatory prayer. And Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, reported in the same sources, said: they would hear *ḥayya ʿalā l-falāḥ* — "come to success" — and not answer.

Sit with that. The caller was the muezzin. The caller was the dīn. The caller, behind all of them, was Allah. And the call wasn't a one-time summons. It was a standing invitation, going out five times every day, for a lifetime. The same call that — if you are within earshot of a masjid right now — is still going out today.

[PAUSE]

**Second — *ilā s-sujūd*: "to prostration."**

Look at *what* they were called to. From the root *s-j-d* (س ج د) — *sujūd*, the act of putting the highest part of you, your face, on the lowest place, the ground. The single most concentrated act of submission a human body can perform.

And here is the architecture beginning to reveal itself. Go back to verse 42: there, on the Day, they are *called to sujūd and cannot*. Here, in verse 43, they *were called to sujūd* — and the unspoken contrast hangs in the air: *and could.* The same call. The exact same word, *sujūd*, in both verses. The only thing that changed is the body's ability to answer it. We will come back to this symmetry at the end, because it is the whole verse folded into a mirror.

**Third — and this is the word that should keep us up at night — *wa hum sālimūn*: "while they were sound."**

The root is *s-l-m* (س ل م). And you know this root. It is the root of *salām*, peace. It is the root of *salāmah*, soundness and safety. It is the root of *islām* itself. *Sālimūn* — sound, whole, healthy, intact, with nothing wrong, fully able.

The commentators are unanimous and plain about its meaning here. Al-Muyassar: *aṣiḥḥāʾ qādirūn ʿalayhā* — healthy, fully capable of it. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī: *muʿāfūn aṣiḥḥāʾ* — sound and well. There was no excuse. No bad back. No illness. No barrier. Their bodies bent perfectly. Their knees worked. Their foreheads could reach the ground with ease.

**They were called to prostrate, and they were completely able to prostrate, and they did not prostrate.**

Now feel the full weight of *s-l-m* sitting at the end of this verse. The root of *islām* — submission — is used to describe the precise window in which they *had every capacity to submit and withheld it.* They possessed the *salāmah*, the wholeness. What they withheld was the *islām*, the surrender. The body was sound. The will was not.

Al-Muyassar names the disease exactly: they refused *taʿaẓẓuman wa-stikbāran* — out of self-magnification and arrogance. The legs could bend. The pride would not.

[PAUSE]

What does this final phrase reveal? **The tragedy of these people is not that they couldn't. It is that they could — easily, daily, for years — and chose not to, until the day the "could" was taken away.** And the verse names that window of ability with the very root of the surrender they refused: while they were *sound*. While they were *whole*. While they still belonged, by capacity, to the people of *salām*.

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have laid bare. *Khāshiʿah* — the humility is coming for everyone; you only choose its form. *Tarhaquhum dhillah* — there is a lowliness you walk into and one that overtakes you. *Wa hum sālimūn* — and the door between those two destinies stays open only as long as you are sound.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, about the life you and I are living right now, today, with our backs still working.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The window called "while you are sound"

I want to start somewhere very ordinary.

You have a task you mean to do. Something that matters — call a parent you've drifted from, fix a relationship, start the thing you keep promising yourself you'll start. And you don't do it today. Not because you've decided against it. You fully intend to. You just assume — without ever saying it out loud — that the door will still be open tomorrow. That *later* is a real place you can get to. That the capacity you have today is simply a permanent feature of you.

This is the most universal assumption of the human heart: **that the window stays open.** We don't procrastinate because we think the chance is worthless. We procrastinate because we are certain the chance will *wait* for us.

Now bring that into this verse, because this is exactly the assumption it dismantles.

These people were not, in their own minds, refusing forever. Day after day they heard *ḥayya ʿalā l-falāḥ*, and day after day some part of them surely thought: *not now. Later. There's time.* The call would come again tomorrow, and their backs would still bend tomorrow, and surely there would be some tomorrow when they'd answer. The window felt permanent. *Sālimūn* — sound — felt like a description of who they simply were.

And then verse 42 arrives. The day comes when they are called one final time — and the body that bent so easily for a lifetime will not bend. *Sālimūn* is gone. It was never a permanent feature. **It was a window.**

Here is what the verse is teaching, and it is something almost no one builds their life around: *your ability to turn to Allah is itself a borrowed thing, and it has an expiry date you cannot see.*

We treat repentance, prayer, the turn back to Allah, as something that lives entirely in our will — as if the only ingredient is *deciding* to do it, and deciding is always available. But that is not true. The turn requires two things: the *will* to turn, and the *capacity* to turn. And the capacity — the sound back, the working heart, the breath in your chest, the very lucidity to form the intention — that part is not yours. It is on loan. The word *sālimūn* is Allah quietly reminding you that the soundness you're standing in right now is a gift with a closing time, not a possession with your name on it.

Let me deepen this, because there's a movement most people miss. We imagine the danger of delay is that we might *die* before we repent. That's real, but it's not the sharpest edge here. The sharper edge is this: **you can lose the capacity to turn while you are still alive.** The heart can harden. The habit of refusal, repeated daily — *kānū yudʿawna*, called and called and called — can calcify into a back that simply will not bend anymore, long before the body fails. Every unanswered call doesn't just pass; it makes the next call a little easier to ignore. The arrogance al-Muyassar named — *istikbār* — is not a single decision. It is the sediment of a thousand small refusals.

So the window isn't only closed by death. It is closed, quietly, from the inside, every time we hear the call and tell ourselves *later* while we are still perfectly *sound*.

And now turn it over — because the same verse that terrifies also liberates. If you are reading this, if you can hear an adhān and feel even a flicker of "I should answer," then by definition you are still inside the window. You are still, in the language of this ayah, *sālim*. The door these people are weeping over on the plain of Judgment — that door is, for you, *still open.* The verse is not only a verdict on them. It is an alarm for you, sounded in time.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the specific, concrete thing. The next time you hear the adhān — the literal call these people ignored — let it land as what it is: a renewal of an offer with an expiry date. Don't negotiate it into "later." When *ḥayya ʿalā l-falāḥ* reaches you and you are sound enough to stand, stand. Treat one prayer this week — just one — as if it were the last call before the window closed. Because the only thing separating you from the people in this verse is a soundness you did not earn and cannot keep.

### Theme Two: The bow that lifts you and the bow that buries you

Let me start with something you already know in your body.

There is a difference between apologizing to someone because your heart broke open and you *wanted* to — and apologizing because you were caught, cornered, and had no choice left. The words can be identical. "I'm sorry." Same posture, same lowered head. But everyone present knows they are not the same act. One is dignity. The other is defeat. One draws people toward you. The other shrinks you in front of them.

The body knows the difference between a bow you *gave* and a bow that was *taken from you*.

Now hold that next to what we found in the language. *Khāshiʿah* — the lowered eyes — is the believer's crown in Surah al-Muʾminūn and the condemned's shame here. *Dhillah* — lowliness — is the servant's sweet station before Allah and the rebel's crushing burden here. The same postures. The same roots. And the verse has shown us that *the lowering is coming for everyone.* No created being escapes the Day of Judgment with its head unbowed. The universe ends with all of creation low before its Lord.

So this is the choice — the only real choice the verse leaves on the table: **not whether you will bow, but whether you will bow as a worshipper or as a captive.**

Let that reframe what sujūd even *is*. We tend to experience prostration as a cost — a thing we owe, an obligation, a lowering we'd rather not perform five times a day. But this verse exposes that framing as backwards. The sujūd of the dunya is not the price of dignity. **It is the *purchase* of dignity.** It is the one chance to do *by choice, in private, while sound*, the very thing that will otherwise be done *to you, in public, by force.* The worshipper on the prayer mat is buying — cheaply, daily — an exemption from the humiliation on the plain.

Go deeper. Why does the *voluntary* bow lift you while the *forced* one buries you, when the physical motion is the same? Because what Allah is after was never the geometry of your spine. It was your *will*. Sujūd is the will laying itself down. When you choose it, you have *given* Him the one thing that was actually yours to give — your refusal — and surrendered it. When it is extracted from you on the Day, the will is no longer in play; there is nothing left to surrender, because reality itself has overridden you. The bones bend either way. But the self can only be *offered* once — while you are sound. After that, it can only be *overruled*.

And here is a paradox worth letting sit without rushing to tidy it: the people who refused to be lowered are the most lowered of all. They guarded their pride — *taʿaẓẓuman wa-stikbāran*, self-magnifying, refusing to bend — and that very guarding is what delivered them to *dhillatun* too vast to name. Meanwhile the believer who threw his pride to the floor five times a day, who made himself small, is the one who rises on that Day with a face, the commentators say, *brighter than snow.* The one who clung to *ʿizzah* lost it. The one who embraced *dhull* before Allah was clothed in honor. **What you refuse to surrender, you lose. What you surrender, you are given back, transfigured.**

This is the universal struggle, isn't it. Every one of us has a pride that does not want to bend — to a parent, to a truth, to God. We experience the bending as loss. The whole machinery of the ego screams that lowering yourself is diminishment. And this verse, with the bodies of the condemned, testifies to the opposite: the lowering you refuse does not protect you. It only reschedules itself to the worst possible moment and arrives without your consent.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here it is, concrete. The next time you go into sujūd — tonight, hopefully — change what you think you are doing. You are not paying a debt. You are taking, freely and in private, the dignified version of a bow that the whole world will one day take by force. So make that one sujūd *count*. Linger in it a breath longer than the body wants to. Offer Allah the one thing only you can give Him while you are sound — your will, laid down on purpose. Tell Him, with your forehead on the floor, *I am giving You now, gladly, what I never want extracted from me later.*

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture revealed

Now let me show you the structure, because it has been waiting beneath us the whole time.

Verses 42 and 43 are built as a mirror. Set them side by side:

- **Verse 42 — the Day:** they are called to *sujūd* → and they **cannot** (their backs locked, *lā yastaṭīʿūn*).
- **Verse 43 — the world:** they were called to *sujūd* → and they **could** (*wa hum sālimūn*), and refused.

The same call — *al-sujūd* — sits at the center of both. The exact same invitation. The two verses are identical except for one variable: **ability.** In the world, the will refused what the body could do. On the Day, the body refuses what the will would now give anything to do.

That is the proof, built into the architecture. The verse is not merely describing a punishment. It is showing you that the punishment is *the exact shape of the refusal, reflected back.* They turned away from the call while able; they are now turned away from the call while desperate. The mirror is the justice.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same truth approached from two sides.

The *words* told us: the humility (*khāshiʿah*), the lowliness (*dhillah*), and the surrender (the root of *sālimūn* is the root of *islām*) are all realities that arrive for every soul — the only variable is whether they come by your choice or by force.

The *themes* told us: there is a window, named "while you are sound," in which choice is still possible — and within that window, the bow you give lifts you, while the bow you withhold only waits to bury you later.

Both arrive at one place: **the difference between the saved and the condemned is not whether they bowed. It is *when*.**

### Questions to carry

- When the adhān reaches me, what do I actually do in the next sixty seconds — and what does that reveal about whether I believe the window is open or assume it is permanent?
- Is there a sujūd I keep postponing — a surrender to Allah I have decided to make "later" — while I am, right now, perfectly *sound*?
- Where in my life am I guarding a pride I think is protecting me, the way these people guarded theirs — and what is it actually costing me?
- If "sound" is a window and not a possession, what would I do differently today if I truly believed it could close from the inside, while I'm still alive?
- The believer's *khushūʿ* and the condemned's *khushūʿ* are the same posture. What turns one into a crown and the other into a chain — and which one am I building?

### One-sentence distillation

The humility you refuse to offer Allah freely while you are *sound* will be extracted from you by force when you no longer can — so bow now, willingly, while the word "sound" still describes you.

### The closing invitation

This verse is not, in the end, about *them*. The grammar gives them away — *kānū yudʿawna*, "they used to be called." The call is the thing. And the call has not stopped. It went out at dawn and it will go out at sunset, and somewhere within earshot of where you sit it is going out today, while you are sound. The people in this ayah are not a foreign species. They are simply those who heard the same call you hear and kept choosing *later* until *later* ran out.

You are still inside the window. That is not a small thing — it is the entire mercy of being alive. So let this not become information that satisfies the mind and leaves the heart where it found it. Let it move you to the floor. Not because you must. Because the door is open, and one day it will not be, and the bow is so much sweeter given than taken.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who call us to prostration five times each day — let us answer the call while we are sound, before we are among those who are called and cannot. Make our bowing a thing we give You freely and gladly, and spare us the humiliation that overtakes the necks that would not bend. And let the *khushūʿ* You receive from us be the crown of worshippers, never the shame of the turned-away. *Āmīn.*
