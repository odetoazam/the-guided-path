# When the Finger Turns

### A Reflection on Surah Al-Qalam, 68:30–31

> **فَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ يَتَلَاوَمُونَ ۝ قَالُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا طَاغِينَ**
>
> *Fa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatalāwamūn. Qālū yā waylanā innā kunnā ṭāghīn.*
>
> "So they turned to one another, blaming each other. They said: 'O woe to us! Indeed, we were transgressors.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing in the Ash

Before we open a single word, let me ask you to stand somewhere.

It is just after dawn. The light is grey and thin. A group of brothers — they inherited a garden from a righteous father, a garden so generous that its owner used to leave a known portion of every harvest for the poor of the town. The father has died. The sons have done the math. They've looked at the harvest, looked at the poor who come every year expecting their share, and they have made a decision.

They will go out **early** — before anyone is awake. They will harvest in **secret**. They will deny the poor their portion. And the night before, they swore an oath to do exactly this — *layaṣrimunnahā muṣbiḥīn*, "we will surely harvest it in the morning" — and they swore it, the Quran tells us pointedly, **without saying "in shā' Allah."** They left Allah out of the sentence entirely. As if the garden were theirs to command. As if tomorrow were a thing they owned.

[PAUSE]

While they slept, a visitation from your Lord went around the garden. By the time they woke, it was *kaṣ-ṣarīm* — black as a moonless night, harvested down to the dirt. They set out whispering to each other, *"don't let a single poor person enter on you today"* — striding out in the full confidence of the cruel and the comfortable.

And then they saw it.

At first they couldn't even believe it was their own land. *"Surely we have lost our way,"* they said. Then the truth landed: *"No — we are deprived."*

This is the moment our two verses begin. The brothers are standing in the ash of everything they planned to hoard. And the Quran does something extraordinary here. It does not narrate what they lost. It narrates **what they said to each other.** It turns the camera away from the ruined garden and onto their faces.

Because the real subject of this parable was never the garden.

Here is the question I want you to carry into these two verses. When everything a group of people built together collapses because of something they did together — **what is the very first thing they do?** And what is the second thing — the thing that actually matters, the thing most people never reach?

Watch. The Quran is about to show you both, in nine words, with a precision that should stop your breath.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Aqbala* — They *Turned Toward* Each Other

Let me teach you a small thing about Arabic before we touch the verse.

There is a root in Arabic — **q-b-l** — and it is one of the warmest roots in the language. From it comes *qabila*, "to accept." From it comes *qabūl*, "acceptance." From it comes *istiqbāl*, the welcome you give a guest. And from it comes the most familiar word of all: **qibla** — the direction you *face* in prayer. The whole family of this root is about **facing, turning toward, receiving, welcoming.**

Now here is the form Allah uses: **أَقْبَلَ**, *aqbala*. This is Form IV of that root — and its meaning is "to turn toward, to advance upon, to come forward to face someone." When you *aqbala* on a person, you turn your whole self in their direction. It is the body language of attention. It is what you do when someone walks into the room and you turn to meet them.

So listen to how the verse opens:

> **فَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ**
>
> *Fa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍ* — "So they turned to face one another."

Notice the **فَ** at the front — *fa-*, "so, immediately, at once." There is no pause between the discovery and this. The instant they understood they were ruined, *they spun to face each other.*

Now — sit with this. Allah chose the verb of *welcome*, the verb of *turning toward in attention*, the cousin of the word *qibla*. He could have said they shouted. He could have said they wept. Instead He says they **turned to face one another** — they gave each other their full attention, the way you face the thing that matters most to you.

And what did they do with all that focused attention?

[PAUSE]

Here is something I want you to notice, and it is so easy to miss. The preposition is **عَلَىٰ**, *ʿalā* — "upon." Not *ilā*, "toward." *ʿAlā.* And *ʿalā* in Arabic carries the weight of *over, upon, against.* They turned *upon* one another. The very same verb that can mean to welcome a guest is here bent, by a single preposition, into turning **against** each other.

Do you feel what the language just did? It took the posture of *qibla* — the posture of facing what you love, facing your Lord — and it aimed it sideways, horizontally, at each other, as an accusation. All that intensity of attention that should have turned **upward** to the One who ruined the garden, they spent **across**, on the brother standing next to them.

This is the first reflex. We will name it in a moment. But hold the image: people who should be falling to their knees facing the *qibla*, instead squaring up to face each other.

### Element Two: *Yatalāwamūn* — The Blame That Bounces

Now we come to the word that the whole verse is built around. And to feel it, you need one more piece of Arabic grammar — and I promise you, once you have it, you will never read this verse the same way again.

Arabic verbs come in **forms** — patterns stamped onto the bare root that change its meaning. You don't need to memorize them; you just need to feel two of them.

Take the root **l-w-m**, which simply means *to blame*. The plain verb is *lāma* — "he blamed."

Now there is a special form in Arabic — **Form VI**, the pattern *tafāʿala* (you can hear it: ta-FAA-ʿa-la, with a long stretched *aa* after the first letter). And Form VI has one job above all others: it expresses **mutual, reciprocal, back-and-forth action between multiple parties.** *Kataba* is "he wrote"; *takātaba* is "they wrote **to each other**." *Qātala* is "he fought"; *taqātala* is "they fought **one another**." Form VI is the form of the circle — action passing back and forth between people with no one outside it.

So take *blame* — *l-w-m* — and pour it into the mold of Form VI, and you get:

> **يَتَلَاوَمُونَ**
>
> *yatalāwamūn* — "they are **blaming one another**, back and forth, in a circle."

Say it slowly. *Ya-ta-LAA-wa-MOON.* Listen to the shape of it — the long *aa* in the middle, the way the sound itself swings out and comes back, *lāwa*, like a thing thrown and returned. The morphology is not describing the blame from the outside. **The morphology is the blame.** The very pattern of the word — *tafāʿala*, the reciprocal mold — traps the meaning in a loop. The accusation leaves one mouth, lands on a brother, bounces to the next, and comes back around. *You told us to do this. No — you were the one who said to go early. It was your idea to leave nothing for the poor. You agreed!*

[PAUSE]

And here is the absence I want you to read as carefully as you read any word that is present. Look at what the verb does **not** say. *Yatalāwamūn* — "blaming one another." Blaming one another **for what?** Arabic grammar would happily let Allah attach an object here, a *for-this* or *for-that*. He leaves it open. He does not specify.

Why?

Because in the circle of blame, **the content doesn't matter — only the direction does.** When a group is busy blaming each other, they are not actually conducting an inquiry into what went wrong. They are performing a ritual whose only purpose is to make the accusation point *anywhere but at me.* The Quran leaves the "for what" blank because, in that moment, *they* don't care about the "for what" either. The blame is not in service of truth. It is in service of escape. The grammatical gap is the spiritual diagnosis.

Now stay with me, because there is a connection here that runs across the whole Quran, and it is built on this exact root, *l-w-m.* Travel forward to Surah Ibrahim, to the scene on the Day of Judgment when Shayṭān himself stands up to address the people he misled. And what does he say to them?

> **فَلَا تَلُومُونِي وَلُومُوا أَنفُسَكُمْ**
>
> *"So do not **blame me** — **blame yourselves**."* (14:22)

Same root. *Lā talūmūnī* — don't blame me. *Lūmū anfusakum* — blame yourselves. Even Shayṭān, in the end, knows the secret these brothers have not yet learned: **blame thrown sideways is wasted; the only blame that ever heals is the blame you turn upon your own soul.** The garden-owners in our verse are doing the first thing. *Yatalāwamūn* — they're throwing it sideways, around the circle. And the entire weight of the parable now hangs on a single question: will they break out of the circle?

### Element Three: *Innā Kunnā Ṭāghīn* — The Word Bigger Than the Crime

And then — verse 31 — they break out.

> **قَالُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا طَاغِينَ**
>
> *Qālū yā waylanā innā kunnā ṭāghīn* — "They said: 'O woe to us! Indeed, we were transgressors.'"

Three things happen in this sentence, and each one is a turning.

**First**, *yā waylanā* — "O woe to *us*." Not "woe to you." Not "this is your fault." *Waylanā* — the "us" is attached. For the first time, the pronoun is plural and inclusive. The circle of *you-you-you* has finally closed into *we.* The finger that was pointing sideways has turned and is now pointing at the whole group, themselves included. This is the grammatical moment the blame stops bouncing and starts landing where it belongs.

**Second**, look at the verb of being: *innā **kunnā*** ṭāghīn — "indeed we **were**." *Kunnā* is past tense, and it is a confession of a settled state. They are not saying "we made a mistake last night." They are saying "we **were** — this is what we had become." They are naming a condition, not an incident. That is what real recognition sounds like. It does not minimize the act into a slip. It owns the state of the soul that produced it.

**Third** — and this is the word I have been waiting the whole reflection to show you — *what* do they confess to being? Here is where you must read with your full attention.

They do **not** say *innā kunnā bukhalā'* — "we were stingy."

They do **not** say *innā kunnā khāṭi'īn* — "we made an error."

They say: **إِنَّا كُنَّا طَاغِينَ** — *"we were **ṭāghīn**."*

The root is **ṭ-gh-y**, *ṭaghā.* And you must understand what this word means, because the brothers chose it with terrible accuracy. *Ṭughyān* is not ordinary sin. *Ṭughyān* is **overflowing past the limit. Bursting the boundary. Overstepping the line that was set for you.** It is the same root from which the Quran builds the word **Ṭāghūt** — the false god, the tyrant, the thing that oversteps its rightful place and demands what isn't its due.

And here is the stepping stone — the physical image hidden inside this word. Travel just one surah forward, to Surah Al-Ḥāqqah, where Allah describes the great flood of Nūḥ:

> **إِنَّا لَمَّا **طَغَى** الْمَاءُ حَمَلْنَاكُمْ فِي الْجَارِيَةِ**
>
> *"Indeed, when the water **overflowed** (ṭaghā), We carried you in the sailing ship."* (69:11)

Do you see it? The **same root.** *Ṭaghā al-mā'* — the water *overstepped its bounds*, broke past every shore and limit, and drowned the world. That is the physical picture of *ṭughyān*: water that will not stay within its banks.

So when these brothers say *innā kunnā ṭāghīn*, they are not just admitting they were greedy. They are diagnosing themselves with the precision of a surgeon. They are saying: **there was a limit — a ḥadd, a boundary that Allah drew. The poor had a known right in this harvest, a portion that was theirs by the decree of the One who grew the fruit. And we did not merely fail to give it. We burst the bank. We overflowed past the line. We took what was set aside for others and called it ours.** Their sin was a flood. They had become water that would not stay in its banks — and like every flood, it drowned the very ones who unleashed it.

[PAUSE]

The word is bigger than the crime. And that is *exactly* why it is the truth. The crime looked small — a quiet decision to harvest early. But every transgression begins as a small overstepping of a small limit, and the soul that will overstep *here* is the soul that, given water enough, will drown a world. They finally named the disease and not just the symptom. That is what tawba sounds like.

---

### Bridge: From the Word to the World

So look at what these nine words have built. A verb of *facing* (*aqbala*) bent sideways by a single preposition into facing-against. A verb of *blame* poured into the reciprocal mold (*yatalāwamūn*) so that the very shape of the word becomes a closed loop. And then the breaking of the loop — *waylanā*, the "us"; *kunnā*, the owned state; and *ṭāghīn*, the word that names a small greed as a cosmic flood.

The grammar has shown us a journey: from blame thrown sideways to truth turned inward.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about three brothers in an ancient orchard, but about the human heart. About *your* heart, and mine.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Circle of Blame Is a Prison We Build to Avoid the Stairs

Let me start where you already live.

You have been in the room. Maybe it was a project at work that failed — months of effort, and on the day it collapsed, the meeting wasn't about *fixing* it. It was about *who.* "Well, I flagged this in March." "You signed off on the timeline." "Nobody told me." The energy in that room was enormous — people leaning in, fully animated, *fully turned toward one another* — and not one ounce of it was moving the situation forward.

Or maybe it was closer than work. A family after a financial ruin. Siblings around a parent's estate. A marriage in a hard season, where every conversation becomes an exchange of invoices — *you did this, well you did that* — each one perfectly remembered, perfectly itemized.

That room is *fa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatalāwamūn.* That is the verse. You have been inside it.

And here is what the Quran is teaching us about why we go there. The circle of blame is not really about finding fault. **It is the thing we do to avoid the one move that frightens us more than ruin itself: turning the finger around.**

Think about the geometry of it. *Yatalāwamūn* is **horizontal** — blame travels sideways, across the circle, person to person. It is a closed loop, and a closed loop has a strange comfort: as long as the accusation keeps moving sideways, it never has to stop on *me*, and it never has to climb. Because there are only two other directions blame can go, and both of them are terrifying.

It can go **down and in** — to myself. *I* did this.
Or it can go **up** — to Allah. *O Allah, this came from You, and I have wronged my own soul before You.*

The horizontal circle exists precisely to avoid the vertical staircase. As long as we are busy blaming each other, we never have to climb the stairs of repentance. The blame circle is a **prison we build with our own hands to keep ourselves from the very freedom we are screaming for.**

[PAUSE]

And notice the cruelty of it — these are *brothers.* They committed this together, planned it together, swore the oath together. In the moment they most need each other — standing in the same ash, facing the same loss — they turn on each other. Shared sin does not bind people. It *isolates* them, each one scrambling to push the weight onto the next. There is no loneliness like the loneliness of a circle of people all blaming each other.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. The next time you feel that pull — the hot, fast, *righteous* pull to say "but it was *your* fault" — recognize it for what it is. It is not a path forward. It is the prison door swinging open, inviting you in, dressed up as justice. The single most spiritually productive sentence available to a human being in the wreckage of a shared failure is not "you did this." It is *"yā waylanā"* — *woe to us* — and then the climb begins.

### Theme Two: Real Repentance Names the Flood, Not Just the Puddle

Now let me take you somewhere subtler, because the brothers did something in verse 31 that most of us, even when we *do* repent, fail to do.

Here is how we usually apologize. We minimize. "I overreacted." "It was a stressful week." "I shouldn't have said it *like that*." Notice what every one of those does — it shrinks the sin down to a manageable, forgivable size. It turns the flood into a puddle. *It wasn't really transgression — it was just a slip, a bad day, a tone problem.*

We do this because naming the sin at full size feels unsurvivable. If I admit that what I did was not a slip but *ṭughyān* — a genuine overstepping of a limit my Lord set — then I have to face the kind of person I was when I did it. And that is the hardest thing a human being ever does.

But watch the brothers. They had every exit available. They could have said: "We were just being prudent." "Times were hard, we needed the harvest." "We were going to give *something*, eventually." Instead, standing in the ash, they reached for the **biggest, most honest, most damning word available** — *ṭāghīn.* We didn't slip. **We burst the bank. We were the flood.**

[PAUSE]

And here is the paradox at the center of this — the thing I want you to sit with and not resolve too quickly. **Naming the sin at its true size is not despair. It is the doorway to mercy.** The brothers who say "we were transgressors" are *closer* to Allah in that sentence than they were on every prosperous day they spent counting fruit and planning to cheat the poor. The full-sized confession is not the end of hope. It is the first true thing they have said in the entire story.

Because mercy cannot enter a sin you have shrunk. If I bring Allah a puddle, He forgives a puddle — and I walk away still secretly the kind of person who builds floods. But if I bring Him the flood, name it, own the whole overflowing extent of it — *that* is the soul that gets remade. Allah's forgiveness is as vast as you are willing to be honest. The size of the mercy meets the size of the confession.

And notice — there is more in this story than ruin. The brothers don't stop at *ṭāghīn.* The very next verses have them turning, fully, upward: *"Perhaps our Lord will give us in exchange something better than it — indeed, toward our Lord we turn in hope"* (68:32). The flood named becomes the heart turned. The confession at full size becomes the hope at full reach. That is the architecture of return: you cannot ask your Lord for *better* until you have admitted, without shrinking it, exactly how bad it was.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practice. The next time you go to make tawba — to Allah, or even to a person you wronged — notice the instinct to shrink the word. Notice yourself reaching for "slip," "mistake," "bad day." And then, gently, reach instead for the true word. Not to crush yourself — but because the true word is the only one mercy can find its way into. *Innā kunnā ṭāghīn.* Say the real size of it. That is not where hope dies. That is where it begins.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look now at how the linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight, seen from two directions.

The **grammar** showed us a journey of direction. *Aqbala ʿalā* — facing, but bent sideways into facing-against. *Yatalāwamūn* — the reciprocal mold, the closed horizontal loop of blame. And then the break: *waylanā* (the inclusive "us"), *kunnā* (the owned state), *ṭāghīn* (the flood named at full size). The morphology traced a finger moving from **sideways to inward and upward.**

The **themes** showed us the same movement as a spiritual law. The circle of blame is the horizontal prison we build to avoid the vertical stairs. And repentance only works when it names the flood, not the puddle — when the confession is as honest as the sin was large.

Two roads into one truth: **The whole spiritual life turns on the direction you aim the finger.** Sideways is prison. Inward and upward is freedom. The garden was already ash; nothing the brothers said could regrow it. But the *direction they turned* — that decided whether they walked out of that orchard as ruined men or as returning servants. And the entire parable was placed in Surah Al-Qalam — a surah defending the noble character of the Prophet ﷺ against arrogant, wealthy deniers who were sure their blessings were theirs to command — as a mirror held up to anyone who has ever felt entitled to a blessing they were only ever lent.

### Questions to Carry

1. When something I shared in goes wrong, where does my finger go *first* — and how long does it stay there before it turns?
2. Is there a circle of blame I am currently inside — a relationship, a family knot, a work wound — where I am waiting for the *other* person to break it first? What would *yā waylanā* — "woe to *us*" — cost me, and what might it free?
3. When I last repented, did I name the flood or the puddle? Which word did I reach for — and which one was true?
4. The brothers left Allah out of their plan — they swore to harvest "in the morning" without saying *in shā' Allah.* Where in my life am I making plans as if tomorrow is a thing I own?
5. The poor had a *known right* in that garden. Whose known right am I currently treating as my discretionary generosity?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When what we built together collapses, the soul's first reflex is to throw blame sideways in an endless circle — and its only salvation is to turn the finger inward and upward and name the sin at its full, flooding size.

### The Closing Invitation

You will stand in ash again. We all do — the failed thing, the broken plan, the blessing stripped overnight. When you do, you will feel the old horizontal pull, the *fa-aqbala*, the spin toward the nearest face with an accusation ready on your tongue. This is the invitation: in that exact moment, before the circle closes, let one word rise instead. *Waylanā.* *Us.* And then climb. The garden may not regrow. But the one who turns inward and upward in the ash is closer to their Lord there, in the wreckage, than they ever were among the fruit.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, when our plans collapse, keep our fingers from turning against one another, and turn them instead toward our own souls and up toward You. Give us the courage to name our transgression at its true size — *innā kunnā ṭāghīn* — not to despair, but because Your mercy is as vast as our honesty. And as You taught these servants to hope, let us too say: perhaps our Lord will give us something better than what we lost. *Āmīn.*
