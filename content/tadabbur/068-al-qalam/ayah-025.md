# The Morning They Set Out Able

## Surah Al-Qalam, Ayah 25

> وَغَدَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ حَرْدٍۢ قَـٰدِرِينَ
>
> *wa-ghadaw ʿalā ḥardin qādirīn*
>
> "And they set out early, bent on a fixed purpose — thinking themselves fully able to carry it out."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Verse

Let me set the scene, because this verse cannot land on you cold. It is one frame in a story, and if you don't feel the story, the verse is just four words.

There was a man who owned a garden. A good man. When his fruit ripened, he would harvest it — but he never harvested all of it for himself. He left a portion for the poor, the passerby, the one with no garden of his own. This was his habit, his quiet covenant. The garden was a blessing, and he treated it as a trust.

Then he died. And his sons inherited the garden — the trees, the fruit, the wealth. But they did not inherit his heart.

They looked at the portion their father gave away every year and they saw not generosity but leakage. Loss. Inefficiency. *Why should the poor eat from what is ours?* And so, the night before the harvest, they made a plan. A careful plan. They swore an oath to one another — the Quran tells us just a few verses earlier — that they would harvest the garden at first light, **secretly**, before anyone could see, before any beggar could arrive, before any poor hand could reach. They swore it. And — this detail matters — **they swore it without saying "in shā' Allah."** Without a single breath of "if God wills." They spoke of tomorrow as though tomorrow belonged to them.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what they did not know.

While they slept — while they dreamed of the morning's clean, private harvest — a visitation came. The Quran calls it *ṭāʾif* — something that circled the garden in the dark, *min rabbik*, **from your Lord**, while they were sleeping. And by the time the sun was thinking of rising, the garden was *kal-ṣarīm* — like a field already cut to the ground. Black. Barren. Ash where the fruit had been.

They did not know this.

So they woke. They called to one another in the early dark — *let's go, while it's still cool, while no one is watching.* And they set off, whispering, "Let no poor man enter upon you today." Whispering. Conspiring. Efficient.

And then comes our verse. The camera holds on them as they march down the road toward the garden:

**وَغَدَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ حَرْدٍۢ قَـٰدِرِينَ**

They set out, in the early morning, *with hardened resolve* — and the verse ends on one word, the word that will break in their hands: **qādirīn.** *Able. Capable. In control.*

Here is the hook. Hold onto it. **This is the most confident moment in the entire story — and it is the last confident moment they will ever have.** The very next verse begins, *falammā raʾawhā* — "and when they saw it…" The word "able" is still warm in their mouths when they round the bend and see the ash.

Why does Allah freeze them *here*? Why give us this snapshot of a people at the absolute summit of their certainty, one footstep before the fall? What is hidden inside these four words that makes them, I want to suggest to you, a mirror for every plan you have ever made?

Let's go find out.

---

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: غَدَوْا — The Eager Morning

Before we touch the word, let me give you a small picture from your own life.

You know the feeling of waking before your alarm on the day of something you've been waiting for. The flight. The first day. The thing you planned. There is a particular energy to *going early* — it is the body of someone who is ahead of the day, who has beaten the clock, who is in front of events rather than behind them. To rise early and move is to feel like a master of your own time.

Arabic has a precise word for this, and it is the first word of our verse.

**غَدَوْا — *ghadaw.*** From the root *gh-d-w* (غدو). And this root does not mean simply "to go." It means specifically **to go in the early morning** — the *ghuduww*, the dawn journey. The Arabs paired it with its opposite, *rawāḥ*, the evening return. *Ghaduww wa rawāḥ* — the going-out at dawn and the coming-back at dusk, the whole rhythm of a working life. So when Allah says *wa-ghadaw*, He is not just telling us they walked. He is telling us they **rose with the dawn and went out eager**, ahead of the sun, ahead of the poor, ahead — they believed — of everyone.

Notice what this single word does to the emotional temperature. These are not lazy men. These are not procrastinators. These are *early risers*. Disciplined. Motivated. They are doing what we praise people for doing — getting up before the world, executing the plan, seizing the morning.

[PAUSE]

And now feel the irony Allah has loaded into it.

They rose early — to arrive at something that was *already over*. Their eagerness was real; their timing was a tragedy. All that dawn energy, all that being-ahead-of-the-day — it was carrying them at speed toward ash. The earlier they rose, the faster they reached their grief.

Here is what I want you to sit with. **There is a kind of diligence that is simply efficient travel toward the wrong destination.** The word *ghadaw* is the Quran's gentle, devastating observation that being *early* and being *energetic* and being *disciplined* guarantees nothing — if the thing you are rushing toward has already been decided somewhere you cannot see. They were not punished for sleeping in. They were punished while doing everything "right" by the world's measure. Their alarm went off; their plan was crisp; their bodies were ahead of the sun.

And it did not matter at all.

Stay with that discomfort. We will come back to it. For now, one statement: *the verse opens with the posture of a person fully in command of their morning — precisely so that the rest of the verse can take that command away.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: حَرْدٍ — The Word That Means Three Things at Once

Now to the heart of the verse. The strange word in the middle: **حَرْدٍ — *ḥard.***

First, let me teach you something about how the Quran sometimes works, because this word is a perfect example of it. Most of the time a word has one core meaning and some shades around it. But occasionally the Quran reaches for a word whose *several meanings are all true at once*, and the richness is the point. The classical commentators, when they reach such a word, don't argue "it means A, not B." They lay the meanings side by side, like several camera angles on one scene. Let me show you why this word demands exactly that.

Al-Baghawī, gathering the early authorities, tells us plainly: *"al-ḥard fī al-lugha yakūn bi-maʿnā al-qaṣd wa-l-manʿ wa-l-ghaḍab"* — **in the language, *ḥard* carries the meaning of (1) aim/purpose, (2) withholding/prevention, and (3) anger.** Three meanings. Let's take them one at a time, and watch them fuse.

**First: *qaṣd* — aim, intent, resolve.** Al-Qurṭubī anchors this firmly. He reports from Ibn ʿAbbās and others: *al-ḥard al-qaṣd* — *ḥard* is purpose, direction, the setting of one's face toward a goal. The Arabs said *ḥaradtu ḥardak* — "I aimed at your aim, I intended your intention." Qurṭubī even quotes the old line of rajaz poetry about a flood that came *yaḥrudu ḥarda l-jannati l-mughilla* — rushing **straight toward** the fruitful garden, deliberate, targeted, undeflected. So the first layer of *ḥard* is: **deliberate, hardened resolve.** They went out *on purpose*, with their whole will pointed in one direction.

**Second: *manʿ* — withholding, prevention.** This is the meaning al-Jalālayn reaches for first: *manʿ li-l-fuqarāʾ* — "a withholding from the poor." Al-Muyassar spells out the same: they went *ʿalā qaṣdihim al-sayyiʾ fī manʿ al-masākīn* — "upon their evil purpose of *barring the needy*" from the fruit. So *ḥard* is not just resolve in the abstract — it is resolve *with a specific cruel content*: the determination **to keep the poor out.** The Arabs used the same root for a year with no rain (*ḥāradat al-sanah*) and a camel that gives no milk (*ḥāradat al-nāqah*) — a withholding, a closing-off of what should have flowed. The word itself is a clenched fist.

**Third: *ghaḍab* — anger, resentment.** Underneath the resolve and the withholding sits a heat. A grievance. *Why should our wealth leak away to others?* The withholding is not cold accounting alone; it is animated by a kind of resentment toward the very existence of the poor man's claim.

[PAUSE]

Now — do you see what Allah has done? He did not choose three words. He chose **one** word, *ḥard*, and let all three meanings collapse into a single sound. Because **these are not three separate things. They are one thing seen from three sides.** A hardened *resolve* (qaṣd) — to *withhold* (manʿ) — driven by *resentment* (ghaḍab). That is the complete anatomy of stinginess. Resolve gives it spine. Withholding gives it action. Resentment gives it fuel.

And feel the *sound* of the word. *Ḥard.* It begins with the constricted, breathy ḥāʾ — a sound made by squeezing the throat. It rolls through the rāʾ. And it slams shut on the dāl — a hard stop, a door closing. The word *sounds like* what it means: something tightened, then sealed. Set it next to the open, flowing *ghadaw* that came before it. They went out *flowing* with morning eagerness — *toward a clenched, sealed-shut purpose.* The very music of the verse moves from open to closed, from dawn-light to a fist.

One statement to carry: *ḥard* is the Quran's single-syllable X-ray of the miserly heart — resolve, withholding, and resentment fused into one closed shape.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: قَادِرِينَ — The Word That Breaks

And now the last word of the verse, the word everything has been built to deliver: **قَادِرِينَ — *qādirīn.* "Able. Capable. In power."**

Let me first show you what it is, grammatically, because the grammar is doing quiet, lethal work.

*Qādirīn* is an active participle from the root *q-d-r* (قدر) — the root of *qudra*, **power and ability**, and of *qadar*, **divine decree**. Hold that doubling in your mind; we will need it. As a participle in the accusative, *qādirīn* functions here as a *ḥāl* — a **circumstantial description**. In English we'd render the structure: "they set out… *as ones who were able*," or "*in the state of believing themselves able.*" It describes the *condition* in which they marched. Their inner posture. Their self-image as they walked.

Now — here is the absence I need you to see, because it speaks louder than any word present.

In Arabic, *qādir* — "able" — almost always wants an object. Able **at what?** Able **upon what?** The natural construction is *qādirīn ʿalā kadhā* — "able to do such-and-such." But Allah leaves the object **unspecified.** The verse ends on "able" — and then simply stops. No object. The sentence's ability has nothing attached to it.

The commentators feel this gap and rush to fill it — and watch *how* they fill it. Al-Jalālayn: *qādirīn ʿalayhi fī ẓannihim* — "able to do it **in their estimation.**" Al-Muyassar: *fī ghāyat al-qudrah ʿalā tanfīdhihi fī zaʿmihim* — "at the very height of power to execute it **in their own claim.**" Al-Qurṭubī: *yaẓunnūna annahum tamakkanū min murādihim* — "**thinking** they had secured what they wanted."

Do you hear the word that every single commentator adds? *In their estimation. In their claim. They thought. They presumed.* The grammar leaves "able" hanging with no real-world object — and the mufassirūn tell us why: **because the object existed only in their heads.** They were able *in their imagination*. The "ability" had no anchor in reality, because reality — the garden — was already ash. The empty object-slot in the Arabic is *exactly* the empty space where their power actually was: nowhere.

[PAUSE]

Now bring back the doubling I asked you to hold. The root *q-d-r* gives both *qudra* — human ability — and *qadar* — divine decree. This is not a coincidence the Arabic language stumbled into; it is a window. **Human "ability" and divine "decree" are spelled with the same letters — and in this story, they meet head-on.** The brothers march out clothed in *qudra*, "we are able," while the only true *Qādir* — the only One with real power — has already issued His *qadar* over the garden in the night. Their lowercase ability is walking, chest out, straight into the path of the uppercase Power that has already moved.

And the word does not just sit there — *it shatters in the next verse.* The instant verse 25 ends on "**able**," verse 26 opens: *falammā raʾawhā qālū innā la-ḍāllūn* — "and when they **saw** it, they said, *we are surely lost.*" From *qādirīn* to *ḍāllūn* — from "we are able" to "we are ruined" — in the space between two verses. Allah let them finish the sentence "we are in control" and did not even give them a new paragraph before reality arrived. The proximity *is* the message. **The distance between "I've got this" and "I've lost everything" is exactly one bend in the road.**

One statement to carry: the verse hands them the word *able* with no object — because their ability was an opinion they held about themselves, and reality had already voted against it.

---

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words, in sequence, have built. *Ghadaw* — they went out diligent, early, ahead of the day. *ʿAlā ḥard* — carrying a resolve that was, at its core, a clenched determination to withhold. *Qādirīn* — wrapped in the warm certainty that they were the ones in control.

Diligence. Resolve. Confidence. By every worldly metric: an effective team executing a clear plan with discipline.

And every word of it was hollow, because the one fact that mattered lived in a place none of their planning could touch.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about an ancient word, but about reality itself, and about you.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Illusion of Control

Let me start where you live.

You have a phone full of plans. A calendar. Maybe a project at work with a Gantt chart, dependencies, a launch date. You have, somewhere in you, the quiet assumption that *if you plan well enough, the future is yours.* Psychologists even have a name for the way we systematically overrate this: the **illusion of control** — our deep, persistent tendency to believe we have more command over outcomes than we actually do. We feel more confident about a coin we flip ourselves. We feel safer driving than flying, though the numbers say otherwise, because *we* are holding the wheel. The hand on the wheel feels like power.

The brothers had their hands on the wheel. Their plan was, honestly, *good*. Harvest at dawn — sound. Do it quietly — strategic. Coordinate the night before — disciplined. Beat the poor to the fruit — efficient, by their cold logic. If you handed their plan to a consultant, the consultant would approve it. There is no flaw in the *execution.*

The flaw is in the **frame.** They built a flawless plan inside a world they did not actually run.

[PAUSE]

This is the thing the verse exposes with surgical precision. It does *not* show us bunglers. It shows us competent, confident planners — *and then quietly notes that the outcome had already been written in the dark, while they slept.* The *ṭāʾif* circled the garden *while they were sleeping* — meaning the decisive event of the entire story happened in the **one window where they had zero input.** Not while they argued. Not while they negotiated. While they were *unconscious.* The most important thing that happened to their wealth happened at the exact moment they were doing nothing at all.

And isn't that the shape of so much of life? The diagnosis that was already growing while you planned the year. The market that turned while you slept. The phone call that was already coming while you rehearsed a different conversation. We make our plans in the daylight of our own minds, and reality is busy in a night we cannot see into.

Now — hear me carefully, because the Quran is not telling you to stop planning. The man who *built* the garden planted trees; that is planning too. The lesson is not "don't make plans." The lesson is sealed in what the brothers *failed to say* a few verses earlier. They swore to harvest tomorrow **without saying *in shā' Allah*.** And the Prophet ﷺ was taught, in a related teaching of the Qur'an, never to say "I will do that tomorrow" except that you add *"in shā' Allah"* — *if God wills* (18:23–24).

**"In shā' Allah" is not a verbal tic. It is the correct frame.** It is the difference between a person who says "I will harvest tomorrow" — claiming a tomorrow they do not own — and a person who says "I will harvest tomorrow, *if Allah wills it*" — holding their plan in an open hand, acknowledging the One in whose grip the night actually rests. The brothers' sin was not their alarm clock. It was their grammar: they conjugated the future in the first person, as if it were theirs to conjugate.

[PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow morning?

Not your diligence. Rise early. Make the plan. Execute it well — *ghaduww* is not the enemy. What changes is the **silent clause** under every plan. You wake, you look at your calendar, and somewhere beneath it you say: *this is my intention; the outcome is His.* You hold the wheel — and you remember Whose road it is. That single inner sentence is the entire difference between the father, who planted and gave and trusted, and the sons, who planted nothing, gripped everything, and lost it all by morning.

The core insight: **you are responsible for the resolve; you were never the owner of the result.** Plan in the daylight. Bow to the night you cannot see.

### Theme Two: The Hand That Closes

Now let me take you to the second thing buried in this verse — buried, specifically, in the word *ḥard*, that clenched fist of a word.

Remember what *ḥard* was: resolve + withholding + resentment. The brothers' entire energy was organized around **keeping someone out.** And I want to ask a question that sounds almost too simple: *why did this, of all sins, bring the whole garden down overnight?*

They didn't worship an idol in the story. They didn't murder. Their crime, stated plainly, was: **they decided not to share the fruit with the poor.** And for *that*, the garden was reduced to ash before sunrise. Why does the Quran treat the closing of a hand as something so seismic?

Here is the door in. Let me ask you about your own sense of what you *own.*

When provision comes to you — the salary, the bonus, the harvest of your particular effort — there is a voice that says, very reasonably: *this is mine. I earned it. I rose early; I did the work; the fruit is the wage of my labor.* That voice feels like simple justice. And the brothers had that voice, turned up loud. The garden was *theirs.* The fruit was *theirs.* Why should it leak to people who did not plant it?

[PAUSE]

But watch what the story has already told you, and what *ḥard* quietly exposes. The garden was not the *product* of the brothers at all. **They inherited it.** They did not plant those trees. They did not coax the first harvest from the soil. They woke up one day as owners of a green, fruiting blessing they had done nothing to create. The "wage of my labor" story was, from the very beginning, a lie they told themselves. The garden was a *gift* — and a gift that had *always*, under the father, carried a built-in portion for the poor. The poor man's share was not charity the father invented. It was a clause that came *with* the garden, part of its very deed.

So when the sons resolved to withhold — *ḥard* — they were not protecting "what they earned." They were **amputating a clause from a gift they were only renting.** They treated a trust as a possession, and then locked the door of the possession against the very people the trust was meant to reach.

And this is why the closing of the hand is seismic. Because **stinginess is, at root, a false theology.** To clench against the poor is to declare: *this came from me, flows to me, and stops where I say it stops.* It is to put yourself at the source of the river instead of somewhere along its middle. The miser is not merely uncharitable; he is *confused about where he stands in the universe.* He thinks he is the wellspring. He is only a channel — and a channel that dams itself does not become a lake. It becomes a swamp. Or, in this story, ash.

[PAUSE]

Feel how the two themes are actually one. The brothers said, in effect, two things at once: *"the future is mine"* (no *in shā' Allah*) and *"the wealth is mine"* (the *ḥard*, the withholding). Both are the same error wearing two coats — **the error of imagining yourself an owner where you are only a steward.** Owner of time; owner of fruit. And the verse strips both coats off in four words: they *went out* (as if they owned the morning), *withholding* (as if they owned the fruit), *able* (as if they owned the power) — and reality, which actually owned all three, was waiting around the bend.

So what changes tomorrow?

Something concrete. Look at the next portion of provision that comes to you and find, deliberately, **the poor man's clause inside it.** It is there — the Quran says it elsewhere with startling directness: *"in their wealth was a known right for the one who asks and the one deprived"* (cf. 70:24–25). Not a leftover. Not an optional kindness from your surplus. A *known right*, built into the deed of the gift, the way the poor's portion was built into that garden from the father's first harvest. To give it is not generosity flowing *down* from you to them. It is **delivery** — handing the rightful owner their parcel, which merely passed through your hands on the way.

The core insight: **the open hand is not weakness leaking out; it is honesty about where the wealth was always going.** The clenched hand — *ḥard* — does not preserve the garden. In this story, and in the deepest law of things, it is the surest way to lose it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that we've done the work, let me show you the shape of the thing, because the passage is built with terrible precision, and verse 25 is its hinge.

Trace the arc. They **swore** to harvest and shut out the poor (17–18) — and refused to say *in shā' Allah.* Then, in the dark, *while they slept*, **a visitation from their Lord** passed over the garden and left it like a field cut to the stubble (19–20). They **woke and called** to one another at first light (21–22). They **set off whispering**, "no poor man enters today" (23–24). And then — the summit — **they went out, resolved, *able*** (25).

That is the inhale. Verse 25 is the held breath at the top of the climb.

Then the exhale, immediate and total: *"and when they saw it"* (26) — *"we are lost"* (26) — *"no, we are deprived"* (27). And the just one among them asks the question that breaks the whole story open: *"Did I not say to you — why do you not glorify [Allah]?"* (28). The structure is a mountain: every verse climbing toward "**able**," and then the sheer cliff on the other side. Allah placed *qādirīn* — "in control" — at the *exact* peak, so that the fall would have the longest possible distance to travel. The architecture *is* the lesson: **the higher the certainty of "I am able," the steeper the drop to "I am lost."**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are one truth approached from two roads.

The words showed us: *ghadaw* (the diligence that guarantees nothing), *ḥard* (the clenched fist of withholding), *qādirīn* (the ability with no object, because the object lived only in their heads).

The themes showed us: the *illusion of control* (we are not the owners of outcomes) and the *closed hand* (we are not the owners of wealth).

And both roads arrive at a single place: **these brothers mistook stewardship for ownership** — of their time, their power, and their fruit — and the verse lets them stand at the very peak of that mistake, "able," for one frozen instant, before reality, which owned all of it, came around the bend.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. Where in my life am I saying "I will do this tomorrow" with the silent, unspoken assumption that tomorrow is *mine* — never adding, even in my heart, *in shā' Allah*?

2. When provision comes to me, which story do I tell myself first: *"I earned this"* — or *"this passed through me"*? Where did I plant the trees, and where did I merely inherit the garden?

3. Is there a clenched hand somewhere in my life right now — a *ḥard* — a resolve to withhold that I have dressed up as prudence or as justice?

4. When was the last time I felt completely *qādir* — completely on top of things, in full control? And what was happening, in the dark, that I could not see?

5. If a visitation passed over *my* garden tonight, would I, like the just brother, already have been the one saying *"why do you not glorify Him?"* — or would I be among those who only remembered Him *after* the loss?

### One-Sentence Distillation

They rose early, hands clenched against the poor and hearts certain of their own power — never knowing that the only One with real power had already decided, in a night they slept through, that the garden was no longer theirs.

### The Closing Invitation

I am not asking you to stop planning, stop working, stop rising early. The father in this story planned and planted and rose early too — and he is the *hero*. The invitation is smaller and harder than that. It is to add one silent clause beneath every plan and one open seam in every closed hand: *the morning is His; the fruit was always partly theirs.* Live as the father lived — diligent and giving and unclenched — and not as the sons, who had everything the father had except the one thing that mattered: the knowledge of where they actually stood.

### A Du'a

O Allah, You are *al-Qādir* — the only One truly able — and we are channels of a provision we did not create. Loosen the *ḥard* in our hands before the dawn ever finds it there; let us be among those who glorify You *before* the loss and not only after it. And do not let us be the ones who set out one bright morning, certain we were able, toward a garden You had already taken back.

Āmīn.
