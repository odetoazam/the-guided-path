# The Whisper Before the Ruin

## Introduction

---

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ

**فَتَنَادَوْا۟ مُصْبِحِينَ**
**أَنِ ٱغْدُوا۟ عَلَىٰ حَرْثِكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰرِمِينَ**
**فَٱنطَلَقُوا۟ وَهُمْ يَتَخَافَتُونَ**
**أَن لَّا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا ٱلْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُم مِّسْكِينٌ**

*Fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīn — An igh-dū ʿalā ḥarthikum in kuntum ṣārimīn — Fanṭalaqū wa-hum yatakhāfatūn — An lā yadkhulannahā al-yawma ʿalaykum miskīn.*

"And they called one another at morning — 'Go early to your harvest, if you are going to cut it.' And they set out, while they were whispering to one another: 'Let no poor person enter upon you today.'"

---

There is a moment in these ayahs — one of the most cinematically precise moments in all of Quranic narrative — where four people are walking.

They are walking with purpose. They are organised, mobilised, moving together. They've called each other at dawn. They've set out. They're whispering the final arrangements among themselves.

And they have no idea they are walking toward ash.

[PAUSE]

To feel the weight of what these four ayahs are doing, we need to understand where we are in Surah Al-Qalam — and what was happening in Mecca at the moment these words descended.

Surah Al-Qalam is among the earliest revelations. The Prophet ﷺ had barely begun to speak when Mecca pushed back — not with arguments, but with a label. *He is mad.* That was their strategy: if they could attach the word *majnūn* to him, the message would never find a listener. The label does the work of silencing without ever engaging the content.

Allah opens this surah with something extraordinary — a divine oath by the pen itself, by the ink that flows, by the act of writing: *Nūn. By the pen and what they inscribe.* Then immediately: *You are not, by the favour of your Lord, a madman.* The surah begins as a defence. A vindication. A declaration that the one they call mad is the one who carries the highest character.

But then — and this is where the surah shifts registers — Allah doesn't just defend. He challenges. He turns to those making the accusation and says, in effect: *You want to talk about what kind of person is truly lost? Let me show you a mirror.*

The parable of the Garden Owners is that mirror.

---

A family inherited a garden from their righteous father. This detail matters — the father gave to the poor not because the law compelled him, but because he understood something about the nature of what he held. Wealth, in the Quran's theology, is never purely private property. It passes through your hands. The poor have a portion embedded within it before you ever touch it — وَفِي أَمْوَالِهِمْ حَقٌّ لِّلسَّائِلِ وَالْمَحْرُومِ — in their wealth is a known right for the one who asks and the one who is denied (51:19). The father knew this. He gave accordingly.

When he died, his children looked at each other and made a calculation. *Why share what is ours?* They consulted. They agreed. They took an oath — the Quran uses the word *aqsamū*, from the root of swearing, the kind of oath that names itself seriously, that commits — they swore they would harvest the garden **muṣbiḥīn**: at first light, at dawn, while the poor were still sleeping, before a single beggar could arrive at the gate.

That is 68:18.

Then three verses happen. Three verses that change everything.

In 68:19: *and a circling visitor (ṭāʾif) from your Lord came around it while they were sleeping.* A night visitor — from your Lord. The divine agency is named explicitly. Something came in the night, something sent, something purposeful. While they slept.

In 68:20: *and it became like al-ṣarīm.* The garden entered morning as something already cut, already scorched, already stripped to nothing.

The garden is gone. The garden was visited in the night and left as ruins.

**And they don't know.**

This is where we enter. Ayahs 21 through 24. Dawn. Four people waking up. Calling each other. Setting out. Walking in close formation. Whispering the final plan.

Toward a garden that is already ash.

[PAUSE]

In four ayahs, Allah shows us something we rarely get to see with this kind of precision: the full anatomy of organised miserliness. Not just greed as a private feeling, but greed as a collective project — how it recruits, how it mobilises, how it whispers, and who it names as its target. And all of it running — perfectly, on schedule, with complete confidence — into the moment of its own devastation.

Let's go in carefully.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

---

### Element One — The Form of Mutual Calling

The passage begins with one word:

**فَتَنَادَوْا۟**

*Fa-tanādaw.*

Say it aloud. Notice how it opens outward — the long vowels, the reaching quality of the sound. *Tah. Naa. Daw.* Something expansive is happening in these syllables. That is not accidental.

Before we touch this word, let me show you a structure in Arabic that you need to understand — because once you see it, you'll find it everywhere in the Quran and it will never stop arresting you.

In Arabic grammar, verbs are constructed according to ten primary patterns, called *awzān* — scales or forms. Form I is the baseline: simple, direct action. *He went. She called. They saw.* One person doing something.

Form VI takes the same root and wraps it in a different frame entirely. Its pattern is *tafāʿala* — and what it adds to the verb is **mutuality**. Reciprocity. The action is happening not from one person outward, but *between* people, back and forth, each doing it to the other. It describes a shared, networked action — an action that circulates within a group.

Think about the difference between "they talked" and "they talked *to each other*, going around, each picking up where the last one left off, passing the conversation between them." Form VI is the second one. It implies multiple participants, all active, all engaged in the same direction, all weaving the action between themselves.

**تَنَادَوْاْ** is Form VI of the root *n-d-w* (ن-د-و) — to call, to summon, to cry out. So *tanādaw* doesn't mean one person called. It doesn't mean a voice rang out. It means **they called each other** — each one calling the next, the summons travelling through the group, everyone both calling and being called.

Al-Qurtubi confirms this reading plainly: *"nādā baʿḍuhum baʿḍan"* — each one called the other. Al-Baghawi says the same: when morning came, they called one another. Al-Muyassar adds the content that was being passed through these calls: go early, go to the harvest, go now.

[PAUSE]

But sit with the Form VI for a moment before we move on. Because it's doing more than describing an action.

It is describing a **structure**.

This family did not make a private decision acted upon in silence. They built a network around their decision. Ahmad called Khalid, who called his brother, who called the fourth. Each voice activated the next voice. And with each activation, something happened — each person receiving the call received with it something that looked like permission. *You agree? So do I. We're doing this together.* The mutual call is the mechanism by which a private temptation becomes a collective institution.

This is the anatomy of organised sin. Not the lone miser counting coins in a room — that person at least has to face their conscience alone. But a group that calls each other, mobilises together, reinforces each other — they create a social legitimacy that the conscience of any one person could never supply alone. The Form VI is the signal: *this is what greed looks like when it scales.*

We know this pattern. We see it around us and within us.

A family gathering that begins with a quiet side conversation — *you agree we're not telling so-and-so about the inheritance until after?* — and by the time the family sits down together, the private arrangement has become a shared policy that feels almost official. A workplace culture where senior people whisper before the public meeting to make sure certain voices won't be heard in the room. A community that zones its neighbourhoods in ways that quietly, systematically keep the struggling out — calling each other to meetings, building consensus, creating the kind of collective momentum that makes individual complicity feel like simple participation.

*Tanādaw.* They called each other. It is the first move. The first step in every architecture of shared exclusion.

And now — notice the word that ends this ayah. Just one more word, and it stops everything.

### Element Two — The Word That Echoes

**مُصْبِحِينَ**

*Muṣbiḥīn.* At morning.

This will look like simple time-stamping if you're not reading carefully. But there is an echo in this word that, once you hear it, you cannot unhear.

Go back three ayahs. Go back to 68:18, where the oath was made:

*إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا **مُصْبِحِينَ***

*"When they swore they would harvest it — muṣbiḥīn."* At morning.

The exact same word. The exact same grammatical form. In the oath: *muṣbiḥīn.* In the execution: *muṣbiḥīn.*

Allah is mirroring back to us — word for word — the plan and its execution. They said they would do it at morning. And they are doing it at morning. The conspiracy ran without error. Every detail of the plan was honoured.

[PAUSE]

Let me show you what مُصْبِحِينَ actually means beneath the translation.

The root is ص-ب-ح — *ṣ-b-ḥ* — carrying the meaning of morning, of dawn, of something entering daylight. The Form IV pattern (indicated by the *mu-* prefix — a Form IV active participle) creates the meaning: *one who enters morning.* The plural *muṣbiḥīn* is being used here as a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial marker in Arabic grammar, describing the condition in which the action takes place. "They called each other, *[as they were] entering morning*."

Form IV of this root is not causative here. It is what we might call temporal-denominative — it places the actors at the threshold. Not yet in full day. At the liminal edge of dawn. The moment of maximum fresh confidence, when sleep has cleared and the plan is still intact and the day ahead still feels full of possibility.

And between the oath (*muṣbiḥīn*, 68:18) and the execution (*muṣbiḥīn*, 68:21), three ayahs passed. Three ayahs in which a night visitor came. Three ayahs in which the garden became ash.

The echo of the word is the Quran's way of saying: human precision ran into divine action and didn't know it. They planned at dawn. They executed at dawn. The universe moved in the space between, while they slept.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer here that requires you to hold 68:20 alongside 68:21. In 68:20, the garden's destruction is described like this: *fa-aṣbaḥat ka-l-ṣarīm* — it entered morning like al-ṣarīm. Al-ṣarīm: something cut, stripped, harvested out, rendered dark and empty. A field after everything has been taken.

And in 68:22, the owners describe themselves as *ṣārimīn* — those who cut, those who harvest. They have named themselves the cutters.

The garden entered morning already cut. Not by them. By Allah.

They called themselves the ṣārimīn. They are racing at dawn to do the cutting. And Allah, quietly, in the night, in the space covered by those three intervening ayahs, was the ṣārim first. The garden is already ṣarīm — already cut — before the ṣārimīn arrive.

This is not poetic coincidence. This is the Quran using root-repetition as theological argument. *The thing you were planning to do — I already did. Not in the way you intended. But done.*

### Element Three — From the Call to the Whisper

Now something happens in these four ayahs that deserves its own attention — because it is a movement, a descent, a narrowing.

We've seen Form VI at full volume: **تَنَادَوْاْ** — they called each other, loud, expansive, mobilising, the dawn call going through the house.

Then they set out.

And here is the shift — listen:

**فَٱنطَلَقُوا۟ وَهُمْ يَتَخَافَتُونَ**

*Fanṭalaqū wa-hum yatakhāfatūn.* They set out — while they were *yatakhāfatūn.*

**يَتَخَافَتُونَ**

Say this quietly. As quietly as you can manage.

Notice how the sounds themselves refuse to open. The *kh* (خ) is deep in the throat, guttural, suppressed. The *f* (ف) is a whisper of breath forced through teeth. The *t* (ت) is a muffled tap. Put them together: خَفَتَ — the root means to press the voice down, to make it barely audible, to swallow the sound. The word *sounds* like whispering.

And look at its form. *Ya-ta-khā-fa-tūn.* That *ya-ta-* prefix with the long *ā* between the first and second radicals — from broader classical Arabic morphology, this is Form VI: *tafāʿala.* The same form as *tanādaw*. Mutual. Reciprocal. They are not just each whispering privately — they are **whispering to each other**, the secret circulating between them in hushed tones, back and forth.

Form VI at full volume. Then Form VI at a whisper.

[PAUSE]

This arc — from the loud mutual call to the hushed mutual whisper — is the Quran mapping the two movements of every organised conspiracy.

The loud call is the recruitment phase. You mobilise openly because mobilisation requires other people to hear you. But then, once the group is moving, the volume drops — because what you're protecting now is not the mobilisation. It's the secret. The whisper is the pivot from *organising for ourselves* to *protecting against others*.

And who is the other? What is the whispered secret? Ayah 24 tells us:

**أَن لَّا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا ٱلْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُم مِّسْكِينٌ**

*"That no poor person should enter it upon you today."*

Now look at the grammar here.

*Lā yadkhulannahā.* The particle *lā* followed by the verb marked with an emphatic *nūn* at the end — *yad-khu-LAN-na-hā*. In Arabic, this construction signals an emphatic, absolute prohibition. Not a hesitant no. Not a preference. An emphatic, categorical — *this must absolutely not happen. Under no circumstances. This will not occur.* The *nūn* of emphasis is doing real grammatical work: it is underscoring the certainty of this instruction.

The object of all that emphatic certainty?

One word.

**مِّسْكِينٌ.**

A poor person. One. Unnamed. Just: a poor person.

[PAUSE — sit here]

The word *miskīn* comes from the root *s-k-n* — stillness, settling, immobility. To be a *miskīn* is to be in a condition where poverty has *stilled* you. Wealth moves — it buys passage, it opens doors, it creates motion through the world. Poverty stops that motion. A *miskīn* is the one whose lack has rendered them still, immobile, unable to move freely through the life that wealth flows through with ease. The Arabic language has built an entire theological meaning into this word: poverty as a kind of imposed stillness on a person who would otherwise move.

And the emphatic prohibition is: *this still person must not enter.*

Now consider the whole architecture of what these four ayahs have shown us.

An oath was taken. A house was awoken. Mutual calls went through the family at dawn — Form VI, the network activated, everyone calling and being called. A march was begun. And then, in close formation, walking together toward the gate, a whisper passed between them — Form VI again, but now suppressed, muffled, the secret turning in on itself — and the whisper said: *the still one. The poor one. Not today. Not this gate. Not one of them.*

The whole machine — the oath, the dawn, the mutual call, the early departure, the organised march — it all narrows, finally, to this: **keeping one immobilised person out of a garden.**

That is what miserliness, at its most organised, is doing. All that infrastructure. All that planning. All that coordination. To protect a gate from one person who cannot even move freely.

And they are whispering it — because somewhere in the architecture of who they are, they know this is not a thing that can be said in daylight. A thing whispered is a thing that knows it cannot withstand examination.

They are whispering it as they walk toward a garden that is already ash.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

---

### Theme One — The Community Sin Builds

There is a version of this story the Quran could have told. The lone miser. One person, privately counting, deciding privately, acting alone. That story exists — it's a real spiritual condition and the Quran addresses it elsewhere.

But it didn't tell that story here.

Here: a family. Multiple people. Each calling each other. Walking together. Whispering together. Moving as a unit.

Why? Because there is something the Quran understands about collective sin that needs to be named.

[PAUSE]

When greed stays private, there are natural internal resistances. The conscience speaks in the quiet. The memory of the father — who used to give, who understood this garden was never purely his — is still there, pressing. The lone miser at least has to silence that internal voice alone, and that voice does not silence easily.

But when greed becomes collective, something else happens. The voices reinforce each other. Ahmad calls Khalid, and Khalid's response — *yes, let's go* — is experienced by Ahmad as something close to validation. *You agree? Then I'm not wrong.* Khalid's call back to the third brother carries the same social signal. By the time they're all walking together, each person has received the others' agreement as a kind of permission — a moral endorsement from the group. The collective creates a legitimacy that no one conscience inside the group supplied.

This is why *tanādaw* — Form VI, mutual calling — is the first verb of these four ayahs. It is not just a grammatical observation. It is a theological diagnosis of how organised miserliness works. The mutual call is the mechanism.

We recognise this pattern when we look for it honestly.

A neighbourhood that quietly agrees to resist certain kinds of rezoning — *we're just protecting property values* — and the consensus circulates through the homeowners' association until what began as the reluctance of one or two people becomes the official position of a community, and every individual can point to the group and say *it's not just me*. A family that makes a private arrangement before a gathering — hushed calls between siblings, the terms agreed before anyone sits down — so that by the time the meeting happens, the decision has already been made and what follows is theatre. An industry that whispers its labour policies before the press release so that every executive is already aligned and the alignment itself is mistaken for rightness.

*Tanādaw.* They called each other. And each call was the next person's permission to proceed.

[PAUSE]

And now the whisper.

The shift from taNĀdaw (loud) to yatakhāfatūn (suppressed) is the shift from the inside of the group to the edge where the group meets the world. They called each other loudly to mobilise. They whisper now to protect the secret from reaching anyone who shouldn't hear it.

This distinction matters.

The loud call says: *we are doing this together.* It is the language of shared identity.
The whisper says: *not this person. Not today.* It is the language of shared exclusion.

Collective sin has two registers, always. The register it uses internally — open, reinforcing, building solidarity. And the register it uses about its excluded — quiet, careful, muffled. The group speaks freely to itself and in whispers about who doesn't belong.

And the content of the whisper, when it's finally said plainly in 68:24, is the most clarifying sentence in the whole parable. Not *let's be efficient with the harvest.* Not *let's process this before the market opens.* The whisper names a specific person. A *miskīn*. The category of person whose embedded right in any wealth is one of the Quran's most persistent theological insistences. 

The Quran returns to the rights of the *miskīn* and the *sāʾil* across dozens of surahs. It is not a peripheral concern. It is structural to the Quran's understanding of property. When Allah establishes that in people's wealth there is a *ḥaqq maʿlūm* — a known right — for the asker and the deprived (51:19), He is not making a charitable suggestion. He is describing a property right that preceded the owner's possession.

The father understood this. The garden was given to him in trust, and he kept the trust by honouring that embedded right. When he died and his children inherited, they inherited a garden that still had that right in it. They didn't inherit the portion that belonged to the *miskīn*. They inherited the garden around it.

And the whisper in 68:24 is the moment where they reveal that they know — somewhere, in some register below full consciousness — that what they are doing is appropriating someone else's portion. You whisper what you know cannot be said in the open. The whisper is the evidence of a partially intact conscience.

[PAUSE]

They whispered it. They walked toward the gate. And the gate, when it opened, would reveal something no whisper had accounted for.

### Theme Two — Confidence Walking Toward What Is Already Gone

Here is something the Quran gives us in these four ayahs that is almost unbearable to sit with once you see it.

We — the listeners — know two things at once.

We heard 68:19: the night visitor came. We heard 68:20: the garden became ash. We know what happened to the garden while they slept. And now we watch — four people wake up at dawn, call each other with confidence, march out together, whisper their final instructions, and walk toward the gate.

They are running the plan in perfect form. The plan is running toward ruins.

[PAUSE]

I want to ask you to stay with that image before moving on. Because it is not just a story about people long ago. It is a mirror.

Think about the last time you couldn't let a plan go — even after the situation that made the plan sensible had dissolved. The argument you kept making after the relationship it was part of had already ended. The career path you kept working, kept preparing for, after every signal in the industry said the path was closing. The project you kept optimising after the problem it solved had been superseded. We map reality with plans, and we are deeply attached to our maps — more than we often know. When the territory shifts underneath us, the map doesn't always update. We keep navigating by what we knew when we made the map.

These four people are the archetype of that experience.

They made a plan when a garden existed. The garden stopped existing — in the night, while they slept, while they were not looking. The plan continued anyway. Dawn came, and the plan activated — on schedule, with precision, each step executed correctly — into its own ruins.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something about Allah's presence in these four ayahs.

In 68:19: *a ṭāʾif from your Lord came.* Allah is named.
In 68:20: *it became like al-ṣarīm.* The passive construction implies divine agency.

And then — in 68:21. 68:22. 68:23. 68:24.

No mention of Allah. No divine action. No interruption. Just four people walking.

This is not because Allah is absent. It is because Allah has already acted. The garden was visited in the night. The action is done. And the most devastating thing that can happen now is nothing — just let them walk.

Classical Islamic scholarship identifies a concept relevant to this moment: *istidraj* — the gradual leading-forward that precedes a reckoning. The granting of space to proceed. Not approval. Not indifference. The allowing of someone to move forward in their wrong until reality, fully assembled, corrects them completely.

In 68:21-24, we are watching *istidraj* in slow motion. Allah has withdrawn from the narration. These four ayahs belong entirely to the humans — their call, their march, their whisper, their plans. They own these ayahs. And every step they take toward the gate makes the moment of arrival more total, more complete, more impossible to walk back from.

[PAUSE]

There is something in this that speaks to a pattern we can recognise in our own experience.

The times in life when we were most confident in our plans were sometimes the times most removed from an accurate reading of reality. Confidence is not the same thing as correctness. A deep sense of certainty about the rightness of a direction can coexist with a complete misreading of what is actually there. The feelings of *we've got this, we're ready, we've planned, we've called each other and we're moving* — these are real feelings. They are not automatically connected to truth.

And the Quran, in these four ayahs, gives us the image of this with startling vividness. The call was real. The march was real. The whisper was real. The confidence was real. The garden was ash.

[PAUSE — major pause]

There is one more thing to say about the whisper itself — about what they whispered and what that whisper cost them.

The *miskīn* they named — the poor person at the gate they were racing to exclude — was not incidental to the disaster. The Quran's structure is pointing us somewhere.

In 68:28, when the disaster has been discovered and one of their number speaks, he says: *Did I not say to you, why don't you declare your Lord's glory?* The one voice among them who had doubts. The one who might have questioned the plan.

And in 68:32-33: *Perhaps our Lord will substitute for us something better than it... Such is the punishment, and the punishment of the Hereafter is greater, if they only knew.*

The structure of the parable moves from the excluded *miskīn* to the destroyed garden to the recognition that a share was owed and wasn't given.

When you cut the poor person's portion from the plan — the plan has a way of cutting itself.

Not as a magical transaction. Not as a formula. But because the plan was already built on a misunderstanding of what was owned. They didn't own the *miskīn*'s portion. They never did. The father gave it not as a gift but as a rendering — returning what was embedded in the wealth to its prior claimant. When the children tried to protect what was never fully theirs, they lost everything — including the part that was.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture of the Four Ayahs

Now that we've moved through the language and the themes, let me show you how these four ayahs are structured — because seeing the structure reveals what they're doing together.

**Loud → Quiet**
The passage begins with Form VI at full volume: *tanādaw* — they call each other expansively. It ends with Form VI suppressed: *yatakhāfatūn* — they whisper to each other.

**Outside → Inside**
The call goes outward, through the household, activating everyone. The whisper turns inward, the secret circulating within the group, protected from anyone outside.

**General → Specific**
They call each other to go to the harvest — general mobilisation. They whisper about a specific person, a specific category, a specific gate, a specific day.

**Then → Now**
Their oath was tied to *muṣbiḥīn* — the morning they would come. Their execution is happening *al-yawm* — today. The abstract plan has become the concrete present.

The passage zooms in. Wide-angle: a family waking and calling each other at dawn. Close-up: four people walking in close formation, barely moving their lips, exchanging a hushed certainty about one still person they've decided to exclude.

That zooming in is the Quran directing our attention to the core of what this parable is actually about. Not an abstraction about property rights. Not a philosophical position. A whisper. Four people, close together, keeping their voices down, about one miskīn at the gate.

---

### What Language and Theme Have Shown Together

The linguistic journey showed us the structure: from loud Form VI to quiet Form VI, from the broad call to the specific whisper, from the plan to the emphatic prohibition.

The thematic reflection showed us the meaning: organised miserliness builds its own community, names its victim, and marches with total confidence toward what is already gone.

These are the same insight from two directions.

The architecture of greed is confident **because** it networks — the mutual calls create a false social legitimacy. And it walks into ruins **because** that confidence was never about reading reality. It was about protecting what it had already decided to protect, regardless of what reality was doing.

And in between — the echo of *muṣbiḥīn*, the garden already *ṣarīm*, Allah absent from the narration while the humans march forward — the Quran gives us the unbearable image of human certainty operating in a world that has already moved past it.

### Questions to Carry

These are not rhetorical. Sit with each one — not now, but later, in the quiet.

1. Where in my life has a collective agreement — a network of mutual reinforcement — made something feel acceptable that I would not have accepted alone? Who called whom, and what did that call give permission for?

2. What do I whisper that I would not say aloud? What does the register of the whisper tell me about what part of me already knows?

3. Is there a *miskīn* I have named — not necessarily in the word, but in the structure of my decisions — someone whose access to what I guard has been quietly, emphatically arranged against? What is the difference between how I have arranged things and how my father — my predecessors in faith and life — arranged theirs?

4. What garden am I racing toward this morning with the confidence that it's still there? What happened in the night while I was sleeping, while I was not looking, that I haven't yet discovered?

5. They were precise. They were organised. They were early. And they were wrong. What in my own life am I executing with precision that may need a pause — not for longer planning, but for asking what reality actually looks like right now?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

*In four ayahs, Allah gives us the complete anatomy of organised miserliness — its loud collective mobilisation, its confident confident march at dawn, its hushed conspiracy, and its named victim — all unfolding in the direction of a garden that has already been reclaimed.*

---

### The Closing Invitation

After sitting with this — not as obligation, but as invitation — one question worth carrying into tomorrow morning when you wake at whatever your own *muṣbiḥīn* is:

*What has arrived in the night that I haven't seen yet?*

Before you marshal your resources. Before you call your people. Before you make the final arrangements and begin to move — consider whether the share you've quietly planned to exclude has a prior claim. Consider whether your confidence and Allah's prior action are two different things, running in parallel, and the difference between them will announce itself at the gate.

The garden, if it is still there, was always in part a trust. The father knew this. He rendered what was embedded in the wealth before he kept his own portion. When we inherit — whether a literal inheritance, or the inheritance of health, or time, or knowledge, or influence — we inherit it with the *miskīn*'s portion still inside it.

The plan that excludes that portion has a way of running into ash.
