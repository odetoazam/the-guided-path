# We Are Lost — The Two Words a Ruined Garden Pulled Out of Them

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the two verses first. Hold them lightly; we are going to spend a long time inside them.

> فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهَا قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لَضَآلُّونَ ۝ بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ
>
> *Fa-lammā ra'awhā qālū innā la-ḍāllūn — bal naḥnu maḥrūmūn.*
>
> **"But when they saw it, they said, 'Surely we are lost.' — 'No — rather, we are deprived.'"**

Eight words. Two short sentences. And almost nothing happens on the surface: some men walk up to a garden, look at it, and speak twice. That's it. No thunder in the verse, no fire, no angel. Just a look and two sentences.

And yet inside those two sentences is one of the most precise X-rays of the human heart in the entire Qur'an. Because what you are watching here is a soul being handed the truth about itself in installments — and you get to see exactly how it takes the truth, and how it resists it, and the moment its resistance finally breaks.

### Let's rebuild the world they walked out of

To feel this, you have to know what these men were the night before.

They owned a garden. Not a struggling patch of dirt — a *generous* garden, a fruit-heavy inheritance. The surah has just told us (68:17-18) that they swore an oath — they would go out early, at the cool edge of dawn, and harvest every last fruit. Why early? So the poor would not see them coming. So they could strip the garden clean before any beggar arrived, before any needy neighbour could claim the share that their generous father used to leave behind.

And the Qur'an freezes on one chilling detail in their oath: **وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ** — *"and they made no exception."* They did not say "if God wills." They spoke about tomorrow as if tomorrow belonged to them. As if the fruit was already counted in their hands and the dawn was already theirs to command.

So picture them. The night is over. They wake confident. They are walking out to the garden in the grey light, probably whispering to each other to keep their voices down — *don't wake the poor, don't let anyone follow us* — congratulating themselves on the cleverness of the plan. Their hearts are full of fruit they have not yet picked.

And then they arrive. And they lift their eyes.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to sit with before we go a single step further.

These men are about to say two things in a row. First: **"We are lost."** Then, a beat later: **"No — we are deprived."**

Two confessions, back to back. And the question that should not let you sleep tonight is this: **why two?** Why doesn't the truth land in one sentence? What changes in the half-second *between* those two statements? Because something does change — something enormous — and the Arabic marks the exact moment it changes with a precision that English cannot carry.

By the time we are done, I want you to see that the distance between "we are lost" and "we are deprived" is the distance every one of us travels when reality finally catches up with the story we were telling ourselves. [PAUSE]

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: فَلَمَّا — the hinge the whole story turns on

Before we touch the meaning, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds a sentence like this, because the very first word is doing structural work most translations throw away.

In English we'd say, "When they saw it, they said..." — smooth, flat, no drama. But the Arabic opens with **فَلَمَّا** — *fa-lammā*. The *lammā* is a particle that grammarians call a particle of *"when,"* but more precisely it carries the sense of **"at the very moment that"** — it ties two events together so tightly that the second is the *immediate consequence* of the first. And the *fa-* on the front adds *sequence and suddenness* — "and then, at once."

Here's an everyday version of this structure. Imagine telling a story: *"I was completely calm — and the moment I opened the front door..."* And you stop. You don't even finish. Everyone listening already feels the floor drop, because that structure — *"the moment I..."* — is built to deliver a shock. It exists to mark the instant before everything changed.

That is exactly what **فَلَمَّا** is doing. Notice something: the verse does not describe the journey. It does not say "they walked, and they talked, and they planned, and they arrived." Allah compresses the entire confident morning into nothing and drops us at the single instant of impact — *the moment they saw it.*

Stay with this for a second. The whole night of God's decree happened off-screen. While they slept, the surah has told us, a visitation from their Lord went around the garden and left it like a harvested field, black, barren (68:19-20). All of that — the real event, the decisive event — happens in silence, in the dark, with no witnesses. And these men walk toward it carrying yesterday's confidence into a world that has already been completely rearranged without their knowledge.

**فَلَمَّا** is the seam between the two worlds. On one side of that word: men who own a garden. On the other side of that word: men who own nothing. And they cross from one to the other in a single step, mid-sentence, without warning.

Here's what's remarkable. How many of our own *fa-lammā* moments happen exactly like this? The decisive thing — the diagnosis already true in the body, the company already insolvent, the marriage already over in the other person's heart — has *already happened*, often while we slept, while we planned, while we counted fruit we did not own. And we are simply walking toward the moment we will *see* it. The seeing is not the event. The seeing is just when we are finally let in on an event that is already finished.

The word **فَلَمَّا** is Allah telling you: there is a seam in every life like this. And you do not get to choose which side of it you are standing on. [PAUSE]

### Element Two: لَضَآلُّونَ — the wrong diagnosis

Now we arrive at the first thing they say. **قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لَضَآلُّونَ** — *"They said: surely we are lost."*

I want to slow all the way down here, because this single word — **ضَآلُّونَ**, *ḍāllūn* — is the heart of the first verse, and it is far stranger than it looks.

The word comes from the root **ض-ل-ل** (*ḍ-l-l*). And here is the first thing to feel before we even define it: **say it out loud.** *Ḍāllūn.* That heavy, throated **ḍād** — a letter so distinctive that Arabic is sometimes called *"the language of the ḍād."* Then the doubled, drawn-out **lām**: *ḍaaaal-lūn.* The word itself *wanders.* It stretches. It doesn't resolve cleanly; it drags its feet. The mouth, making this word, performs the very thing the word means — being strung out, off-course, unable to land. Hold that; we'll come back to it.

Now the meaning. The root **ḍ-l-l** is the root of *being lost, going astray, losing the way.* And — this is the key — it is a single root that lives in **two different countries at once.**

In one country, it is utterly physical and innocent. You lose your way on a road. You take a wrong turn. The classical commentators give us this reading directly: when these men saw the blackened, burnt ruin where their lush garden should have been, their first instinct was denial — *"this can't be it. We must have walked to the wrong place. We're lost — we've taken a wrong turn somewhere."* This is the plain reading al-Ṭabarī foregrounds: *ẓannan minhu annahum qad aghfalū ṭarīq jannatihim* — supposing they had simply missed the road to their garden, and that what they were now looking at was some *other* garden. Al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar say the same: they looked at the devastation and concluded *"this isn't our garden — we've lost the way to it."*

Feel how human that is. This is the very first thing the mind does when reality is unbearable: it questions the *reality*, not the self. *I must have the wrong address. There must be a mistake. This can't be happening to me — I think I'm in the wrong place.* The first move of a heart in shock is to assume the map is broken, never that the world has actually changed. Al-Ṭabarī catches the precise emotional texture of it in two words: *ankarūhā wa shakkū fīhā* — they *refused* the sight of it and *wavered*, asking themselves, *is this even our garden, or not?*

But now — the *other* country this same root lives in. Because **ḍalāl** is not only the word for losing a road. It is *the* Qur'anic word for **losing your way spiritually** — for misguidance, for being astray from the truth itself. *Ghayr al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa lā al-**ḍāllīn*** — the very last word of Al-Fātiḥah, the word we say seventeen times a day, *"those who are astray."* Same root. Identical word, almost.

So when these men cry out **إِنَّا لَضَآلُّونَ**, the surface meaning on their lips is *"we've lost the road."* But the word they reach for — without meaning to — is also the word for *"we are the misguided ones."* They have spoken more truth than they intend. They think they're saying "we took a wrong turn on the path." What is actually coming out of their mouths is the diagnosis of their whole condition: *we were astray.* Not on the road. In the soul. The wrong turn was not made this morning at a fork in the path — it was made last night, in an oath that left no room for the poor and no room for God.

Now let me add the grammatical layer, because it locks this in. **ضَآلُّونَ** is an *active participle* — the *ism al-fāʿil*, the "doer" form. It means *"the ones who are doing the straying,"* the ones actively wandering. There is, buried in the grammar, a faint claim of *agency* — even an unconscious one: *we have wandered, we have gotten ourselves lost.* It still leaves the door cracked open. If you've merely *lost your way*, you can be *found.* You can retrace your steps. A wrong turn is, in principle, *fixable.* You just need to find the right road again.

So this first confession, for all its accidental honesty, is still **denial wearing the costume of a small problem.** *We're a little lost. We'll sort it out. This is a navigation error.* Hold onto that crack of false hope in the active participle — because the next word is going to slam it shut. [PAUSE]

### Element Three: بَلْ ... مَحْرُومُونَ — the correction that costs everything

Now watch the second sentence arrive. It does not begin with "and." It begins with one of the most decisive words in the Arabic language: **بَلْ** — *bal.*

Let me teach you *bal* on its own, because everything hinges on it. **بَلْ** is the particle of *retraction and correction.* It doesn't add to the previous statement — it *cancels* it and replaces it. You say something, then you say *bal*, and what comes after *bal* deletes and overrides what came before. The closest English is *"No — rather..."* or *"Actually, no..."*

You use this every day. *"I'll be there in five minutes — no, actually, make it twenty."* That little *"no, actually"* is *bal.* It's the sound of a person catching their own error in real time and overruling themselves, out loud.

So look at what is happening between the two verses. On the most natural reading these are the *same men*, in the *same breath.* First: *"We're lost — we've taken a wrong turn."* And then — **بَلْ** — *"No. Stop. That's not it either."* The truth comes in, knocks the first explanation aside, and they correct *themselves*, mid-arrival.

It's worth knowing the classical commentators part ways at exactly this seam. Al-Muyassar and al-Jalālayn take it as one and the same group revising its own verdict: they first deny the place — *this can't be ours* — and then, *the moment they realised it truly was their garden* (*lammā ʿalimūhā* / *fa-lammā ʿarafū annahā hiya jannatuhum*), they overrule themselves with *bal*. Al-Ṭabarī, though, hears two voices among the brothers: some, *supposing they had simply lost the road* to their garden, cry *"surely we are lost"* — while *the one who knew it was their garden, and that they had not missed the road at all*, answers them back — *"no — rather, we are deprived."* Either way the spiritual movement is identical. Whether it is one mouth correcting itself or one brother correcting another, the same comfortable explanation dies on the same word.

This is the moment I asked you to wait for. This **بَلْ** is the exact half-second where reality finally breaks through the denial. Watch the sequence the commentators trace — whether inside one mind or across the group: first comes denial and doubt (al-Ṭabarī's word, again, is that they *ankarūhā wa shakkū fīhā* — they refused it and wavered, *is this even our garden?*) — *we're lost.* Then recognition forces its way in — *no, this IS the place.* And the instant they can no longer pretend it's the wrong address, the real diagnosis lands. *bal.* And out of their mouths comes the word they have been running from: **مَحْرُومُونَ.**

**مَحْرُومُونَ** — *maḥrūmūn* — from the root **ح-ر-م** (*ḥ-r-m*): to be *forbidden, denied, cut off, deprived.* "We are the deprived ones. We are the ones who have been *cut off.*" Al-Ṭabarī spells out what, exactly, they have been cut off from: *ḥurimnā manfaʿat jannatinā bi-dhahāb* — *we have been deprived of the benefit of our garden, by the loss of its fruit.*

And here is where the grammar delivers the knockout. Remember **ضَآلُّونَ** was an *active* participle — *we are the ones doing the wandering.* But **مَحْرُومُونَ** is a **passive participle** — the *ism al-mafʿūl*, the "done-to" form. It does not mean "the ones who deprive." It means **"the ones to whom deprivation has been done."** The ones it was *done to.*

Let that contrast ring. Listen to the arc of the whole two verses through the grammar alone:

- First word about themselves: **ḍāllūn** — *active.* "We are *doing* something — wandering. We can still act. We can find the road."
- Second word about themselves: **maḥrūmūn** — *passive.* "Something has been *done to us.* It is finished. We are on the receiving end of a verdict we did not write and cannot reverse."

They move, in the space of one **بَلْ**, from a problem they imagine they can still *fix* to a sentence that has already been *passed on them.* From subject to object. From "we took a wrong turn" to "we have been shut out." This is the precise grammatical signature of a heart giving up its last illusion of control. [PAUSE]

And now — the layer that makes this almost unbearable. *Who* deprived them? The passive form leaves the doer unnamed, but the whole parable has been screaming it. Go back to their plan. What were *they* going to do at dawn? **Deprive the poor.** Cut the needy out. Withhold what was owed. The Arabic word-world of their crime and the Arabic word-world of their punishment share the *same root* of denial and withholding. They set out to make the poor **maḥrūm** — deprived — and they wake to find the word has come home to roost on themselves. **مَحْرُومُونَ.** *We are the deprived ones now.* The exact thing they engineered for others has been handed, whole, to them.

And this is not a flourish read into the verse — the classical mufassirūn make the connection in so many words. Al-Jalālayn glosses **مَحْرُومُونَ** as *deprived of its fruit because we prevented the poor from it* (*ḥurimnā khayrahā bi-manʿinā al-fuqarāʾ*); al-Muyassar reads it identically — deprived of the garden's good *bi-sabab ʿazminā ʿalā al-bukhl wa manʿ al-masākīn*, "because of our resolve upon stinginess and our withholding from the needy." The punishment, in their hands, is not a random calamity that merely happens to rhyme with the crime. It names the crime by name.

So the word they finally choose to name their own ruin is the very word that describes the wrong they were on their way to commit. The punishment is not just a punishment. It is a *mirror.* [PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

Look at what these eight words have given us. **فَلَمَّا** — the seam, the instant the two worlds meet, the decisive event already finished in the dark. **ضَآلُّونَ** — the active participle, the first denial, the wrong diagnosis that nonetheless leaks the truth. And **بَلْ مَحْرُومُونَ** — the retraction, the passive participle, the verdict received, the crime reflected back.

But all of this — the seam, the denial, the correction — is not finally *about* a garden, or about eight men in the Arabian dawn. The garden is the stepping stone. Let's now ask what these words reveal not just about a parable, but about the way every human heart meets the truth about itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The future you were so sure of

Let me start not in the garden but in your phone.

Open your calendar. Look at next Tuesday. There it is — a meeting at 2pm, an appointment, a plan, sitting there in crisp black letters as though it were *already real.* We do not write "2pm meeting, God willing." We write "2pm meeting." Full stop. With total confidence. We speak of next month, next year, "when the kids are older," "when I retire," "once the renovation's done" — we narrate a future we have never once been promised, in the flat declarative tone of someone reading off facts.

We are, all of us, all day long, doing the exact thing these men did. *We make no exception.* **وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ.**

This is what their whole catastrophe grew out of — not the stinginess alone, but the *posture beneath it*: the assumption that tomorrow's dawn was theirs to spend. They didn't say "we'll harvest at dawn, *in shā Allah*" — not because they forgot a pious phrase, but because, in their hearts, the *in shā Allah* felt unnecessary. The fruit was as good as picked. The future was as good as owned. The most balanced of them names this very omission afterward (68:28): *alam aqul lakum lawlā tusabbiḥūn* — "Did I not tell you, why do you not glorify God?" — which al-Muyassar reads as a direct rebuke of the missing exception itself: *hallā tastathnūn wa taqūlūn: in shāʾ Allāh* — "why would you not make the exception and say, *if God wills*?" The unspoken *in shā Allah* was, to the one clear-eyed man among them, the whole hinge of the disaster.

And the Qur'an treats this not as a small etiquette slip but as the root posture that has to be corrected. In Sūrat al-Kahf — in the *other* great garden story, where one man boasts to his believing companion — Allah says: *"And why, when you entered your garden, did you not say, 'Mā shāʾ Allāh, there is no power except through Allah'?"* (18:39). And He says directly to the Prophet ﷺ, and through him to all of us: *"And never say of anything, 'Indeed I will do that tomorrow,' except [that you add], 'if God wills'"* (18:23-24).

Now — let's be careful and honest here, because there's a shallow version of this lesson and a deep one. The shallow version is: *say the magic words "in shā Allah" or your plans will be destroyed.* That turns it into a superstition, a verbal toll you pay to keep your garden safe. That is not it.

The deep version is this: **"in shā Allah" is not a charm. It is a confession of the truth about who actually holds tomorrow.** It is the tongue admitting what is *already factually the case* — that the dawn is not yours, the breath is not yours, the next heartbeat is on loan. The men in the garden didn't lose the garden *because* they failed to say a phrase. They lost it because they genuinely believed, in their bones, that they were in control — and the missing phrase was just the visible symptom of that invisible disease.

Here's what's remarkable, and here's where the **فَلَمَّا** comes flooding back. Notice that the decisive event happened *while they slept.* They had zero involvement in the actual turning point of their lives. The one moment that mattered most — the night the garden was taken — they were unconscious for. They contributed *nothing*. They were not consulted.

That is the truth "in shā Allah" is pointing at. **The most important things that happen in your life happen on the other side of a door you cannot see behind.** While you sleep, while you plan, while you count the fruit — the real decree is being written. To say "in shā Allah" is simply to stop pretending you're the author of a story you haven't even read ahead in.

So what shifts tomorrow? Not that you start sprinkling a phrase over your sentences like seasoning. It's that you let a small crack of humility open under *every* confident plan you make. When you say "I'll see you next week," let a quiet inner voice add: *if He wills.* Not as fear — as *relief.* Because the alternative — believing the future is on your shoulders, that you must hold tomorrow up by force of will — is exhausting, and it is a lie. The man who has handed tomorrow back to its Owner sleeps easier than the man guarding fruit he thinks he controls. [PAUSE]

### Theme Two: What you withhold is withheld from you

Now I want to start somewhere uncomfortable. I want you to think about the thing you are clutching.

Everyone has one. The money you could give but keep finding reasons not to. The apology you're owed but also the one you owe and won't make. The knowledge, the time, the help, the praise, the forgiveness — the thing that someone near you needs, that you *could* extend, and that you are quietly keeping your hand closed around. We all have a closed fist somewhere.

The men in this parable had a closed fist around their fruit. Their crime was not that they harvested — harvesting is good, the garden is a gift to be used. Their crime was the *withholding*: the deliberate engineering of a dawn raid to make sure the **miskīn**, the poor, got *nothing.* Their father had left a share; they planned to leave none. They wanted to be, for the poor, the agents of **ḥirmān** — deprivation. To make the needy **maḥrūm.**

And we have already seen what came back to them in the mirror of the grammar: **بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ.** *We are the deprived ones now.* The exact condition they were manufacturing for others arrived, fully formed, at their own feet.

I want to be precise about what this *is* and what it *isn't*, because it's easy to flatten it into a vending-machine theology — *be stingy, get punished; be generous, get rich.* That's not the teaching, and the Qur'an is more honest than that. The teaching is deeper and stranger: **the moral shape of how you treat others has a way of becoming the moral shape of your own life.** What you do to the world tends to circle back and describe you. The hand that closes around what others need eventually finds itself empty. Not always in fruit — sometimes in something far more important.

Think about it in the texture of ordinary life, where you can actually watch it happen. The person who withholds warmth slowly finds the world has gone cold around them. The one who is always quick to deny others the benefit of the doubt becomes the one no one extends grace to. The one who hoards — time, credit, affection — finds, decades later, a strange poverty in a life that should have been full. They became **maḥrūm.** Not because lightning struck. Because the closed fist is a posture, and a posture you hold long enough becomes the shape of your whole life. You spend years making others deprived, and one **fa-lammā** morning you look up and realise the deprivation has settled onto you.

And here is the mercy hidden inside this hard teaching — because there *is* mercy here, and the parable itself shows it. Watch what happens *after* our two verses. These men do not stay in their ruin cursing their luck. Verse 28: the most balanced among them says, *"Did I not tell you — why do you not glorify God?"* Verse 29: they say, **سُبْحَانَ رَبِّنَا إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ** — *"Glory be to our Lord; truly, we were the wrongdoers."* Al-Muyassar notes that they say this *baʿda an ʿādū ilā rushdihim* — *after they had returned to their right minds*: the ruin did not merely flatten them, it brought them back to their senses, and the first thing a restored mind does here is declare God free of any injustice — *tanazzaha Allāhu rabbunā ʿan al-ẓulm fī-mā aṣābanā* — "our Lord is far above any wrong in what has befallen us; rather, *we* were the ones who wronged ourselves."

Do you see the final movement? **ḍāllūn → maḥrūmūn → ẓālimūn.** "We are lost" → "we are deprived" → "*we were the wrongdoers.*" Three stages, and only in the third do they finally point the finger in the right direction — *at themselves.* The losing of the garden was a catastrophe. But it cracked something open that the garden, full and green, had kept sealed shut: it brought them, at last, to *sujūd* in their words, to "Glory be to our Lord." The deprivation **maḥrūm** of fruit became the doorway out of the deeper deprivation — the deprivation of *truth* about themselves that their wealth had been feeding.

So what shifts tomorrow? Find the closed fist. Just one. The thing you are withholding that you know, in the quiet, you could give. And open it — not because giving buys you anything, but because the alternative is to keep practising the posture of **ḥirmān** until it becomes *you.* Open the fist before a **fa-lammā** morning has to come and pry it open for you. ## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now that we've done the work, stand back and look at the shape of these eight words, because the structure itself is the proof of everything we've said.

Watch the descent, marked at every step by the grammar:

1. **فَلَمَّا** — the seam. The decisive event, already finished in the dark. They had no part in it.
2. **إِنَّا لَضَآلُّونَ** — *active* participle. The first, false diagnosis: "we took a wrong turn; this is fixable; we can find the road." Denial, with the truth leaking through the word.
3. **بَلْ** — the retraction. The half-second the denial breaks.
4. **نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ** — *passive* participle. The verdict received: "this was done to us; it is final." And the crime reflected back in the mirror of the root.

The movement from **active** to **passive** *is* the whole spiritual journey of these men in miniature: from a soul that still imagines itself in charge — *we are doing the wandering, we can undo it* — to a soul that finally admits it is on the receiving end of a reality far larger than itself. And it takes a single, devastating **بَلْ** to cross from one to the other.

### Bringing it together

See now how the two themes were never two things. They are the front and back of one truth.

Theme one said: **you do not own tomorrow.** Theme two said: **what you withhold returns to describe you.** And both meet exactly at **مَحْرُومُونَ** — because the men who were *most certain they controlled the future* (they swore an oath about dawn with no exception) were the same men *most determined to deprive others* — and the punishment fused both errors into one word. The future they thought they owned was taken. The deprivation they meant to author was handed back. Their two diseases were healed — or at least exposed — by a single ruined garden and the two confessions it pulled out of them.

The linguistic discovery and the lived lesson are the same insight from two directions: **a closed hand and a presumptuous heart are the same posture** — both are a refusal to admit Who actually holds the garden. And reality, gently or violently, will always correct that posture. The only question is whether we let the correction in at "in shā Allah," or whether we wait for the *fa-lammā.*

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life am I, right now, speaking about tomorrow as though I own it — and what would change if I genuinely believed I might not see it?
2. What is the closed fist I'm pretending is closed for good reasons? Who is my *miskīn* — the one I'm quietly keeping the fruit from?
3. When the next *fa-lammā* moment comes — and it will — which word will be on my lips first: "this can't be happening" (**ḍāllūn**), or "Glory be to my Lord" (**subḥāna rabbinā**)?
4. Has any deprivation in my own life ever turned out to be the doorway that cracked me open to the truth — the way the lost garden brought these men, at last, to their Lord?

### One-sentence distillation

When the garden they were sure they owned was suddenly gone, two confessions fell out of them — *"we are lost," then "we are deprived"* — and the grammar of those two words traces the exact journey of a heart giving up the illusion that it was ever in control.

### The closing invitation

You don't have to wait for a burnt field at dawn. The whole point of being *shown* these men is that you don't have to *become* them. You can do, tonight, in soft humility, what they could only do standing in the ashes: hand tomorrow back to its Owner, and open the fist before it has to be pried open. Let the word be *in shā Allah* now — so it never has to be *bal naḥnu maḥrūmūn* later.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, You who hold every tomorrow we so easily call our own — loosen our grip on the futures we do not own, and open the fists we keep closed against the ones who need us. Do not let us be the ones who must lose the garden to remember its Giver. And if You ever take from us, let the first word on our tongues be not "this can't be" — but "Glory be to You, our Lord; truly, we were the ones who wronged ourselves." Āmīn.*
