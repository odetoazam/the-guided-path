# The Life Inside a Single "Then"
### A reflection on Sūrah Al-Muʾminūn, verses 15–16

<!-- ayah:23:15 -->
> ثُمَّ إِنَّكُم بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ لَمَيِّتُونَ

<!-- ayah:23:16 -->
> ثُمَّ إِنَّكُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَامَةِ تُبْعَثُونَ

*Thumma innakum baʿda dhālika la-mayyitūn.*
*Thumma innakum yawma l-qiyāmati tubʿathūn.*

**"Then indeed, after that, you are to die. Then indeed, on the Day of Resurrection, you will be raised."**

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood, because these two verses do not land the way we usually hear them. We tend to file them under "reminders about the afterlife" — true, familiar, a little abstract. That is not how they arrived.

They arrived at the end of something breathtaking.

Go back three verses. Allah has just walked the listener, slowly, through the making of a human being. He begins with clay — *sulālah min ṭīn*, an extract from mud. Then a drop, *nuṭfah*, placed in a secure lodging. And then something extraordinary happens in the grammar itself, though we will come to that. Watch the pace of verse 14: We made the drop into a clinging-form, and made the clinging-form into a lump, and made the lump into bones, and clothed the bones with flesh — one, then the next, then the next, rapid, cascading, like a master's hands moving faster than the eye can follow. And then, after all that speed, a pause, and a leap:

<!-- ayah:23:14 -->
> ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَاهُ خَلْقًا آخَرَ

**"Then We produced him as another creation."**

Something crosses over there. The assembled biology becomes a *someone*. A self. A person who can be spoken to. And the verse closes on the highest note of praise in the whole passage:

**"So blessed is Allah, the best of creators."** *Fa-tabāraka Llāhu aḥsanu l-khāliqīn.*

Now hold that. You are standing at the summit of a hymn to life. The clay has become a heartbeat. The drop has become a face. Allah has just called Himself, in the presence of what He has made, *the best of creators.* The listener's chest is full. This is the peak.

And it is precisely at the peak — one breath after "the best of creators" — that verse 15 says:

**Then, indeed, after that, you die.**

Feel that. Not "and someday, in the fullness of things, mortality visits." No. Directly off the summit of the most beautiful thing Allah describes making — a living human self — comes: *then you die.* The masterpiece is completed and, in the same sentence-family, marked for the grave.

And here is the world you have to inhabit to feel the weight of it. The people first hearing this in Mecca did not struggle to believe Allah could create. They looked at their own children and conceded it. What they choked on — what they mocked, openly, in the streets — was the *next* verse. *A-idhā mitnā wa kunnā turāban* — "when we have died and become dust, we'll be *brought back*?" Bones scattered in a valley, reassembled? That, to them, was the joke. That was the claim they treated as an insult to intelligence.

So these two verses are not a gentle reminder. They are the hinge of an argument. The One who just showed you, stage by undeniable stage, that He built you from a drop, now says two things you would rather not hear standing side by side: you will die, and you will be raised. And He arranges the very grammar of these sentences to press on exactly the places your heart resists.

Here is what I want us to chase today. There is a word in these verses that Allah repeats three times across this passage — a tiny connective particle. Most translations render it "then" and move on. But that little word is carrying the entire weight of your life inside it. And once you see what it's doing, verse 15 and verse 16 stop being a reminder about the future.

They become a description of *right now.*

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The word that holds your whole life — *thumma*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we go near the verse, because Arabic is more precise about *time between events* than English ever bothers to be.

In English we have basically one workhorse for stringing events together: "and," or "then." "I woke up and made coffee." "I graduated then got a job." The word doesn't tell you whether five minutes passed or fifteen years. You have to guess from context.

Arabic refuses to be that vague. It has (at least) three different connectors, and choosing between them is a decision about *time itself.*

There is **wāw** (*wa*) — "and" — pure joining, no claim about order at all. "I bought bread and milk." Doesn't matter which first.

There is **fāʾ** (*fa*) — "and then, immediately" — sequence with no gap. Cause tumbling straight into effect. "I heard the alarm, *fa* I jumped up." One thing triggers the next, instantly, no space between.

And there is **thumma** — "then... after a while." Sequence *with a gap*. The grammarians call this quality **tarākhī** — delay, spacing, an interval that opens up between the two events. "I graduated, *thumma* I got married." Years live inside that word.

Now — everyday test. If I say in Arabic, "he stood up *fa* he fell," you picture a stumble: he rose and immediately toppled. If I say "he stood up *thumma* he fell," you picture something else entirely: he stood, he walked, he lived a while, and *later* he fell. The word *thumma* has quietly inserted a whole stretch of unspoken time.

Now watch what Allah does. <!-- ayah:23:14 --> Return to the womb passage. The material stages — drop to clinging-form to lump to bones to flesh — are strung together with **fa, fa, fa, fa.** Immediate. No gap. Bang, bang, bang. The body assembles at speed. That is biology doing what biology does, and Arabic clocks it as fast.

Then, to leap from assembled flesh to *a living human self* — "**thumma** We produced him as another creation" — Allah switches the connector. Not *fa.* **Thumma.** Because between a complete body and an ensouled person there is a gap that biology cannot cross at speed. That crossing gets the word of delay, the word of the leap.

And then verse 15: "**Thumma**, indeed, after that, you die." And verse 16: "**Thumma**, indeed, on the Day of Resurrection, you are raised."

Do you see the shape now? Three *thummas*. Three leaps, each one a threshold no cascade of *fa* can carry you across:

The leap into being a person.
The leap into death.
The leap into resurrection.

The *fa*'s are the parts a science textbook can narrate — cell, tissue, bone. The *thumma*'s are the three doors only "the best of creators" can open.

But here is the one I want you to sit with. **Stay with me.** Look again at verse 15. "Thumma innakum baʿda dhālika la-mayyitūn." *Then, after that, you are ones-who-die.*

Where, in that sentence, is your entire life?

It's not mentioned. Your childhood, your first heartbreak, your career, your marriage, the child you raised, the decade you spent building something, the argument you never resolved, the prayers, the sins, the whole vast interior world you call *my life* — none of it appears. All of it — **all of it** — is compressed into the *tarākhī*, the gap, hiding inside that one word *thumma.* You were made... *then* (and inside that "then" is everything you will ever do) ...you die.

Your whole life is the delay inside a single connective particle.

[PAUSE]

We experience our lives as enormous. Sprawling. The main event of the universe. And Allah, without cruelty, with the plain accuracy of the One who sees the whole arc, files it in the space between two words. Not to belittle it — but to *locate* it. To show you its true proportions. The thing you think of as the story is the pause between two chapters He considers more decisive: the day you became a soul, and the day you'll be raised as one.

This is what this small word reveals: **you are not living your life. You are living inside a "then."** And a "then" has, by its very grammar, a *next.*

### Element Two: The grammar of the denied, laid over the undeniable — *la-mayyitūn*

Now I need to show you something about how Arabic *emphasizes*, because Allah has built a very particular kind of emphasis into the word for death, and once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

In Arabic, you can say a thing three ways depending on *who you're talking to.*

If I'm telling you something you already accept — "the sun rose" — I just say it. Plain. No reinforcement. The grammarians call this the *ibtidāʾī* mode: statement to an open mind.

If I'm telling you something you're *unsure* about, hesitating over, I add one emphasizer. "*Indeed* the sun rose." I'm nudging a wavering listener.

And if I'm telling you something you are actively *denying* — arms folded, shaking your head, refusing it — I pile the emphasizers on. "*By God, indeed* the sun *truly* did rise." Two, three reinforcements stacked up. This is the *inkārī* mode: speech aimed at a denier. The rule is simple and human: **the more the listener resists, the heavier the emphasis.** You don't swear a triple oath about something nobody disputes.

Hold that rule. Now look at how Allah states death:

<!-- ayah:23:15 -->
> إِنَّكُم ... لَمَيِّتُونَ

There are *two* emphasizers wrapped around this. There is **inna** — "indeed," the emphatic particle at the front. And there is that small **la-** prefixed onto the word itself — *la-mayyitūn* — a second, independent emphasizer the grammarians call the *lām al-ibtidāʾ*, and here specifically the *lām muzaḥlaqah*, "the lām that slid down" the sentence to land on the predicate. *Inna* plus *lām.* Double emphasis. The grammar of arguing with a denier.

Now stop and feel how strange that is.

Of everything in existence, what is the *least* deniable? **Death.** It is the one appointment no one has ever missed. Every human who has ever lived has either died or is going to. There is no controversy. There is no counter-evidence. It is the single most reliably confirmed fact available to a conscious being.

So why — *why* — does Allah wrap the most certain thing in the language reserved for the thing you *refuse*?

[PAUSE]

Because He is not describing what you *know.* He is describing how you *live.*

Sit with this. You *know* you will die — intellectually, if I ask you, you'll say "of course." But look at how you actually move through your days. You make five-year plans as though the fifth year is promised. You postpone the apology, the phone call, the repentance, on the silent assumption that later exists. You scroll past the news of a death the way you'd scroll past weather. Functionally, in the machinery of the heart, **you are a denier of death.** Not in your creed — in your calendar. Not in your words — in your priorities.

And Allah, who knows the human being from the inside, does not address the creed. He addresses the calendar. He reaches for the exact grammar you'd use with someone shaking their head *no* — because on the level that governs your actual choices, your head is shaking *no.* The double emphasis is not making death *more true.* It cannot be more true. The double emphasis is Allah raising His voice to reach the part of you that has its fingers in its ears.

And there's one more thing in this word, quiet but load-bearing. Notice that *mayyitūn* is not a verb. Allah does not say *tamūtūn* — "you *will* die," an event out ahead of you. He uses a **noun-form**, a participle: *mayyitūn*, "**ones-who-die**," "mortals." A verb describes something you *do*, out there in the future. A noun describes something you *are*, right now. He is not telling you death will *happen* to you. He is telling you mortality is your *description.* It is stamped on you presently, the way "tall" or "brown-eyed" is stamped on you. You are not someone who will one day die. **You are, this moment, a dying thing.** The grave is not an event on your horizon. It is an adjective on your name.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The one thing you cannot do for yourself — *tubʿathūn*

Now watch what happens in verse 16, because Allah has built verse 15 and verse 16 as near-perfect twins — and the tiny places where the twins *differ* are where the whole meaning hides.

Lay them side by side:

**Verse 15:** *Thumma innakum — baʿda dhālika — la-mayyitūn.*
**Verse 16:** *Thumma innakum — yawma l-qiyāmati — tubʿathūn.*

Same "thumma." Same "innakum." Same shape: connective, emphasis, time-phrase, predicate. They are twins. So look hard at the three places they refuse to match.

**First difference — the emphasizer that vanishes.** Death got *two* emphasizers, *inna* and the *lām.* Resurrection gets only *one.* Look: *la-*mayyitūn, but plain *tubʿathūn* — no *lām* in front of it. The double-stress of the denied is on *death*, and it thins out on *resurrection.*

That should stop you, because it's backwards from what you'd expect. The Meccans denied *resurrection*, not death — so shouldn't resurrection get the heavier grammar? One way to sit with this: death is the denial *everyone* shares, believer and scoffer alike — we all live as though it won't come, so death needs the loudest volume to break through universal denial. But resurrection, for the one who has just conceded verses 12 through 14 — who has just watched Allah build a person from a drop — hardly needs the hard sell. If you accept that He made you the first time from almost nothing, the second time is the *easier* claim, not the harder one. The grammar eases off because, once you've truly seen the first creation, the argument is already won.

**Second difference — who is acting.** In verse 15, *mayyitūn* is an active form: *you* are the ones-who-die. Grammatically, dying is *yours*; it's your state, your attribute, something your own body does. But verse 16 — *tubʿathūn* — is **passive.** Not "you rise." "You *are raised.*" Something is done *to* you. You are the object, not the subject. You contribute nothing.

And notice the gap this opens: in a passive verb, *the one doing it is not named.* Who raises you? The verse doesn't say. Grammatically, the Agent is withheld. But of course it isn't a mystery — it's withheld precisely because it's obvious, and because naming Him beside so total an act would almost diminish it by stating what only He could possibly do. **You die on your own. You cannot rise on your own.** Every corpse in every graveyard proves the first half and is helpless before the second. Resurrection is the one thing in your entire existence that is one hundred percent someone else's verb.

**Third difference — and this is the one that undoes me.** Look at the two time-phrases. Death: *baʿda dhālika* — "**after that.**" Vague. Unspecified. Sometime. Later. But resurrection: *yawma l-qiyāmah* — "**the Day of the Standing.**" *Named.* A specific, appointed, defined Day with its own title.

Feel the reversal. In your gut, which of these feels like the fixed appointment and which feels like the vague rumor? Death feels *certain* and *dated* — it's coming, it's real, it's on the schedule. Resurrection feels *hazy* — a someday, a maybe, a doctrine. But the grammar says the *opposite.* Allah gives *death* the fuzzy phrase — "after that, whenever" — and gives *resurrection* the fixed, named, titled appointment. **To Allah, the vague event is your death. The definite one is your rising.** We have it exactly inverted. We treat the grave as the hard fact and the Standing as the soft one. Heaven's grammar treats the Standing as the hard fact and the grave as the soft "sometime."

[PAUSE]

And now hear the two verses as sound, because the sound seals it. Verse 15 ends on *la-mayyitūn.* Verse 16 ends on *tubʿathūn.* They rhyme. *-ūn... -ūn.* Death and resurrection are bound to each other by the very music of the line. You literally cannot say the death-word here without your mouth being shaped toward the rising-word that answers it. The verse will not let death be the last sound. **Something always rhymes after the grave.**

[PAUSE — major]

**The bridge.** So the words have shown us a shape. A tiny connective that swallows your whole life. A double-stress that treats the surest thing as if you were denying it — because you are. And a pair of twin verses whose differences quietly reverse everything you assumed: the grave is the vague appointment, the rising is the fixed one; dying is yours, being raised is entirely His.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about grammar — but about how you are actually living, tonight.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The most sophisticated denial in human history

Let me start nowhere near the Qurʾan. Let me start with your phone.

There's a reason the silence is unbearable. Have you noticed? The instant there's a gap — waiting for the kettle, sitting at a red light, lying in bed before sleep — the hand reaches. Not always because there's anything to check. Because the empty moment is *dangerous.* Because in the empty moment, sometimes, a certain thought tries to surface, and we have gotten extraordinarily good at not letting it.

A psychologist named Ernest Becker spent a career on this and won a Pulitzer for the argument that nearly everything human beings build — our status, our legacies, our frantic productivity, our need to matter, even a good deal of our cruelty — is, at bottom, a machine for *not thinking about the fact that we die.* He called it the denial of death. A whole field grew from it, "terror management," and its experiments keep finding the same eerie thing: quietly remind people they're mortal, and they don't become reflective — they clutch harder. Harder at money, at their group, at being right, at anything that feels like it might outlast them.

And ours may be the most advanced death-denying civilization that has ever existed. We moved dying out of the home and into the sealed hospital wing. We renamed the industry that handles our dead until the word "death" barely appears in it. We built a trillion-dollar apparatus — skincare, biohacking, "longevity," "your best life," curated and filtered and endlessly *young* — whose real product is the feeling that this doesn't apply to you. And in our pockets, the most effective anti-mortality device ever invented: a rectangle of pure distraction, engineered so that no silence lasts long enough for the thought to arrive.

We are not a people who don't know we'll die. We are the people who have spent the most, and built the most, to *avoid feeling it.*

Now — here is what these verses say about that.

Remember the grammar. *Inna* and the *lām* — the double emphasis, the raised voice you use with someone whose fingers are in their ears. For fourteen centuries people have read that emphasis and assumed it was there because death is *important.* But we've seen it's stranger than that. **Allah addresses you as a denier of death** — because He can see the phone in your hand and the plans in your calendar and He knows that whatever your tongue confesses, your life is organized around *later.* The verse is not information you lack. It's a hand gently pulling your fingers out of your ears.

And notice the mercy in *how* He does it. He doesn't shame the denial. He doesn't say "how dare you forget." He simply *raises His voice* — inna, lām — and turns death from a verb into your very name: *mayyitūn*, you-mortal-thing, not "you who will die someday" but "you, a dying creature, now." He is doing for you exactly what you will not do for yourself in the red-light silence. He is making you sit with it.

Because here's what the deniers, ancient and modern, never understood: **facing death is not the enemy of living. It is the beginning of it.** The one who has truly absorbed *la-mayyitūn* — I am, right now, a dying thing — is the only one who stops sleepwalking. The delayed apology gets made. The prayer gets prayed on time instead of "later." The grudge held for eleven years suddenly looks insane to carry into a grave that could open this week. The person who lives with death in view is not morbid. They are *awake.* They are the only one in the room who knows what the moment is worth.

So let this be concrete. Tonight, before you sleep — and I mean tonight — do the thing the phone exists to prevent. Lie there for two minutes without the screen. Let the thought Allah named come up: *I am a mortal thing. There is a fixed, named Day I am walking toward.* Don't dramatize it. Just don't run from it. And then ask one question: *if the "after that" of verse 15 came for me this year — what is the one thing I would be desperate to have fixed?* Whatever answer surfaces in that silence is the thing your denial has been hiding from you. Start there tomorrow.

### Theme Two: You are not the author. You are the sentence.

I want to talk about the story you tell about yourself, because we live in the golden age of self-authorship.

The whole culture is a workshop for it. "I built this." "Self-made." The profile that lists what *I* achieved, *I* founded, *I* overcame. We are taught, relentlessly, that we are the authors of our lives — that with enough discipline and the right morning routine and sufficient *grit*, you write your own story, you are the protagonist and the pen. And there's something intoxicating in it, because it's *partly* true and it feels like power. Control. Authorship.

Now go back to the grammar of these verses, because Allah quietly dismantles that story — not with a lecture, but with the difference between an active form and a passive one.

Look at how existence is bracketed. At the front end — verses 12, 13, 14 — you were *created.* Made. From clay you didn't choose, in a womb you don't remember, through stages you didn't design and could not have survived if left to you. You were, grammatically and actually, *acted upon.* You are the object of the sentence, not its author. And at the far end — verse 16 — *tubʿathūn*, "you are **raised**." Passive again. Acted upon again. You will contribute exactly as much to your resurrection as you contributed to your conception: **nothing.**

So look at the whole arc. It opens with something done *to* you. It closes with something done *to* you. Two great passive verbs, two acts entirely in Someone else's hands — bracketing your existence like the two covers of a book.

And what's in between? What's the one verb in this passage that's genuinely *yours*? *Mayyitūn* — you *die.* In this passage, the one thing framed as your own is the one thing you'd least want to claim.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that does to the self-made story. You did not author your entrance. You will not author your exit into the next life. The bookends are His. **You are not the author of your existence. You are a sentence being spoken by Someone else** — and the humbling, liberating truth is that this was never bad news.

Because think about what self-authorship actually costs the people who believe it. If I built myself, then I am *responsible* for holding myself up — every day, forever, alone. Every failure is a crack in the only foundation I have. Every rival who's ahead of me is proof I'm losing the story I'm supposed to be writing. The self-made man is the most exhausted man alive, because he has appointed himself god of a universe he cannot actually control, and some part of him knows it, and that's the anxiety no morning routine ever fixes.

The one who has heard *tubʿathūn* — "you *will be raised*" — is set free from that post. Not free from effort; free from the crushing lie that the outcome was ever his to guarantee. He works, he strives, he *dies* trying — that verb is his — but he lays down the impossible job of authoring the beginning and the end. Those belong to the best of creators, the One who has already done the harder creation and named the day of the second one.

Let me bring it all the way home. Picture the two moments that bracket a human life, the two we actually witness. A parent stands over a newborn — and every honest parent will tell you the overwhelming feeling is not pride of authorship. It's *awe.* "I did not make this. This was *given.*" And years later, someone stands at a graveside and lowers a body into the earth — and there, too, no one in that group believes for one second that they are in control. Both moments strip the self-made story bare. At both ends of a life, standing over the crib and standing over the grave, the human being instinctively knows the truth these verses state: *we receive our beginnings and our endings. We do not write them.*

So here is the concrete shift. This week, catch yourself in the sentence "I made this." Your success, your family, your recovery, the thing you're proud of. And without denying your effort, add three words to it: *"I was given."* Say it about the thing you're proudest of. Notice what loosens in your chest when you do — because the grip you've been holding it with, the grip that's exhausting you, is the grip of an author who's afraid of losing his manuscript. You were never the author. **You are the beloved sentence.** And the One writing you has already told you how the story ends: not in the grave — *baʿda dhālika*, sometime — but on a named and appointed Day, standing, raised, before Him.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture

Step back now and look at the whole staircase, from verse 12 to verse 16, because the design only becomes visible from here.

Allah builds this passage on two kinds of connective. The **fa**'s — immediate, no gap — carry the parts a laboratory can narrate: drop, clinging-form, lump, bones, flesh. The material assembly. Fast. Explicable. And then, three times, at the three moments no laboratory can follow, He switches to **thumma** — the word of the gap, the leap:

*Thumma* — and dead matter becomes a living soul.
*Thumma* — and the living soul returns to dust.
*Thumma* — and the dust is raised into "another creation" all over again.

Three thresholds. And notice where the staircase ends. It does *not* end on verse 15, on death. It could have. "You were made, then you die" — a complete, closed, godless sentence, the sentence our whole civilization lives inside. But Allah adds one more step. There is a *thumma* after the grave. **The staircase overshoots death.** The last word is not *mayyitūn.* The last word is *tubʿathūn.* Something always rhymes after the grave.

And see now how the two twin verses, read together, quietly overturn everything. We came in assuming death was the certain, dated fact and resurrection the vague doctrine. The grammar reversed it: death got the fuzzy "after that"; resurrection got the named Day. We came in assuming our lives were the main event. The grammar folded our whole life into the *tarākhī*, the pause inside one small word. We came in believing we author ourselves. The grammar bracketed us between two passives — created, raised — and left us only one verb of our own, the verb of dying. Everything the human being is most confident about, these two verses gently turn upside down.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the two themes were never two things. They are one insight seen from two angles.

The grammar of the *denied* laid over death (Theme One) and the grammar of the *passive* laid over resurrection (Theme Two) are telling you the same thing: **you are not in charge, and you have been pretending otherwise, and Allah loves you enough to say so out loud.** The double emphasis breaks your denial that you'll die. The passive verb breaks your fantasy that you run the show. And the little word *thumma*, three times, locates your enormous, sprawling, precious life exactly where it belongs — as the meaningful pause between the day He made you a soul and the day He raises you to stand.

We started at a summit — "the best of creators" — and were told, in the next breath, "then you die." It felt like a fall. It was the opposite. The best of creators does not abandon His masterpiece to the dirt. He tells you the dirt is a *thumma*, not a period. The most beautiful thing He described making does not end in a grave. It ends standing, on a Day He has already named.

### Questions to carry

- When the silence comes tonight — the red light, the moment before sleep — and your hand reaches for the phone, what exactly is it you don't want to feel? Sit with the answer before you reach.
- If death is your present *description* and not just your future event — if you are, right now, a *mayyit* — what in your life is built as though "later" were promised to you?
- You have inverted the two appointments: treating the grave as the hard fact and the Standing as the soft rumor. What would change if you believed the grammar — that the Day of Standing is the *more* certain of the two?
- Name the thing you are most proud of having "built." What loosens if you say of it, honestly, *"I was given this"*?
- If the *thumma* after the grave is real — if the last word over you is not "died" but "raised" — what fear that governs your days is actually smaller than you've been treating it?

### One sentence to keep

**Your whole life is the pause inside a single "then" — spoken by the One who has already named the Day He will speak you back into being.**

### The invitation

You do not have to fear the silence anymore. The thought you've been outrunning — *I am mortal* — is not the darkness. It's the doorway. Let it come. Let *la-mayyitūn* be true in your chest, not just your creed, and watch how it wakes you up: how it makes the prayer urgent and the apology possible and the grudge absurd. And then let the rhyme finish. Don't stop the verse at the grave. Let *tubʿathūn* land — you will be *raised*, by the best of creators, on a named Day, into another creation. You were made once from a drop. He is not going to lose you in the dust.

### A closing duʿāʾ

O Allah, best of creators — You who made us from a drop we do not remember, and named the Day You will raise us though we cannot imagine it — pull our fingers from our ears. Let us feel, and not merely know, that we are mortal, so that we wake before the "after that" arrives. Loosen our grip on the story we think we authored, and let us rest in the truth that our beginning and our end are in Your hands alone. And when the last *thumma* comes for us, raise us among those who stood before You awake. Āmīn.
