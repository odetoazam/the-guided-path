# Two Watchers, One Word
### A Tadabbur of Surah Ad-Dukhan, Ayah 59

> [ayah:44:59] فَٱرْتَقِبْ إِنَّهُم مُّرْتَقِبُونَ
>
> *Fa-rtaqib innahum murtaqibūn*
>
> "So watch and wait — indeed, they too are watching and waiting."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Last Word

Let me begin by telling you where we are standing.

A surah in the Quran is not a random collection of verses. It has a shape. It rises, it builds, it turns, and then — like a piece of music resolving its final chord — it *closes*. And the way a surah closes is never accidental. The last word a surah leaves ringing in your ears is the word Allah chose to send you back into your life carrying.

So before we open this ayah, I want you to feel the weight of *where* it sits. This is the **final verse** of Surah Ad-Dukhan. Everything in the surah has been moving toward this moment.

Think about what this surah has just walked you through. The smoke — *ad-dukhan* — a sign of reckoning that wraps the sky. The story of Pharaoh and his people, who watched the signs, who were given chance after chance, and who in the end were drowned, the sea closing over them while "the heaven and the earth did not weep for them." The surah has spoken of *yawm al-fasl* — the Day of Decision, the Day of Separation, when everything that was tangled together in this world finally gets pulled apart and sorted. It has shown you the tree of Zaqqum boiling in the bellies of the wretched, and in the very same breath, the gardens and springs and fine silk of those who were conscious of their Lord.

[PAUSE]

And then, just before our ayah, comes a line of extraordinary intimacy. Allah turns to His Messenger ﷺ and says: *We have made this Quran easy upon your tongue, that perhaps they might be reminded.* Made it easy. Smoothed it onto your tongue. We did the lowering, We did the easing — your job was only to carry it to them.

And the people? After the smoke, after Pharaoh, after the Day of Decision, after the easy and clear reminder placed gently on the Prophet's own tongue —

they still turned away.

Now imagine you are the Messenger ﷺ. You have delivered everything. You have been mocked. They are waiting for you to die, whispering that this movement will collapse the moment you are gone. The pressure is immense. What does Allah say to him? What is the closing instruction, the last word of the entire surah?

Not "argue with them harder." Not "despair." Not even "make du'a against them" — not here.

Four words. Two of them are the *same word*.

**فَٱرْتَقِبْ إِنَّهُم مُّرْتَقِبُونَ**

"So watch and wait — indeed, they too are watching and waiting."

Here is what I want you to feel the hunger for, the question we are going to chase: **why would Allah hand the Prophet and his enemies the exact same word?** Both are watching. Both are waiting. If you only heard the sound, you could not tell which is which. And yet these two watchings could not be more opposite — one will end in light, the other in ruin. How can a single word hold two destinies that are about to fly apart forever?

Stay with that. Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Root That Means "To Be Watched"

Before we touch the ayah, I want to teach you something about how Arabic works, because without it the verse stays flat.

In Arabic, almost every word grows out of a three-letter root — like a seed that can sprout into a whole family of related meanings. You plant the same three letters in different patterns and you get a noun here, a verb there, an intense form, a place, a person who does the action — but all of them carry the DNA of that original seed.

The seed in our ayah is **ر-ق-ب — rā, qāf, bā.** *Raqaba.*

Now here is the thing about this particular seed. Before it ever means "to wait," its oldest and most physical meaning is **the neck.** *Ar-raqaba* is the neck — and by extension, in classical Arabic, a whole person, a soul, especially a captive whose neck is under another's control. You free a *raqaba* — you free a neck, meaning a slave.

Sit with that image for a second. The neck. Why does "the neck" become the root for "watching"?

Because **to watch something carefully, you crane your neck.** You lift your head, you fix your gaze, you hold your neck still and turned toward the thing you are guarding. The one who watches over a flock, who guards a camp through the night, who keeps his eyes on the horizon waiting for the enemy or the awaited rider — his defining posture is the held, turned neck. From *raqaba*, the neck, comes *raqīb* — **the watcher, the guardian, the one who observes and does not look away.**

[PAUSE]

And now I need you to feel the weight of where else this seed grows in the Quran.

**Ar-Raqīb** — The All-Watchful — is a name of Allah Himself.

> "Indeed Allah is, over you, *Raqīb* — ever-watchful." *(an-Nisa 4:1)*

> "...and You were, over them, *Raqīb* — the Watcher." *(al-Ma'idah 5:117)*

This is the root that gives us the concept the scholars of the heart call **murāqaba** — to live in the awareness that you are watched. Not watched by a camera. Watched by the One who sees the black ant on the black rock on the black night, who knows the treachery of the eyes and what the chests conceal.

So when Allah, at the very end of Ad-Dukhan, tells His Prophet **fa-rtaqib** — "so watch, so keep vigil" — He is not reaching for some neutral word like "look" or "see." He is reaching into the family of *ar-Raqīb*. He is asking the Prophet ﷺ to take up a posture that belongs, in its highest form, to Allah Himself: the steady, unwavering, neck-held vigil of the one who watches and does not look away.

The Prophet is being told: *take up the watch.* Hold your gaze on the horizon. Something is coming.

That is the first layer. Hold it. We are about to see why the form of the word matters as much as the root.

### Element Two: Form VIII — The Watching You Take Up Inside Yourself

Now let me teach you one more piece of Arabic machinery, because this is where the ayah opens like a door.

I said the root is a seed and the patterns are how it sprouts. Arabic verbs come in numbered "forms" — Form I is the bare seed, *raqaba*, "he watched." But you can add letters to deepen or angle the meaning. Our ayah does not use the bare form. Both words in this verse — the command *irtaqib* and the description *murtaqibūn* — are in **Form VIII**.

Form VIII takes the root and inserts a *tā* after the first letter: *r-t-q-b*, *irtaqaba*. And what Form VIII characteristically does — across the whole language — is make the action **reflexive, self-involved, taken up for and within oneself.** It turns an action you might do casually into an action you *engage in*, that you take *upon* yourself, that involves your own stake.

Here is an everyday way to feel it. The difference between *raqaba* and *irtaqaba* is something like the difference between "he watched" and "he set himself to watch" — "he took up the watch." It is the difference between glancing at the door and *waiting up* for someone you love, ears straining at every sound on the stairs. One is an action of the eyes. The other is an action of the whole self, with the heart leaning forward into the future.

[PAUSE]

So when the lexicons gloss *irtiqāb*, look at what they say it means: not just to watch, but **to watch *for* something — to await, to be on the lookout, to keep vigil in expectation.** The watching and the waiting fuse into one act. You are not watching the present. You are watching *for* a future that has not arrived yet, leaning your whole self toward it.

This is why the four classical commentators in front of us — al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī — every single one of them, the moment they reach *fa-rtaqib*, reach for the same word to explain it: **intaẓir.** *Wait. Expect. Await.*

Al-Jalālayn: *"fa-rtaqib — await their destruction; innahum murtaqibūn — they await yours."*

Al-Baghawī: *"fa-rtaqib — await the victory from your Lord; innahum murtaqibūn — they await your defeat, by their own delusion."*

Do you feel what Form VIII has done? It has taken the Prophet's command and filled it with the future. He is not told to *look at* his enemies. He is told to **lean forward into what is coming** — to take up an active, expectant, whole-hearted vigil for the promise of his Lord. The watching is not anxiety. It is the steady forward lean of someone who *knows the visitor is on the way* and is keeping the lamp lit.

And notice — **this is the closing instruction of the surah.** After all the smoke and judgment, Allah does not leave His Prophet looking *backward* at the rejection. He turns his face *forward*, toward the horizon, and tells him: keep the watch. Something is coming.

### Element Three: One Word, Two Watchers — The Symmetry That Hides an Abyss

Now we arrive at the feature that should stop us in our tracks.

Look at the verse again. Really look.

**فَٱرْتَقِبْ** — *fa-rtaqib* — "so watch" (to the Prophet).
**مُّرْتَقِبُونَ** — *murtaqibūn* — "watchers" (the deniers).

It is **the same word.** Same root, *rqb*. Same Form VIII. The command given to the Prophet and the description given to his enemies are carved from one identical block of language. If you closed your eyes and only heard the sound — *irtaqib... murtaqibūn* — you would hear an echo, a mirror, two halves of one symmetry.

Let me teach you why this is a deliberate device and not a coincidence. In Arabic rhetoric, when you assign **the same action to two opposed parties**, you are doing something very pointed. You are placing them on the same stage, in the same posture, doing the same thing — *so that the listener is forced to ask: then what is the difference?* The symmetry is a trap. It pulls you in with its balance, and then it makes you discover that beneath the identical surface lie two destinies that are about to fly apart forever.

[PAUSE]

Both are watching. Both are waiting. Both have their necks turned to the horizon. From the *outside*, in this world, in this moment, you cannot tell them apart. The believer waits. The denier waits. Time passes the same way for both. The sun rises on the patient Prophet and on the scheming chiefs of Quraysh alike.

**This is the abyss hidden inside the symmetry.** Notice what Allah is teaching about the nature of *this life*: right now, in the waiting, the two look the same. The vindication has not come. Both parties stand on the deck of the same ship, watching the same sea. The world does not yet show whose watch is true and whose is delusion.

But look closer at the *grammar*, because the grammar quietly tells you the difference the surface hides.

To the Prophet, Allah gives a **command — a verb — *irtaqib*.** A fresh, living act. An imperative. *You — do this now.* It is dynamic, forward-moving, freshly placed in his hands by his Lord. His watching is *commissioned*. It comes from above. It is obedience.

To the deniers, Allah gives a **noun — a participle — *murtaqibūn*.** Not "they are commanded to watch" — *"they are watchers."* A description of a fixed state they are already locked into. And the commentators add the devastating little phrase: *bi-zaʿmihim* — "by their own delusion," "according to their own claim." Their watching is not commissioned by anyone. It is self-generated. They appointed themselves to wait for the Prophet's death, and they are *stuck* in that posture, a settled condition, watching for a victory that the rest of the surah has already told you will never come.

[PAUSE — let this land]

So here is the abyss. One word. Two watchers.

One watches *because his Lord told him to* — and what he watches for is **guaranteed**.
The other watches *because he decided to* — and what he watches for is **a delusion**.

One is leaning toward a promise. The other is leaning toward a mirage.

And the terrifying mercy of it is this: **the surah ends before the resolution.** Allah does not, in this final ayah, tell you how it turns out. He does not say "and you will win and they will lose." He leaves both of them standing on the deck, watching the sea. Because *that is where you and I live too* — in the watching, before the resolution, when the true watch and the false watch still look identical from the outside.

The whole question of your life is hidden in which kind of watcher you are.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these four words have shown us.

A root, *rqb*, that ties human watching to the watching of *ar-Raqīb*, the One who never looks away. A verb-form, Form VIII, that turns watching into an active, expectant, forward-leaning vigil of the whole self. And a symmetry — one word handed to two opposed parties — that hides an abyss: that in *this world*, in the waiting, the true and the false look the same, and only the unseen ending will tell them apart.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the two things every one of us is doing right now, whether we admit it or not: **we are all being watched, and we are all waiting.**

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Are Always Being Watched — The Question Is by Whom

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a peculiar feeling that defines modern life, and you have felt it. It is the feeling of being *observed.* You walk down a street and there are cameras. You type a message and somewhere it is logged. You hesitate before posting something because you imagine the eyes that will read it — the people who will judge, the screenshot that might outlive you. Psychologists have a name for the version of this that lives inside our heads: they call it the *imaginary audience* — the constant sense, especially sharp in the young, that we are on stage, that someone is watching, that we must manage how we appear.

And here is what that watching *does* to us. It makes us perform. It makes us anxious. It splits us in two — the self we are and the self we display. The gaze of the world is a heavy, fragmenting, exhausting thing. It watches to *judge*, to catch, to compare. You contort yourself under it. And the cruelest part is that it is *fickle* — the same crowd that watches you with approval today watches for your stumble tomorrow.

Now hold that exhausting, fragmenting gaze in one hand.

And in the other hand, take the root we uncovered: *rqb. Ar-Raqīb.* Allah, the All-Watchful, who is *Raqīb* over you. *(an-Nisa 4:1)*

[PAUSE]

Here is the transformation the ayah offers, and I want you to feel how total it is. The deniers in our verse made themselves *murtaqibūn* — watchers. They appointed themselves the audience of the Prophet's life, watching for his fall. The Prophet ﷺ was the most *watched* man in Makkah — every word weighed, every move tracked by hostile eyes waiting for him to break.

And what does Allah do? He does not tell him to escape the watching. He tells him to **answer one gaze with another.** *Fa-rtaqib* — *you* take up the watch too. But your watch is anchored not in the fickle crowd. Your watch is anchored in the One who is watching *them*, watching *you*, watching the *outcome* — *ar-Raqīb*.

Do you see the liberation in this? The whole exhausting weight of the world's gaze is broken the moment you remember a deeper gaze beneath it. The crowd watches your *surface* — and its watching is delusion, *bi-zaʿmihim*, "by their own claim." Allah watches your *heart* — and His watching is the truth that will outlast every camera.

The believer is not the one who escapes being watched. **That is impossible — you will always be watched.** The believer is the one who *chooses which gaze is real.* When you pray in an empty room, you are choosing *ar-Raqīb* over the imaginary audience. When you do the good deed no one will ever see and tell no one, you are saying: *the only audience I am performing for is the One who never looks away.* And the moment you do that, the world's gaze loses its power to fragment you. You are not two selves anymore — the displayed and the hidden. You become one self, lived entirely in front of *ar-Raqīb*.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Pick one act tomorrow that no human being will ever know about — a charity, a restraint, a kindness, a remembrance whispered in the car. Do it *specifically* as a private appointment with *ar-Raqīb*. Feel what it does to your chest to perform for an Audience of One. That is *murāqaba*. That is the believer's answer to a watching world.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Two Waitings — Why Patience Is Not Passive

Now the second thing every one of us is doing: **waiting.**

Let me name the human experience first. Waiting is one of the most spiritually revealing things you ever do, because *waiting exposes what you believe.*

Think about the difference between two people in a waiting room. One is waiting for results that they are dreading — every minute stretches, the stomach knots, the mind spins disaster after disaster. The other is waiting for someone they love to walk through the arrivals gate — and the *same* minutes feel completely different; they lean forward, they keep checking, but the leaning is *joy*, not dread. Same act. Same clock. Opposite interiors.

That is *exactly* the structure of our ayah. *Fa-rtaqib innahum murtaqibūn.* Two parties, both waiting, the same word — but one is the waiting of arrivals, and the other is the waiting of dread. And what determines which kind of waiting you are doing is **what you believe is coming.**

The Prophet ﷺ is told to wait — *irtaqib* — and the commentators tell us what he waits *for*: **the promise of his Lord.** Al-Muyassar expands it beautifully: *await what I have promised you of victory over them... they will come to know whose is the triumph and the high word, in this world and the next — it is yours, O Messenger, and for those who follow you in faith.* His waiting is the waiting of arrivals. The visitor is *certain*. The leaning-forward is not anxiety — it is the lamp lit in the window for the guest who is already on the road.

The deniers are *murtaqibūn* too — but they wait *bi-zaʿmihim*, "by their delusion," for a victory that will never knock on the door. Theirs is the waiting room of dread, except they do not even know it is dread, because they have convinced themselves the bad news is good news. They lean forward into a mirage.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the movement I want to walk you through, because it overturns something we usually get wrong.

We tend to think of *sabr* — patience — and of *waiting* as **passive.** As doing nothing. As enduring, gritting teeth, letting time drag over us. But look at what Form VIII just taught us. *Irtiqāb* is not passive. It is **active, engaged, forward-leaning vigil.** The Prophet is not told to *resign* himself. He is told to take up a *post*, to keep watch like a guard on a wall, *because something real is coming and his job is to be awake when it arrives.*

This reframes the entire spiritual meaning of waiting. The believer's waiting is not the slumped waiting of one who has given up. It is the *alert* waiting of one who knows the promise is true and refuses to fall asleep at his post. It is patience with its eyes open and its neck turned to the horizon.

And feel the placement again — **this is the last word of the surah.** Allah does not end Ad-Dukhan with the punishment, or with the gardens, or with the argument. He ends it with *you, awake, at your post, leaning toward a promise.* That is the posture He wants you to carry out of the surah and back into your week.

Because your life, right now, is the waiting room. You have not yet seen the vindication. The good you do has not yet visibly paid off. The patience you are exercising in that hard marriage, that illness, that injustice at work, that du'a you have made a thousand times with no answer yet — *none of it has resolved.* You are *murtaqib*. You are watching. And so, often, are the people waiting for you to fail.

The whole question is: **what do you believe is coming?** If you believe, with the certainty the surah has spent fifty-eight verses building, that *yawm al-fasl* is real, that the promise is true, that *ar-Raqīb* misses nothing and forgets no one — then your waiting transforms. It becomes the waiting of arrivals. You keep the lamp lit. You stay awake at your post. Not because it is easy, but because **you know who is coming.**

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Take the one thing you are most exhausted from waiting for — the unanswered du'a, the unresolved hardship. Tomorrow, consciously change the *posture* of that waiting. Stop slumping under it. Instead, treat it as a *post you are keeping* for *ar-Raqīb*, who sees the waiting and has promised that no atom of patience is lost. Lean forward, not in anxiety, but the way you would lean toward a door behind which someone you love is about to appear. That single shift — from dread-waiting to arrival-waiting — is the difference between the two watchers in this ayah.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: Why It Had to Be One Word

Now, with all the work behind us, look at the design of this verse and see if it doesn't take your breath.

Four words. **فَٱرْتَقِبْ إِنَّهُم مُّرْتَقِبُونَ.** The first word and the last word are the *same word* — *irtaqib* and *murtaqibūn*, the watch given and the watch described, framing the whole verse like two pillars cut from one stone. And held between those two identical pillars is a single tiny word: **هُم** — *hum*, "they." The whole verse is: *Watch — THEM — watchers.* The believer's command on one side, the deniers' state on the other, and between them only the thin word "they."

Allah could have used two different words. He has a vast vocabulary of waiting — and notice He *does* elsewhere: in nearly identical closing verses like *fa-ntaẓir innahum muntaẓirūn* (32:30) and *wa-ntaẓirū innā muntaẓirūn* (11:122), He uses a *different* root, *naẓara*. But *here*, in Ad-Dukhan, He chose *rqb* — the root of *ar-Raqīb*, the root of *murāqaba*, the root that carries the held neck and the watching that answers to the All-Watchful. He chose the word that makes the believer's watching an echo of *divine* watching. That choice is not decoration. It is the whole point.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the *same insight* arriving from two directions.

From the **words**, we learned: one root, *rqb*, ties human watching to *ar-Raqīb*; one form, Form VIII, turns watching into forward-leaning vigil; one symmetry hands two enemies the identical word so that we are forced to find the abyss beneath the matching surface.

From the **themes**, we learned: every human being is *being watched* and is *waiting* — and the entire weight of a life comes down to *whose gaze you live before* and *what you believe is coming.*

They are one truth. **The believer and the denier are doing the identical thing — watching, waiting — and from the outside, in this world, you cannot tell them apart. The difference is invisible. The difference is the unseen.** It is whether your watch is anchored in *ar-Raqīb* or in the fickle crowd; whether your waiting leans toward a *promise* or a *mirage*. And that difference, invisible now, is the entire difference between the gardens and the Zaqqum that the surah has just laid before you.

The surah ends in the waiting room *on purpose* — because that is where you are sitting right now.

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose gaze am I actually living in front of, hour by hour — the imaginary audience of the crowd, or *ar-Raqīb* who never looks away?
2. When I am *murtaqib* — when I am waiting — is mine the waiting of dread or the waiting of arrivals? What does that reveal about what I truly believe is coming?
3. Is there a watching I have appointed *myself* to, *bi-zaʿmī*, by my own claim — waiting for something that, if I were honest, is a mirage?
4. What would change in the next hard hour of my life if I treated my patience as an *active post I am keeping* rather than a weight I am slumping under?
5. The deniers and the Prophet looked the same from outside. If someone watched only my surface, could they tell which watcher I am? Could *I*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

In this world the believer and the denier do the very same thing — they watch and they wait — and the entire difference between them, invisible now and decisive forever, is whether the gaze they live before is *ar-Raqīb* and the future they lean toward is His promise.

### The Closing Invitation

You will walk out of this reflection back into a life of being watched and a life of waiting. None of that will change. What can change is the *anchor.* You do not have to escape the gaze — you have to choose the true one. You do not have to stop waiting — you have to remember Who is coming. Let the last word of Ad-Dukhan become the posture of your week: awake at your post, neck turned to the horizon, leaning toward the promise of *ar-Raqīb* — not in dread, but in the quiet certainty of one who keeps the lamp lit because he knows the visitor is already on the road.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, *ar-Raqīb*, who watches over us and never looks away — let us live before Your gaze and no other, until the self we hide and the self we show become one self lived for You alone. Make our waiting the waiting of those who lean toward Your promise and not toward a mirage, and keep us awake and faithful at our post until the watch is over and the One we awaited arrives. Amin.
