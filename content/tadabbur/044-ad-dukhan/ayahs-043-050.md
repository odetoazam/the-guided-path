# The Tree You Disputed, The Names You Crowned Yourself With

### Surah Ad-Dukhan 44:43–50

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing these words in front of you, in three layers.

The Arabic:

> إِنَّ شَجَرَتَ ٱلزَّقُّومِ ۝ طَعَامُ ٱلْأَثِيمِ ۝ كَٱلْمُهْلِ يَغْلِى فِى ٱلْبُطُونِ ۝ كَغَلْىِ ٱلْحَمِيمِ ۝ خُذُوهُ فَٱعْتِلُوهُ إِلَىٰ سَوَآءِ ٱلْجَحِيمِ ۝ ثُمَّ صُبُّوا۟ فَوْقَ رَأْسِهِۦ مِنْ عَذَابِ ٱلْحَمِيمِ ۝ ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ ۝ إِنَّ هَـٰذَا مَا كُنتُم بِهِۦ تَمْتَرُونَ

The transliteration:

> *Inna shajarata-z-Zaqqūm * ṭaʿāmu-l-athīm * kal-muhli yaghlī fi-l-buṭūn * kaghaly-il-ḥamīm * khudhūhu faʿtilūhu ilā sawāʾi-l-jaḥīm * thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihī min ʿadhābi-l-ḥamīm * dhuq innaka anta-l-ʿazīzu-l-karīm * inna hādhā mā kuntum bihī tamtarūn.*

And in accessible English:

> "Indeed, the tree of Zaqqum is the food of the sinful one — like molten dregs, it boils in the bellies like the boiling of scalding water. *Seize him and drag him into the very midst of the Blaze, then pour over his head from the torment of scalding water.* *Taste! Indeed you — you were the mighty, the noble one! Indeed, this is the very thing you used to dispute.*"

---

### Rebuilding the world

To feel what these words did to the first people who heard them, you have to feel the Mecca they were spoken into.

This is a Meccan surah. The Muslims are few, mocked, and weak. And among those doing the mocking are men of enormous standing — men with names, lineage, wealth, the kind of social weight that makes a whole city lean their way when they speak. They sit in the shade of the Kaʿba and laugh at the idea that bodies will be raised, that there is a reckoning, that the poor freed slave praying over there will one day stand level with the chief of the clan.

When the Quran first mentioned a *tree in the Fire* — a tree that grows where there is no soil, no water, only flame — they seized on it as a joke. *A tree? In fire? Fire eats trees, it does not grow them.* One narration even has them mockingly calling for dates and butter, *"tazaqqamū"* — "have some Zaqqum!" — treating the warning as a punchline at dinner.

So understand the climate. This is not a passage that arrives into a room of believers nodding along. It arrives into a room of laughter. Into a culture where a handful of powerful men have crowned themselves with titles, and where the central thing being *argued about* — disputed, batted back and forth as a debate — is whether any of this is real at all.

Hold onto that word: *disputed.* Because the passage ends on it.

### The hook

There is a single letter in these eight verses that appears nowhere else in the entire Quran the way it appears here. One word, spelled in a way it is never spelled anywhere else in the Book — and the classical scholars noticed it more than a thousand years ago and wrote it down as a marvel.

There is a name in here so heavy in the mouth that you almost choke saying it — and that is the point.

And there is a sentence near the end where Allah takes two of His own most beautiful names and places them on the tongue of the tormentors as a taunt.

Three things hiding in thirty-eight words. Let's go and find them — slowly, and in order.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The tree that is spelled with its mouth open

Let me first teach you something about how Arabic words end, because the whole insight rests on it.

Many Arabic nouns that are feminine end in a sound we write with a special letter called *tāʾ marbūṭa* — literally the "tied" or "knotted" tāʾ. You can see it: ة. It is drawn like a little closed loop, almost like a knot pulled tight. And it behaves like a knot: when you stop on the word, when you pause at it, the sound *closes* — it softens into a quiet *h*. The mouth shuts. Think of the word *shajarah* — "a tree." You say *shajarah*, and you stop, and the ending seals itself off: *shajarah*.

There is another way to write that ending: the *open* tāʾ, ت — *tāʾ maftūḥa*, the "stretched-out" tāʾ. This one does not close. It stays a hard, open *t*.

Now here is the everyday version of the idea. Think of the difference between writing someone's name with a period at the end and writing it with a dash trailing off — *Sarah.* versus *Sarah—*. One is closed, finished, sealed. The other is left open, hanging, unfinished, still reaching for something. Same name. The punctuation changes what the eye feels.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the very first phrase:

> إِنَّ شَجَرَتَ ٱلزَّقُّومِ

*Inna shajarata-z-Zaqqūm* — "Indeed, the tree of Zaqqum."

The word for "tree," *shajarah*, is here written **شَجَرَتَ** — with the **open** tāʾ. Not the closed knot. The stretched, open *t*.

Now, here is what the classical scholars themselves recorded. Al-Qurtubi, in his great commentary, makes a striking observation. He says — and I am translating his words directly:

> *"Everything in the Book of Allah that mentions the* shajarah *— the tree — you stop on it with the* hāʾ *[the closed ending], **except for one single instance in Surah Ad-Dukhan**:* inna shajarata-z-Zaqqūm."*

Let that land. Of every tree in the entire Quran — the tree of Adam, the blessed olive tree, the trees of the gardens — **every single one** is written with the closed, knotted ending. **Every one but this one.** The one tree in the Quran spelled with its mouth held open is the tree of Zaqqum, the tree of the Fire.

This is not a theory. This is the documented written form of the muṣḥaf, noted by the scholars as the one exception in the Book.

[PAUSE]

Now — the grammar of *why* this happens is a technical matter of how the word sits in the sentence, and the scholars discuss that. So let me be careful and honest with you: the *cause* of the spelling is a known orthographic and grammatical matter. What I am about to offer is not the cause; it is what this unique shape *invites us to feel*.

Every other tree in the Book is sealed shut at the end — *closed, finished, at rest.* This tree alone is left open. The mouth does not close on it. The sound does not come to rest. There is something fitting, something that makes the skin prickle, in the realization that the one tree that will never let its eaters rest, the one tree whose fruit boils and never finishes its work inside them, is also the one tree the Book itself refuses to seal shut on the page.

It is written open. Like a mouth that cannot close. Like a wound that will not knit. Like a sentence with no period — a torment with no full stop.

What this single open letter reveals: **even the spelling refuses to give this tree any rest.** The eye sees what the soul will feel.

[PAUSE — let this one sit]

### Element Two: The name that the mouth cannot say cleanly — *Az-Zaqqūm*

Now stay with the same phrase, and move from how it is *written* to how it *sounds*.

Let me teach the principle first, because the Quran uses it constantly and once you see it you cannot unsee it. Sometimes a word *sounds like the thing it means.* In English we have small examples — "crash," "thud," "slither." The body of the word carries the meaning before your mind even translates it. Arabic does this at a far deeper level, and the Quran is its master. There are verses about ease that flow like water, and verses about punishment that are full of hard, blocked, choking consonants you have to fight your tongue to produce.

Now say this name out loud, slowly: **az-Zaqqūm.**

*Zaq — qūm.*

Feel where it happens in your mouth. The *zā* hisses. Then you hit the **qāf** — a heavy letter struck from the very back of the throat, near where you would gag — and here it is *doubled*, with a shadda, so you strike it twice, hard, *qq*, a catch deep in the throat. Then the *ūm* — the lips press shut at the end, sealing the mouth as if something will not come back up.

The root itself, *z-q-m*, is connected by the lexicographers to swallowing something foul, gulping down something repugnant with difficulty. The word *is* the act of choking it down. You cannot pronounce "Zaqqum" gently. You cannot make it pretty. The name fights your throat the way the food will fight the body.

And listen to what the early commentators say the thing actually *is.* Al-Jalalayn describes it as **"min akhbath ash-shajar"** — *of the foulest, most vile of trees* — bitter, a tree known from the region of Tihama, which Allah causes to grow in the Fire itself. Here the detail is worth holding. Tihama is the coastal lowland of western Arabia, the very landscape that surrounded Mecca — and the Meccans who first heard this warning were not encountering an alien plant from a foreign world. They knew this bitter tree from their own caravans and their own hills. Allah took the worst thing already growing in their landscape and told them it grows, perfected in its vileness, at the root of the Blaze. Al-Muyassar adds the dimension of *who* eats it: the tree that comes up from the very root of the Blaze, its fruit the food of *ṣāḥib al-āthām al-kathīra* — the one weighed down with great and many sins, the greatest of which is associating partners with Allah.

[PAUSE]

So put the layers together. A tree whose written name will not close. A spoken name that gags the throat. A meaning that *is* the choking-down of the foul. The form, the sound, and the meaning are all saying the same single thing, three different ways, before you have even reached what it *does.*

What this name reveals: **Allah did not just describe the horror — He built a word you have to suffer slightly just to pronounce.** The mercy in this is real, by the way. He is letting you taste a shadow of it now, in the safety of recitation, so that you might never taste the substance of it later.

[PAUSE]

---

Before moving to the thunderclap of verse 49, it is worth sitting with the simpler word that appears earlier and can too easily slide past in translation: *al-athīm* — "the sinful one" — the one for whom all this food is prepared. Al-Tabari pauses at it and gives its derivation: *athīm* comes from *athima ya'thamu fa-huwa athīm* — "to sin, he sins, so he is athīm." He is *dhū al-ithm*, "the possessor of ithm," the one in whom that quality has taken up residence as a defining characteristic. And then al-Tabari sharpens the lens further: in *this* passage, he specifies, the ithm being pointed to is not the full catalogue of human wrongdoing but one specific posture — *al-kufr bi-rabbihi* — disbelief in his Lord, above and beyond other sins. The food of Zaqqum is not a generic sentence on a generic class of sinners. It is the precise consequence of one particular stance toward truth: standing before the evidence and refusing it, again and again, until the refusal became the grain of the soul.

Al-Jalalayn, terse as always, names the type without hesitation. On *ṭaʿām al-athīm* it says: the food of Abu Jahl and his companions, "possessors of great sin." The scholars are already threading the passage before we reach its climax. The *al-athīm* of verse 44 who is made to eat this food and the one who hears the divine taunt of verse 49 are the same human figure, tracked across two moments of the same reckoning. What he is forced to eat and what he is forced to hear both circle back to *who he declared himself to be.*

### Element Three: *"Taste! Indeed you — you were the mighty, the noble!"*

Now we come to the verse that, the first time you truly understand it, can take the breath out of you.

> ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ
>
> *Dhuq innaka anta-l-ʿazīzu-l-karīm.*
>
> "Taste! Indeed you — you were the mighty, the noble one."

Let me teach two small grammatical things first, because the whole force of the verse lives in them.

**First**, the word *anta* — "you." In Arabic, you do not normally need it. The verb and the pronoun-endings already tell you who is being addressed. So when Arabic *adds* the standalone pronoun *anta* — "you" — on top of a sentence that did not need it, it is doing something deliberate: it is *pointing a finger.* It is emphasis, isolation, a spotlight. Not just "you are" but **"YOU — you, specifically, you of all people."**

**Second**, the two names: *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Karīm.* These are not ordinary adjectives. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty, the Invincible, the One who cannot be overpowered. *Al-Karīm* — the Noble, the Honored, the Generous beyond limit. These are two of the **most beautiful Names of Allah Himself.** When you say *Yā ʿAzīz, yā Karīm,* you are calling on your Lord.

Now hear the verse again with those two facts in hand. As the sinful one is being dragged by the neck into the midst of the Fire and scalding water is being poured over his head, a voice says to him:

**"Taste. Indeed YOU — *you* — are the Mighty, the Noble."**

[PAUSE]

This is mockery. It is the most devastating sarcasm in all of human language, because it is divine. And to feel the full edge of it, you need the occasion behind it.

Among the narrations the commentators preserve for this verse — and I want to be honest that the classical sources record this as *a* major reported occasion, not the only word ever said — is the account of one of the chief opponents in Mecca, the man the Muslims came to call Abu Jahl, "the father of ignorance." It is reported that he boasted of himself, standing between the two mountains of Mecca, that there was none *aʿazz* — none mightier — and none *akram* — none nobler — than himself. He crowned his own head with those two titles. He took the language that belongs to Allah and pinned it to his own chest.

And so on the Day when it all comes due, the words he gave himself are read back to him — not as praise, but as the caption over his torment. *"Taste it. After all — aren't you the Mighty? Aren't you the Noble? You said so yourself."*

[PAUSE]

Sit with the mechanism of this, because it is terrifying and it is just. He is not punished with someone else's words. He is punished with *his own.* The titles he stole become the rope they drag him with. He wrote his own taunt, years before he heard it.

He did not merely sin; he set himself up as a small god. And the verse strips the costume off in front of the whole creation: *here is your "might" — being dragged by the neck. Here is your "nobility" — scalding water poured over your head from above, beginning at the crown, where the titles were stored.*

Now the classical commentary returns with doubled force. Al-Jalalayn had already identified the *al-athīm* of verse 44 — the one for whom this food is specifically prepared — as Abu Jahl and his companions. The passage is a unified portrait, integrated across all eight verses: the same man is *kāfir* (al-Tabari's precise specification of the ithm), the same man is named as *al-athīm* who eats the Zaqqum (al-Jalalayn's reading of verse 44), and the same man hears his own boast handed back as a taunt (verse 49). The scholars read one thread straight through. He did not just receive the punishment as if from outside — he received the *completion* of what he had set in motion by naming himself the way he did.

There is something here even deeper than one man's story. *Al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Karīm* are real — they are *genuinely* the Mighty and the Noble. The point is not that might and nobility are bad. The point is *to whom they belong.* The sinful one's crime was not wanting honor. It was claiming, for himself, a station that is Allah's alone. He did not merely sin; he *displaced* the divine. And what justice does here is not invent a new punishment from outside — it assembles the punishment from his own material. The taunt on the Day is built from the vocabulary of his own boast.

What this verse reveals: **self-coronation with divine names is not merely presumptuous — it is a promissory note, redeemable on the Day.** Whatever title you claimed for yourself will be read back to you at precisely the moment when its falseness is most visible. The language you misappropriated becomes the sentence delivered over you.

### Element Four: The choreography of verses 47–48 — *"Seize him — drag him — pour"*

After the food of Zaqqum and the portrait of the one who eats it, the surah shifts register. It becomes operational. Three commands arrive in rapid succession across verses 47 and 48, each addressed in the plural — not one agent but many, the gathered forces of the reckoning:

*Khudhūhu* — "Seize him."

*Faʿtilūhu ilā sawāʾi-l-jaḥīm* — "Drag him into the very middle of the Blaze."

*Thumma ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihī min ʿadhābi-l-ḥamīm* — "Then pour over his head from the torment of scalding water."

The word *ʿatl* — "drag" — carries a specific quality of violence in the classical lexicon. It is not a leading, not an escorting. It is to haul something against its nature, to yank what resists. The one who would not come when called — who argued, mocked, and disputed — is now dragged. The will that made him resist in this life is overridden entirely, and the body goes where he never once intended to go.

*Ilā sawāʾi-l-jaḥīm* — into the *middle* of the Blaze. *Sawāʾ* is from a root meaning equal, level, the exact midpoint — not at the edge, not near the edge, but the equidistant center, where the fire is most perfectly uniform in every direction. The one who thought himself the most important man between two mountains — the most central figure in Mecca's social world — is placed at the center of something else entirely.

Then: *ṣubbū fawqa raʾsihī* — "pour over his head." The word *ṣabb* describes a pour from above, a cascade, a waterfall of affliction. It falls first on the head — the seat of thought, the locus of identity, the place where a man wears his crown. The scalding water begins at the top, where the claims were stored, and works its way through.

[PAUSE]

Look at the architecture of it. First the food: what enters the body from within, the Zaqqum that boils in the belly. Then the assault from without and above: the scalding water poured over the head. The body is caught between what rises from below and what descends from above. There is no inside that is safe. There is no outside that offers relief. And precisely in that moment of total exposure — between the food rising and the water falling — the taunt arrives: *"Taste. Indeed YOU are the Mighty, the Noble."*

The sequence is not accidental. *Khudhūhu, faʿtilūhu, ṣubbū* — seize, drag, pour — three commands without pause, without deliberation, without the smallest gap in which dignity could take shelter. What he experienced in the world as the long luxury of power and argument meets its inversion in a chain of imperatives with no space between them.

### Element Five: The last word — *tamtarūn*

Verse 50 closes the whole passage with a word that carries the weight of everything argued in Mecca.

*Inna hādhā mā kuntum bihī tamtarūn.*

"Indeed, this is the very thing you used to dispute."

*Tamtarūn* comes from the root *m-r-y*, and the classical lexicographers trace this root to the original action of milking — the pulling and releasing, the back-and-forth motion against a source, testing whether it will yield. From that physical image came the sense of *doubt in its active form*: not a passive not-knowing, but an arguing, a going back and forth on a matter as if to see whether it will give differently if you pull from another angle. *Imtarā fī al-amr* — "he disputed the matter" — carries the sense of hands still moving, the argument still going, the thing still being tested.

This is precisely what they *did* with the truth. They did not simply reject it once and walk away. They held it, pulled at it, called for dates and butter to go with their "Zaqqum," debated its absurdity in the shade of the Kaʿba, traded jokes about it at their gatherings. The truth was a live thing they kept actively disputing. *Tamtarūn* is imperfect — continuous, habitual, ongoing. You *used to* dispute this. As a practice. Repeatedly. Over years.

And now: *this.* Here. What you are standing in the middle of. *This* is it — not something else, not something that could be debated further. The very thing. What your hands kept pulling at, as if it might yield on the next attempt — it did not yield. It arrived.

[PAUSE]

Notice the shift from singular to plural. Verse 49 said *"you"* — singular, the one being dragged, the self-proclaimed *ʿAzīz* and *Karīm*, the specific man. Verse 50 widens the lens: *kuntum bihī tamtarūn* — "you all used to dispute it." The whole community of mockers is gathered into the final word. What was done by a man was done by a movement. The ones who laughed at the tree, the ones who called for butter and dates, the whole salon of elegant dismissal — all of them are addressed together. *This is what you all used to argue about.* The company that formed around the denial is assembled at its resolution.

The passage began with a tree that was laughed at. It ends with the truth of the tree surrounding everyone who laughed.

What the last word reveals: **the passage closes exactly where the Meccan argument began.** Verse 50 does not add new information. It closes the loop. *The thing you disputed — you are now in the answer.*

[PAUSE]

## PART 2: WHAT THESE EIGHT VERSES LEAVE BEHIND

Pull back now from the close reading and look at the whole shape.

It begins with a food. It ends with a word for arguing. Between them: a derivation chain that specifies kufr, a tree known from the local landscape transplanted to the Fire's root, a dragging, a pouring, and a taunt assembled from the victim's own vocabulary.

The surah will turn immediately after verse 50 to its mirror image — the *muttaqin*, the ones who maintained reverence, in a *maqām amīn*, a station of security, among gardens and springs, dressed and facing one another as honored guests. The darkness of verses 43–50 is not all the surah has to say. The diptych is deliberate: this panel and that one, placed side by side so the reader stands between them and feels the distance between the two destinations.

But these eight verses carry a teaching of their own, independent of the mirror. And it is this:

**The things we argue with, the titles we give ourselves, and the way we treat warnings when they arrive into a world of laughter — all of it is on record.** Not as a held grudge, but as a structural fact. What you claimed will be returned to you as testimony. What you disputed will be returned as the ground you stand on.

The man who called himself *aʿazz* and *akram* between two mountains was not punished for wanting greatness. He was punished for taking names that are not human property — and, as al-Tabari's specification of ithm makes clear, for the disbelief that made such a taking possible in the first place. When you have refused the One to whom those names belong, when you have rejected the evidence again and again until kufr became the grain of you, the self-coronation is not a separate offense — it is the same offense wearing a different face. And the punishment is not invented from outside him. It is *assembled from his own words.* Divine justice, at this level, completes the sentence the sinner began.

There is a mercy hidden in all of this, if you can hold it. Every time you hear the name Zaqqum and feel your throat tighten slightly — feel the *qāf* catch at the back — you are being given something. A small rehearsal. A moment in which the abstract becomes briefly physical, in the safety of recitation, at a distance that will not harm you. The Book is showing you the destination at the far end of a particular path, with enough vividness that the path loses its appeal. The mercy is in the clarity. The mercy is in the warning issued before the gate is closed.

And the alternative is right there, forty-three words later. The *muttaqin* are in gardens. The security that waits on one side is as real as the scorching on the other. The Book does not give you the dark panel without the light panel immediately adjacent.

That is the diptych. This is the dark side of it.

Look at it long enough to carry something away, and then turn the page.
