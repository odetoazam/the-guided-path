# If You Show Me Their Punishment, Don't Place Me Among Them

## Surah 23, Ayahs 93-94 — Al-Mu'minun

> قُل رَّبِّ إِمَّا تُرِيَنِّي مَا يُوعَدُونَ
> رَبِّ فَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي فِي ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

> *Qul rabbi imma turiyanni ma yu'aduna,*
> *rabbi fa la taj'alni fi-l-qawmi-z-zalimin.*

> "Say: My Lord, if You should ever show me that which they are promised — my Lord, do not place me within the wrongdoing people."

---

## INTRODUCTION — The World Inside This Du'a

To understand what is happening in these two short ayahs, we have to feel the ground the Prophet ﷺ is standing on as he is taught to say them.

Surah Al-Mu'minun is a long surah. It opens with one of the most beloved openings in the Quran — *qad aflaha-l-mu'minun*, "the believers have indeed succeeded" — and then walks through a careful catalogue of who they are. People of khushuʿ in their salah. People who turn away from idle talk. People who guard their trusts and their covenants. People who keep the appointment with the prayer.

That's the first frame. The successful ones.

Then the surah pivots. It walks through human creation — sperm-drop, clinging clot, lump of flesh, bones clothed in flesh. It walks through the prophets — Nuh, the long story; then a generation after; then Musa and Harun; then "the messengers, one after another." And every time a prophet is sent, the same script repeats: *the leaders of his people who disbelieved said...* The mockery. The rejection. The accusation of madness.

By the time we arrive at the section we are sitting with today, the surah has done something specific. It has placed the Prophet ﷺ in a long line of messengers who lived their entire lives in cities full of mockers. Surrounded. Outnumbered. Facing the same script, generation after generation.

And then Allah tells him: *qul* — say.

Say what? Say a du'a.

Now — pause for a moment. What kind of du'a would you expect at this point?

After everything the Prophet ﷺ has endured? After Quraysh's mockery? After Ta'if? After the boycott? After the leaders saying *innama anta basharun mithluna*, "you're just a man like us"?

You might expect a du'a *against* them. *O Allah, hasten their punishment. O Allah, defeat them. O Allah, vindicate me.*

But that is not the du'a Allah teaches him.

The du'a Allah teaches him is a prayer about the Prophet's own self. Not about their destruction at all — the destruction is taken as a given, a thing already promised — but about whether *he* will be standing in the wrong place when the storm arrives.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The classical commentators are direct about what this prayer is asking. Al-Tabari reads the du'a as: *"do not destroy me with what You destroy them; deliver me from Your punishment and Your wrath."* That is the primary register: shared destruction. The Prophet ﷺ — the most beloved human being to walk the earth — is taught to fear being *destroyed together* with the wrongdoers. Not afraid that he has done what they did. Not afraid of some personal failure. Afraid of being numbered inside the category of the people upon whom Allah's punishment descends.

Why?

That is what we are going to spend the next hour with.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

Three things in the Arabic of these thirteen words are doing structural work. We will move through them one at a time.

### Element One: The Doubled Intensification — *Imma turiyanni*

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small piece of Arabic grammar, because without it the next minute will feel flat.

In English, we have one word for "if." We say "if it rains, take an umbrella." That's it. One conditional.

In Arabic, conditionality has gradations. There is *in* — a neutral conditional, "if it should happen." There is *idha* — "when, at the time that," more certain. There is *law* — counterfactual, "if it were the case (but it isn't)." Each one has its own weight.

But there is also a special construction: *imma*. *Imma* is *in* fused with an extra particle *ma*. That extra *ma* — the classical grammarians call it *ma al-zaʾida*, the "added *ma*" — does not change the meaning of the conditional. It *intensifies* it. Like the difference between "if" and "if ever" or "should it ever come to pass."

Now there's a second device. In Arabic, you can attach to a verb something called the *nun al-tawkid* — the "energetic nun" or "nun of emphasis." It is a heavy *nn* sound clipped to the end of the verb, and it functions a bit like underlining a word in writing. It says: *this is going to happen, mark my words.*

Hold those two pieces in your mind. The intensified conditional, *imma*. And the emphatic nun, *-anni*.

Now look at the verb in our ayah:

**إِمَّا تُرِيَنِّي**

*Imma* — the intensified conditional.
*Turiyanni* — the verb "you show me," with the energetic nun fused right onto it.

Both at the same time.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does. It is grammatically possible to say in Arabic, "if You show me what they are promised" with much lighter machinery. *In turini* would have done the job. The Quran could have used the simpler construction.

Instead, Allah stacks two emphasis markers on top of each other. *Imma turiyanni.* "If You should *ever truly* show me." "Should it absolutely come to pass."

What does this mean for the prayer?

It means the Prophet ﷺ is not being taught to ask hypothetically about something that probably won't happen. He is being taught to pray for a contingency he is being told to take *seriously*. The grammatical scaffolding itself signals: this thing — the punishment of the wrongdoers — is real enough that you must prepare for it. Real enough that you must place yourself, in advance, on the right side of the line.

Here is what is remarkable. Allah is the One who already knows whether this will happen in the Prophet's lifetime or not. Allah is the One issuing the du'a. And Allah designs the verb-form so that the speaker, the Prophet ﷺ, is forced to *take seriously* the day of reckoning even as he prays.

This is a teaching about prayer itself. **The grammar of how you ask shapes the seriousness of what you ask about.** Allah teaches His Prophet ﷺ — and through him, us — to pray about consequence as if consequence were certain to arrive.

[PAUSE]

That is the first weight in this ayah. The doubled intensifier sets the temperature of the whole prayer. *This is not academic. This is real. Pray accordingly.*

### Element Two: The Vocative, Twice — *Rabbi… Rabbi*

Look again at the two ayahs as one continuous prayer:

**رَّبِّ** إِمَّا تُرِيَنِّي مَا يُوعَدُونَ
**رَبِّ** فَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي فِي ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

The address — *rabbi*, "my Lord" — appears twice. Once at the opening of the conditional. Once at the opening of the request.

In Arabic, when you address someone, you typically use the vocative *ya* — *ya Rabbi*, "O my Lord." But here Allah teaches a more intimate form: *Rabbi*, with the *ya* dropped, the suffix shortened, the call clipped down to the most familiar register a servant has. Like the difference, in English, between calling out "Father!" and saying simply "Dad."

Now — once is normal. Once would have been enough. The second sentence is grammatically a continuation of the first. The Prophet ﷺ has already addressed his Lord. Repeating *rabbi* is, technically, not necessary.

So why is it there?

Al-Tabari noticed something precise in the grammar here. He observed that the *fa* in *fa la taj'alni* — "so do not place me" — is the apodosis, the response-clause, to the earlier conditional *imma turiyanni*: if [this is shown to me] → then [do not place me]. These two parts grammatically belong together across the whole of the prayer. But the second calling of *rabbi* intervenes between them, interrupting the expected flow of the conditional structure. Al-Tabari's point is that this works — the *fa* is still valid — precisely because the entire request is being addressed to Allah at both ends, with the vocative holding open the relationship from the first word to the last.

Sit with its function.

**The first thing.** The repetition is not redundancy — it is *re-grounding*. After the conditional clause has been laid out — *imma turiyanni ma yu'aduna*, "if You should ever show me what they are promised" — there has been a long, heavy thought. The mind has gone out into the future, into a scene of divine punishment, into a contingency. Before issuing the actual request, the heart returns home. *Rabbi.* Back to You. Back to the One I am asking. The heart does not let the request drift away from the relationship.

**The second thing — and this is heavier.** The second *rabbi* is positioned right at the door of a request that is, frankly, audacious. The Prophet ﷺ is about to ask Allah not to place him *in* a particular group of people. He is about to ask for a kind of categorical separation. Before stepping through that door, he steadies himself by re-saying the word *rabbi*. As if to remind himself: *I am asking the One who places people wherever He wills. And I am asking Him on the basis of being His servant — not on the basis of any other claim.*

[PAUSE]

There is a teaching here about how we make hard requests of Allah. The big ones. The ones where you are asking Him to rearrange your life, to remove you from one place and put you in another, to spare you from the consequences of being where you are.

You don't make those requests in one breath. You don't rush them out. You return to the relationship before you make the request. *Rabbi… rabbi.* You re-anchor in who He is to you before you ask Him for what you need.

Notice this is being taught not to a beginner. It is being taught to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. The one with the most direct relationship to Allah of any human who ever lived. Even *he* is taught to repeat the vocative before the request. To re-ground in *rabbi* before issuing *fa la taj'alni*.

What does that teach us about how we should approach our own difficult du'as?

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Preposition That Holds the Whole Prayer — *Fi*, Not *Maʿa*

This is the heaviest move in the ayah. It is one preposition. One word. Two letters. And the entire weight of the du'a hangs on it.

Look at the request:

**فَلَا تَجْعَلْنِي فِي ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ**

"Do not place me **fi** the wrongdoing people."

In Arabic, there are two prepositions that an English ear might collapse into one. *Maʿa* and *fi*.

*Maʿa* means "with." Companionship. Alongside. *Anta maʿi* — "you are with me." Two distinct things adjacent to each other, but each preserving its own boundary. You can be *maʿa* a group and still be clearly *not* one of them.

*Fi* means "in." Containment. Insidedness. *Al-maʾu fi-l-kuwbi* — "the water is in the cup." The thing inside is enclosed within the container — bounded by its walls, sharing its fate. What happens to the container happens to what is within it.

The Quran uses both prepositions, deliberately, in different places. In Surah At-Tawbah, Allah commands: *kunu maʿa-s-sadiqin* — "be with the truthful" (9:119). *Maʿa.* Be alongside them. Take their company. The truthful retain their distinct identity, and you are placed adjacent to them.

But here, in our ayah, the Prophet ﷺ is taught to ask Allah: *do not place me* **fi** *the wrongdoing people.*

Not "with." **In.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment. What is the difference?

To be *maʿa* the wrongdoers is to be next to them. Sitting beside them in the marketplace. Walking past them in the street. You are still you. They are still them. There is air between you.

To be *fi* the wrongdoers is to be *inside* them. To have crossed the membrane. To be no longer distinguishable from them. From above — from the perspective of the One who places people — you are part of the pattern. Part of the group. When His judgment falls on the group, it falls on what is *inside* the group.

The classical commentators read this preposition in a specific, concrete way. Al-Tabari says the prayer asks: "do not destroy me with what You destroy them — deliver me from Your punishment and Your wrath." Al-Muyassar echoes this exactly. The *fi* is not only a spatial marker — it is about the mechanism of shared consequence. To be *fi* a people is to be inside the category that receives the punishment as a unit. The destruction does not discriminate between individuals within the group; it falls on the *qawm*, and it falls on all who are found within it.

Who is *al-qawm al-zalimin* here? The classical commentators are specific: al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both identify them as *al-mushrikin* — the polytheists of Makkah, the Quraysh who rejected and mocked. The promised punishment in view is the punishment for their shirk and their sustained rejection of the messenger. The prayer is rooted in a historical moment: the Prophet ﷺ living among them, knowing what has been promised to them, asking Allah to keep him categorically separate from what is coming for them.

[PAUSE]

This is why the preposition is doing the work the rest of the ayah cannot do.

The Prophet ﷺ is not asking, "do not let them be near me." That would be unrealistic — he was sent to them, to live among them, to call them. He is not asking for physical removal. He is asking for *categorical* removal. *When You sort, when the moment of reckoning comes and You divide humanity into who-belongs-to-what, do not put me inside this group's category. Do not let me be found within the pattern that is about to be erased.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Notice what is not in the prayer. The Prophet ﷺ does not say "do not let them harm me." He does not say "remove them from my path." He does not say "destroy them quickly." The prayer is not about *them* at all.

The prayer is about whether *he* will be located *inside* their category when the reckoning arrives.

That is a stunning thing for a prophet to be afraid of. It is even more stunning when you realize Allah is the one who taught him this fear.

What is Allah signaling? That the categories Allah uses to sort humanity are real, and that proximity to a category — long enough, intimate enough, unprotested enough — can move you *into* it, not just adjacent to it.

This is why so many other ayahs in the Quran echo this same warning with different language. *Wa la tarkanu ila-lladhina zalamu* — "do not lean toward those who do wrong" (11:113). *Innakum idhan mithluhum* — "you would then be like them" (4:140). The Quran keeps returning to one anxiety: that we will become indistinguishable from what surrounds us.

The two-letter word *fi* in this ayah is a compressed warning about the entire phenomenon. **Watch your prepositions. Watch where you are located. Adjacency is one thing. Being inside is another.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have looked at three small features. The doubled intensifier — *imma turiyanni* — that makes the contingency real. The repeated vocative — *rabbi… rabbi* — that re-grounds the request in relationship at both ends, wrapping the entire conditional inside an address to Allah. The preposition *fi* — that turns the entire du'a into a prayer about not being counted among those upon whom the punishment falls as a unit.

Each of these, on its own, is a grammatical observation. But put together, they reveal something the surah is doing at a structural level. It is teaching the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every reader — that proximity to a condemned people is a real spiritual danger. That the categories Allah uses to sort humanity are not visible to the naked eye. And that the only way to ensure you are not inside the wrong category is to ask the One who does the sorting.

Before we move forward: notice what the prayer does *not* contain. It does not ask for victory over the mushrikin. It does not ask for revenge. It does not ask for protection from their harm. It asks only for categorical distance from their consequence.

That reticence is itself a teaching.

## PART 2 — THE STAKES OF THE PRAYER

### Theme One: Why Does a Prophet Pray This?

There is a question that many people feel but do not always voice when they sit with this du'a:

If the Prophet ﷺ is divinely protected — if Allah has conferred upon him *ʿisma*, the prophetic preservation from sins that would compromise his mission — why does he need to pray not to be placed among the wrongdoers?

A man who cannot fall into what they fell into does not need to worry about being categorized with them. So what is happening?

First: we should be precise about what *ʿisma* means. The classical theologians — across the Ashʿarī and Māturīdī schools — define prophetic *ʿisma* as Allah's preservation of His prophets from any sin or error that would undermine the prophetic office: corrupting the message, leading people astray, any moral failure that would make them unworthy of trust. It is a comprehensive protection, not a narrow one. The prophets will not be confused with the people they were sent to correct.

So *ʿisma* means the Prophet ﷺ will not become *one of* the wrongdoers through his own action. What it does not handle is the question of his location. He was sent to the mushrikin. He lived among them. He was commanded to call them until the last possible moment.

And the Quran is teaching him: nearness has its own risk. Not the risk of *becoming* them — but the risk of being *found* among them when the reckoning comes. Of being present inside the circle of people upon whom the judgment descends as a unit.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir frames this du'a specifically as a prayer for the moment when *calamity strikes* — not only the eschatological punishment, but any moment of divine reckoning that falls upon a people while the Prophet ﷺ is among them. The prayer is: *if I am present, if I am witnessing that event — do not let me be counted among the ones upon whom it falls.*

The Prophet ﷺ is being taught to pray for his *location in the moment of judgment*, not for his moral status. Those are two different things. His moral status is secured by *ʿisma*. His location is not secured by anything except this prayer.

This distinction matters enormously when we ask: what does this du'a mean for us?

We are not protected by *ʿisma*. We have no such guarantee. If the Prophet ﷺ is taught to pray this prayer, we need it far more urgently.

### Theme Two: The People, Not Just the Actions

Look again at what the prayer asks for: *do not place me fi-l-qawm al-zalimin* — "in the wrongdoing people."

Not "do not let me do what they do." Not "protect me from their specific sins." **The wrongdoing *people.*** The collective.

There is something important here about how the Quran thinks about collective identity. A *qawm* — a people, a group — is more than a sum of individuals sharing a location. A *qawm* has a shared trajectory. A shared fate. When a *qawm* is described in the Quran as having been warned, and having rejected the warning, they become a unit of reckoning. What comes to them, comes to the *qawm* — including whoever is inside it.

The history of divine punishment in the Quran — the people of Nuh, the people of Lut, the people of ʿAad and Thamud — follows this grammar. Punishments fall on peoples, not just individuals. Lut ﷺ was commanded to leave with his household before the destruction came. Not because everyone still inside the city had personally committed every wrong — but because *being inside* in the moment of its destruction was a categorical danger. The prayer the Prophet ﷺ is taught here reaches back to that same logic.

[PAUSE]

There is a difficult implication here, and it is worth sitting with rather than rushing past.

Collective consequence is one of the most challenging aspects of divine justice for modern readers. We feel instinctively that people should face consequences individually, for what they individually did. Why should a city be swept when its leaders carry the greater guilt?

The Quran does not answer this question abstractly. But the du'a we are sitting with contains a response encoded in its grammar: if you are asking *not to be swept up*, the address is to Allah, not to a philosophical argument. The Prophet ﷺ does not protest the logic of collective reckoning. He does not ask for its abolition. He asks to be *outside* of it. That is a form of reverence in itself — acknowledging the reality of divine judgment while placing yourself entirely in the hands of the One who exercises it.

### Theme Three: The Positive Pole of the Prayer

Something the text does not say explicitly, but which the classical commentators surface, is that this du'a has a positive counterpart.

Al-Tabari's reading of the prayer closes with: *"but make me among those whom You are pleased with, from Your close servants."* Al-Muyassar echoes the same. The prayer is not merely *remove me from them* — it implies *place me somewhere else*, among those who receive Your favor. The two halves belong together.

The structure of the du'a as we have it in the ayah contains only the negative request: *do not place me inside the wrongdoers.* But the full logic of the petition — as the classical commentators heard it — carries an implicit positive: *place me among Your awliya instead.* Asking to be separated from one category is simultaneously asking to be joined to another.

This matters for how we make this du'a. It is not a du'a of escape alone. It is a du'a of reorientation. The person who says *rabbi fa la taj'alni fi-l-qawmi-z-zalimin* is not just asking to avoid punishment — they are positioning themselves toward belonging to a different community entirely. The community of those with whom Allah is pleased.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Four: What This Looks Like for Us

The hadith literature — Ibn Kathir connects this du'a specifically to narrations recorded by Imam Ahmad and al-Tirmidhi about calling on Allah when calamity and fitna approach — places this prayer in a very practical context. It is not a prayer for ordinary times. It is a prayer for precisely the moments when the categories are shifting around you, when a reckoning is building, when you can feel something coming but cannot yet see where the lines will fall.

There is a tradition in which the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have warned: when a punishment falls on a people, it falls on all who are found among them, and then they will be raised according to their deeds (*a'māl*). The word is deeds — what they actually did, how they actually lived, what they were actually part of. The raising mirrors the living.

This is where the du'a becomes a daily practice rather than a historical artifact.

We live surrounded by collective patterns. Systems of injustice that have their own momentum. Communities that drift toward outcomes none of their individual members may have intended. The question the du'a presses on us is not "did I personally do the worst thing?" The question is: *am I inside this? Have I been drawn into the pattern? Would a reckoning that falls on this group find me within it?*

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ was inside the city of Quraysh. He walked their streets. He knew their leaders by name. He was, in every external sense, *among* them. But something kept him *not fi* them. His refusal. His protest. His continuous call. The fact that no one watching from above would confuse his trajectory with theirs.

The question the du'a leaves us with is: what keeps *us* not-fi the patterns we are surrounded by?

It is not just moral purity in private. It is the kind of active distinction that makes you visible as *not inside*. The word spoken. The thing refused. The line drawn. The prayer made — specifically, by name, to the One who does the sorting — that when the accounting comes, He will not find you counted among what you never truly chose.

*Rabbi fa la taj'alni fi-l-qawmi-z-zalimin.*

"My Lord, do not place me inside the wrongdoing people."

That prayer is not said once. It is said in every moment of proximity to what threatens to absorb you. And it is said — as the Prophet ﷺ modeled — not with accusation toward others, but with anxiety about yourself.

[PAUSE — final pause]

## CLOSE — The Prayer's Last Note

There is one more thing to notice before we leave.

The surah is called Al-Mu'minun — the believers. It opened with a roll call of who the believers are. It ends by teaching the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every believer — what to fear.

The fear is not that they will be attacked. The fear is not that they will be defeated. The fear is that they will be *sorted wrong*. That on the day when Allah delivers what He has promised to the mushrikin of Makkah — or to the people of any city, in any age — the believer will be found *inside* the wrong group rather than *alongside* those whom Allah favors.

And so the surah that opened with *qad aflaha-l-mu'minun* — "the believers have succeeded" — closes with a du'a about what it means to remain a believer when you are surrounded by those who have not.

You remain one by asking to remain one.

*Rabbi fa la taj'alni fi-l-qawmi-z-zalimin.*

And — following the full spirit of what the classical commentators heard in this prayer — by asking for its positive twin: *make me among those with whom You are pleased, from Your close servants.*

Both halves together. The turning away, and the turning toward.
