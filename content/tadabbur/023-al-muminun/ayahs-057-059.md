# The Fear That Pulls Closer

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 57–59

> [ayah:23:57] إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ هُم مِّنْ خَشْيَةِ رَبِّهِم مُّشْفِقُونَ
> [ayah:23:58] وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم بِـَٔايَـٰتِ رَبِّهِمْ يُؤْمِنُونَ
> [ayah:23:59] وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم بِرَبِّهِمْ لَا يُشْرِكُونَ

*Inna 'lladhīna hum min khashyati rabbihim mushfiqūn*
*Wa 'lladhīna hum bi-āyāti rabbihim yu'minūn*
*Wa 'lladhīna hum bi-rabbihim lā yushrikūn*

**"Indeed, those who — from awe of their Lord — are tenderly apprehensive. And those who, in the signs of their Lord, believe. And those who, with their Lord, do not associate anything."**

---

## Introduction — Building the World

I want you to picture two people, both standing in front of the same speaker.

The first has done nothing. He's in the back, making jokes with his friends. He's not afraid of anything, because he hasn't risked anything. He hasn't tried, hasn't prepared, hasn't laid anything on the line. So he's relaxed. Loose. Confident in a vague way.

The second has spent a year preparing. He's rehearsed every sentence, walked through every scenario in his mind a hundred times. He's done the work. And he is **trembling**. His hands are shaking. He cannot eat. The closer he gets to standing up, the more terrified he becomes.

Now ask yourself: which of these two cares more?

Which of them has more love for what's about to happen? Which of them has more *understanding* of what's at stake?

Notice something about where the trembling actually comes from — and this matters for everything that follows. He is not afraid simply because he tried hard. He is afraid because he now *understands what is being weighed*. The preparation gave him knowledge. The fear is the knowledge speaking.

[PAUSE]

The Quran is about to give us a portrait of who actually wins. And the answer is going to feel — at first — completely upside-down. Because the winners in Surah Al-Mu'minun are not the people who feel safe. They are the people who tremble.

This passage you're about to encounter sits inside one of the most quietly radical movements in the entire Quran. Surah Al-Mu'minun opened with a confident declaration: *qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn* — the believers have won. The surah then traces what those winners look like from the outside: their prayer is humble, their speech is purified of emptiness, their wealth is purified through giving, their bodies are guarded, their trusts are kept, their witness is true.

But then — right around where we are now — the surah turns. It pivots from the outside to the inside. It stops describing what the winner does and starts describing what the winner *feels*. And what the surah is about to reveal is something so strange, so contrary to our intuitions about religion, that the Prophet ﷺ's own beloved wife will misunderstand it.

A few ayahs after the ones we're entering, ʿĀ'isha (raḍiya 'llāhu ʿanhā) will ask the Messenger ﷺ a question. The verses describe people *whose hearts are fearful while they give*. And ʿĀ'isha — sharp, brilliant, asking the right question — says: *Is this about people who steal, who drink, who commit adultery, and they're afraid of being caught?*

And the Prophet ﷺ will answer her: *No, daughter of the Truthful One. These are people who fast and pray and give in charity — and they fear it won't be accepted.*

[PAUSE]

That answer changes everything. Because it tells us that **the people most afraid in this religion are not the people farthest from Allah. They are the people closest to Him.**

The verses we're about to read are the foundation of that paradox. They describe the inner world that produces the racer. The state of soul that produces the trembling giver. The interior architecture of the winner.

There is a word in this passage that doesn't quite mean "fear" the way you think it does. There is another word that comes from the Arabic word for **twilight**. There is an absence that you might miss if you're not looking. And there is a parallel between these believers and a description of *angels* that the Quran will use a few surahs later.

Let's go in.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Khashya* — The Fear That Comes From Knowledge

Before we open the word, I want to teach you something about Arabic.

In English, we use one word — "fear" — for many different states of mind. The fear of a child hearing thunder. The fear of a soldier before battle. The fear of a parent watching a child cross the street. The fear of an employee being called into HR. The fear of a poet standing before something beautiful. The fear of a worshipper standing before God.

We call all of these "fear" because we don't have words for the differences. But the differences are everything.

Arabic does have words for the differences. And the Quran uses them precisely.

There is a word *khawf* — fear of harm, fear of consequence, the fear that wants to run away. *Khawf* arises from imagining what something might do to you. It's the fear of the unknown threat. The Quran uses *khawf* often, and it's a real and legitimate fear. We are sometimes told to have it.

But there is another word — and it is the word we encounter here. *Khashya*.

[PAUSE]

*Khashya* is not the fear of harm. It is the fear of **majesty**. It is the trembling that arises not because something is about to hurt you, but because something is real beyond your capacity to comprehend. *Khashya* is what the heart does when it actually understands who it is in front of.

The classical lexicons make this distinction explicit: *khawf* is fear arising from anticipation of pain; *khashya* is fear arising from **knowledge of greatness**. *Khawf* requires imagination of harm. *Khashya* requires recognition of glory.

And this is why the Quran tells us, in Surah Fāṭir, that *the only ones among His servants who possess khashya of Allah are the people of knowledge.* Not because everyone else is exempt — but because *khashya* is a function of how clearly you see. The more clearly you see Him, the more you tremble. The trembling is not in spite of your knowledge. The trembling **is** your knowledge — the trembling is what knowledge of Him *feels like* when it actually lands in a heart.

Here is what makes this portrait so startling. Ibn Kathir notes that the people described in this ayah have *already arrived at the station of Ihsan* — they have faith, they do righteous deeds, they have reached spiritual excellence. And yet the deeper they go, the more the trembling intensifies. This is not the awe of the spiritually undeveloped. It is the mark of those who have gone deep enough to *actually see* how great He is. Al-Hasan al-Basri captured this with devastating precision: *The believer combines Ihsan with awe — while the disbeliever combines evil deeds with a sense of security.* The further along the path, the more awake you become to the magnitude of what you are walking toward. The one who has done nothing feels relaxed. The one who has arrived trembles most.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The fear that runs from harm is generated *by you* — you imagine the threat, your nervous system reacts. But *khashya* is not generated by you. *Khashya* is what happens to you when something larger than you is recognized.

The fear of harm is **outgoing**. It pushes you back from the threat.

The fear of majesty is **incoming**. It pulls you forward.

Stay with that distinction. Because we're about to see Allah do something with the grammar that proves this exact point.

Look at the ayah again:

*Inna 'lladhīna hum **min khashyati rabbihim** mushfiqūn*

**"Those who — *from* the awe of their Lord — are apprehensive."**

The word *min* here is not a casual preposition. In classical Arabic, this *min* is *sababiyya* — it indicates source, origin, the well from which something flows. The believers' state of *mushfiqūn* — a word we'll come to in a moment — is described as flowing **from** the *khashya* of their Lord.

In other words: their inner state is not something they manufactured. It is something that **descended** upon them from awareness of who Allah is.

The fear isn't theirs. The fear is *Allah's reality* arriving in their hearts.

This is the difference between someone trying to be scared because religion told them to be scared — and someone whose heart has actually woken up to who they are standing before. The first is exhausting. It drains. It can't last. The second is the natural response of a soul that has finally seen.

You don't have to *try* to feel small in front of an ocean. You just have to actually look at the ocean. *Khashya* is what happens when the heart actually looks at Allah.

[PAUSE]

And here's the beautiful, almost devastating thing the Quran is telling us. The believers in this ayah are not described as *generating* their fear. They are described as **being acted upon by it**. The *khashya* is the verb being done to them. They are receivers, not producers.

This is why a person can't simply will themselves into proper fear of Allah. You can't grit your teeth and try harder to be afraid. *Khashya* doesn't come from effort; it comes from *seeing*. And seeing comes from being shown.

Which means the way to grow in this fear is not to try to feel afraid. It is to ask Allah to **show Himself** to your heart more clearly. *Khashya* is what your heart does naturally when He answers that prayer.

And this descent does not stay interior. Al-Tabari's reading of this ayah is pointed: the *khashya* that arrives in the heart makes the mushfiqun *constant in obedience* — دائبون في طاعته — and *earnest in their pursuit of His pleasure* — جادّون في طلب مرضاته. The trembling is not a passive state. It is a motor. What descends into the heart as awe rises back out of the hands as action. The fear that pulls closer does not settle into stillness — it becomes worship.

### Element Two: *Mushfiqūn* — The State Born at Twilight

We've now established what they're feeling *from*. Now we ask: what does it produce in them? What is the *state* the *khashya* descends into?

Allah uses a word here that is one of the most psychologically subtle words in the entire Quran. *Mushfiqūn*. From the root *sh-f-q*. Plural active participle, Form IV.

I want to teach you this root by going to the place where Arabic uses it most often. Not in religion at all. Look at the western horizon at sunset, just after the sun goes down. There's a moment — maybe twenty minutes long — where the sky is neither day nor night. The sun is gone, but the light is still there. The world has a soft red glow. Birds are settling. The wind shifts. Everything is held in a kind of trembling balance.

That moment, in Arabic, is called *al-shafaq*.

[PAUSE]

It is the **boundary state**. The threshold. Not yet dark, not yet light. Suspended between two realities.

Now take that word and watch what happens when you turn it into a state of soul.

*Ishfāq* — the verbal noun from this same root — describes a state of soul that lives in the boundary. A state that is neither hope nor despair, neither relaxation nor terror. It is the trembling care of someone who holds something precious and is acutely aware that they could lose it.

The classical lexicographers describe *ishfāq* as a tender, anxious, attentive concern. It is the word you use for what a parent feels watching a sleeping infant breathe. It is not the fear of imminent disaster. It is the *quality* of care that is so soft, so attentive, so aware of how easily this could go wrong, that the heart cannot fully relax.

You know this feeling.

You know it from the first time you held something irreplaceable in your hands. A newborn. A piece of glass passed down from a grandmother. A friend in a hospital bed. You know that state where every nerve is awake, where you cannot put it down, where you do not want to look away even for a moment, because you are aware — *acutely* aware — of how precious this is and how thin the membrane is between holding it and losing it.

[PAUSE]

That is *ishfāq*.

The classical commentators anchor this word first in something harder and more urgent. Al-Muyassar reads *mushfiqūn* as وجلون — trembling, fearful — specifically of what Allah has warned them about. Al-Tabari connects it directly to that persistent, active obedience we saw earlier: they are in this state, and the state moves them. The twilight etymology is not the *leading* meaning in the tafsir tradition; it is the root's poetic depth, the quality that distinguishes this awe from an awe that hardens and distances. Both layers are real: a serious, sustained fear — and a fear that carries the texture of vigil rather than dread, of watchfulness rather than paralysis. The "tender" connotation is not imposed on the word from outside; it lives in its structure. But the tradition foregrounds the trembling first, and the texture of that trembling second.

Notice what this word does that *khawf* could not have done. *Khawf* would have given us the fear of punishment — and that fear, while real, can produce distance. Because what you fear, you avoid. *Khawf* of Allah, taken alone, can make a person want to **stay away** from the One they fear.

But *ishfāq* is the opposite. The mother *mushfiqa* over her sleeping child does not move away from the child. She moves *closer*. She watches more attentively. She becomes *more* present, not less. The very fear she feels is what binds her to the bedside.

This is the secret of the believer's relationship with Allah. The trembling does not push them away. **The trembling is the form their love takes.**

[PAUSE]

There's one more thing I want you to notice, and it requires looking at where else the Quran uses this exact word.

In Surah Al-Anbiyāʾ, ayah 28, Allah describes the **angels**. The mighty, immaculate angels who never disobey. And He says of them: *wa hum min khashyatihi **mushfiqūn*** — "they, from awe of Him, are *mushfiqūn*."

The *exact same phrase* used for the angels is the phrase used here for the believers.

Stay with this. The believers in 23:57 are described in language identical to the description of angels. Not because the believer reaches angelic perfection — we are mortal, fallible, full of forgetting — but because the **inner state** Allah is honoring in the believer is the same inner state He has placed in the heart of every angel: the trembling reverence in the presence of the One.

You and the angels have something in common. Not your record. Not your innocence. **The shape of awe in your heart when you actually recognize Him.**

That is what the racers in this surah carry. That is what makes them race.

### Element Three: The Architecture of Negation — *Lā Yushrikūn*

Now look at the third ayah:

*wa 'lladhīna hum **bi-rabbihim lā yushrikūn***

**"And those who, with their Lord, do not associate anything."**

I want you to notice something strange. The first two ayahs describe **presences**: they have *khashya*, they have *ishfāq*, they have *īmān* in His signs. But the third ayah describes an **absence**: they do *not* associate anything with Him.

Why end with the negative? Why describe what they don't do, after spending two ayahs describing what they do?

[PAUSE]

There's a deep psychological reason for this — and to see it, you have to understand what *shirk* actually means at the level of the heart. We usually think of shirk as a dramatic act — bowing to an idol, invoking a saint. And that is certainly shirk. But the Quran's conception of shirk is far subtler and more intimate than that.

Al-Tabari reads this ayah with surgical precision. Those who do not commit shirk with their Lord are those who *make their worship purely for Him* — يخلصون لربهم عبادتهم — and crucially, **they do not perform it to be seen by anyone from His creation** — لا يراؤون بها أحدا من خلقه. They make their deeds purely for His face — لوجهه خالصا. They direct their obedience and worship to Him alone, to the exclusion of every other witness.

[PAUSE]

This means the third ayah is not merely prohibiting the obvious sin. It is describing the subtlest war in the believer's inner life — the war against hidden shirk. The war against the divided attention that prays to Allah while also wanting to be *seen* praying. The war against the charity given with one hand toward Allah and one eye toward the room.

Riyā' — performing worship for human witnesses — is not a minor ethical lapse in the classical understanding. It is a form of association: installing a second recipient alongside Allah in the deed itself. And the believer described here has won that war. Their worship has become — wholly, entirely — a private transaction between a servant and his Lord. No audience. No performance. No second recipient.

And now the architecture of these three ayahs becomes fully visible.

The first describes their *feeling-state* — mushfiqun, trembling in awe, moved by a fear that descends from knowing who He is, and that propels them into constant obedience.
The second describes their *cognitive stance* — believing in His signs, his proofs in the Book and in creation, surrendering their intellect to the evidence.
The third describes their *volitional purity* — refusing even the most subtle rival for His claim on their deeds, dedicating every act to His face alone.

Feeling. Knowing. Intending. All three surrendered. All three pointed only at Him.

This is the interior architecture of the winner. This is who races to good deeds. This is who the surah has been building toward from its opening line.

And it begins not with achievement — not with a list of things done and boxes checked — but with a trembling. A trembling that comes not from effort, not from anxiety, not from guilt, but from *recognition*. From having seen, even partially, who it is you are standing before.

The racer trembles. The trembling makes them run.
