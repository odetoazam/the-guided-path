# The Accusation Is a Confession

## The Passage

> [ayah:23:23] وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِۦ فَقَالَ يَـٰقَوْمِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مَا لَكُم مِّنْ إِلَـٰهٍ غَيْرُهُۥٓ ۖ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ
>
> [ayah:23:24] فَقَالَ ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ مِن قَوْمِهِۦ مَا هَـٰذَآ إِلَّا بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُكُمْ يُرِيدُ أَن يَتَفَضَّلَ عَلَيْكُمْ وَلَوْ شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ لَأَنزَلَ مَلَـٰٓئِكَةً مَّا سَمِعْنَا بِهَـٰذَا فِىٓ ءَابَآئِنَا ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ

## Rebuilding the World

I want you to stand where the first listener stood, because this passage does not land until you feel the ground under it.

The Prophet ﷺ is in Mecca. And Mecca is not going well.

By the time Sūrat al-Muʾminūn is being recited, the pattern has set in. He stands up among his own tribe — the people whose children he watched grow, the merchants he traded with, the men who once called him *al-Amīn*, the trustworthy — and he tells them one thing: there is no god but Allah. And they have a set of responses ready. He's after power. He wants to be a chief. He's a poet, no — a magician, no — a madman. His own uncle curses him. The notables of Quraysh — the ones with the keys to the Kaʿba, the ones whose entire status rests on the gods stationed around that House — laugh, or sneer, or say: *let's just wait. Time will deal with him.*

And into that exact climate, Allah begins to tell him a story.

Notice **where** in the surah this story arrives. The surah opened with the believers who succeed — *qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn*. Then it turned to the signs in creation: it took you inside the womb and grew a clot into bone and clothed the bone in flesh; it built the seven paths above you; it sent the rain down in due measure and grew the gardens and the olive tree. Sign after sign in the world you can touch.

And then, at verse 23, it turns from the signs *in creation* to the signs *in history*. It stops pointing at the sky and starts pointing at the past. *And We had certainly sent Nūḥ.* This is the first of the messengers in the sequence — the oldest story humanity has, the very first time the call went out and the very first time it was refused.

So here is what Allah is doing for His Prophet ﷺ, and it is an act of mercy before it is anything else. He is saying: *you think this is about you and Quraysh? Let Me show you how old this is. Let Me show you the very first man who stood where you stand.* And then He records what Nūḥ's people said — and the Prophet ﷺ, listening, hears his own tribe's words coming out of the mouths of people who died thousands of years before Quraysh was born.

[PAUSE]

Here is the thing to carry into the text. Nūḥ speaks for one verse. His people answer for two. And in those two verses they say four different things about him — and **every single one of them turns out to be a description of themselves.**

That's the hook. Watch how they try to describe Nūḥ, and watch how they keep, helplessly, describing the mirror.

# PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: He said *"O my people."* They said *"this."*

Let me start with something so small you could recite this passage a hundred times and never stop on it. It's about two words — one from Nūḥ, one from his people — and the distance between them is the whole tragedy.

But before we open the text, I want to teach you something about how Arabic — and honestly, how every language — handles the difference between drawing someone near and pushing them away.

Think about how you address a person when you love them and how you refer to a person when you've written them off. When my daughter walks in the room, I say her name. When a stranger cuts the line, I don't say his name — I say *"this guy."* *"Can you believe this guy?"* The moment a human being becomes *"this,"* something has happened. I've stopped treating them as a *someone* and started treating them as a *something*. A name is an act of relationship. *"This"* is an act of distance. You point at objects. You call to people.

Arabic marks this precisely. There is the **vocative** — *yā* — the particle you use to *call* someone, to turn your face to them and address them directly. And there is the **demonstrative** — *hādhā*, "this" — the word you use to *point at* something in front of you.

Now watch what Nūḥ does.

His very first word to a people who will reject him, mock him, and eventually be drowned — his first word is *yā qawmi*. **"O my people."**

Sit with that possessive. *Qawmī.* **My** people. He doesn't say *"O people"* or *"O you who disbelieve."* He claims them. He binds himself to them in the very breath he uses to call them to God. The word *qawm* comes from the root that means *to stand* — a *qawm* is the group you stand with, the ones you rise alongside. And he calls them *mine*. This is a man leaning toward the people who will hurt him. Everything in the address is warmth, ownership, nearness. *Come here. You are mine. Let me tell you what will save you.*

[PAUSE]

Now hear their answer.

*Mā **hādhā** illā basharun mithlukum.* **"This** is nothing but a man like you."

They do not say his name. They do not say *"Nūḥ."* They do not even say *"he."* They say ***hādhā*** — **"this."** They point at him the way you point at an object on a shelf. The man who called them *"my people"* — they refuse to grant him even a pronoun. They downgrade him from a *someone* to a *this*.

Notice something, and let it sit: they weren't only rejecting his message. Language this precise is doing something to the man himself. He reached toward them with a word of belonging, and they answered by pushing him out to arm's length and pointing. **He humanized them. They objectified him.** In one exchange you can hear the entire shape of what rejection does — it cannot leave the messenger a person, because a person you might have to *answer*. A *"this"* you can dismiss.

And here is the quiet devastation of it. Which of them was actually alone in that moment? The man surrounded by his tribe, calling them *"mine"* — or the crowd of chiefs who could only point at one solitary human being and call him *"this"*? He had claimed them. They could not even claim him with a name.

**What this one word reveals:** the distance between Nūḥ and his people was never a distance of information. He knew who they were — *my people*. They refused to know who he was — *this*. The gap between *"O my people"* and *"this"* is the gap between a heart that reaches and a heart that has already decided not to.

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Two: *Yatafaḍḍala* — the accusation that names its own maker

Now to the heart of what they accused him of. And this is where the Arabic does something you cannot get from any translation, because the accusation is built out of a word that quietly incriminates the people saying it.

First, let me teach you the tool, because the whole insight lives in a verb *form*.

In Arabic, a verb has a base meaning carried in its root, and then it can be poured into different *forms* — patterns that bend the meaning. One of these, what the grammarians call **Form Five**, the *tafaʿʿala* pattern, very often carries a specific flavor: it is **reflexive and self-directed**. It bends the action *back onto the doer*. It's the difference between *learning* and *teaching yourself*, between *raising* and *raising yourself up*, between *knowing* and *pretending to know*. Form Five frequently means: to *seek*, *claim*, or *make* something *for oneself*.

Hold that. Now the root.

The root here is *f-ḍ-l* — *faḍl*. And *faḍl* is one of the most beautiful words in the Qurʾan. It means **grace, bounty, favor** — the good that overflows beyond what is owed. When the Qurʾan speaks of Allah's *faḍl*, it means His generosity, the gift you did not earn. *Faḍl* is, at its core, a **gift that comes down from above**.

So now take that gorgeous root and pour it into that self-seeking Form Five, and you get the word they hurl at Nūḥ:

*yurīdu an **yatafaḍḍala** ʿalaykum.* **"He wishes to make faḍl for himself over you."** He wants to grab the superiority. He wants to elevate *himself* above you. He wants to take the high position and stand on your necks.

Do you see what they've done? They took the word for *grace descending from God* and twisted it into *a man clawing his way up over other men.* In their mouths, the language of gift becomes the language of the power grab.

And here is the question the whole element turns on. **Why is that the accusation?** Nūḥ has asked for nothing. He has no army, no throne, no wealth he's demanding. He said seven words: *worship Allah, you have no god but Him.* Where, in that, is the bid for power?

It isn't in Nūḥ. **It's in them.**

Stay with me here, because this is the mechanism. The *malaʾ* — we'll come to that word — were the chiefs, the ones at the top of the pyramid. Their entire world was a ladder. Every man was either above you or below you; every relationship was a question of who outranks whom. So when a man stands up and speaks, they have exactly one category to file him under: *he's climbing.* They cannot imagine a human being who is not climbing, because **they have never, in their own lives, done anything that wasn't climbing.** The word *yatafaḍḍala* didn't come from watching Nūḥ. It came from looking in a mirror and describing what they saw.

Let that reframe the whole verse. This is not primarily a statement *about* Nūḥ. It is an involuntary confession *about them*. When you accuse a sincere man of an angle, you are not reporting on him. **You are handing over the map of your own interior.** You are telling the room: *this is the only motive I have ever known, so it must be the one driving him.*

[PAUSE]

**What this word reveals:** they reached for the ugliest thing they could say — *he wants to lord it over you* — and in reaching for it, they told us exactly what was in their own hearts. The root of grace, bent by their tongues into the shape of a grab for power, because a grab for power was the only shape their hearts knew how to make.

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: The two coverings — *jinnah* and *kafarū*

And now the final blow they land, in verse 25, and it's the one that seems most personal, most cutting — until you look at the root and realize it has quietly circled back and named *them* one more time.

They say: *in huwa illā rajulun **bihi jinnatun**.* **"He is nothing but a man afflicted with madness."** *Bihi jinnah* — there is *jinnah* in him. He's possessed. He's insane. Don't listen to a word he says; the man's mind is gone.

Now I need you to hear where the word *jinnah* comes from, because the root is doing theology under the surface.

The root is *j-n-n*, and its core, foundational meaning — before it ever meant anything else — is **to cover, to conceal, to veil something from sight.** This one root fans out into a whole family of words, and every one of them is about something hidden. The *jinn* are called *jinn* because they are the hidden ones, veiled from your eyes. The *jannah*, the garden, the Paradise — some of the scholars note it's *jannah* because its trees are so dense they *cover* the ground beneath. The *janīn*, the fetus — the very word the surah used a few verses earlier — is called that because it is *hidden* inside the womb. The *junnah*, the shield, is what you *cover* yourself with in battle.

So what is *jinnah*, madness, in this family of meanings? It is a mind that has been **covered over**. A reason that has had a veil thrown across it. When they call Nūḥ *majnūn*, the picture underneath the word is of an intellect that has been draped in cloth, hidden from the light, unable to see clearly.

That is their charge: *your reason is veiled, Nūḥ. Something has come down over your mind and you can no longer see straight.*

Now.

Go back to verse 24, to the phrase that introduced these people. *Faqāla al-malaʾu alladhīna **kafarū**.* "The eminent ones **who disbelieved**." That word — *kafarū*, they committed *kufr* — what is its root?

*K-f-r.* And the core, original meaning of *k-f-r*, before it ever meant *disbelief* — is **to cover, to conceal, to bury something out of sight.** The farmer is called *kāfir* in classical Arabic because he *covers* the seed with soil. The night is described as *kāfir* because it *covers* the world in darkness. To commit *kufr* is, at root, to take a truth that is plainly there and **throw a covering over it** — to bury what you know.

Do you feel what has just happened?

[PAUSE]

They accuse Nūḥ of *jinnah* — of having a **covered mind**. And the Qurʾan has already told us who they are, using a root that means the exact same thing: they are the ones who **covered** the truth. *Kafarū.*

There are two coverings in this passage, and they belong to opposite parties. One group has thrown a veil over reality and refuses to see it. And that same group turns to the one man in the valley who *is* seeing clearly, and says: *you're the one who's covered. Your mind is veiled. You can't see straight.* **The buried accuse the seer of blindness. The veiled call the clear-eyed man mad.**

I want to be careful and honest here, because the skill of tadabbur requires it: the shared meaning in these two roots is a documented fact of the Arabic lexicon — *j-n-n* and *k-f-r* both turn on the sense of *covering*. That much is solid ground. Whether the Qurʾan intends us to hold the two coverings up against each other — that is an invitation the language offers, one way of sitting with why these particular roots sit in these particular mouths. But once you've seen it, it's very hard to un-see. The whole exchange becomes a scene of two veils, and the tragedy is that only one party knows which of them is veiled.

**What these roots reveal:** the deepest thing the disbelievers said about Nūḥ — *he is covered, he cannot see* — was the truest thing that could be said about themselves. They diagnosed in him the exact disease they were dying of.

[PAUSE — major]

---

**The bridge.** Look back at what these three words have shown us. *Hādhā* — they could not grant him a name. *Yatafaḍḍala* — they accused him of the ambition that filled their own chests. *Jinnah* — they called *covered* the one man who could see, while a covering lay over their own hearts. Three accusations, and not one of them landed on Nūḥ. Every one of them ricocheted and hit the wall behind the accuser.

So let's stop asking what these words tell us about *Nūḥ's people*. Let's ask what they tell us about **us** — because you and I have stood on both sides of this exchange, and the mercy of the passage is that it shows us both. Let's ask what this reveals not about an ancient tribe, but about the human heart itself.

# PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: When you can't believe anyone is sincere

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You tell a friend you've started volunteering at the food bank on Saturdays. And before the sentence is even finished, they tilt their head and say: *"Nice. Looks good on the résumé, huh?"* Or you mention that someone at work stayed late to help a struggling colleague, and the person you're talking to says: *"Mm. He's angling for the promotion."* Or a public figure gives to charity, and the entire comment section knows, instantly, with total confidence, that it's a tax write-off and a PR stunt.

Have you noticed there's a certain kind of person who cannot let an act of goodness stand as an act of goodness? Every kindness has an angle. Every gift is a transaction in disguise. Every sincere word is a move on a board they're sure everyone is playing. And the striking thing — the thing I want you to hold onto — is that these people are usually *certain*. They're not puzzled by your motives. They *know* them. They can tell you exactly why you *really* did it, and they'll say it with the confidence of someone reading their own diary.

Because that's what they're doing.

Here's the truth underneath the cynicism, and it's not a comfortable one. **You cannot see in another person a motive you've never once felt in yourself.** The person who's sure everyone has an angle is a person who has *always* had an angle. The one who assumes every kindness is a transaction is telling you, without meaning to, that they have never given anything for free. Their certainty about your motives is really a report on the only motives they've ever known. The cynic isn't a good judge of others. **The cynic is an honest witness about himself.**

Now look back at the valley.

The *malaʾ* — and here's the word, from the root *m-l-ʾ*, which means **to fill**. The *malaʾ* were the ones who were *full*: full of status, full of standing, full of the things that made them who they were, and therefore full of everything they had to lose. And a man walks up to these full men and says, *worship Allah alone* — a message that would empty them, that would flatten their ladder to level ground. And the only sentence they can produce is: *he wants to gain superiority over you.* He's climbing.

He wasn't climbing. **They were always climbing.** They looked at the one man in the valley who genuinely wanted nothing from them — no throne, no tribute, no rung above them — and the sincerity itself was so foreign that they had no file for it. So they reached for the only explanation their world contained: *he's after what we're all after.* The accusation of ambition was not evidence about Nūḥ. It was a confession about the chiefs.

[PAUSE]

And now let me turn this from *them* onto *us*, because it is far too easy to sit in judgment of Nūḥ's people and miss the knife pointed at our own chest.

The question this passage puts to you is not *"are there cynics in the world?"* The question is: **what do your suspicions about other people reveal about the contents of your own heart?**

When you assume the brother who prays in the front row is showing off — where did that certainty come from? When you're sure the sister who wears her deen visibly is doing it for attention — how do you *know* that? When someone does something generous and your first, fast, silent thought is *what are they getting out of it* — I want you to stop, next time, and ask the terrifying follow-up: *why is that the first place my mind goes? What does it say about me that "there must be an angle" is my reflex?*

Because the Qurʾan has just shown you, with surgical precision, that **the accusation is a confession.** The story of the *malaʾ* is not only a story about ancient chiefs who couldn't recognize a prophet. It's a mirror held up to the part of you that can't quite believe anyone is sincere — and it's whispering: *that suspicion isn't wisdom. It's autobiography.*

**What this changes tomorrow:** the next time you catch yourself decoding someone's "real motive," treat it as an alarm bell, not an insight. Turn the lens around. Ask what it says about you that this is the motive you assumed. The people who assume the best of others are usually the ones who have felt the best in themselves. *Ḥusn al-ẓann*, thinking well of others, was never just good manners — it's a report on the health of your own heart. Guard what you assume about people. You're describing yourself.

[PAUSE — major]

## Theme Two: *"Wait for a while"* — the decision disguised as no decision

Again, let me begin where you live, because this one is so ordinary you'll recognize it in your own week.

There's a text you've been meaning to answer. Nothing hard about it — you just haven't. There's the *"we really should sit down and talk about this"* that you and someone you love have been saying for two years and never done. There's the tab open in your browser — the article, the lecture, the thing you *know* you should read — that you'll get to. Later. There's the health thing, the little symptom, that you've decided to *keep an eye on* rather than see a doctor about. And there's the big one, the one that matters most: *I'll get serious about my deen. Not yet. But soon. When things settle down. When Ramadan comes. When I'm older. Later.*

Have you noticed that we have a whole strategy for avoiding decisions, and the strategy is to pretend that *not deciding* isn't itself a decision? We call it *waiting*. We call it *keeping our options open*. We tell ourselves we're being prudent, gathering information, letting things play out. And it feels so much safer than *choosing* — because if you never choose, you can never be wrong. If you just *wait and see*, the burden stays off your shoulders.

Now listen to the last thing the *malaʾ* say. After the objections, after the mockery, after *"he's mad"* — they turn to each other and they issue a command. Not to Nūḥ. To themselves. *Fa-**tarabbaṣū** bihi ḥattā **ḥīn**.* **"So wait concerning him for a time."**

*Tarabbaṣū.* From the root *r-b-ṣ* — to lie in wait, to watch and bide your time. It's that same self-directed Form Five: *make yourselves wait.* And what are they waiting *for*? For *ḥīn* — and here Arabic does something haunting. *Ḥīn* is not a specific time. It's a deliberately **vague** stretch — *a while, some time, an unspecified interval.* They don't even name what they're waiting for. Maybe he'll die. Maybe he'll be discredited. Maybe the whole thing will just... blow over. *Let's not decide. Let's wait. Time will sort it out.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see how *comfortable* that is? It is the most reasonable-sounding sentence in the entire passage. They don't kill him. They don't exile him. They don't even, in this moment, forbid him. They just... defer. They kick the whole terrifying question of *what if he's right* down the road, into the fog of *ḥattā ḥīn* — *for a while* — where it never has to be faced.

And this is the thing I need you to feel: **their "wait and see" was not neutrality. It was their answer.** They *had* decided. They decided *no* — and then dressed the *no* up as *not yet*, because *not yet* let them keep their hands clean. Deferral felt like keeping the door open. It was actually the door quietly closing, in slow motion, so gently that no one had to admit it was shutting.

Now here is where the Qurʾan turns the knife, and you have to know the story to feel it. They said *"wait for a while."* And do you know what came, in that while? **The** *ḥīn* **came.** The rain came, and the springs of the earth burst open, and the water rose over the mountains, and every one of those chiefs who had so calmly said *let's wait and see* — waited, and saw. The *ḥīn* they thought they were controlling arrived on its own schedule, and it did not knock.

[PAUSE]

Bring that home, because it isn't only about a flood five thousand years ago. It's about the thing you keep telling yourself you'll get to.

Every *"later"* is spending a currency you can't count and can't get back. When you say *I'll turn to Allah properly once life settles down*, you are doing precisely what the *malaʾ* did — you're issuing yourself the command *tarabbaṣ*, wait, and pointing at a *ḥīn* that has no date on it. And you're telling yourself the waiting is neutral. It isn't. **The waiting is the answer.** A prayer deferred is a prayer decided against, for today. A repentance postponed is a repentance declined, for now. And the *ḥīn* — your *ḥīn*, the one with no date on it — is coming on its own schedule, and it will not knock either.

I'm not saying this to frighten you. I'm saying it because the *malaʾ* had a chance to say *yes* to a man standing right in front of them, warm-voiced, calling them *"my people,"* and they answered with the softest, most sensible *no* ever spoken: *let's wait.* And the mercy for you and me is that the same door is still open, and *"later"* is still the most dangerous word we own.

**What this changes tomorrow:** find the one thing — you already know what it is — that you've been filing under *"soon."* The prayer you keep meaning to make on time. The person you keep meaning to forgive. The turning-back you keep meaning to do. And do the smallest real version of it *today*, before the *ḥīn* names itself. Not because the sky is falling, but because *"wait for a while"* is exactly how a whole people talked themselves out of their own salvation, one reasonable postponement at a time.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The architecture: truth is brief, evasion is verbose

Now that we've climbed through the words, stand back and look at the shape of the whole thing, because the design is the proof.

Count the verses. **Nūḥ speaks for one.** A single verse. *Worship Allah; you have no god but Him; will you not be conscious?* Clean, short, vertical — every word of it pointing *up*, away from himself, toward the One he's calling them to. And notice how it ends: with a **question turned toward them** — *afalā tattaqūn*, "will you not then be conscious of Him?" An open door. An invitation. He ends by reaching.

**His people answer for two.** Twice the length. And watch the *motion* of those two verses, because they descend like a man losing his footing on a slope. First: *he's a man like you* — leveling him down to the ground. Then: *he wants superiority* — attacking his motive. Then: *God would've sent angels* — a theological objection. Then: *we never heard this from our forefathers* — retreating into the ancestors. Then: *he's mad* — the personal insult. And finally: *wait for a while* — the shrug, the deferral, the walking away.

Do you see it? **Nūḥ needs one verse to say something true. They need two verses and still resolve nothing.** His speech ends in a *question to them*. Their speech ends in a *command to each other* — *tarabbaṣū*, wait — and it ends by turning **away** from Nūḥ entirely, to face one another, to reassure themselves in a huddle. He turned toward them. They turned to each other and away from him.

That is the architecture, and it could not be an accident: **truth is economical, and evasion is verbose.** The one who is right can say it in a breath and open a door. The one who is running needs paragraph after paragraph — a leveling, an accusation, a theology, an appeal to tradition, an insult, and finally a plan to wait — and after all of it, the door isn't opened, it's just quietly walked away from. The whole passage is built so that the *shape* of the speech proves who was sincere before you've weighed a single argument. The short, upward, door-opening voice against the long, downward, door-closing one.

## Bringing it together

And now see that the three words and the two themes were never separate things. They were one insight approached from different sides.

*Hādhā* — "this" — they could not name him, because to name him was to grant him a reality they'd already refused. *Yatafaḍḍala* — they accused him of their own ambition, because a full heart projects its fullness onto everyone it sees. *Jinnah* — they called him covered, because their own covering made clear sight look like madness. And what were those but the two themes wearing linguistic clothes? **The accusation is a confession** — that's *yatafaḍḍala* and *jinnah*, the covered heart describing itself while pointing at another. And **the decision disguised as no decision** — that's *hādhā* and *tarabbaṣū*, the refusal to grant him a name and the refusal to give an answer, both of them a *no* dressed as something softer.

Where we started: a man calling his people *mine*, reaching. Where we've arrived: the recognition that everything his people threw at him was a portrait of themselves, and that their final, gentlest cruelty — *let's wait* — was the *no* they couldn't bring themselves to say out loud.

## Questions to carry

- When you catch yourself sure of someone's "real motive" — where does that certainty actually come from, and what does it say about the motives *you've* known?
- Is there a person in your life you've reduced to a *"this"* — someone you've stopped granting a name, a story, a benefit of the doubt?
- What is the one thing you've filed under *"later"* that you already know, in your chest, is really a *"no"* you don't want to say?
- If the *ḥīn* — the unnamed hour — arrived for you this week, would it find you in the middle of *waiting to get serious*, or in the middle of having begun?
- Whose sincerity have you doubted lately — and what would change if you assumed, just once, that they meant it?

## The one sentence to carry

**Every charge the people of Nūḥ leveled at him was a mirror: they called his sincerity ambition, called his clarity madness, and called their own refusal "waiting" — and none of it was ever about him.**

## The invitation

So here is what I'd invite you to carry out of these three verses. Two small vigilances, held gently.

The first: **watch what you assume about people.** When your mind reaches, fast and certain, for someone's hidden angle — pause, and turn the mirror around. That reflex isn't insight. It's autobiography. And the more good you assume in others, the more you'll discover it was there in you all along.

The second: **stop saying "later" to the things that matter most.** The *malaʾ* didn't refuse Nūḥ with a shout. They refused him with a shrug — *wait for a while* — and the while ran out. Yours is running too, quietly, right now. So do the small real thing today. Make the prayer on time. Send the message. Turn back the inch you've been meaning to turn. Not from fear — from the mercy that the door Nūḥ opened is still, astonishingly, open for you.

## A closing duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who sent Nūḥ to a people who could only see themselves — lift the covering from our hearts before we mistake our own veils for another's blindness. Purify what we assume about Your servants, so that we see sincerity where it lives and think well of those You made. And save us from the softest lie we tell ourselves — *later* — and let the hour find us already turned toward You, not still waiting for a time. *Āmīn.*
