# The Laughter That Forgot

## When the Mocked Turned Out to Be the Winners

## Rebuilding the Moment

I want you to stand somewhere before we begin — somewhere specific.

You are standing at the very edge of Surah Al-Mu'minun. Almost the whole surah is behind you now. And you need to know how it opened, because the surah is a room with a door at each end, and Allah built them to face each other.

It opened like this: *Qad aflaḥa l-muʾminūn* — **"The believers have succeeded."** The believers have flourished, have won, have come through. That was the first breath of the surah. A declaration. Almost a headline. And then the whole surah, all one hundred and something ayahs, becomes the evidence file for that headline. Who these believers are. How they carry themselves. What they endure. What they will inherit.

And now we have arrived at the far door. The closing scene.

Let me set it for you, because the ayahs just before ours are unbearable. The Day has come. The reckoning is over. And there is a group of people in the Fire, and they are pleading. In ayah 107 they cry out, *Rabbanā akhrijnā minhā* — **"Our Lord, get us out of here."** Get us out. And Allah's answer, in 108, is one of the most terrifying sentences in the entire Qur'an. He says: *Ikhsaʾū fīhā wa lā tukallimūn* — **"Slink back into it in disgrace, and do not speak to Me."** It is the word you use to drive away a dog. *Ikhsaʾū.* Be gone. And do not address Me again. The conversation is closed. Forever.

[PAUSE]

Now sit in the silence right after that door slams. That is the temperature of the room when our three ayahs begin. Everything is cold. Everything is final. The pleading has been refused.

And it is into *that* silence — the silence right after Allah has told a group of human beings that He will not speak to them anymore — that He turns and begins to speak *about someone else.* He starts telling a story. Almost gently. *"There was a party of My servants…"*

Here is what you have to feel about the original moment this was revealed. This came down in Makkah. And the first Muslims — Bilāl, ʿAmmār, Suhayb, Khabbāb, the ones with no tribe to protect them, the freed slaves, the poor, the "nobodies" of Makkan society — these people were the joke of the town. The elite of Quraysh would sit in their circles and the Muslims would walk past, and they would nudge each other and grin. *Look at them. Look who Muhammad's God chose. The slaves. The broke ones.* They mimicked the way they prayed. They laughed at the way they wept during recitation. In the sophisticated marketplace of Makkan honor, to be a believer was to be *cringe.* To be the thing people did impressions of at parties.

And these early believers *heard* it. They walked through that laughter every single day. And they had almost no way to answer it, because they had no power, no numbers, no status. All they had was a whispered sentence they kept saying to their Lord.

So when the first Muslims heard *this* passage — this scene of the mockers in the Fire and a verdict handed down on who actually won — do you understand what it did to them? It was not abstract theology. It was Allah leaning down into their exact, daily, humiliating experience and saying: *I saw it. I heard every laugh. I am keeping the accounts. And the accounts do not close in the marketplace of Makkah. They close on a Day I have already scheduled.*

Here is the question I want you carrying through everything that follows, and I am not going to answer it yet.

In this scene, the mockers had all the power and the believers had none. The mockers were doing something *to* the believers. So why — grammatically, in the actual Arabic — does Allah build the sentence so that it is the **believers** who *do something to the mockers?* Why are the powerless ones the subject of the verb, and the powerful ones the object?

Hold that. Let's go into the words.

## PART ONE: The Linguistic Journey

### One — The word Allah uses for people the world threw away: *ʿibādī*

Before we touch the ayah, I want to teach you something small about Arabic that is going to open this whole passage.

In Arabic, you can attach a possessive to the end of a noun — one little letter, a *yāʾ* — and it means "my." *Kitāb* is a book. *Kitābī* is *my* book. *Bayt* is a house. *Baytī* is *my* house. It is the most intimate grammatical move in the language, because it takes a free-floating thing and pins it to *you.* It says: this one is mine. This one belongs to me.

Now. In our first ayah, when Allah describes the people who were being laughed at, He does not call them "the believers." He does not call them "some poor people." He does not even call them "the Muslims." Watch the word He chooses:

*Kāna farīqun min* ***ʿibādī***

<!-- morphology:23:109:6 root=عبد pos=N -->
**"A party of** ***My servants.***"

*ʿibādī.* Servants — *ʿibād*, from the root ʿayn-bā-dāl — and then that little *yāʾ* on the end. **My.** My servants.

Stay with this for a second, because of *where* it lands.

The Makkan elite had a whole vocabulary for these people, and none of it was kind. To Quraysh they were slaves, they were riffraff, they were the followers Muhammad managed to scrape together because nobody important would listen to him. They were, socially, *no one's.* An unclaimed people. That is precisely why they were safe to mock — you can only laugh freely at someone nobody powerful stands behind.

And Allah, the Owner of the heavens and the earth, reaches into that scene and puts His own possessive *yāʾ* on them. **Mine.** These ones — the ones you thought belonged to no one — these are *Mine.*

Notice what that does to the mockery. If you laugh at a homeless man on the street, that is cruelty. If you laugh at the king's own son, dressed in rags for a day, that is *treason* — and you did not even know it. The believers looked unclaimed. They were not. Every laugh was aimed, without the mockers realizing it, at something with an Owner. **You were never laughing at them. You were laughing in the direction of the One who calls them Mine.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is where the grammar starts to move, and I want you to feel the floor shift under the mockers.

Look at *who is being addressed* across these three ayahs, because Allah does something deliberate with the direction He is facing.

In ayah 109 — *"there was a party of My servants who used to say…"* — Allah is speaking in the **third person.** He is describing the believers. *They. Them. Those ones.* The mockers are not even being addressed yet. It is as though Allah is talking about the believers over the mockers' heads, the way a judge reads the character of a victim into the record before he ever turns to the accused.

And then, ayah 110, the sentence *turns.* Suddenly the voice is not describing anymore. Suddenly it is pointing:

*Fa-ttakhadhtumūhum sikhriyyā* — **"So** ***you*** **took them as a mockery."**

Do you feel that? The camera whips around. All at once Allah is no longer speaking *about* a scene — He is speaking *into someone's face.* **You.** *You* did this. This grammatical turn — the tradition calls it *iltifāt*, the sudden pivot of address — is not decoration. It is the sound of a courtroom when the prosecutor stops addressing the jury and wheels around to point at the defendant. The temperature in the room changes with a single suffix.

And it is crueler than a simple accusation, because of what came *right before it.* Allah spent an entire ayah lovingly describing the people they hurt — *My servants, who used to say, Our Lord, we believed* — building the picture of the innocents in warm, first-person, possessive language. And *then,* with that picture still hanging in the air, He turns and says: **and you took them as a joke.** He made you look at the victims first. Then He made you meet His eyes.

**One little *yāʾ*, "My," and the mockery was reclassified.** Not as bad manners. As a crime against the Owner.

That is the first word. Let it settle. There is a heavier one coming.

[PAUSE]

### Two — The most frightening verb in the passage: *ansawkum*

Let me give you the everyday version of a grammatical tool before I show you what Allah does with it, because this one is easy to miss and it is the hinge of the entire passage.

In Arabic there is a family of verbs called Form IV. You take a simple verb — someone *does* something — and you reshape it so that now someone *causes* the doing. *Jalasa* means "he sat." *Ajlasa* means "he *made* someone sit — he sat them down." The doer and the receiver split into two people. One causes; the other undergoes.

Now take the verb "to forget." In Arabic that is *nasiya*, from nūn-sīn-yāʾ. Simple: *he forgot.* Fine. But push it into Form IV and it becomes *ansā* — **"to make someone forget," "to cause forgetting in someone."** Now there is a forgetter and there is somebody who *did it to them.*

Hold that tool. Here is the ayah:

*Fa-ttakhadhtumūhum sikhriyyan* ***ḥattā ansawkum dhikrī***

<!-- morphology:23:110:5 root=سخر pos=N -->First the crime: *ittakhadhtumūhum sikhriyyā* — <!-- morphology:23:110:2 root=أخذ pos=V -->"you *took* them as an object of mockery." And I want you to hear that word, *sikhriyyā*, out loud. <!-- morphology:23:110:5 root=سخر pos=N -->Say it. *Sikh-riyyā.* There is a scrape in the middle of it — that harsh *kh*, the sound you make clearing your throat. It is not a smooth word. It does not glide. It catches and rasps in exactly the place a sneer catches on a face. One way to sit with that: mockery is an ugly act, and Allah gave it an ugly-sounding word to wear. (The reciters, by the way, read this word two ways — some with a *kasra*, *sikhriyyā*, and some with a *ḍamma*, *sukhriyyā* — and the scholars gathered around both, seeing in the one the sense of *ridicule* and in the other the sense of *making someone your servant, someone beneath you to be ordered about.* You do not have to choose. Lay them on top of each other and you get the full ugliness: they mocked them *and* they looked down on them as lesser creatures put on earth for their amusement. Both are true. Both are recorded.)

Now — the hinge. *Ḥattā ansawkum dhikrī.* **"Until they made you forget My remembrance."**

<!-- morphology:23:110:7 root=نسي pos=V -->*Ansaw* — Form IV, the causative. *Made-forget.* And attached to it, *kum*, "you." And its object, *dhikrī* — "My remembrance," with that same intimate *yāʾ* again: <!-- morphology:23:110:10 root=ذكر pos=N -->*My* remembrance.

Now go slowly, because the grammar is about to do something that should stop your breath.

**Who is the subject of that verb?** Who did the making-forget? Read it again: *ḥattā ansawkum* — *until* ***they*** *made you forget.* The subject is the *waw*, the "they." And who is "they"? It is the **believers.** The party of My servants. The ones being laughed at.

Do you see it?

The mockers are the powerful ones. The believers are the powerless ones. And yet in Allah's sentence, the powerless believers are the **subject** — the *doers* — and the powerful mockers are the **object**, the ones something is *done to.* The very question I asked you to carry from the beginning. **The people with no power became the grammatical force in the sentence, and the people with all the power became the thing acted upon.**

And what did the believers "do" to them? *They made them forget Allah.*

Now — did the believers stand there casting spells? Of course not. Here is the terrible mechanism, and it is exact. The mockers became so **consumed** with ridiculing the believers — so busy, so occupied, so entertained by the daily project of laughing at these people — that the mockery ate the space in their hearts where *dhikr*, the remembrance of Allah, was supposed to live. Mocking the God-rememberers *displaced* the remembrance of God. Their obsession with the believers crowded Him out.

So in the truest sense, **the believers really were the cause.** Not by anything they did. By what they *were.* The mockers organized their whole inner life around opposing these people — and a heart that is full of someone can hold no one else. Allah writes it as *"they made you forget Me,"* because that is what it amounts to: *you were so busy with My servants that you had no room left for Me.*

[PAUSE]

Let that land as the warning it is. **You can be destroyed not by what you love, but by what you cannot stop hating.** The mockers did not forget Allah because they were chasing wealth or women or wine. They forgot Him because they could not stop being *fixated on the believers.* The object of their contempt quietly became the master of their attention. And whatever masters your attention has already begun to be your god.

There is a mirror here you cannot unsee once you have seen it. The believers spent their days *remembering* Allah — *rabbanā āmannā.* The mockers spent their days remembering the *believers* — who they were, how they prayed, how to imitate them, when to laugh. Two groups, two obsessions. One filled the heart with *dhikrī.* The other filled it with *them.* And on the Day, one obsession turned out to be the door and the other turned out to be the pit.

*Wa kuntum minhum taḍḥakūn* — <!-- morphology:23:110:17 root=ضحك pos=V -->**"and you used to laugh at them."** And notice the grammar of that little clause too: *kuntum … taḍḥakūn.* It is not "you laughed" — a single event. It is the *continuous past,* the habitual. **You used to laugh.** It was ongoing. It was a *pastime.* It was recreation. It was the thing they did on slow afternoons. That is the horror of it — it never felt like a sin. It felt like *fun.* Nobody schedules their damnation. They just kept laughing, and the laughter kept quietly emptying them out.

That is the second word. Now the verdict.

### Three — The pronoun that overturns the scoreboard: *hum*

One more small piece of grammar, and it is my favorite in the language, because it exists for one purpose: to *insist.*

In Arabic, if you want to say "they are the winners," you can simply say *humu l-fāʾizūn.* Done. But if you want to make it *unshakable* — if you want to slam the door on any other possibility — you can drop in an *extra* pronoun that the sentence does not strictly need. It is called the *ḍamīr al-faṣl*, the "pronoun of separation." It sits between the subject and what you are saying about it, and its entire job is emphasis and *restriction.* It means: *these ones — and I do mean* ***these ones, exclusively*** *— are the thing I am about to say.*

Now hear the final ayah, the verdict, and listen for the doubling:

*Innī jazaytuhumu l-yawma bimā ṣabarū* ***annahum hum-u l-fāʾizūn***

<!-- morphology:23:111:16 root=فوز pos=N -->**"…that** ***they, they alone,*** **are the triumphant."**

*Annahum* already contains "they." Allah did not need to say it again. And then He says it again: *hum.* **They — they — are the winners.** The doubled pronoun is Allah putting His hand flat on the table. Not you. Not the ones who laughed. Not the ones with the status and the circles and the impressions. **These. These ones you could not stop laughing at. They are the winners. And no one else is.**

And the word for winners — sit with it — is *al-fāʾizūn*, <!-- morphology:23:111:16 root=فوز pos=N -->from the root fāʾ-wāw-zāy, *fawz.* *Fawz* in Arabic is not a small win. It is not points on a board. Its root sense is *escape to safety, deliverance, coming through the danger to the far side and arriving whole.* The one who achieves *fawz* is the one who was in mortal peril and got *out* — and got *out with the prize.* Rescued *and* rewarded.

Now feel the cruelty of the timing, and I mean cruelty toward the mockers. Rewind three ayahs. Who was it who cried out to be rescued? *Rabbanā akhrijnā minhā* — the mockers, screaming to be let *out* of the Fire. They wanted *fawz.* They wanted deliverance, escape to safety. And they were told *ikhsaʾū* — slink back, be gone. **The escape they begged for and were denied is the exact word Allah now stamps on the foreheads of the ones they laughed at.** *Al-fāʾizūn.* The ones who *got out.* The mockers reached for the door and it was barred. The mocked *are* through it.

<!-- morphology:23:111:7 root=يوم pos=N -->And one word more: *al-yawm.* **"This Day."** *I have rewarded them* ***this Day.*** In the marketplace of Makkah, the scoreboard read one way for decades. The mockers were up. The believers were down. And a person could have died in the middle of that, still being laughed at, and it would have looked, on that day, like the mockers had won the whole game. But Allah says *al-yawm* — **there is another Day, and on that Day the board is not adjusted, it is *inverted.*** The ones who were at the bottom of every human scoreboard are read out, by name, by the Owner, as the only ones who won.

That is the third word. Now let's leave the grammar and ask what all of this is doing to *us* — because these three ayahs are not a story about the seventh century. They are a diagnosis of something that is happening in your life and mine right now.

## PART TWO: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The tax on being seen worshipping

Let me start you somewhere completely ordinary.

You are nineteen. You are at university, maybe, or you are the only practicing one in your friend group, or you are the cousin at the family gathering. And the *adhān* goes, or it is just that time, and you have to get up and go pray. And there is this half-second — you know the half-second — where you calculate. *If I get up now, they'll notice. Someone will say something. Someone will do the little smirk.* And maybe you do get up, but you do it apologetically. You mumble it. You go find a room quietly, like you are getting away with something. Or maybe you tell yourself you'll pray later, and you sink back into the couch, and the moment passes, and something in your chest that wanted to stand up sits back down instead.

Or you are in a work meeting and someone makes the joke — the little joke about "your people," or about how intense you are, about how you *always* have some rule — and everyone laughs, and you laugh too. You laugh *along.* Because the alternative is to be the one who does not laugh, and being the one who does not laugh is its own kind of exposure.

I want to name what that half-second *is,* because we do not have good language for it and so we do not defend against it. That half-second is a **tax.** There is a tax on being seen worshipping. There always has been. The currency changes — in Makkah it was open ridicule in the street; for us it is the smirk, the "you're so extra," the eye-roll, the group chat, the sense of being *cringe* — but it is the same tax, and it is collected in the same coin: your dignity, in small increments, in front of people whose approval you have quietly made into a god.

Now here is what our ayah does to that scene. It does not tell you the tax is not real. It does not say "just don't care what people think," which is advice nobody has ever successfully followed. It does something far more useful. It shows you **the collector's final balance sheet.**

Because watch who is talking in ayah 109. Allah is describing the believers, and what is the one thing He records them saying? Not their arguments. Not their comebacks. Not one clever thing they said back to the mockers. He records: *Rabbanā āmannā faghfir lanā warḥamnā.* **"Our Lord, we believed, so forgive us and have mercy on us."** That is it. That is the whole recorded speech of the people who won. They were not building a case against their enemies. They were turned *the other way* — face toward Allah, asking Him to cover them and be gentle with them. The mockery was happening *behind* them, and they would not turn around to answer it, because they were busy with the only Face that was ever going to matter.

And Allah *saw the direction they were facing.* That is what He is telling you. **The tax you pay for being seen worshipping is not a loss. It is a deposit, and it is deposited into an account you cannot see from here, held by the One who calls you *ʿibādī* — Mine.**

[PAUSE]

But there is a second, sharper edge on this theme, and I would be failing you if I left it soft.

We have talked about being the one who is mocked. Turn the mirror around. Because you and I are, more often than we admit, on the *other* side of it. We are the ones in the group chat. We are the ones who find the video of the earnest person — the man crying in his duʿā, the woman in full niqāb, the convert who is "trying too hard," the brother who is "so preachy" — and we send it with the laughing emoji. We would never call it mockery. We call it *banter.* We call it *just joking.* We call it *he's being cringe.*

And here is where the second ayah reaches across fourteen centuries and grabs us by the collar. Because Allah did not merely record that the mockers *committed a sin.* He recorded the *mechanism* by which the sin destroyed them: *ḥattā ansawkum dhikrī* — **until it made you forget Me.** The problem was never only that mockery is unkind. The problem is what mockery *does to the mocker.* It fills you. A heart obsessed with what is ridiculous about the devout has no room left to be devout. You cannot laugh at someone's *dhikr* and be doing *dhikr* in the same breath. The laughter crowds Him out.

So the danger of the group chat is not just that you are being mean to a brother. It is that you are quietly, one laughing emoji at a time, building an inner life whose central occupation is *contempt for the people who remember Allah* — and that is the exact spiritual position of the people in this ayah who begged to get out of the Fire and could not.

What does this change tomorrow? Concretely — the next time you feel the smirk aimed at you, do not spend your energy on the comeback. Spend it on the direction you are facing. Say the believers' sentence: *rabbanā āmannā.* And the next time the laughing video lands in your palm and your thumb wants to forward it — feel that pull for exactly what it is: an invitation to let something small and mean take up residence where *dhikr* is supposed to live. Put the phone down. That is not being uptight. That is guarding the one room in the house that Allah asked you to keep for Him.

### Theme Two — The long game nobody is clapping for

Here is a scene you know, even if it has never happened to you, because our whole culture is built around teaching you to dread it.

Someone disrespects you. Publicly. Says the cutting thing, takes the credit that was yours, humiliates you a little in front of people. And you feel the two forces pull. One of them — the loud one, the one the whole world is cheering for — says: *clap back. Say something. You cannot let that slide. If you let them disrespect you, you're weak. Where's your self-respect?* We have entire genres of entertainment devoted to the beauty of the comeback. The perfect clap-back is a cultural sacrament. To *absorb* an insult without answering it is, in the modern moral universe, almost shameful. We do not have a category for dignified silence anymore. We only have "standing up for yourself" and "being a doormat."

And the other force — the quiet one — says: *let it go. Endure it. Say nothing.* And when you follow that one, nobody claps. There is no video of it. There is no satisfying moment. You just swallow it and walk away, and it feels, in the moment, exactly like losing.

Now bring the ayah into that room. Because Allah tells you *precisely what the believers were rewarded for,* and He is very specific about it. He does not say *I rewarded them for their faith.* He said that faith already, back in 109. Watch the word in the verdict:

*Innī jazaytuhumu l-yawma* ***bimā ṣabarū***

<!-- morphology:23:111:10 root=صبر pos=V -->**"I have rewarded them this Day** ***for what they patiently endured.***"

*Bimā ṣabarū.* The little *bāʾ* on the front there is the *bāʾ* of exchange — *in return for, in payment for.* The reward is the *price* paid for one specific thing: their *ṣabr.* Their endurance. And what did they endure? Go back and look — there is no record of them clapping back. No record of a single comeback. The one thing the surah shows them doing is turning to Allah and saying *rabbanā.* They took the mockery, day after day after day, the continuous past tense of it, *kuntum minhum taḍḥakūn,* the endless laughing — and they answered it with **silence toward the mockers and speech toward their Lord.**

And that silence — the thing that looked like losing, the thing nobody would have clapped for, the thing the modern voice would have called weakness — *that* is the exact thing Allah put on the invoice. **The silence was the currency.** Every insult they swallowed without answering was a coin, and they did not know it, and it was being counted, and on *al-yawm* — this Day — the full account was paid out at a rate no one on earth could have imagined: *annahum humu l-fāʾizūn.* They, they alone, are the ones who came through and got the prize.

Do you understand what Allah is quietly telling you about time?

The clap-back gives you your reward *now.* It is a cash economy. Instant. You say the thing, you get the little rush, the group laughs, the score feels settled — and it is *over.* You have been paid in full, on the spot, in the currency of this world, and there is nothing left in the account. Ṣabr is the opposite. Ṣabr feels like putting money into an account with no visible balance and no statements, run by a Bank you have to take entirely on trust. For years it looks like you are just losing money. You endure, you say nothing, you turn to Allah, and the world's scoreboard keeps reading *you are down.* And then one Day the Owner of the Bank stands up and reads the balance aloud, and it turns out the silence you thought was weakness was the single most profitable thing you ever did.

I think of the person who is caring for a difficult parent, quietly, for a decade, with no one watching and no one thanking them — and a sibling who does nothing gets the praise at every gathering. I think of the woman who keeps the marriage dignified and does not air the grievance, and is called foolish for it. I think of the young man who does not retaliate against the coworker who lied about him, and has to watch that coworker get promoted. In every one of those rooms, the loud voice is screaming *you're losing, do something, don't let them.* And the ayah is standing in the corner of every one of those rooms, saying one thing: *bimā ṣabarū.* Keep enduring. It is being counted. There is a Day. And the board does not get adjusted on that Day — it gets **flipped.**

What does this change tomorrow? The next time you are wronged and you feel that furious pull toward the comeback, ask yourself one honest question: *do I want to be paid now, or on the Day?* Because you usually cannot have both. The comeback is a withdrawal. The silence is a deposit. And Allah has told you, in a doubled pronoun that leaves no room to argue, exactly which account He settles in full.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — why this could not be an accident

Now that we have done the work, stand back and look at the shape of what Allah built, because there is a design here that proves the whole thing was authored.

Look at the pronouns across the three ayahs. Watch whose they are.

Ayah 109 opens with **"My** servants" — *ʿibād****ī.*** Allah's possessive. His hand on the believers from the very first breath.

Ayah 111 closes with **"I** have rewarded them" — *jazaytu.* Allah's own first-person verb. His hand on them again at the very last breath.

And what sits in the middle, in ayah 110? *You* took them as a mockery. *You* laughed. The crime. The mockery is placed **exactly between** Allah's opening claim of ownership and His closing act of reward. It is *bracketed.* It is *surrounded* on both sides by Allah — "these are Mine" on one flank, "I rewarded them" on the other, and the sneering, laughing crime penned in helplessly between them.

Think about what the mockers believed they were doing. They thought *they* were surrounding the believers — socially isolating them, encircling them with laughter, hemming them in. And in the architecture of these ayahs, it is precisely the reverse. **It is the mockery that is surrounded. It is the laughter that is enclosed.** Trapped between "My servants" and "I rewarded them," with no way out — begging *akhrijnā,* let us out, and hearing *ikhsaʾū.* The very thing the mockers did to the believers in the world, Allah does to the mockery itself in the verse: He surrounds it, and He shuts the door.

And then the two doors of the whole surah swing shut and face each other. It opened, *Qad aflaḥa l-muʾminūn* — the believers have *succeeded,* from *falāḥ,* the flourishing of the one who planted and reaped. It closes, *humu l-fāʾizūn* — they are the *triumphant,* from *fawz,* the escape of the one who was in danger and got out alive with the prize. **The surah begins by declaring the harvest and ends by declaring the escape.** You have flourished — and you have gotten out. And in case you missed the invitation, look at the *very last ayah* of the surah, ayah 118: *Rabbi ghfir warḥam wa anta khayru r-rāḥimīn* — **"My Lord, forgive and have mercy, and You are the best of the merciful."** Allah closes the entire surah by teaching *you* to say the exact sentence He recorded the winners saying in 109. He is handing you their words. He is handing you the winning ticket and telling you to fill in your own name.

### Where we started, and where we've arrived

We started with a question: why are the powerless the subject of the verbs, and the powerful the object? Now you can answer it yourself.

Because the whole passage is a single overturning. It looks like a scene about the strong and the weak, and it is really a scene about who was awake and who was asleep. The mockers were the ones something was *done to* — they were the object — because they were passive to the thing that mattered: their hearts were being emptied of *dhikr* while they thought they were busy winning. The believers were the *subject* — the ones acting — because the one thing that is truly action, in Allah's economy, is where you turn your face. They kept turning it toward Him. And it turns out that the person quietly saying *rabbanā āmannā* while being laughed at is the most active person in the room, and the person laughing is the one being slowly done-to, being emptied, being decided.

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes were never two things. They are one truth approached from two sides. *ʿIbādī* and *the tax on worship* are the same insight: what looks unclaimed and disposable to the world is stamped as *Mine* by the Owner. *Bimā ṣabarū* and *the long game* are the same insight: the currency of this Day is not collected in this world. It is the same overturning, said once in grammar and once in the language of your ordinary Tuesday.

### Questions to carry

Not rhetorical. Sit with them.

- When you feel the smirk aimed at you for your worship — what direction do you turn? Toward the comeback, or toward *rabbanā*?
- Where in your life are you paying the tax on being seen worshipping — and quietly deciding it is too expensive?
- Is there a group chat, a video, a laugh, that is slowly furnishing your heart with contempt for people who remember Allah? What is it crowding out?
- When you were last wronged — did you take the reward now, or did you make the deposit?
- If Allah read the balance of your silences aloud on *al-yawm* — would there be anything in the account?

### The one sentence to carry

**The laughter they spent on the servants of God was the very sound of their own forgetting — and the silence the believers were mocked for was the coin that bought them everything.**

### The invitation

You will be taxed for this dīn. That is not a threat; it is just the weather of being a believer among people who are not. The smirk is coming. The "you're so extra" is coming. The half-second of calculation before you stand up to pray is coming today, probably.

The invitation of these three ayahs is simply this: **let it be a deposit.** Do not spend your life answering the mockers; spend it facing the One who calls you *ʿibādī.* Pay the tax gladly, in the currency of a little worldly dignity, into an account held by *khayru r-rāḥimīn* — the best of the merciful — who has already scheduled the Day He reads the balance aloud. And when that voice inside you says *you're losing* — recognize it, and answer it with the only word that flips the board: *rabbanā.*

### Duʿā

*O Allah, whose hand is on the ones the world throws away — count us among the ʿibādī You call Your own.* Give our tongues, when we are laughed at, the believers' sentence and not the mocker's: *rabbanā āmannā faghfir lanā warḥamnā.* Guard the room in our hearts that is meant for Your remembrance, and never let our contempt for anyone empty it. And when every worldly scoreboard has gone dark, let us be among those You name on that Day — *humu l-fāʾizūn* — the ones who endured, and got out, and came home. For You are *khayru r-rāḥimīn.* Āmīn.
