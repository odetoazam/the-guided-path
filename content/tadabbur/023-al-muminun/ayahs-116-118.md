# The Throne and the Whisper: When the Exalted King Becomes "My Lord"

## Introduction

Here is where the surah ends.

> [ayah:23:116] فَتَعَـٰلَى ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمَلِكُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۖ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ رَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ ٱلْكَرِيمِ

*Fa-taʿālā-llāhu-l-maliku-l-ḥaqq. Lā ilāha illā huwa, rabbu-l-ʿarshi-l-karīm.*

**So exalted is Allah, the Sovereign, the Real; there is no god but He, Lord of the Noble Throne.**

> [ayah:23:117] وَمَن يَدْعُ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَـٰهًا ءَاخَرَ لَا بُرْهَـٰنَ لَهُۥ بِهِۦ فَإِنَّمَا حِسَابُهُۥ عِندَ رَبِّهِۦٓ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ

*Wa man yadʿu maʿa-llāhi ilāhan ākhara lā burhāna lahu bihi fa-innamā ḥisābuhu ʿinda rabbih. Innahu lā yufliḥu-l-kāfirūn.*

**And whoever calls upon, besides Allah, another god for which he has no proof — his reckoning is only with his Lord. Indeed, the deniers will not succeed.**

> [ayah:23:118] وَقُل رَّبِّ ٱغْفِرْ وَٱرْحَمْ وَأَنتَ خَيْرُ ٱلرَّٰحِمِينَ

*Wa qul rabbi-ghfir wa-rḥam wa anta khayru-r-rāḥimīn.*

**And say: My Lord, forgive and have mercy, and You are the best of those who show mercy.**

### Rebuild the world

Sit with where these words arrive.

You are in Mecca. Not the Mecca of a million pilgrims and glass towers — the Mecca of a small, harassed community, in a city that runs on gods. Three hundred and sixty of them stand around the Kaʿba. There is a god for the harvest and a god for the rain, a god for the tribe and a god for the journey, a goddess for fate and a goddess for fortune. Worship is not a private conviction here. It is the operating system of the entire society — its economy, its calendar, its politics, its sense of who is safe and who is exposed. To question the gods is not to lose an argument. It is to threaten the pilgrimage traffic, the shrine revenue, the alliances, the whole machinery by which Mecca eats.

And into this, for a hundred and fifteen verses, the surah has been building an argument that is really a single, sustained act of clearing the ground. It opened — and hold onto this, because we will return to it at the summit — by announcing exactly which human beings actually *make it*: the believers, described stroke by stroke, the ones humbled in prayer, the ones who turn from what is empty, the ones who guard their trusts. Then the surah widens. It walks you through your own creation — a drop, a clot, a lump, bones clothed in flesh. It takes you out to the rain that Allah sends down "in due measure," to the gardens it raises, to the ships that carry you. It marches the prophets past you — Nuh mocked by his people, Musa and Harun called liars, ʿIsa and his mother made a sign. It lets the deniers speak their objections out loud: *When we are dust and bones, are we really to be raised again?* It shows you the Day those objections collapse — the trumpet, the faces, the moment a man will beg to be sent back and be refused.

And then, after all of that — after the whole cosmos and all of history have been called as witnesses — the surah gathers itself for its final breath. It is about to say the one thing everything else was for.

Everything you have felt building — the pressure of a society drowning in false gods, the long patient argument, the scenes of judgment — all of it is about to resolve. Not into a thunderclap. Not into a curse on the enemies of God.

Into something you would never have predicted.

### The hook

Watch the altitude in these three verses. The first word lifts God *above* — above the Throne, above every rival, beyond reach. And the last word Allah teaches His Prophet to say is not a word about conquest, or vindication, or even about the truth being proven at last.

It is one syllable of need, spoken upward: *Rabbi.* **My Lord.**

The most exalted verse in the surah and the most intimate word in the surah are pressed together in the same breath. [PAUSE] What is that doing there? Why does the God who is declared utterly, dizzyingly *above* end the entire surah being called, by name, *mine*?

Stay with that question. It is the whole thing.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The word that rises as it means it — *fa-taʿālā*

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because if I don't, the first word of these verses will slide past you as ordinary praise, and it is anything but ordinary.

In Arabic, a verb has a bare root — a skeleton of usually three consonants — and then it gets *shaped*. The same root can be poured into different molds, and each mold does something to the meaning. Think of it like a single metal poured into different casts: same substance, radically different object. Take a simple root that means "to do." Put it in one mold and it means "he did." Put it in another and it means "he made someone else do it." Put it in yet another and it means "he pretended to do it," or "they did it back and forth to each other."

One of these molds — the grammarians call it Form VI — carries the flavor of *mutual, sustained, reaching action*, or of a quality taken to its fullest, self-contained height. When a root passes through this mold, it often means not just "to be X" but "to be X in a way that is complete in itself, needing nothing outside it."

Now the root here is ʿayn-lām-wāw <!-- morphology:23:116:2 root=علو pos=V -->, and its bare meaning is *height* — to be high, to rise, to be elevated. The plain form would just say "high." But Allah does not use the plain form. He pours this root of *height* into that Form VI mold: **taʿālā** <!-- morphology:23:116:2 root=علو pos=V -->.

Say it slowly. *Fa — ta — ʿaa — laa.* Feel what your mouth does. The word opens on a soft breath and then *climbs* — two long open vowels, one after the other, *ʿaa-laa*, the sound lifting and lengthening and not coming back down. This is a word that physically ascends in the mouth of the one who says it. **The word for "being exalted" is built to be spoken like an ascent.** The sound is not decoration on the meaning. The sound *is* the meaning, rehearsed by your own tongue before your mind has caught up.

And the mold tells you *how* He is high. Not high the way a mountain is high — high relative to a valley, needing the valley to be high at all. Form VI here says: high in a way that is total, self-standing, that does not borrow its elevation from anything beneath it. Allah is exalted not *over* the false gods as if they were real competitors He happens to outrank. He is exalted in a category they never entered.

Notice what comes immediately after: **الْمَلِكُ الْحَقُّ** <!-- morphology:23:116:5 root=ملك pos=N --> — *al-Malik, al-Ḥaqq* — the Sovereign, the Real. And here is something the classical readers loved to sit with, so let me build it with you rather than hand it to you. *Al-Malik* <!-- morphology:23:116:5 root=ملك pos=N --> is the King, the one whose authority is actual, whose kingdom is not a claim but a fact. *Al-Ḥaqq*  — from the root that means truth, reality, the thing that is genuinely *there* as opposed to the thing that only seems to be. To call God *al-Ḥaqq* is to say: He is not merely *true* the way a correct statement is true. He is *the Real* — the one whose existence is the standard by which we measure what is real and what is only a rumor of existence.

So watch what these three words do together in a single line. **He rises** (taʿālā). **He alone truly rules** (al-Malik). **He alone truly is** (al-Ḥaqq). Height, sovereignty, reality — the ground being cleared with three strokes. And on ground this clear, what could possibly still be standing beside Him? The next words answer before you can ask: **لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ** — *there is no god but He.* Of course. After al-Ḥaqq, the Real, everything else is exposed as what it was — a shadow that owed its whole appearance to the light it was blocking.

We will hold this rising word in our hands for the rest of the journey. Remember how high it went. [PAUSE]

### Element Two: The proof that could never exist — *lā burhāna lahu bih*

Now the verse turns to the one who worships something else, and I want you to watch a single small clause, because Allah has hidden an entire argument inside a description.

Here is the person: **وَمَن يَدْعُ مَعَ اللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا آخَرَ** <!-- morphology:23:117:3 root=دعو pos=V --> — *whoever calls upon, besides Allah, another god.* And then Allah describes that "other god" with four words: **لَا بُرْهَانَ لَهُ بِهِ** <!-- morphology:23:117:9 root=برهن pos=N --> — *for which he has no burhān.*

*Burhān* <!-- morphology:23:117:9 root=برهن pos=N --> is not a soft word for "a reason" or "a feeling." In Arabic *burhān* is the heavy word — decisive, conclusive proof. The kind of evidence that ends the discussion. The kind a judge cannot wave away. Allah did not say the idolater has *weak* evidence, or *outdated* evidence, or evidence that was fine once. He said: he has *no burhān* — none, at the level of conclusive proof — for the thing he has bet his entire life on.

Now here is the part the grammarians want you to slow down for, and it is easy to walk straight past. On the surface this clause reads like a mere fact about a particular worshipper: *this* man happens to have no proof. As if another worshipper of another false god might, on a better day, produce some. But read the structure again. The description "for which he has no proof" attaches to *another god* as such — to the very category of a rival to Allah. And a rival to *al-Ḥaqq*, the Real — the one we just watched clear the ground — is by definition a thing with no reality behind it. So there could never *be* a burhān. **The clause is describing an impossibility as though it were a missing item.** It is not that the shelf where the proof should sit happens to be empty. It is that there is no such thing as the object that shelf was built for, and never was.

Read the absence as carefully as the presence. Allah does not argue with the idolater's evidence — He notes that the space where evidence should stand is, and always has been, vacant. And notice what He does *not* do next. He does not thunder. He does not describe a punishment in this line at all. He says, almost quietly: **فَإِنَّمَا حِسَابُهُ عِندَ رَبِّهِ** — *his reckoning is only with his Lord.* The word *innamā* <!-- morphology:23:117:15 pos=P --> is a particle of restriction — it means *only, nothing but*. His account is *only* with his Lord. It is filed. It is sealed. It is not yours to prosecute and not the Prophet's to force. The matter is closed and set down before the One who will open it at the proper time.

One way to sit with this: the deepest emptiness in a human life is often the one thing we will never let ourselves examine. We will audit everything — our finances, our diets, our calendars — except the object at the very center, the thing we have organized our whole existence around. And Allah's response to the man who has built his life on a foundation with no *burhān* is not to seize him. It is to let the appointment stand. His reckoning is with his Lord. [PAUSE]

### Element Three: When the voice turns — from *taʿālā* to *Rabbi*

I want to end the linguistic journey where the surah ends, because the final verse does something with *who is being spoken to* that will change how you hear the whole passage.

Let me first name the tool. In Arabic — and this is one of the Qur'an's most powerful and least noticed devices — the speaker can suddenly *turn*, mid-passage, and change who is being addressed. The grammarians call it *iltifāt*, literally a "turning of the face." One moment the text is speaking *about* someone in the third person — *he, they, it.* The next moment, without warning, it wheels around and speaks *to* someone directly — *you.* We feel this instinctively even in English. There is a difference between someone in a room saying "*he* has been struggling lately" and that same person turning, looking straight at you, and saying "*you* have been struggling lately." The words carry the same information. The second one enters your chest.

Now trace the turning across these three verses.

Verse 116 speaks *about* God in the most distant, exalted register available: **fa-taʿālā-llāh** — *so exalted is Allah.* He is spoken of as one speaks of a majesty on a throne far above — third person, elevated, transcendent. Then the Throne itself: **rabbu-l-ʿarshi-l-karīm** <!-- morphology:23:116:14 root=عرش pos=N --> — *Lord of the Noble Throne.* The imagery could not be more vast: a throne, and the immensity of the One enthroned above it. Verse 117 stays in the distant register — *whoever calls... his reckoning is with his Lord... the deniers will not succeed.* We are still, so to speak, in the throne room, watching from the floor, the vaulted heights above us.

And then verse 118 turns. **وَقُل** <!-- morphology:23:118:2 root=قول pos=V --> — *and say.* The face of the discourse wheels toward the Prophet ﷺ: *you* — say. And what he is told to say is itself a turning, the sharpest in the surah: **رَّبِّ** <!-- morphology:23:118:3 root=ربب pos=N --> — *Rabbi. My Lord.*

Feel the distance that just collapsed. Two verses ago God was *taʿālā* — the word that climbs out of reach, the King of the Noble Throne, exalted in a category nothing else can enter. And now, in the same breath of the same passage, He is being addressed with a possessive suffix so small it is barely a sound: *Rabb-**i***. *My* Lord. The most transcendent word in the surah and the most intimate word in the surah are separated by a single verse — and the movement runs entirely *downward toward you*, from the throne to the whisper.

And listen to what the mouth does now, because the sound turns too. The rising, open, unreachable vowels of *ta-ʿaa-laa* give way to a cluster of short, urgent, close syllables: **rabbi-ghfir wa-rḥam** <!-- morphology:23:118:5 root=غفر pos=V --> — *my Lord, forgive, have mercy.* These are clipped. Bare imperatives, no ornament, the way you actually speak when you need something and there is no time to be elegant. *Ighfir. Irḥam.* Forgive. Have mercy. This is not the language of the throne room. This is the language of a child at a parent's knee, of the mouth that has stopped performing and started asking.

The One who is *taʿālā* — utterly above — is the same One you call *Rabbi* — intimately yours. Grammar just walked us the entire distance from the Throne to your own tongue. [PAUSE]

### The bridge

So the words have shown us three things. A God who rises beyond every rival, self-standing in a height nothing shares. A rival-worship whose central object was always a shelf built for a proof that could never sit on it. And a voice that travels, in three verses, from the most distant name of God to the most intimate.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human life actually works. Because these are not only facts about God. They are a diagnosis of us.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: What it actually means to "make it"

Let me start somewhere far from the mosque.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that the most "successful" people you know are carrying, and it has a strange shape. It is not the exhaustion of failure. It is the exhaustion of *arrival that didn't work.* The promotion came and the relief lasted a weekend. The number in the account crossed the threshold that was supposed to mean "safe," and safe never showed up. The house, the title, the followers, the degree — each one was going to be the thing that finally let them exhale, and each one, on arrival, quietly handed them the next threshold. There is a word people use for this now — the *hedonic treadmill*, the machine that resets the finish line the instant you cross it, so that you are always running and never *there.* We have measured this in studies and named it in podcasts. And underneath the studies is a very old ache: *I thought success would feel like arriving, and it feels like running.*

Here is what these verses say about that ache. And to hear it, you have to know something about how the surah is built — a thing hidden in a single word.

The verb here in verse 117 is **يُفْلِحُ** <!-- morphology:23:117:25 root=فلح pos=V --> — *yufliḥu*, from the root fā-lām-ḥāʾ, which means to *succeed, to prosper, to attain what you were reaching for.* Its deepest sense in the old Arabic is agricultural: *falāḥ* is what the farmer gets when the seed he buried, tended, and could not control finally breaks the soil and yields. It is the harvest. It is the payoff of long patience. To have *falāḥ* is to be the person for whom it all, at last, *came good.* And Allah says here of the one who built his life on a god with no proof: **لَا يُفْلِحُ الْكَافِرُونَ** <!-- morphology:23:117:27 root=كفر pos=N --> — *the deniers will not succeed.* Whatever they harvest, it will not be *falāḥ.* The crop will come up, and it will be ash.

Now hold that word, because I am going to show you where you have heard it before. Go all the way back to the very first verse of this same surah — the opening line, before creation, before the prophets, before any of it: **قَدْ أَفْلَحَ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ** — *the believers have succeeded.* Same root. Same harvest-word. *Aflaḥa.* The surah **opens** by declaring who gets the harvest — and it **closes**, here in our verses, by declaring who never will. The whole surah is stretched like a rope between those two poles of a single word. Everything in between — your creation, the rain, the prophets, the Day — is the surah answering one question it asked in its first breath: *who actually makes it?*

And here is the answer these verses press into you. *Falāḥ* — real success — was never a threshold you cross. It is not the promotion or the harvest arriving. It is *what you were reaching for being real in the first place.* The denier's tragedy in verse 117 is not that he worked and lost. It is that he reached, with everything he had, toward a god with *no burhān* — toward a finish line painted on a wall. He can run at it forever. He can even, in worldly terms, reach it. And it will not yield, because there was nothing behind it to yield.

So the treadmill is not a malfunction of your effort. It is the honest response of a false god. A thing with no reality behind it *cannot* give you rest, no matter how fast you run at it, because rest is not one of the things it possesses to give. You are not failing to arrive. You are arriving, again and again, at something that was never there.

What changes tomorrow if you take this seriously? One concrete thing: before you pour another year of your one life into a finish line, ask the question of verse 117 about it — *where is its burhān?* Not "is it impressive." Not "will people respect it." *Does the thing I am reaching for actually have the reality to give me what I am asking it for?* Most of what exhausts us fails that question in a sentence. And the ones that pass it — they were never idols. They were always ways of reaching toward the One who is *al-Ḥaqq*, the Real, the only finish line that is actually *there* when you arrive. [PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Why the ones who "made it" are the ones told to ask for mercy

Now watch what should be an impossible move, and let me set it up with a scene you know.

Picture the end of anything you have ever worked hard and long for. The final whistle. The signed deal. The last exam handed in. What is the natural posture of that moment? It is the raised arms. It is the exhale, the fist, the *we did it.* Vindication has a body language, and that body language is *upward and outward* — chest open, chin up, the pose of the one who has been proven right. This is not a flaw. It is deeply human. After a long fight, the body wants to stand tall.

Now look at how Allah ends this surah — a surah that has, over a hundred and eighteen verses, comprehensively *won the argument.* The false gods have been cleared away. The deniers have been told, flatly, they will not succeed. The Real has been exalted above His Throne. By every human instinct, this is the moment for the raised arms. This is the victory verse. The surah has earned its fist in the air.

And what does Allah teach His Prophet to do at the summit of the argument? **وَقُل رَّبِّ اغْفِرْ وَارْحَمْ** — *and say: My Lord, forgive and have mercy.*

Not *we were right.* Not *they have lost.* Not even *thank You for the victory.* The last instruction of the entire surah is to *lower yourself and ask to be forgiven.* At the exact moment the human heart reaches for the fist, revelation reaches for the knee.

Sit with how strange that is, and don't let it resolve too quickly. The believers were named "successful" in verse one. The Prophet ﷺ, the most God-conscious of all creation, is the one being addressed. These are the people who, by any account, have *made it.* And the word placed in their mouths at the finish line is not a word of arrival. It is *ighfir* — forgive me. *Irḥam* — have mercy on me.

Here is what that does, and it is the answer to the ache we opened with. Remember the exhaustion of the ones who arrived and found no rest? Part of what breaks them is a belief they never say out loud: *when I finally make it, I will have earned my standing. I will no longer be someone who needs.* Arrival, in the treadmill logic, is supposed to be the end of neediness — the day you graduate from asking. And it never comes, partly because that graduation does not exist. There is no altitude of success at which a human being stops being a creature who needs mercy.

So Allah, in the last breath of a surah about success, cuts the tie between *making it* and *no longer needing.* He shows the summit and puts a plea for forgiveness on it. As if to say: *this — the open hand, the "forgive me," the posture of the one who still needs — this is not what you do until you succeed. This is what success looks like.* The one who has truly *made it* is not the one who has stopped asking. It is the one who, even standing at the top, turns his face upward and says *Rabbi, irḥam.*

And notice — read the absence — how the surah refuses to end on the enemies at all. It could have. The natural close after "the deniers will not succeed" is a line about their punishment, a final word about *them.* Instead the very last thing Allah does is turn the Prophet's face *away* from the losers and *toward his Lord*, and put mercy — not judgment — in his mouth. The surah does not end looking down at those who fell. It ends looking up, asking to be caught.

Hear the last words themselves: **وَأَنتَ خَيْرُ الرَّاحِمِينَ** <!-- morphology:23:118:7 root=رحم pos=V --> — *and You are the best of those who show mercy.* The root raḥima — mercy, the same root as the womb, the *raḥim*, that holds and nourishes what cannot yet hold itself — appears *twice* in this final verse: *irḥam* <!-- morphology:23:118:7 root=رحم pos=V -->, have mercy, and *ar-rāḥimīn* <!-- morphology:23:118:7 root=رحم pos=V -->, the merciful ones. The surah that began by naming the successful *ends on the word for mercy, said twice*, the very last sound a long settling note — *rā-ḥi-mīn* — like a held breath finally let go.

What changes tomorrow? Stop waiting to arrive before you allow yourself to ask. The next time you catch yourself thinking *once I fix this, once I reach that, then I'll be right with God* — recognize the treadmill in it. The Prophet ﷺ was told to say *forgive me and have mercy* not *after* he succeeded but *as* his success. Bring the open hand into today, unearned, mid-struggle, exactly as you are. That is not the posture of someone who failed to make it. According to the last verse of Surah Al-Mu'minun, it is the only posture anyone who *made it* was ever in. [PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — the view from the summit

Now that we have climbed, look back down at the shape of what we climbed, because it was built with a precision that could not be accidental.

**The first architecture is the rope across the whole surah.** Verse one: *qad aflaḥa-l-muʾminūn* — the believers have succeeded. Verse 117, near the last breath: *lā yufliḥu-l-kāfirūn* — the deniers will not succeed. The same root, fā-lām-ḥāʾ, the same harvest-word, opening and closing a surah of a hundred and eighteen verses like two hands holding a single question. Everything between them — your body knit in the womb, the rain in its measure, Nuh and Musa and ʿIsa, the trumpet and the faces — all of it is the long answer to the question the first word asked: *who, in the end, gets the harvest?* And the last verses tell you the harvest was never a threshold. It was whether the thing you reached for was *real.*

**The second architecture is the descent inside these three verses themselves.** Watch it as a single motion. Verse 116 places God at the maximum height: *taʿālā* — exalted beyond reach — *rabbu-l-ʿarshi-l-karīm*, Lord of the Noble Throne, the imagery vaulting upward. Verse 117 stays high and distant — *their reckoning is with their Lord.* And verse 118 descends, all the way, to *Rabbi* — my Lord — and to two clipped, intimate imperatives whispered from a bowed head. The passage begins at the Throne and ends at your own tongue. It starts as high as language can point and lands as close as language can reach.

And here is why those two architectures are one architecture. The rope of the surah asks *what is success?* The descent of these verses answers it *in the shape of the verses themselves.* Success is not climbing up to the Throne and planting your flag. Success is the Throne coming down to you — the *taʿālā* becoming *Rabbi* — the utterly Exalted One letting you call Him *mine*, and then asking Him, from wherever you are, to forgive and have mercy. The linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same discovery from two sides: the false god has no *burhān* and so can never give you rest; the true God is *al-Ḥaqq*, the Real, and He does not make you ascend to Him to be held — He teaches you to say *Rabbi* right where you are.

### Where we started, where we arrived

We began with a question that felt like a puzzle: why does the most exalted verse in the surah sit in the same breath as its most intimate word? Now it is not a puzzle. It is the whole point. *Because the greatness of God is not a wall between you and Him. It is the very thing that makes His nearness worth having.* A small god you could reach would be no comfort — it would have no *burhān* and no rest to give. Only the God who is *taʿālā*, exalted beyond every rival, is worth calling *Rabbi.* The height and the nearness are not in tension. The height is *why* the nearness saves you.

### Questions to carry

- When you imagine yourself finally "having made it," what exactly are you picturing — and does that thing have a *burhān*, or is it a finish line painted on a wall?
- Where in your life are you still telling yourself *I'll be right with God once I've arrived* — and what would change if you brought the open hand into today instead?
- Is there something at the very center of your life that you audit least — the one object you have never dared to ask "where is its proof?"
- When you succeed at something, what does your body do — reach up in vindication, or bow in need? What would it mean to let *Rabbi, irḥam* be the shape of your victories?
- The surah refuses to end looking down at those who fell, and turns instead to ask for mercy. Where are you still standing in judgment over others when you could be turning your own face upward?

### The one sentence to carry

**The One who towers above the Throne is the very One you get to call "my Lord" — and the only success that was ever real is to end, even at the summit, with an open hand asking Him to forgive.**

### The invitation

So let this surah end in you the way it ends on the page. Not with the fist, but with the open hand. Not with *I was right*, but with *Rabbi, irḥam.* You do not have to arrive first. You do not have to earn the right to ask. The last word Allah placed in the mouth of the best of creation was a plea for mercy — offered not after the victory as a formality, but *as* the victory itself. Carry that home. When you next reach the top of some small climb of your own, or the bottom of some fall, say the words that close the surah, and mean them, and know that you are standing exactly where the successful have always stood.

### A du'a from these words

*Ya Allah — You who are exalted above Your Noble Throne, beyond every rival, self-standing in a height nothing shares: we do not ask You to come down to our level, for Your height is our hope. We ask only what You taught us to ask at the very summit. Rabbi-ghfir wa-rḥam — my Lord, forgive us and have mercy on us. Untie us from every finish line that has no proof behind it, and let the last posture of our lives be this one: the open hand, turned upward, toward the best of those who show mercy.* Ameen.
