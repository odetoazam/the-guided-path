# The Calculation That Goes Wrong

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 55–56

> أَيَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّمَا نُمِدُّهُم بِهِۦ مِن مَّالٍ وَبَنِينَ ۝ نُسَارِعُ لَهُمْ فِى ٱلْخَيْرَٰتِ ۚ بَل لَّا يَشْعُرُونَ

> *A-yaḥsabūna annamā numidduhum bihī min mālin wa banīn — nusāriʿu lahum fī al-khayrāt — bal lā yashʿurūn.*

> **"Do they reckon that what We extend to them — of wealth and children — that We are racing to them with good things? Nay, but they do not perceive."**

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

To hear what Allah is doing in these two ayahs, we have to inhabit a particular kind of confidence. Not the loud, militant disbelief of an enemy on the battlefield. Something quieter. Something that lives inside many of us right now without our noticing.

Picture a man in Makkah. He has rejected the message. But his rejection isn't an angry one. It's a comfortable one. He looks at his caravans returning from Yemen, full. He looks at his sons gathered around him at the end of the day. He looks at his standing in the tribe — the seat he is offered without asking, the ear he is granted without demanding it. And he does a calculation in his head. A small, almost invisible calculation. He says: *if I were truly on the wrong path, would all of this be coming to me? If God were displeased, would my hands be this full?*

He doesn't say this out loud. He may not even say it to himself in words. It is a felt logic. A background hum.

[PAUSE]

Now picture yourself.

The promotion came through. The investment paid off. The child got into the school. The follower count climbed. The body cooperated this week. The relationship is, against all odds, working. And somewhere underneath the gratitude — which is real, which is sincere — a smaller voice is also speaking. A voice that says: *this means I'm doing something right. This is the universe responding to me. This is how the story of someone favored looks from the inside.*

That is the voice these two ayahs are addressing.

This passage sits at the hinge of the surah. The believers have been described in their humility — eyes lowered in prayer, turning from idle talk, guarding their trust. The prophets have been paraded across the gallery — Nuh in the storm, Musa with the staff, Maryam with the child. And then, in ayah 53, Allah names what the people did with all of this clarity: they fragmented it. They split God's message into sects, each rejoicing in what they had — *kullu ḥizbin bimā ladayhim fariḥūn.* Each faction satisfied with its own piece of the broken mirror.

And then in ayah 54: *fa-dharhum fī ghamratihim ḥattā ḥīn* — "leave them in their confusion until a time."

You'd expect the next ayah to describe their punishment. Instead, Allah does something more terrifying. He diagnoses what is happening in their hearts *while* He leaves them. He tells us what they are thinking. He tells us why they don't run. Why they don't fear. Why they don't doubt their own footing.

**Because the world keeps responding to them.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook I want you to carry with me into this reflection. The Quran is going to suggest that the most dangerous moment in a human life is not when things are going badly. It is when things are going well — and you are misreading what that means.

The two ayahs in front of us are not about poor people or rich people. They are about a calculation. A specific, almost invisible accounting that the heart performs without permission. And they are asking: when you add up your blessings, what total are you arriving at?

Let's open the words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Means "To Calculate"

The ayah opens with a question: *a-yaḥsabūna* — "do they reckon?"

Before we look at the word itself, I want you to feel what the question is doing. Allah is not informing us. He is not narrating. He is asking. And the question is rhetorical in a particular way — it is the question a teacher asks a student who is about to make an error so large that the teacher cannot let it stand. *Do you really think this is what's happening?*

Now to the verb.

Arabic has many words for the act of thinking. *Ẓanna* — to suppose, to assume. *Ḥasiba* is different. It comes from the root **ḥ-s-b**, which is the same root that gives us *ḥisāb* — accounting, reckoning, the calculation done at the end of a transaction. From the same family come the words for the Day of Reckoning, *yawm al-ḥisāb*. From the same family comes *ḥasīb* — sufficient, accounting-able.

When you *ḥasiba* something, you are not just guessing. You are adding it up. You are doing the math.

Notice what this implies. The people described here are not stupid. They are not careless. They have looked at their lives, taken inventory, and arrived at a sum. The wealth, plus the sons, plus the standing, plus the ease — all of it adds up, in their internal ledger, to one conclusion: *we are favored.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment. The Quran is not addressing people who have abandoned reason. It is addressing people who have used their reason — and used it incorrectly. They have reasoned their way into delusion.

This is what makes the diagnosis so devastating. Most of us imagine that *ghaflah* — heedlessness — looks like sleep. Like distraction. Like not thinking. The Quran is suggesting something more unsettling: heedlessness can wear the costume of careful thought. You can be doing the math, and the math can still be wrong, because you have left out a variable.

What variable?

That is what the rest of the ayah is going to reveal.

But notice already what the verb has done. By choosing *yaḥsabūna* over *yaẓunnūna*, Allah is dignifying the error before correcting it. He is not saying *they fantasize.* He is saying *they calculate.* And then He is going to show that the calculation was done with a missing column.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: The Verb That Should Stop You Cold

Now we come to what I think is the most stunning word in these two ayahs. The word that, once you see what it is doing, you cannot un-see.

*Nusāriʿu lahum fī al-khayrāt.*

"We are racing to them with good things."

The verb is *nusāriʿu*, from the root **s-r-ʿ**, which means speed, swiftness. But it is not the simple form of the verb. It is Form III — *sāraʿa* — and Form III in Arabic carries a very specific meaning. It is the form of mutual or intensive engagement. *Qātala* (Form III, from the same root as fight) doesn't mean to fight; it means to fight *with* someone, to engage in combat. *Sābaqa* doesn't mean to race; it means to race *against* someone. Form III implies a counterpart. A partner in the action. A competitor.

One early commentator, Mujahid, glossed *nusāriʿu lahum* as "we increase for them, we extend their rope" — reading the apparent swiftness not as God rushing in approval but as the steady, uninterrupted continuation of provision. The gifts keep arriving quickly, but that quickness is not the speed of a Lord hastening to please. It is the speed of rope running through an open hand.

So *sāraʿa* doesn't just mean "to hurry." It means **to race** — and racing implies someone you are racing *with* or *against*. It implies urgency tied to competition.

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again with that knowledge. Allah is asking, with what I can only describe as a kind of holy incredulity: *Do they think We are racing to them with good things?*

Racing against whom? Against time? Against another god? Against their own desires, like a panicked servant trying to please a master? The form of the verb makes the assumption sound absurd the moment it is named.

This is the diagnostic genius of the verse. The believer hears *nusāriʿu* and feels, immediately, how strange it would be for the Lord of the heavens and the earth to be in a hurry to deliver to anyone. He has eternity. He has all power. He doesn't *race.* He doesn't compete. There is no one keeping score against Him. The very idea is misshapen.

But the rejector — the one in the comfortable chair, the one with the full hands — he assumes exactly this. He assumes that the world's responsiveness to him is God hurrying. *The dunya is hastening to me. Doors are opening to me. Numbers are climbing for me.* He reads speed as favor. Quickness as approval.

And Allah's question lands like a slap delivered in slow motion: *do you really think that is what is happening?*

[PAUSE]

There is something else hidden in *nusāriʿu lahum fī al-khayrāt* that the listener should notice. The phrase *fī al-khayrāt* — "in good things" — is the same phrase Allah uses for the *believers* elsewhere. *Fa-stabiqū al-khayrāt* — "race one another to good things" (2:148). *Sāriʿū ilā maghfiratin min rabbikum* — "race to forgiveness from your Lord" (3:133).

The believers are commanded to race. The rejectors imagine that *Allah* is racing — to them.

Classical commentators noted that *khayrāt* here refers specifically to the goods of the Hereafter — not the worldly goods, which have already been named as wealth and children. Read this way, the delusion reaches an eschatological depth: they are not merely imagining that God hastens to send worldly provision. They are imagining that He hastens to deliver them the standing and rewards He reserves for His righteous servants. They have read their Tuesday as a preview of their final accounting. And this is why the misreading is so difficult to survive: it is not a mistake about this life alone. It is a mistake about the next one too.

Do you see what has been inverted?

The believer races *toward* good. The deluded heart imagines that good is racing *toward* it. The first posture is a posture of seeking; the second is a posture of being sought. The first knows itself as a servant who must run to the table. The second has come to believe that the table runs to him.

This is not a small inversion. This is the entire spiritual error of the verse, distilled into the direction of one verb.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Word That Names a Sensory Failure

Then comes the closing.

*Bal lā yashʿurūn.* "Nay, but they do not perceive."

Two small features I want to draw your attention to.

First, *bal*. This particle is one of the great hinges of Arabic. It does not simply mean "but." It is the particle of *iḍrāb* — overturning. It says: *no, set aside what came before; the truth is the opposite.* When you hear *bal*, you are hearing the speaker reach across the table and gently turn the chessboard. Everything you just understood is now reframed.

So the structure is: *Do they reckon We are racing to them? — No. Set that aside. The truth is...*

And what is the truth?

*Lā yashʿurūn.* "They do not perceive."

The verb *yashʿurūn* comes from the root **sh-ʿ-r**. The same root that gives us *shaʿr* — hair. *Shiʿr* — poetry, the kind of speech that moves through the senses and the heart. The verb *shaʿara* in its core meaning is sensory perception — the way a hair on the skin registers a brush of air. It is the most subtle, immediate kind of knowing. You don't have to think to *shaʿara*. You just feel.

[PAUSE]

This is what makes the closing so devastating.

Allah does not say *they do not know.* He does not say *they do not understand.* He says *they do not feel.*

Their failure is not intellectual. It is sensory. The truth is so close to them — closer than the hair on their arm — and they cannot register it. The signal is being broadcast at a frequency their hearts have stopped picking up. They are so deep in the comfort that they have lost the capacity to feel its weight as test.

Notice the cruelty — and the mercy — of this particular description. It is cruel because it locates the failure in a place the person cannot reach by argument. You cannot reason your way back into a sense that has gone numb. And it is merciful because it explains *how* a person can sit inside a clear test and not feel it. Their senses are dulled. They are not lying about not seeing the test. They genuinely don't see it. The numbness is real.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider — beyond what the grammar strictly says — is something the classical scholars wrote about under the name *istidrāj.* That when Allah wills to bring someone gradually to ruin, the first thing that goes is not the wealth or the children or the standing. The first thing that goes is *yashʿurūn.* The capacity to feel that something is off. The hair-trigger of conscience that used to fire when one stepped over a line. The small unease that used to follow a missed prayer. That goes first. And after it goes, everything else can keep flowing — the deals can keep closing, the bodies can keep cooperating, the seats can keep being offered — and the heart will register all of it as confirmation.

The deepest failure in these two ayahs is not theological. It is perceptual. **A heart that has stopped sensing what is actually happening to it.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

What have these three words shown us?

A wrong calculation. *Yaḥsabūna* — they have done the math, but a column is missing.

A wrong direction. *Nusāriʿu* — they have imagined God racing toward them, when the believer's posture is to race toward Him.

A failed sense. *Lā yashʿurūn* — they cannot feel any of this, because the feeling-organ has gone numb.

These are three failures, but they are one failure seen from three angles. The intellect adds wrongly because the heart cannot feel what it is missing. The heart cannot feel because the soul has been asleep too long inside its own comfort.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Rope of Blessing

I want to begin not in the Quran but in a phenomenon you have already seen.

There is a particular kind of person — you have met them — who experiences a long, uninterrupted run of good fortune, and somewhere in the middle of that run, they change. Not dramatically. Not into a villain. But subtly. They become a little more sure of themselves. A little less patient with people who haven't received what they have. A little quicker to advise others on what those others should be doing differently. The success is being reread, in real time, as virtue.

You have seen this in CEOs whose autobiographies imply that their methods are universal laws. You have seen it in influencers whose advice carries the unspoken footnote: *and look at me, the system worked.* You have seen it, perhaps, in a parent whose well-behaved child becomes, in their hands, evidence of their parenting genius — until the second child arrives with a different temperament and the theory cracks.

What is happening in all of these cases is a small, invisible inversion. The person has begun to read their outcomes as evidence of cosmic alignment with their choices. The world is responding to me, therefore I am right.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again.

*A-yaḥsabūna annamā numidduhum bihī min mālin wa banīn — nusāriʿu lahum fī al-khayrāt? Bal lā yashʿurūn.*

The Quran is naming the spiritual version of this exact phenomenon. It is saying: this is not a personality quirk. This is not a sociological pattern. This is one of the central traps Allah lays for the soul that has refused His call. He keeps the goods coming. He extends the wealth. He sends the children. He opens the doors. And the heart, doing its quiet calculation, reads all of it as approval.

The classical tradition has a name for this. *Istidrāj* — from the root that means "step by step," "stage by stage."

Classical commentators paired *istidrāj* with a companion concept: *imlāʾ* — giving rope, deliberate prolongation. The two describe the same process from different angles. *Istidrāj* names the direction: step by step, upward, toward an edge the climber cannot see. *Imlāʾ* names the mechanism: the rope simply keeps coming, uninterrupted, with no signal that it will stop. Al-Tabari reads the verb *numidduhum* — We extend to them — as precisely this double operation, the giving of rope and the leading step by step working together. And the classical commentators identified the specific shape of the inner misreading that makes all of it possible: it is not merely "I am favored." It is "I am receiving what I have *earned* and *deserve*." The provision has been silently re-categorized from gift into wages — payment owed to a person of standing in God's sight. Once grace has been converted into merit in the heart's accounting, the misreading becomes almost impossible to dislodge from the inside. The person is no longer receiving. They are collecting.

Allah says elsewhere, in Surah Al-Aʿraf 7:182: *sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn* — "We will lure them step by step from where they do not know." And in Surah Al-Qalam 68:44: *sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn wa umlī lahum — inna kaydī matīn.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with the image. *Istidrāj* is climbing a staircase. The person experiencing it does not feel that they are being lured. They feel that they are ascending. Every step they take seems to lead to a higher view. The wealth grows. The children prosper. The deals close. The body cooperates. Every step is solid under their foot.

What they cannot see — because *bal lā yashʿurūn* — is that the staircase is not leading where they think. They are not climbing toward heaven. They are climbing toward the edge of a cliff that they will only see at the very last step. And by then, they will be moving too fast to stop.

This is the rope of blessing. Allah does not always punish the rejector with sudden ruin. Sometimes He punishes him by *not* punishing him. By extending. By provisioning. By letting the prosperity build until it has become the very thing that prevents the heart from turning back.

[PAUSE]

Here is the most disturbing part of this teaching. *The rope feels like favor on the way up.*

The man whose business is succeeding does not feel, on Tuesday morning, that he is being lured. He feels that he is winning. The woman whose home is full does not feel that she is being tested. She feels that she is blessed. *And both of them might be right* — wealth and family are real blessings, mentioned by Allah in many places as gifts. But they might also be wrong. Whether the gift is favor or rope depends on something the gift itself cannot tell you.

It depends on what is happening inside.

Is the gift drawing me closer to the Giver, or further from Him? Is my prayer sweeter than it was a year ago, or thinner? Am I more frightened of displeasing Him, or more relaxed? Am I more generous with what I've been given, or more protective? Am I sensing, somewhere in my chest, the weight of the *amānah* that comes with each blessing — or has that sense gone quiet?

The blessing alone is silent on which kind of blessing it is. Only the heart that receives it can answer. And only an awake heart can answer truthfully.

[PAUSE — major]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes the question I ask when something good happens.

The instinctive question — the one our ego trains us to ask — is *what does this mean about me?* The man gets the promotion and asks: *what does this say about my abilities, my future, my standing?*

The Quranic question is different. The Quranic question is: *what does this require of me?* Not *what does this prove* but *what does this demand?* Not *am I rising* but *am I responding?*

The wealth that came is a question Allah is asking. The child that was born is a question Allah is asking. The opportunity that opened is a question Allah is asking. And the question, in every case, is the same: *now that I have entrusted this to you — what will you do with it that proves your heart is awake?*

If I cannot answer that question, the blessing has begun to do its other work on me. The rope is tightening. And I will not feel it.

Until I do.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Architecture of Numb Comfort

I want to spend the rest of our time on a single word from the closing of the ayah, because I think it diagnoses our era with a precision that should leave us shaking.

*Lā yashʿurūn.* They do not feel.

Modern life — the life that most of us are living, whether we are wealthy or middle-income, whether we are religious or secular — is structured to produce exactly this state. Numbness inside abundance.

Think about what an average week contains. The phone is always charged. The food is always available. The temperature in the room is always controlled. The entertainment is endless. The information is infinite. The notifications are constant. Every appetite that arises is quickly fed. Hunger is brief. Boredom is briefer. Silence is rare. Discomfort is exceptional.

[PAUSE]

What happens to a heart that lives in this environment for long enough?

The hair-trigger of conscience that the Quran calls *yashʿurūn* — the fine sense that registers when something is off — it gets dulled by the constant stimulation. The way a person who eats sweetened food at every meal can no longer taste the natural sweetness in fruit. The way someone who listens to loud music for hours stops being able to hear the subtle textures of an unaccompanied voice. The receptors saturate. They stop firing.

And then the test arrives — and the heart cannot register it as test. It registers as a normal Tuesday. A normal scroll. A normal purchase. A normal evening. The signal is being broadcast at full strength, but the receiver has learned to filter that frequency out.

This is what the Quran is naming. Not the loud sin. Not the dramatic departure. The quieter, more terrifying condition: *the inability to feel that anything is wrong.*

[PAUSE]

The companions, may Allah be pleased with them, were terrified of this state. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb is reported to have wept when victories came. When the keys of empires landed in his hands, he asked: *am I being given dominion, or am I being lured?* He had read these ayahs. He knew that the goods coming did not, by themselves, settle the question. He had to look inside. And he had to *feel.*

Imagine, for a moment, what it means that the second Caliph of Islam, with the wealth of two empires flowing through Madinah, was sitting in his patched cloak asking himself whether the inflow was favor or rope. What did he know that we have forgotten?

He knew that the heart's main job, the one job that no tool can do for it, is **to feel correctly what is happening to it.** And he knew that this feeling is the first thing comfort kills.

[PAUSE]

Let me ask you something, and please do not answer quickly.

When was the last time you felt unsettled by a blessing?

Not unsettled by a hardship — that is normal. Not unsettled by a fear — that is normal. But genuinely unsettled by something good arriving in your life. The promotion that made you ask, *what is being asked of me now?* The income that landed in your account and made you say, *Allah, what is this for?* The smooth week that made you whisper, *Allah, do not let my ease become my distance from You.*

If you cannot remember the last time, that is a data point. Not a verdict. Not a sentence. A data point. It means the receiver is dulling. It means the instinctive response to good things has shifted from *what does this require of me* to *finally, this is mine.* And the shift is exactly what the ayah is naming.

[PAUSE]

The practical work is small but precise.

When something good arrives — a piece of news, a gain, a relief — pause before celebrating. Not for long. Five seconds. And in those five seconds, ask one question: **am I closer to Allah right now than I was an hour ago, or am I about to be further?**

If the gift is bringing you closer — your first instinct is gratitude, your second instinct is to give some of it away, your third instinct is to ask what He wants from you with it — then the blessing is functioning as blessing. *Numidduhum* — extending — is acting as the believer's extension is meant to act. As fuel for closeness.

If the gift is putting distance — your first instinct is self-congratulation, your second instinct is to protect it, your third instinct is to start feeling like you have arrived — then the blessing is functioning as something else. The rope is in your hand. And the only sane response is to drop it.

This is not about refusing blessings. Allah does not ask us to refuse what He sends. He asks us to receive it without losing the capacity to feel what it actually is.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now look back at the two ayahs and see what has been built.

> *A-yaḥsabūna annamā numidduhum bihī min mālin wa banīn — nusāriʿu lahum fī al-khayrāt — bal lā yashʿurūn.*

There is a mirror inside this passage. Look at the verbs.

*Numidduhum* — We extend. The first verb of God's action.
*Nusāriʿu* — We race. The second verb of God's action — but as they imagine it.

These two verbs sit on either side of the misunderstanding. *What Allah is actually doing* (extending — slowly, deliberately, for a purpose only He knows) is being misread as *what they imagine He is doing* (racing — urgently, eagerly, in approval of them).

The error is the slippage between these two verbs.

And then *bal lā yashʿurūn* closes the architecture. Because the only way to live inside that slippage — the only way to mistake extending for racing, the only way to read provision as endorsement — is for the sense organ that should detect the slippage to have gone silent.

The whole spiritual diagnosis fits into one sentence: **they have mistaken Allah's patience for Allah's pleasure, because they have lost the capacity to feel the difference.**

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions.

The verbs taught us *what* the misreading is — a calculation that confuses extending with racing. The themes taught us *why* the misreading happens — comfort dulls the perceptive faculty until the test cannot be felt as test.

And both converge on the question we are now standing in front of: **what is happening inside me, right now, in response to what Allah is sending?**

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

I'll leave you with five. Sit with them. Let them work on you.

1. What blessing in my life right now have I started reading as evidence that I am favored — when I should be reading it as a question Allah is asking me?

2. When something good arrives, what is my first instinct? Self-congratulation, or the trembling question *what is this for?*

3. Where in my life have my senses dulled to the point that something is clearly off and I cannot feel that it is off?

4. If Allah were to remove one of the comforts I have come to assume — not the wealth, not the children, just one of the small daily comforts — would my prayer survive it? Would my certainty survive it? Or have I built my faith on top of the very things that were meant to be tests?

5. Am I racing toward Allah — *fa-stabiqū al-khayrāt* — or have I quietly started imagining that He is racing toward me?

[PAUSE]

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah does not always punish the heedless heart by withholding — sometimes He tests it by extending, and the only protection is a heart awake enough to feel the difference between favor and rope.

[PAUSE]

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow morning, when you wake to whatever your life currently is — the apartment, the obligations, the bank balance, the people who are or are not in your home — try one thing. Before you reach for the phone, before you measure the day by what is coming, ask:

*Allah, what You have already given me — is it bringing me closer to You, or is it slowly carrying me away?*

And then sit with the answer for ten seconds before you move.

That ten seconds is the practice of *yashʿurūn.* It is the work of keeping the receiver clean. It is the small, daily refusal to be lulled by abundance into a calculation that adds up wrong.

This is not paranoia. This is not refusing the gifts. It is honoring them by asking them what they want from you. It is staying awake inside your own life.

[PAUSE]

### Du'a

> O Allah, do not let what You extend to me become the very thing that distances me from You. Keep alive in my chest the small, sensitive instrument that knows the difference between Your favor and Your rope. And when comfort arrives, let me feel — *truly feel* — what it is asking of me, before I forget to ask.
>
