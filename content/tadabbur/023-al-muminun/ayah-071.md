# If Truth Followed Their Desires

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayah 71

> وَلَوِ ٱتَّبَعَ ٱلْحَقُّ أَهْوَآءَهُمْ لَفَسَدَتِ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتُ وَٱلْأَرْضُ وَمَن فِيهِنَّ ۚ بَلْ أَتَيْنَـٰهُم بِذِكْرِهِمْ فَهُمْ عَن ذِكْرِهِم مُّعْرِضُونَ

> *Wa law-ittabaʿa-l-ḥaqqu ahwāʾahum la-fasadati-s-samāwātu wa-l-arḍu wa-man fīhinn. Bal ataynāhum bi-dhikrihim fa-hum ʿan dhikrihim muʿriḍūn.*

> **And if the truth had followed their desires, the heavens and the earth and whoever is in them would have collapsed into ruin. Rather, We have brought them their reminder — yet they, from their own reminder, are turning their shoulder away.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, you have to stand inside a very specific argument.

In the late Makkan years, the Quraysh leadership was not refusing the Quran on philosophical grounds. They had a more practical objection. The Quran, as it stood, did not flatter them. It did not preserve their hierarchy. It did not honor their forefathers. It did not arrive through the right courier — through one of the chiefs, through one of the wealthy ones, through someone whose résumé matched the magnitude of the message.

So they made demands. *Bring a different Quran. Or change this one.* (10:15) *Why was this Quran not sent down to a great man from the two cities?* (43:31) *We will never believe until we are given the like of what was given to the messengers of Allah.* (6:124)

They were not rejecting truth in the abstract. They were lobbying for an edit. They wanted truth to keep its title but soften its content. They wanted revelation, but on their terms.

And surrounding them, the entire late Makkan passage of Surah Al-Mu'minun has been answering this lobbying campaign. *Have they not pondered the speech?* (23:68). *Or do they not recognize their messenger, so they reject him?* (23:69). *Or do they say he is possessed by a jinn?* (23:70).

Then, [PAUSE], comes 23:71. And Allah does not answer them on the level of negotiation. He answers them on the level of cosmology.

### The Hook

Imagine, just for a moment, that your wish came true. Not a small wish. The biggest one. The wish that reality itself would adjust to your sense of what is right.

What if, every time you preferred a thing, the universe rearranged itself to make that thing true?

You may think this would be paradise. Allah, in this ayah, will tell you something else. He will tell you that this is not paradise. **This is the end of the world.**

And then He will say something stranger still — that the very thing you are turning your shoulder from is not just a reminder of Him.

It is your own honor.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to enter this ayah through three doors: the *if*, the word for *desire*, and the word for *reminder*. Each one will, on its own, change something in how you read the verse. Together, they will redraw your map of how truth and the human heart actually fit.

### Element One: The "If" That Could Not Happen — لَوْ

Before we touch the ayah, sit with a small grammatical fact about Arabic.

Arabic has more than one word for "if." There is **إِنْ** (*in*), and there is **لَوْ** (*law*). They look like cousins in English translation — both come out as "if" — but they are doing very different work.

**إِنْ** is the *if* of open possibility. *If it rains tomorrow, take the umbrella.* It might rain; it might not. The conditional is live.

**لَوْ** is the *if* of impossibility. It is the *if* a parent uses with a child after the fact: *If you had told me you were going there, I would have come with you.* But you didn't tell me. So I didn't come. The condition did not happen, the result did not happen, and we both know it.

Grammarians call this the **counterfactual conditional**. It describes a world that does not exist. Its only purpose is to illuminate the world that *does* exist by contrasting it with the world that doesn't.

So when you encounter لَوْ in the Quran, you are being asked to do a particular kind of mental work. You are being invited to imagine a world that Allah is about to dismantle.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the ayah:

> *Wa **law** ittabaʿa-l-ḥaqqu ahwāʾahum...*
> *And **if** the truth had followed their desires...*

This لَوْ is not asking, "Could truth follow their desires?" It is announcing, before the sentence is even finished, **that this scenario is impossible**. It is the *if* of "this did not happen, this could not happen, but watch what would unfold if it did."

And notice something. The natural expectation, the moral expectation, the religious expectation, is the reverse of this sentence. We expect to be told: *if their desires followed truth, all would be well.* That is how the world gets fixed in our usual moral imagination — humans align themselves to truth.

Allah inverts the sentence. He puts truth in the position of the follower. He puts human desire in the position of the leader. **He is letting us watch what happens when the load-bearing thing in the universe asks permission from the thing it is supposed to be carrying.**

Stay with that for a moment. The ayah is structurally upside-down on purpose. It is a thought experiment in which the foundation tries to obey the building it was meant to hold up.

What happens? *La-fasadati-s-samāwātu wa-l-arḍu wa-man fīhinn.* The heavens and the earth and everyone in them would collapse into ruin.

Not just "things would be bad." Not "morality would suffer." **The cosmos would dissolve.** Because the cosmos is held up *by* truth, not despite it. Truth is not a feature added on top of reality. Truth is what makes reality continue to exist from one second to the next.

What this single particle لَوْ has done is convert a political dispute into a metaphysical one. The Quraysh thought they were negotiating wording. Allah is showing them: **what you are asking for is not an edit. It is the end of everything.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word *Hawā* — Why Allah Calls Desire a Falling

The word for "desires" in this ayah is **أَهْوَاء** (*ahwāʾ*), the plural of **هَوَى** (*hawā*). And before we say what it means, we have to say what its root literally does.

The root **ه-و-ي** (*h-w-y*) is the root of *falling*.

When a stone drops from a height, the verb is *hawā*. When a star falls, the verb is *hawā*. Surah An-Najm opens with it: *wa-n-najmi idhā **hawā*** — "by the star when it falls." When Allah describes the fate of those who reject Him, He says their dwelling is *Hāwiyah* — the bottomless pit, named from this same root, the pit whose defining feature is that you keep falling.

Now hold that root meaning in your mind, and look at what it does to the moral vocabulary.

In Arabic, when you want to say "he desires" or "he is inclined toward," you can use the verb **هَوِيَ** (*hawiya*) — the same root. To desire something, in this language, is **to fall toward it**.

The classical lexicographers — Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī among them — note this is not accidental. The Quran uses *hawā* for a very specific category of human wanting: the wanting that pulls you *downward*. Not all wanting. Not the wanting of food when hungry, nor the wanting of knowledge, nor the longing for Allah. *Hawā* names the gravitational pull of the lower self — the appetite that, if obeyed, takes you in only one direction, which is *down*.

So when this ayah says *if the truth had followed their **ahwāʾ***, it is not saying "if the truth had followed their preferences." It is saying:

**"If the truth had followed the things that pull them downward."**

[PAUSE]

This is what makes the counterfactual collapse so total. Truth, by its nature, holds things up. *Hawā*, by its nature, pulls things down. If the upholding thing starts following the downward-pulling thing, then there is nothing left holding the universe at the height it is meant to occupy.

And there is one more grammatical detail you should not miss. *Ahwāʾahum* is plural. Their **falling-things**. Plural. Whose falling? Mine? Yours? His? Hers? Each of them has their own. And they contradict.

The Quraysh chiefs themselves did not agree. Abu Jahl wanted one thing; Abu Sufyan wanted another; Walid ibn al-Mughira wanted a third. Their *ahwāʾ* did not even agree with each other's *ahwāʾ*. So the ayah is not just asking, "what if truth followed desire?" It is asking, "what if truth followed *all the contradictory desires of all the people who have ever lived*, all at once, all pulling in different downward directions?"

The image is no longer a building leaning on a faulty foundation. The image is a building asked to lean on a thousand faulty foundations, each pulling it in a different direction, all of them pulling it down.

Of course the heavens and the earth would collapse. There is no other possible result. **Truth cannot be the servant of contradictory descents and remain truth.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Word That Means Both *Reminder* and *Honor* — ذِكْر

Now the ayah pivots. *Bal ataynāhum bi-dhikrihim fa-hum ʿan dhikrihim muʿriḍūn.*

That word **بَلْ** (*bal*) — "rather" — is doing something specific. It is a particle of correction. It cancels the previous frame and replaces it with a sharper one. The ayah has just said, "if truth followed your desires, everything would collapse." Then it says: **No. Forget that scenario. Look at what We actually did.**

What did Allah actually do? *Ataynāhum bi-dhikrihim.* "We brought them with their *dhikr*." We came to them, carrying their *dhikr*.

Now, the word **ذِكْر** (*dhikr*).

Most translations render *dhikr* as "reminder." That is correct, and it is also incomplete.

The root **ذ-ك-ر** (*dh-k-r*) covers a constellation of meanings in classical Arabic:
- **To remember**, to bring to mind
- **To mention**, to speak of
- **To remind**, to call attention to
- **Honor, eminence, mention-worthiness, reputation**

This last meaning is critical and is often missed in translation. In classical Arabic poetry and in the Quran itself, *dhikr* is what someone is *known for*, what is *said about* them, the **honor that attaches to their name**. When the Quran says of Ibrahim *wa-tarakna ʿalayhi fī-l-ākhirīn* — "and We left upon him among later generations" — it then explains: *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm* — peace upon Ibrahim. His *dhikr* is preserved. His mention. His honor.

The same root gives us *dhakar* — the male — because in tribal Arabia the male was the one whose name was carried forward. And it gives us the verb *dhakara* — to mention, to remember, to praise.

So when Allah says **ataynāhum bi-dhikrihim** — "We brought them with their *dhikr*" — He is not just saying, "We brought them a reminder."

He is also saying:

**"We brought them their honor."**
**"We brought them what they will be remembered for."**
**"We brought them the thing that, if accepted, would attach itself to their names forever."**

The Quran is — for the people who receive it — their reminder *and* their reputation. Their wake-up call *and* their crown. The classical mufassirūn (al-Ṭabarī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr) all preserve this dual reading: *dhikr* here means both the message that reminds them of Allah *and* the honor that elevates them in this world and the next. The composite picture is one reality seen from two angles — the Book is simultaneously corrective and dignifying.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the closing of the ayah with that meaning loaded into the word:

*fa-hum **ʿan dhikrihim** muʿriḍūn.*
"so they, **from their own dhikr**, are turning away."

Read once: they are turning from their reminder.
Read again: they are turning from their honor.
Read once more: they are turning from the very thing that would have caused their names to be remembered.

The Quraysh wanted *dhikr* in the worldly sense. They wanted to be remembered. They wanted reputation. They wanted their lineage preserved. They schemed and warred and traveled and traded for *dhikr*.

And Allah is saying: **We brought it to your door. Not the cheap kind. The real kind. The kind that lasts. And you turned your shoulder from it.**

That word *muʿriḍūn* deserves its own moment. It comes from the root **ع-ر-ض** (*ʿ-r-ḍ*), which means "the side." A *muʿriḍ* is not someone who shouts *no* and walks away. A *muʿriḍ* is someone who **turns their side to you**. They present their shoulder rather than their face. They do not engage; they orient themselves *away* while remaining in the room.

This is a colder rejection than direct refusal. It is the rejection of avoidance. The rejection of the slight pivot. The rejection that does not even bother to argue back.

And what they are pivoting away from, the ayah has just told us twice — *ʿan dhikrihim* — *from their own dhikr*. The thing that was theirs. The thing that came addressed to them. The thing whose acceptance would have made them mentioned forever in the registers of the heavens.

They turn their shoulder to their own crown.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Three doors. The counterfactual *if* told us that truth cannot bend to desire without the cosmos coming down. The word *hawā* told us why — because human desire is, by its very root, a falling. And the word *dhikr* told us what we are losing when we turn — not just guidance, but our own honor.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Every World Built on "My Truth" Has Already Begun to Collapse

There is a phrase that has become almost untouchable in our cultural moment. *My truth.* As in: *I'm just speaking my truth.* As in: *That may be your truth, but it's not mine.*

I want to be careful here. There is a real and good thing this phrase is reaching for — the dignity of a person's experience, the right to name what happened to them, the refusal to be silenced. Those are real. Those are not what this ayah is addressing.

But there is also a second thing the phrase has come to mean. It has come to mean: **truth is downstream of preference**. What is true is what feels right to me. What is real is what I choose to see. The world's job is to validate what I have already decided.

This is the world the ayah is dismantling.

Notice how the ayah does not engage on the moral level. It does not say, "if truth followed their desires, *they* would be ruined." It does not even say, "*society* would be ruined." It says **the heavens and the earth**. The whole architecture. Everything that holds.

Why? Because Allah is making a point about how reality is structured. **Reality is held up by truth.** Not by your truth. Not by my truth. By *the* truth — the load-bearing pillar that does not move when we lean on it. If that pillar starts following the leaner, the building does not stand.

Here is the test you can run on your own life. Pick something in your life that is currently painful. A conflict. A debt. A pattern you keep repeating. Now ask yourself: **am I describing this situation as it is, or as I wish it were?** Am I aligning my map to the territory, or am I asking the territory to redraw itself to match my map?

We do this constantly. We do it in our marriages, where we narrate the story so that our spouse is the problem. We do it in our work, where we explain why the feedback was unfair rather than examining whether it was accurate. We do it in our worship, where we choose the rulings that flatter the life we already wanted to live.

[PAUSE]

Each of these is a small private experiment in the cosmology this ayah is rejecting. Each of these is a tiny *if the truth had followed my desires.*

And here is what the ayah is whispering to us: **the reason these arrangements never bring peace is the same reason the cosmos itself would not survive them.** Truth does not stop being truth because you preferred otherwise. The bill you did not want to pay does not unmake itself. The conversation you did not want to have does not become unnecessary. The feedback you dismissed does not become incorrect. The reality you turned your shoulder to is still *there*, exerting its pull.

You can build a small world inside your skull where truth follows your *hawā*. But you cannot live in it for long. Because reality will keep arriving from outside. Bills come due. Bodies age. Children grow up and tell you what they actually saw. The marriage either becomes real or ends. The community either confronts what it papered over or fractures.

The ayah is not threatening you. It is telling you the merciful truth: **you cannot win the argument with reality, and the merciful thing is to stop trying.** The collapse the ayah describes is not Allah's punishment. It is just what happens when you ask a foundation to follow what it was meant to support.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

Here is the practical application. **Find one thing in your life right now that you suspect is true and have been refusing to face.** One. Not a list. Not a project. One thing.

Maybe it is that you owe someone an apology. Maybe it is that the work you are doing is not the work you are meant to do. Maybe it is that the friendship has become harmful. Maybe it is that you have been performing a religious life without inhabiting it. Maybe it is that someone in your family is struggling and you have been pretending not to see.

Whatever it is — name it. Out loud, if you can. Without yet committing to any action. Just **stop asking truth to follow your *hawā* on this one matter**. Stop rotating reality to make it bearable. Let it be what it is, in front of you, for sixty seconds.

You will discover something unexpected. It is easier to face than to run from. Running from a truth costs more energy than facing it ever does. The collapse the ayah describes happens slowly, quietly, expensively, in the small interior worlds where truth has been told to take a back seat. Facing the truth is not the disaster. The avoidance was the disaster. **The face-to-face is the relief.**

### Theme Two: You Have Been Turning Your Shoulder From Your Own Crown

Now the second movement. This one is harder, in a different way. The first theme is about cosmic architecture. This one is about something more intimate.

Almost every human being is, on some level, hungry for *dhikr*. We want to matter. We want our name to be mentioned with respect. We want to be remembered after we die. This is not vanity, or not only vanity. It is one of the deepest human longings — the longing not to be erased.

Cultures organize themselves around this hunger. The Romans built monuments. The Pharaohs built tombs. The Quraysh of Makkah genealogized themselves obsessively, memorizing fourteen generations of lineage so the names would not be lost. Today we build LinkedIn profiles, monetized followings, brand identities. The medium changes. The hunger does not.

We are constantly trying to manufacture *dhikr* for ourselves.

[PAUSE]

And here is the strange tenderness of this ayah. Allah is saying to a people who are *desperate* for *dhikr*: **I have brought it to you.** Not the brittle kind. Not the kind that depends on stone and metal. Not the kind that vanishes when the empire falls. The kind that is recorded in the registers above the seven heavens. The kind that survives the ending of the world.

*Ataynāhum bi-dhikrihim* — We came to them, carrying their honor.

And what did they do? *Fa-hum ʿan dhikrihim muʿriḍūn.* They turned their shoulder.

Read this carefully. They did not turn their shoulder to a stranger. They did not turn their shoulder to a foreign demand. **They turned their shoulder to their own crown.** The thing that was *theirs.* The thing that had their name on it. The thing that, had they accepted it, would have written them into the longest book in existence.

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself something uncomfortable.

How much of what you suspect is true about your relationship with the Quran is, in fact, *iʿrāḍ* — the slight pivot, the shoulder turned, the avoidance that does not even rise to the level of refusal?

You do not deny the Book. Of course not. You believe in it. You honor it on the shelf. You kiss it before you place it down. You have memorized portions. You have feelings about it.

But when was the last time you *sat with it*? When was the last time you read an ayah twice slowly and let it interrogate you? When was the last time you ran to the Quran when something hurt, the way you run to your phone? When was the last time you let it actually shape what you decided that week, rather than confirming what you had already decided?

This is what *iʿrāḍ* looks like in our generation. Not denial. **The slight pivot.** The Book is in the room, and we are presenting our shoulder to it. We are facing the screen.

And the ayah is saying — quietly, without scolding, almost mournfully — *that thing you are turning your shoulder from is your own honor.*

You are not just losing guidance when you avoid the Quran. **You are forfeiting your name in the registers that matter.** Because the people who carry the *dhikr* of Allah are the ones whose *dhikr* He carries. *Fadhkurūnī adhkurkum* — *remember Me, I will remember you* (2:152). Mention Me, and I will mention you. Carry My *dhikr*, and I will carry yours.

The Quraysh wanted to be remembered. They built their reputations through generosity at the Kaʿbah, through caravan diplomacy, through pre-Islamic poetry contests, through tribal feuds that lasted forty years. They wanted *dhikr*. They worked for it.

And history did remember them — but only because the Quran they rejected immortalized them as the people who rejected it. Their names survive because the Book they pivoted away from preserved their pivot. Allah's *dhikr* of them carried their *dhikr* forward, but inverted — not as the bearers of the message, but as its first refusers.

Imagine the alternative. Imagine if the Makkan elite had received the Quran. Imagine *Abū Lahab* renamed *Abū Nūr*, recorded in the Book not for his hands but for his hands rebuilt. Imagine. The honor was on offer. They turned their shoulder.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

Here is the practical application. **Open the Quran tonight and read one ayah — any ayah — and let it look at you for sixty seconds before you close the book.** Do not rush to apply it. Do not Google a tafsir. Do not even understand it perfectly. Just **face it.** Reverse the pivot. Turn your shoulder back. Stop being a *muʿriḍ*, even for one minute, with one ayah.

Then — and this is the part most of us skip — ask yourself: *what would this ayah ask me to change if I let it?* Not what it generally asks of humanity. What it asks of *me*, today, in my specific life, with my specific resistances. Sit with the question. Let it be uncomfortable.

You are not doing this for productivity. You are not doing this for self-improvement. You are doing this because **the Book that came to you carried your name on it**, and you have been pretending it was addressed to someone else. Open it. Let it call you by your name.

That is what the reversal of *iʿrāḍ* feels like. It is not dramatic. It is just the small turning of the body back toward what was always facing you. And in that small turn, the *dhikr* the Quraysh forfeited is being offered to you, again, tonight.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Let's see how the pieces fit.

The ayah is a counterfactual followed by a *bal* — an *if-it-had-been* followed by a *no, instead*. The structure is: **the impossible scenario** → **the actual reality** → **the human response to the actual reality**.

Notice the symmetry. In the impossible scenario, *truth follows desire* and *the cosmos collapses*. In the actual reality, *Allah brings them their dhikr* and *they turn from it*. In both halves, there is a movement: in the first, downward (*hawā* — falling — collapse — *fasād*). In the second, sideways (*iʿrāḍ* — the shoulder turn — the slight pivot).

The ayah is mapping two ways the human response to truth can break. **Either you ask truth to follow you, and reality collapses. Or you turn your shoulder to truth, and your honor walks past you.** The first ruins the world. The second ruins you. There is no third option that does not involve facing it.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two angles.

Linguistically, *hawā* is a falling and *muʿriḍ* is a turning of the side. Both are described in terms of *direction*. One goes down. The other goes sideways.

Thematically, "my truth" is the philosophy of the falling, and avoidance is the practice of the sideways turn. One is the cosmology of *hawā*. The other is the daily mechanics of *iʿrāḍ*.

The ayah, then, is mapping the two great refusals — the metaphysical refusal of letting reality be itself, and the existential refusal of letting the Book speak to you. And it is showing you that they are connected. The person who needs reality to follow their preference is also the person who has turned their shoulder to the very thing that could correct their preference.

The cure for both is the same: **face it.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one place in my life where I have been quietly asking truth to follow my *hawā*? What is the cost I have been paying to maintain that arrangement?

2. If *dhikr* means both *reminder* and *honor* — what part of my own honor have I been forfeiting through my avoidance of the Quran?

3. What does it look like, in concrete terms, to turn my shoulder *back*? Not dramatically. Just by ten degrees. What would change in tomorrow's hour?

4. The Quraysh wanted to be remembered. We want to be remembered. What kind of *dhikr* am I currently building my life around — the kind that survives me, or the kind that vanishes when the screen powers down?

5. What is the single ayah I have been avoiding because I suspect it would ask something of me I am not ready to give?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Reality cannot bend to your falling, and the only thing you forfeit by refusing to bend to reality is your own name in the only book that lasts.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to fix everything tonight. The ayah is not asking that. It is asking only one thing: that you *stop the pivot*. Stop the slow sideways rotation. Face the Book again. Face the truth about one situation in your life. Face Allah, who is closer to you than your jugular vein and is currently *holding the heavens up* on your behalf so that you can finish reading this and have somewhere to live tomorrow morning.

The cosmos is being held up by truth right now, in this very moment, while you read. That is His gift. **The least we can do is stop demanding that the same truth follow our preferences.**

### Du'a

*O Allah, You have held the heavens and the earth on the foundation of Your truth, and You have brought us our dhikr — our reminder and our honor — when we did not deserve it. Forgive us the small turns of the shoulder, the daily avoidances, the quiet asking that reality bend to our falling. Reverse the pivot in us. Make us those who face the Book that was sent to call us by name, and write our names in the registers where dhikr does not decay. Āmīn.*
