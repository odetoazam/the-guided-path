# Mu'riḍūn: What the Believer Turns Away From

> [ayah:23:3] وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَنِ ٱللَّغْوِ مُعْرِضُونَ
>
> *Walladhīna hum 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn*
>
> *And those who turn away from al-laghw — empty, idle, purposeless speech.*

---

## Opening — Where We Are

Surah al-Mu'minun opens with a declaration: *qad aflaḥa-l-mu'minūn* — the believers have succeeded. Then, instead of describing their reward first, Allah does something unusual. He stops the camera. He rewinds. And He says: *here is what they look like.* Seven qualities, listed one after the other, each beginning with the same relative pronoun — *alladhīna hum* — *those who, they.*

The first quality named is what they do **inside** their prayer: they are *khāshi'ūn* — humbly present. Their bodies are still, their hearts are gathered, their attention is collected at one point.

And then — immediately — the second quality.

Not their generosity. Not their courage. Not their knowledge. Not even their other forms of worship.

The second quality the Quran names is **what they don't pay attention to.**

> *Walladhīna hum 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn.*

[PAUSE]

Stop and feel the strangeness of this. Out of every possible second-place quality Allah could have chosen — out of every positive virtue the believers might be famous for — He chose to describe an **absence**. He chose to define them by **what they turn their backs on**.

You can almost feel the camera move. In ayah 2, you were standing inside a masjid, watching them in prayer. By ayah 3, the camera has stepped outside, into the marketplace, into the conversations, into the open noise of life — and Allah is telling you: **watch where they don't look. Watch where they don't linger. Watch where they show their backs.**

And the question that has to land on us first, before we go anywhere else, is this:

**Why is the second-most-important thing about a believer something that they don't do?**

Hold that question. Don't answer it yet. We're going to spend the next hour earning the answer.

## Rebuilding the World — Two Worlds, One Word

To feel what this ayah was doing when it landed, we have to inhabit two worlds at once. The world of the original listener. And our own.

### The World That First Heard This

The Meccan society into which this surah descended was a society that worshipped speech. Arabic poetry was the highest cultural form. The marketplaces of Mecca had specific corners — corners with names — where poets would gather and trade verses. Tribal boasts, ridicule of rivals, witty barbs, satirical takedowns, gossip about other clans — this was the air people breathed. To be a person of standing in pre-Islamic Arabia was to be a person who could **command speech**: to mock skillfully, to praise lavishly, to remember every insult and return it ten-fold in verse.

And into this society, where words were the currency of identity, came a Prophet ﷺ saying that **a believer is someone who turns away from a category of speech entirely.**

Not just rejects falsehood. Not just refuses to lie. Allah will name those things elsewhere. Here, He names something quieter, something more pervasive, something that even good people in that society would have considered harmless: **al-laghw.** Speech that doesn't matter. Talk that has no destination. Conversation engineered to fill silence rather than carry meaning.

For the early believers, what made this hard wasn't the prohibition of evil — anyone with a conscience could sense that. What made this hard was that turning away from laghw meant turning away from the **fabric of social life**. The casual mockery. The harmless gossip. The witty exchanges in the market. The endless commentary on who said what to whom. The early believers stood out because in a society that filled its mouth with talk all day, there were now people who chose, deliberately, to be quieter. To not enter that current. To turn the body, physically, away.

### The World That Hears This Today

Now slide forward to your own life.

You are alive in the highest-volume laghw environment that has ever existed in human history. Every screen in your pocket and on your wall is engineered — by armies of engineers — to capture your attention for things that, by your own admission, do not matter. The infinite scroll. The notification. The takes. The takes about the takes. The ten-second clip. The thirty-second clip. The thread arguing about the clip. The reply guy under the thread. The screenshot of the reply guy.

Try this experiment, just for a moment. Look at the last hour of your phone use, in your head — not in shame, just observationally — and ask: **how much of that, by your own internal standard, would you call laghw?**

Not haram. Not even harmful. Just **empty**. Just attention spent on something that did not feed your soul, did not carry weight, did not move you toward anything you actually care about. Time you cannot account for, because there is nothing to account.

That is *al-laghw*. The exact word. Fourteen-hundred years before any of us was born, Allah named the most modern problem we have, and listed turning away from it as the **second quality** of those who succeed.

[PAUSE]

So here is the hook. Here is what we are going to spend the next hour with:

**The believers' second quality — listed before zakah, before chastity, before keeping trusts, before guarding their prayers — is an attention discipline.** And the word Allah uses for *turning away* doesn't mean ignoring it in the mind. It means turning your **body** so that your back is to it. Why does the Quran describe attention as a posture? And why is what we don't look at more important than what we do?

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to go through three elements in this ayah, slowly. Each one is going to ask us to first understand a small piece of how Arabic works, then return to the word, and then sit with what it's actually doing.

The three elements are:

1. **The word *al-laghw*** — the genus that has no edges.
2. **The word *mu'riḍūn*** — a posture, not a thought.
3. **The structural pairing** between *fī ṣalātihim* in ayah 2 and *'ani-l-laghw* in ayah 3 — *in* the prayer and *away from* the empty.

---

## ELEMENT ONE: *al-laghw* — The Genus Without Edges

### A Small Lesson on the Definite Article

Before we open the word, we need a tool. Arabic has a definite article — *al-* — that we usually translate as "the." But in classical Arabic grammar, *al-* doesn't always mean "the specific one we already mentioned." Sometimes it means **the genus itself** — the whole category, in its general nature.

Think of it this way. If I say in English, "the lion is a noble animal," I don't mean *that particular lion over there*. I mean **lion-as-such.** The whole species. English has to use awkward phrasings to do this. Arabic does it just by adding *al-* to a generic noun. Grammarians call this *al lil-jins* — *the* of the genus.

When you see *al-* attached to an abstract noun like *al-ḥaqq* (the truth), *al-ṣabr* (patience), *al-laghw* (empty speech) — Arabic is not pointing at a specific instance. It's pointing at the **whole category** of the thing. The entire territory.

### Now, the Word

> *al-laghw*

The root is *l-gh-w*. The classical lexicons trace this root through a remarkable cluster of meanings. *Laghā* means to make a sound that has no meaning — like the chatter of a sparrow or the ambient noise of a crowd, where you hear something but nothing is being communicated. *Laghīya* in the language of accounting means **to not count** — when something is dropped from a tally because it doesn't qualify, doesn't add up, doesn't register as one of the items. In fiqh you'll find the word in the phrase *yamīn al-laghw* — an oath that **doesn't count**, that has no legal weight, because the person spoke without intention.

Hold all three of those together. *Laghw* is sound that doesn't communicate. *Laghw* is what gets dropped from the count. *Laghw* is speech that **doesn't bind** because there was no real intention behind it.

Now feel what kind of category that is. *Laghw* is not the same as *kadhib* (lying). It's not the same as *ghība* (backbiting). It's not the same as *fuḥsh* (obscenity). Allah has separate words for all of those, and He will use them elsewhere. *Laghw* is broader and quieter than any of them. *Laghw* is the entire territory of **speech and attention that has no weight in front of Allah.**

It includes the obviously bad, yes. Gossip. Mockery. Idle slander. But it also includes the seemingly innocent. The conversation at work that exists only to fill the silence. The thirty-minute argument about a TV show. The video you watched because the thumbnail was bright. The thread you scrolled because the title made you angry. The hours of small talk that, when added up, produced nothing — no relationship deepened, no idea clarified, no remembrance of Allah, no comfort to a hurting person, no good. **Just minutes that do not count.**

### And Notice What Allah Doesn't Say

Here is the absence to notice — and we have to read absence as carefully as we read presence.

Allah says *al-laghw*. He uses the definite article of the genus. He does **not** say *laghwin shadīd* — severe laghw. He does **not** say *al-laghwi-l-mukhillu bil-ʿibādah* — the laghw that disturbs worship. He does **not** specify that He means only the harmful kind.

He says **al-laghw**. The whole territory. The genus.

This is the Quran refusing to give you a loophole. If Allah had specified, "the harmful laghw," every one of us would have spent our lives in conversation with our nafs about which laghw is harmful enough to count. *This kind of scrolling is harmful, but that kind is fine. This conversation is laghw, but that one is just relaxing. This thirty minutes is wasted, but the next thirty minutes are recovery.*

The undefined genus closes that door. Allah names the **whole category** and leaves the specific contents of it for you to recognize in your own life. The believers — the ayah says — turn away from **al-laghw**. From the thing itself. Wherever they meet it, in whatever shape it takes that day, in whatever new form their century has invented.

Stay with that. The Quran is not naming for you a specific list of forbidden activities. It is naming a **disposition**. The disposition of **defaulting away** from that whole territory.

### One More Sound Note

Notice what *al-laghw* sounds like when you say it aloud.

*Al-laghw.*

The "gh" in Arabic is one of the throat letters — *ghayn* — a thick, heavy, almost gargled sound made deep in the throat. The word itself feels heavy, sticky, slightly unpleasant in the mouth. You almost have to clear your throat to get it out. Compare that to lighter, airier words in the same surah — *aflaḥa* (succeeded), *khāshi'ūn* (humble) — those flow.

*Al-laghw* doesn't flow. It clogs.

This is the Quran doing what it does so often: making the **sound of the word match the experience of the thing.** Laghw, in your life, doesn't feel light. After thirty minutes of doom-scrolling, you don't feel airy. You feel **clogged**. Slightly heavy. Slightly off. The sound of the word in Arabic carries that exact texture.

[PAUSE]

So *al-laghw* is the genus, with no edges, of speech and attention that does not count. The word itself feels in your mouth the way the experience feels in your soul.

## ELEMENT TWO: *mu'riḍūn* — A Posture, Not a Thought

### A Small Lesson on Form IV in Arabic

Arabic verbs come in patterns called "forms." Form I is the basic root. Forms II through X add layers — they intensify, make causative, make reflexive, make reciprocal. The classical grammarians say each form carries its own flavor.

Form IV — the *af'ala* pattern — is **the form of deliberate causation**. Whatever the basic verb means, Form IV makes it into something the subject **does on purpose, makes happen.** *'Aẓuma* (to be great) → *a'ẓama* (to make great, to magnify deliberately). *Salima* (to be safe) → *aslama* (to surrender oneself, to actively submit). The Form IV is not passive. It is **chosen.**

Now look at our word.

> *mu'riḍūn*

This is the active participle of *a'raḍa* — Form IV of the root *'-r-ḍ*.

The Form IV here is doing significant work. It's not saying *they happen to not be involved with laghw*. It's saying **they actively, deliberately turn themselves away from it.** It is a chosen posture. It is something they do, not something that happens to them.

### Now, the Root

The root *'-r-ḍ* is one of the most spatial roots in Arabic. Its base sense is **side, breadth, width**. *'Arḍ* means width, as opposed to length. *'Āriḍ* means a side, a flank. The classical lexicons all converge on the physical, geometric sense first: this root is about **the sides of things.**

So what does it mean to *a'raḍa 'an* something — to perform Form IV with the preposition *'an*?

Literally, in the original physical sense: **to turn your side to it.** To present your flank to the thing. To pivot your body so that you are no longer facing it.

The Arab poets used this word for camels turning their sides to a watering hole they refused to drink from, for warriors turning their backs on a fight they refused to enter, for travelers turning their faces away from a road they refused to take. The image is **bodily**. It is a **posture** before it is an attitude.

When the Quran says the believers are *mu'riḍūn 'an al-laghw*, it is not saying they intellectually disapprove. It is not saying they have opinions about it. It is saying that their **bodies have turned**. They have presented their flank. Their backs are to it. Their faces are pointed elsewhere.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with how strange this is.

In English, when we want to say someone avoids something, we use mental verbs. *They ignore it. They dismiss it. They tune it out.* All of those are head-words. Mind-words. They suggest a posture of the mind that the body might or might not match.

Arabic does not let us off that easily. The word the Quran chose is a **body word**. *Mu'riḍūn* is a posture you could photograph. If a photographer were trying to capture a *mu'riḍ* visually, they would shoot someone with their face turned away, their shoulder presented, their feet pointed in a different direction.

Why does this matter? Because the Quran is telling us something the modern attention economy proves every single day: **passive avoidance does not work.** You cannot stay in front of the thing and just decide not to look at it. You cannot keep the screen on and decide not to be drawn in. You cannot stay in the conversation that is becoming gossip and decide internally that you are not participating. The mind doesn't have that strength against an environment engineered to capture it.

What works is **turning the body.** Putting the phone down and walking away. Leaving the conversation. Closing the tab. Pointing your feet elsewhere. The believers don't sit in front of *al-laghw* with steely resolve. They **leave**. Their bodies move. Their attention has a posture, and the posture is *turned away.*

### One More Layer — the Pronoun *hum*

Now look at the order of the words in the ayah:

> *walladhīna **hum** 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn*

In Arabic, the pronoun *hum* — they — was not strictly required here. The relative pronoun *alladhīna* already establishes the subject. Grammatically, you could have said *walladhīna 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn* and it would still make sense.

But Allah inserts *hum*. He puts in an extra pronoun.

Classical grammarians called this *al-ḍamīr al-fāṣil* in some constructions, or simply emphasis through pronominal reinforcement. The function is roughly: **they — they specifically — those very ones — turn away from laghw.** It's the difference between saying "the people who turn away from laghw" and saying "the people who, **they**, turn away from laghw — that's who they are."

The pronoun is doing **identity work**. It's saying: this isn't just one of their behaviors. This is **who they are**. This is not a habit they happen to have. This is a defining trait. To know them is to know that they turn away from laghw.

Notice that this same construction repeats throughout the seven qualities — *alladhīna hum fī ṣalātihim khāshi'ūn*, *alladhīna hum 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn*, *alladhīna hum lil-zakāti fā'ilūn*, and so on. Every quality gets the pronoun. Allah is not listing what believers do. He is listing **what they are**. The seven qualities are not a behavior checklist. They are a description of identity. And the second item in that identity is: **the kind of person whose attention is turned away from what doesn't count.**

[PAUSE]

So we have, now, three things on the table about this single word:

— *Mu'riḍūn* is Form IV, meaning the turning is **deliberate, chosen, active.**

— The root means **the side, the flank** — so the turning is **bodily**, a posture, not just a mental dismissal.

— The added pronoun *hum* makes this not just a behavior but **an identity** — these are *the kind of people* whose attention has this default direction.

That is one word. Carrying that much weight. Set into a sentence of seven words.

## ELEMENT THREE: The Pairing — *fī* and *'an*, Inside and Away From

### A Small Lesson on Arabic Prepositions

In Arabic, prepositions are not interchangeable. Each one creates a different spatial relationship between you and the thing the preposition governs. *Fī* means **in, inside, contained within**. *'Alā* means on, on top of. *Min* means from, originating in. *'An* means **away from, off of, departing from.** *Ilā* means toward, ending at.

Classical Arabic doesn't waste prepositions. When the Quran chooses one, it is creating a precise geometry between the subject and the object.

### Now, the Pairing

Look at ayahs 2 and 3 together:

> *Alladhīna hum **fī ṣalātihim** khāshi'ūn.*
> Those who, they, **in their prayer**, are humble.
>
> *Walladhīna hum **'ani-l-laghwi** mu'riḍūn.*
> And those who, they, **away from al-laghw**, are turning.

The architecture is unmistakable. Allah uses two opposite prepositions for two opposite spaces.

*Fī* — in, contained, inside. The believer is **inside** the prayer. Fully there. Present in the box. Their attention is in one place.

*'An* — away from, departing, withdrawing. The believer is **away from** al-laghw. Outside the box of empty speech. Their attention has departed from one place.

Two prepositions. Two opposite directions. **One unified attention discipline.**

Stay with this. The believers are described, in two consecutive ayahs, by two prepositions that are spatial opposites — *fī* (in) and *'an* (away from) — and the same trait connects them: **where they put their attention.**

Inside their prayer, their attention is fully **in**. Their khushū' is the gathered presence of someone who is not letting their mind wander to what's outside.

Outside their prayer, their attention is fully **away from** al-laghw. Their i'rāḍ is the chosen withdrawal of someone who is not letting their attention drift to what's empty.

Both are **forms of presence-by-design.** In one space, presence by being all-the-way-in. In another space, presence by being all-the-way-out. The believer is not a person whose attention drifts — neither inside salah nor outside it. The believer is a person whose attention has **two defaults**: into what matters, away from what doesn't.

### And Notice the Order

Why this order? Why is khushū' in prayer named first and i'rāḍ from laghw named second?

Because **the inside trains the outside.**

The five daily prayers are the gymnasium where the believer's attention learns its discipline. For five short windows a day, the believer practices being all-the-way-in. They practice noticing when the mind drifts and bringing it back. They practice gathering. And then they get up off the prayer mat and walk into their day, and the **same attention** that has just been trained to be present with Allah is now the attention they carry into the conversations, the screens, the marketplaces.

A person who has practiced *being in* for five minutes can recognize, in the rest of their day, when they are **being scattered.** The two states become legible to them because they have a contrast. They know what presence feels like — they tasted it in sajdah this morning — and so they can recognize, three hours later in front of a screen, that what they're doing now is **the opposite of that.** Not present. Not gathered. Not in. Drifting. Empty. *Laghw.*

And once they can recognize it, they can turn from it.

Khushū' in prayer is what gives the believer the **diagnostic** for laghw outside it. Without the prayer's training in presence, you cannot reliably feel the difference between attention that is doing something and attention that is being eaten. You become numb to the contrast. You assume the scattered state is just what attention is. Prayer teaches you that no — there is another state — and from there, you can begin to turn.

[PAUSE]

So the order of the ayahs is the order of cause and effect. **First the inside is set right. Then the outside follows.** The believer who is khāshi' in salah becomes, by extension, the believer who is mu'riḍ from laghw. The two are not separate items on a list. They are one trained attention working in two directions.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## THEME ONE: Attention Is the Currency of Your Life

I want to start somewhere that has nothing to do with religion.

In the early 2000s, before most people understood what was being built, a few engineers in Silicon Valley realized something that would change human civilization. They realized that **attention** — your attention, my attention, billions of people's attention — was a measurable, harvestable resource, and that nobody was protecting it.

Up until then, advertising had been a passive game. You ran an ad in a newspaper, you bought a slot on TV, and you hoped that some percentage of people would notice. Attention was sort of assumed — you couldn't really capture it on demand.

But the screen in your pocket changed that. A screen with a notification, a feed, an algorithm, an infinite scroll, a haptic buzz — that screen could **request** your attention thousands of times a day. And once it had your attention, it could measure exactly how long you held it, what kept you scrolling, what made you stop, what made you click. And from that measurement, the system could optimize. It could learn what kind of content captured your specific attention most reliably. And then it could deliver more of that, faster, with sharper hooks, in tighter loops.

What was being built — and you are alive in the middle of it — is the **largest, most precisely engineered attention-capture machine in human history.**

The whole model only works if your attention is treated as **free**. If it has no price. If you don't notice when it gets taken. If you don't experience your own scattered, drained, vaguely numb state at the end of an evening as a **cost** that was extracted from you.

Now hear the ayah again, in this light.

> *Walladhīna hum 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn.*

For fourteen-hundred years, this ayah has been telling believers that their attention has a **direction**, that the direction can be **chosen**, and that the people who succeed are people whose direction defaults **away** from what doesn't count. Allah was naming the discipline that the modern world is now begging us to discover, fourteen-hundred years before there was a word for "the attention economy."

Stop and feel the gift of that. The Quran is not late to your problem. The Quran arrived at your problem fourteen centuries before your problem was invented.

[PAUSE]

But here is where the second movement of this theme has to land. Because if we just say "the Quran knew about phones," we are flattering the text and missing what it's actually doing.

The Quran is not warning you about a technology. The Quran is naming a **structure of the heart** — *the heart that defaults toward what does not count* — and telling you that the believers' identity is shaped by reversing that default.

The phone is just one form of laghw. Before there were phones, there was gossip in the marketplace. Before there were marketplaces, there was idle chatter at the well. The technology changes. The structure of the heart that gets eaten by laghw is the same structure the Quran is addressing in this ayah. **The pull toward emptiness is older than any platform.** The platforms just make it more efficient.

Which means turning off your phone is not the goal. **Becoming a different kind of person** is the goal. A person whose attention has a default direction. A person whose body, when it senses laghw beginning, **turns**. A person who can be in front of a screen and not be eaten by it, because their attention is not lying flat and available — it has a posture, and the posture is turned.

A person whose entire day is structured by two attention disciplines: deep into what matters, deep away from what doesn't. *Fī ṣalātihim* and *'ani-l-laghwi*. Both being practiced, on alternating loops, all day long.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means that the practical discipline is not about deleting apps — though it might involve that. The practical discipline is about **building a contrast in your attention**. About making sure that some part of every day is spent practicing presence — in salah, in dhikr, in real conversation, in the silent space before sleep — so that when you encounter laghw, you can **feel the difference** and your body can **turn**.

If your attention has never been deeply present anywhere, you have no contrast. Laghw becomes the water you swim in, not noticed, not named, not turned from. The first move is not to fight laghw. The first move is to **build the contrast** that lets you recognize it. That is why the ayah on khushū' comes first.

And then — once the contrast is built — *mu'riḍūn* becomes possible. Not as a struggle. As a reflex. As an identity. *Hum 'ani-l-laghwi mu'riḍūn.* That's just who they are. They turn. The way a healthy body turns away from spoiled food without needing to think about it.

That is the first invitation of this ayah. **Stop letting your attention be free.** Notice that it has a price. Notice when it is being taken. And start practicing — through the prayer first, then through everything else — the slow training of an attention that has a direction.

## THEME TWO: The Two Directions of a Believer — Into the Sacred, Away from the Empty

I want to start this theme with an image that the Quran uses elsewhere, because I think it makes this concrete in a way that nothing else will.

In Surah al-Furqan, ayah 72, Allah describes the *'ibād al-Raḥmān* — the servants of the Most Merciful — and includes among their qualities this:

> *Wa idhā marrū bi-l-laghwi marrū kirāman.*
> And when they pass by laghw, they pass by it with dignity.

I want you to see that image. *Marrū* — they passed. *Bi-l-laghw* — by laghw. *Marrū kirāman* — they passed by it with dignity, with nobility, with the bearing of a person who refuses to stop and engage.

Picture a person walking through a marketplace. Off to one side, there is a fight breaking out — two men shouting insults, drawing a crowd. Off to another side, a circle has formed around someone telling a humiliating story about an absent third party. Further on, a vendor is hawking something with deliberately misleading promises, and people are gathering to laugh.

Most people, walking through, would slow down. They would lean in. They would catch the punchline. They would maybe linger long enough to hear the end of the insult, just to see who won. *They would mix into the laghw.*

The *'ibād al-Raḥmān* — they keep walking. *Marrū kirāman.* Their step doesn't break. Their head doesn't turn. They pass through the marketplace of laghw like a noble person passes through a place that does not deserve their attention. Not by stopping to denounce it. Not by lecturing the people in it. Just by **moving through, with their body pointed elsewhere.**

That is what *mu'riḍūn* looks like in motion. That is what i'rāḍ from laghw is, lived out in a real walk through a real place full of real noise.

[PAUSE]

Now bring it back to our ayah.

The believer in Surah al-Mu'minun has two directions. **Into the sacred, away from the empty.** Both are forms of motion. Both are the same person, with the same trained attention, oriented in two different directions depending on what is in front of them.

When prayer is in front of them, they are *fī* — they are **in**. They go in deeply. They gather their attention from wherever it has scattered and they bring it into the prayer with them.

When laghw is in front of them, they are *mu'riḍūn 'an* — they are **turning away from**. They withdraw their attention. They don't let it land. They don't let it linger. They pass by with dignity. They keep walking.

Two directions. One discipline.

Why does this matter so much?

Because most of us live with **only one direction**. We have, at best, the first one — we go into prayer when prayer is in front of us, more or less. But we have not built the second. When laghw is in front of us, we do not turn. We engage. We linger. We mix in. And then, hours later, we wonder why we feel hollowed out.

The believer in this ayah is described as a person with a **complete** attention discipline. Both directions, both trained. Allah does not list khushū' alone and then move on. He lists khushū' and then immediately, in the very next breath, lists i'rāḍ. They go together. They are halves of one identity. **A person who is deeply in what matters, and deeply out of what doesn't.**

[PAUSE]

Now I want to push on this a little harder, because there is something paradoxical here that we shouldn't rush past.

Both directions — being deeply *in* and being deeply *out* — are forms of **presence**. That sounds wrong, because we usually think of "out" as absence. But this is a particular kind of absence. *Mu'riḍūn* is not zoning out. It's not numbing. It's not just disengaged drifting. It's **chosen withdrawal.** It's a presence that says: *I see what this is, and I am choosing to point myself elsewhere.*

You could call this *active absence*. The believer is **actively absent** from laghw. Their non-engagement is not accidental, not lazy, not numb. It is **selected**. It is the same presence-of-self that they bring into salah, only here it is exercised by **leaving** instead of by **arriving**.

Compare this to the alternative. A person who is just numb. A person whose attention is disengaged because they have been so eaten by overstimulation that they no longer feel anything. That person is "not engaged with laghw" in some technical sense, but they are not *mu'riḍ* either. They have no direction. Their attention is just **flat, drained, available to whatever lands on it next.**

The believer is the opposite of flat. The believer's attention has a **shape**. A posture. A direction. Even when they are not in prayer, their attention is not flat — it is leaning, it is oriented, it is poised to turn away when laghw appears and to gather when something worth gathering for appears.

This is what khushū' produces, when it is real. It produces a person whose attention has been trained to **have a direction even when nothing demands it.** Whose default state is not scatter, but orientation. Whose resting attention is already pointed somewhere.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means I need to start thinking about my **whole day** as one continuous attention practice, not as five small windows of prayer separated by twenty hours of drift. The prayer is not where the attention practice happens; the prayer is where the attention is **calibrated** so that it can be carried — with the same posture, the same gathered orientation — into the rest of the day.

It means asking, after a long stretch of phone use or empty conversation, not just *was that wrong?* but *what direction is my attention pointed in right now? Is it in something? Is it turned away from something? Or is it just flat, lying open to whatever lands?*

It means recognizing that **flat attention** is not neutral. It is the state in which laghw eats you, because there is no direction to push back against the pull. The believer's attention is never flat. It is either in something worth being in, or turned away from something not worth lingering on. There is always a posture. There is always a direction.

That is the second invitation of this ayah. **Stop letting your attention go flat.** Build, day by day, an attention that has two trained directions — deeply in, and deeply away. So that whether you are in sujud or walking through a noisy marketplace or sitting in front of a screen, you are still you. Still oriented. Still gathered. Still walking with dignity.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Now Visible

Now that we've walked through this slowly, look back at ayahs 2 and 3 together:

> *Alladhīna hum **fī ṣalātihim** **khāshi'ūn.***
> *Walladhīna hum **'ani-l-laghwi** **mu'riḍūn.***

The architecture is exquisite once you see it.

| | Ayah 2 (Inside Prayer) | Ayah 3 (Outside Prayer) |
|---|---|---|
| **Preposition** | *fī* — in, contained within | *'an* — away from, departing from |
| **Direction** | Into | Out of |
| **What is on the other end** | *ṣalāt* — the worship | *al-laghw* — the empty |
| **The trait** | *khāshi'ūn* — gathered presence | *mu'riḍūn* — chosen withdrawal |
| **Form of attention** | Active presence | Active absence |

Two ayahs. Two prepositions. Two postures. **One attention.**

The believer is described not by what they believe in their head, not even first by what they do with their hands or their wealth — those come later in the list — but by **the shape of their attention.** Allah names the architecture of the believer's *focus* before He names anything else they do, because everything else flows from this. A person whose attention is gathered into Allah and turned from the empty will, eventually, give zakah, guard their chastity, keep their trusts, protect their prayers. The seven qualities are a tree, and **attention is the root.**

That is why Allah listed *mu'riḍūn 'ani-l-laghw* as the second quality. Not because empty talk is the second-worst sin. But because **what you turn your attention away from is the first proof that what you give it to is real.**

If you say you love Allah but cannot pull your attention away from laghw, the love is not yet load-bearing. If you can pull your attention away from laghw — bodily, deliberately, by identity — then your love has begun to **cost** you something, and a love that costs is a love that is real.

---

## Bringing It Together

Everything we discovered linguistically and everything we discovered thematically is the same insight, viewed from two angles.

The **linguistic** insight: this ayah uses an undefined genus (*al-laghw*), a Form-IV bodily verb (*mu'riḍūn*), an emphatic identity pronoun (*hum*), and a precise spatial pairing (*fī* / *'an*) — and every one of those choices is doing the same work: **describing attention as a trainable posture that defines the believer.**

The **thematic** insight: in an attention economy that engineers your distraction, and at a moment in human history when our hearts are scattered as never before, Allah names — as the **second** mark of those who succeed — the discipline of turning the body away from the empty, so that the attention given to the sacred is not stolen back the moment we leave the prayer mat.

Same insight. Two doors into it. Both lead to the same place: **the believer is the kind of person whose attention has been trained to have a direction.**

## Questions to Carry

These are not rhetorical. Sit with them tonight, not as challenges, but as quiet diagnostics.

1. If you had to write down, honestly, the categories of *laghw* in your own life — not haram, just empty — what would the list be? And which one is the easiest to name and the hardest to turn from?

2. Allah used a body word (*mu'riḍūn*) instead of a mind word. Is there a place in your life where you keep trying to turn away **with your mind** while staying physically in front of the thing? What would it look like to turn the **body**?

3. The believers' khushū' in prayer comes first; the i'rāḍ from laghw comes second. Is your prayer training your attention enough that you can **feel the contrast** when you walk back into the world? If not — what would deepen the prayer first?

4. The pronoun *hum* makes turning from laghw an **identity**, not a behavior. Is there a part of you that is willing to be *the kind of person* who is known for what they don't engage with? Or do you fear being seen as boring, or distant, or out-of-touch?

5. Which one is more uncomfortable to imagine: being more present in your prayer, or being more absent from your screens? What does that asymmetry tell you about where the next move actually is?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The second mark of those who succeed is an attention trained to two directions: deeply present in what matters, and bodily turned away from what does not — because the believer is recognized not only by what fills their heart, but by what they refuse to let in.**

## The Closing Invitation

If anything shifts after sitting with this ayah, let it not be a resolution to delete an app or to cut a habit, although those may follow. Let the shift be deeper than that. Let it be a quiet recognition that **your attention has a price**, that it is the most precious thing Allah gave you in this life, and that He is calling you — through the words of this ayah — to begin treating it as such.

Begin small. Notice, the next time you find yourself in front of laghw, what it would mean to actually **turn**. Not to argue with yourself. Not to negotiate. Just to point your body somewhere else. Even once a day, that act is the early gesture of *mu'riḍūn*. The first time the body learns the motion. After enough times, the motion will become a posture. After enough postures, the posture will become an identity. After enough identity, you will be — perhaps — among those Allah described in this ayah.

And remember, always, that the prayer is the place this is trained. Without khushū' in salah, i'rāḍ from laghw is impossible to sustain. With it, it slowly becomes who you are.

## Du'a

Yā Allah, the One Who placed in our chests the attention that finds You — train our attention to have a direction. Make us, in our prayers, of those who are gathered, present, turned fully toward You. And when we leave the prayer mat and walk into a world full of noise, make our bodies turn away from al-laghw — not with effort, but as a reflex of who we have become. Let our attention bear the mark of love for You: full when it is on You, withdrawn when it is on what does not count. Make us, yā Rabb, among *those who, they, away from al-laghw, are turning.*
