# How Long Did You Remain?

## Where We Are Standing

To feel these four verses, you have to know where the surah has been.

Surah Al-Mu'minun opens with one of the most confident sentences in the whole Qur'an: *qad aflaḥa-l-mu'minūn* — the believers have already succeeded. Not *will* succeed. *Have.* The surah begins at the finish line and then walks backward to show you the race. It lists the qualities of those who won. And then, remarkably, it takes you inside the human body — the *nuṭfah*, the clinging clot, the lump of flesh, the bones, the flesh clothing the bones — and it lingers over the sheer craftsmanship of how a human being is assembled, stage upon deliberate stage, until it exclaims: *fa-tabāraka-Llāhu aḥsanu-l-khāliqīn* — so blessed is Allah, the best of shapers.

Hold onto that. The surah has already shown you, in slow motion, how carefully you were made.

Then the surah moves through the messengers — Nuh, Musa, the long line of men sent to people who mocked them — and it keeps circling one particular kind of denial. Not denial of God, exactly. These were people who often admitted a creator. What they could not stomach was the *return*. Listen to their voice earlier in the same surah: *"When we are dead and become dust and bones, are we really going to be raised again? We were promised this, and our fathers before us — this is nothing but the tales of the ancients."* That was the sound of Mecca. Not atheism. Something more like a shrug. *Sure, maybe someone made us. But this whole business of standing before Him afterward? Legends. Bedtime stories.*

They lived, most of them, as if this world were the whole story. As if the years they were counting — the harvests, the caravans, the ages of their sons — were the only ledger that would ever be read.

Now come to our four verses. The surah has arrived at its own ending, and it does something merciful and terrible at once: **it lets you watch the reunion.** It fast-forwards past death, past the grave, past the dust and the bones they laughed about, and it drops you into the moment the shrug meets its answer. A voice asks them a question. And the question is not the one they were bracing for.

Here is what should keep you leaning in: the voice does not open with *why did you deny Me?* It does not open with a list of sins. It opens by asking them, of all things, **how long they were gone.** [PAUSE] Why would that be the first question? Why start the final accounting with a question about *duration* — about the count of years? Stay with that. The whole passage turns on why He asks that first.

## Part One — Walking Into the Words

### The verb that has already answered the question

Before we touch the verse, let me hand you a small tool, because Arabic does something here that English cannot do in a single word.

In Arabic there are several verbs for *staying somewhere*. Some of them carry permanence — to dwell, to settle, to take up residence, the way you'd describe living in the house you were born in and will die in. And there is another verb, a different one entirely, for the kind of staying you do when you are passing through. It is the word you would use for waiting in a departure lounge. For pausing at a rest stop on a long drive. For lingering somewhere you both know you will leave. In everyday Arabic you would use it like this: *I only stayed a moment* — I lingered, I tarried, and then I moved on. The word itself smells of transience. It presumes a departure.

Now look at the question Allah puts in that scene:

*Kam **labithtum** fī-l-arḍ.*

He does not ask, *how long did you live on the earth?* He does not ask, *how long did you dwell, how long did you reside.* He reaches, out of all the verbs available, for **the verb of the traveler.** *Labith.* How long did you *tarry* on the earth. How long were you *lingering* there before you moved on.

Notice what has happened before a single answer is given. **The question has already smuggled in the verdict.** The grammar of the question treats the earth as a waystation. The verb assumes the stay was temporary the way a rest stop assumes you have a destination. The people in this scene spent entire lifetimes believing the earth was home, the permanent address, the whole account — and the very first word out of the exchange quietly re-files their entire existence as a *pause between two points.*

Let's sit with how devastating that is. Imagine someone who spent forty years renovating a house they were only renting, arguing with the neighbors over a fence line, sinking every dirham into the walls — convinced it was theirs forever — and then, on the last day, the owner arrives and asks, gently, *"So, how was your stay?"* The word *stay* does all the work. It doesn't argue with the tenant. It doesn't say *you were wrong.* It just names the arrangement correctly, and in naming it correctly, it dissolves forty years of self-deception in a single breath. That is what *labith* does. **It does not accuse them of being wrong about the world. It just calls the world by its real name, and lets the correction fall where it falls.**

And here is the mercy hiding inside the severity — because *labith* is not only a word for them, in that scene. It is a word for you, now, reading this. If the earth is a place you *tarry*, then you are not losing your home when you die. **You cannot lose what was never your home.** You were always a traveler. The only real question the verb leaves open is whether you traveled *toward* something — or whether you unpacked your bags at the rest stop and called it your life.

### The people who counted everything, and can no longer count at all

Look again at how the question is phrased. He does not simply ask *how long.* He adds a strange, almost clinical detail:

*Kam labithtum fī-l-arḍi **ʿadada sinīn** — how long did you tarry on the earth, by the count of years?*

*ʿadad* — a count, a number, a reckoning. He invites them to answer in the currency they trusted most: the tally of years. Because that is what a certain kind of life is. It is a life of counting. We count our age. We count our years of experience. We count the years until retirement, the years married, the years since we lost someone. We build whole identities out of numbers — *I've been doing this for twenty years, I'm not starting over now.* The count of years is how a worldly life measures its own worth. He meets them exactly there. *Fine. Answer Me in years. Give Me the number you were so proud of.*

And watch what happens.

*Qālū labithnā yawman aw baʿḍa yawm — they say: we stayed a day. Or part of a day.*

The people who counted everything **cannot produce a number.** The whole edifice of years collapses into a single blurred day — and then they hedge even that: *or part of a day.* They reach for the tally they spent a lifetime keeping, and their hands come up empty. Whatever felt like eighty years, whatever felt like an unbearably long life while they were living it, has shrunk in the memory to something that barely lasted an afternoon.

And then comes the line I cannot get past. Having failed to count, they say:

*Fa-sʾali-l-**ʿāddīn** — go ask the ones who keep count.*

Feel the full weight of that. The Arabic word for *the ones who keep count* comes from the very same root as *the count of years* in the question. So the root of *counting* appears at both ends of the exchange — He asks them to count, and they answer by pointing to *someone else who counts.* **They defer. They pass the question down the line.** *Don't ask us — ask the record-keepers, ask whoever was tracking this, we can't say.*

Here is the quiet horror in it. On the earth, these were the counters. Counting was their whole religion — assets, ages, achievements, the careful arithmetic of a life measured in what it accumulated. And in the one moment where the count finally matters, where a true reckoning of their time is actually being requested, **they discover they were never really counting anything at all.** The years they thought they were banking were water running through their fingers the whole time. The obsessive tally turns out to have been a tally of nothing. So they hand the question off to someone else, because their own count has vanished.

Think of the person who spends thirty years building a number in a bank account, checking it, growing it, letting it define whether the day was good or bad — and then one afternoon is told the currency was quietly discontinued years ago and everything in the account is worth nothing. The tragedy is not only the loss. It is that all that *counting*, all that anxious daily arithmetic, was spent on a currency that was never going to be honored. That is what these verses show a certain kind of life to be. Not evil, necessarily. Just — *miscounted.* A lifetime of careful accounting in a currency that doesn't cross over.

### "You stayed only a little" — the correction they didn't ask for

Then the voice speaks a third time, and does something I want you to catch precisely, because the grammar is doing something the translation flattens.

*Qāla in labithtum **illā** qalīlan — He says: you did not remain except a little.*

In Arabic, when you want to say something is *only* a certain way — nothing more, no exceptions — you build it out of two pieces: a negation, and then an exception carved out of the negation. Literally the structure is: *you did NOT stay… EXCEPT a little.* It is the most emphatic way the language has of saying *only.* It first denies everything, and then lets back in a single small thing. English says *"you stayed only a little."* Arabic says *"you stayed nothing — nothing at all — apart from a little."* The negation swallows the whole life, and then hands back a sliver.

Now notice who is being corrected, and in which direction.

The people already low-balled it. *They* said *a day, or part of a day* — they had already shrunk eighty years to an afternoon. You would think that is small enough. And yet the voice **corrects them downward again.** *No. Even that was too generous. You stayed only a little.* Even their own diminished estimate of their lives is still an over-estimate. Whatever the smallest word for *brief* is that they can reach for, the reality was smaller.

And then the line closes with four words that break the heart:

*law annakum kuntum **taʿlamūn** — if only you had known.*

*If only you had known.* Not *if only you had believed.* Not *if only you had obeyed.* **If only you had known** — this, the thing you are only now seeing clearly. If only you had known, while you were down there counting your years, that the whole count would one day fit inside part of a single day. If only you had lived with that knowledge live in your chest.

And here the whole passage folds back on itself, because remember what they said one verse earlier: *ask those who keep count* — they deferred their *knowing.* They pushed the knowledge off onto someone else. And now the answer comes: *if only YOU had known.* The knowledge they shrugged off, passed down the line, treated as somebody else's department — that was the one thing they most needed to hold themselves. **The knowing they outsourced on earth is the knowing whose absence damns them here.** [PAUSE]

Let me be careful and honest with you about one thing before we leave the words. The line does not say *if only you had known* and then finish the sentence. Arabic can leave the answer to *"if only"* unspoken on purpose, so that your own imagination completes it — *if only you had known… everything would have been different. You would have lived another way entirely.* One way to sit with that open ending is this: the sentence is deliberately left hanging so that **you** are the one who finishes it, now, while there is still time to finish it with your life instead of with your regret.

---

Before we cross over from the words to what they open onto, hold the shape of what we've found. A verb that files your whole life as a brief stay. A count that vanishes in the hand of the one who trusted it. A correction that shrinks even the smallest estimate smaller still. And a sentence about *knowing* left unfinished, waiting.

All of that has been about *how long.* Now the passage turns, and asks something else entirely.

## Part Two — What the Words Open Onto

### The trip that felt endless until you were driving home

Think about the last long journey you took that you had really been looking forward to. Not a chore-trip — a real one. Maybe you saved for it for a year. In the weeks before, it loomed enormous in your imagination, this vast stretch of time that seemed like it would last forever. And then you were in it, and the days had a strange density — each morning felt full, whole, complete.

And then you were in the car on the way back. Or on the plane home. And someone said, *"Can you believe it's over already?"* And you couldn't. It had evaporated. The thing that had loomed for a year, that felt endless from the inside, was suddenly a single bright smear in the rear-view mirror. *It went so fast.*

Everyone knows this. It is one of the most reliable facts of being human, and psychologists have a whole literature on it — the way our sense of time is elastic, how a summer in childhood felt like a geological age and a year in your forties disappears between two breaths, how we chronically over-estimate future time and radically under-estimate time already lived. The old people in your family have been trying to tell you this your whole life. *It goes by so fast. Blink and you'll be my age.* You nodded politely and did not believe them, because from the inside, from where you stand at twenty or thirty, life feels like a road with no visible end.

Now hear the verse again, standing in that feeling:

*We stayed a day, or part of a day.*

This is not a punishment being invented for them on the Day of Judgment. **It is the truth about time finally seen from the only vantage point high enough to see it whole.** Every human being who has ever aged has caught a glimpse of this from the passenger seat of a car on the way home from somewhere. The Qur'an is simply showing you the view from the very end of the road — where the *entire* journey, cradle to grave, collapses into that same *"can you believe it's over already?"* And it is showing you that view *now*, in advance, decades early, as a gift. Because the old people were right, and the verse is the old people's wisdom raised to the scale of the cosmos: **it goes so fast. And you will not believe how fast until you are looking back at all of it.**

So the question that presses on you is not *how do I make life longer.* You can't. The verse forecloses that. Even the shortest guess is corrected shorter. The question the verse forces is: **if the whole of it is going to feel like part of an afternoon, what do I want to have been true of that afternoon?**

Let me bring that all the way home to something small. You know the feeling of a Sunday evening, when the weekend you were so looking forward to is nearly gone, and there is that faint ache of *where did it go — I meant to do more with it.* Multiply that Sunday-evening ache by a whole life and you have the sound of *labithnā yawman aw baʿḍa yawm.* And here is the mercy: **you get to feel your Sunday-evening ache on Saturday morning.** The verse is Saturday morning. It is the ache delivered early, while the weekend is still in your hands. What you do with a day changes completely depending on whether you feel that ache at the start of it or only at the end. Most people feel it only at the end. The believer is someone who has agreed to feel it at the start — and to live the day accordingly. Not frantically. Just *awake.*

### The exact fear of the modern heart — named, and answered

Now we arrive at the verse the whole scene was built to deliver. Everything before it — the question about years, the collapsing count, the correction — was clearing the ground for this. Watch the turn:

*A-fa-ḥasibtum annamā **khalaqnākum ʿabathan** wa-annakum ilaynā lā turjaʿūn.*

*Did you then think that We created you in vain — and that to Us you would not be returned?*

Sit with the word in the middle: *ʿabath.* It is worth saying aloud, because the sound of it carries its meaning — *ʿa-ba-th* — there is something abrupt and dismissive in it, a word that sounds like a hand waved at something not worth the effort. *ʿAbath* is what a child does idly, aimlessly, with no goal — doodling in the margin, kicking a stone down the road, activity that produces nothing and means nothing and is heading nowhere. It is the opposite of purpose. To make something as *ʿabath* is to make it pointlessly, for no reason, as a shrug of the hand, a thing that could just as easily not have been made at all.

Now hear what fear this word is naming — because it is the precise fear of a huge number of people alive right now, and maybe of you on your worst nights. It is the fear underneath the modern crisis of meaning. The suspicion that the whole thing is *ʿabath.* That we are cosmic accidents, protein arrangements that lucked into consciousness on a wet rock spinning around an average star, and that all our loves and griefs and striving are just chemistry pretending to matter. That there is no author, no arc, no return, no reckoning — just the aimless doodle of matter, and then the lights go out. A great deal of modern anxiety, the low static hum of *what is the point of any of this,* is exactly the fear that we were made — or worse, *not even made, just happened* — as *ʿabath.*

And here is what stops me every time. The Qur'an does not tiptoe around that fear. It does not pretend the fear is unthinkable. **It puts the fear directly into a question and aims it back at the one who holds it.** *Did you actually think that? That you were made for nothing?* The verse walks straight up to the darkest suspicion a human heart can hold about itself and says the words out loud: *ʿabathan.* Made in vain.

And then — this is the architecture — it answers not with a lecture but by *reminding you of what you already watched earlier in this very surah.* Go back to the beginning: the *nuṭfah*, the clot, the lump, the bones, the flesh laid over the bones, stage after deliberate stage, *the best of shapers.* The surah spent verses showing you the almost obsessive *craft* of how a single human being is assembled. And now, at the end, it asks: **did you think all that craftsmanship was for nothing?** You do not build like that for *ʿabath.* No one designs in that kind of detail — nervous system, hearing before sight, the whole staged miracle — as an idle doodle. The meticulousness of the making is itself the refutation of the meaninglessness. The surah answers its own final question with its own opening. *Look at how you were made. Now tell Me again that it was for nothing.*

Let me put this where you live. There is a particular ache that people carry — I have felt it, maybe you have — the ache of feeling *interchangeable.* Replaceable at work. Forgettable in the crowd. The sense that if you vanished, the machine would close over the gap in a week and roll on. That ache is a small, daily taste of the fear of *ʿabath* — the fear of not mattering. And the verse does not answer that ache by telling you to build a bigger legacy or accumulate more so you'll be remembered. It answers it from a completely different direction: **you were made on purpose, by One who does not make anything for nothing, and you will stand before Him personally.** You are not a rounding error in a headcount. You were shaped. And *shaped things have a Shaper who intends something by them.*

[PAUSE]

Then look at the last three words, because they turn the whole thing:

*wa-annakum ilaynā lā **turjaʿūn** — and that to Us you would not be returned.*

Notice the verb is not *return.* It is *be returned.* You are not the one doing the returning. Something is done *to* you — you are brought back, delivered back, restored to an owner. It is passive on purpose. On earth, the deepest instinct of the modern self is autonomy — *no one owns me, I answer to no one, I define my own truth, I am the author of my own meaning.* And the verse quietly removes the ground from under that. *You will be returned.* Like a borrowed thing goes back to the one it belongs to. Not by your choice. By your nature.

We hear that word — *owned, returned, belonging to another* — and something in the modern ear flinches, because we've been trained to hear it as diminishment. But turn it over. *Return* only makes sense if there was an origin. You cannot be *returned* somewhere you never came from. So the very grammar of the word tells you: **you have a home you came from, and a home you are going back to, and the stretch in between — the part you called your whole life — was the *labith*, the brief tarrying, the rest stop on the road between them.** The word *turjaʿūn* is the road curving back to its source. And far from being a threat, this is the deepest answer to the fear of *ʿabath* that a heart could receive: you are not a wanderer with no address. You are not driftwood. **You belong to Someone, and you are going home.** The thing the anxious modern heart most fears — being owned, being answerable — turns out to be the exact thing it most needs: to matter to Someone enough to be *wanted back.*

What does that change tomorrow? Concretely: it changes what you do with a moment nobody is watching. If you are *ʿabath* — an accident heading nowhere — then a private moment, a hidden choice, a kindness no one sees or a cruelty no one catches, all of it is weightless, because there is no ledger and no return. But if *ilaynā turjaʿūn* is true — if every one of us is being carried back to the One who shaped us — then there is no such thing as an unwitnessed moment. The private choice has the same weight as the public one. That is not a burden. For anyone who has ever done good in the dark and wondered if it counted, it is the most consoling news there is: **it counted. It all counted. You will be returned to the One who saw.**

## Bringing It Together

Now stand back and look at what these four verses actually are, as a piece of design — because the architecture is the proof that none of this was accidental.

Look at the pattern of speech: *He said… they said… He said.* A question, an answer, and then a second word from the same voice — but the second word does not continue the topic of the first. Watch the pivot:

- **First**, He asks about *duration*: how long. *(112)*
- **They answer** about duration, and their count collapses. *(113)*
- **He corrects** the duration downward — *only a little.* *(114)*
- **Then He drops duration entirely** and asks about *purpose*: did you think you were made for nothing? *(115)*

Do you see what the first three verses were *for*? They were never really about how many years anyone lived. **The entire conversation about time was a set-up.** It was the voice gently walking them to a confession — *yes, it was brief, it was nothing, it flew by* — and the instant they concede that their life was a blink, the trap closes with exquisite mercy: *So. This blink. Did you think a blink this brief was given to you for no reason at all?* The brevity they just admitted becomes the very argument for purpose. Precisely *because* the stay was so short, it could not have been idle. You do not hand someone a single, unrepeatable afternoon as a pointless doodle. The shortness is not the tragedy. **The shortness is the evidence that every moment of it was weighted.**

And now see how the two halves of our reflection — the words and the world — were the same insight approached from two sides. The verb *labith* said your life is a brief stay. The word *ʿabath* said a brief stay is not the same as a meaningless one. Put them together and you have the whole message: **a rest stop still has a destination.** The traveler who tarries briefly is not a traveler going nowhere. The brevity and the purpose need each other. Take away the purpose and the brevity becomes unbearable — *why suffer through even a short pointless thing.* Take away the brevity and the purpose loses its urgency — *I'll get to it, there's always more time.* Held together, they produce the one posture the whole surah has been building toward from *qad aflaḥa-l-mu'minūn*: **live awake, live briefly, live on purpose, because you are going home and you will be asked.**

### Questions to Carry

Do not rush these. Let them find you over the next days.

1. You keep a count of something — you know what it is. Money, followers, square footage, the years of a career you're too invested in to leave. When the voice asks you to produce that count on the last day, will it be a currency that crosses over, or one that was quietly discontinued while you were still banking it?

2. If you genuinely felt, first thing tomorrow morning, the ache you normally only feel on a Sunday evening — *where did it go, I meant to do more* — what is the one thing you would do differently with the day? What is stopping you from feeling that ache now, on the Saturday morning that your whole life still is?

3. When the fear of *ʿabath* comes for you — on the sleepless night, in the crowd where you feel interchangeable — where do you go for the answer? Do you try to out-build the fear with more achievement, or do you go back, like the surah does, to the fact that you were *shaped*?

4. *If only you had known* — the sentence Allah left unfinished. Finish it in your own voice. *If only I had known ___.* What is the blank? And what would it cost you to start living as though you already know it?

### One Sentence to Keep

**Life was never long enough to have been pointless — its very shortness is the proof that every moment of it was for something, and you will be returned to the One who knows what.**

### The Invitation

You are not being asked to be afraid. Read the passage again and notice — there is no fire in it, no chains, no punishment described. There is only a question about time, and a question about purpose, delivered to people who ran out of chances to answer them well. The whole scene is played out in front of you precisely so that *you* still have the chance they lost. That is the mercy of being shown the end of the road while you are still on it. The travelers in the verse are addressed, but you are the one being spoken to. Their *"if only we had known"* is your *"now I know."*

So carry the traveler's lightness — you cannot lose a home that was never yours — and carry the traveler's seriousness — you are going somewhere, and you will arrive. Let the shortness of the road stop frightening you and start *focusing* you. And when the day feels heavy or meaningless or too small to matter, remember the meticulous craft the surah opened with, and hear the question underneath everything: *did you think you were made for nothing?* You weren't. **You were shaped, on purpose, and you are wanted back.**

### A Closing Du'a

O Allah, You who shaped us stage by stage and called none of it in vain — let us feel now, while there is still time, how briefly we are tarrying here. Do not let us be among those who kept careful count of everything except the one thing that mattered. Let us finish the sentence *if only I had known* with our lives and not with our regret. And when we are returned to You — for we will be returned to You — let us arrive as travelers who knew, all along, where the road was leading. Āmīn.
