# How Are You Bewitched? — The Three Questions and the Diagnosis They Deliver

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 84–89

> قُل لِّمَنِ ٱلْأَرْضُ وَمَن فِيهَآ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ ۝ سَيَقُولُونَ لِلَّهِ ۚ قُلْ أَفَلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ ۝ قُلْ مَن رَّبُّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ ٱلسَّبْعِ وَرَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ ۝ سَيَقُولُونَ لِلَّهِ ۚ قُلْ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ ۝ قُلْ مَنۢ بِيَدِهِۦ مَلَكُوتُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ وَهُوَ يُجِيرُ وَلَا يُجَارُ عَلَيْهِ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ ۝ سَيَقُولُونَ لِلَّهِ ۚ قُلْ فَأَنَّىٰ تُسْحَرُونَ

*"Say: To whom belongs the earth and whoever is in it, if you truly know?
They will say: To Allah. Say: Will you not then take heed?
Say: Who is Lord of the seven heavens and Lord of the Magnificent Throne?
They will say: To Allah. Say: Will you not then be conscious of Him?
Say: In whose hand is the dominion of every single thing — He grants refuge while none can be granted refuge against Him — if you truly know?
They will say: It is Allah's. Say: Then how are you bewitched?"*

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

To enter these ayahs, you have to understand something that surprises most modern readers.

The Meccans Muhammad ﷺ was speaking to **were not atheists**. They were not people who had never heard of Allah. They were not, in any modern sense, "non-believers" in a creator God.

The Quran tells us, again and again — when you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they say *Allah*. When you ask them who sends down the rain, they say *Allah*. When you ask them whose hand holds everything, they say *Allah*.

So what was the problem?

The problem was this: **they admitted the truth and did not let it touch them.**

They could say "Allah created the heavens" and then walk to the Ka'bah and circle a stone idol. They could say "Allah owns everything" and then deny that He had any right to tell them how to live. They could agree, in principle, with everything the Prophet ﷺ said about who Allah is — and reject everything he said about what that meant.

This is the world these ayahs enter. Years of preaching. Years of clear evidence. Years of patience. And the Meccan response was not denial. It was something stranger and more dangerous than denial. It was **acknowledgment without consequence**. Knowing without moving.

The surah has been building for eighty-three ayahs. The believers have been described. The prophets' stories have been told. The moral collapse of the rejecters has been exposed. And now we arrive here — at the rhetorical climax. Three questions. Three identical answers. Three increasingly sharp responses from Allah.

[PAUSE]

What I want you to feel, as we begin, is this: these ayahs are not arguing with atheism. They are arguing with something far more familiar to you and me. They are arguing with **the version of belief that doesn't change anything**.

And the final word of these six ayahs is one of the most surgical diagnoses in the entire Quran.

How are you **bewitched**?

Stay with me. We have to earn that word.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Architecture of Three

Before we look at any single word, look at the **shape**.

Three questions. Three answers. Three responses. The shape itself is teaching.

Notice what the three questions do — not what they say, but what they *do*.

**Question 1:** "To whom belongs the earth and whoever is in it?"
**Question 2:** "Who is Lord of the seven heavens and Lord of the magnificent Throne?"
**Question 3:** "In whose hand is the dominion of every single thing — He grants refuge while none can be granted refuge against Him?"

Watch the scope expand.

The first question stays where you can see it. *The earth and what's in it*. This is the visible creation. Trees. Mountains. People. Animals. The world your eyes confirm every morning.

The second question lifts your gaze. *The seven heavens and the great Throne*. This is the unseen creation — what is above, what is beyond, what scripture testifies to but the eye cannot capture. Classical commentators describe the Throne (*al-ʿArsh al-ʿaẓīm*) as the greatest of all created things and the highest of them — the summit of the created order, the pinnacle of the unseen realm. When the Meccans admitted that Allah was Lord of even this, they were conceding not just visible sovereignty but the sovereignty of the entire spiritual architecture — the very realm they claimed their idols had special access to.

The third question transcends both. *The malakūt of every single thing.* Not just earth. Not just heavens. **Every single thing** — including the realm of refuge itself. Including protection. Including the very category of safety.

Each question is bigger than the last. Each one demands a deeper admission. And yet — and this is what's stunning — **the answer is exactly the same every time.**

سَيَقُولُونَ لِلَّهِ.
*They will say: To Allah.*

Three times this phrase repeats, identical, like a heartbeat. *Lillāh. Lillāh. Lillāh.*

Notice something. This is not Allah hoping they will say it. The verb is سَيَقُولُونَ — *they WILL say*. The سَ prefix is the future tense in Arabic. It is a prediction, almost a guarantee. Allah is telling His Prophet ﷺ: *"Ask them. They will admit it. You already know what they will say."*

[PAUSE]

This is the pre-condition for the entire passage. Allah is not entering a debate. He is **closing one**. He is saying: *the verdict you would give if I asked you is already written. You already know. You have always known.*

So why isn't it changing anything?

That question — *why isn't this knowledge changing them?* — is what the final words of each ayah are about to answer.

Here's what this triple structure invites us to consider: **the Quran's central concern is not whether you know that Allah is Lord. Its concern is what to do with you when you already know and it isn't moving you.**

That is a different problem from disbelief.

That is the problem these ayahs were sent for.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Three Different Responses — and the Verb That Changes

Now look at what Allah tells His Prophet ﷺ to *say back* after each admission.

**After Question 1 (the earth):** قُلْ أَفَلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ — *Say: Will you not then take heed / remember?*

**After Question 2 (the heavens):** قُلْ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ — *Say: Will you not then be conscious / guard yourself?*

**After Question 3 (the dominion of all things):** قُلْ فَأَنَّىٰ تُسْحَرُونَ — *Say: Then how are you bewitched?*

Three responses. Three different verbs. And here is where Arabic grammar is doing something I need you to see clearly.

Let's first take the structure: the first two responses begin with **أَفَلَا** — "will you not then?" This is an interrogative-with-negation construction. It is gentle pressure. It is a teacher pausing and saying: *"Wait. Given what you just said, isn't there something obvious you should be doing?"*

But the third response drops أَفَلَا entirely. It uses **فَأَنَّىٰ** instead. Different particle. Different texture. *Annā* in Arabic carries the sense of *"How is it even possible?"* — disbelief, astonishment, sometimes accusation. The teacher has stopped asking. The teacher is now staring at you.

Now watch the verbs.

**Verb 1: تَذَكَّرُونَ** (tadhakkarūn) — from the root dh-k-r, "to remember." This is form V (تفعّل), an intensive reflexive form. It does not mean simply to recall a fact. It means to bring something deep into yourself, to *let it sit in you*, to recollect it actively. Allah is not asking, "do you remember the fact?" He is asking, "do you bring this truth into your interior?"

And classical commentators point to something specific that this interior work is supposed to yield. Al-Tabari reads the tadhakkur here as calling for a particular inference: will you not remember that the One who was capable of creating all of this from nothing is therefore capable of *reviving you after your death* — of restoring you as whole beings after your decay? The chain is creation → resurrection → accountability. You already admitted the first premise. The tadhakkur is the invitation to follow it forward to its conclusion, all the way to the day your deeds are weighed. Remembering, in this reading, is not a warm spiritual feeling. It is a chain of reasoning you owe to the evidence you already hold.

**Verb 2: تَتَّقُونَ** (tattaqūn) — from the root w-q-y, "to guard, shield, protect." This is form VIII. It is the root from which we get *taqwā*. It does not mean fear in the sense of terror. It means the active posture of placing a barrier between yourself and harm because you have *grasped that you are exposed*. Allah is now asking, "do you not, given this knowledge, take protective stance?"

Al-Tabari reads the taqwā here with sharp specificity: will you not fear Allah's punishment for your disbelief in Him, and for your rejection of what His messenger has brought? The Meccans have just admitted that Allah rules the seven heavens and the great Throne — the very realm their idols were supposed to navigate. That admission should produce fear. Not vague unease, but the specific, urgent recognition that you have conceded the Lordship of the One whose instructions you are refusing — and the gap between what you admitted and how you are living has a consequence.

So the first response is internal: **let it touch you, let it carry you to its full conclusion**. The second is behavioral: **let it shape your conduct with the fear your own admission demands**. The intensity is rising.

[PAUSE]

But now — the third verb. And this is the one that should make you sit up.

**تُسْحَرُونَ** (tusḥarūn) — from the root s-ḥ-r, "to bewitch, enchant, cast a spell upon."

Look carefully at the form.

تَذَكَّرُونَ — *you* remember. **Active.**
تَتَّقُونَ — *you* guard yourselves. **Active.**
تُسْحَرُونَ — *you are bewitched.* **Passive.**

Do you see what just happened?

In the first two responses, you were the subject. The action was yours to do. *Will you not remember? Will you not guard?* These are calls to act.

In the third response, **you are no longer the subject.** Something is happening *to* you. You have become the object. An invisible actor is doing this to you, and you have lost the agency to even resist.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most devastating grammatical shifts in the entire Quran.

The first two questions still treat you as a person with a will. *You can still remember. You can still take precaution. You haven't, but you could.*

The third response says: *we are past that. Something has captured you. The thread between what you know and what you do has been **cut by a force outside you**, and that is why your knowledge is sitting in you doing nothing.*

You are not failing to act on the truth.

You are being **acted upon**, and that is why you can't.

What this invites us to consider is something we'll explore in the second half of this reflection: **what is the spell?** What is the bewitchment Allah is naming? Because if your knowledge of Allah is not translating into your living, then according to this ayah, *something else is doing the translating*.

We'll come back to that.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: مَلَكُوتُ and the Asymmetric Attribute

Before we leave the linguistic journey, I want you to spend time in one ayah — the third question, ayah 88 — because Allah did something there that He didn't do in the first two.

He named an attribute that has no counterpart.

قُلْ مَنۢ بِيَدِهِۦ مَلَكُوتُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ وَهُوَ يُجِيرُ وَلَا يُجَارُ عَلَيْهِ
*Say: In whose hand is the dominion of every single thing — and He grants refuge, and none can be granted refuge against Him.*

Two pieces. Let's take them one at a time.

**First: مَلَكُوتُ** (malakūt). Most translations render this simply as "dominion" or "kingdom." That undersells it.

The root م-ل-ك (m-l-k) gives you *milk* (ownership), *mulk* (kingdom), *malik* (king). Standard nouns. But *malakūt* is none of those. It is the فَعَلُوت pattern — a rare intensive, almost mystical form, used in Arabic to elevate the noun beyond its ordinary register.

If *mulk* is the visible kingdom — what a king holds in his hand, what his territory looks like on a map — then *malakūt* is the absolute, inner, unseen sovereignty *behind* the visible. It is the dominion of the realm before any of it appears. It is sovereignty *as such*.

(When the translation above renders *malakūt* as "absolute dominion," the word "absolute" is not a separate Arabic term — it is the translator's attempt to carry the intensification of the -ūt suffix into English. The root and meaning are unchanged; what the morphological form amplifies is the register, the depth and totality of the sovereignty being named.)

So Allah is not saying "Who owns everything?" — He already asked something close to that in question one. He is asking, "**In whose hand is the inner reality, the unseen sovereignty, the absolute jurisdiction over every single thing that exists?**"

Each question has been deepening. The first reached for the visible. The second reached for the heavens, crowned by a Throne described by classical commentators as the greatest of all created things and the highest of them. The third reaches for the *category of sovereignty itself* — and places it in the hand of Allah.

[PAUSE]

**Second: يُجِيرُ وَلَا يُجَارُ عَلَيْهِ** — and *He grants refuge, and none is granted refuge against Him.*

Listen to the sound. *Yujīr — wa lā yujāru ʿalayh.* Same root, j-w-r, which gives you ijāra — granting protection or asylum. The word lives in the world of refuge: when a weaker tribe came under the protection of a stronger one, when a man fled his enemies and sought sanctuary, when someone needed someone bigger to stand between them and harm.

Allah is saying: He is the One who grants refuge.

And then immediately: **none can grant refuge against Him.**

Same root. Same word, almost. But active against passive. One side blank.

Stay with this. Every other being you can think of can be both the protector and the protected. A king grants protection to his subjects, but if a stronger king attacks, even a king flees to a higher protector. A father shields his child but turns to his Lord when illness comes. Every being in creation is, in some sense, *both* — a refuge for those below and a refuge-seeker before what is above.

But Allah is **only the refuge.** The other half is empty. There is nothing above Him to flee to. There is no higher tribunal. There is no greater hand to step in between.

[PAUSE]

This is what Arabic grammarians call an asymmetric attribute. A pair that should have two halves but has only one. The grammar itself is hollowed out, because the reality is hollowed out — there is no upper position because there is no one above.

And then — this is the moment — He asks: *In whose hand is all this, if you truly know?*

And they say: **Lillāh.** *To Allah.*

And He says: *Then how are you bewitched?*

[PAUSE]

## Part 2: The Spell and What Breaks It

Now we're ready for the question the whole passage has been building toward.

**What is the bewitchment?**

Ibn Kathir frames these three questions around a distinction the Quran draws repeatedly: **Tawhid al-Rububiyyah** — the acknowledgment of Allah's Lordship, that He alone created and controls and sustains — and **Tawhid al-Uluhiyyah** — the act of worshipping Him alone, of submitting to His command, of ordering your life by His direction.

The Meccans had the first with complete clarity. They were, in a real sense, monotheists about *who* Allah is.

What they refused was the second. They would not let the *who* determine the *how they lived*.

And this is precisely what makes the position incoherent. If you admit that Allah owns the earth and everything in it, that He rules the seven heavens and the great Throne, that all sovereignty — visible and unseen — belongs to Him alone — then the logical, unavoidable conclusion is that **He alone deserves to be worshipped, obeyed, and feared.** The bridge from Rububiyyah to Uluhiyyah is not a leap of faith. It is a step of logic. You've already given everything to Him in your words. All that remains is to give it in practice.

The spell is what keeps you on the near side of that step.

[PAUSE]

And this is why the three responses build the way they do — each one attempting to force the premise all the way to its conclusion, and each one being quietly refused.

*Will you not remember?* Al-Tabari reads the tadhakkur here as demanding a specific inference, not a general piety. *Will you not remember that the One who created them from nothing is the One capable of recreating them after their death — of restoring them as whole beings after their decay?* Creation → resurrection → accountability. The Meccans admitted the first premise. What they refused was the conclusion it demanded: that the Creator would call them back, and that on that day, the distance between what they had acknowledged and how they had lived would be on full display.

This is not a soft, interior feeling being asked for. It is a chain of reasoning. It runs: *you said Allah made everything from nothing. Then He can recreate what He has already made. Then He will judge.* The tadhakkur is the willingness to follow your own premise all the way to where it leads — and to let that end govern how you live today.

*Will you not then fear Him?* The taqwā here, al-Tabari notes, is pointed: will you not fear His punishment for your disbelief in Him and your rejection of what His messenger has brought? The Meccans have just admitted that Allah rules the seven heavens and the great Throne — the very realm their idols were supposed to navigate on their behalf. That admission should produce fear. Not vague unease but specific, urgent recognition: you have conceded the Lordship of the One whose instructions you are refusing, and the gap between that admission and your practice is not a private matter.

*Then how are you bewitched?* The first two responses still assumed you had a will that could be appealed to. The third has abandoned that assumption. Something is operating between your knowledge and your will. Something has gotten inside the mechanism.

[PAUSE]

It is worth pausing on what the Quran is and is not saying when it uses the word *siḥr* here.

The Prophet ﷺ's opponents had more than once accused *him* of being a sāḥir — a sorcerer who bewitched people into following him. The Quran's answer here is one of its sharpest reversals: the ones casting accusations of enchantment are themselves under the deepest enchantment of all. They are not neutral observers of a strange phenomenon. They are the patients.

But notice what kind of spell this is.

It is not described as dramatic, or exotic, or obviously supernatural. The spell the Quran names here is the human condition of **knowing without moving** — of having truth arrive in the mind and stay there, inert, producing nothing, changing nothing. The mechanism of conviction is intact. The object of conviction is correct. But somewhere between understanding and will, between the mind and the limbs, something has been cut.

Classical commentators describe the forces that do this cutting without romanticizing them. The love of worldly standing. The grip of ancestral custom. The fear of what the community will say. The comfort of familiar practice even when you know it is wrong. These are not exotic forces. They are the ordinary weights that press on the thread between what you know and what you do, until the thread snaps.

And what the Quran names this, with precise and almost clinical vocabulary, is *siḥr*. A spell. Something being done *to you* by forces you have allowed to settle in. Something that turns a person from an agent into an object — from someone who acts on truth to someone truth passes through without producing motion.

[PAUSE]

Now — and here is the pivot toward you — this is not a reflection about seventh-century Meccans.

These ayahs were revealed in Mecca. But they were preserved in a Book for everyone who comes after. And the question they carry is not about polytheism in the ancient world. It is about the structure of the human condition under any sky, in any century.

Do you know that Allah owns everything?

Yes.

Does that knowledge change what you are anxious about? Does it determine what you reach for when you are afraid? Does it shape the hour you are not sure what to do with? Does it free you from needing someone else's approval — because the only approval that will finally matter has already been admitted into your understanding?

If the honest answer is: *sometimes, but not always — not the way it should* — then the question these ayahs end with is being put to you too.

*Fa-annā tusḥarūn.*

How are *you* bewitched?

Can you name what sits between your knowledge of Allah and the way you actually live?

[PAUSE]

The invitation here is not guilt. The Quran's diagnosis is not accusation for its own sake.

Look at the verb again: *tusḥarūn* is passive. **You are being acted upon.** The text does not say you are a fool for being enchanted. It says you are enchanted. And the mercy in that framing is that a spell can be broken.

A spell breaks when you name it. When you stop calling the gap between your knowledge and your practice "normal" or "human" and start calling it what the Quran calls it — a condition that has a cause and can have a cure. When you go back to the premise — *Lillāh*, to Allah — and this time decide you will follow it all the way to where it leads.

The logic is already in your mouth. You have been saying it all along.

You just haven't followed it all the way home.

## Du'ā

*Rabbī, I know that to You belongs everything I can see and everything I cannot. I have said it. I say it still. But I confess that something sits between what I know and how I live — some attachment, some fear, some habit of turning away before the truth has reached my hands and my hours and my choices. Name the spell for me. Break it. Lead my knowledge all the way through, until what I admit with my mouth has entered my will and moved my life. You are the One who grants refuge, and none can be granted refuge against You. There is nowhere else to run. Take me all the way back to what I already know — and this time, let it change something.*

## Closing

These six ayahs do something that most religious appeals do not do.

They do not argue with the intellect. They do not threaten with punishment. They do not comfort with reassurance.

They **diagnose**.

They look at the gap between your stated beliefs and your lived reality and they name what is happening there with precision. Not ignorance. Not malice. Not stupidity.

*Bewitchment.*

And the naming is the beginning of the cure.

Because to be told you are under a spell is to be told: you are not broken at the core. You are not wicked beyond recovery. You are captured. And what has been captured can be freed.

The three questions of this passage are not arguments to win in debate. They are questions to *return to* — whenever the distance between your knowledge and your life becomes impossible to ignore. When you realize that you have been saying *Lillāh* with your mouth while your hands move in a different direction entirely.

Come back here.

Ask yourself: To whom belongs the earth and everyone in it?
Ask yourself: Who is Lord of the seven heavens and the great Throne?
Ask yourself: In whose hand is the malakūt of every single thing — the refuge, the sovereignty, the unseen dominion of all that is?

And when you hear the answer rise in you — the answer you already know, the answer you have always known —

This time, follow it.

Let it reach your limbs.

Let it change something.
