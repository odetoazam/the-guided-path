# The Du'a of the Blessed Arrival: Between Boarding and Landing

## Surah 23, Ayahs 28–30

---

### The Text

> [ayah:23:28] فَإِذَا ٱسْتَوَيْتَ أَنتَ وَمَن مَّعَكَ عَلَى ٱلْفُلْكِ فَقُلِ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى نَجَّىٰنَا مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ
>
> [ayah:23:29] وَقُل رَّبِّ أَنزِلْنِى مُنزَلًا مُّبَارَكًا وَأَنتَ خَيْرُ ٱلْمُنزِلِينَ
>
> [ayah:23:30] إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَـَٔايَـٰتٍ وَإِن كُنَّا لَمُبْتَلِينَ

> *"And when you have settled — you and those with you — upon the ship, say: 'All praise belongs to Allah, who has delivered us from the wrongdoing people.'*
>
> *And say: 'My Lord, let me alight at a blessed landing-place, and You are the best of those who bring to land.'*
>
> *Indeed, in that are signs — and We are ever testing."*

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuild the Scene

Stand for a moment where Nuh — peace be upon him — stands.

Nine hundred and fifty years of inviting his people. Nine hundred and fifty years of being mocked, dismissed, threatened, ignored. Then a command: *build a ship*. On dry land. With no river large enough to float it. The mockery sharpens. They pass by his construction site and laugh.

And now — finally — the sky is heavy. The earth begins to gush. He boards the ark with a small remnant of believers, two of every kind, and watches the world he tried for centuries to save begin to disappear under water.

You would expect, at this moment, one of two reactions. Either trembling fear at what is unfolding outside the hull. Or fierce vindication: *finally, they see I was right*.

Allah teaches Nuh neither.

[PAUSE]

He teaches him two acts of remembrance — a *hamd* for the moment he boards, and a du'a for the moment he lands. And then a single sentence that quietly rewrites the meaning of everything between those two moments.

### The Hook

Notice something strange before we even begin.

Allah does not teach Nuh a prayer for the *flood itself*. There is no supplication in this passage for the storm, for survival, for the destruction of his enemies. The drama everyone would expect a prayer about — the catastrophe — gets no prayer.

The hamd and the du'a Allah teaches him are for **boarding** and for **landing**.

Why? What is Allah saying about where the real test of a believer's life actually lives? And why — in a passage about the most dramatic rescue in human history — does He close with a sentence that says, almost casually: *and We are ever testing*?

That is what we are going to sit with.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *istawayta* — "When you have come to rest upon"

Let's begin with a word most translations slide past.

Allah does not say *"when you have boarded the ship"* or *"when you have entered the ark."* He says: **فَإِذَا ٱسْتَوَيْتَ** — *fa-idhā istawayta*.

Before we open the word, let me teach the concept it carries.

In Arabic, the root **س-و-ي** (s-w-y) carries the meaning of evenness — being level, being equal, being squared and balanced. From this root comes *sawiyy* (a sound, well-formed person), *musāwāh* (equality), and the verb *sawwā* (to make level, to put right, to finish off).

Form VIII of this root — *istawā* — adds reflexivity and intensification. It means: to come to rest in a settled, balanced state upon something. To assume a position of stable poise. It is not the verb of *getting on board*. It is the verb of *being securely settled*.

This is the same verb used when Allah describes Himself as having *istawā* upon the Throne. The same verb used for Nuh's ark when it finally rests on Mount Judi: *wastawat ʿalā al-jūdiyy* (11:44). The same verb used for the moon when it has come to its full balance.

Al-Tabari renders the phrase as *iʿtadalta fī al-safīna* — *you became balanced, level, within the ship*. Al-Muyassar adds that this settled state carries an implicit condition: *āminīn min al-gharaq* — safe from drowning. The moment of *istawayta* is not merely physical boarding; it is the moment when the door is sealed, the vessel holds, and the threat of the water beneath can no longer claim you. The hamd does not come at the first step onto the plank. It comes when the hull has proven itself.

Now look again at this ayah.

> فَإِذَا ٱسْتَوَيْتَ أَنتَ وَمَن مَّعَكَ عَلَى ٱلْفُلْكِ
> *"When you have come to rest — you and those with you — upon the ship..."*

Allah is not saying: *the moment you climb aboard, say the hamd*. He is saying: *the moment you are settled, balanced, at rest upon the ship — that is when you say it.*

Stay with me. This is doing real work.

There is a moment — every traveler knows it — between the chaos of departure and the calm of having actually left. The bags are stowed. The door is sealed. The vessel is moving. There is nothing more for you to do. **You are now in the hands of what carries you.**

Allah is saying: *that* is the moment of the *hamd*. Not when you are still loading. Not when you are still anxious about whether you will make it. The moment when the work is done and you are now riding what you cannot control.

[PAUSE]

This is precise. The believer's *al-ḥamdu lillāh* is not blurted out in panic. It is spoken from a place of *istiwā'* — settled, balanced, recognising who is now carrying you.

Notice something further. *Istawayta* is a singular verb addressed to Nuh — *you* (masculine singular) have settled. Then Allah adds: *anta wa-man maʿaka* — *you and whoever is with you*. He does not say *"you all have settled."* He says *"you have settled, you and those with you."*

The grammatical singular keeps Nuh in the foreground. **He is the one taught the remembrance. He is the one who carries the others into the language of gratitude.** Even on the ark, leadership is not loud. It is teaching the ones with you what to *say*.

### Element Two: *anzilnī munzalan mubārakan* — "Land me at a blessed landing"

Now move with me to the second ayah.

> وَقُل رَّبِّ أَنزِلْنِى مُنزَلًا مُّبَارَكًا

This is one of the most sonically dense phrases in the Quran. Listen to it: *anzil-nī munzal-an mubārak-an*. Three words, but the same root — **ن-ز-ل** (n-z-l) — sounds twice, and a second root that keeps the alliteration of "M" rolling through every word.

Let's open this slowly.

The root *n-z-l* means *to descend, to come down, to alight*. From it come some of the most theologically loaded words in the Quran: *anzala* (He sent down — used for revelation), *tanazzul* (gradual descent — used for the angels and the Quran), *manzil* (a stopping-place on a journey), and *nuzul* (the welcoming hospitality given to a guest who has just arrived).

When Nuh is taught to say *anzilnī*, he is not asking, "*let me get off the boat*." He is asking, "*cause me to descend, to alight, to be brought down*."

[PAUSE]

Notice the grammar. *Anzilnī* is form IV of the root — the **causative** form. Form I is *nazala* (he descended). Form IV is *anzala* (he caused to descend; he brought down). Nuh is not saying *I will land*. He is saying *You — Allah — bring me down*.

He is the *object* of the verb of his own arrival.

This is staggering. Nuh has just been saved from the most catastrophic event in human history. He could easily say, when he steps off the ark, *"I survived. I made it through."* Instead, Allah teaches him to recognise that even his arrival on dry land is something *Allah is doing to him*, not something he is doing.

He is not the one who lands. He is the one whom Allah lands.

Classical commentators are consistent on this. Al-Tabari, citing Mujahid directly, specifies that *munzalan mubārakan* was understood by the early exegetes as referring to the precise moment when Nuh would descend from the ship — *li-Nūḥin ḥīna nazala min al-safīna*. The du'a is not for the journey; it is for the threshold. It is spoken when the foot is about to touch the earth that was drowned and has now been given back.

Now look at the noun: *munzalan*. This is what the Arabic grammarians call a **maṣdar mīmī** — a verbal noun beginning with *m-* that simultaneously carries the meaning of the *act* of landing, the *place* of landing, and the *moment* of landing. The single word fuses all three. He is asking for a blessed *act* of being brought down, at a blessed *place*, at a blessed *time*.

And the qualifier: *mubārakan*. From the root **ب-ر-ك** (b-r-k). Linguistically, *baraka* originally referred to the way a camel kneels and stays put — *baraka al-baʿīr*. From this came the meaning of *that which has settled and remained, the lasting good that doesn't move on*.

So a *barakah* is not a flash. It is not a windfall. It is good that **stays**. Good that takes root. Good that you don't have to keep grasping at because it has knelt down beside you and chosen to remain.

When Nuh is taught to say *munzalan mubārakan*, he is asking: *bring me down at a place where the good stays. Where what You give doesn't evaporate. Where I don't just land safely but where the landing itself becomes a beginning that lasts.*

[PAUSE]

Now hear it whole.

> رَّبِّ أَنزِلْنِى مُنزَلًا مُّبَارَكًا وَأَنتَ خَيْرُ ٱلْمُنزِلِينَ
> *"My Lord, alight me at a blessed alighting — and You are the best of alighters."*

The phrase ends with *khayru-l-munzilīn*. *Munzilīn* is the active participle of the same form IV verb: *the ones who cause to land, the ones who bring down*. Allah says: *anta khayru-l-munzilīn* — **You are the best of those who land others.**

The implication is delicate but profound: there are many things in a person's life that can "land them" somewhere — circumstances, decisions, other people, fortune, calculation. Many forces *bring you down* to wherever you end up. The believer asks not just *"land me well,"* but: *"YOU land me — because You are the best of all the things that could land me."*

Every other "lander" lands you somewhere conditional. Allah is the only one who lands you in *barakah* — in good that stays.

Al-Muyassar draws the scope of this du'a wide: the instruction belongs not only to Nuh but is a teaching from Allah for His servants — *taʿlīm min Allāh li-ʿibādihi* — that when they alight, when they arrive anywhere, they should say this. The prayer was given to a prophet in a moment of world-historical proportion, but the scholars read it as prescription, not only as history. Every believer stepping off every vessel — every arrival at every new place — is invited into this du'a. Allah taught a prophet and preserved the words in scripture so that generations who never saw an ark could still arrive, every time, asking to be landed in good that stays.

### Element Three: *wa-in kunnā la-mubtalīn* — "And We are ever testing"

Now to the third ayah, the divine commentary that recasts everything.

> إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَـَٔايَـٰتٍ وَإِن كُنَّا لَمُبْتَلِينَ

Two halves. Two emphatic constructions. Two layers of meaning.

The first: *inna fī dhālika la-āyāt* — *"indeed in that are signs."* Standard Quranic emphasis: *inna* (indeed) + *la-* (the lām of emphasis) + *āyāt* (signs, plural, indefinite — *signs of every kind*).

The second is where the linguistic work lives: **وَإِن كُنَّا لَمُبْتَلِينَ**.

Here we need to teach a small grammatical point first.

In Arabic, the particle *in* (إن — short, no shadda) usually means *"if"*. But there is another *in* — the **muḳaffafa min al-thaqīla** — the "lightened" form of *inna* (إنّ — emphatic "indeed"). Grammarians know it from one telltale sign: when *in* appears alongside the *lām* of emphasis (*la-*), it is no longer the conditional *if*. It is the lightened form of *indeed*.

Look at the structure: *wa-in kunnā **la**-mubtalīn*. The *lām* is there. So this is not, "*and if We have been testers*." It is: **"And indeed, We have most certainly been ever-testing."**

[PAUSE]

This is not a hypothetical. It is not Allah saying "if We were the kind to test, We are." It is Allah declaring a divine constant.

Now let's open *mubtalīn*. Active participle, form VIII, from the root **ب-ل-و** (b-l-w). The form I verb *balā* means *to test, to try, to examine, to wear down through use* — the same root used to describe how a garment becomes *bāli*, threadbare, after long use. Testing in Arabic is not just an exam. It is the *wear pattern* of a thing — what reveals its true nature through repeated use.

Form VIII — *ibtalā* — adds the sense of *deliberate, sustained subjection to test*. The active participle *mubtalī* therefore means: *one who is constantly, deliberately putting things through trial to reveal what they truly are*.

And the form is plural — *mubtalīn* — using the plural of majesty: **We are ever-testers.**

Now hear the full second half: *"And indeed, We have most certainly been the ones constantly subjecting to trial."*

This is one sentence. After the most cinematic rescue scene in scripture. After teaching Nuh how to praise on boarding and how to ask for blessing on landing. After the mockers have just been swept beneath the waves. Allah closes with: **and We are ever testing.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Three linguistic moves, building one revelation:

*Istawayta* told us: the believer's gratitude is spoken from a place of settled rest, not panicked relief. *Anzilnī munzalan mubārakan* told us: the arrival is something Allah does to us; we ask not just to land safely, but to land in good that stays. *Wa-in kunnā la-mubtalīn* told us: the entire scene — boarding, riding, landing — sits inside a divine constant called testing.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a believing life is actually shaped.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Hamd and the Du'a of Transition — and the One Allah Did Not Teach

You have probably noticed it without naming it. There are seasons in a person's life where the ground feels firm, and there are seasons where it feels like flood. But there is a third kind of season people rarely talk about: **the in-between**. The transition. The leaving-but-not-yet-arrived.

You have left a job but the new one hasn't started. You have left a relationship but you haven't yet arrived in yourself. You have been delivered from a sickness but you haven't yet found out who you are without it. You have moved out of a house, a city, a country, a stage of life — but the new home has not yet become home.

These are the *ark* seasons. You are no longer where you were. You are not yet where you will be. **You are riding something you cannot steer.**

Now look at what Allah teaches Nuh.

A hamd and a du'a. One for the moment of *boarding* — when the leaving has just happened. One for the moment of *landing* — when the arriving is about to happen. **Nothing for the middle.**

Why?

The hamd Nuh is taught for boarding is *al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī najjānā* — praise that speaks backward. It names what has been left behind: *the wrongdoing people*. You were there. Now you are not. *Before you look forward to where you are going, name what you have been saved from.* This is not triumphalism — it is theological orientation. Before a person can ask for a blessed arrival, they must first recognize that they are no longer in the place that could not hold them.

The du'a for landing speaks forward. *Rabbi anzilnī munzalan mubārakan* — do not let what I step into be ordinary. Do not let me arrive at the bare minimum. Let the place I am being put down become a place where good takes root and remains.

And between these two? Nothing. No prayer for the storm. No petition during the flood. **The hamd marks the departure; the du'a marks the arrival; the transit belongs to Allah.**

[PAUSE]

This is a profound spiritual architecture. We live in a world that wants us praying hardest in the middle — petitioning through the crisis, bargaining during the storm. And of course, supplication in hardship is part of the believer's life. But what Allah teaches Nuh about the ark suggests something deeper: the *orientation* of the believer's remembrance is not primarily toward crisis management. It is toward **proper marking of beginnings and endings**.

Name what you are leaving, with gratitude that you are leaving it. Name where you are arriving, with the understanding that where you land is something Allah is doing to you. The middle — which feels like the whole — is already held by His decree. It does not need your verbal framing. It needs your trust.

### Theme Two: What You Were Saved *From*

Look carefully at the specific words of the hamd Nuh is taught:

> الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي نَجَّانَا مِنَ الْقَوْمِ الظَّالِمِينَ

*"All praise to Allah who has saved us from the wrongdoing people."*

Not: *from the flood*. Not: *from the water*. Not: *from death*.

From **the wrongdoing people**.

Al-Tabari is explicit: *al-ẓālimīn* here means *al-mushrikīn* — the polytheists, those who gave to others what belongs only to Allah. Al-Muyassar reads the same: *al-kāfirīn* — those who covered over the truth. The classical scholars are not contradicting one another; they are converging. The *ẓulm* of these people was not merely moral failing — it was the most fundamental distortion: refusing to place the divine where it belongs, and building an entire world on that refusal.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the hamd differently.

Nuh is not saved from a storm. He is saved from a civilization that had — over nine hundred and fifty years — become structurally oriented away from God. The water does not drown the wrongdoers arbitrarily; it marks the end of a world built on *ẓulm* as its foundation. And Nuh's rescue is not escape from physical danger. It is emergence from an entire *way of inhabiting the world*.

This shapes what the gratitude is *for*.

When you say *al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī najjānā min al-qawmi-l-ẓālimīn*, you are naming: Allah has moved me out of a situation that was doing damage not merely to my comfort, but to my orientation. A person can be rescued from a harmful relationship and the gratitude is real. A person can be rescued from a culture of cynicism, from a community of despair, from an environment of spiritual distortion — and the gratitude runs deeper still. It is gratitude not merely for safety, but for *rectification of direction*.

Notice, too, the verb: **نَجَّىٰنَا** — *najjānā*. The root *n-j-w* means to come out clearly, to emerge from what had ensnared. It is not passive survival. It is a positive emergence into clarity. The Arabic grammarians note that *najjā* (Form II) carries the intensive, completed sense of the rescue — not "He allowed us to escape" but "He fully extracted us, He brought us out clear." When Allah teaches Nuh to say *najjānā* on the ark, He is teaching him to recognize his rescue not merely as escape from punishment but as emergence into a clearer existence.

Nuh praises Allah *before the ark even moves*. He does not wait to see land appear on the horizon. He praises on *boarding* — from the moment of *istawā'*, settled rest upon the vessel. Because even before the landing is known, the leaving is already a gift.

### Theme Three: *Wa-in kunnā la-mubtalīn* — When the Declaration Follows the Story

There is something almost unsettling about the third ayah when you hear it in sequence.

Read the passage as a narrative arc: Nuh boards — *al-ḥamdu lillāh*; the mockers are drowned; he is given the du'a of the blessed landing; the journey of the ark across the world's last catastrophe — and then, as the frame pulls back from this entire epic: **and We are most certainly the ever-testing.**

The sentence is in the first-person plural — Allah speaking about Himself. It is placed after the rescue, not before the trial. It does not arrive as warning; it arrives as *explanation*.

[PAUSE]

Why after? Why not before, as motivation for Nuh to endure?

Because it is not addressed to Nuh. It is addressed to *you*. The reader. The one who just witnessed the whole arc — the years of rejection, the mockery, the ark, the flood, the hamd, the du'a of the landing. Having seen all of that, you are now given the interpretive key: **none of that was exceptional. All of it was ibtilā'. And ibtilā' is what We do. Always.**

The Quran says elsewhere: *alladhī khalaqa al-mawta wa-l-ḥayāta li-yabluwakum ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalā* — "Who created death and life to test you as to which of you is best in deed" (67:2). Death and life themselves are instruments of the divine testing. Nuh's nine hundred and fifty years and the flood and the rescue are not outside this framework. They are its most vivid illustration.

What does this mean for you, standing in your ark season?

It means: the test does not end when you land. A *munzal mubārak* — a blessed landing — is not release from *ibtilā'*. It is *ibtilā'* in its most subtle form. You are now tested by good that stays.

[PAUSE]

This is what the third ayah is quietly delivering. The flood was testing by hardship. The landing — with its barakah, its settled good, its community, its fresh beginning — is testing by ease. And Allah is *mubtalī* in both registers. The active participle does not pause. There is no part of a believer's life — not the ark, not the shore — that stands outside the divine economy of trial and revelation.

But the sentence is not oppressive. It is clarifying. Once you know that testing is the texture rather than the interruption, you stop waiting for life to *stop being a test* before you begin living it properly. The hamd on boarding becomes possible even before you know where the ark is going. The du'a for the landing becomes possible even before you can see the shore.

## PART 3 — THE LIVING PRACTICE

### For Every Arrival

Al-Muyassar, having grounded this du'a in the specific context of Nuh stepping off the ark, draws the instruction wide: this is a teaching from Allah for His servants — that when they alight, when they arrive anywhere, they should say this.

This is the move from history to practice that the Quran consistently makes.

The words were given to a prophet in the most consequential moment of embarkation in human memory. But they were preserved in the Book not as archaeology — as the record of what someone once said — but as *instruction*. Allah records Nuh's du'a so that you, stepping off a plane in a city where you know no one, or finishing a degree that cost you years of your life, or walking out of a hospital after weeks of illness, or entering a new role that terrifies you — so that *you* have something to say when your foot touches the new ground.

*Rabbi anzilnī munzalan mubārakan wa-anta khayru-l-munzilīn.*

My Lord, let me alight at a blessed alighting. And You are the best of those who bring me down.

[PAUSE]

Notice what the du'a does to the moment of arrival.

Without it, an arrival is just a logistical fact. The plane landed. The job started. The semester began. With it — with the du'a said deliberately, from a place of *istiwā'*, of settled recognition — the arrival becomes a threshold. You are naming: *I did not navigate here by my own calculation. I was brought. And I am asking the One who brought me to make the landing count.*

This is not a formula for guaranteed success. It is a reorientation of the self at a moment of change. The du'a is the act of recognising, before the first full day begins, that the next chapter is something Allah is doing to you — and that you are asking Him to do it well.

### For Every Departure

And the hamd on boarding applies equally.

When you have come to rest upon a new vessel — when you have left something and are now riding the leaving — before you look forward to where you are going, name what you have been saved from. Not with bitterness. Not with triumphalism. With simple theological clarity: *Al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī najjānā* — all praise to Allah who has saved us.

The ones on Nuh's ark were a small community. The people they had been among — who had mocked, who had turned away — were not strangers. They were neighbors, family, the only world Nuh and his companions had known. There was grief in the departure, certainly. But there was also a recognition that cannot be spoken except in *hamd*: **what I am being saved from was doing me damage I could not repair myself.**

[PAUSE]

The word *najjānā* carries this weight. To be *najjā*-ed is to be extracted from an ensnaring — brought out into clarity from a situation whose distortion you may not even have fully seen while you were inside it. When Allah teaches Nuh to say *najjānā* on the ark, He is teaching him to recognize the rescue not merely as escape from punishment but as emergence into a clearer existence: clearer about who Allah is, clearer about what the world is for, clearer about what it means to inhabit it faithfully.

This is what the hamd of departure is: *I am clearer now than I was. The place I was in was ensnaring me — in ẓulm, in misalignment, in a world built on the wrong foundation. And I am being carried out of it. Al-ḥamdu lillāh.*

### The Shape of a Believing Life in Transit

Here, then, is the full architecture these three ayahs reveal.

Every ark season — every transition — has a theology:

**At boarding**: speak *hamd* from a place of settled rest. Name what you have been saved from. Don't scramble for the words — the Quran has given you the words. Say them from *istawā'*, from balance, not from panic.

**In the middle**: the transit belongs to Allah. The flood is in His hands. You were not given a supplication for the storm because the storm is not the point. Your task in the middle is not to narrate it but to ride it with the trust of one who has already spoken the hamd on one end and the du'a on the other.

**At landing**: speak the du'a before you step off. Ask to be brought down into good that stays. Ask the best of all landers to be the one who lands you. Not as magic, but as the deliberate act of a person who knows that arrival is not something they achieve — it is something Allah does to them.

And underneath all of it: *wa-in kunnā la-mubtalīn*. The testing never stops. This is not despair — it is the most orienting sentence in the passage. Because once you know that testing is the constant, you stop being surprised by it. The hamd on boarding, the du'a on landing, the quiet trust in the middle — these are not exceptional acts for exceptional circumstances. They are the believer's normal posture in a life that is, always and certainly, an *ibtilā'*.

## CLOSING

### Between Two Acts of Remembrance

Nuh — peace be upon him — steps onto the ark in a world that is ending. He steps off the ark into a world that is beginning again. Between those two moments: nine hundred and fifty years of prophecy on one side, and whatever remained of his life on the other.

Allah teaches him to mark both moments.

Not the storm between them. Just the boarding. Just the landing.

Because the one who marks the boarding in *hamd* and asks for the landing in *du'a* has already placed the entire transit — the flood, the uncertainty, the long riding of what cannot be steered — in the hands of the One who is *khayru-l-munzilīn*. The best of all landers.

You will have ark seasons. You may be in one now. And if you are: you have been given, in these three ayahs, everything you need to inhabit it faithfully.

Say the *hamd* when you board. Ask for the *munzal mubārak* when you land.

And in between — trust the Carrier.
