# Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 19-20

## The Tree from Mount Sinai: When Provision Becomes Light

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

فَأَنشَأْنَا لَكُم بِهِ جَنَّـٰتٍۢ مِّن نَّخِيلٍۢ وَأَعْنَـٰبٍۢ لَّكُمْ فِيهَا فَوَٰكِهُ كَثِيرَةٌۭ وَمِنْهَا تَأْكُلُونَ ۝ وَشَجَرَةًۭ تَخْرُجُ مِن طُورِ سَيْنَآءَ تَنۢبُتُ بِٱلدُّهْنِ وَصِبْغٍۢ لِّلْـَٔاكِلِينَ

**Transliteration:**

*Fa-anshaʾnā lakum bihi jannātin min nakhīlin wa-aʿnāb. Lakum fīhā fawākihu kathīratun wa-minhā taʾkulūn. Wa-shajaratan takhruju min ṭūri Saynāʾa tanbutu bi-d-duhni wa-ṣibghin lil-ākilīn.*

**Translation:**

*"And by means of it We brought forth for you gardens of palms and grapevines — in which for you are abundant fruits, and from them you eat. And a tree, issuing from Mount Sinai, which grows producing oil — and a dipping for those who eat."*

---

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine you have just been told, in the previous ayah, that the rain falling on you is not random. It is *measured.* Every drop is on a register. Allah said: "And We sent down water from the sky in measure, and We settled it in the earth — and We are surely able to take it away."

That last phrase is heavy. *We are able to take it away.* The water that runs your fields, your mouths, your blood — it is on loan. He could call it back.

And then, immediately, before you have time to recover from that warning, He says: *fa-anshaʾnā* — "and by means of it, We caused to grow up..."

He is showing you what He does with what He could take away. He is showing you abundance.

Notice the rhetorical structure. Ayah 18 ends with a threat. Ayah 19 opens with a gift. The threat exists not to terrify you but to make the gift legible. *You did not earn the gardens. They are mercy folded into measured water.*

But what we want to sit with today is not the agriculture itself. It is what Allah does in the very next breath. Because after listing palms and grapes — the staples, the named, the plural — He pauses. The list could have ended there. It would have been a complete passage about food. Instead, He adds one more thing. A single tree. Unnamed. From a mountain He bothers to identify by name.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Why, in a passage about provision, does Allah suddenly mention **Mount Sinai** — the mountain of Musa's revelation? Why does He name the mountain but not the tree? And why is the tree's gift not just food, but **oil**?

Stay with that question. Because the answer reorganizes how we see the entire created world.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Fa-anshaʾnā* — The Drama of What You Cannot See

Let me teach you something about Arabic verb forms before we touch the word.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns. The simplest is Form I — the bare action. *Nabata* — it grew. Plain growth. But Allah does not say *nabata* here. He uses Form IV. *Anshaʾa.* And Form IV adds something that doesn't translate cleanly into English: it is **causative and originative.** It means *to cause to come into being from a state of non-being.* It is the verb of bringing into existence.

Think about the difference. *Nabata* describes what a plant does. *Anshaʾa* describes what was done to it before it was a plant. The seed in soil. The invisible cellular machinery. The decision that *this* will become a date palm and *that* will become a grapevine. The drama happens before there is anything to look at.

Now look at what Allah says: *fa-anshaʾnā lakum bihi* — *We originated for you, by means of it (the water)...*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The verb is in the past tense. *We brought into being.* But the gardens are still here. The palms are still standing. So why past tense?

Because the act of origination is **finished**. The miracle has already happened. You are walking around inside the result. You eat the fruit of a decree that was issued before you ever existed. Every grape on every vine is the leftover of a divine sentence already spoken.

This is the way Allah teaches you to read creation. Not as ongoing chaos, not as physics generating itself, but as the **visible aftermath of an invisible decree.** What you see is the trailing edge of *anshaʾnā.*

And then — *lakum* — *for you.* Not for itself. Not for the soil. Not for biology. *For you.*

Stay with how strange that is. The plural address. *Lakum* — for the collective "you." The grapevine in a field you have never visited, that fed someone whose name you will never know — was, in some sense, *for you.* The economy of provision is a single household, and you are inside it.

So in one verb, *anshaʾnā*, Allah has done three things:
- He has named Himself the originator, not just the cause
- He has told you the act is complete, the world you see is the receipt
- He has tied the gift to *you* personally, even if you weren't there when the seed went in

What this single verb invites us to consider is that **everything you see growing is the past tense of a divine sentence whose subject is "We" and whose indirect object is you.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Wa Shajaratan* — The Pivot of "AND"

Now we come to the gem.

Let me teach you something about Arabic lists before we touch the word. When Allah lists, He has a particular precision. Items joined with **wa-** ("and") are sometimes equal members of a category, and sometimes — when the *wa-* shifts the rhythm — they are something else: a category-break. A turn.

Look at how the previous ayah moved:
- gardens
- of palms
- and grapevines
- in which are fruits
- abundant
- and from them you eat

That is one long, smooth list of agriculture. Plural, definite, abundant, named.

Then ayah 20 begins: *wa shajaratan.* **"And a tree."**

Notice three things about this break:

**First, it is singular.** *Shajaratan.* One tree. After the plural cascade — palms, grapevines, fruits — Allah pivots to a single tree. He has zoomed in.

**Second, it is indefinite.** *Shajaratan*, with the *tanwīn* — *a tree.* Not *the tree.* Not *the olive tree.* He does not name it.

**Third, the noun has been pulled out of the *min* construction.** The grapes and palms came in a "from" structure — *jannātin min nakhīlin wa aʿnāb* — "gardens *from* palms and grapes." But the tree is not "*from* the tree." It is grammatically standing alone, in the accusative, attached to the same verb *anshaʾnā.* The tree is being **set apart** from the gardens.

[PAUSE]

What is happening here?

Allah is signaling: *this one is different.* The previous list was about bulk provision. Calorie food. Volume. The thing that fills stomachs. He named those plants because their identity is in their species: a date is a date, a grape is a grape, you know what to do with them.

But this one — He doesn't tell you what species it is. He tells you what mountain it comes from.

And that is the linguistic shock. **In a passage about plants, Allah identifies a tree not by its botany but by its geography of revelation.**

What an Arabic reader hears in *wa shajaratan takhruju min ṭūri Saynāʾ* is not "and a tree from a mountain." They hear: *and a tree from THE mountain* — *ṭūr Saynāʾ* — the mountain Allah has named in His own speech repeatedly. The mountain where Musa was called. The mountain where the Tablets came down. The mountain whose name is sworn upon at the opening of Surat At-Tin: *wa-ṭūri sīnīn.*

The tree is unnamed because the mountain is the name.

[PAUSE]

So the linguistic move is this: Allah took a list of food and quietly slipped into it the mountain of revelation. He didn't say "and revelation also nourishes you." He said something stranger and more powerful — *a tree comes out of the mountain where revelation came down, and that tree gives you oil.*

He has collapsed two landscapes that we keep separate. The agricultural landscape — the visible world of soil and rain and harvest. And the revelatory landscape — the unseen world of prophets receiving words on mountaintops. He is telling you: **they are the same landscape.** The same mountain that birthed the Tablets birthed the tree whose oil burns in your lamps.

What this construction invites us to consider is that there is no neutral geography in the Quranic worldview. The earth is layered. The same soil that grows your food is the soil where prophets stood. *Sacred history is not a story that happened somewhere else. It happened in the dirt under your feet.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Tanbutu bi-d-Duhni* — The Tree That Grows Its Own Light

Let me teach you about a small Arabic preposition — *bi-* — before we touch the phrase.

The letter *bā'* in Arabic is one of the most loaded particles in the language. It can mean *with*, *by means of*, *in*, *because of.* In a phrase like *tanbutu bi-d-duhn*, it can be read at least two ways, and the classical scholars hold both.

Reading one: **the *bā'* is *bā' al-musāḥaba* — the *bā'* of accompaniment.** Then *tanbutu bi-d-duhn* means *it grows along with its oil* — the oil is **not separate from the growth.** The tree does not grow first and then, later, get squeezed for oil. The oil is *part of how it grows.* The tree is, from its first cell, an oil tree.

Reading two: **the *bā'* is *bā' al-ta'diya* — the *bā'* that turns the verb transitive.** Then *tanbutu bi-d-duhn* means *it grows producing oil* — the oil is **what it grows.** The fruit is itself a vessel of oil; the tree's growth is oil-making.

Notice that both readings are working toward the same destination: **this tree's nature is oil.** The oil is not a side product. It is what the tree IS.

This matters. Because in the Quran, *duhn* — oil, fat, the unctuous substance — is connected to two things: **light** and **healing.** Olive oil for centuries was what burned in lamps. It was what was rubbed on wounds. It was what anointed kings and priests. *Duhn* is the substance that, when you add it to the world, the world becomes brighter and softer.

Now hear the next phrase: *wa ṣibghin lil-ākilīn.* "**And a *ṣibgh* for those who eat.**"

The word *ṣibgh* literally means **dye.** A coloring agent. Something that, when you add it, **transforms whatever it touches.** Linguistically, classical lexicographers note: *ṣibgh* is what you dip your bread in — the oil and seasoning that *colors* the food, that gives it flavor and richness it didn't have on its own.

So the tree's gift has two functions:
- *duhn* — oil that lights
- *ṣibgh* — dipping that transforms

And both functions are about **changing what the oil touches.** The lamp without the oil is dark. The bread without the dipping is plain. Add the oil — and there is light. Add the dipping — and there is flavor.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah just did. He compared two trees in two ayahs.

The palm and the grape: their gift is themselves. You eat the date. You eat the grape. The fruit is the food.

The tree from Mt. Sinai: its gift is **what it adds to other things.** Its oil lights *other* things. Its dipping transforms *other* foods. The tree's purpose is not to be eaten as itself — it is to make *everything else* brighter and richer.

[PAUSE]

This is the load-bearing observation. *Some provisions sustain you in themselves. Others change the quality of everything they touch.* Allah grew both.

What this construction invites us to consider is that creation is not flat. Not all blessings are the same kind of blessing. Some fill you. Others **illuminate** you. And the ones that illuminate come, in this passage, from the mountain of revelation.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have three linguistic discoveries:

- *anshaʾnā* — the past-tense verb that tells us what we see is the receipt of an already-issued decree
- *wa shajaratan* — the pivot from named, plural, abundant gardens to a single, unnamed, set-apart tree from the mountain of revelation
- *tanbutu bi-d-duhn wa ṣibghin* — a tree whose nature is oil, whose gift is to transform everything it touches

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Kinds of Provision — Calorie and Light

Let me start with something you already know.

You have, in your kitchen, two kinds of food. There is the food that fills you up — bread, rice, meat, the foundational stuff your stomach needs. Without it, you starve. And there is the food that *flavors* — salt, oil, herbs, the things that make the foundational food edible. You can survive without the second category. But meals without it are joyless. The first keeps you alive. The second makes life worth being alive in.

Now zoom out. Your life has the same two categories.

There is what you need to *survive*: a job, a place to sleep, food, basic relationships, physical safety. Without it, you cannot live. And there is what you need to *thrive*: meaning, beauty, contemplation, prayer, the kind of love that makes you weep, the kind of moment when an ayah lands and rearranges your week. You can survive without the second category. People do. They live for decades on calorie alone. But the years feel grey. The body is fed and the soul is starving.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is mapping that exact distinction onto creation itself.

The palms and the grapes — *jannātin min nakhīlin wa aʿnāb* — these are the calorie. The dates feed Bedouins through deserts. The grapes provide sugar, water, sustenance. They are abundant — *fawākihu kathīrah.* They are plural. They fill you up. *Wa minhā taʾkulūn* — and from them you eat. The verb is direct. You eat. You are filled.

The tree from Sinai — *wa shajaratan* — this is something else. Its oil lights lamps. Its dipping transforms bread. It is not the foundation; it is what makes the foundation **mean** something.

And here is the staggering thing: **Allah grew both.**

He didn't only give you what you need to survive. He gave you what you need to *feel alive.* The same hand that watered the grapevine also planted, on the slope of a sacred mountain, a tree whose specific job is to make your nights have light and your bread have flavor.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment. Because most of us have a theology of provision that stops at the calorie. We thank Allah for our food, our health, our income — the things that keep us breathing. We are correct to do this. But we miss something.

The tree from Sinai is in this passage to tell you: **Allah's care for you extends past your survival.** He cares whether your evenings are dark or lit. He cares whether your food is grey or flavored. He cares whether your soul is fed, not just your body.

The believers earlier in this surah were described in seven attributes — humility in prayer, turning from idle speech, giving zakāh, guarding chastity, keeping trusts, guarding prayers. Notice: those are not survival behaviors. Those are **flavor behaviors.** They are the *ṣibgh* of the soul. They take a life that is technically functioning and color it with meaning.

The structure of the surah is being mapped onto the structure of creation. Just as Allah made gardens for your stomach AND the tree for your light — He made laws for your survival AND practices for your soul. The believer is the one who eats from both trees.

**Practical application:** Look at your week. Are you eating only from the palms and grapes — the survival food, the necessary work, the obligations? Or are you also reaching for the oil from Mt. Sinai — the contemplative practice, the unhurried prayer, the time spent inside an ayah, the reflection that doesn't "do" anything but slowly transforms how you see everything else? Both grew from the same rain. Both are halal. Both are gifts. But one of them is what gives the other its flavor.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Geography Underneath Your Feet

Let me tell you about something most people misunderstand about the Quran.

When the Quran names places — Mt. Sinai, the valley of Bakkah, the cave of Thawr, the lote tree at the boundary — most of us treat those names as historical labels. *That happened back then, over there.* Sinai is in Egypt. The cave is in Mecca. The lote tree is in another dimension. We file them away as set pieces from sacred history.

This ayah is doing something very different.

When Allah says *takhruju min ṭūri Saynāʾ* — "issuing from Mount Sinai" — He is putting Mt. Sinai inside a list of trees you eat from. He is not saying, *let me tell you something about ancient history.* He is saying: *the mountain where revelation came down is also the mountain whose tree feeds you right now.*

Stay with how strange this is.

The same mountain on which Musa, peace be upon him, stood when Allah spoke to him — that mountain has soil. That soil grows plants. One of those plants is the olive tree. That olive tree produces oil. That oil is what burned in the lamps of believers for fourteen hundred years. That oil is what is being burned, in some house tonight, while a Muslim reads this ayah.

**Sacred history did not stop. It is still happening, in you, every time you eat.**

[PAUSE]

This is what istidlāl al-maʿqūl bi-l-maḥsūs is doing here — using the perceptible to point to the imperceptible. The physical olive tree is the stepping stone. The unseen reality is this: *the world you live in is a single, layered text where revelation, geography, food, and light are all manuscripts of the same Author.*

Most worldviews split these. Science describes the agriculture. Religion describes the revelation. History describes the mountain. Theology describes the meaning. Each discipline has its own building, its own department, its own jargon, and they don't talk to each other.

The Quran is collapsing the partition. It is saying: **the mountain is the mountain.** It is one mountain. It received revelation, and it grows oil, and the oil lights homes where revelation is read, and there is no seam between these things because there is one Author.

Now think about what this does to how you see your environment.

The bread you eat was watered by rain that was measured. The oil you dip it in came, in some ancient sense, from a mountain whose other gift was the words now on your shelf. The lamp you read by, even if it is now an LED, descends from a tradition of olive lamps that lit mosques and homes for a thousand years. The very reading you do at night is wrapped inside a chain of objects that all lead back, in this ayah, to a single mountain on which Musa stood.

[PAUSE]

There is a phrase that emerges from this: **sacred geography.** The earth is not flat in the moral sense. Some places are more saturated with meaning than others. Some seeds carry more than calories. Some oils carry more than fuel. The ayah is teaching you to read the world the way Allah reads it — as a unified composition where every detail rhymes with every other.

What changes when you start to see the world this way?

You stop being a tourist in your own life. You stop thinking, *I'm just eating breakfast,* or *I'm just lighting a lamp,* or *I'm just walking to work.* Every act becomes a rhyme inside a larger poem. The bread is the rain is the cloud is the wind is the order is the Lord. The oil is the tree is the mountain is the prophet is the revelation is the Lord.

The believer is the one who has learned to read the earth itself as a layered ayah.

**Practical application:** This week, when you eat, pause for a single second before the first bite — and ask: *what is the chain of objects and choices that put this in front of me?* Not as a guilt trip, not as exhaustive bookkeeping. Just as a tiny act of seeing. Bread → wheat → soil → rain → cloud → atmosphere → sun → divine measurement. The chain runs all the way back. Allah did not need to tell you this. He told you anyway, by sliding Mt. Sinai into a list of food, so you would learn to see.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at what Allah has built across these two ayahs.

**Ayah 19 — the Garden:**
- Plural, named, abundant
- *Lakum* — for you
- Direct consumption: *wa minhā taʾkulūn*
- The gift is the fruit itself

**Ayah 20 — the Tree:**
- Singular, unnamed, set apart
- *Min ṭūri Saynāʾ* — from a mountain that is named
- Indirect transformation: oil for lighting, dipping for flavoring
- The gift is what it does to *other* things

Two ayahs, two trees, two kinds of provision. The first feeds your survival. The second feeds the part of you that needs to see in the dark and taste the flavor underneath the flavor.

And the entire surah, *Al-Muʾminūn,* is structured the same way. It opens with the believers — *qad aflaḥa l-muʾminūn* — those who have already succeeded. And then it lists not their food but their *ṣibgh* — humility, restraint, trust, prayer. The qualities that color a life.

Then it describes creation — water, gardens, palms, grapes, and the tree from Sinai. The agricultural list mirrors the spiritual list. **The believers are the ones who eat from both trees.**

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Anshaʾnā* told us that the world we see is the trailing edge of a finished decree. Theme one took that further: that decree included not just what you need to live but what you need to feel alive.

*Wa shajaratan* set apart a single tree from the gardens. Theme two took that further: the tree's location — Mt. Sinai — is not decorative. It is collapsing the partition between the agricultural world and the revelatory world.

*Tanbutu bi-d-duhn wa ṣibghin* told us this tree's nature is to transform what it touches. Both themes take that further: **revelation is the oil of the soul.** It lights what was dark. It flavors what was plain. It does not replace your bread — it makes your bread mean something.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time you ate from the *palm and grape* tree — the survival tree — but neglected the *oil* tree, the contemplative tree that flavors everything? What did your week feel like in that period?

2. If Allah grew the oil tree on the same mountain where He spoke to Musa, what does that tell you about how He arranged your own life? Where, in the geography of your days, has He placed the trees of light?

3. *Ṣibgh* means "dye" — a coloring agent. What is the *ṣibgh* of your spiritual life? What practice colors everything else? And if you cannot name one, what does that absence mean?

4. The grape and palm are named because their identity is in their species. The tree from Sinai is unnamed because its identity is in its mountain. Where in your life have you confused species for source — focused on the form of a practice and forgotten where it actually came from?

5. The same rain made both trees. What might it mean to thank Allah, with the same breath, for what fills your body and what lights your soul?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah did not only give you food — He grew, on the very mountain of revelation, a tree whose oil would light your nights and flavor your bread, so that you would learn that survival and meaning come from the same hand.

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this is small but significant.

You do not need to overhaul your life. You need to add one thing to the way you see it. The next time you read the Quran by lamplight — even an electric one — let your mind walk back: oil, tree, mountain, revelation, Lord. The chain is short. It only takes a second. But that second is the difference between eating breakfast and eating an ayah.

The next time you sit down to a meal, notice what is foundation — the bread, the rice — and notice what is *ṣibgh* — the oil, the salt, the herbs that color it. Then ask: in the rest of my life, do I have *ṣibgh*? Do I have practices that color my hours? If not, that absence is not a moral failure. It is an invitation. The tree from Sinai is still there. The oil is still being grown. You only need to reach.

### A Du'a Emerging from These Words

Yā Allah, You who originated the gardens before we ever knew to ask for them — let us not eat without seeing. Yā Allah, You who grew the tree from the mountain of revelation, You who joined the soil of survival to the soil of light — make us among those who eat from both trees. Color our days with the *ṣibgh* of remembrance, and let the oil of Your speech burn in our hearts the way it has burned in the lamps of the believers before us. Āmīn.
