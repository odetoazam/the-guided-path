# The Guardians, the Door, and What Lies Beyond

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Verses 5–7

> **وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ لِفُرُوجِهِمْ حَـٰفِظُونَ ۝ إِلَّا عَلَىٰٓ أَزْوَٰجِهِمْ أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَـٰنُهُمْ فَإِنَّهُمْ غَيْرُ مَلُومِينَ ۝ فَمَنِ ٱبْتَغَىٰ وَرَآءَ ذَٰلِكَ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْعَادُونَ**

*"And those who are guardians over their private parts — except with their spouses or those their right hands possess, for in that they are not blameworthy. But whoever seeks beyond that — those are the transgressors."*

---

## Introduction: A Surah Building a Person

Surah Al-Mu'minun opens by promising something extraordinary. **"Qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn"** — *the believers have already succeeded.* Past tense. Done deal. As though Allah is showing us the photograph of who has already crossed the finish line, and now He's going to walk us through the seven traits that put them there.

We've already been with these traits. We've sat with **khushū'** — the trembling presence of a heart in prayer that knows Who it stands before. We've sat with the turning away from **laghw** — the empty speech that drains the soul without our noticing. We've sat with **zakah** — the moving of wealth as worship.

And now — at the structural heart of the seven — Allah brings us here. To this. To the body itself.

Stop and notice where this falls. The opening traits were about what the believer *does* with their attention, their words, their wealth. The closing traits will be about what they do with their *trusts and testimonies and prayers* — the public structure of their life. But right at the center, before moving outward again, Allah places something so intimate it almost feels out of place in a register this lofty.

[PAUSE]

He's going to talk about the body. Specifically, the most private part of it.

This placement is not accidental. The Quran does not treat the body as embarrassing. It does not slip the body in apologetically between the "real" matters of worship. It places the body **at the architectural center** of the believer's portrait — because the body is where the inner faith and the outer life touch each other every single day.

And here's what's stunning about how Allah handles it. He does not give us a list of forbidden acts. He does not enumerate the gradations of transgression. He gives us **three verses with a perfect three-part architecture**: a rule, an exception, a boundary. And inside those three verses, He performs a kind of moral surgery that I want you to feel by the end — surgery that removes both the wound of shame and the wound of license at the same time.

What we are going to discover is that Allah is not, in these verses, primarily concerned with restriction. He is concerned with **identity**. He is going to give the believer a name — *ḥāfiẓ*, guardian — and tell him that his body is a trust like the Quran is a trust, and that being a guardian of it is not a posture of suppression but a posture of *honoring*.

Stay with me. The world the listeners of these verses lived in was a world where the body was either a marketplace or a shame. Polite Arab society had layers of euphemism around it; tribal society had layers of pre-Islamic licentiousness around it. Into both at once, Allah speaks. He neither shames the body nor releases it. He **guards** it.

Let's see how.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: Ḥāfiẓūn — The Identity of a Guardian

Before we open the word, I want to teach you something about Arabic that we'll need to see what Allah does here.

In English, when we want to describe what someone does, we usually use a verb. *He guards. She prays. They give.* And when we want to describe who someone *is*, we usually use a noun. *He is a guard. She is a worshipper. They are givers.*

Arabic has a word-form that does both at once. It's called the **active participle** — *ism al-fā'il* — "the noun of the doer." It takes a verb and freezes it into an identity. Not "he guards" but "he is one-who-guards." Not an action being performed but a person being defined.

Now here's the thing: the entire portrait of the successful believer in Surah Al-Mu'minun is built on this form. It's not "those who pray" — it's *those whose prayers contain khushū'*. It's not "those who turn away" — it's *muʿriḍūn* — "ones-who-are-turners-away." It's not "those who guard" — it's *ḥāfiẓūn* — "ones-who-are-guardians."

Allah is not telling us what the believers *do*. He is telling us **who they are**. He is showing us seven facets of an identity, not seven items on a checklist.

[PAUSE]

Now look at our verse:

> [ayah:23:5] وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ لِفُرُوجِهِمْ حَـٰفِظُونَ
>
> *Wa-alladhīna hum li-furūjihim ḥāfiẓūn.*

Word by word: *And those who — they — over their private parts — are guardians.*

The word *furūj* — plural of *farj*, the private part — carries within it the full scope of what this guardianship is protecting against. Al-Muyassar is explicit: the *ḥāfiẓ* guards from *zinā*, *liwāṭ*, and every indecency — *kull al-fawāḥish*. The protection is comprehensive, not bounded to a single category of transgression; the entire domain of sexual violation is placed under the same word of commission. Al-Tabari observes that the verse's grammatical focus falls specifically on the genitals of men — the verse's primary rhetorical address in its original proclamation was to the male believers and heads of household — while other classical commentators read the seven-trait portrait as describing the *mu'minūn* in their universal character, encompassing all who believe. Both observations are accurate and not in conflict: the grammatical address and the theological portrait operate at different levels of the text.

The word **ḥāfiẓūn** is the plural active participle of the verb **ḥafiẓa**. And the root **ḥ-f-ẓ** is one of the most consequential roots in the Quran. Let's sit with it for a moment.

This root means *to protect, to preserve, to guard, to keep watch over, to memorize, to commit safely to memory.*

Where else does this root appear?

It appears in Allah's name **al-Ḥafīẓ** — the Preserver, the One who guards everything in existence from dissolving back into nothing. It appears in the verse **innā naḥnu nazzalnā al-dhikra wa innā lahu la-ḥāfiẓūn** — *We sent down the Reminder, and We are its Guardians.* It is the title given to the **ḥāfiẓ al-Qur'ān** — the one who has memorized the Book and now carries it inside the safe of his heart.

And here — in this verse — it is the title given to the believer who guards his body.

Stay with me here. This is not a coincidence of vocabulary.

Allah is using the **same word** for the protection of the Quran and the protection of the body. The same word for what He does with His revelation, for what the memorizer does with the verses, for what the believer does with his own flesh.

What is this telling us?

It is telling us that **the body is not less sacred than the Book**. It is telling us that the believer's body is a deposit — an *amānah* — placed inside him for safekeeping. Just as the *ḥāfiẓ al-Qur'ān* understands that the verses inside him are not his property but a trust placed in him by his Lord, so too the believer understands that his body is not his property but a trust placed in him by his Lord.

Notice something. We do not let just anyone touch the *muṣḥaf*. We do not write graffiti on its pages. We do not throw it on the floor. We do not let it be defiled. *Why?* Not because it is *ours* and we are possessive over our things. The opposite. Because it is **not ours** — it belongs to its Owner, and we are caretakers of something more sacred than we are.

The body, says this verse, is the same. **You are not your own.** Your body is a *muṣḥaf* of flesh — a mobile manuscript of divine craftsmanship — and Allah has appointed you its guardian.

This is why the language is **li-furūjihim ḥāfiẓūn** and not **furūjahum yaḥfaẓūn**. The little preposition **li-** — "for" or "over" — does work that's almost impossible to translate. In classical Arabic this is sometimes called the *lām al-taqwiyah* — the *lām* of strengthening. It strengthens the verbal energy onto the object. It is as though Allah is saying: *over their bodies — and here especially — they are guardians.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel. The modern conversation about chastity is almost always framed in the negative. *Don't do this. Don't touch that. Don't let yourself be tempted.* The Quranic framing is **completely different**. It is not negative. It is *vocational*. You are not being told to suppress something. You are being **commissioned**. You have been entrusted. You are a *ḥāfiẓ* — a guardian, a memorizer, a preserver. The body is your *amānah* and you are its keeper.

When you understand chastity this way, the entire interior architecture changes. It is no longer about resisting a constant pressure. It is about *honoring* a constant trust.

A scholar guards the *muṣḥaf* not because he is afraid of it but because he loves it.

The believer guards his body not because he is afraid of it but because he is its keeper.

This is what *ḥāfiẓūn* gives us. One word. An identity, not an injunction.

### Element Two: Warā'a Dhālika — The Geometry of the Boundary

Now we move to the second verse and into the third, and I want you to watch closely what Allah does — because what He does here is structural, and it is brilliant.

> **إِلَّا عَلَىٰ أَزْوَاجِهِمْ أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَانُهُمْ فَإِنَّهُمْ غَيْرُ مَلُومِينَ ۝ فَمَنِ ابْتَغَىٰ وَرَاءَ ذَٰلِكَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْعَادُونَ**

The first thing to notice — before we get to the language — is the **shape** of these three verses.

Verse 5 establishes a positive identity: *they are guardians.*

Verse 6 establishes the lawful zone — and opens two entries into it. The first is *their spouses*: wives joined by the contract of marriage, those whom, as al-Tabari specifies, "Allah has made lawful through *nikāḥ*." The second is *those their right hands possess* — which classical commentators (al-Tabari, al-Muyassar) unanimously understood as the institution of *milk al-yamīn*, the female slaves whose intimate relations were legally recognized in the early community. This second category carries live scholarly conversation in our time: some contemporary Muslim scholars hold that in the absence of legal slavery — an institution abolished across the world — this entry no longer stands as an operative permission; others maintain that the Quranic text addresses the legal world as it was, and that slavery's abolition closes this door by circumstance rather than by reinterpretation. The classical sources are unanimous on the phrase's meaning. Rendering it as "those bound by lawful covenant" would be a modernist gloss that misrepresents both the text and the scholars who have grappled with it honestly. The conversation about its contemporary application is real and worth engaging — but it must be engaged as a conversation, not resolved by quietly rewriting what the words say.

Then — and this is what I want you to feel — the verse adds a reassurance so complete it deserves its own pause: *fa innahum ghayru malūmīn* — *they are not blameworthy.* Al-Tabari unpacks this with a remarkable triple negation: the one who keeps within the lawful zone is *not reproached, not condemned, not committing any sin that would merit blame*. Three ways of landing the same assurance, stacked — as though Allah knows that even inside what He has permitted, the heart may still carry a residue of unearned shame, a quiver of conscience that has not yet received the message. Al-Muyassar arrives at the bedrock more simply: *because Allah has made them ḥalāl*. The permission is not an accommodation of weakness. It is divine authorization. Inside the lawful zone, you are not merely allowed — you are *sanctioned*.

Verse 7 establishes a boundary: *whoever seeks beyond that, those are the transgressors.*

Rule. Exception. Boundary.

This is the exact architectural template the science of Islamic jurisprudence — *fiqh* — would later be built on. The general principle. The lawful exception. The line beyond which lies prohibition. Allah is teaching the very *grammar* of how to think about a sacred limit, in three short verses, before any of His scholars had been born.

But the part I want us to live in for a few minutes is the phrase in verse 7:

> **فَمَنِ ابْتَغَىٰ وَرَاءَ ذَٰلِكَ**
>
> *Fa-man ibtaghā warā'a dhālika.*
>
> *Whoever seeks beyond that.*

There are two words here doing extraordinary work. Let's take them one at a time.

**Ibtaghā.** The verb. It comes from the root *b-gh-y*, which can mean *to seek, to desire, to pursue*. But notice the form. This is not the simple Form I (*baghā*) — *he sought*. This is **Form VIII** (*ibtaghā*) — *he sought out for himself, he deliberately and reflexively pursued.*

In Arabic, the Form VIII pattern adds a layer of *intent* and *self-investment* to the verb. It is the difference between *I noticed* and *I went and looked for it*. Between *something happened to come my way* and *I made it my project*. Allah is not describing someone who stumbled. He is describing someone who *went looking*.

Sit with that. The transgressor in this verse is not painted as a victim of circumstance. He is painted as someone who **set out**, who **pursued**, who **made it his own seeking.** This matters because the gravity of the transgression is tied to the agency in it. Not "those upon whom this happened." Those who *sought it*.

[PAUSE]

Now the second word — and this is the one I want to spend the most time on:

**Warā'a dhālika.** *Beyond that.*

The literal meaning of *warā'a* is *behind*. In Arabic, what is "behind" can mean what is *past*, *outside*, or *beyond*. And **dhālika** — *that* — is the demonstrative for something distant or just-mentioned. It is the *that* that points back at what was just established.

So the phrase reads: *whoever seeks behind/beyond what was just drawn.*

Now look at what Allah is *not* doing here. He is not listing prohibitions. He is not saying *whoever does X, Y, or Z*. He has not given a single specific transgression a name in these verses. He has drawn a **lawful zone** and then said, in effect: **"and outside this zone."**

Let me show you why this is a stunning rhetorical strategy.

If Allah had given us a list — *do not do A, do not do B, do not do C* — what would the human heart immediately do? It would start asking: *what about D? What about A-and-a-half? What about something just like B but with one detail different?* The list creates the lawyer. The list invites the loophole.

Instead, Allah builds a **wall**. He builds a wall, and He places one door in it, and He says: *inside the door — *azwāj* — is your zone. Outside the wall — anything outside the wall — is transgression.*

This is the difference between **drawing a line** and **describing every step beyond the line.**

The believer does not have to keep an inventory of forbidden acts in his head. He has to know one thing: *am I inside the door, or have I gone behind it?* That's it. The single question replaces the infinite list.

Think of it this way. Imagine a city. You can govern a city by writing ten thousand laws — *do not steal this, do not climb that, do not enter here.* Or you can build a wall around the city and say: *inside the wall is the city. Outside is wilderness.* The first approach produces lawyers. The second approach produces **citizens**.

Allah is making citizens.

[PAUSE]

And there is another thing happening in *warā'a dhālika* that I don't want you to miss. The word *warā'* — *behind* — has a connotation in Arabic of going past something *that was supposed to stop you*. You go *behind* a barrier. You go *behind* a curtain. You step *past* a marker that was there for a reason.

When Allah says *warā'a dhālika*, He is not just saying "outside the lawful zone." He is saying: **"on the other side of the line that was drawn for your protection."**

Hold that. The word is doing the work of the whole verse. The line was *for*
