# The Verdict, Then the First Quality

## The Ayahs

> **قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ**
> **ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ فِى صَلَاتِهِمْ خَـٰشِعُونَ**
>
> *Qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn. Alladhīna hum fī ṣalātihim khāshiʿūn.*
>
> "Successful indeed are the believers — those who, within their prayer, are stilled in humility."

## Rebuilding the World

There is a moment in every human life when someone — a parent, a teacher, a culture, a feed — defines success for you before you have the chance to define it yourself. By the time you are old enough to ask the question, the answer has already been installed. You inherit a verdict. You spend the rest of your life either chasing it or defending yourself against it.

Imagine the early Muslims hearing this surah for the first time. They lived inside a culture that had its own ledger of success. A man was successful if his lineage was strong, his herds were many, his sword was respected, his name was sung at the gatherings. The whole social architecture of Mecca was a constant referendum on who counted and who didn't.

Now picture them — many of them slaves, many of them poor, many of them the second sons of forgotten fathers — sitting in the dust of a Meccan afternoon, listening to these words for the first time.

**Qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn.**

A verdict. Delivered before the trial. Pronounced before the listing of qualifications. As if Allah, knowing exactly which ledger Mecca was using, refused to let the question of "who succeeded?" be answered by anyone else — and answered it Himself.

[PAUSE]

What you must understand before we open these words is that this opening is not motivational. It is forensic. It is Allah quietly removing the world's gavel from the world's hand and placing the verdict into His own.

And here is the hook I want to leave you with before we go in:

Of all the qualities Allah could have named first — belief in the unseen, truthfulness, charity, fasting, jihād — He chose the one that no human eye can audit. He chose the one that lives inside the prayer, behind the face, where no witness can certify anything. The first quality of the successful is **a state of the heart that nobody can verify but Allah.**

Stay with that. We are about to find out why.

## PART 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Qad aflaḥa* — A Verdict Spoken in the Past Tense About a Future That Hasn't Arrived

Before we touch the word, let me teach you a small grammatical fact that will change how you read this surah.

In Arabic, when you want to assert that something has *definitely* happened — not "happened" in the sense of completed-and-over, but "happened" in the sense of *settled, certain, beyond renegotiation* — you reach for a particle. That particle is **قَدْ** (*qad*), and you place it in front of a past-tense verb.

Here is the everyday example. If a friend asks you, "Did you send the email?" and you say, *"arsaltu"* — "I sent it" — that's a fact. But if you say, *"qad arsaltu"* — "I have indeed sent it" — you are doing something more. You are closing the question. You are saying: *don't reopen this. It is done.*

Now look at our ayah.

> **قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ**

The verb *aflaḥa* is past tense. The believers' success is described as if it has already happened. But many of the people being described here are still alive. Some of them haven't even been born. The story of their success is, in any earthly sense, still being written.

So why does Allah speak about it as **finished**?

Notice something. In Arabic rhetoric, there is a category called *al-māḍī al-muḥaqqaq* — "the perfect tense of certainty." When something is so certain in the knowledge of the speaker that the future itself bows to it, you describe it in the past. Allah is not predicting the believers' success. He is **reporting** it. From His vantage, where time does not constrain knowledge, the matter is closed.

Let's sit with this.

When you are a believer struggling — when your prayer is dry, when your tongue stumbles, when your faith feels like a flickering thing in your chest — the world is using a present-tense ledger on you. *Are you succeeding now? Right now? In this paycheck, this relationship, this unanswered prayer?*

Allah is using a different tense entirely. He is looking at you from the end of the story and reporting backward. *Qad aflaḥa.* It is already done. The harvest is already in.

Here's what is remarkable. The next eleven verses of this surah will list the qualities that constitute this success. But notice the order. **The verdict comes first.** Allah doesn't say, "Whoever has these qualities will succeed." He says, "The successful have already succeeded — and here is what they look like." The verdict is not contingent on the description. The description is contingent on the verdict.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the great mercies of how this surah opens. You are not auditioning. You are being recognized.

#### Now the root.

The verb *aflaḥa* comes from the root **ف ل ح** (*f-l-ḥ*), and this is where the soil of the meaning shows itself. Lexically, the root means *to split, to cleave, to break open*. The classical lexicons preserve a beautiful image: the **fallāḥ**, the farmer, is the one called by this root because his work is to split the hard ground so a seed can be placed inside it.

Falāḥ — success — in the Arabic imagination is **agricultural**. It is not the success of the merchant who closes a deal. It is the success of the farmer who, over a long unwitnessed labor, breaks the resistant earth, drops in a seed, waits through seasons, and finally sees green.

Falāḥ is what happens at harvest. But the work of falāḥ is what happens *before* anyone can see anything.

This is why every adhān calls you with the words **ḥayya ʿalā al-falāḥ** — "come to success." The muezzin is not promising you a windfall. He is calling you to the work of the farmer. To split open the hard ground of your morning, plant something living, and trust the seasons.

So when Allah says *qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn*, He is not saying, "the believers got lucky." He is saying: **the believers' long, hidden, patient labor in the soil of their hearts has broken through to fruit. The harvest is in. It is reported in the past tense because, from where I am looking, it is finished.**

What this single particle and verb invites us to consider is that your inner life is not a draft. From Allah's vantage, it is a finished story whose ending He has already approved. Your job is not to author the verdict. Your job is to live up to the one He has already pronounced.

### Element Two: *Al-Muʾminūn* — Not Those Who Believe, But Those Who Have Become Secure

Watch carefully. Allah did not say *qad aflaḥa alladhīna āmanū* — "successful are those who believed." He could have. The Quran uses that phrase often. He said *qad aflaḥa al-**muʾminūn*** — "the believers." And there is a difference.

Let me teach the structure first.

In Arabic, you can describe an action two ways. You can name the action — "those who pray," "those who believed," "those who fast" — using a verb form. Or you can name an **identity** that has formed *because* of the action — "the prayerful," "the believers," "the fasting ones" — using a noun form called the **active participle** (*ism al-fāʿil*).

The difference is enormous. The verb form describes what you do. The noun form describes what you have become.

If I say "she is someone who runs," I am describing an activity. If I say "she is a runner," I am describing an identity. The activity may be occasional. The identity is what you are even when you are sitting still.

*Al-muʾminūn* is the noun form. It is not "those who happen to be believing right now." It is "the ones who have become believers." The action has settled into them. They wear it as a name.

#### Now the root, and this is where it opens.

*Muʾmin* comes from the root **أ م ن** (*ʾ-m-n*), whose primary meaning is **security, safety, the absence of fear**. From this same root come the words *amān* (security, refuge), *amānah* (a trust, something safekept), and *amīn* (the trustworthy one — the title of the Prophet ﷺ before prophethood).

So when Allah names the successful, He calls them *al-muʾminūn* — and the word is doing double work, because the form *muʾmin* is the **active participle of the fourth form** of this root (*āmana*, *yuʾminu*).

The fourth form in Arabic carries a specific meaning. It is **causative** or **factitive** — it means *to cause something to happen, to bring it about, to give it.* If the root means "security," then the fourth form means **to give security**. To make safe. To grant peace.

Stay with me. This is the moment.

A *muʾmin* is not, in the deepest sense, "one who believes." A *muʾmin* is **one who gives security.** One who is so settled in his own trust of Allah that *others are made safe by his presence*. His tongue is safe. His hands are safe. His promises are safe. The world around him exhales when he enters the room.

The Prophet ﷺ said, in a hadith preserved in the Sunan: *"The believer is the one from whose tongue and hand people are safe."* He was not adding a moral commandment. He was unfolding the etymology. He was telling us what the word *muʾmin* has meant all along.

[PAUSE]

So now read the verdict again.

**Qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn.** "The harvest is in for the ones who give security."

Notice what Allah did NOT say. He did not say success belongs to the strong. He did not say success belongs to the certain. He did not say success belongs to those who have correct opinions. He said success belongs to **those whose presence makes others safe**, because that presence can only emerge from a heart that is itself safe in Allah.

Here is what this invites us to consider. The believer's belief is not first a position the mind holds. It is first a **safety the soul has received** — and a safety the soul therefore radiates. You cannot give what you do not have. The *muʾmin* gives peace because he has been given peace. He gives security because he has *been secured*.

And the question that opens out of this — the question we will return to in the closing — is simply: **does your faith make others safe?** Because if it doesn't, the word "believer" may not yet have settled into you as an identity. It may still be a verb you sometimes perform.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Fī Ṣalātihim Khāshiʿūn* — Inside the Prayer, Lowered

Now we arrive at the first quality. And before we touch it, look at where Allah placed it. He has just delivered the verdict. He is about to name seven qualities that constitute the believers' success. Of those seven, He could have started with anything. He starts with this:

> **ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ فِى صَلَاتِهِمْ خَـٰشِعُونَ**

"Those who, **within** their prayer, are *khāshiʿūn*."

There are three things to notice — and the first is so small that you will miss it if you blink.

#### First: the preposition *fī*.

In Arabic, you can describe doing something *during* an activity using several prepositions. The most common is *ʿinda* — "at" or "around." You could also use *ʿalā* — "upon." Allah used neither. He used **fī**.

*Fī* is **locative**. It means *inside*. It treats the noun that follows it as a *space*, a place you can enter and inhabit.

The everyday example. If I say "the book is *ʿalā* the table," it means it is sitting on top of the table. If I say "the book is *fī* the box," it means it is enclosed by the box. *Fī* says: this thing is *inside* that thing.

Allah did not say "those who, *during* their prayer, are humbled." He said "those who, *inside* their prayer, are humbled." The prayer is being treated as a **dwelling**. A place you go into. A space you can be inside.

Let's sit with this.

When you stand for prayer, what is happening, in the picture this preposition is painting, is that you are **entering a structure**. You are walking into a chamber. The first *Allāhu akbar* is a doorway. And the question is no longer "what are you doing during these minutes?" but "what kind of person are you *inside this room*?"

Here's what's remarkable. The successful believer is not described as one who *prays well*. He is described as one whose **interior** state, *once he is inside the prayer*, is a particular kind of state. The prayer itself is assumed. Everyone in this surah's audience prays. The differentiator is not whether you enter the room. It is **what you become once you are in it.**

[PAUSE]

#### Second: the absent action.

Notice what Allah does not mention. He does not say "those who pray." He does not name the *act* of prayer at all. He names only the *interior posture inside* the prayer. The action of *praying* is grammatically presupposed. It is folded into the noun *ṣalātihim* — "their prayer" — as if to say: *of course you pray. That is not the question. The question is what is happening inside your praying.*

Read this absence carefully. There are religious traditions, including parts of our own when we are not careful, that count prayer as a checkbox. *Did you pray? Yes. Done.* This ayah refuses that ledger. It does not even bother to count whether you prayed. It only counts what you were like *while* you prayed.

Allah is not interested in the corpse of an action. He is interested in **whether anyone was home inside it.**

[PAUSE]

#### Third: the word *khāshiʿūn*.

The root is **خ ش ع** (*kh-sh-ʿ*), and the classical lexicons describe its core image with extraordinary precision. *Khushūʿ* is the state of a creature that has **lowered its neck**. The deer that bows its head before something. The camel that kneels. The horse whose ears flatten. It is a **physical lowering** that comes from an **inner recognition** of something greater.

But the word does more. *Khashaʿat al-arḍ* — "the earth became *khāshiʿah*" — is an Arabic idiom for ground that has gone silent, settled, become low and barren and still. *Khushūʿ* is a **settling**. A quieting. The opposite of agitation. The opposite of the heart that is performing while the body kneels.

So *khushūʿ* is not a single thing. It is a composite. It is **the body lowered, the heart present, the agitation gone, the recognition arrived.**

And here is what is remarkable about how Allah constructs this phrase. He does not say "those who *do* khushūʿ" — using a verb. He says "those who *are* khāshiʿūn" — using the noun form, the active participle, the same form we saw with *muʾminūn*. The form that names an **identity**, not an action.

Khushūʿ here is not something the believer *does* in the prayer. It is what the believer *becomes* once he is inside the prayer. The prayer is the room. Khushūʿ is who he turns into when the door closes behind him.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing. The classical commentators — al-Rāghib in his Mufradāt, and the lexicographers of the Lisān — distinguish *khushūʿ* from a related word, *khuḍūʿ*. *Khuḍūʿ* is **bodily** humility. The body bowed. *Khushūʿ* is **interior** humility, of which the bodily lowering is only the outward sign. You can perform *khuḍūʿ* without anyone home inside it. You cannot perform *khushūʿ* — because *khushūʿ* is the home being occupied.

What this invites us to consider is the most uncomfortable question in the religious life: **when you stand for prayer, is anyone home?** The body is in the room. The tongue is moving. The recitation is correct. But is there a *self* inside the building, lowered, still, recognizing?

This is the first quality of the successful. Not the second. Not the seventh. **The first.** Because every other quality — the truthfulness, the charity, the chastity, the trust-keeping — flows from a heart that has learned, five times a day, what it feels like to be **home** in the presence of Allah.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have three discoveries. Allah opens the surah with a verb of certainty in the past tense, reporting from the end of the story that the believers have already broken through to harvest. He calls them not "those who believe" but "the ones who give security" — because the form of the word makes their belief radiate as safety to others. And He names their first defining quality not as a thing they do, but as a state they enter — *inside* the prayer, **stilled, lowered, present**.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Outsourced Verdict

Most of us live inside a verdict we did not choose. Somewhere between the ages of eight and eighteen, a definition of success was downloaded into us. From parents who wanted the best for us. From a culture that wanted us to keep the machine running. From an algorithm that wanted us to keep scrolling. From classmates whose envy or admiration we needed.

By the time we are adults, we are spending most of our energy trying to satisfy a verdict that was authored by people who did not love us the way Allah loves us, and did not know us the way Allah knows us, and will not be there at our final reckoning.

This is the deep ache of modern adult life — and it is the ache the opening of this surah is speaking into. You are running, exhausted, in a race whose finish line was drawn by strangers.

Now read the ayah again. *Qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn.* Watch what Allah is doing. He is not asking the world's verdict. He is **overruling** it. The first word of this surah is the sound of a higher court taking jurisdiction.

Here is what shifts when you let this land.

The dunya does not have authority to declare you a failure. It can pronounce. It can post. It can pass over you and choose someone else. But its verdict is not binding. There is a higher court, and that court has already sat, and the verdict — for the one who lowers himself inside his prayer, who keeps his trusts, who guards his tongue and his glance — *is already written.*

This is not a coping mechanism. This is jurisdiction. This is reality.

You do not have to defeat the world's verdict. You only have to remember that it was never the real verdict in the first place.

So what shifts tomorrow? When the LinkedIn post lands, when the family WhatsApp celebrates someone else's milestone, when the algorithm stages another stranger's wedding, another stranger's promotion, another stranger's renovation in front of your eyes — there is a small interior gesture available to you. You can say, quietly, almost inaudibly: *that is not the court that will judge me.* And then you can return to the prayer, which is the only courtroom whose verdict is final.

### Theme Two: Khushūʿ as the Mother of All Virtues

Why does Allah place this quality first?

Imagine a tree. The trunk is what you see. The branches are what bear fruit. But underneath the soil, hidden from every observer, is the root — and without the root, the trunk is firewood and the branches are kindling.

The seven qualities of the *muʾminūn* in this opening passage — the prayer, the turning away from idle speech, the giving of zakāh, the guarding of chastity, the keeping of trusts and covenants, and the safeguarding of the prayer at its end — are the branches. They are what the world can audit. Someone can verify whether you gave the zakāh. Someone can verify whether you kept your word. Someone can verify whether you guarded your chastity.

But *khushūʿ inside the prayer* — that nobody can verify. Nobody knows whether your heart was home in the rakʿah you just performed. Your wife doesn't know. Your imam doesn't know. Your follower count doesn't know. **Only Allah knows.**

This is why it is first.

Because the entire system of a believer's life is built on a foundation that **only Allah can audit**. If the foundation is real, the branches will be real. If the foundation is hollow — if you have learned to perform khushūʿ without occupying it — every branch above will eventually be hollow too. The zakāh will become a tax. The chastity will become a strategy. The trust-keeping will become a brand.

Khushūʿ is the mother of every virtue because it is the one virtue that cannot be performed for anyone but Allah. Once you have learned to be present for an audience of One, you have learned the only stance from which the rest of the religious life can be sincere.

[PAUSE]

Now let me say something that may be uncomfortable.

If you are honest with yourself, most of your prayers do not have khushūʿ. Most of them are the body in the room while the heart is elsewhere — relitigating an argument, planning a meeting, narrating itself. This is the experience of nearly every Muslim who has ever lived. The Prophet ﷺ himself was reported to say that the first thing to be lifted from this Ummah would be khushūʿ — that the time would come when you could enter a great mosque and not find a single person whose heart was *fully* home.

So this ayah is not a verdict against you. It is an invitation. The successful are not those who have *achieved* khushūʿ in every prayer. They are those who have **made khushūʿ their direction** — the place they keep returning to, the home they keep walking back into when they catch themselves wandering.

What this changes for tomorrow is small and concrete. Before you say *Allāhu akbar*, take three breaths. Recognize that you are about to enter a *room* — not perform an *act*. Step into it the way you would step into a chamber where the king is already waiting. And when your mind wanders — and it will — the moment you notice the wandering, *that noticing is a return*. Each return is a small khushūʿ. Allah does not require an unbroken stillness. He requires the heart that keeps coming back.

You do not need to pray better. You need to learn to **come home** more often.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

Allah opens this surah with a verb in the past tense, because the believer's success is being **reported from the end of the story**, not predicted from inside the middle. He names them not by their action of believing but by the identity they have grown into — *the ones who give security* — because real belief makes others safe. And He defines their first quality not as a thing they perform but as a state they **enter** — *inside* the prayer, lowered, settled, present.

These are not three separate findings. They are the same finding from three angles.

A *muʾmin* is someone whose interior life is so settled in Allah that two things follow. **One**, his presence in the world becomes a sanctuary for others. **Two**, his presence in the prayer becomes a sanctuary for himself. And the verdict on his life is therefore not subject to the world's jurisdiction at all — because the work is interior, the audit is interior, and the only Judge who can see the interior has already pronounced: *qad aflaḥa.*

The harvest is in. The seeds you planted in the dark soil of every prayer where you tried to come home — even when you failed, even when you mostly wandered, even when the only khushūʿ you achieved was the noticing itself — those seeds, Allah says, **have already broken into fruit**.

You are not a verdict in progress. You are, by the mercy of His past tense, a verdict already issued. Your job is to live up to the one He has spoken.

### Questions to Carry

- When the world delivers a verdict on me — that I am behind, that I am unseen, that I do not count — whose courtroom am I treating as final?
- Does my presence make others safe? When my tongue moves, when my hand acts, when I enter a room — does the word *muʾmin* describe me by its etymology, or only by my self-identification?
- When I say *Allāhu akbar*, do I treat it as a starting bell — or as a doorway?
- In the last prayer I performed, was anyone home inside the building?
- If Allah has already issued the verdict in the past tense, what am I still trying to prove, and to whom?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The successful are not those whose lives the world certifies, but those whose hearts have learned to come home inside the prayer — because that hidden interior labor is the only foundation a real life can be built on, and Allah has already pronounced its harvest finished.

---

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow at *fajr*, before you raise your hands, pause. Recognize that you are not performing an obligation. You are **walking into a chamber** Allah has built for you, five times a day, where He has already pronounced you successful before you even arrived. Your only task is to **be in the room**. To lower the head. To still the chatter. To let the noticing-of-wandering become a returning. That returning, again and again, is the whole of the religious life.

Do not pray better. Come home more often.

### A Brief Du'ā

> O Allah, You have already pronounced the harvest of those who come home inside their prayer. Make me one of them. Settle my heart in the room when my body enters it. Let my faith become a safety others can rest inside. And on the day all verdicts are read, let Yours be the only one I have lived for. *Āmīn.*
