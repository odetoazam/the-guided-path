# Build Under My Eyes — When the Oven Boils, Don't Speak to Me of Them

## Surah 23:27

> فَأَوْحَيْنَآ إِلَيْهِ أَنِ ٱصْنَعِ ٱلْفُلْكَ بِأَعْيُنِنَا وَوَحْيِنَا فَإِذَا جَآءَ أَمْرُنَا وَفَارَ ٱلتَّنُّورُ ۙ فَٱسْلُكْ فِيهَا مِن كُلٍّ زَوْجَيْنِ ٱثْنَيْنِ وَأَهْلَكَ إِلَّا مَن سَبَقَ عَلَيْهِ ٱلْقَوْلُ مِنْهُمْ ۖ وَلَا تُخَـٰطِبْنِى فِى ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوٓا۟ ۖ إِنَّهُم مُّغْرَقُونَ

*Fa-awḥaynā ilayhi ani-ṣnaʿi-l-fulka bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā fa-idhā jāʾa amrunā wa-fāra-t-tannūru fa-sluk fīhā min kullin zawjayni-thnayni wa-ahlaka illā man sabaqa ʿalayhi-l-qawlu minhum wa-lā tukhāṭibnī fī-lladhīna ẓalamū innahum mughraqūn.*

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, I want you to feel where Nuh ﷺ actually stood when these words came down.

He has been calling his people for, by the Qur'an's own count elsewhere, almost a thousand years minus fifty. Imagine that. A man who has spent the equivalent of forty human lifetimes saying the same sentence: *worship Allah alone*. He has been called a liar. Called a madman. Called a man who wants to elevate himself over them. They have plugged their fingers in their ears in his presence — Surah Nuh tells us this literally — and pulled their cloaks over their faces so they could not even see his expression.

And in *this* surah, just two ayahs before our ayah, the leaders of his people give their final summary verdict: *"This is nothing but a man like you who wishes to take superiority over you... we have not heard of this from our forefathers."* (23:24)

So here is the climate. A prophet has spoken until his throat is dry. The community has decided. They are not going to listen. There is no dramatic confrontation left to have.

[PAUSE]

And then, in this exact moment of total exhaustion — when nothing more is going to be said with words — Allah speaks. He gives Nuh ﷺ a set of instructions that will quietly destroy everything we assume about how God responds to a prophet's pain.

He tells him to build something. He tells him *who* will be watching. He tells him *how* he will know when the moment has come. And then He tells him something that should stop our breath:

**"Do not speak to Me about them anymore."**

That is the line we are walking toward. But we cannot understand it yet. We have to earn it. So let me show you the small architectural pieces first, and then we will stand together at the door that closes.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Bi-aʿyuninā* — "Under Our Eyes"

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, when you say something happens *under someone's eye* — *taḥta ʿaynih* — it is an idiom of supervision, protection, and personal investment. A master craftsman saying *this apprentice trained under my eye* is not telling you about optics. He is saying: *I was personally invested in this. I watched every cut. Nothing went wrong without my noticing.*

Now, Arabic has the singular *ʿayn* (eye) and the plural *aʿyun* (eyes). When the singular is used in the idiom, it means *under supervision*. When the plural is used, the supervision is **multiplied** — it is comprehensive, total, no angle missed.

Hold that. Now look at the ayah.

#### Opening the word

Allah says: *iṣnaʿi-l-fulka* — *make/manufacture the ship* — *bi-aʿyuninā* — **with Our eyes**.

Two layers of plural. *Aʿyuninā* — *Our eyes*. The word for "eye" is in the plural form. And the possessive pronoun "Our" is also plural.

This is the first thing I want you to sit with: Allah, *al-Wāḥid*, the One — does not say *bi-ʿaynī*, "under My eye." He says *bi-aʿyuninā*. **Under Our eyes.** And the classical lexicographers and *mufassirūn* — al-Rāghib, al-Zamakhsharī, Ibn ʿĀshūr — converge on what this construction does.

[PAUSE]

#### The composite sketch

One angle: the plural of majesty. The way a king says *"We decree"* not because he is many, but because the weight of his single decree exceeds what one mouth can carry.

Another angle: the comprehensiveness of divine attention. Allah is saying: *not one nail of this ship will be hammered out of My sight. Not one plank. Not one moment of your labor.*

A third angle, and this is the one that breaks me: when an Arab said *we built it under our eyes*, he meant *we did not delegate this to a foreman.* The plural intensifies that personal involvement. It is the difference between a CEO knowing a project is being built somewhere in his company, and a CEO standing on the construction site every day, watching the welders.

Each of these is a camera angle on the same reality.

#### What it reveals

Now let's stay with this. Nuh has just been told he is alone. His people have rejected him. His son will reject him. His wife will reject him. The text in Surah Hud will tell us that not even those of his household will all board the ship.

In other words: at the moment Nuh begins the most absurd-looking project in human history — building an ocean-going vessel in a region with no ocean — he is **about to be the loneliest man on earth.**

And Allah does not say *I will send you angels to help.* He does not say *I will inspire your followers to comfort you.* He says: ***bi-aʿyuninā***. **Under Our eyes.**

Notice something. **The loneliness of being witnessed by Allah is not loneliness.** This is the inversion the ayah performs. The man who has lost the gaze of his society has gained something the society can never give him: he is being watched, plank by plank, by the One whose gaze constitutes reality.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Every nail Nuh ﷺ drove into that ark was driven into a piece of wood that Allah's gaze was already on. He was not building alone in the world. He was building in the only theatre that finally matters — and the audience of one was Allah Himself, with eyes plural, attention total.

Sit with that for a moment before we move.

### Element Two: *Wa-fāra-t-tannūr* — "And the Oven Boils Over"

#### Teaching the concept first

Imagine I told you: *when you see the kettle boil, leave the house.*

You would think I had lost my mind. Kettles boil all the time. What does a boiling kettle have to do with leaving the house? Why is *that* the signal?

Unless. Unless I knew something you did not. Unless I knew that, on a particular day, the kettle would boil **without anyone turning it on** — and *that* would be your sign.

Hold that thought. Now let's look at what Allah told Nuh ﷺ.

#### Opening the word

*Fa-idhā jāʾa amrunā* — *and when Our command comes* — *wa-fāra-t-tannūr* — **and the oven boils over**.

Two words. *Fāra* — from the root *f-w-r*, which means to boil, to gush forth, to erupt, to overflow. It is used in classical Arabic for boiling water spilling out of a pot, for a spring suddenly bursting, for blood pulsing forth from a wound.

*Al-tannūr* — the oven. The baking-pit. The most ordinary domestic object in the ancient Near East. Every household had one. It is where bread happens. It is where dinner becomes possible. It is the most quotidian, daily, unremarkable place in the entire home.

#### The composite sketch

The classical commentators differ on what *exactly* the *tannūr* refers to here. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said it was a literal oven — water sprang up from inside it, in Nuh's own home, as the appointed sign. Some narrations point to a specific *tannūr* in a specific location. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī suggested it could be a metaphor for the surface of the earth itself beginning to gush. Others read it as the breaking of dawn — the way Arabs sometimes called the eastern horizon a *tannūr* because it glows red like a baking-pit.

Each of these is a camera angle. And here is what I want you to notice: **every angle preserves the same scandalous insight.**

Whether it is a literal kitchen oven, a specific landmark, the earth itself, or the dawn — Allah is telling Nuh that the **end of the world will announce itself through something perfectly ordinary.**

#### Stay with this

This is one of the most counterintuitive moments in the entire Qur'an. The flood that will end an entire human civilization — that will erase generations, that will be remembered for the rest of human history as the great cleansing — does not begin with a thunderclap. It does not begin with a darkening sky. It does not begin with an angel descending visibly.

It begins with **water in an oven.**

[PAUSE]

The single most domestic, repeated, taken-for-granted object in Nuh's house. The thing his wife has used a thousand times. The thing he himself has walked past for nine hundred years.

That is the sign.

#### What it reveals

Why? Why would Allah choose *that*?

Here is what this invites us to consider. **Allah does not need spectacle to deliver the most consequential moments of your life.** Reality does not announce itself by becoming dramatic. It announces itself by making the ordinary suddenly *strange*.

Notice the verb. *Fāra*. To gush. To erupt. The *tannūr* — a vessel designed to *contain* fire and heat — suddenly cannot contain what is inside it. The container fails. Water comes out of a place where water has no business being.

This is the linguistic shape of the moment: **the moment when the ordinary container of your life can no longer contain what is rising inside it.**

[PAUSE]

I want you to feel something here. Most of the time, when we imagine a divine sign, we imagine something we have never seen before. A burning bush. A split moon. A staff that becomes a serpent.

But the sign Allah gave Nuh ﷺ was the opposite. It was something he had seen ten thousand times — and one day, it would behave wrongly. The familiar would crack. The expected would betray expectation.

How many of the most important warnings in your own life have come exactly that way? Not as a thunderclap. As a strangeness in something you stopped noticing years ago. A conversation that suddenly does not flow the way it always did. A morning prayer that suddenly tastes different. A verse you have read fifty times that suddenly stops you. **The ovens of your life boiling over.**

This is what the ayah trains us to watch for.

### Element Three: *Wa-lā tukhāṭibnī* — "And Do Not Address Me"

This is the heart of the ayah. The line we have been walking toward.

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, the verbs of speaking are not all the same. *Qāla* means *to say*. *Kallama* means *to speak to* or *to converse*. But *khāṭaba* — Form III of the root *kh-ṭ-b* — is something more specific. It is **the verb of bilateral address.** It implies a back-and-forth. A petition and a hearing. A speaker who expects a response.

Form III in Arabic morphology often carries this meaning of *mutuality* — doing the action *with* or *toward* another. *Qātala* means to fight (mutually). *Kātaba* means to correspond (mutually). *Khāṭaba* means to engage in address that *expects* engagement back.

So when Allah uses this verb in negation — *lā tukhāṭibnī* — He is not just saying *do not say something to Me.* He is saying: **do not enter into dialogue with Me about this. Do not open the channel of petition. Do not bring this case before My court.**

#### Opening the word

*Wa-lā tukhāṭibnī fī-lladhīna ẓalamū.* **And do not address Me concerning those who have wronged.**

Now let's pause. Who is being told this?

A prophet. The most beloved category of human being in the cosmos. A man whose nine hundred and fifty years of patience have just been concluded. A father who, we will learn from Surah Hud (11:45), is going to look at the rising water and see his own son refusing the ark, and is going to cry out, *"My Lord, my son is of my family, and Your promise is true."*

To this man — *this* man — Allah says, **"Do not speak to Me about them anymore."**

[PAUSE]

#### Stay with this

I need you to feel the weight of what just happened in the grammar.

The door of *duʿāʾ* — supplication — is the single most open door in the universe. The Prophet ﷺ told us that *duʿāʾ* is the very essence of worship. The Qur'an tells us *udʿūnī astajib lakum* — *call upon Me and I will respond to you.* We are taught that no Muslim raises their hands in supplication except that Allah is shy to send them away empty.

That door is **always** open.

Except. **Except for this moment.** Except when a divine decree has been finalized about a people who have refused, century after century, to even hear. At that moment, Allah closes the door — not from harshness, but because the matter is no longer in the realm of negotiation. It has crossed into the realm of execution.

#### What it reveals

Here is the inversion. **The closing of the door is itself a mercy** — but only for those whose hands are still on the inside.

Think about what Allah is sparing Nuh ﷺ. He is sparing him decades of intercession that would not be answered. He is sparing him the agony of a father pleading for a drowning son who, until the last moment, chose his own pride. He is sparing him the spiritual whiplash of asking Allah for what Allah has already decreed will not be given.

In Surah Hud (11:46), when Nuh ﷺ does cry out for his son, Allah responds: *"O Nuh, indeed he is not of your family. Indeed, his deed is other than righteous, so do not ask Me for that of which you have no knowledge. I warn you, lest you be among the ignorant."*

That conversation — that gentle but firm rebuke — is the conversation Allah is *trying to spare him from* in our ayah. The instruction comes early. *Before* the flood. *Before* the heart breaks. *Do not speak to Me about them.*

[PAUSE]

#### And then the seal

*Innahum mughraqūn.*

**Indeed, they are drowned.**

Look at the verb form. *Mughraqūn* is a passive participle. In English we have to say *"they will be drowned"* because the flood has not yet happened. But Arabic does not. Arabic states it as **a completed reality.** They *are* drowned. Already. As far as the divine decree is concerned, the matter is done.

The flood has not begun. The ark has not been finished. The oven has not boiled. But in the ledger of divine knowledge, the page is already turned.

This is what Nuh ﷺ has been spared from arguing against. **A reality that, from Allah's vantage, has already happened.**

---

#### Bridge

So we have three things now, three pieces of architecture.

First: *bi-aʿyuninā.* The work happens under God's gaze. Loneliness in society is not loneliness in the cosmos.

Second: *wa-fāra-t-tannūr.* The sign comes through the ordinary. Reality cracks open through what you stopped noticing.

Third: *wa-lā tukhāṭibnī fī-lladhīna ẓalamū.* There is a moment — late, very late — when the door of asking closes. And the closing is itself a mercy.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about what it means to live as someone whose work is witnessed, whose signs are quiet, and whose pleas have limits.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Building Under the Gaze That Replaces All Other Gazes

I want to start somewhere very modern.

There is a cluster of research, summarized in a popular concept called *the observer effect on performance* — or in more colloquial terms, *the watched-pot phenomenon*. People work differently when they know they are being observed. Studies of factory workers, of performers, of even handwashing in hospitals, all show the same thing: **observation changes effort.**

But here is the painful inversion every adult eventually discovers: the observation we crave most is rarely the observation we receive. We work for bosses who barely look up. We post for audiences who scroll past. We labor in rooms where the people whose approval we want are looking somewhere else.

A man builds a business for twenty years. His father, whose praise he was unconsciously chasing the entire time, never quite says the thing he was waiting to hear. A woman raises children for thirty years. The community whose admiration she was earning, in some quiet corner of her heart, looks straight past her and praises someone else.

This is the structural pain of human work. We labor under a gaze that is mostly absent.

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again with this in your chest.

*Iṣnaʿi-l-fulka bi-aʿyuninā.* **Build the ship under Our eyes.**

What Allah is offering Nuh ﷺ — and through him, offering every believer — is the **transposition of the audience.** The replacement of the absent audience with the present one. The reorientation of effort from a gaze that drifts to a gaze that does not.

The Prophet ﷺ taught us that *iḥsān* is *to worship Allah as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then know that He sees you*. We tend to read that as a description of ritual — how to pray, how to fast.

But this ayah extends *iḥsān* into **labor**. Into building. Into manufacturing. Into the carpentry of an absurd-looking project in a desert. The same gaze that is on the prayer is on the hammer.

Here is what changes when you internalize this.

The work that no one notices is not unnoticed.
The kindness no one saw was seen.
The hour you stayed up alone was not alone.
The verse you tried to memorize and forgot was witnessed in the trying.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Take one piece of work in your life right now that nobody is watching. The dawn prayer no one sees you offer. The hour of study you do without telling anyone. The kindness you show to a parent in private. The piece of yourself you are slowly trying to build that no human has yet noticed.

Then say to yourself, before you do that work: ***bi-aʿyuninā***. *Under His eyes.*

Watch what changes in the quality of the act. Not in the outcome. In the act itself.

The ship Nuh ﷺ built was not ultimately about whether the wood was perfectly cut. It was about *who watched the cutting.* And so it is with everything you build that matters.

### Theme Two: The Mercy of the Closed Door

Let me start with something painful that many of us have lived.

There is a particular agony, known to almost every parent, every spouse, every friend who has loved someone deeply: **praying for someone who does not want to be saved.**

You watch them choose, again, the thing that is destroying them. You speak. They turn away. You wait. They refuse. You pray. The prayer feels heavy in your chest, and you cannot tell if Allah is hearing it, or if He is hearing it and the answer is *no*, or if the door of asking for this person was quietly closed years ago and you have been knocking on a sealed wall.

Anyone who has loved an addict, anyone who has tried to bring a wandering loved one back to faith, anyone who has buried their hopes for a child who has chosen a path of self-destruction — knows this agony.

And the pious response, the one we are usually given, is: *keep praying. Don't stop. Allah hears.*

That is true. **Almost always.**

But this ayah tells us about the *almost*.

[PAUSE]

#### Let the ayah illuminate

*Wa-lā tukhāṭibnī fī-lladhīna ẓalamū.* **Do not address Me concerning those who have wronged.**

The Qur'an is doing something here that the modern self-help vocabulary does not have language for. It is acknowledging that **there are decrees that have been finalized.** That after sufficient warning, sufficient mercy, sufficient time — *nine hundred and fifty years*, in Nuh's case — there comes a moment when the matter passes from the realm of *prayer for change* into the realm of *prayer for acceptance*.

This is not a failure of *duʿāʾ*. It is a reorientation of *duʿāʾ*.

When Allah tells Nuh ﷺ *do not address Me concerning them*, He is not saying *I no longer hear you*. He is saying **the page on which their fate was written has been signed and dated, and you would be expending the rest of your prayer-life arguing against a closed file.**

So let your prayer turn elsewhere. Pray for those still in the ark. Pray for the pairs that will board. Pray for your own steadfastness during the rising water. Pray for the faith of the generations that will descend from this ship. *Those* prayers are the ones now in season.

[PAUSE]

#### The terrible kindness in this

I want you to feel how *kind* this instruction actually is.

If Allah had not told Nuh ﷺ this, Nuh would have spent the rest of his life — through the flood, through the landing on Judi, through the rebuilding — praying for people who were already beyond reach. And every prayer would have come back unanswered, and every unanswered prayer would have cracked his heart a little further.

Allah is sparing him. He is saying: **stop. They are mine now. Their account is closed. Yours is still open. Look forward, not back.**

This is what the closing of one door inside the unlimited mercy of *duʿāʾ* actually means. It is not Allah refusing to hear. It is Allah refusing to let His servant exhaust himself on a battle that has been concluded — so that his strength can be poured into the battles that are still being fought.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Here is the application. And it is delicate. So please hear me carefully.

This ayah is **not** a license to give up on people. The default for a believer is that the door of intercession is open. We pray for our parents whether they pray or not. We pray for our wandering siblings, our lost friends, our struggling children. We do not abandon them at the first refusal, or the tenth, or the hundredth. **Allah did not tell Nuh ﷺ to stop praying for his people until 950 years had passed.**

But this ayah tells us something else, and it is this: **at some point, you may need to give your prayers permission to grieve as well as to plead.**

Not every prayer for a loved one needs to be a prayer to *change* them. Sometimes the prayer is: *Ya Allah, I have asked for so long. You know what is best for them. Let me carry the love I have for them as a gift back to You. Show me what to ask now.*

That is a prayer the ayah opens for us. It is the prayer of the believer who has reached the edge of their pleading and finds that, instead of an empty wall, there is the gentle hand of Allah saying — *I have heard every word. Now look forward. The ark is being built. The pairs are gathering. There is more for you to do.*

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at what the ayah actually does, structurally.

It moves Nuh from **looking back** to **looking forward**, in three exact steps.

**Step one:** *Bi-aʿyuninā wa-waḥyinā.* The gaze and the revelation that will accompany you. Where you stand right now is witnessed.

**Step two:** *Idhā jāʾa amrunā wa-fāra-t-tannūr.* The sign that will mark the turning. Watch the ordinary. Watch the kitchen.

**Step three:** *Lā tukhāṭibnī fī-lladhīna ẓalamū.* Stop arguing the case that has been closed. Walk forward.

And bracketed inside step three is the entire human cargo: *fa-sluk fīhā min kullin zawjayni-thnayni wa-ahlaka.* **The pairs. Your family. The future.**

The ayah is teaching Nuh ﷺ — and us — how to **transition from the work of warning to the work of preserving.** And the hinge between the two is the closing of one door so that another can be entered.

### Bringing It Together

The three linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight from two directions.

The witness who replaces all witnesses (*bi-aʿyuninā*), the sign hidden in the ordinary (*fāra-t-tannūr*), and the door that closes in mercy (*lā tukhāṭibnī*) — these three are the architecture of how a believer survives the ending of one chapter and the beginning of another.

You are watched. So work in the dark.
You are warned. So watch the kitchen.
You are released. So stop arguing the closed file.

Build the ark.

### Questions to Carry

1. What work in my life right now am I doing for an audience that is barely looking? What would change if I rebuilt that work *bi-aʿyunih* — under His gaze instead?

2. What ordinary thing in my life have I stopped noticing? What if a sign were already gathering inside it?

3. Is there someone I have been praying for, for years, in a way that exhausts rather than nourishes me — and might Allah be inviting me to *change the prayer* rather than to stop it?

4. What is the ark I am supposed to be building right now, in a desert, while the people around me think I am wasting my time?

5. When the *tannūr* of my life finally boils over — am I the one who will recognize the sign, or the one who will dismiss it as nothing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the gaze you crave grows distant and the door you have been knocking on closes, Allah is not abandoning you — He is reorienting your work, your watching, and your prayer toward the ark that is still waiting to be built.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this ayah trying to figure out who in your life has crossed the threshold of *mughraqūn*. That is not your file. That is His.

Leave this ayah with three quiet practices.

**Watch your work** — even the work no one sees — as work being done under His eyes.

**Watch the ordinary** — your dawn, your kettle, your familiar verses — for the moment something unexpected breaks through.

**Watch your prayers** — not to silence them, but to ask whether some of them are ready to evolve. From pleading for a wall to fall, to pleading for the strength to walk past it toward the ark.

### A Brief Du'a

Ya Allah, You who said to Your prophet *build under Our eyes* — let every quiet act of worship I do today be done in that gaze, and let that gaze be enough.

Ya Allah, You who hid the sign of the flood inside an oven — let me have the eyes to see Your signs in the ordinary places of my life, the small strangenesses You are placing in my path.

Ya Allah, You who closed one door for Nuh ﷺ so that another could open — when I have prayed long for someone and the prayer has not turned, teach me what to ask now, and do not let me be among those whose hearts harden against Your decree.

*Āmīn.*
