# The Submerged Heart and the Bellowing Too Late

## Surah Al-Muʾminūn 23:63-65

> بَلْ قُلُوبُهُمْ فِي غَمْرَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْمَالٌ مِّن دُونِ ذَٰلِكَ هُمْ لَهَا عَامِلُونَ ﴿٦٣﴾ حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَخَذْنَا مُتْرَفِيهِم بِٱلْعَذَابِ إِذَا هُمْ يَجْأَرُونَ ﴿٦٤﴾ لَا تَجْأَرُوا ٱلْيَوْمَ ۖ إِنَّكُم مِّنَّا لَا تُنصَرُونَ ﴿٦٥﴾

> *Nay — their hearts are in a flood from this, and they have other deeds besides, which they are committed to doing. Until, when We seize their luxury-softened ones with the punishment, suddenly — they bellow. Bellow not today. From Us, you will not be rescued.*

---

## Introduction: The World Beneath the Words

Picture a house where the basement is slowly flooding.

At first nothing is wrong. The owner walks past the basement door and feels nothing different. Upstairs, the lights still work. The kitchen still smells like coffee. He goes about his day.

But somewhere below, water is seeping in. Slow at first. Then steady. Then it begins to climb the walls.

Months pass. The owner notices something — a vague dampness in the air, a slight musk in the laundry, a coldness in the floorboards he can't quite place. But the lights still work. The coffee still smells right. He files the strange dampness away under "I'll deal with it later" and keeps living.

By the time he finally opens that basement door, the water has reached the ceiling beams. The foundation is rotting. The wiring is shot. He didn't notice because he didn't look. He didn't look because nothing on the surface was demanding that he look.

[PAUSE]

This is the picture Allah is about to give us. Except the house is a heart, the water is something He will name with one of the most terrifying words in the Quran, and the basement door is something we have been walking past for years.

The surah we are inside has just finished describing the believers in the most beautiful terms. They race toward good. They tremble at their Lord. Their hearts are afraid even when they're doing right. We sat with that picture and we hoped — *may we be among them.*

Then Allah turns. And the turn is brutal. He turns to the people who do not race, who do not tremble, who do not even *hear* the call. And He doesn't describe their disbelief. He doesn't list their crimes. He gives us, instead, one word for what has happened to their hearts. One word that explains everything.

The word is **غَمْرَةٍ** — *ghamra*.

Stay with me. We have to earn this word before we open it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: غَمْرَةٍ — *Ghamra*. The Flood Inside the Chest.

Before we touch the Arabic, let me ask you a question.

What is the difference between *forgetting* something and being *too full* to remember it?

If I ask you what you ate last Tuesday, you've forgotten. The information was light — it slipped out. But if I ask you what you talked about with your father the last time you saw him, and you say "I don't know, my mind has been so full lately" — that's different. That's not forgetting. That's *flooding*. The information didn't slip out. It got pushed under, drowned beneath everything else you were carrying.

Arabic has a word for that second kind of forgetting. It is not *nasiya* (to forget). It is not *ghafala* (to be heedless in the soft sense). It is **ghamr** — to *submerge*.

The classical lexicons are precise about this root. غ-م-ر — Lisān al-ʿArab tells us — describes water *so abundant that it covers what is beneath it.* It is what the desert Arabs called the part of a river so deep your foot couldn't find the bottom. It is what swordsmen called the moment when the enemies were so many they were *covered over* by them — *ghamarahum al-qitāl*, "the fighting submerged them." The same root gives us *ghimr* — a swallowing rancor, a hatred that has *flooded* the chest. And it gives us **ghamra** — the state of being underwater, the condition of submergence itself.

So now let's open the ayah.

> بَلْ قُلُوبُهُمْ فِي غَمْرَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا

Allah does not say *their hearts have forgotten this.* He does not say *their hearts have turned away from this.* He does not even say *their hearts are heedless of this.*

He says **their hearts are *in a submergence* from this.**

Let's sit with what this changes.

A heart that has forgotten can be reminded — call it, and it turns. A heart that is heedless can be startled — shake it, and it wakes. But a heart *in ghamra* is not nearby. It is not a few feet away under a thin layer. It is *under the water*. It is in the basement that has filled to the ceiling. You can stand at the door and shout the truth at it, but the truth has to travel through everything that has been poured on top of it before it reaches the heart. And the further down the heart sinks, the less the truth can reach.

Here is what is remarkable. Notice that Allah says the heart is in ghamra **مِّنْ هَٰذَا** — *from this.* The flood doesn't just exist. It exists *from* something. The flood has a source.

What is *hādhā* — *this*?

Al-Tabari is unambiguous: هَٰذَا is the Quran. This is the identification the classical tradition anchors to. The other descriptions found in classical commentary — *this warning, this guidance the Messenger ﷺ has brought* — are not competing readings but names for what the Quran *is*. Each is a camera angle on the same referent. The flood-source is the very thing that should have saved them.

[PAUSE]

Read it that way and the ayah becomes terrifying.

The Quran is recited to a heart. If the heart receives it, the Quran becomes light — it lifts the heart, opens it, makes it spacious. If the heart resists it, the Quran does not vanish. It accumulates. It collects. Every recitation that was meant to part the waters and let the heart cross becomes, instead, *more water* — pooling on top of the heart that wouldn't open. Until the heart isn't just unguided. It is *submerged in the very thing that was sent to lift it.*

Al-Tabari adds a precision here that sharpens this further. He describes the ghamra as covering the heart from understanding *"the admonitions, the moral lessons, and the rational proofs"* that Allah deposited in His Book — *al-mawāʿiẓ, al-ʿibar, al-ḥujaj*. The flood does not merely block the text. It blocks access to what the text *contains* — its warnings, its parables, its arguments. The submerged heart is sealed off not from words but from meaning. It cannot reach the admonition that might have moved it, the lesson embedded in a story, the proof that should have stilled its objection. The water is between the heart and all of that.

This is what Allah means when He elsewhere says وَنُنَزِّلُ مِنَ ٱلْقُرْآنِ مَا هُوَ شِفَآءٌ وَرَحْمَةٌ لِّلْمُؤْمِنِينَ ۙ وَلَا يَزِيدُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ إِلَّا خَسَارًا — *We send down of the Quran what is healing and mercy for the believers, and it increases the wrongdoers only in loss.* The same rain. Two soils. One garden. One mudslide.

What this invites us to consider is something most of us never measure. We measure how much Quran we've heard. We do not measure what happens in the heart that *doesn't open* to what it heard. The unopened recitation does not stay neutral. It piles up.

A clear statement of what this single word reveals: **the heart that refuses guidance is not merely without it — it is *underneath* it.**

### Element Two: هُمْ لَهَا عَامِلُونَ — The Grammar of Total Commitment

> وَلَهُمْ أَعْمَالٌ مِّن دُونِ ذَٰلِكَ هُمْ لَهَا عَامِلُونَ
> *And they have deeds besides that — for them, doers, they are.*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open this.

In Arabic, you can say "they are doing it" in a few different ways. The fastest way is just the verb: يَعْمَلُونَ — *yaʿmalūn*, three syllables, done. The verb in Arabic already contains its subject — *they* — built into its conjugation. You don't need to add a separate pronoun.

But Allah does add one. He says **هُمْ لَهَا عَامِلُونَ** — *hum lahā ʿāmilūn.*

Three things are happening at once.

First, He puts the pronoun **هُمْ** (*they*) separately, when the grammar didn't require it. In Arabic rhetoric, this is called *taqdīm al-mubtadaʾ* — fronting the subject as its own emphatic element. The rough English equivalent is the difference between *"they're doing it"* and ***they*** *are the ones doing it.* The pronoun stops being decoration and starts pointing.

Second, He fronts **لَهَا** (*for them / to them / toward them*) before the participle that should follow the verb. Word order in Arabic carries weight — what gets pulled forward is what is being highlighted. He pulls the *attachment* forward: *to those deeds.*

Third — and this is the one most people miss — He uses an *active participle* (ʿāmilūn) instead of the present-tense verb (yaʿmalūn). The verb describes an action happening now. The active participle describes a state of being one who does. The verb is "they're working." The participle is "they are workers."

Put it together and the literal flavor is: *And as for them, to those deeds, they are committed-doers.*

What does this construction reveal about the human reality?

Here is what is remarkable. Allah is not telling us they *do* other things besides ignoring guidance. He is telling us they are *defined* by those other things. Their identity has migrated. The deeds beneath the surface that are flooding the heart are not random tasks they happen to be performing. Those deeds have become *who they are.* They have a self that is built around them. *Hum lahā ʿāmilūn* — they belong to those deeds, not the other way around.

Notice something else. The ayah began with **بَلْ قُلُوبُهُمْ** — *their hearts.* Then it said the hearts are in a flood. Then it pivots: *and they have deeds besides.* Then the emphatic structure: *they are doers of those.*

Watch the architecture. The heart is submerged → the deeds remain. The relationship has flipped. Normally, the heart steers the deeds. The heart loves something, so the body acts. But once the heart is underwater, the *deeds* are what's running. The body is on autopilot. Habits, routines, ambitions, schedules, projects — they keep going. They keep *being done*. But there is no longer a heart awake at the center, deciding. The man is *operated by* his deeds rather than operating them.

And al-Muyassar adds a layer of cosmic patience that makes this even weightier. Those deeds — the deeds the submerged heart keeps performing — happen inside a window of divine respite. Allah grants them time: *He delays them so they may do those deeds,* and so bring upon themselves what awaits. The respite is not mercy extended indefinitely. It is the completion of the record. The door stays open not because it will never close, but because the case must be made complete before it does. Every deed performed in that window, in that productive obliviousness, is another line in a ledger that is being kept with perfect precision.

This is one of the most subtle and modern psychological observations in scripture.

A man can be deeply, demonstrably busy — productive, even successful — *while his heart is at the bottom of a flooded basement.* The deeds keep happening. The career advances. The to-do list shortens. None of this is evidence of life. It is evidence only that the machinery is still running. *Hum lahā ʿāmilūn* — they are committed-doers. Of what? Of *something other than the thing the heart was made for.* Of *deeds besides that.* And God is watching every one of those deeds, in patient silence, as the ledger fills.

What this invites us to consider — gently — is the question: *what would you find if you opened the basement door of your own life right now?* Would the lights be on at the top of the house, with the work getting done? Probably. Would something be flooding underneath, undetected, that has been there a long time?

A clear statement of what this construction reveals: **a person can be relentlessly productive in the wrong direction, and the productivity itself — watched, recorded, and granted time by divine patience — becomes the proof that the heart has gone under.**

### Element Three: يَجْأَرُونَ — The Sound of Cattle in a Human Throat

Now we come to the word that, once you understand it, you will never read this ayah the same way.

> حَتَّىٰ إِذَا أَخَذْنَا مُتْرَفِيهِم بِٱلْعَذَابِ إِذَا هُمْ يَجْأَرُونَ
> *Until, when We seize their luxury-softened ones with the punishment, suddenly — they bellow.*

Two structural notes first, then the word.

Notice **حَتَّىٰ إِذَا** — *until, when.* Until in Arabic is a temporal limit — the flood goes on, the deeds go on, the unawareness goes on, *until.* And then **إِذَا** appears twice in this short ayah. The first *idhā* is conditional — *when We seize them.* The second *idhā* is what Arabic grammarians call **إذا الفجائية** — the *idhā* of suddenness, the *idhā* of *behold!* — used when something erupts unexpectedly into the scene. The first idhā is "when the trigger is pulled." The second idhā is "and *just like that*..."

The structure tells us: the punishment doesn't arrive after a slow buildup of awareness. It arrives, and *suddenly, immediately, without warning, they are doing this.*

Doing what?

**يَجْأَرُونَ** — *yajʾarūn.*

The root is ج-أ-ر. And here is what the lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib, Tāj al-ʿArūs — agree on with striking unanimity:

***Al-juʾār* is the bellowing of cattle.**

It is the deep guttural lowing of a cow that has lost its calf. It is the heavy frightened sound of an ox under stress. It is the noise that comes out of an animal in an animal's throat — primal, formless, without language. The classical Arabs used it for the cry of camels and oxen. Then, by extension, for the sound of a human voice when the human voice has been stripped of everything that makes it human — articulation, self-possession, dignity — and left with only its rawness.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. This is one of the most devastating word choices in the Quran.

Allah is describing human beings. Wealthy human beings. Powerful human beings — that word *mutrafīn*, which we'll come to in a moment, denotes the elite who have been softened by abundance. These are not the desperate poor. These are the men with summer estates, the men with consultants and chauffeurs, the men whose words used to bend rooms.

And when the punishment seizes them, Allah does not say *they cry out.* He does not say *they wail.* He does not say *they call for help.* He says *they bellow.*

Al-Tabari notes, with almost anthropological precision, that *juʾār* means *raising the voice* — and immediately cites the pre-Islamic poet al-Aʿsha to fix the word's range. The Aʿsha uses it in a religious context: *"sometimes prostration, and sometimes juʾār"* — the two postures of a worshipper before his lord. In classical usage, the word bridged the animal cry and the full-throated outcry of supplication — the raw, unguarded sound a person makes when they have nothing left but their voice and their need.

The irony is devastating. *Juʾār* in the classical tradition could name the very intensity of worship — the moment when prayer becomes a cry, when the believer has no more composure and only raw reaching. And now that same *juʾār* arrives, finally, in the throats of these men. But not as worship. As panic. Al-Muyassar captures what the other sources leave implicit: when they cry, they cry *in humble beseeching, calling for help* — *yatadarraʿūna mustaghīthīn*. The posture is now right. The head has dropped. The arrogance is gone. Something in the form of the cry resembles what prayer always was. But the time has passed.

They are, finally, crying in the right direction. And the door is shut.

[PAUSE]

The cry that arrives too late uses the same throat that refused to speak in time. The tragedy is not only the decades of silence that preceded it — it is the prayer-shaped sound that finally emerges when there is no longer anyone to answer.

A clear statement of what this word reveals: **the sound of a heart that waited too long is not silence — it is something louder than silence, something animal and desperate, something that has the shape of supplication without the soul of it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Four: مُتْرَفِيهِمْ — The Ones the World Made Great

We need to pause on the word Allah uses to identify *who* gets seized.

> إِذَا أَخَذْنَا مُتْرَفِيهِم بِٱلْعَذَابِ
> *When We seize their mutrafīn with the punishment*

The root ت-ر-ف describes being given such abundance that one becomes negligent, softened, self-indulgent — the word implies a process, not a state. The *mutraf* is not simply wealthy. He has been *made* by wealth into someone whose comfort has become his walls. He has been given enough ease that the hard edges of reality — consequence, mortality, accountability — never pressed in on him the way they press in on everyone else.

Al-Tabari transmits from Ibn Zayd a specification that changes the picture. Ibn Zayd says plainly: *"al-mutrafūn: al-ʿuẓamāʾ"* — the *mutrafūn* are the great ones. The nobles. The chiefs. Not merely the comfortable in general, but specifically the men of status and influence — the ones whose example a community follows, the ones whose approval shapes what is said and what is left unsaid.

This is important. Allah does not say He seizes *some* of them, or even *all* of them. He says He seizes their *mutrafīn* — the leading class. The people who had the most resources to hear, the most exposure to the truth, and who built instead the culture of silence that made the ghamra possible for everyone below them. The ones whose wealth insulated them from urgency, and whose influence spread that insulation to others.

When punishment arrives, it seizes them first — the noble, the powerful, the great. The ones who benefited most from the flood they helped maintain. And their cry — the bellow of these formerly great men — is the most terrible of all, because they had every instrument at their disposal to avert it.

[PAUSE]

What is also worth noting: the construction is **مُتْرَفِيهِم** — *their* mutrafīn. The possessive connects the leaders to the community. This is a community whose own greatness — its luminaries, its establishment, its celebrated — has been sealed off from the Quran. The problem is not at the margins. It is at the center. The ghamra went deepest in exactly the people who should have been the lifeguards.

A clear statement of what this word reveals: **luxury does not merely soften the individual — it produces a class of the spiritually untouchable, whose elevation becomes, in the end, the measure of how far they had to fall.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Five: لَا تَجْأَرُوا ٱلْيَوْمَ — Bellow Not Today

> لَا تَجْأَرُوا ٱلْيَوْمَ ۖ إِنَّكُم مِّنَّا لَا تُنصَرُونَ
> *Bellow not today. From Us, you will not be rescued.*

Notice the shift.

For two ayahs, Allah has been speaking *about* them. Third person: *their* hearts, *their* deeds, *their* mutrafīn, *they* bellow. The narrative held them at a distance. We were watching from outside the scene.

Then the scene changes. Suddenly He is speaking *to* them. Second person: *you* will not be rescued.

The shift from *hum* to *antum* is the closing of the distance. They were being described. Now they are being addressed. The gap that permitted hope — the gap of narration, of this-is-about-them-not-you — collapses.

**لَا تَجْأَرُوا** — *Do not bellow.* He uses their exact word as the prohibition. He does not say "do not weep" or "do not call" or "do not ask." He takes the specific animal-sound they are making and prohibits it by name. The word they are using to cry out is the word He uses to silence them. Their cry is met with its own name — and the naming deflates it. What was a desperate reaching upward becomes, in this one line, a sound He has already heard, already identified, and already placed outside the range of what will now change anything.

**ٱلْيَوْمَ** — *today.* The word *yawm* in these sequences carries the full weight of irreversibility. This is not any day. This is the day that was always coming — the day that all those submerged years were moving toward. *Today* is what *hattā* was pointing at from the beginning: *until...* Until today. And today is different from every day that preceded it, because today is when time runs out.

**مِّنَّا لَا تُنصَرُونَ** — *From Us, you will not be rescued.* The preposition *min* marks the source: rescue will not come *from Us.* And if it does not come from the One in whose hands all rescue lives, there is no other source to try. The cry reaches upward. The answer comes back: *not from here. Not today. Not any longer.*

[PAUSE]

This is the closing of the trap. And it closed long before this moment. The trap was not set in the instant the punishment arrived. It was set the first time a heart heard the truth and filed it under "later." It was set in the slow accumulation of days when the basement was quietly filling. By the time the ghamra reached the ceiling — the door was already sealed from the other side, not by cruelty, but by the logic of a record that was always being kept.

The response to the bellow is not a punishment. It is an answer. A true answer. *You will not be rescued — from Us.* The *from Us* is precise. It names the only place rescue ever came from. It names it as the place it will no longer come from. Not because the capacity is gone. Because the time is.

## The Architecture of These Three Ayahs

Now pull back and see all three ayahs as a single movement.

Ayah 63 is diagnosis: the heart is under the water, and the body is still running. Ayah 64 is the seizure: when the flood that was internal becomes external — when what was growing silently below erupts above — and the response is animal. Ayah 65 is the closing: the second person address, the prohibition, the declaration of helplessness.

The movement is: *inside* to *outside* to *shut.*

What is also structurally remarkable is the absence of any middle scene. There is no moment where someone tries to warn the mutrafīn before the punishment arrives. There is no negotiation. There is no scene of gradual realization. The ayahs move directly from *they are committed-doers of their deeds* to *suddenly they bellow.* The **إذا الفجائية** — the idhā of suddenness — is doing theological work. The nature of this particular blindness is that it does not give warning. The basement floods and floods and floods, and then — *suddenly* — the door opens and everything is underwater. The nature of a covered heart is that it does not feel its own covering.

This is why the Quran elsewhere asks: *"Then do they not reflect upon the Quran, or are there locks upon their hearts?"* (47:24). The locks are not put there by God. They are built from the inside — deed by deed, refusal by refusal, every recitation that slid past without opening anything. And once the locks are set, the covering is complete. The heart is in the basement with the water at the ceiling.

[PAUSE]

The lesson is not that these people were uniquely wicked. The lesson is that the process is invisible. The ghamra does not announce itself. The *mutraf* does not wake up one morning and declare himself sealed off from the Quran. He goes about his day. His lights still work. The coffee still smells right. The business still runs. *Hum lahā ʿāmilūn* — they are committed-doers. Of so much. Of almost everything. Just not of the one thing their hearts were built for.

And all of it, every busy productive sealed-off day of it, is being watched and recorded and given time inside a divine patience that will one day, suddenly, close.

## Sitting With These Ayahs

Before you close this reflection, consider three questions slowly.

*What would you find if you opened the basement door of your own heart right now?* Not your calendar, not your prayer record, not your productivity metrics — the basement. The place where the slow water has been going that you have not checked lately.

*What deeds are you most committed to — and do you have a heart that is steering them, or a habit that is running without one?* The grammar of *hum lahā ʿāmilūn* is not describing lazy people. It is describing people who are busy to their core. Busyness is not the opposite of ghamra. Busyness is sometimes its evidence.

*When was the last time the Quran landed somewhere and changed something — and when was the last time it went past without opening anything?* Both happen. The question is the ratio, and the direction of travel.

[PAUSE]

These ayahs are not a warning about a distant, historical people who refused to hear. They are a clinical description of a mechanism — a mechanism as available today as it was in Mecca, one that does not require extraordinary wickedness to engage. It requires only the ordinary human genius for living on the surface of things while something deeper fills without being noticed.

The surah placed the trembling believers just before this passage. *Those* people were afraid even when they were doing right. Their hearts were open enough to fear. *These* people are not afraid because their hearts cannot feel the water rising. The difference is not in the deeds that show. It is in what is happening, quietly, in the basement that no one is checking.

May Allah keep our hearts above the waterline. May He make the Quran a light that lifts rather than a flood that submerges. May He give us hearts that tremble before it reaches us — hearts that hear the admonition, receive the lesson, meet the proof — rather than hearts sealed off from all of that, running on autopilot in a house whose basement we have not opened in years.
