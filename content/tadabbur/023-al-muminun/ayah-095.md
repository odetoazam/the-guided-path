# We Are Surely Able: The Power Held Back

## The Ayah

[ayah:23:95] وَإِنَّا عَلَىٰٓ أَن نُّرِيَكَ مَا نَعِدُهُمْ لَقَـٰدِرُونَ

**Transliteration:** Wa innā ʿalā an nuriyaka mā naʿiduhum la-qādirūn.

**Translation:** "And indeed, We are surely able to show you what We promise them."

---

## Where We Are in the Surah

We have traveled a long road to get here.

Surah Al-Mu'minun opened with the qualities of believers — humility in prayer, truth in speech, faithfulness in trust. Then it moved through the stories of past prophets and their rejection: Nuh, Hud, Musa, ʿIsa. Each one came. Each one was rejected with almost the same formula: *He is just a man like you. He is just a man who has fabricated a lie.*

By the time we reach this ayah, we have watched generation after generation refuse the messengers Allah sent them. The pattern doesn't break. It repeats.

Then the surah turns to the present — to the Prophet ﷺ standing in Mecca, surrounded by people doing exactly what every prior generation did. They mock him. They call him possessed. They demand to see the punishment he warns about, almost as a dare.

And then comes a moment that breaks your heart open.

In the ayah just before this one, Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ to say: *"My Lord, if You should show me what they are promised — my Lord, do not place me among the wrongdoing people."*

The Prophet ﷺ is being taught a du'a. **Don't let me be there when the punishment lands.** The classical commentators approach this supplication from different but complementary angles. Al-Tabari reads the divine response that follows as a consolation against grief — the implied wound is the grief of rejection, years of carrying a true message to people who refuse it (*lā yaḥzunnaka takdhībuhum*: let not their rejection grieve you). Ibn Kathir frames the supplication as one taught precisely *when calamity is imminent* — a posture of holy distance from the punishment that is drawing near. And from what the Seerah tells us of the Prophet ﷺ — a man the Quran elsewhere describes as *ra'ūfun rahīm*, overflowing with compassion — the prospect of witnessing destruction fall upon even those who rejected him would have been its own weight. These readings are not competing; they are layers of a single ache held in a single du'a.

And then Allah responds. Not with reassurance about the Prophet's safety. With something else entirely.

**"And indeed, We are surely able to show you what We promise them."**

[PAUSE]

Why does Allah respond to the Prophet's plea — *don't place me among them* — with a statement of capacity? "We are surely able." Why is this Allah's answer? And why, in the very next breath, does Allah pivot to: "Repel evil with what is best"?

There is something here about how power and restraint speak to each other in the divine speech. Let's listen.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element 1: The Triple Lock — إنّا...لـ...قادرون

In Arabic, when you want to emphasize that something is true, you have layers. You can use one emphasis. You can stack two. You can stack three.

In English, if you wanted to insist something was real, you might say: *"It's true."* If you wanted more emphasis: *"It really is true."* If you wanted to drive it home: *"Believe me when I say — it is absolutely, certainly true."*

Arabic builds this same structure into its grammar. Each particle is a hammer-blow. You can choose how many to swing.

In this ayah, Allah uses **three**.

First: **إنّا (innā)** — "Indeed We." The particle *inna* is itself an emphasizer. It says *this is not casual speech; this is being asserted with weight.*

Second: **لـ (the lām of emphasis)** — attached to the predicate. The lām before a word says *and especially this, this part — this is what I am locking in.*

Third: **قادرون (qādirūn)** — not a verb, but a noun-form (active participle) in the plural. We'll come back to this in a moment.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The grammatical sentence "We are able" could have been said in Arabic with one short word: نقدر (*naqdiru*). One verb. Done.

But Allah doesn't say *naqdir*.

Allah says: **wa innā ʿalā an nuriyaka mā naʿiduhum la-qādirūn.**

Stretched out. Built like a fortress. Every clause emphasized. Every gate locked.

Why?

Because of what it is responding to.

The Prophet ﷺ has just been taught a du'a that contains, inside it, a quiet ache. Not a doubt about Allah's truth — but the kind of weight the human heart carries when it has been delivering a message no one wants to receive: *Will the promise actually come? When? Am I going to live to see it? Is anyone listening?*

When you're the only one telling the truth in a room of mockers, your soul whispers: *Are you sure?*

And Allah — who knows the heart of His Beloved better than the Beloved knows it himself — does not respond with logic. He responds with **lock-tight, triple-bolted emphasis**: We — and indeed We — and certainly — are able.

You did not mishear, beloved. The promise is not provisional. The promise is not at the mercy of their mockery. **We — are — able.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The heart that hears triple emphasis is the heart that needed it.

---

### Element 2: نُريَك — Not "Tell You," But "Show You"

The verb in this ayah is unusual. It is not "We will *tell you* what We promise them." It is not "We will *inform you*" or "We will *narrate to you*."

It is **nuriyaka** — from the root ر-أ-ي, which means *to see*.

In its base form, *raʾā* means "he saw."

But Allah uses **Form IV** — *arā* (أرى) — which is the causative form. Causative in Arabic is when you take a verb and apply it to someone else. Not "he saw" but "he made [someone] see." Not "see" but **show**.

So *nuriyaka* means: **We will make YOU see it. We will show YOU.**

[Teach the concept]

Think about the difference in your own life between being *told* something and being *shown* something. Imagine someone says: "There's a beautiful view from the top of that mountain." That is information. You can choose to believe it or not.

Now imagine they take you up the mountain themselves. They walk you to the edge. They turn you toward the horizon.

*That* is being shown.

When something is shown, it is no longer in the realm of testimony. It is in the realm of witness. You become an eyewitness.

[Bring it back]

Allah did not say: "We are able to *describe* what We promise them." He said: "We are able to **show you**."

Why?

Because the Prophet ﷺ has been doing the work of describing. For years. He has been saying: there will be a punishment, there will be a reckoning, there will be a day when this becomes real. And the disbelievers have been responding: *show us, then. We don't want descriptions. We want sights.*

And Allah, here, does not address the disbelievers' demand. He addresses the Prophet's exhaustion.

*Beloved, the very thing they keep demanding to see — We can show you. They do not even know that the moment they get what they ask for, you will already have been honored. The reality you have been describing is not a description. It is a sight. We can render it visible whenever We will.*

There is a tenderness in this verb that the simpler verb *to tell* would have lost.

[PAUSE]

### Element 3: مَا نَعِدُهُم — The Promise That Keeps Promising

In Arabic, verbs carry tense. *Waʿada* means "he promised" — past, finished. *Yaʿidu* means "he promises / is promising / will promise" — present-continuous.

Here, Allah uses the present-continuous form: **naʿiduhum** — "We **are promising** them" / "We **keep promising** them."

This is significant.

Because the promise of punishment for the wrongdoers is not a one-time announcement. It is not something Allah said once and the disbelievers happened to miss. It is a continuous promise, repeated through the Quran, through every messenger, through every warning, again and again and again.

The verb form refuses to let this become past tense.

Even right now, as you sit reading this, Allah is still promising them.

[Sit with it]

There is a profound mercy hidden inside this present-continuous verb. The promise is ongoing because *the door is still open*. As long as Allah is still *promising* — present tense — the moment of fulfillment has not yet arrived. As long as the verb remains in *naʿiduhum* and has not switched to a past form (*waʿadnāhum* — "We promised them") that signals completed delivery, there is still time.

**The grammatical tense of this verb is, itself, evidence that the warning is also an invitation.**

The same God who is able to show His Beloved the punishment is, in the same breath, still in the act of promising — not yet executing. The Prophet's heart is reassured by Allah's capacity. But the disbelievers themselves are still standing in the window of the open door.

[PAUSE]

### Element 4: قادرون — The Standing Posture of Power

The final word: **qādirūn**.

This is not a verb. *Naqdir* would have been "We are able / We can." A verb. An action.

*Qādirūn* is an **active participle**, plural — the form that names someone *characterized by* the action. Not "We can" but "We are *the ones who can*."

In English we would say: *He runs* (verb) versus *He is a runner* (participle). The verb describes an act. The participle describes a *state of being*.

[Teach the concept]

Imagine a fireman, off-duty, sitting at a coffee shop. Even though he is not actively putting out fires right now, he is still *a fireman*. The participle names his standing capacity, not the moment of action.

When Allah uses **qādirūn** rather than *naqdir*, He is not just saying "We can do this thing." He is saying: **the capacity to do this is part of who We are. Standing. Permanent. Not turned on for this occasion.**

This matters because the Prophet ﷺ might worry: *will Allah's power still be there when the time comes?*

The participle answers: **the power is not summoned for the event. The power is the constant. It does not need to be activated. It is who We are.**

[PAUSE]

Whatever moment the Prophet ﷺ is in — moments of being mocked, moments of being threatened, moments when his soul felt the weight of unbelief pressing on him — Allah is, *right then*, the One characterized by capacity. Not "going to be" able. Not "could become" able. **Already, always, fundamentally able.**

That is the closing word of the ayah. That is the note Allah leaves the Prophet on.

---

### The Linguistic Bridge

Look at what these four pieces have built.

A triple emphasis that says *believe Me without hesitation*. A causative verb that says *I can show you, not just tell you*. A present-continuous verb that says *the door is still open while I speak*. And an active participle that says *My capacity is not summoned for the occasion — My capacity is who I am.*

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the relationship between divine power and divine response. Because the very next ayah is about to do something that, in any other narrative, would not make sense.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Power Held Back

Imagine you are walking home and you see someone keying your car. They are scratching it deeply. They have not seen you yet. You have your keys, your phone, your full physical strength. You are *able* to confront them. You are able to call the police. You are able to shout, to chase, to act.

What does it mean if, knowing you are able, you decide instead to walk past — and only address it later, calmly, after the moment has passed?

Two things must both be true for that walking-past to mean anything:
1. **You were able.**
2. **You chose not to act in that moment.**

If you were not able, your inaction would not be a choice. It would be helplessness. **Restraint is only meaningful when paired with capacity.**

[Bring it to the ayah]

This is exactly what the ayah is establishing.

The disbelievers in Mecca are mocking the Prophet ﷺ. They are tormenting his followers. They are openly daring Allah to punish them as proof of His existence.

And Allah does not punish them.

What is the Prophet ﷺ to make of that? His own heart could whisper: *does Allah see this? Is the promise real? Does the absence of punishment mean the absence of capacity?*

Allah's answer is this ayah. **The capacity is locked in.** Triple-bolted. Active-participled. Causative-verbed. Present-continuous-promised.

And then — *and only then* — the very next ayah says:

**ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ السَّيِّئَةَ — "Repel evil with what is best."**

Do you see what just happened?

Allah first establishes that He is able to bring the punishment **at any moment, in any way, with full capacity**. Only after this is locked in does He say to the Prophet ﷺ: *now respond to them with kindness.*

**Restraint without capacity is humiliation. Restraint with capacity is mercy.**

The forbearance of the Prophet ﷺ in Mecca — and the forbearance of his community to this day — is not the forbearance of the powerless. It is the forbearance of those whose Lord is *qādirūn* and *chooses* to delay. The patience is dignified, not desperate. It is held inside divine power, not in its absence.

But here the classical tafsir tradition adds a dimension that "restraint-as-mercy" alone does not fully capture. Al-Tabari, commenting on this very verse, records a precise reason for the delay: *"We only defer this so that the Scripture may reach its appointed term (ليبلغ الكتاب أجله)."* This is not mercy as a vague divine disposition. It is restraint governed by a *decree*. There is an *ajal* — a fixed and appointed term, already inscribed in divine knowledge — at which the promise will land. The delay is not open-ended. It is not contingent on whether the disbelievers might eventually change. It is exactly the length it was always going to be: no shorter, no longer. The power is held back not indefinitely but *precisely*, for as long as the appointed term runs — and when that term expires, the full weight of what was promised becomes inevitable.

This matters. Restraint-as-mercy tells you that Allah is patient with creation. Restraint-as-decree tells you that the appointed term is real — fixed, running, and counted — and the mockers are not escaping it. They are moving *toward* it, one breath at a time, within a timeline settled before the earth was spread.

[Sit with it]

How many times in our own lives have we mistaken Allah's delay for Allah's distance? How many times have we wondered: *if You can, why don't You?* And how often have we missed that the very fact that He hasn't, while He can, is the greatest gift to whoever still has time?

The disbelievers' continued breath, in this very moment in the surah, is **proof of Allah's restraint**, not proof of His weakness. Every breath the wrongdoer takes is a breath He could have stopped. The fact that He didn't is mercy. The fact that He could have is justice. The fact that there is a fixed ajal governing when that changes is the hinge on which both turn. **All three are true at once.**

This is what *qādirūn* in its standing form, paired with *naʿiduhum* in the present tense, is doing: **Capacity now. Promise now. Appointed term — already written. Fulfillment — not yet.**

[PAUSE]

#### What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

Maybe it changes the way I read every moment when wrongdoing seems to go unanswered — in the world, in my own life, in the lives of those I love. Maybe it changes me from someone who interprets delay as absence into someone who interprets delay as a still-open door — *and* as the running of a decree I cannot see but can trust. The door is open not because the promise is weak, but because the ajal has not yet arrived. Mine and theirs.
