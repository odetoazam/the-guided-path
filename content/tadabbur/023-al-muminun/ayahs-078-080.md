# The Triple Witness — Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 78–80

## Introduction

> [ayah:23:78] وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنشَأَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَـٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ ۚ قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ
> *Wa-Huwa-lladhī anshaʾa lakumu-s-samʿa wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidah — qalīlan mā tashkurūn.*
> "And it is He Who originated for you hearing, the sights, and the inner-hearts — little are you grateful."

> [ayah:23:79] وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى ذَرَأَكُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ
> *Wa-Huwa-lladhī dharaʾakum fī-l-arḍi wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn.*
> "And it is He Who scattered you across the earth, and to Him you will be gathered."

> [ayah:23:80] وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ وَلَهُ ٱخْتِلَـٰفُ ٱلَّيْلِ وَٱلنَّهَارِ ۚ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ
> *Wa-Huwa-lladhī yuḥyī wa-yumītu wa-lahu-khtilāfu-l-layli wan-nahār — afalā taʿqilūn.*
> "And it is He Who gives life and causes death, and to Him belongs the alternation of night and day — will you not then reason?"

### Rebuilding the World

To stand inside these ayahs you have to feel what came just before them.

For more than fifty ayahs, Surah Al-Mu'minun has been showing us a particular kind of human being. Not a stranger. Not a monster. Just a person who has been *given everything* — and who has built a way of looking at the world that allows him to see *none of it as a gift*.

The surah opened with the believers and what they will inherit — the highest gardens, where they remain. Then it walked us through the stages of our own creation: a drop, then a clinging thing, then a chewed lump, then bones, then flesh — and then *another creation*, that mysterious leap when something inside us became *us*. Then it took us out into the wider creation: the rains pouring down measured from the sky, the gardens we eat from, the cattle we ride and drink from. Then the messengers came — Nūḥ, then generations after him, then Mūsā and Hārūn, then ʿĪsā and his mother. Each of them delivered the same message. Each of them was met with the same shrug.

By the time we arrive at our three ayahs, the surah has been describing the rejecters in a particular way. They were tested with hardship and did not soften. They were given relief and did not turn. They heard ancient signs and called them *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — old people's stories. The picture being painted is of a person whose **doors of perception are open but whose response is locked.**

And now — right here — the surah does something remarkable. It stops describing *them* and turns to address *us*. Three statements arrive, one after another, each opening with exactly the same phrase: **"And it is He Who…" / "And it is He Who…" / "And it is He Who…"**

Classical commentators introduce this passage by noting its audience with care: these three ayahs are addressed specifically to those who deny the reality of resurrection after death. That framing matters. The pivot here is not merely from description to exhortation — it is from narrative to *argument*. The surah is not only saying: look at what you have been given and be grateful. It is saying: look at what He has already done, and reason about what He can do.

[PAUSE]

Three times in a row, the same opening. Three times in a row, the claim renewed. Three times in a row, the witness re-presented.

### The Hook

Every word in these three ayahs is famous. Hearing. Sight. Heart. Earth. Gathering. Life. Death. Night. Day. There is nothing exotic here. Nothing requires a footnote.

And yet — Allah did not arrange them carelessly. He chose to give us *one set of faculties*, then *one trajectory through the earth*, then *one rhythm of time*. He chose to begin each statement with the same phrase. He chose to end each with a different rebuke. He chose to make hearing singular while making sight and heart plural. He chose the verb "scattered" — *dharaʾakum* — for our distribution across the earth, when He could have said simply "created."

Stay with me. There is a quiet architecture in these three ayahs that, once you see it, never lets you read your own life the same way again.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — The Triple "And It Is He Who"

Before we touch the words themselves, let us teach a small thing about how Arabic builds emphasis.

In English, if you wanted to insist that a particular person — and only that person — did something, you might say: *"He's the one who built this house."* The phrase *the one who* tightens the sentence. It says: not someone else, not by accident, but specifically him.

Arabic has a parallel structure: **هُوَ الَّذِي** — *Huwa-lladhī* — literally "He is the one who." The pronoun *huwa* establishes the subject. The relative pronoun *alladhī* opens a clause that defines him by what he has done. Together they create a sentence that locks onto a single agent and says: *this — and no one else.*

Now look at our three ayahs. Every single one of them opens with the same phrase:

- وَهُوَ الَّذِي أَنشَأَ — *And it is He Who originated…*
- وَهُوَ الَّذِي ذَرَأَكُمْ — *And it is He Who scattered you…*
- وَهُوَ الَّذِي يُحْيِي وَيُمِيتُ — *And it is He Who gives life and causes death…*

Notice something. Allah did not say: "He originated for you hearing, sight, and hearts; He scattered you across the earth; He gives life and death." That would have been *one sentence* with one subject and three verbs. Compact. Efficient. Easier to memorize.

Instead, He chose to **restart the claim three times.** He chose to repeat the entire frame — *And it is He Who, and it is He Who, and it is He Who* — as if the rebuke is not in any one statement but in *the act of repeating itself.*

Let's sit with this. Why would you say the same opening three times when you could say it once?

Imagine a person standing in a courtroom, and a witness rises and says: *"It was him."* Then sits down. Then stands up again and says: *"It was him."* Then sits down. Then stands up *a third time* and says: *"It was him."*

Each time the witness rises, the silence before he speaks gets heavier. By the third time, the room is no longer absorbing information. It is absorbing **insistence.** The witness is not adding new evidence. He is forcing you to face the *same* evidence again, until you cannot pretend you did not hear it.

This is what Allah is doing. He is not describing three separate phenomena. He is **bearing witness three times to the same fact**: that *He* is the One behind everything you experience.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is remarkable. After each rising of this witness, the closing of the ayah changes:

- After the first: *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — *"little are you grateful."*
- After the second: *wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn* — *"and to Him you will be gathered."*
- After the third: *afalā taʿqilūn* — *"will you not then reason?"*

Notice the escalation. The first closing is a *complaint* — you have not given the response you owe. The second closing is a *prediction* — the day is coming when you will be brought back regardless. The third closing is a *question* — at what point does your mind start working?

The triple opening creates the rhythm. The three different closings escalate inside that rhythm. Allah is not asking once — He is **asking the same question in three different registers**, daring you to find a register in which you can still ignore Him.

Here's what is remarkable. The three closings each address a different faculty. Gratitude (*shukr*) is something the **heart** does. Being gathered is something the **eyes** will see. Reasoning (*taʿqil*) is what the **intellect** does. And those three faculties — heart, sight, intellect — are *exactly* the three things named at the beginning of ayah 78: *as-samʿ wal-abṣār wal-afʾidah.*

The architecture quietly closes: the gifts named in 78 become the means of response demanded in 78, 79, and 80.

### Element Two — The Singular Hearing, the Plural Sights and Hearts

Now look closely at the first ayah.

> أَنشَأَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَـٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ
> *anshaʾa lakumu-s-samʿa wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidah*

Three faculties are listed. *Samʿ* — hearing. *Abṣār* — sights. *Afʾidah* — inner hearts.

Stay with me. Read it once more in Arabic and listen to the shape of the words.

*As-samʿa* — singular. One word. One form.
*Al-abṣāra* — plural. The plural of *baṣar*.
*Al-afʾidah* — plural. The plural of *fuʾād*.

Notice something. In a list of three things, two are plural and one is singular. Why?

This is not an accident of grammar. The classical commentators paused at this asymmetry and they noticed several things — and rather than choose between their explanations, let us let them stand together as different angles on the same reality.

**One angle:** the word *samʿ* in Arabic is a *maṣdar* — a verbal noun. A verbal noun in Arabic does not need to be pluralised even when it refers to many people doing the action. *Samʿ* is the very *act of hearing* itself. There is only one *act of hearing* — even when a thousand ears are doing it. *Baṣar* and *fuʾād*, by contrast, are nouns referring to *organs* or *centers*, and so when many people are addressed, the plural form is natural.

**A second angle:** what enters our ears is, by and large, the *same* thing. Sound is public. When a man speaks in a room, every ear in the room receives the same words. But what enters our eyes — what we *see* — varies wildly. Two people in the same room are looking at different things, focusing on different details, framing the world from different angles. And what enters our hearts — *afʾidah* — varies even more. Two people hearing the same verse may be undone by it or untouched by it. Hearing has a unity to it. Sight and heart fragment.

**A third angle:** hearing is the first faculty. The fetus hears in the womb before its eyes ever open. Hearing is the *gateway*. It comes first, alone — a single threshold. Sight and inner cognition develop afterward, multiplied across the years and across people.

Now let each of these angles add to the others. The picture that emerges is this: **hearing is the door, single and shared; sight and heart are the rooms, many and personal.**

Look at what this means for revelation. Allah's speech enters the *door* first. It comes through the ears. And He places the *door* singular — meaning every human being has the *same access* to His speech through that gate. No one is born with a defective version of the door. Then it travels into the *rooms* — into how each person sees what they have heard, and into how each person's inner core responds to it. Those rooms are private, plural, and varied.

Here's what is remarkable. The order of the list is **the order of revelation's journey through you.** Allah's words enter your *samʿ* — the single shared door. They become *abṣār* — your particular vision of the world they describe. They settle into *afʾidah* — your inner cores, where they either burn or do not.

And the rebuke — *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — "little are you grateful" — applies most painfully to the third faculty. Because the door cannot help being open. The vision cannot help framing what enters. But the *afʾidah* — the burning core — is the place where gratitude is supposed to *catch fire*. And it does not. Most of what enters the door of hearing dies somewhere on the way to the heart.

[PAUSE]

A small note before we move on. The word *afʾidah* is not the same as *qulūb*. *Qulūb* (the more common Quranic word for "hearts") comes from a root that means *to turn over, to alternate, to change*. *Afʾidah* comes from *fuʾād*, which the lexicons connect to *fluttering, burning, the inner kindled core.* When Allah here chooses *afʾidah* and not *qulūb*, He is choosing the word that names **the heart at its most alive and most working** — the trembling core that burns *because* it comprehends.

And the classical commentators on this specific verse are careful to hold both registers together. Al-Tabari glosses *al-afʾidah* here as the hearts *by which you comprehend* — the faculty of understanding — and al-Muyassar specifies that they were given *so that you may understand*. The *fuʾād* does not merely feel the truth; it is the faculty that grasps and processes truth as truth at all. He gave you not just the burning organ but the comprehending core inside it. Understanding and feeling are not two things happening separately — they are one act, lit from within, at its most whole when it is both trembling and grasping at once. And what does that comprehending core do, confronted with all this? *Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.*

The faculty most fitted for gratitude — the one that can both feel and fully understand why gratitude is warranted — is the one that least produces it.

### Element Three — *Dharaʾakum* — He *Scattered* You

Move to the second ayah.

> وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى ذَرَأَكُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ
> *Wa-Huwa-lladhī dharaʾakum fī-l-arḍ.*

The verb here is **ذَرَأَ** — *dharaʾa*. Translators often render it as "created" or "multiplied," but those translations flatten something important.

In classical Arabic, the verb *dharaʾa* is not the general word for creation. The general word is *khalaqa* — *to create*. *Khalaqa* speaks to the bringing-into-being. *Dharaʾa* is something more specific. The lexicographers connect it to the way a farmer **scatters seed** across a field. To *dharaʾa* is to bring forth and *spread* — to multiply by dispersal.

Imagine the gesture. A man stands at the edge of a field. He reaches into a sack of seed. And then — with a single sweep of the arm — he sends a fistful of grains arcing through the air. They land in dozens of different places. Some on rich soil. Some on rocky ground. Some near water. Some in the open. Each one will grow where it falls, into a particular plant rooted in a particular spot.

That is the gesture inside the verb.

Now read the ayah again. *Wa-Huwa-lladhī dharaʾakum fī-l-arḍ* — "And it is He Who *scattered* you across the earth."

You did not assemble in your country by accident of geography. You did not choose your coordinates. You were *scattered* — placed, distributed, sent out — into a particular patch of earth, a particular century, near particular people whose voices and habits would shape you without your permission. And yet every one of those coordinates came from the same hand.

There is something quietly devastating in this. The Quran could have said: *He put you in the earth.* That would have been enough. But *dharaʾakum* — He *scattered* you — carries the gesture of intention behind distribution. A farmer does not scatter seed carelessly. He scatters it *across* a field because the whole field is the canvas. You are not a mistake of place. You are a distribution. You are where the hand sent you.

And then the ayah turns. *Wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn* — and to Him you will be gathered.

Notice the contrast in direction. *Dharaʾa* is centrifugal — it sends out, spreads, distributes. *Hashr* is centripetal — it draws back, collects, concentrates. You were scattered outward; you will be gathered inward. The scattering was His act. The gathering is also His act. You did not choose your origin point in the earth and you will not choose your destination beyond it.

Classical commentators note that the gathering is not merely reunion — it is the prelude to reckoning. You will be gathered, then resurrected from your graves to the standing of account. Al-Muyassar makes the stakes precise: the gathering will be so that He may recompense you for what you did — *of good or of evil*. Both. Not only your failings. Every good the scatter produced is also being counted. The *hashr* is not a homecoming without consequence. It is a homecoming with *full consequence*, in both directions.

[PAUSE]

This changes how you read the first ayah. The faculties given to you in 78 — hearing, sight, the comprehending heart — were given *for the period of the scatter*. They are the equipment you carry through this particular patch of earth, in this particular century, among these particular people. You were scattered with those faculties in your possession. The question at the gathering will not be whether you had them. The question will be what you did with them while scattered.

### Element Four — *Ikhtilāf al-Layl wa-n-Nahār* — When Time Belongs to Someone

The third ayah gives us two things at once. The first is a double act: *yuḥyī wa-yumītu* — He gives life and causes death. The second is an ownership claim: *wa-lahu ikhtilāfu-l-layli wan-nahār* — and to Him belongs the alternation of night and day.

Let us take the double act first.

The root **ح-ي-ي** — *ḥayiya*, to live — is paired here with the root **م-و-ت** — *māta*, to die. The form *yuḥyī* is a causative: He *causes to live*, He *gives* life. The form *yumītu* is also causative: He *causes to die*, He *brings about* death. Neither life nor death in this ayah is a passive event. Both are active bestowals.

Here is something worth dwelling on: the life referred to in *yuḥyī* is not only the life that will follow death in the Hereafter. Classical commentators note that the same act — the breathing of the spirit into what was lifeless — already happened to each of us once, in the womb, after the stages of our formation had passed over us. We were once sperm drops, themselves without life. Life was given to us through a precise act of bestowal. We have already experienced *yuḥyī*. The *yuḥyī* awaiting us at resurrection is not the first time He has done this work — it is the second. The One who can animate a drop of fluid into a walking, hearing, comprehending person, who has *already done so*, is not beyond animating that same person again.

This is the argument the three ayahs have been quietly building.

If He created your faculties from nothing — *anshaʾa*, originating where there was no prior material — then recreating them is not impossible, only a second occasion of what He already did. If He scattered you into existence from nothing, He can gather you back. If He breathed life into what was lifeless once, He can do so again. *Afalā taʿqilūn* — will you not reason?

[PAUSE]

Now look at the ownership claim that follows: *wa-lahu ikhtilāfu-l-layli wan-nahār* — *to Him belongs* the alternation of night and day.

The root here is **خ-ل-ف** — the same root as *khalīfa* (successor, one who comes after), *khalafa* (to come after, to replace), *ikhtilāf* (alternation, succession, differing). *Ikhtilāf al-layl wa-n-nahār* is the rhythmic *succession* of night and day — each coming after the other, each making way for the other, neither capable of staying permanently because the other is ordained to follow.

But notice something. Allah does not say: *He created night and day* — though He created them. He does not say: *He made them alternate* — though He made them. He says: *to Him belongs* their alternation. The ownership claim sits on the *rhythm itself* — on the reliable pattern of succession that you have never once had to manage.

You have never, in your life, stayed awake wondering whether the sun would rise. You have never had to check whether night was coming. The alternation *belongs to Him* in the sense that He holds it in its pattern — and you live inside that pattern without paying for it, without maintaining it, without even noticing it most of the time.

Here the architecture is now complete. Ayah 78 gave you your *internal* domain: the faculties inside you — hearing, sight, the comprehending heart. Ayah 79 gave you your *spatial* domain: the scatter of your life across the earth, and its inevitable gathering back. Ayah 80 gives you your *temporal* domain: the life and death that define your duration, and the night-day rhythm that structures every hour of your scatter.

Three domains. Three witnesses. *Wa-Huwa-lladhī. Wa-Huwa-lladhī. Wa-Huwa-lladhī.*

He owns all three.

## Part 2: The Argument Hidden in the Architecture

You may have noticed something about the way the classical commentators introduce this passage. They frame the audience carefully: these three ayahs address those who deny the reality of resurrection after death. That framing changes everything.

If the only message of these ayahs were gratitude — *be grateful for your faculties, your scatter, your life-and-death, your night-and-day* — then the three closings would all sound similar. They would all call to the heart. But they do not.

The first closing calls to *shukr* — gratitude. The second states *hashr* — a fact about what is coming. The third demands *taʿqil* — reasoning.

The ayahs are not just evoking gratitude. They are making an *argument.*

Here is the argument, stripped to its frame:

He created your faculties from nothing — *anshaʾa*, originating where there was no prior material, no prior ear, no prior eye, no prior comprehending center. He scattered you across the earth from coordinates you did not choose. He gave you life when you were a sperm drop that had no life, animating what was inert through the blowing of the spirit. And He holds in His possession the entire rhythmic structure of time — the alternation that runs without your supervision.

Now: what kind of Being does that make Him?

One who creates from nothing. One who animates the lifeless. One who governs the rhythm of time without being dependent on it.

And the question the third ayah asks — *afalā taʿqilūn* — will you not reason? — is not rhetorical dismissal. It is a genuine logical challenge. *Think.* If He created these things from nothing once, what stops Him from recreating them? If He breathed life into a drop that had no life, what prevents Him from breathing it again?

The *taʿqil* is the invitation to work the argument. Not to feel it. Not to be moved by it — though being moved is good. To *think it through*, with the *afʾidah* He gave you for exactly that purpose. The comprehending heart was not given only for warmth. It was given for this: to follow a line of reasoning all the way to its conclusion, and to recognize that conclusion as truth.

[PAUSE]

And here the architecture of the triple witness becomes its own argument. You were given hearing — *samʿ* — the single shared door through which His word enters. You were given sight — *abṣār* — the faculty that frames the world the word describes. You were given the comprehending heart — *afʾidah* — the faculty that processes what enters and grasps it as truth. All three were given for exactly this moment: the moment when the argument arrives and you have everything you need to follow it.

The rebuke *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — little are you grateful — lands hardest at the level of the *afʾidah*, because the comprehending heart is the faculty that should *understand* what it has been given well enough to be grateful. Gratitude requires understanding. You cannot be truly grateful for something you have not recognized. The *afʾidah* is the faculty of recognition — and it is the faculty most guilty of not recognizing.

This is why the three closings are arranged as they are. *Qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — you have the faculties to understand and you are not using them to their fullest expression, which is gratitude. *Wa-ilayhi tuḥsharūn* — whether your faculties are working or not, the gathering is coming. *Afalā taʿqilūn* — use the reasoning faculty before the gathering, not after.

The three rebukes are three forms of the same mercy: I gave you everything you need. I am telling you what is coming. I am asking you to use what I gave you before you arrive there.

## Part 3: The Doors You Walk Through Every Day

These three ayahs describe three thresholds that every human being crosses multiple times in a single day.

The first threshold is perception. Every morning you wake and the door of hearing opens before you open your eyes. Sound enters — before sight, before thought — and the single shared door receives whatever is around it. Then sight follows, fragmenting the world into your particular frame. Then the comprehending heart begins to work, asking: *what does this mean? what is required of me? what is real here?*

You cross this threshold every morning and do not notice it.

The second threshold is presence. You are in a particular place on this earth, among particular people, in a particular time in history, dealing with particular conditions that are yours and not someone else's. This was not assembled randomly. You were scattered here. The question is not whether you chose your coordinates — you did not. The question is what you do from your coordinates while the scatter lasts. Every good the scatter produces is being counted. Every harm also.

You cross this threshold every time you look up from what you are doing and remember where you are.

The third threshold is time. Night comes and the rhythm shifts. Day comes and it shifts back. You are inside a pattern that was ordained before you arrived and will continue after you leave. Every dusk is a small reminder: the alternation belongs to Him. You live inside His clock.

You cross this threshold every evening, usually without noticing.

What would it mean to cross each of these thresholds *with recognition*? Not dramatically. Not with ritual. Just with a moment of: *this is from Him.* The hearing He gave me. The place He put me. The morning He turned on.

*Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.* The text does not say *nothing.* It says *little.* As if to say: there is gratitude in you. There is capacity for it. The door of hearing is open. The eyes are framing the world. The comprehending heart is working. What is needed is the small act of letting what you already perceive travel all the way to recognition — all the way through the *afʾidah*, where understanding becomes gratitude and gratitude becomes the truest use of the faculty He gave.

## Closing Supplication

*Allāhumma, You are the One Who originated for us hearing, sight, and the hearts that comprehend — do not let us carry these gifts through our scatter without once recognizing whose hand gave them. Make the comprehending heart comprehend its Giver. Make the sight that sees Your signs frame them as signs and not as scenery. Open the door of hearing each morning to the sound of Your speech and let it travel all the way.*

*You scattered us across this earth into coordinates we did not choose. We ask You to make our scatter purposeful — to make each place You put us a place where something of Your trust is fulfilled. And when You gather us back, gather us among those who used the scatter well, for good and not for harm.*

*You gave life once to what was lifeless, and You can give it again. You give life and You cause death, and the rhythm of time belongs to You. We ask for life that is grateful, and death when we are ready, and time spent recognizing the One to Whom all of it belongs.*

*Afalā taʿqilūn — will we not reason? Make us of those who do.*
