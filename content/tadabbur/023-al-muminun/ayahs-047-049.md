# The Two They Could Not See

## Introduction — Where We Stand in the Surah

Let's place ourselves carefully.

We are deep in Surah Al-Mu'minun. The surah opened with the believers — *qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn* — and it has been moving, ever since, through the long arc of how human beings receive or refuse the messengers Allah sends them. We have walked through Nuh and his ark. We have walked through a generation after him who were destroyed. And now, just before our three ayahs, the surah has named Musa and his brother Harun, sent to Pharaoh and his elite — *al-mala'* — with signs.

Then comes the response.

Three ayahs. Just three. Listen to them again, slowly:

> [ayah:23:47] فَقَالُوٓا۟ أَنُؤْمِنُ لِبَشَرَيْنِ مِثْلِنَا وَقَوْمُهُمَا لَنَا عَـٰبِدُونَ
> [ayah:23:48] فَكَذَّبُوهُمَا فَكَانُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلْمُهْلَكِينَ
> [ayah:23:49] وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا مُوسَى ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

*Faqālū anu'minu libasharayni mithlinā waqawmuhumā lanā ʿābidūn. Fakadhdhabūhumā fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn. Walaqad ātaynā Mūsa-l-Kitāba laʿallahum yahtadūn.*

"So they said: 'Shall we believe in two humans like ourselves — while their people are servants to us?' So they denied the two of them, and they became among those who were destroyed. And We had certainly given Moses the Book — so that perhaps they would be guided."

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word, let's rebuild the world this was spoken into.

Egypt was, by every standard available, the most sophisticated civilization on earth. Pharaonic Egypt had architecture that still stands four thousand years later. It had a priesthood with cosmological knowledge. It had a court system, a tax system, a granary system, a religious system stitched together by myth and ritual into a seamless theological state.

And threaded through the foundations of that civilization was an entire people held in subjugation. Bani Isra'il. The Israelites. Generations of human beings whose labor and obedience sustained the machinery of empire — men and women required to do whatever Pharaoh commanded, without recourse, without recognized standing, without voice. The Quran elsewhere (28:4) is explicit about what this looked like: Pharaoh *istaḍʿafa* them — made them powerless, divided and subjugated them — slaughtering their sons and leaving their women, a calculated dismemberment of a people's capacity to resist. But more than the physical, it was the complete political subjugation that made the Israelites, in the eyes of the Egyptian elite, simply a ruled people. A people whose obedience went without question. A people who belonged to the system the way limestone belongs to a wall.

Now into that court walks Musa. And Harun. Two men. Of that people.

And they say: *worship Allah alone. There is no god but Him.*

[PAUSE]

What Ibn Kathir and the classical commentators foreground most sharply is the disease of *bashariyya* — the offense of a human prophet. This, Ibn Kathir notes, was the same sickness that afflicted every nation before them: *they were of a similar mentality*, drawing an unbroken line from Nuh's opponents to Fir'awn's court. Previous generations too had said: how can we follow a man like ourselves? The arguments across centuries are interchangeable; what changes is only the name of the prophet being refused. The refusal of *bashariyya* — the insistence that a genuine messenger from the Divine could not be merely human — was not an Egyptian disease. It was a human one.

But these three ayahs show us more than the pattern. They show us *why* this particular refusal ran so deep in this particular court — and the chiefs themselves name the reason without realizing it. The *bashariyya* argument was not purely theological. It was propped up by something structural, something the chiefs could not see because it was the water they swam in. And the word that names that structure slips out of their mouths in the very sentence of their refusal.

### The Hook

Now hear the response again: *Shall we believe in two humans like ourselves — while their people are servants to us?*

There is something hiding inside that sentence. Something so revealing that once you see it, you cannot un-see it. The people of Pharaoh, in their refusal, will accidentally tell us exactly *why* this particular rejection ran so deep. And then Allah, in just twelve more Arabic words, will perform an act of architectural reversal so quiet, so devastating, that the mighty civilization that just spoke does not even realize it has been answered.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will work through three linguistic features. Each one earns the next.

### Element One: The Word ʿĀbidūn — When Submission Confesses Too Much

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word.

In Arabic, there is one root that does enormous theological work. It is the root *ʿayn-bā-dāl* — *ʿabada*. From this root come some of the most central words in the entire Quran: *ʿabd* — slave, servant, worshipper. *ʿibādah* — worship. *aʿbudu* — I worship. *naʿbudu* — we worship. The root means, at its core: *to be in a state of complete submission, devotion, and service to another*. To make oneself fully owned by another.

In Islam, this root is reserved — almost ferociously reserved — for the relationship between the human being and Allah. *Iyyāka naʿbudu*. *You alone do we worship.* Because to give that complete submission to anyone or anything other than Allah is *shirk* — the sin Allah does not forgive.

Now hold that in your mind, and let's open the ayah.

The chiefs of Pharaoh look at Musa and Harun and they say: *waqawmuhumā lanā ʿābidūn.*

> *And their people are, to us, ʿābidūn.*

[PAUSE]

The word they choose. Not *khadām* — servants in the working sense. Not *ʿummāl* — laborers. Not *raqīq* — the standard word for the enslaved. *ʿĀbidūn.* Active participle plural from that root of total submission.

Here al-Tabari offers us something essential. He glosses *ʿābidūn* in this ayah specifically as *muṭīʿūn mutadhallilūn* — the obedient, the subservient — and he makes a striking linguistic observation: the Arabs would call *ʿābid* anyone who submitted to a king, not only those giving religious devotion. He cites the people of al-Ḥīra as his proof — they were known as *al-ʿIbād*, the ʿibad, precisely because of their political submission to the Persian kings, not because they worshipped them as gods. So what Pharaoh's chiefs meant was: *our obedient political subjects, our subordinates who yield to our authority*. A claim of suzerainty. A statement about the way the world was ordered.

And yet — and this is where the word carries truth beyond the speaker's intention — they could not make that claim in neutral language. The root they reached for, the word that most naturally named *total submission to another*, was the same root the Quran reserves for the Creator. They wanted to say: *they are our political subordinates, under our command.* What the language forced them to say was: *they submit to us the way the human being should submit only to God.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. Sit with what they have just said about themselves.

They have said: *the Israelites are, to us, what we are supposed to be to Allah.*

Al-Tabari is right that they meant political suzerainty — *they are our obedient subjects* in the way the people of al-Ḥīra were the Persians' subjects. But this is precisely the indictment. Because to claim that kind of total submission from other human beings — the kind the Arabic language cannot describe except with the root of worship — is to have installed oneself in the place that belongs only to the Divine. The claim of empire and the claim to divinity are, in this language, the same claim. The chiefs didn't need to declare themselves gods. They just needed to use the word *ʿābidūn*. The word made the declaration for them.

This is what classical linguists call *ḥaml ʿalā al-lughah* — the way the word carries truth even when the speaker doesn't intend it. The Pharaonic elite chose *ʿābidūn* because in their mental world, that is how they actually saw the relationship — as total submission owed to them. The Quran preserves their word because the word is the diagnosis.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. *ʿābidūn* is in the active participle form — meaning ongoing, continuous, present-state. Not "they were once subjugated by us" — *they are, right now, in continuous submission to us.* The grammar makes the situation feel permanent, structural, unchangeable. Which is exactly how empire always describes itself to itself: *this is just how the world is. They submit to us. That is the natural order.*

And here is what is remarkable: this is the reason Musa and Harun are rejected.

Read it once more: *Shall we believe in two humans like ourselves — while their people are to us ʿābidūn?*

The chiefs are not arguing theology. They are not engaging the message. They are saying: *we cannot accept truth from them, because if we did, we would have to acknowledge that the submission they give us belongs to someone else. And we will not stop.*

Every refusal of truth in human history has this shape. The intellect produces the argument; the social position produces the refusal. The argument is the costume the refusal wears in public.

What this single word reveals: **the rejection of revelation is almost never about the revelation. It is about what accepting the revelation would cost the person rejecting it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Dual That Could Not See Two People

Let me teach you a feature of Arabic that English does not have.

Arabic has three numbers, not two. English has singular and plural. *One thing*, *many things*. Arabic has singular, dual, and plural. *One thing, two things, many things.* The dual — *muthannā* — has its own grammatical form, its own pronouns, its own verb endings. When two of something appear, Arabic does not say "two ones." It marks them grammatically as a *pair*.

Now look at how the chiefs speak. Look at every dual marker:

- *libasharayni* — "in two-humans" — the dual ending *-ayn* on *bashar*
- *mithlinā* — "like us" — they place themselves as the plural and the prophets as the dual pair
- *qawmuhumā* — "the people of *those two*" — the dual possessive *humā*
- And in the next ayah: *fakadhdhabūhumā* — "they denied *those two*" — dual object

Across two short ayahs, the dual grammatical form appears five times. It is the rhythm of the passage. *Those two. Those two. Those two. Those two.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what they are not saying. They are not saying *Musa* and *Harun*. They never use the names. The chiefs of Pharaoh do not name them. They reduce them to a category — *bashar*, mere humanity — and then they reduce them further to a unit — *those two*. Not even two individuals. A pair. A pair of nobodies.

There is a particular kind of cruelty in this. To strip a person of their name is to strip them of being a person to you. To say *those two* is to refuse to acknowledge that there is a Musa, a Harun, a brother and a brother, each with a face, a story, a calling. The chiefs collapse them into a grammatical pair and then collapse the pair into a class — *like us, except they're from those people.*

This is the sociology of contempt encoded into grammar. The Quran is not just reporting what Pharaoh's chiefs said — it is preserving the *form* of their speech because the form is the indictment.

[PAUSE]

And now — this is what I want you to feel — look at what Allah does in the very next ayah.

*Walaqad ātaynā **Mūsa**-l-Kitāb.*

*And We certainly gave **Musa** the Book.*

The name. Allah uses the name. The name the chiefs would not say.

[PAUSE]

The grammatical pivot is so quiet you can miss it. For two ayahs, the prophets are *those two, those two, those two.* And then in ayah 49, Allah names him. Musa. Singular. Human. A person.

The chiefs would not dignify him with personhood. Allah dignifies him with revelation.

You see what just happened? The grammar of the passage is performing the theology of the passage. Power dehumanizes those it dominates. Allah re-humanizes them by name. The Quran is showing us, in the very structure of its sentences, that what Pharaoh refuses to grant — recognition, individuality, name — Allah grants in abundance.

What this grammatical pattern reveals: **dehumanization always precedes destruction. And Allah's response to dehumanization is to take the very person being denied and make them a recipient of revelation.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Open Pronoun — *Laʿallahum Yahtadūn*

Now let's open the third feature, and this is where the architecture of these three ayahs really shows itself.

The final clause of ayah 49 is: *laʿallahum yahtadūn.* "So that perhaps they may be guided."

In Arabic, the pronoun *hum* — "they" — must refer to someone. Pronouns need an antecedent. So when Allah says *laʿallahum yahtadūn* — "so that perhaps **they** may be guided" — we have to ask: *they who?*

This is one of those places in the Quran where the classical commentators sat for a long time, because the pronoun is doing more than one thing at once. Let me walk you through what they saw, because it is going to land.

[PAUSE]

**Reading one:** *laʿallahum* refers to Pharaoh and his chiefs — the people in ayahs 47 and 48. Allah gave Musa the Book so that perhaps *they* — the deniers — might still be guided. But this reading carries a haunting weight, because we have just been told in the previous ayah that they were already destroyed. *Fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn.* So under this reading, the guidance is offered to people whom Allah, in the same passage, has just said are gone. The mercy is extended to those who already cannot receive it. Some classical readers held this — and they took from it the staggering point that *Allah's offer of guidance outlasts even the lifetimes of those it is offered to. The Book is sent for them; they refused; the Book remains in the world; the offer never closes.*

**Reading two:** *laʿallahum* refers to Bani Isra'il — the people of Musa, the very *qawm* whom Pharaoh's chiefs called *ʿābidūn* in ayah 47. Al-Tabari plants his flag here explicitly: *laʿallahum yahtadūn* means "so that his people from Bani Isra'il might be guided by it and act according to what is in it." And he identifies the Book specifically as *al-Tawrāh* — the Torah. Under this reading, the architecture of the passage is breathtaking: *the very people Pharaoh's chiefs cited as the reason to reject the prophets — whose submission was the argument — are the people for whom revelation descends.* The despised become the carriers of guidance. The mighty are silenced; the subjugated are addressed.

**Reading three:** *laʿallahum* refers to humanity at large — every generation that the Book of Musa would reach. The Torah, then the People of the Book through the centuries, then ultimately back-pointing to the Quran's own audience.

These are not three competing answers. These are three layers of one truth. Allah deliberately leaves the pronoun open because all three are true at once. The Book was sent for the deniers, *and* for those who had been subjugated, *and* for the centuries that would inherit it. Revelation is structured for everyone the empire would dismiss as either too proud or too low to receive it.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the architectural pivot of the whole three-ayah unit:

- **Ayah 47:** The chiefs speak. They reject Musa and Harun because of class.
- **Ayah 48:** They are destroyed. One sentence. No drama. Just *fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn.* Al-Muyassar specifies the manner: *bialghurq fī al-baḥr* — by drowning in the sea. The mightiest administrative civilization of the ancient world, drowned. One clause.
- **Ayah 49:** Allah turns away from the destroyed and addresses what comes next. *And We gave Musa the Book.*

Three ayahs. The arc is: pride → consequence → continuation. The empire rejected the messenger; the empire ended; the message went on. The Book did not need the empire's approval to exist. Revelation does not depend on the consent of the powerful.

What this open pronoun reveals: **Allah's revelation is structurally indifferent to the approval of the powerful. It is addressed not to those who control the world, but to those the world's controllers have written off — and it outlasts every civilization that refused it.**

[PAUSE]

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURAL REVERSAL

We have traced the linguistic evidence. Now let's pull back and see the full shape.

### The Three-Beat Pattern

Ibn Kathir makes an observation that cuts across the whole surah, not just these three ayahs. The same mentality — *they were of a similar mentality* — connects Fir'awn's court to every prior nation the surah has named. We have already walked through Nuh dismissed by his people, a generation after him destroyed, then Musa and Harun dismissed in turn. Each time, the form of rejection is identical: *how can we follow a man like ourselves? Someone from that people?* Each time, the consequence is identical. And each time, the mission continues.

This is the surah's architecture at the level of its narrative spine. Surah Al-Mu'minun opens with the flourishing of the believers — *qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn* — and it will close with it. In between, it traces every major pattern of rejection and renewal in prophetic history. These three ayahs are the hinge of that surah-long pattern: the empire's no, the empire's end, and the Book that survives both.

[PAUSE]

Notice the compression. The Quran has given us entire surahs — Taha, al-A'raf, al-Shu'ara', al-Qasas — that extend and deepen the encounter between Musa and Fir'awn. Epic confrontations, signs, dialogues, the magicians' repentance, the night departure, the sea. But here, in Al-Mu'minun, the entire Pharaonic chapter collapses into its barest structure: rejection, destruction, revelation. Two ayahs of rejection. One sentence of destruction. One sentence of what came after.

This compression is itself a statement. The surah is not interested here in the drama. It is interested in the *pattern*. And the pattern is: **the powerful refuse. The powerful end. The message continues.**

[PAUSE]

### The Double Reversal Inside ʿĀbidūn

Let's return, one last time, to that word. Because the reversal built into these three ayahs is more precise than we have said.

The chiefs of Pharaoh said: *we will not believe in those two, because their people are, to us, ʿābidūn.*

They said this as the reason for refusal. As a statement of the way things were. As evidence that the natural order made Musa and Harun unworthy of belief.

And then al-Tabari, in his commentary on ayah 49, gives us what happened next: Allah gave Musa *al-Tawrāh* — the Torah — so that his people from Bani Isra'il might be guided by it and act according to what is in it.

[PAUSE]

The people who were *ʿābidūn* — who submitted to Pharaoh, who were the political subordinates of the Egyptian empire, who were cited as the very reason to dismiss the prophets — those people became the carriers of the revelation. The Torah came not to the courts of Egypt but to the camp of Israel. Not to the mala' who gathered around Pharaoh's throne, but to a people whose submission the mala' had weaponized as an argument against truth.

The chiefs said: *we cannot follow those two, because their people are beneath us.*

Allah said: *I am giving those people My Book.*

The chiefs named the reason for their refusal. Allah named it the address of revelation.

[PAUSE]

This is the divine reversal the surah was building toward. Not merely a military victory — though the sea accomplished that. Not merely a reversal of political fortune — though that came too, and came precisely because of what 28:5-6 promises: *We willed to bestow favor upon those who were subjugated in the land, to make them leaders and make them the inheritors.* The deepest reversal runs below the political. It is about the root itself.

The root *ʿayn-bā-dāl* was weaponized by the chiefs of Pharaoh. They bent it toward themselves — *their people submit to us* — the way you might bend a word to describe something it was never meant to describe. And then Allah sent the Torah. And the Torah's central imperative, the commandment at the heart of the covenant given to Musa, is: *you shall have no other gods before Me. You alone* — you who were called *ʿābidūn* by those who thought themselves your masters — *shall give your total submission only to the One who created submission itself.*

The root that was weaponized in their mouths was returned, by revelation, to its only rightful object. *Iyyāka naʿbudu wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn.* You alone we worship, You alone we ask for help. When that word passed from the chiefs of Pharaoh into the Torah, it was not the same word anymore. It had been liberated. And in being liberated, it liberated the people who received it.

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest grammar lesson these three ayahs contain. Every empire that claims total submission from human beings has bent the root of worship toward itself. And every act of divine revelation is, among other things, a act of reclamation — taking back the word that belongs to God and returning it to God. When you truly worship only Allah, you are incapable of giving anyone else the *ʿabdiyya* that belongs to the Creator alone. The freedom the Torah announced was not merely political. It was ontological.

### Why the Mala' Could Not See

There is one more observation worth sitting with.

The chiefs said: *shall we believe in two humans like ourselves?*

The phrase is *mithlinā* — like us. They are pointing to equivalence of human nature. *They are just men, as we are men. Why should we follow them?*

But the comparison contains a category error. They are comparing Musa and Harun to themselves — to powerful men, men with court positions, men with authority derived from the empire's recognition. And the comparison fails, they think, because Musa and Harun have no court, no authority, no power structure behind them. They come from the wrong people. They stand in the wrong hall.

What they cannot see is that Musa and Harun carry something that has no equivalent in the language of courts: *a commission from the Creator of courts*. The very equivalence the chiefs cite as disqualification — *they are human, like us* — is the precise qualification for the mission. A messenger must be human, because a messenger must speak to humans in a language they can live by, embody in a body like their own, and follow without supernatural excuse. This is the meaning of *bashariyya* turned right-side up. The humanity of the prophet is not the weakness in the argument for prophethood. It is the argument.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari traces this pattern across the nations before Fir'awn who said the same thing about their own messengers. The pattern reveals not a problem with the messengers, but a problem with the *measuring stick*. The mala' evaluated prophets the way they evaluated courtiers — by rank, by backing, by whose people were whose, by the existing hierarchy of recognition. They could not apply a different measure because they could not imagine a different source of authority. Their entire world was built on the assumption that worth descends from the top of the human pyramid. The idea that it might descend from outside the pyramid entirely — from the One who built all pyramids — was not a thought they had the categories to think.

This is not a condemnation unique to Fir'awn's court. It is a description of what concentrated power does to the imagination. It narrows the frame until the only questions that seem real are questions that the existing order can process. And the question *What if this man speaks for the Creator of existence?* is a question the existing order has no mechanism to adjudicate — because the mechanism of adjudication is itself part of what is being questioned.

[PAUSE]

What the chiefs of Pharaoh could not see: **revelation does not require the permission of the powerful to exist. It requires only the permission of the One who is most powerful.**

### The One-Sentence Civilization

Return one more time to ayah 48.

*Fakadhdhabūhumā fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn.*

"So they denied the two of them, and they became among those who were destroyed."

Al-Muyassar's specification — *bialghurq fī al-baḥr*, by drowning in the sea — adds a weight the single clause carries without announcing. The mightiest empire of the ancient world. The most sophisticated bureaucratic-religious civilization on earth. The builders of things that still stand. The people whose *mala'* — whose elite, whose court — sat in judgment on the two men from the people they owned.

One Arabic sentence.

[PAUSE]

Notice the structure of that sentence: *fakadhdhabūhumā* — "so they denied those two" — then immediately, without transition, without elaboration: *fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn* — "and they were among those destroyed." The two *fa*-s — "so" and "and" — chain the denial and the destruction as tightly as cause and effect. There is no gap between the verdict and its consequence in the grammar. The sentence does not mourn the empire. It does not describe the sea. It does not name the pharaoh by name. It simply closes the file.

*Fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn.* They were among those destroyed.

The phrase *al-muhlakīn* is important. It is not *uhliku Fir'awn* — Pharaoh was destroyed. It is *mina-l-muhlakīn* — they were among those who were destroyed. Among. Meaning: there is a category. Meaning: there have been others. Meaning: Fir'awn and his mala' did not receive a unique fate. They were entered into a list that already existed, a list that runs from Nuh's deniers forward through history. They joined the pattern. They did not make the pattern.

And what this does is shift the focus. The empire that thought it was the center of the world — *the* civilization, *the* order, *the* natural arrangement of things — is named here as a member of a category. One entry among many. The people who looked at two men and said *those two — from those people? Never* — those people are filed under a heading that precedes them and will follow them.

[PAUSE]

The Book continues. The empire is a footnote.

## Closing Reflection

Three ayahs. Let's hear them one more time, with everything we now carry.

> فَقَالُوٓا۟ أَنُؤْمِنُ لِبَشَرَيْنِ مِثْلِنَا وَقَوْمُهُمَا لَنَا عَـٰبِدُونَ
> فَكَذَّبُوهُمَا فَكَانُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلْمُهْلَكِينَ
> وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا مُوسَى ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَهْتَدُونَ

The chiefs spoke, and in speaking, diagnosed themselves. *ʿĀbidūn*: a word that meant to assert political hierarchy and ended up confessing, by the very logic of its root, that they had claimed for themselves what belongs only to God. *Mithlinā*: a comparison that meant to dismiss and ended up revealing the blindness of a measuring stick calibrated entirely to itself. And the Dual — that grammatical stripping of names — that ended with Allah naming Musa anyway, in full, in revelation.

[PAUSE]

The empire is gone. *Fakānū mina-l-muhlakīn.* One clause. Entered into the list. The sea closed over them. Four thousand years have passed. What they built — the architecture, the temples, the bureaucratic infrastructure — we visit in museums. What they claimed — *their people submit to us, in continuous and permanent submission* — does not survive. The Torah, the Book Allah gave to the people the chiefs cited as the reason to dismiss the prophets, shaped three monotheistic traditions, a moral vocabulary, a sense of human dignity that is still doing its work in the world. *Laʿallahum yahtadūn.* So that perhaps they would be guided. They were. And through them, others were guided. And through those others, others still.

[PAUSE]

The open pronoun at the end of ayah 49 — *laʿallahum yahtadūn* — is still open. Al-Tabari reads it as Bani Isra'il. But the grammar refuses to close. It reached for Pharaoh's chiefs, who were already gone — and the mercy of the reaching is not negated by their being gone. It reached for Bani Isra'il, who received it and became its carriers through centuries of exile and return, loss and memory. It reaches across the centuries to the Quran's audience. And now, through the Quran itself — which considers the Torah a revelation and Musa a prophet — it reaches to us.

The offer does not close.

[PAUSE]

Which means the question these three ayahs are asking us is not primarily a historical one. It is not: *wasn't it terrible what Pharaoh's chiefs did?* The question is: *what are the ʿābidūn in my own world — the relationships of submission, the structures of recognition I move through — that make it hard for me to hear truth from certain directions? What names am I not saying? Whose commission am I not able to see, because I am measuring by the wrong measure?*

The mala' of Fir'awn had a measuring stick. It was calibrated to power, to lineage, to which people belonged to which other people. And because of that measuring stick, they could not see Musa. Not because Musa was invisible — he was standing in front of them. They could not see what Musa carried. The commission. The weight of the address. The fact that the Creator of the universe had named him and sent him and would outlast every objection they raised.

[PAUSE]

We all carry measuring sticks. We use them to decide whose words are worth attending to, whose claim deserves a hearing, whose people are capable of bearing truth. The ayah does not say our measuring sticks are wrong in the same way Pharaoh's were. But it asks us to interrogate them. It asks us to notice when the argument we are making against a truth is really an argument about status. When the word we reach for to describe someone else accidentally confesses something about our own claim to dominance.

The surah opened with *qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn*. The ones who flourish are the believers. And part of what that flourishing requires — these three ayahs show us — is the capacity to receive truth from wherever the Sender chooses to send it. Not from the courts only. Not from the powerful only. Not from the people who already hold submission over others. But from two brothers. From those two. From those two, who turned out to carry the world.

*Walaqad ātaynā Mūsa-l-Kitāb. Laʿallahum yahtadūn.*

And We gave Musa the Book. So that perhaps they would be guided.

Perhaps we would be guided.
