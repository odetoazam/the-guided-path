# When Knowing Is Not the Problem
## Surah Al-Mu'minun 23:69-70

> [ayah:23:69] أَمْ لَمْ يَعْرِفُوا۟ رَسُولَهُمْ فَهُمْ لَهُۥ مُنكِرُونَ
> [ayah:23:70] أَمْ يَقُولُونَ بِهِۦ جِنَّةٌۢ ۚ بَلْ جَآءَهُم بِٱلْحَقِّ وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لِلْحَقِّ كَـٰرِهُونَ

> *Am lam yaʿrifū rasūlahum fahum lahu munkirūn? Am yaqūlūna bihi jinnah? Bal jā'ahum bil-ḥaqq, wa aktharuhum lil-ḥaqqi kārihūn.*

> *"Or have they not recognized their Messenger — so that they are rejecting him? Or do they say, 'In him is madness'? Rather — he came to them with the truth. And most of them, to the truth itself, are averse."*

---

## Introduction: The Argument Before the Ayah

[PAUSE]

Picture a courtroom. A lawyer is cross-examining a hostile witness who refuses to accept the prosecution's case. The lawyer doesn't argue the case directly — instead, she narrows the witness's options.

*"Is your objection because you've never met the defendant?"* No.
*"Is your objection because the defendant seems mentally unfit?"* No.
*"Then your objection is to the truth itself."*

This is the structure of what we are about to read. Allah is not giving us new information about the messenger. He is methodically eliminating every excuse the rejecter could invent — and then naming what is really happening.

Place yourself in Mecca, around 615 CE. The Prophet ﷺ has been preaching for several years. The Quraysh — his own tribe, his own kin — are rejecting him. But the Prophet ﷺ is not a stranger to them. They called him *Al-Amīn* — "the trustworthy" — before he ever spoke a word of revelation. They had given him their valuables for safekeeping. They had asked him to arbitrate the placement of the Black Stone. There was no argument anywhere in Mecca that this man's word was not good. None.

And yet now, when his word is *most* important — when it concerns their very destiny — they reject him.

Why?

This is the question the passage answers. And the answer it gives is so unsettling that it has become one of the most quoted diagnostic verses in the entire Quran for understanding why people reject what they know to be true.

[PAUSE]

The strand running through Surah Al-Mu'minun is the strand of *recognition*. The believers in the opening of the surah are people whose hearts recognized something. The destroyed nations were people whose hearts refused to recognize something. The whole surah is an extended meditation on what happens inside a human being when truth arrives — and why the same evidence that converts one heart hardens another.

Verses 69 and 70 are the diagnostic core of that meditation.

**The hook:** When someone refuses to accept what's true, we usually assume the problem is somewhere on the path between the message and them. Maybe the messenger wasn't credible. Maybe the evidence wasn't clear. Maybe they didn't understand. What this ayah reveals is that the problem is rarely in the path. The problem is at the destination. And once you see it, you cannot unsee it — not in others, and not in yourself.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Yaʿrifū* — Two Kinds of Knowing

Before we open the word, let me teach the concept it relies on.

In English, the verb *to know* covers a startling range of mental relationships. I *know* the capital of France. I *know* my mother. I *know* how to ride a bicycle. I *know* that the universe is expanding. All of these use the same word — but every one of them describes a different kind of knowing.

Most languages with any depth distinguish at least two of these. Spanish distinguishes *saber* (to know a fact) from *conocer* (to know a person). French distinguishes *savoir* from *connaître*. German has *wissen* and *kennen*. The split is so universal that linguists call it the **knowledge-of-fact** versus **knowledge-by-acquaintance** distinction.

Arabic makes this distinction with surgical clarity. There are two roots:

- **ʿ-l-m** (`ʿalima` / `yaʿlamu`) — to know information, to be aware of facts, to apprehend with the mind.
- **ʿ-r-f** (`ʿarafa` / `yaʿrifu`) — to recognize, to be personally acquainted with, to know through direct experience.

If I tell you the temperature in Tokyo, you now have *ʿilm*. If you grew up in Tokyo, you have *maʿrifa*. *ʿIlm* lives in the mind. *Maʿrifa* lives in the body, in the memory, in the texture of repeated encounter.

Now open the ayah:

**أَمْ لَمْ يَعْرِفُوا رَسُولَهُمْ**
*Am lam yaʿrifū rasūlahum?*

Notice something. Allah does not say *am lam yaʿlamū rasūlahum* — *did they not have information about their messenger?* He says *am lam yaʿrifū* — *did they not recognize him? Were they not personally acquainted with him?*

[PAUSE]

This is not a small distinction. This is the entire architecture of the verse.

If Allah had used *ʿilm*, the question would be about data: did the message reach them, did they hear about him, were they informed? That kind of question has obvious answers in many directions. People could have legitimately failed to be informed.

But *maʿrifa* is something else. *Maʿrifa* asks: *do they know him as one knows a face one has lived beside for forty years?*

And Allah is not actually asking. The construction *am lam* is a rhetorical denial — *or have they not...?* — that expects the answer *of course they have*. Allah is forcing the questioner to admit something he already knows: yes, they recognize him completely.

Let's sit with what this means. The Quraysh did not encounter the Prophet ﷺ as a stranger arriving on a caravan. They encountered him as a man who had eaten in their houses, played with their children, walked their streets at every hour, slept under their roofs during sandstorms, traded in their markets for forty years.

You cannot manufacture *maʿrifa*. It accumulates. It is built from thousands of small unrecorded moments — how a person handles money returned from a debt, how he speaks when angry, how he treats a slave who breaks a cup. By the time he stood on Mount Ṣafā and called them, there was not a single Qurashī of adult age who did not have decades of *maʿrifa* of him.

Here is what is remarkable. The verse is not arguing that they should have believed because they had information about him. It is arguing something far more devastating: **they had the deepest possible kind of knowledge of him, and they rejected him anyway.**

This means rejection cannot be solved by more information. The thing we usually try to give people — more facts, better arguments, clearer evidence — was never the missing piece for the Quraysh. They had the missing piece in saturation.

The Quran will say this even more explicitly elsewhere: *yaʿrifūnahu kamā yaʿrifūna abnā'ahum* — "they recognize him as they recognize their own sons" (2:146). The *maʿrifa* is at the level of recognizing the face of one's own child. And still they reject.

What this single verb invites us to consider is that the obstacle to faith, in most human beings most of the time, is not at the level of knowing. It is somewhere else entirely. The verse has not yet told us where. But it has eliminated the first plausible location.

## Element Two: *Bihi Jinnah* — The Indefinite Madness

Now Allah moves to the second possible excuse:

**أَمْ يَقُولُونَ بِهِۦ جِنَّةٌۢ**
*Am yaqūlūna bihi jinnah?*
*"Or do they say: in him is jinnah?"*

I want you to notice something almost everyone misses. The word is *jinnah* — indefinite, not definite. Not *al-junūn* — *the* madness, full mental illness — but *jinnah*, an indefinite touch, a brush, a hint of madness.

The root **j-n-n** in Arabic is the root of concealment, covering, the unseen. From it comes:
- *jannah* — a garden (whose ground is *concealed* by foliage)
- *janīn* — a fetus (concealed in the womb)
- *jinn* — beings concealed from human sight
- *junūn* / *jinnah* — a state in which the mind is *covered*, veiled

*Jinnah* in classical usage is specifically the lighter, indefinite version. It carries the sense of *something has touched him* — a momentary covering, an episode, a touch from the unseen. Not a settled diagnosis of insanity. More like: *something's not quite right with this man right now.*

This matters because the Quraysh, who had thirty or forty years of *maʿrifa* of Muhammad ﷺ, could not honestly call him a madman. They knew his judgment in finance. They knew his composure in feud. They had *just* asked him to arbitrate the Black Stone because they trusted his sanity above their own. To accuse him of *al-junūn* — total derangement — would have been laughable. The whole town would have known it was a lie.

But *jinnah* — the indefinite, hedged version — is the kind of accusation a community makes when it needs an excuse but cannot quite commit to it. *Maybe something has come over him. Maybe he's been touched. Maybe he's had an episode.*

This is the language of social plausible deniability. It is the exact register a community uses when it knows the person is fine but needs a reason to dismiss what he is saying.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. Allah's structure is becoming clear. He has just listed two excuses:

1. They don't really know him — *no, they recognize him deeply.*
2. He's mad — *no, and even they can only manage the indefinite hedge.*

Allah is showing us that the rejecters cannot even fully commit to their own pretexts. They reach for the *strongest* available excuse and find that it gives way under the slightest pressure. The accusation is itself shaped by the truth they cannot escape.

What does this single grammatical detail invite us to consider? That when someone reaches for an excuse to reject what they know is true, the excuse is often itself transparent. The hedge in their language — *maybe, kind of, sort of, something's off* — is the thumbprint of a person who knows better and is buying time.

## Element Three: *Lil-Ḥaqqi Kārihūn* — The Fronted Object

Now we arrive at the diagnostic clause itself. After eliminating the two excuses, Allah delivers the actual answer with the corrective particle **بَلْ** — *bal*.

*Bal* in Arabic is one of the most precise particles in the language. It does two things at once: it cancels what was just suggested, and it substitutes the real answer. *Not this — but this.* It is the linguistic equivalent of a teacher firmly setting aside a wrong guess and writing the correct answer beneath it.

**بَلْ جَآءَهُم بِٱلْحَقِّ**
*Bal jā'ahum bil-ḥaqq* — *Rather, he came to them with the truth.*

Notice that Allah does not say *with a truth*, or *with truths*, or *with a message*. He says *bil-ḥaqq* — *the* truth, definite, singular, total. The thing he brought is not negotiable, not graded, not partial. It is *the* truth itself.

And then comes the clause we have been building toward:

**وَأَكْثَرُهُمْ لِلْحَقِّ كَـٰرِهُونَ**
*Wa aktharuhum lil-ḥaqqi kārihūn.*

Translated word-for-word in Arabic order: *And most of them — to the truth — are averse.*

I need you to see what Arabic is doing here that English cannot easily do.

In normal Arabic prose, the expected order would be: *kārihūna lil-ḥaqq* — *averse to the truth.* The participle first, the object after. That is the grammatically neutral order. That is what you would say if you were just describing aversion in general.

But Allah does not write it that way. He fronts the object. He places **لِلْحَقِّ** — *to the truth* — *before* the predicate **كَـٰرِهُونَ** — *averse*.

Arabic grammarians have a technical term for this. They call it *taqdīm mā ḥaqquhu at-ta'khīr* — *the fronting of what would normally be delayed.* And they have a settled principle about what this fronting does:

> **Fronting produces specificity, exclusivity, or emphasis on what is fronted.**

When Allah moves *lil-ḥaqq* to the front, He is not just describing the rejecters' state. He is narrowing it. He is saying, in effect:

*It is to the truth — and not to anything else — that they are averse.*

[PAUSE]

Let this land. The neutral statement would be: *they have an aversion problem.* The fronted statement is something different and much sharper: *what they are averse to is the truth, specifically.* Not the messenger personally — they recognize him. Not his sanity — they cannot honestly impeach it. Not his sincerity, not his lineage, not his eloquence.

It is the truth itself that bothers them.

This is the diagnostic finding the whole passage has been pointing toward. Every other excuse has been peeled away. What remains, named with grammatical precision, is an aversion targeted directly at the truth as truth.

And one more grammatical detail before we step back: Allah says **أَكْثَرُهُمْ** — *most of them* — not *all of them*. *Akthar* is a precise word. It means *the greater part*, the majority, but not the totality. This is mercy. The Quran will not write off a whole people. There is always a remainder. There is always someone in any rejecting nation whose aversion has not yet hardened, who is still reachable. The door is left open.

But for the *akthar* — for most — the situation is that of fronted, exclusive aversion to the truth itself.

[PAUSE]

## Bridge to the Themes

What has the linguistic construction shown us?

It has shown us that Allah is not describing the surface of rejection. He is naming its core. The Quraysh's stated objections — *we don't know him, he's a bit unhinged, he's not from us, he's just a man, he wants to be a king* — are all surface. Underneath the surface, when every excuse has been eliminated by the structure of *am... am... bal*, what remains is a single aversion, fronted for emphasis, aimed precisely at the truth itself.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about the Quraysh, not just about words — but about the human heart.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Conclusion Comes First, Then the Reasons

Picture this. A man is sitting in a doctor's office. The doctor says: *Your tests show you have a serious condition. You will need to change your diet, your work hours, your stress levels — completely. Within a year.*

What happens next is one of the most studied phenomena in modern psychology.

The patient does not, in most cases, evaluate the evidence neutrally. He starts looking for ways out. *Maybe these tests are unreliable. Maybe this doctor is alarmist. Maybe my uncle had this and he was fine. Maybe I should get a second opinion. Maybe the science is contested. Maybe my body is different.*

The interesting thing is not that he asks these questions. The interesting thing is *the order in which the questions arise.* He did not start with curiosity about the tests. He started with the unwillingness to change. Then he generated reasons.

Psychologists call this **motivated reasoning**. The conclusion arrives first — *I do not want this to be true* — and the mind, with extraordinary creativity, retrofits a chain of reasoning that justifies the conclusion already taken. The reasoning *looks* like the reasoning that produces conclusions. But it works in reverse: it begins at the destination and walks backward to find a path.

This is what verse 70 is naming.

When Allah says *aktharuhum lil-ḥaqqi kārihūn* — most of them are averse, specifically, to the truth — He is not merely describing emotion. He is identifying the engine that runs underneath every excuse. The aversion is the conclusion. The excuses are the path back from the conclusion. *He's a madman, he's just a poet, he's possessed, he wants power, he eats food and walks in markets* — these are not the reasons for rejection. These are post-hoc justifications for an aversion that arrived first.

Let the ayah illuminate this. The structure of the passage — *am, am, bal* — is itself a diagnostic protocol. Allah models for us how to find the aversion underneath the excuses. He does it by methodically dismantling every reason offered and showing that none of them holds. When every reason fails, what is left is the engine: *they don't want this to be true.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the part that should make us pause. We use this engine too.

We use it when an old friend confronts us with something honest about ourselves and we look for reasons to dismiss the friend. We use it when our doctor tells us something we did not want to hear and we look up internet articles to contradict her. We use it when a verse of the Quran asks something of us we did not budget for, and we go searching for an interpretation that absolves us of having to change.

The Quraysh's mistake is not exotic. It is the most ordinary human move there is. It is what we do every time the conclusion offends our existing life and the mind gets to work building a way out.

The ayah is not just a diagnosis of *them*. It is a mirror.

Now connect this to the universal struggle. The single hardest spiritual practice in any tradition is not believing in the unseen. It is **catching ourselves in the act of motivated reasoning**. Because by the time we are doing it, the work is mostly done already. The conclusion is in place. The reasons feel real. They feel like reasons. They have all the texture of reasons.

The only way to catch it is to do what Allah does in this ayah: methodically eliminate every excuse and ask honestly — *what is left underneath? What conclusion am I protecting?*

**Practical application:** The next time you find yourself unable to accept something — a piece of advice, a verse of the Quran, a hard truth from a spouse or a parent — try this. Imagine that you fully accepted it for sixty seconds. What would have to change? What would you have to give up? What would you have to do that you currently are not doing?

The shape of the cost is the shape of the aversion. And the aversion is almost always the real reason, with the arguments arriving afterward.

## Theme Two: The Truth as Threat

There is a moment in many lives — and you may have had it — when a friend is trying to tell you something true about yourself, and you suddenly find yourself fighting them not on the substance, but on some technicality of how they said it.

*You're using the wrong tone. You don't have the right to say this. This isn't the right time. You're being unfair. You don't understand my situation.*

If you watch yourself in those moments, you can sometimes catch what is actually happening. The friend is right. You know they are right. And precisely *because* they are right, every nerve in you is firing to find an exit. Any exit. The technicality does not have to be strong. It only has to be available.

The Quraysh of Mecca were doing the same thing on a civilizational scale. The truth was right. They knew it was right. And precisely because it was right, every nerve in their social, political, and economic life was firing to find an exit. *He eats food. He walks in markets. He's just one of us. He has a touch of jinnah. Why didn't God send an angel?* These are the technicalities of a city looking for any exit it can find.

Now ask the deeper question. *Why* is the truth so often experienced as a threat?

Verse 70 names the truth as *al-ḥaqq* — definite, singular. *Al-ḥaqq* in Arabic does not mean a true sentence. It means *that which has the right to be the case*. It means *reality*. It means *what is actually so, regardless of what we wish*. Al-ḥaqq is the name of God Himself: *Al-Ḥaqq*, the Real.

When such a truth arrives, it does not arrive politely. It arrives as a claim. It says: *because I am real, you must reorder yourself around me.*

And this is the threat. The truth, when accepted, requires the rearrangement of a life. The deeper the truth, the deeper the rearrangement. The Prophet's ﷺ message did not request a small adjustment from the Quraysh. It requested the complete dismantling of the social, economic, and theological order on which their entire civilization rested. It said: the gods you have inherited are not gods. The slaves you own are equal to you in the sight of the Real. The wealth you've stockpiled is not yours. The honor you take in lineage is empty. The judgment day will rearrange all current rankings.

Of *course* most of them were averse to that truth. Not because the truth was unclear. Because the truth was clear enough to demolish the world they were standing on.

[PAUSE]

Let the ayah illuminate something about ourselves.

Every one of us is standing on a small version of that world. We have arranged our lives. We have made compromises. We have decided what we will tolerate in ourselves and what we will not look at. We have built a self-image and a daily structure that holds together.

And then truth comes. A verse of the Quran. A piece of honest counsel. A small voice in the chest at three in the morning. A khutbah that keeps replaying in our head. A friend who loves us enough to not lie.

Truth at that scale does not just *inform* us. It *threatens* the arrangement. And the heart, defending the arrangement, performs the same ancient move the Quraysh performed: it generates *jinnah*. It generates technicalities. It generates *am... am... am...*

The ayah is asking us to notice this in ourselves.

This does not mean every uncomfortable claim is true. It does not mean we should accept every confrontation. The world is full of false claims dressed as hard truths. Discernment matters.

But the ayah is teaching us a question to hold up to our own resistance: *Am I rejecting this because it is false, or because it is asking something of me?*

If the answer is *because it is false*, you can walk away clean.

If the answer is *because it is asking something of me* — that is the moment to stay. That is the moment Allah is naming. That is *lil-ḥaqqi kārihūn* in your own chest, and the only response that does not destroy you is to bow your head, accept the cost, and let the truth do its work.

**Practical application:** Identify one thing in your life right now where you have an unresolved resistance — a piece of advice you keep dismissing, a duty you keep deferring, a verse you keep skipping. Sit with it for ten minutes and ask: *what would I have to change if this were true? What is the cost? Is the cost the actual reason I am resisting?*

You may discover the *am, am, bal* of your own heart. And the answer underneath the excuses may not be intellectual at all. It may be that you do not want to pay the price the truth is asking. Naming that, in itself, is half the journey home.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Architectural Reveal

Now look at the structure of these two verses together.

> **Am** lam yaʿrifū rasūlahum?
> **Am** yaqūlūna bihi jinnah?
> **Bal** jā'ahum bil-ḥaqq.
> Wa aktharuhum **lil-ḥaqqi** kārihūn.

Two negated possibilities. One corrective. One diagnosis with the object fronted for exclusivity.

This is the shape of a clinical examination. *Is it this? No. Is it that? No. Then it is this — specifically.* Allah is not just refuting the Quraysh. He is teaching every reader for all time how to perform this examination on a heart, including one's own.

The structure itself is the lesson. When we encounter our own resistance to a truth, we are meant to ask the same questions Allah asks. *Am I rejecting because I don't know? Am I rejecting because the source is unreliable?* And when those questions yield no honest yes — *bal* — we are meant to admit, with grammatical precision, that what we are resisting is the truth itself, and that this resistance has nothing to do with the messenger and everything to do with what we would have to surrender.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are saying the same thing from two directions.

*Yaʿrifū* (the deeper kind of knowing) and *jinnah* (the indefinite hedge) and the fronted *lil-ḥaqq* are all telling us, in the precision of Arabic grammar, that rejection is not an information problem and not a credibility problem. It is an aversion problem aimed at the truth itself.

Motivated reasoning and the truth-as-threat are telling us, in the texture of human experience, the same thing: the conclusion arrives first as an aversion to what the truth would cost, and the reasons walk backward from the conclusion.

The grammar names what the heart is doing. The heart confirms what the grammar names.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life right now am I performing *am, am, am* — generating excuses for something I already know? What would happen if I let *bal* arrive?

2. When I rejected the last piece of honest counsel that came to me, was the issue truly the messenger, or was it what the message would cost?

3. Is there a verse of the Quran I have been mentally hedging — quietly placing in the *jinnah* category of *probably not literal, probably contextual, probably for someone else* — because accepting it directly would require me to change?

4. What is the small social, economic, or psychological order I have built that the truth would dismantle? Have I been protecting *al-ḥaqq* or protecting that order?

5. Am I willing to be among the *aqall* — the smaller part — who do not turn aversion into argument?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Most rejection of truth is not a failure of knowing — it is an aversion to what knowing would require, with the reasons assembled afterward to protect the heart from having to change.

## The Closing Invitation

The shift this ayah invites is not the acquisition of new information. You already know the things you have been resisting. You know them with *maʿrifa*, not *ʿilm* — the way you know your own face.

The shift is interior. It is the willingness, the next time you catch yourself reaching for *jinnah* — the indefinite hedge, the technicality, the *yes, but* — to stop, set the excuse aside, and ask honestly what the truth is asking of you and whether you are unwilling to pay it.

That single moment of honesty, repeated, is what slowly distinguishes the *akthar* from the smaller part who do not become averse.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who are Al-Ḥaqq, the Real — protect us from being among those who recognize the truth and reject it anyway. When our hearts begin to manufacture excuses, slow us down long enough to hear ourselves. And when we discover that what we are resisting is the truth itself, give us the courage to stop resisting, the humility to bear what it asks, and the trust that what we surrender for Your sake we never lose.*

Āmīn.
