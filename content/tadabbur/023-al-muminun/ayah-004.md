# Doers, Not Givers — When Purification Becomes Identity

## INTRODUCTION

**The ayah:**

> وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ لِلزَّكَوٰةِ فَـٰعِلُونَ
>
> *Wa-lladhīna hum li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn*
>
> "And those who are doers of zakāh."

Five words. One line. The shortest verse in the believers' portrait that opens this surah.

And yet — sit with it for a moment.

You have heard about zakah a thousand times. You know the percentage. You know the categories. You know when it's due. You may have given it many times.

So why does Allah, when He paints the portrait of the truly successful believer at the beginning of Surah Al-Mu'minun, **not** say: *"and those who give zakāh"*?

He doesn't say *"yu'tūna az-zakāh"* — "they give zakāh."
He doesn't say *"yuqīmūna az-zakāh"* — "they establish zakāh."
He doesn't say *"yunfiqūna"* — "they spend."

He says something stranger. Something quieter. Something that, once you hear it, will change how you read every verse about charity in the Quran.

**Lizzakāti fāʿilūn.**

"They are *doers* of zakāh."

Not givers. *Doers.*

[PAUSE]

Before we open this, let's rebuild the world. This is a Meccan surah. The believers are a tiny, persecuted minority. There is no Islamic state. There is no fixed nisab yet, no formal zakah collection. Many of the early Meccan believers were slaves and the poor — they had little wealth to give in any systematic sense.

So when this verse landed on those first ears, it could not have been a tax-code reminder. It was something else. Something deeper. Something that made even a slave with empty hands look at himself and ask: *am I one of the fāʿilūn?*

That is what we are going to recover today. The thing the first listeners heard. The thing that has been quietly waiting for us inside this short, strange line.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three doors to open. The pronoun. The preposition. And the participle.

### Element One: The Emphatic *Hum* — "It Is *They* Who…"

In Arabic, you don't usually need a separate pronoun. The verb already tells you who is doing the action. *Yaktubu* means "he writes." You don't need to add *huwa* — "he."

But Allah, throughout this opening passage, keeps adding the pronoun anyway:

- *alladhīna **hum** fī ṣalātihim khāshiʿūn* — "those who, **they are**, in their prayer humble."
- *alladhīna **hum** ʿani-l-laghwi muʿriḍūn* — "those who, **they are**, from idle speech turned away."
- *alladhīna **hum** li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn* — "those who, **they are**, of zakāh doers."

Why?

There is a principle in Arabic: when you insert a pronoun where one wasn't grammatically needed, it becomes a *ḍamīr al-faṣl* — a "pronoun of separation." Its job is to **lock the predicate to the subject**. To say: this trait is *theirs*. This is what *defines* them.

In English we can almost — almost — capture it by saying:

*"They — and only they — are the ones who do zakāh."*

It is the difference between *"he runs"* and *"he is a runner."* The first reports an action. The second reports an identity.

[PAUSE]

So before we even reach zakāh, Allah has already told us something. He is not describing **what believers do**. He is describing **who believers are**. The deed has been promoted to a name.

Stay with that. Because the moment you read the verse this way — *"they are the doers"* — a question arises. What kind of doing is so deep that it becomes who you are?

### Element Two: The Preposition *Li-* — Toward, Not Of

Now look at the next word. *Li-z-zakāti.* 

In Arabic, the most natural way to construct this would have been: *fāʿilū z-zakāh* — "doers of zakāh," with zakah in a possessive case. Direct. Clean. Object-and-doer.

But Allah doesn't use a possessive construction. He uses the preposition *li-*.

*Li-* in Arabic is one of the most common particles, but it is not neutral. Its core meaning is **direction, dedication, purpose, belonging-toward**. We use *li-* when we say *"this is for you"* — *hādhā laka.* We use *li-* when we say *"I came for you"* — *jiʾtu laka.* It is the preposition of *intentionality*.

So *li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn* is not "doers of zakāh." It is closer to:

*"Doers — toward zakāh."*
*"Doers — for the sake of zakāh."*
*"Doers — oriented to zakāh."*

The preposition is doing structural work. It tells us that zakāh is not the object of a transaction these believers complete and walk away from. It is a *direction* their lives lean in. It is a *vector*. They are tilted toward it.

And there's more. In classical Arabic style, when a prepositional phrase is *fronted* before its participle — when *li-z-zakāti* is placed before *fāʿilūn* instead of after — that fronting carries a flavor of **exclusivity and devotion**. As if to say: *it is to zakāh — specifically, persistently, deliberately — that they are oriented as doers.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. We have not yet translated the word *zakāh*. We have not yet translated the word *fāʿilūn*. We have only opened the pronoun and the preposition — and already the verse has shifted from a tax description to a portrait of orientation. Of a soul that *leans* in a certain direction.

Now let's open the third door.

### Element Three: *Fāʿilūn* — The Word That Refused to Be Specific

Here is where the verse becomes astonishing.

The word *fāʿilūn* comes from the root **f-ʿ-l** — to do, to act, to perform, to enact. It is the most generic, most foundational verb of action in the Arabic language. So basic that classical grammarians literally use the root *f-ʿ-l* as the **template** for every single verb pattern in Arabic. *Fāʿil*, *mafʿūl*, *fiʿl* — these aren't just words. They are the *names of grammar itself*.

When a teacher of Arabic says *"this is the form fāʿala,"* they are using *f-ʿ-l* the way English-speakers use the placeholder "verb." It is the most abstract action-word the language has.

So Allah, when describing what these believers do with zakāh, does not pick a specific verb. He doesn't pick *adā* (to fulfill). He doesn't pick *ātā* (to give). He doesn't pick *aqāma* (to establish). He doesn't pick *anfaqa* (to spend).

He picks the **most general possible verb of doing**.

Why?

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Here is what is remarkable.

If Allah had said *yu'tūna az-zakāh* — "they give zakāh" — He would have specified the action: *handing over*. That would be transactional. A motion of the hand. Once given, complete.

If He had said *yuqīmūna az-zakāh* — "they establish zakāh" — He would have evoked institution and structure. That would be communal.

If He had said *yatazakkawn* — "they purify themselves" — He would have made it purely inward.

But He chose *fāʿilūn*. The infinite, unspecified verb of *doing*.

And here is what this does: **it fits every form zakāh takes — and refuses to be reduced to any one of them.**

Giving wealth? That is included.
Purifying the soul? That is included.
Establishing institutions of charity? Included.
The slave with empty hands who gives a smile, a kind word, a service? Included.
Restraining the lower self — which, in the root meaning of zakāh, *is* purification? Included.

The genericness is not vagueness. It is **comprehensiveness**.

It is as if Allah is saying: *whatever zakāh asks of you in your station, in your wealth or your poverty, in your private life or your public one — they are the ones who do it.*

### The Root of *Zakāh* — And Why the Verb Had to Be Generic

We need to pause here and open *zakāh* itself, because the choice of *fāʿilūn* makes sense only when we know what zakāh actually means in its root.

The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib — preserve two interlocking meanings for the root **z-k-w / z-k-y**:

1. **Purification** — the removal of impurity from something so it becomes clean.
2. **Growth** — the increase, the blessing, the expansion of something so it becomes more.

Both meanings, simultaneously. Always.

That is why the same root that gives us *zakāh* (charity) also gives us *tazkiyah* (the purification of the soul that the Prophets came to perform — *yuzakkīhim*, He purifies them, in 62:2). It is why Allah swears by the soul and the One who *purified* it — *qad aflaḥa man **zakkāhā*** — *"He has truly succeeded who purifies it"* (91:9). It is why Surah Al-Aʿlā says *qad aflaḥa man **tazakkā*** — *"he has succeeded who purified himself"* (87:14).

And do you notice the word that keeps appearing alongside *zakāh* roots? *Aflaḥa.* He has succeeded.

Now look back at how this entire surah opens:

***Qad aflaḥa l-muʾminūn*** — "Truly, the believers have succeeded."

The whole portrait of the successful believer is being drawn under the banner of *falāḥ* — success. And the third trait Allah names is *zakāh* — the very word that, in the language of revelation, is the *mechanism* of *falāḥ*.

[PAUSE]

So now look back at the verse with everything we have:

> *Wa-lladhīna **hum** li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn.*

Word by word now:

- ***Hum*** — locks the trait to their identity.
- ***Li-z-zakāti*** — oriented toward purification-and-growth.
- ***Fāʿilūn*** — the most generic verb of doing, so it can hold every form purification takes.

The closest English we can manage:

*"And it is they — they themselves — whose lives are leaned, in every form of doing, toward purification."*

That is the verse. Not "those who give a percentage of their wealth." Allah did not write that verse. He wrote a verse big enough to hold a Meccan slave with empty hands and a Madinan merchant with a thousand camels in the same sentence.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

The linguistic journey has shown us this: Allah chose every word in this ayah to **prevent the reduction of zakāh to a transaction**. The pronoun makes it identity. The preposition makes it direction. The participle refuses to specify which form of doing — so that every form of purification qualifies.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About wealth. About the soul. About what it means to be successful in the eyes of the One who made you.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Difference Between a Giver and a Doer

Let me start somewhere very modern.

Think about the difference between someone who *exercises* and someone who *is an athlete*.

The first one might run three times a week. They have a habit. They have a behavior. If you asked them about it on a Tuesday, they'd say, *"yeah, I went to the gym yesterday."* The exercise lives in the schedule.

The athlete is different. If you ask the athlete what they do, they don't list their workouts. They say, *"I'm a runner."* The running has stopped being a behavior. It has become an identity. It shapes what they eat, when they sleep, what shoes they wear, what conversations they have, who they marry. The running is *upstream* of every decision, not downstream of one.

[PAUSE]

This is the difference Allah is drawing in this verse.

There is a kind of believer who *gives* zakāh. They calculate the 2.5%. They write the check. They feel a small sting and a small relief. The deed is complete.

And there is a different kind of believer — the kind Allah calls *fāʿil*, *doer*. For them, zakāh is not a deed. It is a posture. It is an upstream tilt. They are the kind of person to whom giving simply *occurs*, the way running occurs to a runner. Not because they're virtuous. Because their soul is leaned that direction.

Where does that lean come from?

It comes from understanding what wealth actually is.

The giver thinks: *this is mine, and I am giving a portion away.*
The doer thinks: *this was never mine to begin with — I am returning it to its purpose.*

And here is what is hidden in the language of *li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn*. The preposition *li-* — "toward." A doer moves *toward* zakāh because zakāh is the *direction wealth was always meant to flow*. Wealth, for the doer, is not a possession to be defended. It is a current to be aligned with.

[PAUSE]

There is a hadith — well attested — in which the Prophet (ﷺ) was asked who is the most generous of the people. He answered: the one who, when asked, gives. But then he added something subtler: *"And the one who, when not asked, still finds his way to it."* The one who is leaning toward it before anyone reaches out.

That is the *fāʿil*. The one who has stopped waiting to be asked.

What does this change tomorrow?

It changes the question you ask yourself. The giver asks: *did I give my zakāh this year?* The doer asks: *am I, in the structure of who I am, leaning toward purification — of my wealth, of my speech, of my time, of my soul?*

The first question can be answered by a calendar. The second cannot be answered without you.

### Theme Two: Why Wealth Is the Mirror of the Soul

Now let's go deeper.

Why, of all the spiritual disciplines, did Allah choose **zakāh** to name in this opening portrait of the believer? Why not fasting? Why not remembrance? Why not patience?

Because nothing in human life mirrors the state of the soul more honestly than the relationship to wealth.

Listen. When you are praying alone, no one sees whether your heart is in it. When you are fasting, no one sees whether you whispered a complaint inside. The soul can hide inside most acts of worship.

But money? Money cannot lie.

What you do with money is the most uncensored signal you give about what you actually believe. If you truly believed that this dunyā is brief and the next world is real — your hand would open. If you truly believed that what you have was given to you — not earned by you alone, but *given* — your grip would loosen. If you truly believed that Allah feeds the bird in the tree, you would not believe that letting go of a portion of your wealth would break you.

So when Allah picks zakāh as a name for the believer, He is picking the **most truth-telling test** there is.

[PAUSE]

And now look at the genius of the root *z-k-y* again. **Purification and growth, simultaneously.**

Most people experience giving as a *loss*. You had a hundred; now you have ninety-seven and a half. The math says you are diminished.

Allah's vocabulary refuses that math. He calls the act *zakāh* — a word that means *both* purification *and* growth. The act that looks like subtraction is being named as an act of increase.

Increase of what?

Of the wealth itself, in barakah — yes.
Of the soul, in liberation from its grip — yes.
Of the social fabric, in the ties between the giver and the receiver — yes.

But the deepest increase is this: the soul that has loosened its grip on wealth has *enlarged*. It can hold more of Allah now. There was a competitor for the throne of the heart, and zakāh has dethroned it. What flows in to fill that throne is something the world cannot calculate.

That is why a poor companion who gave a date could be remembered by Allah forever, and a wealthy man who gave a thousand dirhams in show could have his deed weighed at zero. The doing was the same shape. The *fāʿil* was different.

[PAUSE]

There is something else here, and it lands hard.

Notice that this is a Meccan ayah. The believers being described had almost nothing. Many were slaves. Many had been disinherited for accepting the Prophet (ﷺ). They could not have given much wealth even if they wanted to.

So *li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn* — for them — could not have meant primarily a fixed financial portion. It must have meant: *they were the kind of people who, in any station, oriented their doing toward purification*. The slave who, given a coin, gave half. The poor man who, given a meal, shared. The one who had nothing material left and gave their time, their hands, their dua.

Which means the verse is not waiting for you to be rich before it applies to you. It is asking, right now, in whatever station you are in: *are you a doer? Is your soul tilted toward purification, in whatever form is available to you today?*

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It means you stop telling yourself the lie *"I will be generous when I have more."* That lie has destroyed more spiritual lives than open enmity. The verse is telling you: the doer was a doer when they had nothing. The non-doer will not become a doer when they have everything. The orientation does not appear at a certain bank balance. The orientation either *is* you or it isn't — today, in whatever you have.

You become a *fāʿil* by acting like one in your current poverty. Not by waiting for a future wealth that may or may not arrive.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Opening

Now look back at where this verse sits.

The believers' portrait in Surah Al-Mu'minun opens with a vertical anatomy of purification:

- **Verse 2:** *khushūʿ* in salah — purification of the **heart** in its turning toward Allah.
- **Verse 3:** turning from *laghw* — purification of the **tongue** and **attention**.
- **Verse 4:** *li-z-zakāti fāʿilūn* — purification of the **wealth** and **soul**.
- **Verses 5-7:** guarding chastity — purification of the **body**.
- **Verse 8:** keeping trusts and covenants — purification of **relationships**.
- **Verse 9:** guarding the prayers — purification of **time**.

The whole opening is *tazkiyah*. And verse 4 — our verse — is the only one that *names* the principle by its root word: **zakāh**.

It is not just one item in a list. It is the **hidden title of the list**. Allah placed the name of the principle in the middle of the cascade, like a keystone, and built the rest of the cascade around it.

The whole portrait of the *muʾmin* — the successful believer — is a portrait of the *muzakkī*, the one who purifies. And our verse is the line that announces it.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are saying the same thing from two directions.

The linguistic discovery: Allah used the most generic verb of doing — *fāʿilūn* — so that no single act could exhaust what zakāh asks of you.

The thematic discovery: zakāh was never primarily about a percentage. It was about an *orientation* — a direction the soul leans, a posture toward wealth and toward self, that purifies and grows whatever it touches.

The ayah is one truth said twice: **purification is not a thing you do. It is a direction you face.** The deed comes from the direction. The direction does not come from the deed.

### Questions to Carry

1. In the inventory of your life — your wealth, your time, your speech, your attention — where are you a *giver*, and where are you a *doer*? Where do you transact, and where do you *lean*?

2. What is the lie of "when I have more, I will be generous" doing in your heart right now? When was the last time you noticed it whispering, and what would change today if you stopped believing it?

3. The Meccan believer was called *fāʿil* before zakāh was a fixed institution. What forms of *doing* — beyond money — is your station of life giving you access to right now? Time, skill, presence, words, dua. Which of these are you treating as wealth, and which are you hoarding?

4. If your soul is a vessel, what is currently sitting on its throne? And if zakāh is the act that dethrones the rivals to Allah in the heart — what is the next act of letting-go you have been postponing?

5. When Allah, on the Day, weighs not the *amount* but the *orientation* — what will the tilt of your life be found pointing toward?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The believer Allah praises is not the one who *gave* zakāh, but the one whose entire life had quietly leaned toward purification long before any deed was done.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow, when you encounter wealth — yours or someone else's, abundant or scarce — try a single experiment. Before you act, notice the *direction* of your first thought. Is it tilted toward defending what is yours? Or is it tilted toward releasing what was never yours?

You don't need to do anything dramatic. You just need to notice the tilt. Because the tilt, accumulated across a thousand small moments, *is* you. And the One who described you in this ayah is asking only that the tilt be in the right direction — *li-z-zakāh* — toward purification.

Be a *fāʿil*. Not because you have much. Because the leaning is what He named, and the leaning is available to you now.

### A Brief Du'a

*Yā Allāh, You who named us by the direction of our hearts and not by the size of our hands — make us **fāʿilūn**. Tilt our souls toward zakāh in every form it takes: in the wealth You have given, in the time You have lent, in the words You let us speak, in the trusts You placed on us. Loosen our grip on what was never ours. And on the Day You weigh us, weigh us by the lean of our lives — and let it be found leaning toward You.*

*Āmīn.*
