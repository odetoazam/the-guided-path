# Repel with What is Better — and the Two Refuges from the Prods

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 96–98

> [ayah:23:96] ٱدْفَعْ بِٱلَّتِى هِىَ أَحْسَنُ ٱلسَّيِّئَةَ ۚ نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَصِفُونَ
>
> [ayah:23:97] وَقُل رَّبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَٰتِ ٱلشَّيَـٰطِينِ
>
> [ayah:23:98] وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ رَبِّ أَن يَحْضُرُونِ

*Idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan as-sayyiʾah. Naḥnu aʿlamu bimā yaṣifūn.*
*Wa qul rabbi aʿūdhu bika min hamazāti-sh-shayāṭīn.*
*Wa aʿūdhu bika rabbi an yaḥḍurūn.*

**"Repel evil with that which is better. We know best what they describe.**
**And say: My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the prods of the devils.**
**And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, lest they be present with me."**


## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

Picture the streets of Makkah in the years before the migration. The Prophet ﷺ walks past the same faces every day. He has known many of them since childhood. They knew him as *al-Amīn* — the trustworthy one. And now those same faces twist into mockery as he passes. They call him *majnūn* — possessed. *Shāʿir* — a poet making things up. *Kāhin* — a soothsayer faking revelation. *Sāḥir* — a magician deceiving the crowd.

This is not the dramatic violence of a single battle. This is something harder. This is daily, low-grade, grinding hostility. The kind that wears down the soul not in one blow but in a thousand small ones. A jeer in the market. A relative crossing the street to avoid you. Garbage thrown at your door. A name muttered behind your back.

[PAUSE]

Anyone who has been on the receiving end of sustained mockery knows this feeling. It is not the big wounds that finish a person. It is the small wounds that never close. Every interaction reopens them.

Surah Al-Mu'minun has been building for ninety-five ayahs. It has shown us the believers and their qualities. It has walked us through the staggering wonder of the womb. It has surveyed the prophets and the nations who rejected them. It has dragged us face-to-face with death and resurrection. And now, near its closing, the surah does something tender. It turns to the Messenger ﷺ in the middle of all this hostility — and tells him how to survive it.

Not how to win. **How to survive it without losing his soul in the process.**

### The hook

Allah's instruction has two halves. The first is what he should do. The second is what he should ask for.

Classical commentators read each instruction on its own terms. Al-Tabari treats 23:96 as a command for *ṣabr* and overlooking — a standalone ethical directive — and the du'ā of 97–98 as a distinct request for divine protection. Al-Muyassar similarly frames the first ayah as a self-contained moral instruction: *repel their harm with your goodness toward them.* These are two real and separately valid commands, and a scholar of usūl would be right to hold them apart.

And yet. Sit with the sequence as a lived experience, not a legal ruling.

Most of us, when we read these ayahs, think of them as separate. *Repel with what is better* — that's one teaching. *Seek refuge from the devils* — that's another. We have heard both before. We file them in different folders. But Allah has placed them next to each other — the command immediately followed by the du'ā, without pause, without an intervening ayah. Whether this adjacency is architecturally deterministic or merely sequentially significant is a question each reader must hold honestly. What seems hard to deny is the experiential truth: the person who has just been wronged needs something to interrupt what happens next *inside* them — and the du'ā names exactly that something.

[PAUSE]

What if the reason most of us cannot return good for evil — as a matter of lived practice, not legal capacity — is that we are trying to do it without first making the du'ā Allah placed right after? This is a contemplative reading, not a documented structural claim. But it is one the placement of these ayahs invites. And it is worth sitting with, carefully.

What if the du'ā is not a separate act of piety but, in practice, the interior condition that makes the first instruction achievable at all?

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1: *Idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* — "Push back with the one that is better"

#### The concept first

Before we open this phrase, let's establish something about Arabic. When you want to compare two things, Arabic gives you a special form called *afʿal at-tafḍīl* — the form of preference. *Ḥasan* means good. *Aḥsan* means **better**, or **best**. The shift from *ḥasan* to *aḥsan* is not a small thing. It moves you from a positive quality to a comparison. And comparisons require a baseline.

If I say "respond with good," I have given you a single instruction. Be good in your response.

But if I say "respond with **that which is better**," I have done something different. I have presupposed a scale. I have implied: there is a response that is good — and there is a response that is better than good. Choose the better one.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah says.

#### Opening the phrase

> ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ السَّيِّئَةَ
>
> *Idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan as-sayyiʾah.*

Notice something. Allah did not say *idfaʿ bil-ḥusnā* — "repel with the good." He said *idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* — "repel with **that which is better**."

The construction *bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* is heavier than it needs to be. *Allatī* is a feminine relative pronoun — "the one that." *Hiya* is the separated pronoun "she/it" — and in Arabic grammar, when you insert a separated pronoun into a relative clause like this, you are doing it for emphasis. You are slowing the sentence down. You are saying: *the one — yes, the one — that is better.*

Allah could have said it in two words. He used five. The extra weight is the point.

Stay with this. **Allah is not asking you to be good in your response. He is asking you to be better.**

Better than what?

The Arabic does not specify. But there is a hidden comparison the listener cannot escape. *Better than what they did to you.* The comparison is to the evil itself. Their *sayyiʾah* sets the floor. Your response must rise above it — not match it, not slightly exceed it, but be **better** in a way that is visible.

Al-Tabari, in his commentary, puts a name on what this "better" concretely involves: *al-ighdāʾ wal-ṣafḥ* — overlooking and pardoning. Not the absence of a response, but the presence of a generous one. Mujahid, as al-Tabari reports, glossed it even more directly: *aʿriḍ ʿan ādhāhim iyyāk* — "turn away from their harm to you." The comparative *aḥsan* is not floating virtue. It is, classically, the specific act of declining to make the other person's harm into your defining event.

#### The verb itself

The verb is *idfaʿ*, from the root د-ف-ع. The classical lexicons gather this root around the meaning of pushing, repelling, warding off — to apply force in a direction that moves something away. In modern Arabic, the verb still means "to push." A *dāfiʿ* is a thrust, a pushing-force. To pay something is *dafʿ* — to push the money outward.

This is important. Allah did not say *iṣbir* — be patient. He did not say *atruk* — leave it. He said *idfaʿ* — push it back. **Repel.**

This is not passive. There is no instruction here to absorb the harm and let it sit on you. There is force in this verb. There is action.

But the force is being applied in an unexpected direction. You are pushing back — *with what is better*. The force is real. The medium is mercy.

[PAUSE]

Imagine someone hands you a stone. *Idfaʿ* means push it back. But Allah is saying: when you push it back, push it back as a flower.

The stone returns. Something has been moved. Something has been resisted. But the thing in the air between you has changed.

#### The object placed last

Here is one more thing the Arabic does that translation cannot show. In English we say: "Repel **the evil deed** with that which is better." The object of the verb — the evil deed — comes immediately after the verb. That is normal English word order.

But the Arabic places *as-sayyiʾah* — "the evil deed" — at the very end of the clause:

> ادْفَعْ بِالَّتِي هِيَ أَحْسَنُ ... السَّيِّئَةَ

The verb. The instrument (with that which is better). And only at the end — the thing being repelled.

The Arabic ear hears it as: "Repel — with that which is better — the evil deed." The instrument arrives in your hands before you are even told what you are repelling. Allah equips you with the response before He names the attack.

Al-Tabari's commentary adds something the word order itself gestures toward: *as-sayyiʾah* here is not only personal harm. It is also the mushrikeen's *takdhīb* — their theological rejection of what the Prophet ﷺ brought from Allah. The wrong is both interpersonal (they mock you) and cosmic (they deny the truth). The instruction to repel with what is better covers both registers at once. The *aḥsan* response is the response that does not reduce itself to the level of either attack.

This is the order of a teacher who knows what your nafs will do. The moment Allah names *as-sayyiʾah*, your defensive reflex fires. By placing the instrument first, Allah is saying: **before you even let your mind go to the wrong done to you, here is what I want in your hand.**

It is the order of a parent who hands a child a tissue before saying "you're crying."

#### A note on the historical frame

Al-Tabari includes an observation that deserves acknowledgment. He notes that this command — to overlook, pardon, and repel with goodness — was given *"before the command to fight them"* (*qabla amrihī bi-ḥarbihim*). He reads the ayah within a historical sequence: there was a period of patience, and subsequent revelation addressed the question of fighting. Classical scholars differed on whether and how patience-commands of this era were affected by later revealed legislation. That full question belongs to usūl al-fiqh, not tadabbur. But the honest reader should know that classical scholars placed this instruction in a bounded historical moment — and that the timeless ethical weight of *idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan* exists alongside, not instead of, that contextual reality.

#### What this single phrase reveals

Allah is teaching a kind of response that is **active, forceful, and inverted**. Not passivity. Not retreat. Not turning the other cheek as withdrawal. A push — but a push made of what is better than what was done to you. Returning hostility with kindness is not soft. It is one of the most muscular acts a human being can perform. The Quran names it as a push because it requires the same energy as a fight. It just sends that energy in a different vector.

### Element 2: *Hamazāt* — "the prods"

#### The concept first

In Arabic, when you want to make a noun that names a single instance of an action, you can use the form *faʿlah* — adding a *tāʾ marbūṭah* to the bare verbal noun. *Ḍarb* is "striking" as a general activity. *Ḍarbah* is "a strike" — one specific blow. Then if you want to talk about many of these instances, you make it plural: *ḍarabāt* — strikes, multiple blows.

Now apply this to our root.

#### Opening the word

The root is هـ-م-ز. Lisān al-ʿArab and the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib gather this root around a tight cluster of meanings: to prod, to poke, to push, to prick, to goad. *Hamz* is the action of jabbing something — short, sharp, repeated. The Arabic letter *hamzah* itself takes its name from this root, because the glottal stop is a sharp little cut in the throat — a kind of sonic poke.

Al-Tabari's commentary deepens this considerably. He glosses *hamz* as *al-ghamz* — a squeeze, a pressure applied from the side — and reports from Ibn Zayd that the *hamazāt* of the shayateen are, in their fullest expression, their *khanq*: their strangling, their choking grip. These are not only light jabs. The word carries within it the possibility of escalation: what begins as a prod can become a constriction. The shaytan starts with a poke and, if unopposed, tightens his hold.

If you say the word out loud — *hamz* — you can almost feel it. The opening *h* like a breath. The *m* like compression. And then the *z* — sharp, percussive. The word itself sounds like what it means. A jab.

Now Allah says *hamazāt*. Not *hamz*. Not *hamzah*. Not the singular instance. He uses the plural of the *faʿlah* form: *hamazāt al-shayāṭīn* — **the prods of the devils**. Plural. Repeated. Many.

[PAUSE]

#### What this names

Listen to what Allah is saying about how the shayateen actually attack.

He did not call them *darabāt* — blows. He did not call them *ḥurūb* — wars. He did not call them *darābir* — large strategies.

He called them **prods**.

A blow knocks you down. A war is fought across a battlefield. But a prod? A prod is small. A prod is sneaky. A prod is the sharp little jab in your side that makes you flinch — and once you've flinched, the prodder has already moved away.

Al-Muyassar unpacks what these prods are actually aimed at. The *hamazāt* of the shayateen are their *ighwāʾ* (seduction toward ruin), their *waswasah* (whispering), and their *ṣadd ʿan al-ḥaqq* (blocking from the truth). Notice the three-part anatomy: a pull toward falsehood, a continuous whisper, and a wall erected against clarity. Not a single attack. A coordinated campaign of small pressures. Each *hamzah* is one moment in that campaign — and the campaign has a direction. It is not random interference. It is targeted sabotage of your path toward what is right.

This is exactly how the inner attack works. You are walking through your day. Someone does something to you. And then — a prod. *Say the cutting thing back.* A prod. *Don't let them get away with it.* A prod. *They deserve worse.* A prod. *You are not the kind of person who lets this slide.*

Each one is small. Each one is brief. None of them feels like an attack. They feel like **your own thoughts**. That is the nature of the prod. It is too small to register as foreign.

#### Why plural matters

And the form is plural — *hamazāt*. Many. Successive. Repeating.

The shayateen do not give you one big speech you can refute. They give you many small jabs you cannot even count. By the time you have rebutted one, the next has arrived. By the time you have noticed the second, the first has done its work in your bloodstream. The plural is doing real work here. **You are not seeking refuge from a thing. You are seeking refuge from a stream of things.**

This is why Allah teaches the du'ā in present tense — *aʿūdhu* — "I seek refuge." Not past. Not perfect. Not a one-time act. A continuing seeking. Because the prods do not stop, and so the refuge cannot stop.

[PAUSE]

#### The bridge to the previous instruction

Now look back at the first ayah. *Repel evil with what is better.* And immediately Allah says: *seek refuge from the prods.*

The ayahs are not unrelated. They are linked by exactly this mechanism.

When someone wrongs you, the *sayyiʾah* arrives from the outside. You see it. You can name it. You can decide what to do about it. But the moment you see it, **the prods begin from the inside**. They begin precisely because of the wrong done to you. The sayyiʾah from outside triggers the hamazāt from inside.

And here is the truth: most of us, when wronged, do not respond to the *sayyiʾah*. We respond to the *hamazāt*. We respond to the prod, not the wrong.

Allah is saying: if you want to repel with what is better, you must first refuse the prods. Otherwise the response that leaves your mouth was not authored by you. It was authored by the one prodding you.

Stay with this for a moment.

**The sayyiʾah is what they did. The hamzah is what the shaytan whispers about what they did. Your response will be governed by whichever of the two you are actually answering.**

[PAUSE]

### Element 3: *An yaḥḍurūn* — "lest they be present"

#### The concept first

In Arabic, the root ح-ض-ر means presence — being-there, attendance, being-in-the-moment. To be *ḥāḍir* is to be present, as opposed to *ghāʾib* — absent. A *ḥaḍrah* is a presence, an audience, a gathering. The verb *yaḥḍur* means "he is present, he attends."

This is different from prodding. Prodding is action-from-a-distance. Presence is being-here-with-you.

#### Opening the phrase

Look at how Allah structures the du'ā:

> وَقُل رَّبِّ أَعُوذُ بِكَ مِنْ هَمَزَاتِ الشَّيَاطِينِ
>
> وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ رَبِّ أَن يَحْضُرُونِ

*And say: My Lord, I seek refuge in You from the prods of the devils. And I seek refuge in You, my Lord, lest they be present with me.*

Notice — Allah teaches **two refuges**, not one.

The first is *min hamazāt al-shayāṭīn* — from the prods of the devils. The second is *an yaḥḍurūn* — that they be present.

A casual reading hears these as the same thing said twice. They are not. They are two different things, and the difference is everything.

#### The two threats are not the same

Imagine someone is jabbing you with a stick from across a fence. The first refuge is from the jabbing — the action they perform on you from where they stand. But the second refuge is from something categorically different: from them *crossing the fence and standing next to you*.

Prodding and presence are two different modes of attack.

The prod arrives and passes. It is a discrete event — a thought inserted, a whisper delivered, a jab landed, and then gone until the next one. You can, with effort and du'ā, deflect a prod. You notice it, you name it, you seek refuge, and it retreats.

But presence? Presence is constant. If the shaytan is *with you* — not sending signals from a distance but standing inside your moment of deliberation — there is no gap between his influence and your action. The response forms already shaped. You open your mouth and what comes out feels like you, smells like you, has your face on it. But it was authored somewhere else.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar is precise about the scope of this second refuge: the prayer is that the shaytan not be present in *any of the believer's affairs* — *shayʾ min umūrihī* — not partial protection for selected moments, but a comprehensive ward over every single matter. The second refuge is totalizing because the threat of presence is totalizing. You are not asking to be protected in moments of obvious spiritual peril. You are asking to be protected in all of them, including the ones that do not announce themselves as such.

#### Why Allah mentions *Rabb* again

Look carefully at the construction of the second refuge:

> وَأَعُوذُ بِكَ **رَبِّ** أَن يَحْضُرُونِ

*Wa aʿūdhu bika **rabbi** an yaḥḍurūn.*

Allah instructs the Prophet ﷺ to repeat the name *Rabbi* — "My Lord" — in the second refuge, even though it is a continuation of the same du'ā. The first refuge already addressed Him as *Rabbi*. Why say it again?

In Arabic, you repeat an address when you want to signal renewed urgency. The repetition of *Rabbi* is not redundancy. It is emphasis. It is as though the speaker, having asked for the first protection, turns again and says: *and that second thing — that one especially, My Lord, I need You for that one too.*

The repetition tells us something about the stakes. The first threat — the prods — is severe. But the second threat — presence — is somehow more alarming still. Because a prod can be noticed and resisted. But if the shaytan has taken up residence with you, the resistance itself is compromised.

[PAUSE]

#### What presence actually threatens

Think about this practically.

The *hamazāt* come when you are deciding what to say to someone who has wronged you. They are whispers in the gap between the harm and your response. If you can catch that gap — if you can name the prod as prod and not as your own voice — you have a chance to choose the *aḥsan* response.

But *yaḥḍurūn* is the threat to the gap itself.

If the shaytan is present with you — not whispering from outside but standing inside your moment of deliberation — there is no gap. The response forms already shaped by his influence. This is why the second refuge is grammatically structured as *an yaḥḍurūn* — "lest they be present." The *an* introduces a feared state, not a feared act. It is not *min ḥuḍūrihim* — "from their presence" as a single event. It is *an yaḥḍurūn* — "that the condition of their being-with-me would come to obtain." The fear is of a settled state, not a passing one.

The first du'ā protects against what they do. The second protects against what they are permitted to become: companions in your inner life.

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE

### The two fronts

These three ayahs describe a two-front situation.

The front facing outward: the *sayyiʾah* — the evil done to you by human beings. This is the visible enemy. The one you can see, name, and respond to. The one your nafs immediately wants to match or exceed.

The front facing inward: the *hamazāt* and the threat of *ḥuḍūr* — the prods and the potential presence of the shayateen. This is the invisible enemy. The one you cannot see, which is precisely what makes it more dangerous.

Allah addresses both in sequence: you are told how to handle the outward attack, and you are told to ask for protection from what happens inside you in the moment of that attack. The command and the du'ā are distinct instructions — classical scholars read them separately, and that reading has its integrity. But they address the same moment. The same moment you are wronged, the same moment you must respond.

[PAUSE]

Most ethical instruction in human traditions focuses on the first front. *Be good. Return kindness for harm. Rise above.* This is the teaching in every wisdom tradition. It is real, and it is right.

What makes these three ayahs unusual is that they also address the second front. They name the mechanism by which the first instruction fails. They do not only say "be better" — they diagnose why we struggle to be better, and they provide the tool for that struggle.

The tool is refuge.

### On the word *aʿūdhu*

The verb *aʿūdhu* — I seek refuge — comes from the root ع-و-ذ. To seek refuge is to move toward something solid, to press against it, to let it become your cover. The classical lexicons use the image of a small creature pressing against a rock face, or a child pressing against a parent's side. You are not fighting from this position. You are not running. You are *pressed in*.

When Allah teaches *aʿūdhu bika* — I press in toward You — this is not a passive act. It is the act of choosing where to put your weight. In the moment when the prods begin, when the sayyiʾah has landed and the shaytan is whispering, you make a choice about where to lean. The du'ā is the lean.

And notice: it is taught in the present tense. Not *istaʿadha* — he sought refuge in the past. *Aʿūdhu* — I am seeking refuge, right now, continuously. The seeking does not end. Because the prods do not end.

## CLOSING

### What Allah knows

After the first ayah — *repel with what is better* — Allah adds:

> نَحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَصِفُونَ

*We know best what they describe.*

This is not a throwaway phrase. This is the weight beneath the instruction.

*They describe* — *yaṣifūn* — this is about the accusations. The *majnūn* and *shāʿir* and *kāhin* and *sāḥir*. The descriptions they use for the Prophet ﷺ. The stories they tell about him. The things they say when he is not in the room. Al-Tabari notes that this word — *yaṣifūn* — covers the full scope of what the mushrikeen attributed: their *shirk* and their *takdhīb*, their ascribing of partners to Allah and their denial of the message. Everything they said, about him and about the truth he carried.

Allah says: We know what they are saying. Every word of it.

[PAUSE]

And what follows from that? You do not need to defend yourself to the One who already knows. You do not need to prove yourself to the One who sees. The instruction to repel with what is better is not accompanied by a promise that it will change them. It is accompanied by the statement that **the One who is watching already knows the truth**.

This is a profound relief. The person who knows he is seen by Allah does not need to perform for the crowd. He does not need to correct the record with every wrong person. He does not need his response to the *sayyiʾah* to carry the weight of his entire reputation. *Naḥnu aʿlamu* — We know best. That is enough.

The *aḥsan* response becomes possible, finally, when you stop needing it to do justice for you. When you trust that justice belongs to *Naḥnu* — to the *We* that sees everything and forgets nothing.

### A prayer for the two fronts

End with the du'ā Allah gave.

*Rabbi aʿūdhu bika min hamazāti-sh-shayāṭīn. Wa aʿūdhu bika rabbi an yaḥḍurūn.*

My Lord. I seek refuge in You from the prods of the shayateen.

Not from the people who wronged me. From what happens inside me *because* they wronged me. From the seduction toward falsehood, from the whispering that turns their act into my reaction, from the wall erected between me and clarity about what is right. From the continuous soft voice that says *they deserve what they gave.*

And: I seek refuge in You, my Lord, from them being present with me.

Not only in this moment. Not only when the harm is fresh. But in *any of my affairs*. In the moment I open my mouth. In the moment I pick up the pen. In the moment I decide. In every moment where I could choose the *aḥsan* and might instead choose something that was authored somewhere else.

[PAUSE]

This du'ā is short. It takes seven seconds to say. But it is doing the most important work a person can do in the gap between harm and response.

It is changing the author of what comes next.

*May Allah make us of those who repel with what is better, and may He guard our inner life from the one who would make that impossible.*
