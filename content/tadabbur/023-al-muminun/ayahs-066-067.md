# The Heel-Turn and the Night-Talk

> قَدْ كَانَتْ آيَاتِي تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْكُمْ فَكُنتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنكِصُونَ ۝ مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ سَامِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ
>
> *Qad kānat āyātī tutlā ʿalaykum fa-kuntum ʿalā aʿqābikum tankiṣūn ۝ mustakbirīna bihi sāmiran tahjurūn.*
>
> "My verses were being recited to you, but you would turn back on your heels — arrogant about it, sitting up by night in conversation, speaking ugly things."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where we are

Sūrat al-Muʾminūn is one of the most carefully architected surahs in the Qur'an. It opens with a portrait of the believer — humble in prayer, turning from idle talk, paying zakāh, guarding chastity, returning trusts. Then it moves through the great messengers — Nūḥ adrift in the Ark, Mūsā and Hārūn before Pharaoh — showing that every prophet said the same thing and faced the same arrogance. Then it turns to a magnificent passage about the human heart that *should* tremble: those who give what they give with their hearts in fear because they are returning to their Lord (23:60).

Then, near the end, it pivots. It turns to the people who have heard all of this — the Quraysh, the original audience, anyone who has ever sat in the presence of a verse — and asks a single, devastating question. Three ayahs later, in 23:68, Allah will say: *"Have they not, then, pondered the Word?"*

[PAUSE]

That question — the foundational question of all tadabbur — is famous. It is the verse this entire project is named after. But before we reach it, Allah does something almost no listener notices. He doesn't *just* ask why we won't reflect.

He *diagnoses* exactly what we do instead.

He names the specific behavioral pattern — the actual physical motion, the actual social ritual — that occupies the space where reflection should have been. And the diagnosis, if you let it land, will name a pattern in your own life so precisely that you will feel it has been observed by someone who has been watching you for a very long time.

### The hook

There are two words in these ayahs that have no real translation. One describes a backward motion of the body. The other describes a kind of conversation that only happens after dark.

If we can earn what these two words actually mean — and feel why they appear here, side by side, exactly before the call to ponder — we will understand something most people never see in the Qur'an: that Allah did not just command reflection. He named, with surgical exactness, the *social engine of denial* that prevents it.

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تَنكِصُونَ — the heel-turn

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, when you want to describe how someone moves away from something, the language gives you many verbs. You can say *they fled* (*farrū*). You can say *they departed* (*dhahabū*). You can say *they turned away* (*aʿraḍū*). Each of these has a different texture.

But there is one verb in classical Arabic that means something more specific than any of these. It means: *to retreat backwards, in the very direction you came from.* The classical lexicons describe it as a return from where one came — al-Ṭabarī's gloss is simply *"he returned from where he came"* (*nakaṣa ʿalā ʿaqibihi: rajaʿa min ḥaythu jāʾa*) — and the imagery the Arabic grammarians preserve suggests a posture in which the body remains oriented toward what it retreated from, heels leading the way back, face not quite turned away from what frightened it.

Imagine you walked into a room. Someone in the room says something that frightens you, or convicts you, or asks something of you that you cannot give. You don't turn and run — that would mean facing the door. Instead, you take small steps backward, eyes still on what frightened you, retreating along the exact path you walked in on. Your heels reverse the journey your toes just made.

That motion has a name in Arabic.

It is called **nukūṣ**. The verb is **nakaṣa**. And in our ayah, Allah uses its imperfect form — تَنكِصُونَ — *you keep doing this.*

#### Opening the word

> فَكُنتُمْ عَلَىٰ أَعْقَابِكُمْ تَنكِصُونَ
>
> *Then you were, on your heels, receding.*

Notice that Allah does not say *you turned away.* He says *you receded — on your heels.*

The image is precise. He is describing a body whose toes are still pointing toward the verses. Whose face is still aimed at the recitation. Whose ears are still — technically — within range. And yet whose feet are walking the body backward, step by step, away from what is advancing toward it. Al-Ṭabarī notes that they were rejecting the verses and turning away in aversion when they heard them, *out of hatred for hearing them* — *karāhiyatan minkum li-samāʿihā.* This emotional layer matters: the retreat is not indifference. It is active, motivated recoil from something the self recognizes and refuses.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because if you have ever heard a sermon that you knew was meant for you, and you found that you were physically *still there* — your eyes still on the speaker — but something in you was already retreating, already walking the body back to who you were before the sermon began… you know this verb. Allah has a word for that.

#### Why heels

But notice the second precision. He does not just say *you receded.* He says *you receded **on your heels** —* `ʿalā aʿqābikum`.

In classical Arabic, the heel — *al-ʿaqib* — is the part of the body that *follows behind.* The whole root ʿ-Q-B is about *that which comes after.* A consequence is *ʿāqibah*. A descendant is *ʿaqib*. The heel is named the heel because it is what your toe leaves behind.

So when Allah says *you receded on your heels,* the language is doing something double. It is naming the physical motion — heels-first retreat. But it is also naming what they are *retreating to:* the part of themselves that comes *behind* the verses. The self they were *before* the recitation arrived. The old self. The self the verses were trying to leave behind.

Stay with me here. This is the structure of every retreat from transformative truth.

A verse comes toward you. It is asking you to step *forward* — into a new posture of accountability, generosity, restraint, surrender. The forward motion would mean leaving something behind: a habit, a grudge, an entitlement, a self-image. But forward is costly. So instead, you retreat the way you came. You reverse the path. You return — heels-first — to the *aʿqāb*, the part of yourself that comes *behind* the verse's invitation.

Allah is not just describing physical motion. He is describing the architecture of how a soul refuses growth.

#### What this single word reveals

تَنكِصُونَ — said in the imperfect tense, meaning *you keep doing this, repeatedly, as a pattern* — is not about one moment of disbelief. It is about a habit. An unconscious reflex. The body's rehearsed answer to anything that asks it to advance.

And here is the thing that should shake us: the Qur'an does not describe *kuffār* doing this from a distance. Allah turns directly to the listener — *you* — and says, *this is what you were doing.* The heel-turn is not a foreign behavior. It is the human reflex against any verse that asks too much.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ سَامِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ — the arrogance and the night-talk

#### Teaching the concept first

In pre-Islamic Arabia, after the sun went down and the heat broke, people would gather in small circles around a fire. The Bedouins had a name for these gatherings. They called them *al-samar* — from the root S-M-R, which means *to converse by night.*

The *samar* was not just any conversation. It was the specific kind of talk that happens after dark, in a closed circle, away from public ears. People recited poetry there. They told stories. They mocked their enemies. They debated ideas they would not raise in daylight. The *samar* was where a society did its private commentary on its public life.

The man who participates in such a gathering is called a *sāmir.* The plural is *sāmirūn.* Imagine the scene: a low fire, faces half in shadow, voices kept down, speech that could not be said in the marketplace.

This is the word Allah chooses next. But before we enter the night itself, we need to pause at the very first word of this ayah.

#### مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِ — what their arrogance was actually about

The ayah opens: *mustakbirīna bihi* — *arrogant about it.* About *what*, exactly?

This question turns out to matter enormously. Classical commentators differ on the referent of *bihi* (by it / about it). One reading connects it to the Qur'an itself — they are arrogant *about the verses*, treating the recitation as beneath them. But the reading that Ibn Abbas transmits — preserved by al-Ṭabarī through multiple chains including Ibn Jurayj and Mujāhid — is more historically specific and more devastating: *bihi* refers to the Sacred Precinct itself, the Ḥaram. Al-Ṭabarī records their words explicitly: *"lā yaẓharu ʿalaynā fīhi aḥad"* — *"no one can overcome us here, for we are the people of the Ḥaram."*

[PAUSE]

This reshapes the entire scene. Their arrogance was not abstract. It was triangulated through their custodianship of the Kaʿba. The Quraysh held the keys of the House, organized the Hajj, watered the pilgrims — and in their self-understanding, this made them the divinely validated authorities of the holy. When the Prophet recited in *their* sanctuary, their logic ran something like this: *if Allah truly wished to speak to humanity through someone here, He would have gone through us. We are already His people in this place. Our status as the Ḥaram's guardians proves our standing.* The geography of revelation was being weaponized as a status claim.

Al-Muyassar confirms this reading and adds a detail that makes the picture startlingly precise: their *samar* — the night-talk — was happening *ḥawlahu*, around it — in the very precincts of the Ḥaram, in the shadow of the house they believed legitimated their authority. *Tatasāmarūna ḥawlahu bi-al-sayyiʾ min al-qawl* — they were conversing around it with evil speech.

[PAUSE]

The geography here is important. They were not retreating to some distant quarter to mock the recitation. They were circling the sacred house itself, by night, speaking ugly of what they had heard within its walls that day. The center of the sacred was also the location of the denial. Their administration of the holy had become the very instrument by which they immunized themselves against it.

#### Opening the word

> [ayah:23:67] مُسْتَكْبِرِينَ بِهِۦ سَـٰمِرًا تَهْجُرُونَ
>
> *Arrogant about it — sitting up at night in conversation — speaking ugly things.*

He has just told them they retreat from the verses by day. Now He tells us where they go *at night.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah is not just naming the daytime retreat. He is naming the *nighttime processing.* When the recitation ends, when the sun goes down, when they are gathered in the trusted circle of their own people — in the very shadow of the House they believe validates their authority — the verses come up again. But now they come up *for ridicule.*

The Qur'an is doing something extraordinary here. It is observing — with anthropological precision — that denial does not just happen in the moment of hearing. Denial requires a *follow-up community.* The heel-turn alone is not enough. You also need a circle of people, after dark, who will help you complete the work of dismissing what you heard.

#### The sonic match

Now listen to the Arabic. Read it aloud:

*sāmiran tahjurūn.*

The first word — *sāmir* — is soft. The long *ā,* the *m,* the *r.* It actually *sounds like* murmuring. Like hushed voices around a fire. Try saying it slowly: *sāmiran.* It is the phonetic shape of a low conversation in the dark.

Now the second word — *tahjurūn.* The *hā,* the *jīm.* These are guttural, edged, rasping consonants. The mouth has to work. It is harsh.

Listen to them together: *sāmiran tahjurūn.* The murmur, then the harshness. The smooth, intimate, evening voice — and then the ugly thing it is actually saying.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an is not just *describing* what they did. The Arabic of the verse *enacts* it. The phonetic texture of *sāmiran tahjurūn* is the auditory experience of being in a room where people are speaking softly — and what they are softly saying is repulsive.

This is not decoration. This is Allah's eloquence reaching down into the throat of the listener and making them *hear* the thing being condemned.

#### Two meanings, one denial

Now we come to **تَهْجُرُونَ** — and here we must be careful, because the classical mufassirūn pause at this word.

The root H-J-R has two attested senses in classical Arabic:

The first sense is **to abandon, to forsake, to walk away from.** This is the root of *hijrah* — the migration. To do *hajr* of something is to leave it behind.

The second sense is **to speak *hujr*** — and *hujr,* in Lisān al-ʿArab, is *the speech of the delirious, the obscene, the abusive.* It is what someone says in fever, in drunkenness, or in malicious gossip. The harsh, ugly, unrestrained speech that comes out when restraint has been removed.

Classical commentators read both senses as present here. Don't treat them as alternatives. They are layers of the same act.

When you sit up at night in the trusted circle — in the precincts of the sacred house you believe legitimates your authority — and discuss the verses you heard that day with mockery, you are doing both things at once. You are *abandoning* them as a serious claim on your life. And you are *speaking ugly* about them so that the abandonment can be made permanent in your social world.

The two senses converge on the same behavior. The night-talk is *how* the abandonment is performed.

#### What this phrase reveals

Allah is not describing a society that *disagrees* with the verses. He is describing a society that has built a *nightly social ritual* — conducted in the sacred precinct itself, around the very house that gave them their status — for converting hearing into dismissal. They listen by day. They retreat at the moment of listening. And then they gather after dark to make the dismissal *communal,* so that no individual member of the circle will reverse course alone.

The night-talk is the social glue that makes the heel-turn permanent.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So here is what the linguistic construction has shown us. Allah is not describing two unrelated behaviors — heel-turning by day, night-talking by night. He is describing **a single architecture of denial** with two phases:

**Phase one** — the body retreats, heels-first, from the advancing verse, back to the self that existed before the verse arrived.

**Phase two** — the self that retreated gathers, after dark, with others who also retreated, and together they speak ugly about what made them retreat, so that the retreat hardens into identity.

And the whole structure is held together by a specific form of arrogance: the belief that one's prior status — custodian of the Ḥaram, keeper of the tradition, established authority of the sacred — is itself a theological credential that the verses must pass before they can claim authority over the one who administers them.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how denial actually operates in any human life, including ours.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why we retreat from what advances toward us

You walk into a sermon. The imam, ten minutes in, says something so exact about your marriage, your business, your relationship with your parents, that you cannot believe he has not been watching you. The sentence enters you like a key entering a lock you did not know was on the door.

For a second, you are present. You are *advancing* — toward the truth that has just walked toward you.

And then something else happens. Quietly. You don't notice it at first. You begin to find reasons. *He's generalizing. That's not exactly my situation. He doesn't understand the context. My case is different. He himself probably struggles with this. His delivery was a little harsh.*

Within thirty seconds, you have walked your body back to where it was before the sentence arrived.

[PAUSE]

That, exactly, is *nukūṣ ʿalā al-aʿqāb.* Heels-first retreat. The toes still point at the speaker. The body is still in the room. But the self has reversed the journey it was about to make.

Notice what Allah is *not* saying about these people. He is not saying they walked out. He is not saying they covered their ears. He is not saying they refused to come. He is saying: **they were physically present at the recitation, and they retreated anyway.**

The most dangerous form of denial is not absence. It is presence without forward motion.

When you walk out of a sermon, everyone can see you leave. You face the cost of your departure publicly. But when you retreat heels-first while remaining seated — when you stay in the room and quietly walk yourself back — no one can see it. You appear to be listening. And part of you is. The part that is cataloging reasons the verse does not apply to you.

[PAUSE]

This is why the verb tense matters so much. تَنكِصُونَ is imperfect. It is a *habitual* present-past. Not *you retreated once* — but *you kept doing this, again and again, every time the verses came.* The heel-turn had become the body's automatic answer to recitation. The reflex had become the response.

There is something profoundly honest about this diagnosis. Allah is not condemning them for one moment of weakness under pressure. He is naming a *pattern* — a practiced, repeated posture toward revelation — and He is naming it before asking the question of 23:68 because the question *"Have they not pondered?"* cannot be answered by people who have made retreat into a habit. The habit must be named first.

### Theme Two: The arrogance of those who administer the sacred

The disagreement among classical commentators over the referent of *bihi* is not a mere grammatical footnote. It is a window into one of the most specific and recurring failure modes of religious community.

If *bihi* refers to the Ḥaram — as Ibn Abbas and Mujāhid hold — then what Allah is describing is not generic pride. It is a very precise kind of religious arrogance: the arrogance of those who *administer the sacred* and mistake their administrative role for spiritual authority.

[PAUSE]

The Quraysh held the keys. They organized the Hajj. They watered the pilgrims. In their self-understanding, this made them the divinely validated guardians of the holy. And their logic, when a revelation arrived in *their* sanctuary, ran something like this: *if Allah truly wanted to speak to humanity, He would go through us. We are already His people in this place. The fact that this man bypasses our authority means the revelation is not what it claims to be.*

The Ḥaram had become a mirror they held up to deflect the light. The house of the One God had been converted, in their minds, into a credential for resisting God's Word.

This is a pattern that does not belong to seventh-century Mecca alone. Any community — religious, scholarly, institutional — that has long administered sacred things can begin to believe that its administrative longevity *is* the sacred thing. The long tenure. The inherited position. The generational authority. *We have been the keepers of this for centuries. Surely that is proof that we are right.*

And the night-talk in the Ḥaram's precincts is the nightly maintenance of this self-belief. Gathered in the shadow of the house they guard, they speak ugly of the verses that question whether guardianship and faithfulness are the same thing.

They are not. They never have been.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Three: The social completion of individual retreat

We said the night-talk is the social glue that makes the heel-turn permanent. Let's go deeper into what this means.

Individual denial is fragile. You hear something that convicts you, you retreat, but the retreat is never fully secure. In the quiet of the night, alone, the verse comes back. The key that found the lock. The sentence that knew your name. Alone, you might reverse course. Alone, you might go back.

The *samar* solves this problem.

When you gather with others who also retreated — others who also found the verse too costly, too exact, too demanding — the collective processing of rejection does several things that solitary rejection cannot do:

It validates each person's individual reasons. *You thought it was too harsh? I thought so too.* The private rationalization becomes social consensus.

It generates *new* reasons. Five people with five different objections, sitting in a circle, combine those objections into a shared position stronger than any single one alone.

It creates *social cost* for reversing course. Once you have spoken against the verse in the circle, returning to the verse means not just personal change but social betrayal. You would have to say, to everyone who was in that circle with you: *I was wrong. The verse was right.* The night-talk has given you something to lose beyond the verse's original demand.

[PAUSE]

This is why Allah describes not just the retreat but the retreat's *communal aftermath.* The verse is not simply about what happens in a single person's heart when revelation comes. It is about what happens to truth when it enters a *community* — and the community's social machinery is designed to neutralize it.

The *samar* in the Ḥaram's shadow is the perfect symbol of this machinery at full operation: a trusted circle, a protected space, voices kept low, every member reinforcing every other member's decision not to be changed. The very name for these gatherings — *al-samar* — belongs entirely to the night, to a world of discourse that exists only after the full light of day recedes. The word and the behavior share the same atmosphere: things said in diminished light, in chosen intimacy, where public accountability cannot reach.

### Theme Four: What the architecture of 23:66–67 asks of the listener

We have been moving through a diagnosis. Let's now ask what the diagnosis is *for.*

Allah does not describe the heel-turn and the night-talk in order to condemn from a distance. He describes them in order to name what stands between a human being and the call of 23:68. *Have they not pondered the Word?* is not a rhetorical question with a closed door behind it. It is an invitation. But it can only be received by someone who has recognized, and stopped, the prior pattern of retreat.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question the two-phase architecture puts to each of us:

In your life, what verse — what sentence, what encounter with the Qur'an — found the lock? What entered you, for a moment, in the way of real recognition?

And what did you do in the thirty seconds after?

Did you advance? Or did you find the reasons? And who did you talk to that night — not to process the recognition, but to dissolve it?

There is nothing more human than this. The Quraysh are not historical villains. They are a mirror. The heel-turn is the universal reflex. The night-talk is the universal follow-up. Every believer who has ever heard a verse and retreated, and then sought out the company that would confirm the retreat, has participated in the structure these two ayahs name.

But the naming is an act of mercy. You cannot stop a pattern you have never seen.

## PART 3: THE SELF-EXAMINATION

### What this means for us

Sūrat al-Muʾminūn opened with the believer — *qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn* — the one who has succeeded. It described a person characterized by *khushūʿ* in prayer, turning from *laghw*, present in *zakāh*, guarding the *farj.* These are qualities of forward motion. Of advancing toward what asks something of you.

The two ayahs we have just spent this time with describe the exact opposite: not the person who advances toward the verses, but the person who retreats from them. And the surah holds both portraits simultaneously, at either end of the same text, asking: which architecture is yours?

[PAUSE]

The heel-turn — *nukūṣ* — does not require that you disbelieve the Qur'an. It only requires that you treat it as something to be managed rather than something to be transformed by. The Quraysh who heard the recitation in the Ḥaram were not all convinced atheists. Some of them knew, at some level, that what they were hearing was not ordinary human speech. Al-Ṭabarī's description of their response as active *karāhiyah* — hatred, aversion — speaks to people who felt the verses' claim and fled it, not to people who felt nothing. Their retreat was not from intellectual indifference. It was from the cost of forward motion.

And the cost of forward motion, for them, was immense. The Ḥaram gave them wealth, status, political authority, social identity. The Qur'an was asking them to surrender all of these to the worship of the One who actually owned the Ḥaram. This is why the two verses say what they say in the order they say it: **retreat first, then arrogance about prior status, then night-talk.** The sequence is: *the verse asks too much → I walk back → I remind myself of my credentials → I enlist others to confirm I was right to walk back → the retreat becomes permanent.*

We need to be honest that this sequence operates in every community, in every era, whenever revelation arrives with a claim that is genuinely costly.

### The test

Here is a way to locate the heel-turn in your own life.

Find the ayah — or the surah, or the teaching — that you have not returned to in a long time. Not because you have moved on in understanding, but because something in you knows that returning to it will restart the motion it once began in you. The unread chapter. The verse you skip past. The dua you do not make because making it would ask something of you.

That avoidance is *nukūṣ* in its quietest form. Not dramatic retreat — just the consistent, habituated preference not to advance in that direction.

And find the circle — whoever it is you talk to about Islam, about Qur'an, about religious life — and ask honestly: does this circle help you advance, or does it help you maintain the distance you keep from what asks too much?

This is not an indictment of community. Community is essential. The *muʾminūn* at the opening of this surah are a community. They share prayer, zakāh, trust, fidelity. Community can be the environment in which forward motion is made possible and durable.

The question is whether your community is the *samar* that hardens the retreat — or something else entirely.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING

### The threshold

In three ayahs, Allah will ask: *"Have they not pondered the Word?"* (23:68).

We now know why He could not ask that question first. The question can only land on soil that has been prepared. And the preparation — the diagnostic work, the naming of the exact behavioral pattern that stands between a person and pondering — is what 23:66–67 does.

He names the heel-turn. He names the night-talk. He names the arrogance that uses the sacred as a shield against the sacred's own demand. And then He opens the door.

*Aflā yatadabbarūna al-qawl.*

Have they not pondered the Word?

[PAUSE]

The word *tadabbur* — pondering — comes from the same root as *dubr,* the back, the rear. To ponder is literally to attend to what lies behind the surface, to follow the word into its depths rather than standing at its threshold.

It is, in the most precise sense, the *opposite* of nakaṣa ʿalā al-ʿaqib. Both words involve the same geography of the behind. Heels-first retreat goes behind — backward, toward the self that existed before the verse. Tadabbur also goes behind — but forward, into the word, deeper and deeper, toward what the word has always been waiting to give.

The one flees from what is advancing toward it. The other advances into what is retreating from it, deeper into the text, past the surface, into the depths where the verse's full meaning lives.

Allah is asking, in 23:68: will you turn around?

[PAUSE]

These two ayahs are the answer to every person who has asked why they cannot seem to open the Qur'an and feel anything. The Qur'an's answer to that question is not primarily a technique for better reading. It is a prior question: what have you been doing with what you already heard? The heel-turn and the night-talk operate silently, below the level of conscious decision, gradually converting the habit of retreat into the assumption that there is nothing there to advance toward.

The call to ponder — *tadabbur* — is the call to reverse that assumption in the only way it can be reversed: one verse at a time, moving forward, heels leaving behind the self that would rather not be changed.
