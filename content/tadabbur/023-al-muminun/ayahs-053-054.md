# The Pieces They Called Scripture
## *Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 53-54*

---

## Introduction

Listen to what Allah says. First in Arabic, then in transliteration, then in a translation that gets us close — though as you'll see, no translation can carry what these two ayahs carry.

> **فَتَقَطَّعُوٓا۟ أَمْرَهُم بَيْنَهُمْ زُبُرًا ۖ كُلُّ حِزْبٍۭ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ**
>
> **فَذَرْهُمْ فِى غَمْرَتِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ حِينٍ**

*Fataqatta'ū amrahum baynahum zuburā, kullu ḥizbin bimā ladayhim fariḥūn. Fadharhum fī ghamratihim ḥattā ḥīn.*

"So they cut their affair apart between themselves into pieces — each faction rejoicing in what is with them. So leave them in their overwhelming flood for a time."

### Rebuilding the World

Before we can hear what these ayahs are doing, we have to step back into the moment they arrived in.

Surah Al-Mu'minun has been doing something extraordinary. Across ayahs 23 through 50, it has walked us through messenger after messenger — Nuh, Hud (or perhaps the people of 'Ad), then Musa and Harun, then Isa and his mother Maryam. And each time the same scene repeats. A messenger arrives. The people of his time say *we will not believe a man like ourselves*. The messenger pleads. The community refuses. Allah saves the few and removes the many.

It is one of the most patient passages in the Quran — the same wound, recurring, across generations, across geographies, across languages. Different prophets, different peoples, identical refusal.

And then, in ayah 51 and 52, Allah does something stunning. He gathers all of them — every messenger across history — into a single divine address. He speaks to all of them at once, across the centuries, as if they are standing in one room:

> "O messengers, eat from the good things and work righteousness. Indeed, I — of what you do — am Knowing.
> And indeed this, your community, is one community, and I am your Lord, so be conscious of Me."

[PAUSE]

Sit with what just happened. Allah collapsed time. Nuh and Musa and Isa and Muhammad ﷺ — separated by thousands of years and thousands of miles — Allah speaks to them as if they share one breath. *Your ummah is one ummah. I am your Lord. Be conscious of Me.*

It is the most beautiful theological declaration of unity you can imagine. One God. One message. One community of submission stretching across all of human history.

And then comes the very next word.

**فَ** — *fa* — "so…"

What is the human reply to divine unity?

That single conjunction — that one tiny letter — is bearing theological weight far beyond its size. Because what follows it is the tragedy of every century since the first messenger walked the earth.

### The Hook

Allah has just said: *one*.

Watch what we did with *one*.

Watch the verb He chose. Watch the word He chose for what they cut it into — a word whose dominant classical reading means "scriptures," but whose lexical roots also carry the weight of broken fragments, and which holds both meanings simultaneously in the most devastating way possible. Watch the participle He chose to describe how they *felt* about the cutting. And then watch the response He commands His Prophet to give them — a response so quiet it's terrifying.

Two ayahs. Nine words of Arabic in the first, six in the second. And inside those fifteen words is a diagnosis of every religious schism in history, every political tribe, every echo chamber, every certainty you currently hold and rejoice in without realizing it might be a fragment.

Let's open this slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: تَقَطَّعُوٓا۟ — The Verb Where We Did It To Ourselves

Before we touch the word in the ayah, let me teach you something about Arabic verbs in general.

Arabic verbs have what scholars call *forms* — usually numbered I through X. They're like gears on a bicycle. Form I is the basic action: *qaṭa'a* — he cut. Just plain cutting.

Form II adds intensity or causation: *qaṭṭa'a* — he cut [it] thoroughly, into many pieces, repeatedly. The same root, but doubled, intensified.

And Form V — the form Allah uses here — does something very specific. Form V is the *reflexive* of Form II. When you take a Form II action — an intense, thorough action — and you turn it inward, so that the actor and the object are the same, you get Form V.

In English we have to use a separate word: "to make oneself…" or "to do X to oneself." Arabic does it inside the verb.

Let me give you an everyday example. The Form II verb *'allama* means "he taught [someone]." The Form V verb *ta'allama* means "he taught himself" — that is, "he learned." Same root. Same teaching. But in Form V, the teacher and the learner are the same person.

Now hold that in your mind and look at what Allah says.

The basic verb here is **قَطَعَ** — *qaṭa'a* — "he cut." That's Form I.

Form II would be **قَطَّعَ** — *qaṭṭa'a* — "he cut [it] thoroughly into many pieces, hacked it apart."

And Form V — *taqaṭṭa'a* — would be: "he cut himself thoroughly into many pieces."

That is the verb in this ayah.

**فَتَقَطَّعُوٓا۟ أَمْرَهُم بَيْنَهُمْ**

"They cut themselves apart — thoroughly, into many pieces — between themselves."

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah did not say *fa-qaṭa'nā amrahum* — "We cut their affair apart." He did not say *fa-qaṭṭa'ahum al-shayṭān* — "Shaytan cut them apart." He did not even say a passive: "their affair was cut apart."

He chose a Form V verb that does the most precise grammatical thing imaginable: **the actor and the object are the same.** They did this. To themselves. Thoroughly. Into many pieces.

Stay with this for a moment, because it changes everything about how we read what comes next.

When religious communities fracture, the natural human response is to find someone to blame. *The other sect did this to us. The colonizer divided us. The orientalist confused us. The hypocrites poisoned the well.* And there are real injustices in all of those stories. But Allah, in choosing this verb, refuses to let us off the hook. The grammar itself says: **whatever forces from outside arrived to divide you, the cutting was done by your own hands.** You held the blade. You used it on yourselves. And you did so thoroughly — Form II's intensification is preserved inside Form V's reflexivity. Not one careless cut. Many. Deliberate. Sustained.

And one more thing. Notice the word **بَيْنَهُمْ** — *baynahum* — "between themselves." It's grammatically almost redundant after a reflexive verb. We already know they did it to themselves; why specify "between themselves"?

Because Allah is closing every escape route. *Baynahum* eliminates the possibility that maybe one outside party started the cutting and they only finished it. No. The cutting happened **between them** — among them, within them, in the space they shared. The blade never came from outside.

This is a verb that grants no alibi.

[PAUSE]

Let's hold this and move to the next word, because the diagnosis only gets sharper.

### Element Two: زُبُرًا — The Word That Is Both *Scriptures* and *Fragments*

The word **زُبُرًا** — *zuburā* — sits right after that reflexive verb. It tells us *what* they cut their affair into.

And here we encounter something that has occupied classical lexicographers for centuries.

The root **ز ب ر** — *z-b-r* — has a curious lexical range. In the classical dictionaries — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — you find this root carrying meanings that seem at first to have nothing to do with each other.

On one branch: *Zabūr* — this root — is the **Psalms**, the scripture given to Dāwūd. And more generally, *zabūr* and its plural *zubur* in classical Arabic mean **a written document, a book, a scripture.** "We sent scriptures to you" — *anzalnā 'alaykum zuburā* — uses precisely this word.

On another branch: *zubrah* means **a piece, a chunk, a fragment of something heavy.** When iron is forged, the leftover lumps are *zubar al-ḥadīd* — "pieces of iron." When a mountain is broken, what falls off is *zubar.* It's a word for solid, weighty, broken-off matter.

So which is it here? Did they cut their affair into *scriptures*? Or did they cut their affair into *fragments*?

Now — and this is critical — when you look at how the classical scholars engage this word, a clear weight emerges. Al-Ṭabarī records that the majority of Madinah and Iraqi readers (*ʿāmmat qurrāʾ al-Madīnah wa'l-ʿIrāq*) understood *zuburā* as the plural of *Zabūr* — scriptures, books, written religious canons. On this dominant classical reading, the meaning is: they divided their religion into different *books*, each faction consecrating its own written text and measuring all truth by it.

Al-Ṭabarī is precise about what this looked like historically. He grounds the verse in the fracturing of the community of ʿĪsā — the Jews, who claimed the Torah while rejecting the Gospel and the Quran; the Christians, who claimed the Gospel while rejecting the Quran. Each community took the portion of divine message it would accept, bound it as *their* Zabūr, and used that text to exclude everything Allah sent afterward. Al-Muyassar reads the verse as an explicit warning (*taḥdhīr*) against factionalism and division in religion — the followers of the prophets made their religion into multiple religions after being commanded to remain united, each faction claiming its text as the complete one.

The lexical dimension of *fragments* — *zubrah* as a chunk of broken-off heavy matter — belongs to the same root. It does not displace the dominant reading; it deepens it. Because what the classical scholars identified as *scriptures* and what the lexical history gives us as *broken pieces of iron* are not two competing meanings that demand a choice. They are the inside and outside view of the same act seen simultaneously: from outside you observe fragments; from inside each fragment-holder perceives *their Zabūr.* The word was chosen precisely because it holds both angles at once.

Don't think of these as competing perspectives. Think of them as **two camera lenses aimed at a single horror.**

Because what is sectarianism, really? It is taking a piece of the whole and treating it as if it were the whole. It is fragmenting. **And** it is canonizing the fragment. *We took the portion of the truth we accepted, and we bound it as if it were the entire scripture.* The fragmenting and the scripturalizing are not two acts. They are the same act, seen from two sides.

[PAUSE]

Let me say that again because it matters.

When a community splits, what does each splinter do? It writes its own creed. It produces its own books. It builds its own canon out of the portion it took with it. The cutting **becomes** a new Zabūr. The piece **becomes** scripture.

This is the exact pattern al-Ṭabarī identifies in the Abrahamic communities after their prophets — each group consecrating its portion of revelation as definitive and closing off the rest. But the Quran does not offer this as a cautionary tale about distant people. It offers it as a diagnostic template, and the same pattern appears wherever communities form around partial truth.

Allah's word *zuburā* holds the dominant reading (scriptures) and the secondary lexical dimension (fragments) at once because the reality it names contains both. This is why Allah does not say "they cut their affair into pieces" with a more neutral word like *qiṭa'an* (segments) or *ajzā'an* (parts). He chose the word that carries inside itself the deception that makes the cutting permanent — *they wrote their portion up as scripture, and now they cannot put it back together because doing so would mean abandoning what they have canonized.*

[PAUSE]

Think about how this unfolds wherever communities form around a partial truth. A movement starts. It identifies a real insight that the broader community has neglected. So far, so good — every reform begins with a real grievance, a real perception. But then the movement grows. It produces its own literature. Its own podcasts. Its own scholars. Its own internal references that everyone in the movement knows and outsiders don't. Its own enemies. Its own martyrs.

And at some point — almost imperceptibly — the partial truth has become **the** scripture. The rest of the deen that the movement is not focused on becomes background, then irrelevant, then suspect. And anyone inside the movement who tries to point this out is treated as a defector — because they are attacking *the Zabūr.* Which is, originally, a portion.

*Zuburā.*

Now you can hear the verb and the noun together. *Fataqatta'ū amrahum baynahum zuburā.* They cut themselves — thoroughly, deliberately, between themselves — into portions that they then bound and called scripture.

[PAUSE]

But Allah is not done. Look at the second half of the ayah.

### Element Three: فَرِحُونَ — The Pleasure That Seals the Sickness

**كُلُّ حِزْبٍۭ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ**

"Every faction — with what is by them — rejoicing."

Three things to notice here, and each one is a small earthquake.

**First: the word for faction — حِزْبٍ — *ḥizb*.**

The root *ḥ-z-b* in classical Arabic carries the sense of **a hardened group, banded together, organized for a purpose, especially in opposition to another group.** It is the word the Quran uses elsewhere for *ḥizb al-shayṭān* (the faction of Shaytan) and *ḥizb Allāh* (the faction of Allah). It is a politically charged word. It is the word that gives us the modern Arabic for a *political party.*

So Allah is not saying "every group" in some neutral sociological sense. He is saying "every **organized political bloc** of believers-in-their-own-fragment." The fragment is not just held privately. It is institutionalized. It has structure, leadership, opposition.

**Second: the prepositional phrase — بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ — *bimā ladayhim*.**

This means "with what is *by them*" or "with what is *in their possession.*"

Notice what Allah did **not** say. He did not say *bimā ātāhum Allāh* — "with what Allah gave them." He did not say *bimā ḥafiẓū min al-waḥy* — "with what they have preserved of revelation." He did not say *bimā 'indahum min al-ḥaqq* — "with what is with them of the truth."

He said *bimā ladayhim* — "with what is **by them**, **at them**, **in their hand.**"

This is a phrase that emphasizes **possession.** What they're rejoicing in isn't framed as "the truth they received from God" but as "the thing they have, the thing that is theirs, the thing in their possession." The fragment has been internalized so completely that it is no longer experienced as something received from outside — it is experienced as **mine.** *My understanding. My madhhab. My movement. My read of the deen.*

The grammar is doing the diagnosis. The moment you start saying *my* about a piece of revelation, you have begun to cut yourself apart from the whole.

Al-Muyassar captures the full interior picture: each faction is *muʿjab bi-ra'yihi* — enamored of its own view — *zāʿiman annahu ʿalā al-ḥaqq wa-ghayruhu ʿalā al-bāṭil* — claiming it is on the truth while all others are on falsehood. The possessive warmth (*ladayhim* — what is *ours*) and the dismissive certainty about everyone else's error are not two separate features of tribalism. They are one movement, two faces of the same gesture.

**Third — and this is the heart of it — the participle: فَرِحُونَ — *fariḥūn*.**

This word means *rejoicing, exulting, delighting, finding joy in.* The root *f-r-ḥ* is happy joy — but specifically the kind of joy that is loud, externalized, prideful. It is not the quiet contentment of *sakīnah.* It is celebratory pleasure.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this might be the most painful word in the passage.

Each faction is **happy.** They are not anguished about the cutting. They are not weeping over the fragmentation of the ummah. They are not lying awake wondering if they've gotten it wrong. They are **rejoicing.** Each one delighted with their fragment. Each one celebrating their version. Each one posting, quoting, defending, performing their certainty.

This is the most chilling diagnostic detail in the entire passage. Because if the symptom were *suffering* — if the people who fragmented felt sick over their fragmentation — the cure would be straightforward. Pain motivates change. But Allah tells us the symptom is **pleasure.** The fragmenting feels good. Belonging to your zubur and rejecting everyone else's zubur generates a powerful, addictive sense of correctness. Of clarity. Of being on the right side.

Here's what's remarkable. Modern psychology has a name for this and didn't know it had a name fourteen hundred years ago. We call it *in-group bias* or, more precisely, the *confirmation pleasure* generated by having one's existing beliefs validated. Brain imaging studies show that hearing arguments that confirm what we already believe activates reward circuitry — the same circuits activated by food and money. We don't merely *believe* the things our tribe believes. We **enjoy** believing them. We get a hit.

Allah named this in one word, fourteen centuries before MRI machines. *Fariḥūn.* They don't just hold the fragment. They get high off it.

[PAUSE]

And once you see this, you cannot un-see it in our own age.

Open any social media platform. What you are watching is *kullu ḥizbin bimā ladayhim fariḥūn* in real time. Every faction. Rejoicing. In what is by them. The traditionalists rejoicing in their fragment. The reformists rejoicing in theirs. The political left rejoicing. The political right rejoicing. The secularists rejoicing. The Muslim "boys' club" accounts rejoicing. The Muslim feminist accounts rejoicing. Each one happy. Each one certain. Each one with its own zubur — its scriptures, its references, its in-group humor, its enemies.

And the joy is the seal. The joy is what makes the fragment unliftable. Because anyone who tries to convince you that the fragment is not the whole is, in your felt experience, **trying to take away your happiness.** They are coming for the source of your in-group warmth. Your platform. Your community. Your sense of being on the side that gets it.

This is why sectarianism does not respond to argument. You cannot argue someone out of pleasure. You cannot dismantle a person's in-group by showing them that the doctrine is wrong, because they are not holding the doctrine — they are holding the **joy** the doctrine produces, and the doctrine is just the excuse.

**Fariḥūn.** One participle. The entire psychology of tribalism.

### Bridging to the Theme

So look at what these few words have shown us.

A reflexive verb that places the cutting in our own hands.
A noun that holds inside it the deception by which portions become scripture.
A possessive phrase — *bimā ladayhim* — that names the moment we say *my* about the truth.
And a participle that names the addictive pleasure that locks the fragment in place.

This isn't poetry. This is a clinical diagnosis. Allah has, in nine Arabic words, mapped out exactly how a community that was given *one* religion divides itself into many — and why, once divided, the divisions cannot be undone by ordinary means.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about what Allah does when human beings reach this state.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Pleasure of the Fragment — Why Each Faction Rejoices

I want to start outside the ayah for a moment. Inside something you already know.

Think about the last time you scrolled through a thread of comments and felt yourself getting *satisfied* by them. Not informed. Not enlightened. **Satisfied.** Maybe it was a Twitter thread dunking on people you don't agree with. Maybe it was a YouTube video methodically dismantling a viewpoint you've always disliked. Maybe it was a sermon — a Friday khutbah, a podcast, a lecture — where the speaker named the people who are wrong, and you found yourself nodding, leaning forward, almost smiling.

That was *fariḥūn.*

That feeling — the warm pleasure of in-group correctness — is one of the most powerful drugs the human nervous system manufactures. It is more addictive than anger. It is more addictive than sadness. It is more addictive, in the long run, than most of what is officially called addiction. Because it isn't experienced as an indulgence. It is experienced as *understanding.* As *clarity.* As *finally being among people who get it.*

[PAUSE]

Now let me ask a harder question. **Why does Allah associate this pleasure with *cutting the affair apart*?** Why is the joy of in-group identity the symptom of fragmentation, rather than the symptom of unity?

Here is what I think these ayahs are showing us.

There is a kind of joy that comes from being part of *the whole.* And there is a kind of joy that comes from being part of *the part that is set against another part.* They feel similar from the inside. They use a lot of the same emotional machinery. But they are spiritually opposite.

The joy of being part of the whole is quiet. It does not need an enemy. It does not require that anyone else be wrong for it to feel right. It is the feeling of standing in jamāʿah behind an imam whose madhhab might differ from yours and not even noticing, because you are absorbed in something larger than the differences.

The joy of being part of the part-against-the-part is loud. It is celebratory. It is performative. It cannot exist without an opposing camp to define itself against. It is the feeling of belonging to *our side* — and the word *our* is doing all the work, because *our* presupposes *theirs*, and the warmth of *our* is generated by the coldness of *theirs.*

Allah says of the people who cut themselves apart: *fariḥūn.* The joy is the second kind. It is the joy of the part. And here is the sickness inside it: **the pleasure of the part is generated by the cutting itself.** You cannot have the in-group warmth without the out-group cold. You cannot have the satisfaction of being on the right side without there being a wrong side to set yourself against.

So the cutting is not a tragic byproduct of seeking the truth. The cutting is what produces the *experience of having found the truth.* Without the cutting, the warmth would not be there. Without the *baynahum* — the betweenness, the opposition — there would be no in-group glow.

[PAUSE]

Let this land for a moment, because it has implications you may not want.

It means that the moment you find yourself **enjoying** your religious or political position — not in a quiet, settled way, but in the sense of finally feeling vindicated, finally feeling at home with your tribe, finally being able to laugh at the people who don't see it — at that exact moment, *something is wrong.* Not necessarily with the position. Possibly with how you are holding it.

Because Allah has just told us that one of the diagnostic signs of fragmentation is **fariḥ.** Joy. Pleasure. Rejoicing. The people whose religion has been cut into pieces are not the ones suffering. They are the ones celebrating.

And let me say this carefully, because it's important. Allah is not saying joy in religion is wrong. Allah is not saying community is wrong. Allah is not saying that sharing identity with other Muslims is wrong. The Prophet ﷺ smiled. The companions laughed. There is *farḥ* — *And by that let them rejoice* (Yunus 10:58) — in the gifts of Allah.

But there is a *farḥ* that is generated by the gifts of Allah, and there is a *farḥ* that is generated by the cutting. The first is gratitude. The second is tribalism dressed in religious clothes. The first does not require an out-group. The second cannot exist without one.

**A diagnostic question to carry away.** When you feel the warm hit of religious or ideological belonging, ask yourself: *Could I feel this if no one else were wrong? If everyone agreed with me, would the joy still be here? Or is the joy specifically generated by the contrast — by the existence of those people on the other side whom I am not?*

If the joy depends on the contrast, the joy is *fariḥ al-ḥizb.* Which is a warning sign that I am holding a fragment.

[PAUSE]

Now let me bring this back to the architecture of the passage, because the placement matters.

Allah has just declared, one ayah earlier: **wa inna hādhihi ummatukum ummatan wāḥidatan** — "and indeed this, your community, is one community." That is the divine declaration of unity.

And then immediately: *fataqatta'ū.* So they cut themselves apart.

The juxtaposition is the diagnosis. Allah declared *one*; they responded with *many.* Allah named the unity; they manufactured the fragmentation. And the manufacturing was driven, at every step, by the pleasure of belonging to a part that defined itself against other parts.

What's the practical implication? Let me give you one. There is no movement, no madhhab, no reformist faction, no traditionalist faction, no political party, no online community, no podcast tribe — that is more important than the fact that *Allah has said your ummah is one ummah.* Whatever fragment we are tempted to canonize, the divine declaration in 23:52 stands above it. *One.* And anything we do that turns *one* into *many* is, by Allah's own diagnosis, a cutting that we are doing to ourselves, with our own hands, between ourselves, into pieces we are calling scripture and rejoicing over.

The cure is not to abandon every group. The cure is to hold every group inside the prior, larger frame: *we are one ummah; this group I belong to is a sub-current of the one, not a replacement for it; my joy in it must remain joy in a part of the whole, not joy in being against the rest of the whole.*

That is a hard discipline. But it is the discipline these two ayahs demand.

### Theme Two: The Mercy That Withdraws — Reading **فَذَرْهُمْ** as Divine Punishment

Now we move into the second ayah. And we have to slow down here, because what Allah commands His Prophet to do in response to the fragmentation is, in some ways, more terrifying than anything He could have threatened.

**فَذَرْهُمْ فِى غَمْرَتِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ حِينٍ**

"So leave them in their overwhelming flood — until a time."

Begin where the listener already lives.

Imagine — and most of us have lived some version of this — a friend, a family member, someone you love, who is making a choice you can see is going to hurt them. And you've talked to them. Pleaded. Argued. Shown them the evidence. Cried, maybe. And nothing has worked. They are committed to the choice. They are *happy* about the choice. They are inside an in-group that affirms the choice every time they post or speak. And one day, after years of trying, you stop calling. You stop sending the article. You stop arguing on the family WhatsApp. You don't disown them. You don't hate them. You just… stop.

What is that? In the language of relationships, we sometimes call it acceptance. Sometimes we call it letting go. Sometimes we call it grief.

But there is a darker possibility. Sometimes — and this is the one we don't want to talk about — the silence is not acceptance. It is the silence of someone who has concluded that this person can no longer be reached. *Until something happens that they cannot manufacture themselves.* Until reality breaks the spell. And the silence is not abandonment — it is the recognition that words have stopped being the right tool, because the person is no longer in a place where words can do anything.

[PAUSE]

That is what Allah commands here. **فَذَرْهُمْ — *fadharhum* — "so leave them."**

The verb *dharā / yadharu* means "to leave alone, to let be, to step away from." It is a verb of withdrawal. It is not the verb of punishment — *fa-'aqibhum* would be punishment. It is not the verb of guidance — *fahdihim* would be guidance. It is not even the verb of warning — *fa-andhirhum* would be warning. It is the verb of *letting be.*

And classical commentators have noted, with some concern, that this is one of the most severe responses Allah ever commands. Because it is the response of *no longer arguing.* No longer pleading. No longer sending the warning. Just… leave them. They have made their choice. They have written their fragment into scripture. They are rejoicing in it. There is no further word that will reach them in the state they are in. So step back.

This is sometimes called *istidrāj* in the classical literature — the divine pattern of granting freedom and abundance to those who have rejected guidance, rather than restraining them. *Sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā ya'lamūn* — "We will gradually draw them on from where they do not know." The withdrawal of correction is itself a category of divine response, and in the classical view it is one of the most fearsome.

What does it mean for Allah to *leave* someone? It does not mean Allah ceases to exist toward them — Allah's existence and providence sustain every breath. It means that the active calling, the patient sending of warning after warning, the gentle redirections — these are paused. The person is given exactly what they wanted: to be left alone with their fragment. To rejoice undisturbed.

And — this is the part that should stop us — in the moment of being left, the person rarely realizes it. They do not feel abandoned. They feel, if anything, *vindicated.* Their certainty is not being challenged anymore. Their fragment is going unrebutted. Their in-group is growing. From the inside, this looks like winning. From above, it is the silence of a divine response that has decided this person can no longer hear.

[PAUSE]

Now look at where Allah leaves them. **فِى غَمْرَتِهِمْ — *fī ghamratihim* — "in their overwhelming flood."**

The word *ghamrah* comes from the root **غ م ر** — *gh-m-r* — which means **to cover, to submerge, to drown, to overwhelm with something liquid.** Classical Arabic uses this root for water that rises above your head. *Mā' ghamr* is water deep enough to drown in. *Ghamarāt al-mawt* — "the *ghamarāt* of death" — is the wave of dying that closes over you.

The early commentator Mujāhid, as transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, glossed the word with disarming simplicity: *fī ḍalālihim* — "in their misguidance." The water image and the doctrinal diagnosis are the same image. The *ghamrah* is not a metaphor the Quran is reaching for from outside; it is the precise word for the state of being submerged in one's own misguidance without perceiving the submersion as misguidance — because the water has become the air.

But here's what's remarkable about this image. Allah is not saying *fī ḥabsihim* — "in their imprisonment." He is not saying *fī ẓulmatihim* — "in their darkness." He is using a water image. **They are submerged. They are underwater. They are drowning, but they don't know they are drowning, because their entire perceptual world is the water.**

This is the *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — the pointing toward an unseen reality through a physical image. What is the unseen reality? It is the cognitive state of the deeply tribal. From inside the in-group, the in-group is not a fragment — it is **the whole world.** Everything you see, everyone you talk to, every piece of media you consume, every reference you laugh at, is generated inside this single perceptual chamber. There is no outside. There is no air. The water has closed over your head and become indistinguishable from atmosphere.

And here is the most chilling part of the image. Drowning, in real water, is panicked. The person knows what is happening and fights. But *ghamrah* in the Quran is regularly used for a state in which the person does not feel the drowning. They are submerged but unaware. The water has become so total that it is no longer experienced as wet — it is experienced as the medium in which one lives.

Hold this image and put it next to *fariḥūn.* They are **rejoicing** in their **drowning.** They are partying inside the *ghamrah.* They cannot see that they are submerged because the submersion is total — there is no outside-of-the-water experience to compare it to.

[PAUSE]

This is the modern echo chamber, perfectly described in a single word, fourteen centuries before the platform algorithms.

When you live inside a feed that has been algorithmically tuned to your existing positions, you are inside a *ghamrah.* The water is your information environment. Every link, every video, every account you follow, every recommended account, every comment thread — all of it is the same water. There is no air. And from the inside, you do not feel the absence of contrary perspective, because contrary perspective has been so smoothly removed that its absence is not even visible. You feel, instead, that you are seeing reality. *This is just what the world looks like.*

That is *ghamrah.* And the word names it exactly: a covering so total that you cannot perceive there is anything outside it.

And to such a person, Allah says: *fadharhum* — leave them. Until **حِينٍ** — *ḥīn* — "a time." Indefinite. Unspecified. *Some time.*

The classical commentators have engaged what *ḥīn* refers to with some precision. Al-Ṭabarī reads *ḥattā ḥīn* as pointing to a specific appointed reckoning: "to a time coming to them when My punishment arrives." On this reading the silence is not merely patient waiting — it is an eschatological hinge. The divine withdrawal accumulates toward a moment that is fixed in Allah's knowledge and entirely hidden from theirs. Ibn Zayd, as cited by al-Ṭabarī, reads similarly. Some commentators have said it means until death — the moment when the *ghamrah* is lifted by the undeniable clarity of what follows. The ambiguity is deliberate: *some time, only Allah knows when* — precisely so the submerged person cannot schedule their return to air. *I have until 2030. I have until I'm 60. I have until something specific happens.* No. *Ḥīn.* Some time. Unspecified. The water is around you now. The withdrawal is in effect now. The *ḥīn* could arrive at any moment.

[PAUSE]

So what is Allah doing in this second ayah?

He is showing us the most painful divine response in the Quran's repertoire. Not punishment. Not anger. Not even the threat of hell. Just: *leave them.* In their water. Until some unspecified moment when the water is taken away.

And the reason this is the most painful response is precisely that the people receiving it cannot feel it. From inside the *ghamrah*, the silence of Allah feels like the absence of a problem. The ungrieved fragmentation feels like correctness. The unchallenged in-group feels like being on the right side of history. The fariḥūn just keep rejoicing.

The withdrawal is invisible to the one withdrawn from. That is what makes it terrifying.

### A Second Practical Application

What do we do with this — practically — tomorrow morning?

I think there are two disciplines.

**First:** notice when, in your own life, you have stopped receiving correction. Notice when the people who used to challenge you have stopped challenging you. Notice when you have stopped seeking out perspectives that might unsettle yours. Notice when your information environment has become a single water in which you are submerged.

The withdrawal of correction is not always divine. Sometimes it is just our friends getting tired. Sometimes it is just the algorithm doing its work. But in either case, it is a warning sign. The state of *ghamrah* — of not being challenged, of being entirely surrounded by validation — is exactly the state in which Allah, in this ayah, *leaves people alone.* If you find yourself in that state, **construct your own challenges.** Seek out the disagreement before the disagreement is no longer offered to you.

**Second:** when you are tempted to give up on someone — to *dharhum* them in your own small way — pause. Allah's *fadharhum* is the response of the All-Knowing to a state of complete inaccessibility. We do not have that knowledge. The person we are tempted to write off may still be reachable; the closing of the water may not yet be complete. The discipline of believers is to keep speaking — *udʿu ilā sabīli rabbika bil-ḥikmah* — even when speaking seems to have stopped working, because we are not in a position to know whose *ḥīn* has arrived. Only Allah is.

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Now look back at the full structure of these three ayahs as a unit, because the architecture is what completes the meaning.

> **23:52** — *And indeed this, your community, is one community, and I am your Lord, so be conscious of Me.*
>
> **23:53** — *So they cut their affair apart between themselves into pieces — each faction rejoicing in what is with them.*
>
> **23:54** — *So leave them in their overwhelming flood for a time.*

Two conjunctions — two *fa*'s — link these ayahs into a single sentence of cause and consequence.

*One ummah.* **Fa.** *They cut it apart.* **Fa.** *Leave them.*

The first *fa* is the human response to divine declaration. The second *fa* is the divine response to the human response. And the entire arc is contained in the two letters.

Notice the symmetry. Allah declared **unity** and commanded **taqwā.** They responded with **fragmentation** and exhibited **farḥ.** The four terms map exactly:

- Unity (one ummah) → Fragmentation (zubur)
- Taqwā (Allah-consciousness) → Farḥ (in-group celebration)

And then the divine response: not unity restored, not fragmentation punished, but **withdrawal** — *fadharhum.* Allah does not, in this passage, force them back together. He does not strike them. He simply *steps back.* Because the cutting was theirs to do, and the consequences are theirs to inhabit, until *ḥīn.*

This architecture is a complete theological grammar of how religious communities die. It is a single, terrifying, beautiful map.

### Bringing It Together

Look at what the linguistic discoveries and the themes have shown together.

The Form V verb told us: *the cutting was reflexive — done by us to ourselves.* The theme of in-group pleasure showed us *why* — because the cutting generates the warmth that holds us inside the fragment.

The double-meaning of *zuburā* told us: *the portions became scripture.* The theme of fariḥ showed us *how* this happens — because once you are rejoicing in the fragment, you cannot let go of it without losing the joy, so you canonize it to make the joy permanent.

The water-image of *ghamrah* told us: *they are submerged in their own perceptual environment.* The theme of divine withdrawal showed us *why this matters* — because submersion in a total environment makes correction inaudible, and at some point the divine response is to stop offering correction that cannot be received.

These are not three linguistic insights and two themes laid side by side. They are one diagnosis approached from five angles. The illness is the same illness. Sectarian fragmentation, in-group joy, canonization of the fragment, perceptual submersion, and divine withdrawal are five names for the same spiritual collapse.

### Questions to Carry

Five questions. Not rhetorical. Real ones to sit with.

1. **What is one position you currently hold that you genuinely *enjoy* holding — that feels good to defend, that warms you when you see it confirmed by your tribe? Could you hold that position with the same conviction if there were no in-group celebrating it with you? If not, what does that suggest about the position?**

2. **When was the last time someone you respect challenged a deeply-held conviction of yours — not online, but in person, with knowledge and patience? If you cannot remember, who in your life is currently in a position to do that for you? And if no one is, what does that mean?**

3. **What is the *ghamrah* in your life right now — the information environment, the social circle, the platform, the community, that has become so total that you can no longer easily perceive what lies outside it? When did it become total? Was there a moment you noticed it closing over your head, or did the water rise so gradually you never saw the surface disappear?**

4. **Is there a movement, a madhhab, a sub-community, a sub-current of the ummah that you currently belong to — and that you have, perhaps without realizing it, begun to treat as a replacement for the one ummah Allah declared in 23:52, rather than as a sub-current within it?**

5. **Whom in your life are you currently tempted to *dharhum* — to step back from, to stop calling, to write off as unreachable? Are you certain their *ḥīn* has come? And what would it look like to keep calling, in the way of the prophets, even when the calling seems to fall on submerged ears?**

### One-Sentence Distillation

When we mistake our fragment of the truth for the whole, the joy of belonging to that fragment becomes the water that drowns us — and the most terrifying response Allah can give to such a state is not punishment, but to leave us in it, alone, until a time only He knows.

### The Closing Invitation

Something needs to shift after sitting with this. Not as obligation — as invitation.

Notice, this week, the moments of in-group warmth. Don't necessarily abandon them. Just *notice* them. Catch the small hit of pleasure when your tribe scores a point against another tribe. Catch the satisfaction when a video confirms what you already believed. Catch the joy of finally being among people who get it.

And then ask: **Is this the joy of being part of the whole? Or is this the joy of being part of the part-against-the-part?**

If it is the second, you have found a piece of *ghamrah* in your own life. You don't have to drain it. You don't have to leave the community, leave the platform, leave the movement. But you can begin, quietly, to introduce some air. Read someone you've been told is wrong. Listen to a scholar from a madhhab not your own. Sit, for one majlis, with people who would describe themselves as belonging to a different *ḥizb* than you. Let the water level drop a little. Let yourself remember what surface feels like.

Because Allah declared *one ummah.* And every breath of air we let into our submerged fragments is a small refusal of the cutting.

### Du'a

> Yā Rabb, You declared us one community, and we cut ourselves apart. We took pieces of Your truth and called them our scripture, and we rejoiced in our fragments while You watched.
>
> Do not leave us in our flood. Do not let us be the ones whom You step back from while we mistake Your silence for our victory. Send us correction we can hear. Send us companions who will challenge what we love. Drain enough of the water that we remember there is air, and a sky, and a single ummah whose Lord is You.
>
> And before our *ḥīn* arrives — soften us, return us, gather us. Āmīn.
