# The Severance of Speech: When the Conversation with Allah Ends

## Surah Al-Mu'minun, Ayahs 106–108

> [ayah:23:106] قَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَا غَلَبَتْ عَلَيْنَا شِقْوَتُنَا وَكُنَّا قَوْمًا ضَآلِّينَ
>
> [ayah:23:107] رَبَّنَآ أَخْرِجْنَا مِنْهَا فَإِنْ عُدْنَا فَإِنَّا ظَـٰلِمُونَ
>
> [ayah:23:108] قَالَ ٱخْسَـُٔوا۟ فِيهَا وَلَا تُكَلِّمُونِ

*Qālū Rabbanā ghalabat ʿalaynā shiqwatunā wa kunnā qawman ḍāllīn. Rabbanā akhrijnā minhā fa'in ʿudnā fa'innā ẓālimūn. Qāla ikhsa'ū fīhā wa lā tukallimūn.*

> "They will say: 'Our Lord, our wretchedness overcame us, and we were a people astray. Our Lord, take us out from it — and if we return [to disobedience], we would truly be wrongdoers.' He will say: 'Be driven away into it, and do not speak to Me.'"

## INTRODUCTION

### Where This Moment Sits

Hold this in your mind for a moment. The surah you are reading is called **Al-Mu'minun** — *The Believers*. It opens with one of the most beautiful announcements in the entire Quran:

**قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ** — "The believers have indeed succeeded."

And then Allah tells you who these successful believers are. They are not described by their wealth. Not by their lineage. Not by their tribe or status. They are described, almost entirely, by what happens in **conversation between them and their Lord**.

> *Those who, in their prayer, are in a state of khushūʿ.*
>
> *Those who turn away from idle talk.*
>
> *Those who guard their prayers.*

Look at how much of the believers' identity is built around speech — speech that is intimate, humble, focused, repeated, protected. Salah is not just an action. Salah is a **conversation**. The believer is the one who has not lost the ability to speak with Allah — and not lost the ability to listen.

The surah moves through creation — the drop of water becoming a clinging form becoming bones clothed with flesh. Through prophets — Nuh, Hud (or Salih), Musa, ʿIsa. Through the universal pattern of how every messenger was met. And then it arrives, ayah by ayah, at the final reckoning. The trumpet is blown. The lineages collapse. The scales are set up. Some are saved. And then we arrive at this passage — the last words spoken, the last conversation, between those who refused and the One they refused.

[PAUSE]

And here is what makes this passage almost unbearable: **the surah that began with the believers' speech to Allah ends with the unbelievers being told they may not speak to Allah anymore.**

The very thing that defined the saved — that they spoke, prayed, called, listened — is the very thing taken from those who are lost.

### The Hook

Three short ayahs. Two short pleas, and one short reply. That is all.

But hidden inside these few words are some of the most precise diagnostics of the human soul that you will find in any sacred text. Why their plea fails. Where the cracks in their repentance show. Why even at the gate of the Fire — facing what they face — their language still betrays them.

And then a single word from Allah. One word. A word so heavy that classical scholars hesitate over it. A word that, in the Arabic language, is reserved for **a particular kind of dismissal** — and once you know what that word is used for, you will not forget this passage for as long as you live.

Let's earn it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to look closely at three things — three small features of these ayahs that, once seen, will reorganize your understanding of the entire scene.

The first is what they say in their plea.
The second is the small word *if* in their promise.
The third is the single command that closes the door.

Each one will seem small at first. Stay with me. They are not small.

---

### Element One: "Our Wretchedness Overcame Us" — The Grammar of Refusing Agency

Before we open the ayah, let's teach the concept.

In any language, when you describe a thing that happened to you, you have a choice. You can say:

> *"I lied."*

Or you can say:

> *"The lie came out of my mouth."*

Both describe the same event. But they are not the same statement. The first owns the action. The second turns the speaker into a passive scene where the action just *happened*. Linguists call this **agentive language** versus **non-agentive language**. Therapists notice it. Judges notice it. Children, when they are learning to dodge consequence, master non-agentive language remarkably early. *"It broke."* Not *"I broke it."*

Arabic, like English, lets you do both. And Allah, who taught the Arabic language its own mouth, knows exactly what choice each grammatical structure carries.

Now hold that in mind, and listen to the first thing they say in this ayah.

> **قَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَا غَلَبَتْ عَلَيْنَا شِقْوَتُنَا وَكُنَّا قَوْمًا ضَآلِّينَ**

Three movements. Read it carefully.

**First**, they call: *Rabbanā* — "Our Lord." Already, notice: they still know the word. Even now, they still know whose Lord they are addressing. The vocabulary of belief is intact. It is not the vocabulary that was the problem.

**Second**, they describe their condition: **غَلَبَتْ عَلَيْنَا شِقْوَتُنَا** — *"Our shiqwah overcame us."*

Stay with me on this. There are two words here that deserve attention.

#### The word شِقْوَة (*shiqwah*)

The root **ش-ق-و** is the precise opposite of سعادة (*saʿādah*) — "felicity, blessedness, ultimate flourishing." When the Quran wants to describe the saved in another famous passage, it splits humanity into *shaqī* and *saʿīd* — the wretched and the blessed (Hud 11:105). *Shiqwah* is not a small word. It is not "bad luck." It is not "a rough time." It is the **whole condition of a soul gone wrong** — a misery that goes all the way down to the root.

*(A lexical note: al-Tabari documents two equally well-attested qira'at for this word. The form in the displayed Arabic above is شِقْوَتُنَا — shiqwatunā, with a kasra and no alif. There is a second, equally authoritative reading: شَقَاوَتُنَا — shaqāwatunā, with a fatha and a long alif. Al-Tabari states plainly that both are well-known readings, both transmitted by qualified scholars of recitation, and both carry identical meaning. Whichever form you encounter, the root and its connotations remain exactly as described here.)*

So far, this is honest. They are naming the depth of their condition. Good.

But notice the possessive: **شِقْوَتُنَا** — *our* shiqwah. Not "the shiqwah of the world." Not "the shiqwah Iblis sent us." Theirs. They acknowledge that the wretchedness was their own.

So they got something right. They named it, and they owned it as theirs.

[PAUSE]

But now look at the verb.

#### The verb غَلَبَتْ (*ghalabat*)

The root **غ-ل-ب** in classical Arabic means *to overcome, to conquer, to overpower in a contest.* It is the language of battle. When two armies clash and one wins, this is *ghalaba*. When a wave of grief rises up and you cannot stand against it, *ghalabaka* — it overwhelmed you. The word implies that there were two parties — *you* and *the other thing* — and the other thing won.

Now look at what they have just said. *Our wretchedness — yes, it was ours. But — it overcame us.*

In other words: **"It was bigger than we were. We tried, but it beat us. It was an opponent we couldn't defeat."**

Listen to that with fresh ears.

This is not, *"We chose wretchedness."* This is not, *"We built it, brick by brick, every day for sixty years."* This is, *"It came over us like a tide we couldn't stop."*

It is the language of a flood. It is the language of weather. It is the language of being a small boat in a storm — agency-free, will-free, sovereignty-free. *We were just there. The shiqwah did the rest.*

[PAUSE]

And then the third movement: **وَكُنَّا قَوْمًا ضَآلِّينَ** — *"and we were a people astray."*

Here too, look at the choice. The word *ḍāllīn* comes from **ض-ل-ل** — to wander off, to lose the path, like a camel that strays from the caravan, like a sheep that drifts from the flock. The word, by itself, can carry a hint of *being lost* rather than *choosing wrong.* Of course it can also describe deliberate deviation. But notice: even in the strongest reading, **they are still phrasing it as a directional misfortune.** *We were lost. We were astray. We took a wrong turn.*

They will not — even now, even here — say the words: *"We rebelled. We refused. We knew, and we said no."*

#### Putting it together

Listen carefully to what they have just done. In one sentence, they have:

1. **Externalized** their condition — *the shiqwah* did this, like a third actor.
2. **Cast themselves as the conquered**, not the architects, of their own ruin.
3. **Used the gentlest available verb** for misguidance — *we strayed* — instead of any verb that owns the deliberate refusal.

This is the language of *almost-confession*. It looks like an apology. It carries some of the words an apology would carry. But the grammar — *the grammar* — is still the grammar of someone who is, fundamentally, **a victim of their own life**.

And here is the diagnostic: **this is exactly the same grammar Iblis used.**

Look at Iblis. When Allah confronts him, what does he say? *"Because You misled me…"* (al-Aʿrāf 7:16). The action is sourced *outside* of him.

Look at Adam (peace be upon him). What does he say? **رَبَّنَا ظَلَمْنَا أَنفُسَنَا** — *"Our Lord, we have wronged our own selves"* (7:23). The action is sourced *in* him. He is the agent. He is the architect. The act is named by its true name: *ẓulm*, wronging.

These two sentences — the sentence of Iblis, the sentence of Adam — are the two grammars of the human soul before Allah. **One is the grammar of the saved. The other is the grammar of the lost.** And the difference is not in volume of regret. It is in the **subject of the verb.**

Now look one more time at our ayah:

> *"Our shiqwah overcame us. We were a people astray."*

[PAUSE]

The shiqwah is the subject of the first verb. They are the object — *over us*. And in the second verb, *kunnā ḍāllīn* — the active participle softens it again.

In the very moment when the only thing that could save them is **Adam's sentence**, they speak **Iblis's sentence**. Even now. Even here.

What this single feature reveals is devastating in its precision: **a person can recognize they are in hell and still not have learned the one sentence that would make them belong outside of it.**

[PAUSE]

Before we move to the second element, however, there is a harder dimension to sit with — because the classical commentators saw something in this language that is more theologically serious than the psychological grammar alone.

Al-Tabari, reading this ayah, did not primarily frame *ghalabat ʿalaynā shiqwatunā* as a psychological deflection. He read it as a **metaphysical argument**: the people of the Fire, in his understanding, are saying *"what was foreknown for us in Your prior knowledge and written for us in the Preserved Tablet."* Al-Muyassar reads it the same way: *"our pleasures and desires, predetermined in Your foreknowledge."*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight of that difference?

They are not merely saying: *"We were weak, life overwhelmed us."* They are making a far harder theological claim: *"Our wretchedness was written — by You — in the Book that existed before creation."* This is the qadar argument in its most direct and naked form — an invocation of divine foreknowledge as a defense against divine accountability. And the classical tradition took it seriously as a real argument that had to be understood and answered on its own terms, not dismissed.

The answer, however, is not delivered in words. It is embedded in the structure of the passage itself.

Look at what they say in their very next breath: **فَإِنْ عُدْنَا فَإِنَّا ظَـٰلِمُونَ** — *"If we return, we would be wrongdoers."* A *ẓālim* is someone who does wrong through their own agency. You cannot threaten to be a *ẓālim* if you have no will. You cannot make a conditional promise about your own future choices if you have no capacity for choice. The qadar argument of the first sentence is already being undone by the vow of the second. They invoke predestination to dissolve the past; they implicitly invoke free will to promise a different future — both claims sitting three short ayahs apart, the contradiction not hidden but glowing.

The classical tradition wrestled carefully with how divine foreknowledge and human agency coexist, and reached nuanced answers that affirm both. What it rejected was the use of qadar as a *retroactive defense* — invoked only in the moment of consequence, by those who had spent their entire lives using their will freely to pursue desire and to refuse guidance. This is one of the places where the Quran holds that contradiction up to the light not by arguing against it, but by letting it speak for itself.

### Element Two: The Word *If* — The Conditional That Damns

Let's teach the next concept first.

In every language, the word *if* has a peculiar power. It is the word of conditions. It is the word that says, *"What I am about to commit to is not absolute — it depends."*

Imagine someone at a wedding. The vow is, *"I will love you forever."* Now imagine they said instead, *"If I happen to keep loving you, I will love you forever."* The second sentence is a vow that is not a vow. The very presence of *if* has hollowed it out from the inside.

Children learning to apologize do this all the time. *"If I hurt you, I'm sorry."* Notice: that is not an apology. The *if* is a hidden door. Through that door, the child reserves the right to say later: *"Well, you weren't really hurt, so my apology never had to fire."*

Adults do it too. We have all done it. The conditional *if* in a confession is one of the oldest tricks of the human heart for **looking like we have given something while keeping it back.**

Hold that idea. Now read the second ayah of our passage.

> **رَبَّنَآ أَخْرِجْنَا مِنْهَا فَإِنْ عُدْنَا فَإِنَّا ظَـٰلِمُونَ**
>
> *"Our Lord, take us out from it; and **if** we return [to disobedience], **then** we would truly be wrongdoers."*

This is their second sentence. This is their plea. They have called Allah *Rabb*. They have asked for release: *akhrijnā* — "take us out."

And then comes the crack. **فَإِنْ عُدْنَا** — *"and if we return."*

[PAUSE]

Stop. Read it again. Slowly.

**"If we return."**

Look at what is hidden inside that word. They are at the lip of the Fire. The flames are licking their faces — the previous ayah said, *"The Fire will scorch their faces, and they within it will be grimacing"* (23:104). They have lived through the moment of resurrection, the trumpet, the scales, the descent. And from inside that, in the deepest pit they will ever know, what comes out of their mouth?

**"If we return."**

Not: *"We will never return. We could never return. The very thought is unthinkable. Save us, and we will be Yours, every breath, until we die again."*

But: *"If we return."*

[PAUSE]

The conditional has not died in them. Even the Fire has not killed it. They are still — at this moment, in this pit — **calculating with Allah**. Hedging. Reserving. Holding back, even as they ask. The grammar gives away the heart: **the part of them that bargained with Allah in the dunyā is still bargaining now.**

It is worth pausing on what they are actually asking for. Al-Muyassar reads *akhrijnā minhā* as a request not merely to be released from the pain but to be **returned to the dunyā** — *"take us out and restore us to the world."* This gives the conditional *fa'in ʿudnā* its full sharpness: they are asking to re-enter the arena where every choice was made, where every messenger called, where every sign arrived — and even in making that request, they cannot bring themselves to say *"we will not return to disobedience."* Only: *"if we do."*

The door to the past remains open in their grammar, even as they beg to be released from its consequence.

And then look at the second half of the *if* clause. **فَإِنَّا ظَـٰلِمُونَ** — *"then we would indeed be wrongdoers."*

Now this is interesting. The word here is **ظَـٰلِمُونَ** — *ẓālimūn*. From the root **ظ-ل-م**. And this word — at last, *finally* — is the right word. *Ẓulm* is precisely the agent-owning word that *ḍalāl* in the previous ayah was not. *Ẓulm* says: I did wrong. I committed an injustice. I am the doer.

Do you see what just happened? **They know the right word.** They have it in their vocabulary. They reach for it — but only inside the conditional *if* clause, only as a hypothetical, only as a *"in the imaginary case that we did this again."*

[PAUSE]

They are willing to call themselves *ẓālimūn* in a future they will never live.

And unwilling to call themselves *ẓālimūn* in the past they already lived.

The word that could have saved them — *"Our Lord, we wronged ourselves"* — is right there in their sentence. They have it. They simply cannot deploy it where it would matter: on the life already spent. They pocket the right word and spend the wrong one.

This is the second diagnostic. Conditional repentance is not repentance. It is the *shape* of repentance with the soul of calculation still running inside it. The outward form — calling on Allah, asking for release, acknowledging that more wrong would be wrong — mimics the language of *tawbah*. But *tawbah* is unconditional. It does not come with hedges. It does not come with reserved escape routes. Al-Tabari's reading of their plea puts it plainly: they are asking to be released so that *if* they return to what Allah dislikes of deeds, *then* they would be wrongdoers — with the wording itself holding the door open rather than closing it. A person who has truly broken with their past does not frame their promise as a conditional. They frame it as a severance.

This is what the word *if* costs them.

### Element Three: The Word That Ends All Words — *Ikhsa'ū*

Now we arrive at the word the introduction promised you.

Three short sentences of pleading. Two appeals to *Rabbanā*. One self-description. One request for release. One conditional vow. Then — on the other side of all of it — a single response from Allah.

Not an argument. Not a rebuttal. Not a counter-plea. Not a sentence. One word.

> **قَالَ ٱخْسَـُٔوا۟ فِيهَا وَلَا تُكَلِّمُونِ**

*Qāla: ikhsa'ū fīhā wa lā tukallimūn.*

*He said: Be driven away into it — and do not speak to Me.*

Let's look at the word. **ٱخْسَـُٔوا۟** — *ikhsa'ū*.

It comes from the root **خ-س-أ**. And if you have spent time with classical Arabic — or if you have read certain other ayahs carefully — you will recognize what this root does.

#### What *khasa'a* means

In classical Arabic, *khasa'a* is not a word you use for a person. Let that land. It is not the word you use when you dismiss a subordinate. It is not the word you use when you rebuke a servant. It is not even the word you use when you drive away an enemy.

*Khasa'a* is the word you use to drive away a **dog**. Or a stray animal. With contempt. Without engagement. Without the dignity that even a rebuke would grant. You do not rebuke an animal with arguments. You do not explain to a dog why it should leave. You say *ikhsa'* — *get away* — and the creature is dismissed not as an interlocutor, not as a party worth addressing, but as something that should not be here.

The Quran has used this root before. At 2:65, speaking of those who violated the Sabbath: *كُونُوا قِرَدَةً خَاسِئِينَ* — *"Be apes, contemptibly driven away."* The root is *khāsi'īn* — dismissed with contempt, sent away in disgrace. At 67:4, sight returns from attempting to find a crack in the heavens *khāsi'an wa huwa ḥasīr* — humbled and beaten back, unable to penetrate. In each use, the word carries the same register: something that has been comprehensively turned away, without negotiation, without recognition as an equal party in the exchange.

Now it is the word chosen for the people of the Fire.

[PAUSE]

Ibn Kathir understands *ikhsa'ū* as: *abide therein, humiliated, despised and scorned.* He reads *walā tukallimūni* as: *do not ask for this again, for I will not respond to you.* This is not the silence of distance. This is the silence of a sealed door — sealed not merely against entry, but against appeal.

Think about what this word choice reveals. If Allah had said *"uskutū"* — *"be silent"* — that would be a command given to someone whose voice has been registered, whose plea has been heard, whose existence is acknowledged in the very act of being told to stop. Even in silencing a person, you are recognizing them as a person who speaks.

*Ikhsa'ū* is different. It is not the silencing of a voice. It is the erasure of the **standing** to speak. The people of the Fire are not told their request is denied. They are told, with the word used for a stray dog, that the category of "those whose requests are considered" no longer includes them.

#### Walā tukallimūni

And then the second half: **وَلَا تُكَلِّمُونِ** — *"and do not speak to Me."*

The root **ك-ل-م** gives us *kalima* — word — and *takallama* — to speak. This is the root of all meaningful speech in Arabic. Every conversation is a *kalām*. Every word is a *kalima*. The *Kalām* of Allah is the Quran itself — the divine speech that brought creation into being and guidance into the world.

And now Allah says: *lā tukallimūni.* Do not bring your *kalima* before Me. The very channel through which a human being reaches Allah — speech, du'ā, calling, naming — is closed.

[PAUSE]

Hold this against the opening of the surah.

At the beginning, Allah described the believers as people in *khushūʿ* in their *ṣalāh*. Salah — the five daily conversations, the structured speaking-to-Allah, the repeated calling of *Allāhu Akbar*, the standing and bowing and prostrating before the One who hears. The believer was described not by their wealth or lineage but by the **quality of their speech to Allah**. They guarded their prayers. They turned away from idle talk — *laghw* — not because talk is evil, but because they were protecting something precious: the channel. The conversation.

That is what the believer was: someone who still had the conversation.

And now here is its mirror. Someone from whom the conversation has been taken. Not because they could not speak — their words are right here in the ayah, detailed and theologically aware. Not because their voice failed them. But because **they spent a lifetime refusing to speak the one language that mattered, and now that language has been revoked.**

*Walā tukallimūni.*

Do not speak to Me.

The surah that began with *qad aflaha al-mu'minūn* ends with this. The symmetry is not decorative. It is the entire argument of the surah, compressed into a single prohibition.

## PART 2: WHAT THIS PASSAGE ASKS OF US

We have looked at three elements — the grammar of their plea, the conditional of their promise, and the word of their dismissal. Let us now bring them together and ask what this passage is asking of *us* — those who are still in the conversation, still on the side of the door where speech is possible.

### The Grammar We Use

Take a moment. Think of the last time you spoke to Allah about something that hurt you. About a sin you keep returning to. About a pattern you have not been able to break.

What grammar did you use?

Did you say: *"Conditions are difficult. I was raised this way. My circumstances shaped me. The pressure was too much."*

Or did you say: *"My Lord, I have wronged my own self."*

[PAUSE]

This is not a call for self-flagellation. Islam has never asked us to despise ourselves. The tradition is rich with mercy, with the understanding that human beings are weak, that temptation is real, that the nafs has its gravity. All of this is true.

But there is a difference between bringing your weakness to Allah — honestly, as a human being who is struggling — and deploying your weakness as a defense. There is a difference between *"I am struggling with this, and I need You"* and *"This overcame me, so here we are."*

The people of the Fire know the word *Rabbanā*. They know the word *shiqwah*. They know the word *ẓālimūn*. Every piece of the vocabulary of repentance is in their mouths. What is missing is not the vocabulary. What is missing is **the placement of the subject in the sentence**. *I* did this. Not: *it* did this to me.

Adam's sentence is three words: **ظَلَمْنَا أَنفُسَنَا** — *we wronged our own selves.* The verb is first person. The object is *our own selves* — we are both the doer and the done-to. There is no third actor. There is no flood. There is no storm that arrived without invitation. There is only a person who did something, and a Lord who is hearing the acknowledgment of it.

The people of the Fire had an entire lifetime to speak that sentence. Many of them had messengers. They heard the recitation. They had the words. What they did not do was use Adam's grammar — early, often, before the weight of accumulated refusal became a *shiqwah* that truly was, by then, too heavy to lift alone.

### The Conditionality We Carry

There is something uncomfortable in Element Two that we should not skip past.

We have all made conditional promises to Allah. *"If You get me through this, I will pray more."* *"If things improve, I will give more in charity."* *"If this test passes, I will change."* This is not necessarily the language of bad faith — sometimes it is the language of a creature at its limit, reaching for whatever handhold it can find.

But the question this passage asks is: **when the condition is met, does the vow fire?**

The people of the Fire had their conditions met many times over. They had ease and used it for themselves. They had hardship and used it as a reason to defer. The condition never fired because the heart behind it was not truly committed — it was *calculating*. And you can tell the difference between a conditional heart and a surrendered heart, eventually, by whether the vow actually fires when the condition is met.

The invitation of this passage is not to never have doubt, never have struggle, never ask for ease. It is to notice — in your own du'ā, in your own conversation with Allah — where the *if* is hiding. Where you are speaking the language of the saved while holding a reservation back. Where *ẓālimūn* is on the tip of your tongue but you are deploying it only hypothetically, into an imaginary future rather than into the actual past.

The Fire does not teach this lesson. That is what this passage reveals: the Fire does not transform. The people inside it speak exactly as they spoke outside of it. The grammar that went unexamined in the dunyā is the grammar they carry into the akhirah. The lesson that was available for free — through a messenger, through a verse, through a moment of honest reckoning in the small hours — costs everything once the door has closed.

### The Conversation While It Lasts

And so we return to where the surah began: **قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ.**

The believers have indeed succeeded.

They succeeded not because they were without *shiqwah*. Not because they never strayed. Not because their grammar was always perfect and their confessions always clean. The Quran is too honest about human nature to describe the saved as those who never failed. They succeeded because they **kept the conversation**. They came back to *Rabbanā*. They used Adam's sentence when they needed it. They prayed even when they didn't want to. They called even when they doubted the call would be heard. They did not allow the conversation to go cold.

And here — in these three ayahs — is what the conversation going cold looks like.

[PAUSE]

Three short ayahs. Two pleas and one reply.

*Our wretchedness overcame us* — the grammar of the conquered, carrying a claim older than this moment: that what was written in the Preserved Tablet absolves the hand that moved.

*If we return, we would be wrongdoers* — the grammar of the still-calculating, the right word in the wrong tense, the conditional that never dissolved.

*Be driven away, and do not speak to Me* — the severance of all grammar, delivered in a word that was never meant for a person.

[PAUSE]

This is the passage you return to when you want to understand what is at stake in every conversation you have with Allah before this moment. Every *Allāhu Akbar* at the start of prayer. Every *Rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā* in the small hours. Every moment of du'ā where you could reach for Adam's sentence — or choose the easier one.

The door is open now.

The word *if* in your promise is still yours to remove.

Adam's sentence is still available.

The surah that closes with *walā tukallimūni* opens with *qad aflaha* — and the entire distance between those two phrases is the distance between the grammar you choose and the grammar you carry to the end.

Speak while the speaking is still received.
