# When the Body Becomes the Argument

## *Surah Al-Mu'minun, ayahs 33-34*

---

## Introduction

### The Ayahs

> وَقَالَ ٱلْمَلَأُ مِن قَوْمِهِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَكَذَّبُوا۟ بِلِقَآءِ ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ وَأَتْرَفْنَـٰهُمْ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا مَا هَـٰذَآ إِلَّا بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُكُمْ يَأْكُلُ مِمَّا تَأْكُلُونَ مِنْهُ وَيَشْرَبُ مِمَّا تَشْرَبُونَ
>
> *Wa qāla al-malaʾu min qawmihi alladhīna kafarū wa kadhdhabū bi-liqāʾi al-ākhirati wa atrafnāhum fī al-ḥayāti ad-dunyā mā hādhā illā basharun mithlukum, yaʾkulu mimmā taʾkulūna minhu wa yashrabu mimmā tashrabūn.*

> وَلَئِنْ أَطَعْتُم بَشَرًا مِّثْلَكُمْ إِنَّكُمْ إِذًا لَّخَـٰسِرُونَ
>
> *Wa la-in aṭaʿtum basharan mithlakum innakum idhan la-khāsirūn.*

> "And the chiefs of his people who disbelieved and denied the meeting of the Hereafter — and to whom We had given the comforts of this worldly life — said: 'This is no more than a human like you. He eats from what you eat and drinks from what you drink. And if you obey a human like yourselves, you will surely then be losers.'"

---

### Where We Are

The surah has just closed the longest prophet story in its sequence — Nuh, the flood, the ark, the silence afterward. The reciter has lived through what it feels like when an entire civilization rejects a man for a thousand years.

And then, almost without breath, Allah says:

*"Then We raised after them another generation."*

He does not name the prophet. He does not name the people. He places them in time — *after them* — and lets the story repeat.

Classical commentators do not share this silence. Al-Ṭabarī says plainly that the messenger intended here is Ṣāliḥ (peace be upon him) and his people are Thamūd. Ibn Kathīr notes the scholars' discussion: some held that these were ʿĀd — the direct successors of Nuh's civilization — while others identified them as Thamūd, the people who would ultimately be overtaken by the Ṣayḥah, the devastating Cry. This is the classical biographical reading of the passage, and it is the foundation.

But there is also something worth noticing in how the Quranic text unfolds for the reciter in this surah. The name is withheld in the moment of recitation. The narrative does not pause to introduce its protagonist — it moves immediately to the chiefs' argument. Whatever the tradition knows of Ṣāliḥ's biography, and it knows much, the structure of the passage as it flows through Surah Al-Mu'minun moves like a recurring pattern rather than a single portrait. The believer reading in sequence has just witnessed Nuh's rejection. And now, without the anchor of a name, the same argument begins again. This is a literary-structural reading of the Quranic text alongside the classical biographical identification; both can be held at once.

Here is what the reciter is meant to feel: **we have been here before**.

The arguments the chiefs are about to make are not new arguments. They are the same arguments Nuh's people made. The same shape. The same logic. The same dismissal. Allah is doing something deliberate with this repetition — He is showing us that disbelief has a *grammar*. It speaks the same sentences across centuries. It dresses differently, but underneath, the words are identical.

So when you hear what these chiefs say — listen for the echo. They are not just speaking in the generation of Ṣāliḥ and Thamūd. They are speaking in yours.

---

### The Hook

Of all the arguments these chiefs could have made against the prophet — that he had no miracles, that his message was incoherent, that he had no political legitimacy — Allah preserved *this* one.

He eats and drinks like you do.

That's the entire argument.

[PAUSE]

Why would the Quran enshrine, for all eternity, the most banal, most petty, most physically obvious objection any human being could raise against a messenger? The answer is going to take us into something uncomfortable about how the human mind defends itself against truth.

And before we get there, Allah is going to plant a single word in this sentence that explains everything — a word that names the *cause* of the argument before the argument is even made. A word about us, not about them.

Stay with me.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Triple Description — Three Causes Stacked

Before we touch the chiefs' words, look at how Allah describes them.

He doesn't just say "the chiefs of his people said." He gives them three descriptions, stacked one on top of the other, before they are allowed to speak:

1. **alladhīna kafarū** — those who disbelieved
2. **wa kadhdhabū bi-liqāʾi al-ākhirah** — and denied the meeting of the Hereafter
3. **wa atrafnāhum fī al-ḥayāti ad-dunyā** — and to whom We had given the comforts of this worldly life

Three relative clauses. Three things true of these men. And the order of three things in Arabic is rarely accidental.

Let's sit with this structure.

In speech we usually move from general to specific. *He's a man, a working man, a carpenter.* Each clause narrows. Allah is doing something different here. He moves from **the surface symptom** down to **the deepest cause**.

The surface symptom is *kufr* — disbelief. That's what their mouths are about to do. They're going to disbelieve, out loud, in public.

Beneath that lies *takdhīb bi-liqāʾi al-ākhirah* — denial of the meeting with the Hereafter. This is deeper. Disbelief in any individual messenger is downstream of a more foundational rejection: the refusal to imagine a final reckoning. Once you have decided there will be no meeting, the messenger becomes optional.

But Allah goes one more level down.

**Wa atrafnāhum.**

*And We gave them luxury.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened. The first two descriptions are about *them* — what they did, what they refused. The third description is about **what We did to them**.

The pronoun changes. The voice changes. Allah Himself steps into His own diagnosis and says: *the soil this disbelief grew in — We placed it there.*

This is staggering. The chiefs have not yet opened their mouths and Allah has already located the *root* of what they are about to say. Their argument will be about the prophet's body. But the cause of their argument is their own bodies — bodies that have been pampered, fed, eased, comforted to the point that they cannot hear a voice calling them to die to comfort.

The root is **t-r-f** — *taraf* in Arabic refers to the edge, the extremity. *Atrafa* in Form IV means *to cause to live in extremity of ease*. Not just wealth. Not just comfort. The far edge of comfort. The point where life becomes effortless.

And the verb is in the perfect tense, first-person plural. *Atrafnāhum.* **We did this to them.**

Hold that for a moment. Allah is naming luxury as a *test He administered* — and naming the test as the thing they failed.

Al-Ṭabarī presses one step further: when provision is expanded to the point of extremity, what follows is *baṭar* — the arrogance distinctive to those at the very peak of ease — and then open transgression against the Lord. *Baṭar* is not simply ingratitude; it is ingratitude that has tipped over into boundary-crossing, into treating one's own judgment as sovereign. The path Allah is tracing here runs in stages: expanded provision → extremity of ease (*itraf*) → insolent arrogance (*baṭar*) → transgression against the Lord Himself. By the time these chiefs stand to argue against their prophet, they have already traveled that entire road. The argument is not the beginning of their refusal. The comfort is.

What this single relative clause is doing: it is telling us that before you analyze a man's argument against revelation, look at his standard of living. The argument is rarely the first cause. The comfort is.

### Element Two: Mithlukum — A Word Said Twice

Now the chiefs speak. And the first thing they say is:

*Mā hādhā illā basharun **mithlukum***.

This is no more than a human **like you**.

Then they say it again in the next ayah:

*Wa la-in aṭaʿtum basharan **mithlakum***...

And if you obey a human **like yourselves**...

Twice. *Mithl* — likeness, resemblance, parity — appears twice in two ayahs, attached the same way to the same noun: *bashar*, the human creature.

In Arabic, repetition of a phrase across consecutive sentences is not careless. It is the rhetorical move of *taʾkīd* — emphasis through return. When you say something, then say it again, you are not informing — you are **insisting**. You are trying to lodge a phrase into the mind so that it crowds out competing thoughts.

This is what the chiefs are doing. They are not making an argument. They are **chanting a refrain**.

He is one of us. He is one of us. He is one of us.

[PAUSE]

Why does this refrain need to be chanted? Why does it need to be said twice in twenty seconds?

Because **the prophet's claim threatens to remove him from their category** — and they need him back in it. If he is *not* one of them, then his words are not their words, and his words have authority their words don't have. So the entire rhetorical strategy is to drag him back into *us* by force of repetition.

Notice the asymmetry of the absences here. They never say what he claims to be. They never engage what he is calling them to. They only repeat what he is **not** — *not different from us, not different from us, not different from us.* The argument is entirely subtractive. They are erasing the prophet's specialness rather than examining it.

This is what insecurity sounds like in argument form. When you cannot deal with what someone is, you keep repeating what they are *not*.

And there is a second move buried in *mithl*. The Arabic phrase *bashar mithlukum* — "a human like you" — is not what the prophet himself rejects. The prophets in the Quran often say *yes, I am a human like you* (e.g., 18:110). The lie is not in the description. **The lie is in the inference**.

The chiefs take a true statement — he is a human like you — and use it to draw a false conclusion: *therefore his words have no authority.*

The Arabic literally permits this gap. There is nothing in *mithlukum* itself that demands "and therefore reject him." The conclusion is smuggled in by repetition, not by logic.

### Element Three: The Over-Specified Eating and Drinking

Now look at the strangest grammatical feature in this passage.

*Yaʾkulu **mimmā taʾkulūna minhu** wa yashrabu **mimmā tashrabūn**.*

He eats from what you eat **from it** and drinks from what you drink.

The first clause has *minhu* — "from it" — appended at the end. Most translations smooth this over. But in Arabic it is grammatically redundant. *Mimmā taʾkulūn* on its own already means "from what you eat." The added *minhu* — "from it" — is doing extra work.

What is the extra work?

Pointing.

[PAUSE]

It is as if the chief is gesturing at the food while he speaks. *He eats — from what you eat — from it — right there — that food.* The grammatical redundancy is doing the work of a finger pointing at a plate.

This matters because it tells us *what kind of argument this is*. It is not an argument of theology. It is not an argument of philosophy. It is an argument made by **pointing at a body**. By pointing at digestion. By pointing at the most undeniable, most physical, most embodied fact a person can be reduced to: **he needs to eat, and he eats what we eat.**

And the range of what he eats is categorical, not incidental. Al-Muyassar clarifies that the chiefs are not claiming the prophet ate from the same bowl or the same specific plate. They are claiming the same *genus* of food — *min jinsi ṭaʿāmikum* — the same categories of sustenance that every member of their kind depends upon. The argument is a sweeping biological taxonomy: he belongs to our category of creature at the level of nutrition itself, not merely on a single occasion.

Why does the parallel structure go on to drinking?

*Wa yashrabu mimmā tashrabūn.*

Because the chiefs are not making a single observation. They are making an *exhaustive case for biological identity*. He eats. He drinks. He intakes what we intake. He metabolizes what we metabolize. **Therefore he cannot be other than us.**

This is materialism, twenty-five centuries before it had a name. The reduction of the spiritual to the digestive. The argument that says: *if I can show you that this person's body works like every other body, I have proven there is nothing in him beyond body.*

And here is what the Quran wants you to notice. **The argument is not refuted in the verse.** Allah does not say "no, the prophet does not eat." He does not say "no, his body is different." He lets the chiefs' observation stand. The observation is *true*. He eats what you eat. He drinks what you drink.

What is wrong is what the chiefs **conclude** from a true observation.

The shared body does not entail a shared soul. The shared digestion does not entail a shared destiny. The shared mortality does not entail a shared message.

The chiefs see the body and assume there is nothing else to see. The believers see the body and ask: *what came through this body that could not have come through us?*

---

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Three things have surfaced.

The triple cause has shown us that disbelief has a root system — and the deepest root is **comfort**. The doubled *mithl* has shown us that the rhetorical strategy of unbelief is **dragging the messenger back into your own category by repetition**. The over-specified eating and drinking has shown us the **body being held up as evidence against the soul**.

Now let us ask what these features show us not just about a single prophet's audience — but about what happens inside a human heart whenever truth approaches it from the wrong direction.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Body as Alibi

Picture the scene differently. A man in our own time stands up and says something true that no one wants to hear. He speaks about death. He speaks about a final accounting. He speaks about a life lived for something other than ease.

How do we dismiss him?

We don't engage what he said. That's too dangerous — what he said might be true. We do something subtler. We turn the camera onto **his body**. He's tired-looking today. He's overweight. He's gaunt. He's wearing the wrong shoes. He gets sick like everyone else. *His* car payments are late. *He* has anxiety like the rest of us.

And we say — out loud or silently — *who is he to tell me anything?*

This is the move of the chiefs. The reduction of the messenger to the most embodied, most banal version of himself. The use of his **digestion** as evidence that his **vision** is invalid.

Why does this work on us? Why is it so satisfying when we do it?

Because if the messenger is just a body, then the message is just a noise the body made. And once the message is just noise, we are off the hook. We don't have to change.

[PAUSE]

The body as alibi.

Whenever you find yourself fixating on the human flaws of someone calling you to a higher truth — the teacher's marriage, the scholar's appearance, the friend's history, the imam's ordinariness — pause and ask the harder question. Is this a real evaluation, or am I building an alibi? Am I reaching for the most physical fact about this person because I cannot bear to deal with the most spiritual claim they are making?

Notice that Allah does not refute the chiefs by elevating the prophet above bodily need. He does not counter with a miracle or a display of otherworldliness. He lets the true observation stand. What He does instead — structurally, through the very shape of the passage — is allow the observation and the conclusion to sit side by side so that the reader can see the gap between them. The observation: he eats and drinks like you do. The smuggled conclusion: therefore he has nothing to offer you. The gap between those two statements is where the chiefs' disbelief actually lives. And it is the same gap where ours lives today.

### Theme Two: What "Losers" Actually Means

Now look at the word the chiefs choose to close their argument.

*Innakum idhan la-**khāsirūn***. You will surely be losers.

In the Quranic vocabulary, *khasāra* — loss — ordinarily points to a spiritual deficit: the one who has squandered faith, the one who faces the Hereafter bankrupt of good. That is the standard Quranic usage. And if you come to this word as a reciter who knows the Quran, that is what you expect these chiefs to mean.

But they do not mean that.

Al-Ṭabarī, commenting on this very verse, says explicitly: the chiefs were warning their people that they would be defrauded of their *ḥuẓūẓ min al-sharafi wa al-rifʿati fī al-dunyā* — their share of **honor and prestige in this world** — if they followed the prophet. The "loss" the chiefs are warning about is not spiritual poverty. It is **social demotion**. To follow this man, they are saying, is to descend the hierarchy. To lose your position. To forfeit your standing among people who matter.

[PAUSE]

This is a devastating detail.

The people being addressed — the ordinary members of the community, the ones not yet decided — might have been starting to feel something when they heard the prophet speak. Something true. Something that moved in them. And the chiefs knew this. So they did not engage the truth of what he said. They went straight for the social stakes. *You will lose your place. You will be marked. You will fall.*

This is the oldest political tool available to an elite class threatened by a leveling message: frame the cost in terms of status rather than substance.

And notice what this reveals about the chiefs themselves. They use the word *khasāra* — loss — while having already defined the entire ledger in worldly terms. Honor. Rank. The regard of your peers. These are the only assets they consider real. Of course they cannot hear a prophet who is speaking about a meeting with God. They have already decided what the currency of value is — and it is not that.

Al-Muyassar adds one further dimension: the chiefs define the loss as abandoning their gods, their *āliha*. The system of devotion that structured the social order. To follow a monotheist prophet was not merely a personal theological shift; it was an act of institutional defection. The stakes the chiefs name are real to the social world they inhabit, which is precisely what makes this argument so effective on the surface — and so hollow underneath.

The word *khāsirūn* is being weaponized against its own meaning. The chiefs are calling "losers" the people who would, by following the prophet, gain the only thing that actually matters. And they are calling "winners" the people who would preserve their rank while losing everything else. The Quran preserves this moment precisely so the reader can see the inversion for what it is.

### Theme Three: The Grammar of Disbelief

Step back now and look at the surah's design.

This passage is the *second* prophet story in Surah Al-Mu'minun. The first was Nuh. In that story, the chiefs also refused. Also pointed to the prophet's humanity. Also warned their people of loss. Now we are in the second story, and the argument is structurally identical.

The Quran could have shown us a new argument each time. It does not. It shows us the same argument, said again. And this is not because the Quran is running out of material. It is because **the repetition is the point**.

This is what classical scholars call a *sunnah* of human history — a pattern that repeats with the same logic, the same sequence, the same outcome. Allah expands provision for a people. The comfort softens them. The softened elite, threatened by a leveling message, reach for the most reductive argument available — *he is just like us, he eats what we eat* — and use it to protect what they have built. The pattern ran through Thamūd. It ran through the Quraysh. It runs now.

When the Quran makes you feel *we have been here before*, it is not creating nostalgia. It is offering **recognition**. This is what refusal looks like at the moment it happens. This is what the body-as-argument sounds like when spoken aloud. This is the logic, fully displayed, so you can recognize it in yourself.

Because the hard truth this surah is building toward is not about chiefs who died long ago. It is about the reader who might be doing a version of this right now — not in a public arena, but in a private interior. Using someone's ordinariness to avoid engaging their claim. Using a body's needs to dismiss a soul's message. Reaching for *he is just like us* because *what he is saying* is too demanding to face.

The grammar of disbelief has not changed. Only the names have.

## Part 3: The Personal Mirror

### What Do You Do With This?

The passage ends with the chiefs' warning still ringing: *you will be losers*.

And the surah does not immediately comfort you. It does not say "but the believers remained firm." It moves forward with the story, and what follows — the punishment of the people — arrives later. For now, you are sitting with the chiefs' argument, fully displayed, stripped to its essentials.

Here is what a reflective reading asks of you.

**First**: What have you been given in abundance? Not whether you are rich or poor by any external measure — but whether you have reached a point where life is comfortable enough that you do not want it disturbed. The question of *atrafa* is not about a number in an account. It is about whether your ease has become a wall. Al-Ṭabarī's sequence is worth sitting with: provision expands → extremity of ease sets in → arrogance quietly follows → the Lord's voice becomes difficult to hear. Ask yourself honestly where on that path your feet are standing today.

**Second**: Where have you been using the messenger's body to avoid the messenger's words? This does not only apply to prophets. It applies to any person who carries a word of truth toward you — a parent, a teacher, a friend, a line in a book — and you reach for *they are just like me, they are just a person, who are they* — and in that reaching, escape the claim they are actually making.

**Third**: What is your definition of loss? The chiefs defined it as social demotion — forfeiting one's *ḥaẓẓ* of honor and status in this world. If your deepest fear, underneath all the theology, is what people who matter to you will think — if the calculus running beneath your choices is social standing rather than the meeting with God — then you are operating on the chiefs' ledger, not the prophet's. Al-Ṭabarī preserved their words so we could see exactly what that ledger looks like. It looks reasonable. It sounds practical. And it is the thing that keeps a person from taking a single step.

[PAUSE]

The surah is called Al-Mu'minun — The Believers. It began with a portrait of what the believers *are*: the ones who are humble in their prayer, who guard against the unlawful, who are faithful to their trusts. And then it immediately shows you the *counter-pattern* — the chiefs of Nuh's people, the chiefs of Ṣāliḥ's people, those who have been given everything and find in that everything a reason to refuse.

You are reading this surah to understand which pattern is alive in your life. Not in principle. Right now. Today. In the specific place where comfort meets claim.

The body that eats and drinks is not the enemy. It is a trust. The question is whether you have let it become the argument.

*May Allah protect us from the luxury that makes us deaf, and from the pride that makes a prophet's humanity feel like a disqualification.*
