# When Mercy Cannot Break Them

## The Heart That Ends Where Iblis Begins

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first to what Allah says.

> وَلَوْ رَحِمْنَاهُمْ وَكَشَفْنَا مَا بِهِم مِّن ضُرٍّ لَّلَجُّوا فِي طُغْيَانِهِمْ يَعْمَهُونَ
>
> *Wa law raḥimnāhum wa kashafnā mā bihim min ḍurrin lalajjū fī ṭughyānihim yaʿmahūn.*
>
> "And even if We had mercy on them and removed the affliction they suffer, they would still plunge on in their transgression, wandering blindly."

> وَلَقَدْ أَخَذْنَاهُم بِالْعَذَابِ فَمَا اسْتَكَانُوا لِرَبِّهِمْ وَمَا يَتَضَرَّعُونَ
>
> *Wa laqad akhadhnāhum bil-ʿadhābi famā istakānū li-rabbihim wa mā yataḍarraʿūn.*
>
> "We had already seized them with punishment, yet they did not yield to their Lord, nor did they humble themselves in plea."

> حَتَّىٰ إِذَا فَتَحْنَا عَلَيْهِم بَابًا ذَا عَذَابٍ شَدِيدٍ إِذَا هُمْ فِيهِ مُبْلِسُونَ
>
> *Ḥattā idhā fataḥnā ʿalayhim bāban dhā ʿadhābin shadīdin idhā hum fīhi mublisūn.*
>
> "Until, when We open upon them a gate of severe punishment — there, suddenly, they are sunk in utter despair."

### Where We Are Standing

To hear these ayahs, you have to feel where they sit in the surah.

Al-Mu'minun opens with a portrait. *Qad aflaḥa al-mu'minūn* — "the believers have succeeded." Allah lays out who they are. They guard their prayer. They turn from idle talk. They are gentle stewards of their bodies. They keep their trusts. They return again and again to the prayer. The opening is a portrait of a heart that responds — to revelation, to community, to its own conscience, to Allah.

Then the surah moves through the messengers. Nūḥ. Hūd. Mūsā and Hārūn. ʿĪsā and his mother. One after another, sent to peoples whose response is the same: rejection, mockery, accusations of madness.

And by the time we reach ayah 75, the surah has narrowed its lens onto the rejecters of *this* Messenger ﷺ — the people of Makkah. They have heard the Quran. They have watched the man bring it. They have seen drought. They have seen relief. And they keep doing the same thing.

So Allah pulls back the curtain on what is actually happening inside them. He gives us — the listeners across centuries — a diagnosis of the heart that will not be moved by anything He sends.

### The Hook

There is something Allah sends that should soften any heart: **mercy.** Relief. The lifting of a burden you were carrying. The breath after the long-held breath.

And there is something else Allah sends that should soften any heart: **trial.** The shock. The seizure. The thing that wakes you up at night.

These ayahs say: *there is a heart that resists both.* A heart that mercy cannot reach and trial cannot crack.

And the ayahs do not stop there. They tell us — in a single, devastating word at the end — exactly what that heart turns into.

It turns into Iblīs.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: لَّلَجُّوا — "Lalajjū" — *They Would Plunge On*

Before we approach the word, take a moment with the structure it sits inside.

Allah says: *wa law raḥimnāhum* — "and if We had mercy on them." This *law* in Arabic is the conditional of an unreal or supposed situation. It introduces a hypothetical — *suppose We had done this; here is what would have followed.*

In Arabic rhetoric, when the answer to *law* is especially weighty, that answer is preceded by an emphasizing *lām*. You see it here: *la-lajjū*. That little *l* fused to the front of the verb is not decoration. It is Arabic's way of saying: **what comes next is certain. Not probable. Certain.**

Allah is not guessing what they would do if mercy came. He is testifying.

Now the verb itself. *Lajjū* — from the root *l-j-j* (ل ج ج).

The root has a striking semantic field. The classical lexicographers list, under this root: *lujja* — the deepest part of the sea, the place where you can no longer touch bottom. *Lajja* — the persistent, layered roar of waves. And from there, *lajja fī al-amr* — to plunge headlong into a matter, to push into it persistently, to refuse to come up for air.

Notice the picture. Not just stubbornness. Not mere disagreement. *Lajaj* is the action of someone who **wades deeper** when wisdom would say turn back. It is doubling down. It is the argument that gets louder when the evidence gets clearer. It is the diver who, instead of surfacing, swims toward the dark.

So now hear the ayah again: *if We had mercy on them and removed the affliction they suffer, they would plunge deeper — la-lajjū — in their tughyān.*

Stay with this. Allah is describing a specific psychological reality. You might think relief produces gratitude. *Naturally, when the burden lifts, the person looks up.* You might think relief opens a window in the heart. The ayah refuses this assumption.

Relief, for a certain kind of heart, does the opposite. It is taken as confirmation that no reckoning was coming. That the rules don't apply. That the threat was theater. The lifting of pressure becomes permission. The mercy itself becomes evidence — in the wrong direction.

Here's what's remarkable. The Quran has already told us, earlier, what this looks like in slow motion. *Falammā kashafnā ʿanhumu al-ʿadhāba idhā hum yankuthūn* — "but when We removed the punishment from them, at once they broke their word" (43:50). The pattern is the same. Pressure on, they cry to Allah. Pressure off, they plunge.

*Lalajjū* names the engine of that pattern. Not weakness. **Plunge.** A movement of the will that runs *into* defiance, deliberately, the moment the room is given.

[PAUSE]

And notice: the word for "their transgression" is *ṭughyānihim*. *Ṭughyān* is not generic sin. *Ṭ-gh-y* is the root of the flooding river that breaks its banks — water that exceeds its proper limit. *Ṭāghūt* — the false god who exceeds the limits of creaturehood by claiming worship — comes from this same root. *Ṭughyān* is sin understood as the violation of a boundary. The crossing of a line that was placed for your protection.

Now read the phrase whole: *they would plunge into their flood-of-overflowing.* They would dive into the very thing that drowns them. And they would do it because the mercy lifted.

This is not a description of bad people. This is a description of *what mercy does to a heart that has chosen denial.* Without something to break it, mercy itself becomes another thing to consume. A privilege. A confirmation. A wave to ride deeper.

### Element Two: يَعْمَهُونَ — "Yaʿmahūn" — *Wandering Without Sight*

The verb that closes the first ayah is *yaʿmahūn*. From the root *ʿ-m-h* (ع م ه).

Stop for a moment, because Arabic gives us two words that sound almost the same and English collapses into one. *ʿAmā* (ع م ى). *ʿAmah* (ع م ه). Both translated, in most English Qurans, as "blind."

They are not the same word.

*ʿAmā* is the blindness of the eye. The closing of physical sight. The body that cannot see the wall in front of it.

*ʿAmah* is something else entirely. *ʿAmah* is the blindness of the **heart** and of **judgment** — the wandering of someone whose eyes still work but whose inner compass has stopped pointing. It is the person who can see every road but cannot tell which one to take. It is, the classical lexicographers say, *al-taraddud fī al-ḥayra* — the going-back-and-forth of one who is utterly lost.

So when Allah says of these people *yaʿmahūn*, He is not saying they cannot see. They can see. They have eyes. They have evidence. They have the same sky and the same Quran and the same Messenger ﷺ that the believers have.

What they have lost is **the capacity to read what they are seeing.**

Notice something. There is a particular tragedy here that *ʿamā* cannot capture. The blind man knows he cannot see. He reaches for a wall. He asks for a guide. His blindness is itself a kind of awareness.

The one who is *yaʿmah* does not know he is wandering. He thinks he is going somewhere. He has reasons for his direction. He has explanations. He has, often, a great deal of confidence. The compass is broken and he does not know it is broken — because the same broken compass is what he uses to evaluate his direction.

Here's what's remarkable about the placement.

Allah said: *they would plunge in their tughyan.* And then He completes the picture: *yaʿmahūn.*

The plunge is not aimed at anything. It is not the steady direction of someone going somewhere on purpose. It is *plunging-while-wandering*. **Forceful, and lost.** Energetic, and aimless. Loud, and going nowhere.

This is a portrait of certain modern lives more than the listener may want to admit. The intensity is real. The direction is gone. The motion is mistaken for movement.

Notice what Allah is *not* saying. He is not saying these people are weak. He is not saying they have no agency. They have plenty of agency — that's what *lalajjū* established. What is missing is not power. What is missing is *navigation*.

[PAUSE]

So the diagnosis of the first ayah is now complete. When mercy is given, the heart that has chosen denial does two things at once: it doubles down, and it loses the ability to read where it is going. **Plunge plus wander.** Force without compass.

This is what mercy alone, given to a hardened heart, produces.

### Bridge to the Next Verse

If mercy doesn't reach them, what about its opposite? What about the affliction Allah sends — the seizure, the trial, the *adhāb*?

The next ayah answers that.

### Element Three: اسْتَكَانُوا and يَتَضَرَّعُونَ — *Two Doors That Did Not Open*

> *Wa laqad akhadhnāhum bil-ʿadhābi famā istakānū li-rabbihim wa mā yataḍarraʿūn.*

"We had already seized them with punishment, yet they did not yield to their Lord, nor did they humble themselves in plea."

There are two negated verbs here, and both are doors that should have opened.

**The first door: *istakānū*** — from the root either of *kawn* (with the long *ā* of the Form X *istafʿala*), meaning "to seek a state of stillness, to lower oneself into being-with," or from a related root meaning to become subdued and quiet. The classical lexicographers cluster these around a single felt meaning: *to become small before another, to fold inward, to lose the puffed-up posture of resistance.* The Form X — *ista-* — adds the sense of *seeking* or *moving into* that state.

So *istikāna* is the inner motion of someone who, under pressure, **lets the back bend.** The shoulders drop. The chest unpuffs. The "I" becomes smaller in the presence of the One who is great.

This is the natural human response to being seized. You feel it in your body. The muscles soften. The voice quiets. The eyes drop.

Allah says: this did not happen. *Famā istakānū li-rabbihim.* They were seized — and they did not bend.

**The second door: *yataḍarraʿūn*** — from the root *ḍ-r-ʿ* (ض ر ع). This root is striking. The original physical referent in Arabic is *al-ḍarʿ* — the udder of the female animal, where the young come to nurse. From that comes the verb of the young animal pressing in low, opening the mouth, asking. To *taḍarraʿa* is therefore: to take the posture of utter need before the One who feeds. To press in low. To plead like one who has nothing else.

The Form V — *tafaʿʿala* — carries the sense of *taking on the state*, often gradually, often deliberately. *Yataḍarraʿūn* is not an accident. It is a chosen posture of pleading.

Allah pairs the two. *Istikāna* is the inner motion of bending. *Taḍarruʿ* is the outer motion of pleading. Together, they are the complete response of a creature who has been seized by his Creator: bent within, pleading without.

And both doors — Allah testifies — were closed.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what's happening here. Allah is mapping the full repertoire of how a human heart can respond to being broken. There are really only two: yield within, plead without. Bend the back, open the mouth. *Istikāna* and *taḍarruʿ.* That's the full set.

And the ayah negates both. The negation is doubled: *fa-mā istakānū... wa mā yataḍarraʿūn.* No yielding. No pleading. The seizure landed — and nothing moved.

This is a heart that mercy cannot soften and that pain cannot bend.

What is left?

### Element Four: مُبْلِسُونَ — *Mublisūn* — The Word That Is Iblīs

> *Ḥattā idhā fataḥnā ʿalayhim bāban dhā ʿadhābin shadīdin idhā hum fīhi mublisūn.*

"Until, when We open upon them a gate of severe punishment — there, suddenly, they are *mublisūn*."

The word at the very end of the third ayah is *mublisūn*.

Allah has built the whole passage to deposit you here.

Let me show you what this word is. *Mublisūn* — from the root *b-l-s* (ب ل س). Form IV active participle, masculine plural. The root meaning, attested in every classical lexicon, is **utter despair — the cutting-off from any expectation of mercy or relief, accompanied by silence.** The classical Arabic *ablasa* describes the man who has been struck so completely that he has nothing left to say. No protest. No plea. No defense. The mouth is closed not from peace but from **finality.**

Now here's what should make the listener pause.

The word for the great accursed one — the one cast out, the one who refused to bow to Adam, the one who became the enemy of every human being — is the active participle of *almost the same root in the Arabic ear*: **Iblīs**.

The classical philologists differ on the technical etymology. Some say *Iblīs* is a foreign word, a name carried into Arabic. Others — including a strong tradition in the lexicons — say the name *Iblīs* is from this very root *b-l-s*: he is *the one who despaired of the mercy of Allah.* That is, in fact, what Iblīs *did* in the moment of his fall. The Quran tells us in Sūrat al-Ḥijr that when Allah cursed him, *qāla rabbi fa-anẓirnī* — but his interior state, captured in other ayahs, is precisely this: he had cut himself off from any expectation of return.

What is undeniable, regardless of which etymology you take, is what the Arabic *ear* hears. *Mublisūn* and *Iblīs* live in the same sonic and semantic neighborhood. To call someone *mublis* is to place them, in the Arabic imagination, in **the same room** as the fallen one.

Now read the ayah whole: *until We open upon them a gate of severe punishment, suddenly they are — there, in it — mublisūn.*

They end the journey of their denial in the *exact spiritual state* of Iblīs. Cut off from mercy. Silent. Without plea. Without bend. **Done.**

[PAUSE]

And notice what's been opened. *Fataḥnā ʿalayhim bāban* — "We open upon them a gate." Earlier in the surah, and throughout the Quran, *fatḥ* — opening — is the language of mercy. *Innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā.* The gates of provision. The gates of guidance. The gates of forgiveness.

Allah uses the same verb here. He *opens* a gate. But it is *bāban dhā ʿadhābin shadīd* — "a gate of severe punishment."

The same word. Inverted. The capacity in the universe for opening — which exists for mercy — exists also for its opposite. And when a heart has met every other opening with denial, the last opening is the one that ends the conversation.

[PAUSE]

So here is the full architecture of these three ayahs:

- Mercy comes → they plunge deeper, wandering blindly. (*Lalajjū... yaʿmahūn.*)
- Trial comes → they neither bend nor plead. (*Mā istakānū... wa mā yataḍarraʿūn.*)
- The final gate opens → they enter Iblīs's state. (*Mublisūn.*)

Three offerings from Allah. Three refusals from the heart. One terminal condition.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: What Relief Reveals

Here is something most of us have lived through but may not have named.

You go through a hard stretch. A real one. A health scare. A financial squeeze. A relationship in tatters. A work crisis. During it, you are running to Allah. You are praying differently — longer, more honestly, more raw. You are making promises in the prostration. *Yā Allāh, if You bring me out of this, I will...*

Then it lifts. The diagnosis comes back clean. The money arrives. The relationship steadies. The crisis resolves.

And — and this is what the ayah is asking us to look at — *what happens to your relationship with Allah three weeks later?*

For most of us, if we are honest, the prayers shorten. The promises evaporate. The intensity dissolves. The relief becomes a baseline, the baseline becomes invisible, and within a season we are exactly who we were before — except now with the bonus that, somewhere in the back of our mind, we have *evidence* that things tend to work out.

The ayah is showing us something terrifying about ourselves. **Mercy alone does not transform.** Relief alone does not soften. The heart's response to being saved is, by default, to forget the saving.

The believers in the opening of this same surah are described differently. They are people who, *al-ladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalawātihim yuḥāfiẓūn* — guard their prayers. The plural is significant. They guard them across states. In ease and in distress. The point of the believer is not that he prays in his crises. Anyone prays in their crises. The point of the believer is that **the prayer survives the relief.**

[PAUSE]

The kuffār the ayah describes are not unusual humans. They are humans who have stopped resisting the default.

This is the diagnosis the ayah forces. *Lalajjū fī ṭughyānihim* is the natural drift of any heart that does not actively swim against it. Forgetting Allah after relief is gravity. Remembering Him after relief is work.

Which means — and this is the practical edge — **the most important spiritual move of your life may be the one you make in the first month after a problem resolves.**

That is the moment most people go quiet. That is the moment the kuffār of this ayah are characterized by: relief came, and they plunged. The believer's distinguishing act is not the prayer in the crisis. It is the prayer the week the crisis ends. It is the sajda the night the diagnosis comes back clean. It is the dhikr in the car the morning after the bill got paid.

If we cannot do that, we should be afraid of what we are. Because the ayah is not describing aliens. It is describing a default we share, that some hearts have surrendered to fully and others are still — by the grace of Allah — fighting against.

So when the next relief comes — and it will — ask yourself one question: *did I get smaller before Allah, or larger?* If you got larger, the relief did to you what it did to them. If you got smaller, by the grace of Allah, you are being raised among the *mu'minūn* of the surah's opening, not among the *mublisūn* of its end.

### Theme Two: Ending Where Iblīs Begins

The most haunting word in these three ayahs is the last one. *Mublisūn.* They end the journey in the state of Iblīs.

Let me say what is so unsettling about this.

Iblīs's tragedy is not that he sinned. Many beings have sinned. Adam, ʿalayhi al-salām, sinned. The sin is not the catastrophe.

Iblīs's tragedy is that, after the sin, **he did not plead.** He did not say, as Adam said, *rabbanā ẓalamnā anfusanā.* He did not bend. He did not open the second door. He took the position that there was nothing to be asked for and no one worth asking. He cut himself off from mercy *before mercy cut itself off from him.*

That is *iblās*. That is the spiritual gesture that defines him as a category, not just a name.

And the ayah is saying: this gesture is **not unique to him.** It is a state any human heart can arrive at. Not in a single moment of dramatic refusal — but slowly, through the accumulation of every relief that did not soften and every trial that did not bend. The road to *mublisūn* is paved with **a thousand small refusals to be moved.**

[PAUSE]

This should change how we read the daily small moments.

Every time mercy comes and we don't pause to receive it as mercy — every meal eaten without a *bismillāh*, every salary deposited without a *ḥamdullāh*, every healthy day taken as our right — we are practicing the first refusal of the ayah.

Every time pain comes and we don't bend — every illness met with bitterness instead of *istikāna*, every failure met with self-justification instead of *taḍarruʿ* — we are practicing the second.

These are not catastrophic acts. They are micro-rehearsals. They are the daily strength-training of a particular spiritual posture.

And the ayah is saying: keep training that posture, and one day you will arrive — *idhā hum fīhi mublisūn* — at the place where Iblīs lives. Not because Allah dropped you there. Because every step of the journey, you walked.

There is a ḥadīth that captures the inverse. The Prophet ﷺ said: *innamā yarḥamu Allāhu min ʿibādihi al-ruḥamāʾ* — "Allah only has mercy on the merciful among His servants." Mercy received is mercy practiced. The heart that practices receiving from Allah and giving to creation is a heart that **stays open.** The heart that practices refusing to receive and refusing to give is a heart that **trains itself shut.**

[PAUSE]

So the practical question is not "am I a *mublis*?" The practical question is: **what am I practicing today?**

Am I practicing receiving mercy as mercy? When the morning came with breath in my lungs, did I notice? When the food came to my plate, did I name it? When the trial passed, did I remember the One who passed it?

Am I practicing bending? When the rebuke came — from a spouse, a parent, my own conscience, the Quran I just read — did I let the back curve, or did I straighten it in defense?

Am I practicing pleading? When I needed something, did I ask Allah first, or last?

Each of these is a single rep. The ayah is showing us what an entire life of refusing those reps produces. And it is showing us, by its silence, what an entire life of accepting them produces: **the muʾminūn of the surah's opening.** People who succeed.

The whole surah is a circle. *Qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn* at the start. *Idhā hum fīhi mublisūn* near the end. Two destinations. The same human raw material. The difference is what we did, every day of our lives, with the mercy and the trial.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now look at the three ayahs together.

The structure is a perfect inverted ladder.

| Allah sends... | The believer would... | These hearts did... |
|---|---|---|
| Mercy (relief) | Soften, give thanks | Plunge in tughyan, wander blind |
| Trial (seizure) | Bend, plead | Neither bend nor plead |
| Final gate | (Repent before this) | Enter the state of Iblīs |

Each rung of the ladder is a missed door. Mercy was a door. Trial was a door. There was a door of relief and a door of pain, and both opened toward Allah. The ayahs catalogue the passing of both. Then they show the last door — and it does not open *toward* Allah. It opens *away* from any return.

The horror is that the ladder is built of *Allah's gifts.* Mercy *is* a gift. Trial *is* a gift — Allah names it so throughout the Quran. The final gate is the gift withdrawn. Allah's mercy, in essence, kept knocking. The heart kept not answering. Eventually the knocking became something else.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths converge on one insight.

*Lalajjū* told us that mercy alone, ungrateful, deepens the disease.
*Yaʿmahūn* told us that the deepening is also a losing of the way.
*Mā istakānū wa mā yataḍarraʿūn* told us that even pain cannot reach a heart trained shut.
*Mublisūn* told us where the road of those refusals ends.

And the themes told us: this is not about *them*. This is about a default in all of us that must be actively resisted — every time relief comes, every time pain comes, every day of our life.

The ayah is a mirror. Not a verdict on others. **A mirror.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When your last hardship lifted, what did the prayer look like the week after? Did you pray more, the same, or less than you did during the hardship?

2. When you are rebuked — by a spouse, a friend, the Quran, your own conscience — does your back bend, or does it straighten? Which one are you practicing as a habit?

3. There is a difference between *ʿamā* and *ʿamah*. Where in your life do you suspect you are *yaʿmah* — moving with energy in a direction whose actual destination you have not examined?

4. Iblīs's catastrophe was not that he sinned. It was that, after sinning, he did not plead. Where, today, did you sin and not plead?

5. If the surah opened with *qad aflaḥa al-muʾminūn* and closed with this — what is the single habit between those two states that you can build this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

A heart that refuses to soften under mercy and refuses to bend under trial trains itself, one micro-refusal at a time, into the very state of the one who cut himself off from mercy.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't need to fear that you will become a *mublis* in some sudden, theatrical moment. The ayah is more sober than that. It is showing you that the path to that state is gradual, made of ordinary refusals — to pause for *al-ḥamd*, to bend for *al-istighfār*, to plead with *al-duʿā*.

Which means the path *away* from that state is also gradual. Made of ordinary acceptances. Mercy received. Pain met with bending. Need spoken aloud to Allah.

Choose one of those — today, before the sun sets — and do it. The choice is small. The architecture it is building is not.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, do not let our reliefs make us larger before You. Let every mercy You send find us bending; let every trial You send find us pleading. Save us from the road that ends in the state of Iblīs, and write us, by Your grace, among the believers of whose success You testified at the opening of this surah.*

*Āmīn.*
