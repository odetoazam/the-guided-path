# The Two Restrictions: When Reducing Reality Forces You to Reduce the Messenger

## The Ayah

> [ayah:23:38] إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا رَجُلٌ ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا وَمَا نَحْنُ لَهُۥ بِمُؤْمِنِينَ
>
> *In huwa illā rajulun-iftarā ʿala-llāhi kadhibā, wa mā naḥnu lahū bi-muʾminīn.*
>
> "He is nothing but a man who has fabricated a lie against Allah, and we are not, in him, believers."

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to know who is speaking. We have to feel the air in the room.

A messenger has come to a people. The Quran does not even bother to name him here — the architecture is more important than the identity. We know this much: it is after Nuh. A flood has already cleansed an earlier generation. Now a new people, with new wealth, new cities, new arrogance, has risen up. And Allah, in His mercy that does not exhaust itself, sends them yet another warner. A man. From among them. Speaking their language. Eating what they eat. Walking where they walk.

And the chiefs gather to refute him.

Listen to how they build their rejection across the previous verses. It is structured. It is methodical.

First: **"What is this but a man like you?"** *(v. 33)* — they reduce him.

Then: **"If you obey a man like yourselves, you will be losers."** *(v. 34)* — they threaten the audience.

Then: **"Does he promise you that when you die and become dust and bones, you will be brought out?"** *(v. 35)* — they mock the resurrection.

Then: **"Far, far is what you are promised."** *(v. 36)* — they put distance between themselves and the unseen.

Then comes the foundational claim, the philosophical floor of their entire worldview, in verse 37:

> **إِنْ هِيَ إِلَّا حَيَاتُنَا ٱلدُّنْيَا**
> *"It [reality] is nothing but our worldly life."*

And then — *immediately* — verse 38:

> **إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا رَجُلٌ ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا**
> *"He is nothing but a man who has fabricated a lie against Allah."*

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened. The same Arabic construction. Twice. Back to back.

*In hiya illā... In huwa illā...*

Once to reduce **reality** to dunya. Once to reduce **the messenger** to a liar.

That is not coincidence. That is the architecture of disbelief, exposed by the precision of the Quran. The man who has restricted reality to what he can touch is *forced* to restrict the messenger of the unseen to a deceiver. The two restrictions are the same restriction, said twice.

That is the door we are about to walk through. Stay with me.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We will sit with three pieces of this ayah. The opening restriction. The verb that is doing the accusing. And the strange, layered way they end the verse with a word that names this entire surah.

### Element One: *In huwa illā* — The Cage of "Nothing But"

Let me teach you a small grammatical thing first, because it is going to do enormous work in a moment.

In Arabic, there is a way of saying "only" that doesn't use a word that means "only" at all. It is built out of two parts:

- *Mā* (or sometimes *In*) — meaning "not"
- *Illā* — meaning "except"

You negate the entire universe, then exempt one thing.

"There is nothing in the room — except a chair."

It feels emphatic in English, doesn't it? In Arabic, it is even more so. Grammarians call this construction *al-ḥaṣr* — **restriction**. Or *al-qaṣr* — **confinement**. The image is of a wall closing around the meaning. *Nothing — except this. Nothing else allowed in. Nothing else allowed out.*

It is the linguistic shape of a cage.

Now look again at what these chiefs say:

> **إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا رَجُلٌ**
> *"He is nothing — except a man."*

They are not just describing him. They are caging him. They are walling off every other possibility. *He is not a prophet. He is not someone speaking truth. He is not someone touching something real. He is — only — a man.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The cage they build for him in verse 38 is built with the same materials as the cage they built for reality in verse 37.

> v.37: **إِنْ هِيَ إِلَّا حَيَاتُنَا ٱلدُّنْيَا** — *"It is nothing — except our worldly life."*
>
> v.38: **إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا رَجُلٌ** — *"He is nothing — except a man."*

Same wall. Two prisoners.

The same person who has decided that **existence is small** must also decide that **the messenger is small**. Because if existence is *only* dunya, then anyone who claims to speak from outside dunya must be lying. The two restrictions are not two separate decisions. They are one worldview, said twice.

This is something to sit with, because it tells us something about ourselves before it tells us anything about them.

When a person reduces *reality* — when a person decides that the unseen is fairy tale, that revelation is mythology, that the soul is a chemical pattern, that meaning is something the brain manufactures — that person has not simply made a claim about the world. That person has made themselves *unable* to receive any messenger. The cage you build for the cosmos becomes the cage you build for every prophet sent to you.

Here is what is remarkable: Allah is showing us, in two consecutive verses, that the philosophical move ("reality is only dunya") and the prophetic verdict ("he is only a liar") are the same move. The same grammar, even. *In hiya illā. In huwa illā.* The disbeliever does not first dispassionately examine the messenger and then conclude that he is false. The disbeliever has already decided, by his metaphysics, that *no messenger could possibly be true*. The verdict was reached before the messenger arrived.

[PAUSE]

So when we — in our own century, in our own hearts — feel that pull toward "this world is all there is," we should know what we are accepting along with that. We are not just losing the akhirah. We are losing the *capacity* to hear anyone who comes from outside the dunya. We are pre-rejecting every voice from beyond.

This is what verse 37 and verse 38, read together, are showing us. Reduce the world, and you have already convicted the messenger. The cages match.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Iftarā* — The Verb That Carves Things That Are Not

Now to the verb at the heart of the accusation.

> **ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا**
> *"He fabricated against Allah a lie."*

The word for "fabricated" is *iftarā*. It comes from a root **ف-ر-ي** that, in its most ancient lexical sense, means *to cut, to slit, to split open, especially leather*. A *fariyy* in classical usage was a piece of skin being shaped — cut and stitched into a form it did not naturally have.

From that physical image, the Arabs developed the moral image: *iftarā* means **to fabricate** — literally, to *cut and stitch a lie into the shape of truth*. To take material that is not real and *form* it into something that resembles reality.

That is not the same as a simple lie. A simple lie is *kadhib* — saying what is not so. A *fabrication* is constructing a false thing with effort, with craftsmanship, with intent to deceive. The verb assumes a workshop. It assumes the liar sat down and *built* something.

And notice the form of the verb. It is Form VIII — *iftaʿala*. In Arabic morphology, Form VIII often carries an added intensity, a reflexive force, a sense that the doer is *taking the action onto himself*, owning it. *Iftarā* is heavier than the simpler *farā* would have been. It is — he *deliberately, of his own initiative, took it upon himself to* fabricate.

So when these chiefs accuse the messenger of *iftarā*, they are not accusing him of a slip of the tongue. They are accusing him of a sustained, deliberate, crafted deception of cosmic scale.

[PAUSE]

Now look at *what* they accuse him of fabricating against — not "about" — but **against** — *ʿalā* — Allah.

The preposition *ʿalā* with *iftarā* is not neutral. It carries the sense of fabricating *to the harm of*, *at the expense of*, *imposed upon*. The lie is not just being told *concerning* Allah. It is being constructed *against* Him, charging Him with what He did not say.

Stay with the indictment they have just leveled. They are saying: this man has cut and stitched falsehood, taken it upon himself, and pressed it onto the name of Allah Himself. This is not a small accusation. It is the gravest accusation a human being can make against another human being.

And here is what is staggering: **they are doing the very thing they accuse him of**.

What does *iftarā ʿala-llāh* — fabricating against Allah — actually look like? It looks like inventing claims about Allah that He did not authorize. It looks like saying "Allah cannot do this," or "Allah does not care about that," or "Allah has not sent this." It looks like *taking the name of Allah and shaping it to fit your worldview*.

That is precisely what the chiefs have just done across verses 33–37. They have implied Allah would not send a messenger from among themselves. They have implied Allah would not raise the dead. They have implied Allah's promise is far, far away. They have shaped a god to fit dunya.

So when they say *iftarā ʿala-llāh kadhibā* — they are looking in a mirror and accusing the reflection.

[PAUSE]

This is the psychology Allah is laying bare for us. **The accusation a person hurls most violently is often a confession in disguise.** The disbeliever calling the prophet a fabricator is the fabricator. The one who has reduced Allah to a god-shaped silence is accusing the one who delivered Allah's actual speech.

And brothers and sisters — this should make us afraid for ourselves. Because the verb *iftarā* is not reserved for prophets and chiefs of ancient cities. The Quran uses it elsewhere of *anyone* who attributes to Allah what Allah did not say. Anyone who says "Islam teaches X" when Islam does not. Anyone who pronounces a halal upon what Allah forbade, or a haram upon what Allah permitted, in His name. Anyone who weaponizes "Allah wants this from you" in service of something Allah never wanted.

The accusation in this ayah is the disbelievers' indictment of the messenger. But the verb is a warning to all of us. *Be very careful what you stitch onto the name of Allah.* The workshop of *iftirāʾ* has many doors, and most of them open from the inside.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa mā naḥnu lahū bi-muʾminīn* — A Wall of Three Negations

And now the closing line, which is doing more work than it appears to.

> **وَمَا نَحْنُ لَهُۥ بِمُؤْمِنِينَ**
> *"And we are not — in him — believers."*

A simple translation would be "and we don't believe him." But the Arabic is doing something layered. Let me show you.

In Arabic, you can say "we don't believe in him" in many ways. The simplest would be: *lā nuʾminu bihi* — using a verb. Quick. Forward-looking. Active.

But that is not what they say. They say something heavier:

- They begin with **mā** — a negation particle.
- They then bring forward the detached subject pronoun **naḥnu** — *we*. They didn't have to. The verb form would have already told you who. Detaching *naḥnu* and placing it before the predicate puts emphasis: *as for us — we ourselves*.
- They place **lahū** — "in him" — early, before the predicate, attaching the rejection specifically and personally to him.
- And then comes the strange one. They place a **bā** — the preposition *bi-* — before *muʾminīn*. **بِمُؤْمِنِينَ**.

That extra *bā* is what Arabic grammarians call a *bāʾ zāʾidah* — a "redundant" or "augmentative" *bā*. It does not change the literal meaning. It is not required by grammar. It is added for **emphasis**. Specifically, in negation, the redundant *bā* acts as a kind of *intensifier of denial*. It is the difference between "we are not believers" and "we are not, in any way, by any measure, believers in him."

So you have, stacked together: *mā* + *naḥnu* + *lahū* + *bi-muʾminīn*. **Four layers of emphatic rejection in five words.**

This is not a casual "no." This is a wall. A wall that shouts.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the question: *why do they need so much emphasis?*

If they were simply correct — if they had calmly assessed the evidence and found it wanting — would they need to barricade themselves like this? You don't need four layers of negation when you are at peace with your conclusion. You need four layers of negation when something inside you is *trying to get through*, and you are *holding the door shut*.

Listen to the text. They do not say "we have not yet been convinced." They do not say "we will examine his claim." They do not say "we doubt him." They say: **"We will not — by any measure — be those who believe in him."** They are speaking in the future. They are speaking as a tribe. They are speaking *before* deliberation.

This is what the methodology of the heart looks like when it has chosen to stay closed. The disbeliever often is not lacking evidence. The disbeliever is *bracing against* evidence. The walls are not built because nothing is at the door. The walls are built precisely because something is at the door.

[PAUSE]

And now — the most beautiful, most painful detail in this whole ayah.

The word they choose for "believers" is **مُؤْمِنِينَ** — *muʾminīn*.

That is the name of this surah. *Sūrat al-Muʾminūn.*

The surah opens, in verse 1, with: **قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ** — *"Successful indeed are the believers."* That word. *Al-muʾminūn.* The architectural keystone of the entire chapter.

And here, in verse 38, the disbelievers say: *wa mā naḥnu lahū bi-muʾminīn* — *"and we are not, in him, of the* muʾminīn."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah is doing? He has taken the very word that names the people of *falāḥ* — success, flourishing, the soaring opening of the surah — and placed it in the mouths of those who are *refusing* it. They are *renouncing the very identity the surah is celebrating*. They are saying — explicitly, in the language of the surah itself — *we will not be among the people of this surah*.

Surah Al-Muʾminun is, at its heart, a surah about who the believers are and what becomes of those who reject. And right at the center of that surah, the disbelievers stand up and announce: *we are not muʾminīn. Not now. Not ever. Not in any way.*

They are sealing themselves out of their own surah.

This is why Allah's response in the very next verse — verse 39 — is not anger but a cry to Him: **قَالَ رَبِّ ٱنصُرْنِى بِمَا كَذَّبُونِ** — *"He said, 'My Lord, help me — for they have called me a liar.'"* And then in verse 40: **عَمَّا قَلِيلٍ لَّيُصْبِحُنَّ نَـٰدِمِينَ** — *"In a little while they will surely become regretful."* And then in verse 41, the cry seizes them.

A people who chose, with four layers of emphasis, never to be muʾminīn — became, in a little while, regretful. The wall they built against belief became the wall they could not break out of when they wanted to.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So we have three things now. The cage of "nothing but," matching the cage they had built around reality the verse before. The verb of fabrication — the workshop where they accuse him of stitching what they themselves have stitched. And the wall of four-layered negation built using the very word that names this surah, the word for the people of success, refused.

What this construction shows us is the *architecture of denial*. It is not a single act. It is a structure: a metaphysics that rules out the messenger before he speaks, an accusation that is a confession, and a closing of the heart sealed in the very vocabulary of openness.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Cages Are Always Twins

You know that feeling when someone tells you a truth you are not ready for, and instead of dealing with the truth, you find yourself attacking *them*? You go after their tone. Their motive. Their qualifications. Their hidden agenda. You do not engage the claim — you reduce the claimant.

We've all done it. It is one of the oldest moves of the human ego. Psychologists today have a word for one part of this — *ad hominem* fallacy. Move the focus from *what was said* to *who said it*. If I can shrink the speaker, I do not have to engage the speech.

But the Quran is showing us something deeper than that. The Quran is showing us that the move to shrink the messenger almost never comes alone. It comes paired with a move to shrink reality.

Look back at verses 37 and 38. **In hiya illā ḥayātunā d-dunyā. In huwa illā rajulun iftarā.** *Reality is just dunya. He is just a liar.* You cannot, in the long run, hold one of these without the other.

If you sincerely believe that this dunya is all there is, then *anyone* who claims to speak from beyond it must be deluded or deceptive. There is no third possibility for you. You have already, by your metaphysics, ruled out the existence of any honest messenger of the unseen. So when one comes — and they do come, in every generation, in every form Allah chooses — you are not equipped to evaluate him. You can only file him under *fabricator*. The label was waiting before he arrived.

And inversely — if you sincerely cannot bring yourself to believe a particular messenger, you will eventually have to shrink reality to make your refusal coherent. You will have to argue that there is no real unseen, no real accountability, no real soul, no real Day. Because if those things are real and you have refused the one who delivered news of them, you are in trouble. So the metaphysics shrinks to make room for the rejection. *Of course there is no afterlife — because if there were, I would have to listen to him.*

The cages are always twins. Build one, and the other rises automatically beside it.

[PAUSE]

This is not just about ancient chiefs. This is about how we, today, defend ourselves from any voice that disturbs us.

Think about a moment in your own life when someone — a teacher, a friend, a parent, a scholar, a verse you read late at night — said something that hit you in a place you were not ready to be hit. What did you do? Did you sit with the truth? Or did you, very quickly, find a reason to *reduce* the speaker? "She doesn't really understand my situation." "He's just saying that because of his upbringing." "She has an agenda." "He's biased." "She's not credentialed." "He's not from my school of thought."

Notice what is happening underneath. You are not engaging the claim. You are caging the speaker. And almost always — if you watch your own thought carefully — you will notice that, simultaneously, you are also quietly shrinking the reality the speaker described. *That kind of taqwa is for another era. That kind of patience is unrealistic. That kind of dependence on Allah doesn't fit modern life. That standard is impossible today.*

The reality shrinks to fit the rejection. The cages match.

What Allah is teaching us in 23:37–38 is that the diagnostic for this disease is the parallel itself. **Whenever you find yourself reducing a messenger of truth, check immediately what you have just reduced about reality.** They will be twin moves. They will arrive together. And the cure is not to argue better against the messenger. The cure is to widen reality back open. Let dunya be dunya again — *only* a sliver of what is real. Let the unseen be vast again. And then — only then — will you be able to actually hear what was said.

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow? It changes the question I ask myself when a difficult truth from the Quran or Sunnah lands in my chest. The question is not, *"Am I willing to believe this messenger?"* The question, asked first, is: *"Have I been shrinking reality to make myself unable to believe?"* Because the second question is the one I actually have control over. And widening reality is the door that lets the messenger back in.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Accusation as Confession

There is a word for the human pattern Allah is exposing in this ayah, and modern psychology has spent a hundred years circling it. It is called **projection**. The act of taking what is true of yourself and ascribing it to someone else.

The chief who accuses the prophet of *iftirāʾ ʿala-llāh* — of fabricating against Allah — is the one who has *just spent five verses* fabricating against Allah. He has invented a god who would not send messengers. He has invented a god who does not raise the dead. He has invented a god whose promise is "far, far." He has stitched together, in the workshop of his own metaphysics, an Allah who is convenient to disbelief. And then he turns to the actual messenger and says: *you are the fabricator*.

This is one of the most dangerous patterns in the human soul, because it allows the disease to feel like the cure. The man who is lying about Allah feels, with full conviction, that *he is defending the truth about Allah from a liar*. He has the energy of a defender. He has the moral certainty of a defender. He cannot, from the inside, tell that he has become the very thing he is fighting.

And here is what is sobering. The Quran does not present this only as a sin of the kuffār. It presents *iftirāʾ ʿala-llāh* — fabricating against Allah — as a danger that can enter any heart. *(See 6:21, 10:17, 11:18, 29:68, 61:7, where the same construction appears.)* It enters whenever a person attributes to Allah what Allah has not said. Whenever a person uses the name of Allah to legitimize what is theirs — their preference, their political project, their cultural inheritance, their interpretation taken as the only one. The construction in the workshop never stops. The materials are always at hand.

[PAUSE]

So the question for our own hearts becomes uncomfortable. *Where, in my own life, have I been stitching things onto the name of Allah?*

Have I told someone "Islam says you must..." when actually it was my family that said so? Have I declared something *bidʿah* without the scholarly weight to make that declaration? Have I declared something *halal* because I wanted it to be? Have I quoted a verse stripped of its context because the stripped form served what I already believed? Have I told someone "Allah is angry at you" when I had no authority to issue that verdict on Allah's behalf?

These are not rhetorical questions. These are the doors to the same workshop the chiefs of this surah were operating in. They are open to all of us. And the accusation in this ayah — *iftarā ʿala-llāh kadhibā* — is the one Allah's tongue uses about the worst kind of speech. It should make our own tongues quieter. Not silent — but careful. We should know, in our marrow, that to attribute something to Allah is the heaviest thing a tongue can do.

[PAUSE]

And there is a second movement of this theme, and it is one of mercy.

If accusation is so often confession, then *the things people most loudly attack you for* may tell you — quietly — what *they* are afraid of. The man who accuses you of arrogance is often the one shrinking under his own. The man who accuses you of hypocrisy is often hearing, in your presence, what he has not been able to face in himself. The man who accuses the prophet of fabrication is the one who has spent his life fabricating.

This is not given to us so we can throw it back. It is given to us so that, when we are accused, we can have *mercy*. The one accusing us is often more wounded than we are. The verbs they hurl are mirrors. To respond to projection with projection is to enter their workshop. To respond to projection with patience and prayer — *as the messenger does in the very next verse, calling out to Allah* — is to refuse to enter that workshop and to keep one's own hands clean.

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow? It changes how I receive accusation. When someone hurls a heavy verb at me — *liar, fabricator, hypocrite, traitor* — my first move is no longer defense. My first move is examination: *is this true of me, in any portion?* My second move is gentleness: *what wound is this verb actually describing in the one who threw it?* And my third move is what the messenger in verse 39 does — turn the entire matter over to Allah. *Rabbi-nṣurnī bimā kadhabūn.* *My Lord, help me, for they have called me a liar.* No counterattack. No projection back. Just the matter, lifted up, and placed in the only hands that can resolve it.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Step back and see what Allah has placed in front of us.

Verses 33–38 form a six-step staircase of disbelief. The chiefs:
1. Reduce the messenger to a man (v.33)
2. Threaten the audience against following him (v.34)
3. Mock the resurrection (v.35)
4. Distance themselves from the unseen (v.36)
5. Restrict reality to dunya (v.37) — **In hiya illā...**
6. Restrict the messenger to liar (v.38) — **In huwa illā...**

The two restrictions — verses 37 and 38 — are the load-bearing twins. They use the same Arabic construction. They were composed by the same metaphysics. *Once you have decided that reality is small, the messenger of largeness must be a liar.* The grammar of the two verses is the proof.

And then, the moment the wall of four-layered negation is sealed at the end of verse 38 with the word *muʾminīn* — refused — Allah turns the camera. Verse 39: the messenger cries out. Verse 40: a little while. Verse 41: the cry seizes them.

The architecture is brutal in its precision. They build a cage. The cage closes. The cry comes. *In a little while.* Their *bi-muʾminīn* — their emphatic, future-tensed refusal — does not even survive into the next handful of verses.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed us: a restriction on reality and a restriction on the messenger are built from the same materials. They are not two acts. They are one act, said twice.

The themes showed us: human beings reduce messengers because they have already reduced reality, and they accuse messengers of the very fabrication they themselves have committed.

These are not two findings. They are one finding. *The cage you build for the cosmos becomes the cage you build for the prophet, and the verb you hurl at the prophet is the verb you have been performing in private.*

This is the anatomy of disbelief, exposed in twelve words.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I been quietly shrinking reality — making the unseen smaller, the akhirah more abstract, the soul less real — to make some refusal of mine more bearable?

2. When I attack someone's character instead of engaging their claim, what reality am I simultaneously shrinking to keep the attack coherent?

3. Have I, in the last month, attributed to Allah something Allah did not say? In a conversation, in a fatwa-like pronouncement to family, in a tweet, in a private judgment of someone else's heart?

4. When someone accuses me of something heavy, is my first response defense — or examination?

5. The disbelievers said *wa mā naḥnu lahū bi-muʾminīn* — "we are not, in him, of the muʾminīn." If I am honest, what would my own four-layered, emphatic *no* be aimed at right now? And what would it cost me — like it cost them — to keep it sealed?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The man who shrinks reality to dunya has already convicted every messenger of the unseen, and the verb he hurls at the prophet is, in the end, the verb he has been performing all along.

### The Closing Invitation

When a difficult truth from Allah's Book lands in your chest tomorrow — and it will — notice the first move your soul makes. If it reaches for a way to *reduce* the verse or *reduce* the one who delivered it, pause. That move is the verse 38 move. Before you decide what to do with the messenger, ask what you have just done with reality. Widen the world back open. Let the unseen be as large as it actually is. And then — only then — listen again.

That is the door out of the cage of *In huwa illā.*

### A Brief Du'a

Yā Allah, we ask You: do not let us shrink the world to escape Your messengers. Do not let us hurl at others the verbs we are performing in secret. Keep our tongues quiet about You until they speak only what You authorized. And when accusation comes against us, give us the strength of Your prophet's response in the next verse — to lift the matter to You and ask only for Your help. Make us, by Your mercy, of the *muʾminīn* — and never of those who say, with four walls of emphasis, that they will not be.

Āmīn.
