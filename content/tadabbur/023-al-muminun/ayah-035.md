# Dust and Bones: The Anatomy of Mockery as Argument

## Introduction

Listen to this ayah in Arabic:

> أَيَعِدُكُمْ أَنَّكُمْ إِذَا مِتُّمْ وَكُنتُمْ تُرَابًا وَعِظَامًا أَنَّكُم مُّخْرَجُونَ

*A-ya'idukum annakum idhā mittum wa-kuntum turāban wa-'iẓāman annakum mukhrajūn.*

"Does he promise you that when you have died and become dust and bones — that you will be brought forth?"

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open this verse, let's place ourselves where the original listener stood.

A nation has come and gone. The water of Nuh's flood receded generations ago. The earth is full of new people now — people who have built cities, raised herds, accumulated wealth. They have forgotten the deluge. They have forgotten the warning.

Then a man rises among them. Not from a foreign land. Not with strange features. **From themselves.** He speaks their dialect. They knew his father. They watched him grow up. And now he is saying things that cut against the entire architecture of their lives — that the gods their fathers carved are nothing, that the wealth their hands accumulated will be questioned, and most disturbing of all, that this body which they will lay in the ground will one day stand again before its Lord.

The elites of the community gather. The wealthy. The clan-leaders. The men whose authority depends on the world remaining exactly as it is. They walk through the marketplace, and they see the messenger gathering listeners. The poor are listening. The young are listening. **They have to act.**

So they begin a campaign. Not of violence — not yet. Of ridicule. Of laughter. Of the kind of mockery that makes a thing unrespectable so that its truth no longer matters.

And in that campaign, they find their best weapon. Not a counter-argument about God's existence. Not a philosophical dispute about creation. They find one image — one image that, said the right way, in the right tone, with the right pause for the laughter — collapses the messenger's entire claim.

**Dust. And bones.**

### The Hook

There is something staggering about how this verse is constructed. It is recorded in the Quran, but it is not Allah speaking *to* us. It is the disbelievers speaking *about* the messenger — and Allah preserves their exact words. He does not paraphrase their argument. He gives us the rhetoric in its full original cruelty so that we can study it, **so that we can recognize it when we hear it again.**

Because we will hear it again.

This is not an ancient argument. This is the structure of denial itself — and it is being used today, in conversations you have already had, with words slightly updated but sentences structurally identical.

Let's open this verse and ask: how does mockery work as an argument? And what does Allah want us to see by preserving it?

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We will dwell on three things in this verse. The word they chose for "promise." The strange ordering of "dust and bones." And the verb they chose for resurrection — a verb that is not the usual verb.

### Element One: *A-ya'idukum* — The Mocking Use of "Promise"

Stay with the very first word: أَيَعِدُكُمْ — *a-ya'idukum*.

In Arabic, this is built from the root و-ع-د (*wa-'a-da*), which is the root of وَعْد — *wa'd* — promise.

Let me teach you something about this root before we proceed.

In classical Arabic, scholars distinguish carefully between two related words. **وَعْد** *(wa'd)* — promise — is used overwhelmingly for things that are *good*. When Allah says *wa'ada* in the Quran, it is most often a promise of paradise, of victory, of forgiveness, of meeting Him. The word **وَعِيد** *(wa'īd)*, by contrast — from the same root but a different form — is used for *threats* and warnings of punishment. Two cousins, one root, two emotional registers.

A promise leans forward, hopeful. A threat leans forward, fearful.

So when the disbelievers say *a-ya'idukum* — "does he *promise* you" — they are using the word that should imply something good. Something to anticipate. Something to hope for.

[PAUSE]

But listen to what they wrap that "promise" in. *Does he promise you that when you have died and become dust and bones — that you will be brought forth?*

Notice something. **They are using the language of good news to describe what they want you to feel as bad news.** They are not just disagreeing with the messenger. They are weaponizing his vocabulary against him.

This is the architecture of sarcasm.

In your own life you have heard this. A child says they want to be an artist when they grow up. The cynical uncle says: "Oh, *wonderful* — what a *bright* future." The words are praise. The tone is contempt. The rhetorical purpose is to make the listener feel embarrassed for ever having hoped.

The disbelievers are doing exactly this with the messenger's message. They are not saying "He threatens you." They are saying "He *promises* you" — and then loading the word "promise" with so much absurdity that the very idea of hope becomes laughable.

Here's what's remarkable: by recording their words, **Allah is showing us the mechanism of their unbelief**. They cannot attack the truth of resurrection directly, because they have no proof against it. So they attack the *texture* of the message. They attack its *tone*. They wrap it in a sneer so thick that no one is supposed to be able to ask whether the underlying claim might actually be true.

This is the first thing to notice about denial. **It very rarely engages the actual claim.** It changes the emotional climate around the claim, until the claim cannot be heard.

And there is a second layer here. *A-ya'idukum* — "does **HE** promise **YOU**." Notice the pronouns.

They do not name him. They do not address him. They speak about him to the gathered people in the third person, as if he is not present, as if he is a curious specimen being introduced to a crowd. *"Does he promise you..."*

In Arabic rhetoric, this kind of distancing language — referring to someone in their presence as if they were absent, or referring to them with a pronoun rather than a name — is a deliberate move. It strips dignity. It removes their voice from the conversation. It positions the speaker as the one explaining reality to the audience, while the messenger is reduced to a strange object being pointed at.

[PAUSE]

You have seen this. When someone wants to dismiss another person, they often stop using their name. They become *him*. They become *that one*. They become *this guy who's saying...* The pronoun becomes a small wall.

So in the very first word of the verse, *a-ya'idukum*, three things are happening at once. They are co-opting the language of good news to deliver bad feeling. They are establishing the rhetorical posture of mockery. And they are stripping the messenger of his name to make him easier to dismiss.

**One word. Three operations of disbelief.** And the entire verse hasn't even gotten to its argument yet.

### Element Two: *Turāban wa-'iẓāman* — Dust and Bones in That Order

Now to the heart of their argument. *Idhā mittum wa-kuntum turāban wa-'iẓāman* — "when you have died and become dust and bones."

I want to direct your attention to something easy to overlook. The order of those two nouns.

**Dust. Then bones.**

If you stop and think about what actually happens to a body in the ground, the order should be reversed. Flesh decomposes first — relatively quickly. Bones remain for much longer. Bones turn to dust eventually, after generations, sometimes after centuries. The natural sequence of decomposition is: **flesh, then bones, then eventually dust.**

So why do they say "dust and bones" instead of "bones and dust"?

Let me sit with this with you, because the classical commentators noticed this and offered different ways of reading it. Some said *turāb* refers to the body's flesh-component which decays into dust-like matter, and *'iẓām* refers to the skeletal structure that remains — so the listener is pictured as already a body that is *both* dust *and* bones simultaneously, in different parts. Others said the doubling is for rhetorical intensification — they are not describing a sequence, they are stacking horror upon horror, piling up the conditions of impossibility.

Both readings reveal the same rhetorical move: **the disbelievers are not trying to be biologically accurate. They are trying to maximize the apparent absurdity.** They are saying — in essence — "imagine yourself in the most decomposed, most scattered, most *gone* state possible. You're not just bones. You're dust. You're dust *and* bones. You're past the point of recovery. And he says you'll be brought back?"

This is rhetorical compression. It is the deliberate accumulation of dissolution to make recomposition sound impossible.

Now here's what is even more striking. **Allah does not contest their imagery.**

Throughout the Quran, when Allah responds to this same argument elsewhere, He does not say "you misunderstood — your bodies are not really dust." He accepts the imagery. He says *yes*, you will be dust. *Yes*, you will be bones. *Yes*, you will be scattered. *Yes*, you will be wind-blown across the earth. **And you will still be raised.**

The disbelievers think the imagery wins the argument. Allah uses the same imagery and turns it into the proof of His power.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment. The very condition the disbelievers offer as evidence of impossibility — total dissolution — is the condition Allah will use to demonstrate ultimate capacity. *That* is the rhetorical reversal. And we'll come back to this when we get to the themes.

There is also a subtle linguistic structure here worth seeing. The whole conditional is governed by a doubled أَنَّ — *anna*. The verse reads, structurally:

*A-ya'idukum* — does he promise you —
*annakum* — that you —
*idhā mittum wa-kuntum turāban wa-'iẓāman* — when you have died and become dust and bones —
*annakum mukhrajūn* — that you will be brought forth.

Notice that *annakum* appears twice. The structure piles up. The first *anna* opens the promise. Then the conditional intervenes — the long, dragged-out description of dissolution. Then a second *anna* picks the thread back up to deliver the final claim.

In Arabic rhetoric, this kind of doubled construction is heavy. It slows down the sentence. It forces the listener to wait through the description of decomposition before arriving at the verb of resurrection. The disbelievers are using sentence structure itself as part of the argument — making you *feel* the long decay before you hear the claim of return.

And by the time you arrive at the verb — *mukhrajūn* — the rhetorical work has been done. The listener has been walked through dust, through bones, through dissolution, through all the conditions of "no" — and only then is the claim of "yes" delivered. It is meant to land as obvious nonsense.

[PAUSE]

This is the most sophisticated part of their argument. It is not just *what* they say. It is the *order* in which they make you feel it.

### Element Three: *Mukhrajūn* — Not "Resurrected" but "Extracted"

Now to the final word of the verse. *Mukhrajūn* — "brought forth."

Open your attention here.

The Quran has many words for resurrection. The most common is **بَعْث** *(ba'th)* — to be raised, to be sent up. The word *mab'ūthūn* — "those who will be raised" — appears throughout the Quran and is the standard theological term.

But the disbelievers in this verse do not say *mab'ūthūn*. They say **مُخْرَجُونَ** — *mukhrajūn*.

This is from the root خ-ر-ج — to come out, to exit. The form here is Form IV passive participle, meaning "ones who are made to exit" — *brought out, extracted, expelled, pulled forth.*

This is a different image entirely.

*Ba'th* — to be raised — has a vertical, dignified quality. It is the language of a body lifted up, restored, summoned to standing. It is the word Allah uses when He describes resurrection from His own perspective.

*Mukhrajūn* — to be extracted — is horizontal, undignified, almost mechanical. It is the image of being **pulled out of somewhere**. Out of the dust. Out of the earth. The way a tool is pulled out of a drawer. The way a corpse is exhumed from a grave by force.

[PAUSE]

Why did they choose this word?

Because it isolates the *physical* act of resurrection from its meaning. *Ba'th* — being raised — implies purpose. There is a Raiser. There is a destination. There is a reckoning. *Mukhraj* — being extracted — strips all that away. It pictures only the *mechanism*, the act of being yanked out, separated from your absence and re-presented in space. **Without the meaning, the mechanism sounds ridiculous.**

This is a key technique of mockery. Reduce the sacred to the merely procedural. Strip the meaning. Describe only the apparent absurdity of the physical action.

You have seen this. Someone describes prayer as "bobbing up and down on a carpet." Someone describes fasting as "not eating for no reason." Someone describes pilgrimage as "walking in circles around a stone building." In every case, the rhetorical move is the same: **describe the body, hide the soul, and let the body sound foolish.**

The disbelievers in this verse are doing exactly that. They have reduced *ba'th* — the meaningful event of being summoned before your Lord — to *mukhraj* — the mechanical event of being yanked out of dust. They have stripped the verb of its purpose, and now the purpose-less verb sounds absurd.

Notice what Allah is teaching us by recording this. **He is showing us how language is used as a weapon against the unseen.** The choice of one verb instead of another can determine whether a truth sounds dignified or ridiculous. And the disbelievers are virtuoso practitioners of this.

But there is one more thing about *mukhrajūn* worth seeing. It is **passive**. *Ones who are extracted.* By whom? The verb implies an agent. Someone is doing the extracting.

The disbelievers, by using a passive form without naming the Extractor, leave the resurrection orphaned. There is no actor in their sentence. There is only an event — *being pulled out of dust* — happening, somehow, by no one. And of course, when no Agent is named, the event sounds preposterous. *Who* would do this? *Why* would this happen? The passive without the Agent makes the resurrection sound like a freak occurrence, an accident of nature.

But the moment you put Allah's name back into the sentence — the moment you hear *Allah will extract you from the dust* — the entire claim shifts from absurdity to power.

[PAUSE]

The disbelievers' argument depends on **keeping Allah's name out of the sentence.** Once He is named, the math changes completely. The One who made you from dust the first time is not strained to do it again.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So look at what we have seen. *A-ya'idukum* — using the language of promise to deliver mockery, and refusing to name the messenger. *Turāban wa-'iẓāman* — piling up the conditions of dissolution, in an order designed for rhetorical effect rather than biological accuracy. *Mukhrajūn* — choosing the verb of mechanical extraction instead of dignified resurrection, and stripping out the Agent who would give the verb its meaning.

Three linguistic moves. Three operations of denial. And what they all share is this: **they all attack the texture of the message rather than the truth of the message.** None of them is actually an argument that resurrection is impossible. All of them are techniques to make resurrection *feel* impossible.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the structure of denial itself. About what is happening inside a heart when it speaks like this. And about what is being asked of you when you hear it.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Argument from Imaginative Limitation

Let me start with a story you already know.

You are sitting with a friend. They are explaining something they cannot accept. Maybe it is a religious claim. Maybe it is a scientific result. Maybe it is the testimony of someone who has experienced something they have not. And at some point in the conversation, they fold their arms, lean back, and say:

*"I just can't see how that could be true."*

Stay with that sentence for a moment.

What is actually being said?

On the surface, it sounds like a claim about reality — *that thing cannot be true.* But underneath, it is a claim about the speaker — *I cannot picture it.* These are not the same claim. **The limit of what I can imagine is not the limit of what is real.** And yet, in conversation after conversation, in century after century, this confusion is presented as if it were proof.

This is the core engine of the disbelievers' argument in our verse. *We've died. We've become dust. We've become bones. We are scattered. We are gone.* And then — and this is the only actual reasoning — *therefore* we cannot be brought back.

The "therefore" is doing all the work. And the "therefore" is unjustified.

Because the question is not *can I picture it?* The question is *can the One who made me the first time do it a second time?*

[PAUSE]

The Quran returns to this argument again and again because it is the universal argument. It is not specific to a tribe or an era. It is the move the human heart makes whenever it does not want to face accountability. And the pattern is always the same. **The deniers describe their condition (we are dust), and then mistake their inability to envision a reversal for evidence that no reversal is possible.**

Allah's response, in every instance where He addresses this argument, has the same shape. He turns the deniers' description back into a question:

> *Has there not come upon man a period of time when he was not even a thing worth mentioning?*

> *Then We made the sperm-drop into a clinging clot, and the clot into a lump, and the lump into bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh.*

> *Does man think that We will not assemble his bones? Yes — We are able to put together in perfect order even his very fingertips.*

The argument is always: **you came from less than this.** The dust you describe in your argument against resurrection is the *higher* state. The fluid drop you came from was beneath dust. The non-existence before that was beneath the drop. **Your present existence is the strange thing. Your future re-existence is just the second occurrence of an event that already happened.**

And here is where the modern bridge becomes urgent.

Today, the same argument is everywhere. It does not say *dust*. It says *neurons.* It says *molecules.* It says *atoms.* It says *the brain is just chemistry.* And it draws the same conclusion the deniers in our verse drew: *if I am only this, then I cannot be more than this. If my consciousness is only firing cells, then when the cells stop firing, I am simply gone.*

But notice — this is the same move. **The materialist who points at neurons and says "you cannot be more than this" is rhetorically identical to the disbeliever who pointed at dust and said "you cannot be more than this."** The substance has changed. The argument has not.

And the response has not changed either. *Who made the neurons? Who organized the cells? Who is the Author of the chemistry?* The argument from material reduction works only if you delete the Maker from the sentence. Once He is named, the argument collapses.

This is why the disbelievers in our verse used the passive *mukhrajūn* without an agent. **They had to.** Their argument cannot survive the explicit naming of Allah. It depends, structurally, on keeping Him out of the sentence.

#### The Application

Here is the practical thing this should change in you.

When you encounter denial — in others or in yourself — train yourself to listen for the *missing subject*. Train yourself to hear the passive constructions. Train yourself to notice when a claim about reality is dressed up as a claim about evidence, but is actually a claim about the speaker's imagination.

And when you find yourself saying — even silently, even in moments of doubt — *I just cannot see how that could be* — pause. Recognize that sentence for what it is. **It is a sentence about you, not about reality.** It is the same sentence the deniers in this verse spoke. It is the universal first language of unbelief.

The question is never *can I imagine this?* The question is *what does the One who made me say about this?*

### Theme Two: When Mockery Becomes the Argument

Now let's turn to a deeper question. Why does Allah preserve the deniers' words at all?

The Quran does not have to record what the disbelievers said. It could simply describe them — *they rejected the messenger* — and move on. But again and again, Allah preserves their actual rhetoric. He gives us their phrasing. He shows us how they spoke. **Why?**

Because mockery as argument is a recurring temptation, not just for the disbelievers, but for anyone whose position cannot survive sober examination. And Allah wants us to see the technique so we can recognize it — and so we can refuse to use it ourselves.

Look at what is happening in this verse. The disbelievers do not say *resurrection is false because of X.* They do not present a counter-claim about what happens after death. They do not propose an alternative theology. They simply ask a question — *does he promise you...?* — and they pose it in a way designed to make the answer feel like laughter.

This is not engagement. This is performance.

[PAUSE]

There is a hadith principle in Islamic ethics: *al-istihzā' ḥarām* — mockery is forbidden. Not just because it is unkind. But because **mockery does the work of disbelief without paying the cost of disbelief.** The mocker does not have to construct an argument. The mocker does not have to defend a position. The mocker does not have to face the seriousness of the claim. They simply have to make others *laugh*. And once others laugh, the claim is socially defeated, even if intellectually undefeated.

This is one of the great spiritual dangers of our age. Mockery has become a substitute for thought. On every screen, on every platform, ideas are not refuted — they are mocked. Positions are not engaged — they are made fun of. And the mocker's audience experiences a feeling of victory without ever having to win an argument.

Allah preserves the disbelievers' words to show us this is ancient. Not modern. The technique was perfected before Islam came. And He wants us to see how it works so that:

**One** — when it is used against the truth, we are not deceived by it. The fact that something is being mocked is not evidence against it. Often it is evidence *for* it — because mockery is the rhetorical weapon used when actual arguments are unavailable.

**Two** — when we are tempted to use it ourselves, we recognize what we are reaching for. There is a particular pleasure in mocking what we cannot or will not engage with. That pleasure is a warning sign. It tells you that your relationship to the thing has become defensive rather than genuine. **You are mocking precisely because you do not want to encounter.**

#### The Application

What should this change in your life?

Two things.

First — develop an immunity to the social pressure of mockery. When something true is being laughed at, the easiest move is to laugh along. The laughter feels like belonging. Refusing to laugh feels like exposure. But the deniers in this verse won converts not by argument but by social atmosphere. They made it socially expensive to take the messenger seriously. The believers around them had to choose: be laughed at, or laugh along. **Many people in many ages have chosen the laughter side and lost their souls in the process.**

When you are in a room — physical or digital — where something true is being mocked, the test of your faith is not whether you defend it. Sometimes you cannot, sometimes you should not, sometimes the moment is wrong. The test is whether you *laugh*. Whether you let your face perform the agreement that your heart does not give. Whether you trade your spine for a place in the room.

Second — examine your own mockery. What in your life are you reducing to caricature so that you do not have to take it seriously? What practices are you describing as "bobbing on carpets" so you do not have to face what they actually are? What people are you turning into pronouns — *that one, this guy, those people* — so you do not have to encounter their actual arguments?

The disbelievers in our verse used every one of these techniques. And Allah preserved their words so that we could see ourselves in them, when we are tempted to do the same.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have walked through.

We saw three linguistic moves: the mocking use of "promise," the rhetorical piling-up of "dust and bones," and the choice of "extracted" instead of "raised." Each of them, separately, looked like a stylistic choice. Together, they form a complete rhetorical machine — a machine designed not to refute resurrection, but to make resurrection feel ridiculous.

We saw two themes: the argument from imaginative limitation, where deniers mistake what they cannot picture for what cannot be; and mockery as a substitute for argument, where the rhetorical performance of contempt does the work that an actual counter-claim could not do.

And here is how the linguistic and the thematic come together.

**The deniers' technique is to keep Allah out of the sentence.** Linguistically — through passive verbs, through unnamed agents, through the absence of the Maker. Thematically — through arguments that work only if no one asks *who made me the first time?*

The believer's discipline is the reverse. **Always restore Allah to the sentence.** When someone says *I cannot imagine being raised from dust*, restore the Maker — *who made you from dust the first time?* When someone says *I am only neurons*, restore the Author — *who organized the neurons?* When someone says *therefore it cannot happen*, restore the Power — *therefore He is unable?*

The whole battle is over a single question: **does the sentence about reality contain the One who made reality, or not?**

### Questions to Carry

1. When you encounter a claim you cannot accept, do you ask whether it is *false*, or do you only check whether you can *picture it*? What is the difference, and where in your life have you been confusing the two?

2. In the rooms you spend time in — physical or digital — what is being mocked? When something true is laughed at, what does your face do?

3. Where in your own speech do you refer to people as pronouns instead of names? Who are you keeping at the distance of "that one" or "those people" so that you do not have to encounter them as fully as you encounter yourself?

4. When you describe sacred things — your own practices, others' practices — do you describe the body or the soul? The mechanism or the meaning? When was the last time you let a sacred act be sacred in your own description of it?

5. If you were to write down your most stubborn doubt, and then circle every passive construction in it, every unnamed agent, every place where the Maker is missing from the sentence — what would the doubt sound like once He was put back in?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The deniers' argument depends on keeping the Maker out of the sentence; the moment His name is restored, the argument collapses, and the dust they offered as proof of impossibility becomes proof of His power.

### The Closing Invitation

You will hear this argument again. Probably this week. Probably from someone you love, or in some quiet moment from your own heart.

When you hear it, do not be impressed by its tone. Do not be intimidated by the laughter that surrounds it. Do not mistake the polish of its phrasing for the strength of its case.

Listen for what is missing. Listen for the unnamed Subject. Listen for the passive verb without an Agent. Listen for the description of dust without the One who made dust.

And then — gently, in your own heart first, and out loud only when the moment serves — restore Him to the sentence.

That is the whole work. **Restore Him to the sentence.**
