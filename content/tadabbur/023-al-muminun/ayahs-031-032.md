# Another Generation, The Same Voice
## The Rhythm of Divine Mercy After the Flood

---

## Introduction

Listen to these two short ayahs:

> **ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَا مِنۢ بَعْدِهِمْ قَرْنًا ءَاخَرِينَ ۝ فَأَرْسَلْنَا فِيهِمْ رَسُولًۭا مِّنْهُمْ أَنِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مَا لَكُم مِّنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرُهُۥٓ ۚ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ**

> *Thumma anshaʾnā min baʿdihim qarnan ākharīn. Fa-arsalnā fīhim rasūlan minhum ani uʿbudū Allāha mā lakum min ilāhin ghayruhu — afalā tattaqūn.*

> "Then We brought forth, after them, another generation. And We sent among them a messenger from themselves: 'Worship Allah — you have no god other than Him. Will you not have taqwā?'"

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

To stand here properly, you have to know what just happened in this surah.

The previous ayahs took us through Nuh — peace be upon him — and the Flood. Nine hundred and fifty years of calling. A people who refused. An ark. Water above, water below. And then the verdict: *"So We drowned them — yes, all of them — those who did wrong."*

The earth was scrubbed.

Imagine the silence after that water receded. The strange, washed quiet of a planet that has just lost almost everyone on it. A handful of people on a mountainside, looking down at a world they will have to rebuild from nothing.

You would expect the next sentence in the Qur'an to be a celebration — a triumphant declaration, a moral conclusion, a thunderous *thus perished the wrongdoers*.

But that is not what comes.

What comes is a sentence so quiet, so matter-of-fact, that you might miss what it is doing.

*"Then We brought forth, after them, another generation."*

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us in our tracks. The Flood was meant to be a reset — a punishment so total it should have been the last word. And yet, before the dust has settled, before we have even finished mourning the ones who drowned, Allah is already producing the next generation. And He is already sending them a messenger.

The same call. The same words almost verbatim. The same mercy, again, given to people who had nothing to do with earning it.

Why?

Why does Allah, after such a final-seeming verdict, immediately reopen the door He just closed?

What is He showing us about Himself in this rhythm — this unbreakable pattern of *judgment, then mercy, then judgment, then mercy* — that runs from before Nuh and continues, in your own life, today?

That is what these two ayahs are. They are not a transition between stories. They are a window into how Allah engages a species that keeps forgetting Him.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *Anshaʾnā*: The Verb of Bringing Forth

Before we look at the word, let me give you a small grammatical tool.

In Arabic, when you want to say "I made something," you can choose from many verbs. *Khalaqa* (to create from nothing). *Jaʿala* (to render, to fashion into a state). *Ṣawwara* (to give form). *Banā* (to build).

And then there is *anshaʾa* — Form IV of the root *n-sh-ʾ*.

In everyday Arabic, this verb is used for things that *grow upward*. A young plant pushing through soil. A child rising into adulthood. A building being raised. The word carries the sense of something being *brought into existence and then caused to develop*. Not just creation. *Creation that unfolds.*

Now keep that meaning in your mind. We are going to need it twice.

[PAUSE]

Look at our ayah:

**ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَا مِنۢ بَعْدِهِمْ قَرْنًا ءَاخَرِينَ**
*"Then We brought forth, after them, another generation."*

Allah does not say *khalaqnā* — We created. He does not say *jaʿalnā* — We rendered. He chose *anshaʾnā* — We caused them to rise up.

Notice something. Earlier in this very surah — only seventeen ayahs before this — Allah used the same word about *you*.

In ayah 14, after walking through your formation in the womb — clay, then a sperm-drop, then a clinging clot, then a chewed lump, then bones, then bones clothed with flesh — Allah says:

**ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَاهُ خَلْقًا ءَاخَرَ**
*"Then We brought him forth as another creation."*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The same verb that brings *you* — one human being — into existence as an unfolding new creation, is the verb that brings an *entire generation* into existence after the Flood.

What the verb invites us to see is this: **a generation is not a statistic. A generation is a single act of divine attention.** What He did inside your mother's body — gentle, gradual, deliberate, attentive — is the same kind of act He does when He decides to give the earth another chance with another people. The same patience. The same wanting-it-to-grow.

He does not *manufacture* a replacement civilization. He *raises one up*, the way the root itself describes: upward, unfolding, stage by stage.

Even after the Flood — *especially* after the Flood — Allah's engagement with humanity bears this same character.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Qarnan Ākharīn*: A Generation, Other Ones

Now look at the object of that verb: **قَرْنًا ءَاخَرِينَ** — *qarnan ākharīn* — "another generation."

There are two words here, and both carry weight.

**Qarn** is a fascinating word. Its root, *q-r-n*, literally means *to join, to pair, to link*. From that root we get the *horn* of an animal (the thing joined to its head), the *partner* in a pairing (*qarīn*), and the *century* or *generation* (the cohort whose lives are joined in time).

A *qarn* in classical usage is not just a clump of people who happen to be alive at the same moment. It is a *cohort whose lives are bound together* by a shared era — bound by the same crises, the same questions, the same temptations, the same opportunity to turn toward Allah or away from Him.

The classical commentators are specific about which *qarn* this was. Al-Muyassar identifies them plainly: *hum qawm ʿĀd* — "they are the people of ʿĀd." A mighty civilization that arose after the Flood and became the next great power on earth, the successors of Nuh's generation. The timing of *thumma* — "then" — in this ayah signals not only sequence but succession: generation following generation in the same unbroken divine economy.

Think about your own *qarn*. The people on the planet right now, between roughly your parents' age and your children's age — you share something with them that you do not share with the people of Nuh or the people of Musa. You share the *texture* of this moment. The phones. The news cycle. The wars you have lived through. The diseases. The drift.

A *qarn* is a group who are *joined* — by time, by trial, by chance to respond.

[PAUSE]

Now the second word: **ākharīn** — "another ones." This is a plural form (specifically, the masculine plural in the accusative).

In Arabic you can say *ākhar* (another, singular) or *ākharīn* (others, plural). Allah did not say *qarnan ākhar* — *another generation*, in the singular agreement. He said *qarnan ākharīn* — using a plural agreement that emphasizes their *plurality* and their *otherness*.

Why does this matter? Because it carries a quiet but stunning point: **these are not the same people resurrected. These are not Nuh's people given a second chance. These are different ones — new individuals — who inherit the question that the previous generation failed to answer.**

The question Allah asked Nuh's people — *will you worship Me alone?* — does not die when the Flood comes. It is *passed forward*. To another cohort. Then another. And another. Down through time. The question Nuh's generation refused to answer is the same question being asked of *your* generation. And of you, personally, this morning.

[PAUSE]

The Flood did not end the question.

The Flood only ended *one cohort's chance to answer it*.

### Element Three — *Rasūlan Minhum*: A Messenger From Themselves

Now look at the second ayah:

**فَأَرْسَلْنَا فِيهِمْ رَسُولًۭا مِّنْهُمْ**
*"And We sent among them a messenger from themselves."*

Two prepositions are doing massive work here, and we have to slow down to see them.

**فِيهِمْ** — *fīhim* — "*in* them, *among* them, *within* them." Not *to* them (*ilayhim*). Not *upon* them (*ʿalayhim*). The messenger is not lobbed at them from a distance. The messenger is *placed inside* the cohort, the way you might place a candle inside a lantern. He lives *within* their life.

And then: **مِّنْهُمْ** — *minhum* — "*from* them." From their own stock. From their own families. Eating their food. Speaking their dialect. Wearing their clothes. Walking their roads. Knowing their grandmothers' names.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. Why both? Why say "*among* them" *and* "*from* them"?

Because Allah is closing two doors of escape that the heart loves to use.

The first door: *"He doesn't understand my world."* — closed by *fīhim*. The messenger lives *inside* your world. He knows what it smells like. He knows what it feels like to be tired in it. He has the same neighbors you have.

The second door: *"He's not really one of us."* — closed by *minhum*. The messenger is not a foreign import. Not an angel taking a human shape for a press conference. Not a celebrity flown in from elsewhere. He is *one of you*. His face is a face you have seen in your own family.

[PAUSE]

This is the relatable mercy of Allah. He could have sent angels in glory. He could have written His message in fire across the sky every morning. Instead, He keeps choosing to send *a man* — placed inside the very community that needs him, made of the same material, vulnerable to the same hungers, mortal in the same way.

The messenger is not above his people. He is *of* his people, *among* his people, *for* his people.

### Element Four — The Identified Messenger, the Withheld Name

Now here is something the surah does that should arrest us.

The classical commentators have a clear answer about who this messenger is. Al-Muyassar states it directly: *huwa Hūd ʿalayhi al-salām* — "he is Hūd, peace be upon him," sent to ʿĀd, the civilization that inherited the earth from Nuh's drowned generation. Ibn Kathīr agrees that this is the generation of ʿĀd — "because they were the successors of the people of Nuh" — while acknowledging a secondary scholarly reading that the generation being described is Thamūd, given the nature of the punishment that later overtook them. Whether one reads ʿĀd or Thamūd, both peoples came with messengers named and known in the Islamic tradition: Hūd and Ṣāliḥ, respectively. The classical record has placed a face and a name on this moment.

And yet — the surah does not name him here.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The surah is not hiding Hūd from you. It is doing something else.

If you go through the rest of Surah Al-Muʾminūn, you will see a pattern in what gets named and what does not. Nuh is named (23:23). Mūsā and Hārūn are named (23:45). ʿĪsā is named, with his mother (23:50). But here — in this hinge passage between prophetic cycles — the messenger appears only as "a messenger from themselves." The surah is not making a historical claim about the identity of the prophet. It is making a structural claim about the nature of prophethood itself.

The surah is showing you a *pattern*, not a *roster*.

It is saying: *the name will change. The face will change. The dialect will change. The century will change. The single, unchanging thing — across all of these messengers — is the message itself.*

Look at what this withheld-name moment says:

**أَنِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مَا لَكُم مِّنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرُهُۥٓ ۚ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ**
*"Worship Allah — you have no god other than Him. Will you not have taqwā?"*

Now read this against what Nuh said only nine ayahs earlier (23:23):

**يَٰقَوْمِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مَا لَكُم مِّنْ إِلَٰهٍ غَيْرُهُۥٓ ۚ أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ**
*"O my people, worship Allah — you have no god other than Him. Will you not have taqwā?"*

[PAUSE — major pause]

It is the same sentence.

Almost word for word.

A different century. A different cohort. A different messenger — Hūd or whoever stands in this position in the chain. A different climate, language, culture, civilizational stage.

And the message is *exactly the same*.

This is what the surah's literary withholding accomplishes in this passage. Allah is teaching you to stop looking for the *name* and start hearing the *voice*. Behind every prophet there is one Voice making one call, in one set of words, across all of time.

The messengers are many. The message is one.

---

### Bridge

What the linguistic journey has shown us is this: the verb of unfolding creation (*anshaʾnā*) brings forth a *joined cohort* (*qarn*) of *new individuals* (*ākharīn*), and into the heart of that cohort He places a *brother of theirs* (*rasūlan minhum*), who delivers — *unchanged across centuries* — the same one sentence.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The Rhythm Under Your Life

Begin where you live.

Most of us, when we sin or fall, expect the door to slam shut. We expect that this slip — *this one* — was the one that finally exhausted the patience of the heavens. We carry around, often without knowing it, a tiny private theology of *one strike and you're out*.

And so when we drift — when we miss prayer for a week, when we fall into the same haram we promised we'd leave, when we look up and realize we have been spiritually unconscious for months — we feel as if something has been *closed* against us. As if we have been put on the wrong side of a door that no longer opens.

[PAUSE]

Now read this ayah again with that feeling in your chest.

The Flood was the closest thing in Quranic narrative to a door slammed shut. The water rose. The disbelievers drowned. *All of them.* That is the verdict of 23:27-29.

And then — *immediately* — what does Allah do?

He plants a new garden. He raises up another cohort. He sends another messenger. He repeats the same sentence.

[PAUSE]

The rhythm Allah is showing you is not *one strike and you're out*. The rhythm Allah is showing you is *judgment, then mercy. Closure, then opening. Drowning, then planting.* And then again. And then again.

The Flood is not the end of His engagement with humanity. The Flood is *one beat* in a long, patient music that keeps going.

And here is the personal application. Whatever flood you have been through — the relationship that ended, the years that felt wasted, the chapter that closed in humiliation or grief — Allah's move is the same one He makes after every flood in the Quranic record. He plants. He raises. He sends. He opens the door again.

[PAUSE]

There is a sobering counterpoint alongside the mercy. Ibn Kathīr tells us that when the messenger came to ʿĀd with this very message — *worship Allah alone, you have no god but Him* — they belied him, opposed him, and refused to follow him. The mercy was extended. The call was made. And it was rejected. The rhythm, in other words, does not only include divine sending — it includes human refusing. And it includes, eventually, a second judgment following the second mercy.

What does this mean for you? It means the reopening of the door is not a guarantee of your outcome. It is a guarantee of *another chance*. The door opens again. What you do at the open door is yours.

### Theme Two — The Mercy Hidden in *Minhum*

Go back to that phrase: *rasūlan minhum* — "a messenger from themselves."

The people of ʿĀd, in Islamic tradition, were a powerful civilization. They built, they amassed, they grew proud. And to this proud, powerful generation, Allah did not send an angel. Did not send a supernatural intervention. Did not write the message in clouds above their cities. He sent one of them. A man with ʿĀd hands and an ʿĀd accent and an ʿĀd face — someone who had grown up knowing the same landscape, eating the same food, aging in the same sun.

[PAUSE]

Think about what this costs the messenger.

To be *from* your people and called to *oppose* your people is not a privilege. It is a kind of loneliness that those with comfortable faith rarely reckon with. The messenger shares blood with the people who will reject him. He watches his own community walk toward destruction by refusing what he is offering.

And yet Allah keeps choosing this form. *Rasūlan minhum.* A messenger from among them. Every time.

Why?

Because the argument Allah will not accept from you on the Last Day is *I didn't have anyone who understood my world*. He sent someone who understood it from the inside. Who could not be dismissed as an outsider. Who carried the language of that *qarn* in his very body.

The mercy is hidden precisely in the form that makes refusal most inexcusable.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this means for you personally. When you encounter the message of Islam — through a friend, through an ayah, through a moment of clarity at 3am when everything else has gone quiet — you cannot say *that message wasn't meant for me*. Because it was not sent by a stranger who does not know your world. It was sent by the same God who chose, every single time, to send someone *from inside* the very community that needed it.

The message found a native voice in your language and your era. The question is only whether you will answer it.

### Theme Three — What the Taqwā Question Is Really Asking

The ayah ends with a question. Not a statement. Not a command. A question:

**أَفَلَا تَتَّقُونَ**
*"Will you not have taqwā?"*

Al-Ṭabarī unpacks this question with precision. He explains that *tattaqūn* in this context means: *will you not fear the punishment of Allah for worshipping something other than Him, when He alone is the God you have?* The taqwā being invoked is not a general spiritual sensitivity or a diffuse sense of reverence. It is specifically the fear of what happens when the One who made you is replaced in your heart by something that did not make you.

Al-Ṭabarī also draws out the precise force of the declaration that precedes the question. *Mā lakum min ilāhin ghayruhu* — he explains this as: there is no object of worship that is *fit* (*yaṣluḥ*) to be worshipped other than Him. The word *yaṣluḥ* carries the sense of something being *suited* for a role, *appropriate* to a function. Worshipping other than Allah is not only wrong in the legal sense — it is categorically unfit, like demanding of a created thing what only the Creator can bear. The taqwā question that follows lands on top of this: *having seen that this is the structure of reality, how could you possibly look elsewhere?*

[PAUSE]

This is a finely aimed question.

It is not asking: *do you believe in something?* Almost everyone believes in something.

It is not asking: *are you a spiritual person?* Many people describe themselves that way.

It is asking: *are you afraid of what it means to give your worship — your ultimate allegiance, your deepest orientation, the direction your heart bows in — to something that is not fit to hold that weight?*

The word *taqwā* comes from the root *w-q-y*, which means *to shield, to protect, to guard*. In its spiritual sense it is the self-protecting alertness of someone who knows there are real consequences to real choices. It is not cowering. It is *seeing clearly enough to be careful*.

[PAUSE]

And notice: the question is *afalā* — "will you *not*?" — which in Arabic carries a mild rebuke, almost a disbelief, almost a sadness. *Is it really possible that you would not?* The question assumes that the natural, sane, fitting response to this reality is protective awe. It is stunned that this response might be absent.

The taqwā question is not a threat appended to a theological statement. It is a genuinely bewildered invitation: *you have just been told the most important fact about existence — that there is no god fit to be worshipped but Him — and you will not even take the step of being afraid of what happens when you act otherwise?*

### Bridge to the Close

Three elements of these two ayahs have now opened up beneath us.

A verb that tells you how Allah raises civilizations — the same way He raises a single person: with patient, unfolding care.

A cohort — called *qarn* because they are *joined* — who inherit the same question every previous generation refused to answer.

And a question that ends the messenger's call and hangs, still unanswered, in the air of your own moment: *will you not have taqwā?*

The Qur'an does not narrate this as ancient history. It narrates it as the pattern you are living inside of right now.

## Part 3: What These Ayahs Are Doing in You

Let me end where we began — with the silence after the Flood.

You are, in some sense, always in that silence. You are always on the far side of something that ended. A version of yourself that drowned. A relationship, a chapter, a certainty you used to have. The flood-silence is not only in Quranic history. It is in the in-between moments of every human life.

[PAUSE]

And into that silence — into every silence like that — the same thing happens that happened after the Flood.

*Anshaʾnā.* We brought forth. We raised up. We caused to grow.

Allah does not leave silence alone. He fills it with a new generation, and into the new generation He places a voice, and the voice says what it has always said:

*Worship Allah. You have no god fit to be worshipped but Him. Will you not protect yourself — will you not see clearly what is actually real — will you not have taqwā?*

[PAUSE]

The question was asked of ʿĀd. They refused. The punishment came. The pattern completed — judgment following rejected mercy, as it always does. And then, after them, another generation. And another messenger. And the same question. Again.

*"Then We brought forth, after them, another generation."*

After the Flood: ʿĀd. After ʿĀd: another. After them: another. Down through every civilization that rose and eventually subsided, down through all the messengers who carried the same sentence in different centuries, down to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ who carried it to your era — and now, through the Qur'an, to you.

You are the *qarnan ākharīn* of your own moment. The question has been passed forward to you. A messenger was sent *fīkum* and *minkum* — from within your kind, speaking your language, made of what you are made of. The door that opened after every flood is open now.

What will you answer?
