# All Except One

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to these four short verses. Read them slowly, because they move very fast — and what they cover in fourteen words would take a historian a book.

> [ayah:37:133] وَإِنَّ لُوطًا لَّمِنَ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ
> [ayah:37:134] إِذْ نَجَّيْنَـٰهُ وَأَهْلَهُۥٓ أَجْمَعِينَ
> [ayah:37:135] إِلَّا عَجُوزًا فِى ٱلْغَـٰبِرِينَ
> [ayah:37:136] ثُمَّ دَمَّرْنَا ٱلْـَٔاخَرِينَ

*Wa inna Lūṭan la-min al-mursalīn. Idh najjaynāhu wa ahlahu ajmaʿīn. Illā ʿajūzan fī al-ghābirīn. Thumma dammarnā al-ākharīn.*

"And indeed, Lut was among the messengers. When We saved him and his family — all of them. Except an old woman among those who lagged behind. Then We destroyed the others."

### Rebuild the world

You need to know where you are standing before these words can land.

You are standing inside a procession. Surah As-Saffat is not telling one story — it is walking you down a long corridor lined with portraits. Nuh appears, and his section is sealed: *"Peace be upon Nuh among the worlds."* Ibrahim appears, tested with his son, and his section closes. Musa and Harun appear, and Allah declares: *"And indeed We favored them."* Then Ilyas, just a few verses before our passage, and his section ends with a phrase you are about to hear again: *wa inna Ilyāsa la-min al-mursalīn* — "And indeed Ilyas was among the messengers."

And then Lut.

By the time you reach him, your ear has been trained. You have heard this drumbeat over and over. **Each prophet, no matter how the world treated him, gets the same verdict stamped onto the eternal record.** The surah is doing something deliberate: it is reading out, name by name, a list of men whom their own people called liars, madmen, sorcerers — and behind each name Allah writes, in His own hand, the correction.

Now feel the original moment. These verses came down in Makkah, to a Prophet ﷺ whose own city had turned on him. The people who shared his streets, his marketplace, even his bloodline, were calling him a liar to his face. And into that loneliness, Allah sends down a corridor of portraits, each one whispering the same thing: *this is the pattern. The sent one is rejected. The sent one is vindicated. The ones who reject him are destroyed. And — pay attention — even some who lived in his own house did not make it out.*

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, because it should unsettle you a little.

This passage saves an entire household. *Ahlahu ajmaʿīn* — his family, **all of them.** The word for "all" is right there, sealing the group shut. And then, in the very next breath, before the verse can even end, Allah cuts one person out of that sealed group with a single word.

One word undoes the word "all."

Who was she? She lived under a prophet's roof. She was counted, grammatically, inside his "family." She ate at his table. And she did not make it to morning.

What does it take to be *that close* to salvation — and miss it? [PAUSE]

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verdict Stamped Twice — *wa inna ... la-min al-mursalīn*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because if I don't, you will read right past the most insistent thing in it.

Arabic has a tool for emphasis that English barely has. In English, if I want to insist on something, I either repeat it or I raise my voice: "He really was a messenger. He *was*. I promise you." Arabic does it structurally, with small particles that carry the weight of insistence inside the grammar itself.

There are two of them in this first verse, stacked on top of each other.

The first is **إِنَّ** — *inna*. It is placed at the front of a statement to mean something like "indeed," "truly," "it is a certain fact that." It is the particle you reach for when you expect someone might doubt you. You don't say *inna* about the obvious. You say it about the contested.

The second is the little **لَ** — the *lām* — that you can hear fused onto the next word: *la-min al-mursalīn*. This *lām* is called *lām al-tawkīd*, the *lām* of emphasis. It is a second layer of insistence, landing right on the predicate.

So look at what Allah actually built here:

> وَإِنَّ لُوطًۭا **لَّ**مِنَ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ

Not "Lut was a messenger." Not even "indeed Lut was a messenger." But something the English can barely carry: **"And it is a certain, insisted-upon fact, that Lut — truly — was among the sent ones."** Two emphasis-markers around one quiet name.

Now stay with this. [PAUSE]

Why would you need to insist *twice* on something? You only double the emphasis when the claim has been doubled in its denial. You sign your name twice on a document when the stakes are high and the dispute is real.

The four classical commentators here barely paraphrase the verse, because its meaning is plain — al-Jalalayn simply restates it, al-Baghawi simply restates it. The plainness is the point. **Allah is not arguing. He is recording.** He is entering a verdict into a record that can never be appealed. The people of Lut spent their lives saying *he is a liar, expel him from your town, he is a man who claims to be pure* — and across the centuries, after their dust has settled, Allah reopens the file and writes two words of emphasis over the name they tried to erase: *truly — among the sent.*

There is something else here worth pausing on, something al-Muyassar surfaces before it even gets to the emphasis particles. Its description of Lut opens with *iṣṭifā'* — Allah did not merely commission him but *chose and elected him*: the sending was preceded by a selection. This is worth feeling in your chest. The double emphasis in the verse is not only a response to human rejection — it is an affirmation of a divine decision that predates any human dispute by whatever interval separates divine counsel from earthly time. When Lut's neighbors called him a liar, they were not merely disagreeing with a man. They were disputing a choice Allah had already made and sealed. No wonder the emphasis needs to be doubled. The denial that needed correcting was not a simple misunderstanding — it was a claim aimed at overturning something settled in the divine record long before the dispute began.

And now hear it as a refrain. This is the same construction the surah used for Ilyas only ten verses earlier: *wa inna Ilyāsa la-min al-mursalīn.* The same stamp. The surah is pressing the same seal onto name after name. **The vindication is not custom-made for Lut. It is a law.** Every single one of the sent ones gets it. The world's rejection is loud and temporary; Allah's verdict is quiet and permanent.

Here is what this single construction reveals: **the last word about a believer is never spoken by the people who reject him. It is spoken by Allah — and He speaks it with emphasis.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: Two Verbs From the Same Mold — *najjaynā* and *dammarnā*

Now I want to show you something about the *shape* of two verbs, because Allah chose their shape with care, and the shape is carrying meaning that the translation throws away.

Teach the concept first. In Arabic, verbs come in "forms" — patterns you pour the root letters into, and each pattern bends the meaning. One of these, what scholars call **Form II**, is built by *doubling the middle letter* of the root. And one of the main things this doubling does is **intensify** — it takes a plain action and makes it thorough, complete, emphatic, done all the way through. If the basic verb is "to break," the Form II version leans toward "to shatter into pieces." The doubled letter is like a clenched fist around the action.

Now look at the two great acts in this passage. The salvation and the destruction. And notice that Allah poured *both* of them into this same intensifying mold.

The salvation: **نَجَّيْنَٰهُ** — *najjaynāhu*. The root means rescue, deliverance. But it is not the plain form here; the middle is doubled. This is not merely "We saved him." This is **"We delivered him thoroughly, completely, with nothing left exposed."** Rescue carried all the way through to safety.

The destruction: **دَمَّرْنَا** — *dammarnā*. Root *d-m-r*, ruin. Again, the middle is doubled. Again, the intensive mold. Not "We harmed them," not even "We destroyed them," but **"We brought them to utter, total ruin — destruction carried all the way down."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what Allah has done by matching the molds?

He has placed salvation and destruction in the *same grammatical shape*. The same intensity. The same thoroughness. **The hand that saves completely and the hand that destroys completely is one hand.** There is no half-measure in either direction. When Allah saves, He does not save you partway and leave you stranded on the shore. And when Allah's judgment falls on a people who have earned it, it does not graze them — it goes all the way down to the foundations.

This is *istidlal* — Allah using the physical fact of a flattened city to point at an unseen reality about His own nature: **His mercy and His justice are equally complete.** People want a God who is intense in mercy and casual in justice. The grammar refuses that fantasy. Both verbs are clenched fists. Both go all the way through.

And there is a tenderness hidden in the matched pair. Because if His destruction is total, and His salvation comes from the *same* intensifying mold — then the rescue of the one who clung to Him is just as total, just as thorough, just as without-gaps as the ruin of the ones who turned away. The believer is not saved *barely.* The believer is saved *completely.* [PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Word That Undoes "All" — *ajmaʿīn ... illā*

Now we arrive at the heart of it. And I need you to watch two words that sit only a few syllables apart, because between them a household is split in two.

The first word is **أَجْمَعِينَ** — *ajmaʿīn*. It comes from a root meaning to gather, to collect, to bring together into one. As a word of emphasis after a noun, it means "all of them, every last one, the whole set with no one left out." It is a *sealing* word. When you say *wa ahlahu ajmaʿīn* — "and his family, all of them" — you have closed the door. The group is complete. Nobody is missing. The accounting is finished.

> إِذْ نَجَّيْنَٰهُ وَأَهْلَهُۥٓ **أَجْمَعِينَ**

"When We saved him and his family — all of them."

The sentence sounds finished. The door is shut. Everyone is safe.

And then — the very next word of the next verse:

> **إِلَّا** عَجُوزًۭا فِى ٱلْغَٰبِرِينَ

**إِلَّا** — *illā*. "Except."

Stay with this. Feel the whiplash. [PAUSE]

Arabic has a tool called *istithnā'*, exception. The particle *illā* reaches back into a group that has already been named and *pulls one piece out of it.* And here Allah has done something almost cruel in its precision: He let you hear the word "**all**" — He let the door click shut on the whole family, safe — and then He pried it back open with "**except**" and removed one person who was standing inside.

This is what I asked you to read as carefully as anything else: **read the absence.** The most important person in this passage is the one carved out of it. She is not given a name. She is not given a defense. She is given one word of exclusion and then a category.

And who was she? The commentators tell us plainly. Al-Muyassar: *she was his wife* — *hiya zawjatuhu* — and more than merely old: she was *hārima*, the word meaning deeply, decrepitly aged, worn through to the foundation by the years. She *perished with those of her people who perished — on account of her disbelief — li-kufrihā.* Al-Qurtubi and al-Jalalayn and al-Baghawi all carry the same: the old woman, left among those left behind.

His *wife.* [PAUSE]

Hold the word *hārima* for a moment before moving on. Not young and careless, not reckless with the confidence of someone who has not yet seen enough. Deeply, extremely aged — a woman who had lived an entire long life next to a prophet. If years of proximity were going to accomplish anything, they had a lifetime to work with. If closeness to the messenger was going to soften the inner ground, it had decades. And still the operative word al-Muyassar reaches for is *li-kufrihā* — because of her *disbelief*. Not her crimes. Not an enumerated list of wrongs. Her *kufr* — the inner posture of withholding assent from what her husband was bringing. A lifetime of years next to a prophet, and the inner door never opened. The years did not do the work. Only the heart could.

She was inside the word *ahl* — family. By every external measure she was on the list. She shared his name, his home, his bed, his table. If you had taken a census of "the believing household of Lut," she would have been counted. The grammar nearly counted her — *ajmaʿīn*, all of them — and then one word reached in and uncounted her.

Now the second word, the category she fell into: **ٱلْغَٰبِرِينَ** — *al-ghābirīn*. The root *gh-b-r* carries the sense of *remaining behind, staying, being left over, lagging.* The *ghābir* is the one who stays when others move on, who lingers where he should have left. Some of the lexicographers connect this root to *ghubār* — dust — the residue that settles and remains after everything else has gone. Al-Tabari notes that classical scholars themselves acknowledged disagreement over the precise valence of this word — whether it emphasizes the spatial dimension (those who *remained in the city* when the saved departed) or the moral dimension (those who were *left over as residue*, belonging in their essence to the condemned category). As al-Tabari observes, both readings arrive at the same portrait of the same person.

Hear what Allah did *not* say. He did not say she was saved and then lost. He placed her, with a single phrase, **among "those who remained."** When the command came to leave the city in the night and not look back, the saved walked out — and she stayed in the category of the ones who stayed. Her body may have walked some steps; her *belonging* never left. She remained with the doomed because her heart had never actually departed from them. She was, in the deepest sense, **one of the remainers wearing the costume of the rescued.** [PAUSE]

Here is what these two words, *ajmaʿīn* and *illā*, reveal when you set them side by side: **You can be inside the word "all" and still be removed by the word "except." You can be counted as family and uncounted as faithful. Proximity puts you in the house. Only allegiance gets you out of it.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Look at what the construction has shown us. A verdict stamped twice over a rejected man's name — rooted in a divine election that preceded any human dispute. Two acts — rescue and ruin — poured into the same intensive mold, so that mercy and justice come out equally total. And a single exception-word that reaches into a sealed household and removes the one who was near but not loyal: deeply aged, a lifetime lived in proximity, excluded by the single operative fact of her inner withholding.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About you, and the houses you stand near, and what it actually takes to walk out alive.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Proximity Is Not Salvation

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of person you have met, and maybe — be honest — a particular kind of person you have *been* — who was very near to something true and still did not receive it. Who sat in the right circles, grew up in the right family, attended the right gatherings, and whose heart remained somehow elsewhere the whole time. Who wore the belonging without the allegiance.

The wife of Lut is that person made literary, made permanent, made unforgettable by four words: *illā ʿajūzan fī al-ghābirīn.*

And what should stop you before you move on is that she was not young and careless. She had a full, long life of extreme old age to work with, a lifetime of proximity to a man whom Allah had chosen and sent. If nearness to the messenger was going to do anything, it had decades. And still: *li-kufrihā*. The operative word is interior. Not a record of external crimes. Not a list of transgressions documented by witnesses. Her *kufr* — the posture of the heart that says no, or worse, the posture that says neither yes nor no but simply never commits, never fully crosses the threshold from near to *in.*

This is the passage's quietest warning, and the most personal one.

We can live near truth without ever *inhabiting* it. We can speak the language of faith without fluency in the interior. We can be counted, grammatically, inside the word *ahl* — household, family, community — and still belong, in every meaningful sense, to *al-ghābirīn*: the ones who remain, the ones who linger, the ones who never actually departed from where they started.

The grammar understood this before we did. *Ajmaʿīn* included her. *Illā* reached back and removed her. Not because she was expelled from the outside — but because her belonging had always been misregistered. The exception-particle did not *create* an exile. It *named* one that already existed. [PAUSE]

Here is what this costs you as a reader: you cannot outsource your salvation to your address. The house you live in — the community, the family name, the proximity to people who have genuinely given their hearts — none of it transfers. It is yours to give or not give, and it has to be given from somewhere no external census can reach. **Allah does not save households. He saves the ones inside the household whose belonging runs all the way down.**

### Theme Two: The Landscape of Precedent

Here is something that reframes where the entire passage is sitting — and it comes from what Ibn Kathir draws out of the verses immediately following these four.

Two verses after the destruction of the people of Lut, the surah pivots and addresses the Quraysh directly: *"Verily, you pass by them in the morning and at night — will you not then reflect?"* (37:137-138). Ibn Kathir makes explicit what this means: the ruins of the destroyed cities lay on the actual trade route north, the caravan road the Quraysh traveled to Syria twice a year. They had not merely heard stories about the people of Lut. They had *passed through the landscape of their absence*. They crossed the dead terrain where a civilization had been and was not. They saw, with their own eyes, what happens when the judgment of *dammarnā* falls all the way through.

Now come back to our four verses and hear them differently.

Allah is not asking the Quraysh to imagine a distant event. He is asking them to *remember what they had already seen* and explain why they were still asking the Prophet ﷺ for a sign. The gallery of messengers in As-Saffat is not only a gallery of portraits — it is a gallery whose evidence is written into the landscape, visible from the road, present in the absence. Every prophet vindicated, every city of rejectors in ruins, and the Quraysh passed the proof twice a year and arrived back in Makkah and said: *if only he would show us a sign.*

This is the deepest irony the surah is pressing. **They already had the sign. It was a dead landscape on their regular route.** The question was not whether evidence existed. The question was what kind of seeing they were bringing to what their eyes had already encountered.

And notice that the four compressed verses about Lut accomplish something specific within this context: they strip the story down to its architecture. Not the names of the towns, not the details of the angels, not the specifics of the transgression — just the skeleton of divine action. *Messenger chosen and sent. People denied. Household saved. Household, minus one. Remainder destroyed.* That skeleton fits every prophet in the gallery. It fits the ruins visible from the trade road. It fits the moment the Prophet ﷺ was living in Makkah. **The surah is teaching a grammar of divine action** — and it is asking: *do you recognize the sentence you are inside?*

The Quraysh passed the evidence every season and still would not read it. There is something in us that prefers not to recognize patterns that implicate us. We would rather ask for a new sign than receive the one already given, because a new sign leaves open the possibility that this time might be different, that the pattern does not apply here, that we are not already inside the story. The ruins on the road denied them that comfort. These four verses deny us the same. [PAUSE]

### Theme Three: When Both Verbs Are Clenched Fists

Return, one final time, to the two verbs we studied — *najjaynāhu* and *dammarnā* — and let their matched molds say something that goes beyond grammar into theology.

People often want a God who is warm in mercy and cool in judgment. A God who rescues with full intensity but punishes with reluctance, halfheartedly, as if destruction costs Him something He is not sure He wants to spend. The grammar here will not permit this fantasy.

Both verbs are Form II. Both are clenched fists. The rescue is carried *all the way through to safety*; the ruin is carried *all the way down to the foundations*. And both come from the same hand — both use "We" — both are acts of the same divine agency expressing itself with equal completeness in opposite directions.

This is a portrait of a God whose attributes do not compete with one another. Mercy is not struggling against justice in some internal divine debate, and justice is not a concession to mercy's disappointment. They are both *expressions of the same completeness*. When Allah acts in mercy, He does not hold back the full weight of who He is. When He acts in justice, He does not hold back either. What you receive — in either direction — is the unmediated output of a Being who does nothing partially.

This should produce two things in a person who sits with it.

First: gratitude that is not thin. When you understand that the same intensity that drove the ruins into the ground is the intensity that drove the saved ones out of it — when you understand that *najjaynāhu* is a clenched fist and not a gentle tap — the word *saved* becomes something you cannot toss lightly. You were not barely rescued. You were *thoroughly* rescued, Form II, all the way through. **The mercy you have received is as total as the justice you were spared from.**

Second: sobriety that is not despair. The destruction was complete. The people of Lut did not merely suffer — they were brought to utter, total ruin, *dammarnā*, all the way down. This is not comfortable to sit with, and it should not be. A God who is content to ruin people only partway is a God whose mercy can be trusted only partway. But a God whose justice is this total is also a God whose mercy, when it lands, lands with the same force. **Total justice and total mercy are the same God — and which one meets you depends entirely on which category you are in when the moment arrives.** [PAUSE]

The surah ends Lut's passage not with the ruins but with the question addressed to those who pass by them: *will you not then reflect?* The same question is addressed to every reader who has followed the four verses to this point. You have seen the architecture of divine action. You have watched the sealing word and the exception-particle. You have felt the weight of both Form II verbs. You have met an old woman who spent a lifetime inside the word "all" and was removed by the word "except."

Will you not then reflect.

## CONCLUSION

Four verses. Fourteen Arabic words. And inside them:

A divine verdict — double-stamped, rooted in an election older than the dispute — over a man his own people called a liar.

A household gathered, sealed, pronounced complete — and then, before the sentence can breathe, split open by a single particle of exception.

An old woman, deeply aged, *hārima*, a lifetime lived in proximity to a prophet, removed from the saved by the operative fact of her *kufr* — the inner withholding that no number of years in the right house could compensate for.

Two verbs in the same mold — *najjaynā*, *dammarnā* — pouring rescue and ruin from the same hand with equal thoroughness, equally complete, equally without gaps.

And behind the verses, a landscape: the ruins of the destroyed cities on the trade road north, passed by the Quraysh every season, a permanent address on the geography of divine precedent. The sign that was already there. The evidence that had been under their feet for generations. And still the question — *will you not then reflect?*

You are not the Quraysh. But you pass through your own landscapes of precedent. You have seen what happens when the pattern completes itself. You have stood inside communities, households, families, and watched people who were near the truth and belonged elsewhere. Maybe — be honest — you have watched yourself.

The word *ajmaʿīn* can be said about you. The people who count you may count you correctly, as one of the household of those who sought the face of Allah.

*Illā* does not have to reach in. [PAUSE]

But the particle is only kept at bay by something the grammar cannot see: the inner allegiance, the actual belonging, the heart that has genuinely departed from *al-ghābirīn* and does not look back.

She was called *ahl*. She was nearly *ajmaʿīn*. She was deeply, extremely aged — a full life spent inside the word "all." And she stayed.

Don't stay. [PAUSE]

Fourteen words. All of it is there.
