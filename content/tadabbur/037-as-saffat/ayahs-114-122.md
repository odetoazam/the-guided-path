# The Grace That Came First

## Introduction: A Passage You Are Already Inside

Let me read the words first, slowly, and then I want to tell you something strange about them — something that is true of you, right now, in this moment, before we even begin.

> [ayah:37:114] وَلَقَدْ مَنَنَّا عَلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ وَهَـٰرُونَ
> *wa-laqad manan;nā ʿalā Mūsā wa-Hārūn*
> "And We certainly conferred favor upon Musa and Harun."

And it continues:

> *wa-najjaynāhumā wa-qawmahumā mina-l-karbi-l-ʿaẓīm. wa-naṣarnāhum fa-kānū humu-l-ghālibīn. wa-ātaynāhumā-l-kitāba-l-mustabīn. wa-hadaynāhumā-ṣ-ṣirāṭa-l-mustaqīm. wa-taraknā ʿalayhimā fi-l-ākhirīn. salāmun ʿalā Mūsā wa-Hārūn.*

"And We saved them and their people from the great distress. And We helped them, so it was they who became the victors. And We gave them both the clarifying Scripture. And We guided them both to the straight path. And We left for them, among the later ones: *Peace be upon Musa and Harun.*"

[PAUSE]

Here is the strange thing.

[ayah:37:119] وَتَرَكْنَا عَلَيْهِمَا فِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرِينَ

That is **you**.

[ayah:37:120] سَلَـٰمٌ عَلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ وَهَـٰرُونَ

So when you read this passage — when your tongue forms those words, when your heart whispers *peace be upon them* — you are not just reading a verse. **You are the fulfillment of it.** Allah said, fourteen centuries ago and more, that He would leave a remembrance of these two brothers in the mouths of generations not yet born. And here you are. Born. With their names and a prayer of peace on your lips.

The promise predicted you. The ayah is being completed in the act of your reading it.

[PAUSE]

To understand why that should move you, you have to understand who first heard these words.

This surah, As-Saffat, came down in Mecca — in the hard years. A small band of believers, mocked, boycotted, beaten, outnumbered a hundred to one. The Prophet ﷺ watching his people suffer and being told, in effect, *you are losing*. And into that climate Allah sends down a long procession of prophets — Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Harun, Ilyas, Lut, Yunus — one after another, like a roll call. And almost every name in the roll call gets the same two things stamped on the end of it: a declaration of **peace**, and the line *"thus do We reward those who do good."*

It is a surah built to say one thing to people who feel like they are drowning: *Every messenger before you felt exactly this. Every one of them was outnumbered. Every one of them was rescued. Every one of them was vindicated. And every one of them was given a peace that outlived their enemies, outlived their century, outlived the empires that crushed them.*

Musa and Harun stood before the mightiest state on earth with nothing but a staff and a brother. Pharaoh had armies, treasuries, magicians, an entire civilization. Musa had a speech impediment and a wanted poster.

And the Quran says: *fa-kānū humu-l-ghālibīn* — **it was they who became the victors.**

Today we are going to sit with three words in this passage, and then with two realities they point to. And I promise you, by the end, the small phrase *salāmun ʿalā Mūsā wa-Hārūn* — peace upon Musa and Harun — is going to feel less like a line in a book and more like a thread that runs straight through time and ties you to two men who refused to be afraid. Let's begin.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *Mananna*: The Gift That Comes Before You Earn It

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because if I don't, you will walk right past the most important thing in the whole passage.

In Arabic — and in your life — there are two kinds of giving. There is the giving that comes *after*. You work, you earn, you produce, and then you are paid. A wage. A reward. A return on what you put in. And then there is the giving that comes *before* — before you have done anything at all, before you could possibly deserve it, when you are not even a candidate for it yet. A gift in the truest sense. Not a payment. A grace.

Hold that distinction. Now look at how this entire passage opens.

> **وَلَقَدْ مَنَنَّا عَلَىٰ مُوسَىٰ وَهَٰرُونَ**

The verb is *mananna* — "We conferred favor." It comes from the root **م ن ن**, *mīm-nūn-nūn*. And I want you to notice *where Allah placed this verb*. He did not begin the passage with the rescue at the sea. He did not begin with the victory over Pharaoh. He did not begin with the Book, or the guidance, or the splitting of the water.

He began with **a favor**. *Mananna.*

The four classical commentators are in agreement about what this favor was. Al-Jalalayn says simply: *bi-n-nubuwwa* — "with prophethood." Al-Baghawi: *anʿamnā ʿalayhimā bi-n-nubuwwa* — "We graced them both with prophethood." Al-Muyassar widens it: "We conferred upon Musa and Harun prophethood and messengership." Al-Qurtubi places it in sequence: having just told us how He saved Ibrahim's son from the knife, Allah now tells us of the favor He placed on Musa and Harun.

Al-Tabari, for his part, paraphrases *mananna* with the word *tafaḍḍalnā* — "We showed grace," from the root *f-ḍ-l*, meaning surplus, overflow, what exceeds what is strictly owed. And he pauses to name them as *ibnay ʿimrān* — not "Musa the prophet and Harun the prophet," but "the two sons of Imran." Two men who shared a father before they shared a mission. The grace came to persons with lineage, with history, with blood between them — not to abstract roles. And note that al-Muyassar is careful to pair *nubuwwa* with *risāla* — prophethood *and* messengership — two realities that Islamic theological usage keeps distinct. A *nabī* receives revelation; a *rasūl* is commissioned with a specific mission to a people. Musa and Harun were both. The favor was not only to know. It was to be sent.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with this. Notice the order.

The prophethood came *first*. Before Musa ever returned to Egypt. Before he ever spoke a word to Pharaoh. Before he passed a single test. The favor was not the trophy he received for winning the fight. The favor was the thing Allah gave him *that made the fight possible at all.*

We get this backwards constantly. We believe, somewhere deep down, that Allah's favor is the *reward* at the end of a life well lived. That first you prove yourself, and then — if you manage it — He grants you His grace. We treat divine favor like a wage.

But the grammar of this passage says: **the grace came first.** The capacity to stand before Pharaoh was itself a gift handed over before the standing. Musa did not earn the right to be sent. He was *given* it. *Mananna* — We bestowed, freely, before.

### The Two Faces of the Root

Now here is where this word opens completely. Stay with me.

This same root, **م ن ن**, has a second meaning in Arabic — and it is almost the opposite of grace. *Al-mann*, from the very same letters, also means: **to remind someone of a favor you did them, in order to make them feel small.** To give, and then hold the gift over someone's head. "After everything I did for you." That stinging, deflating reminder of a debt.

Allah uses this exact meaning elsewhere in the Quran, as a warning. He tells the believers: *lā tubṭilū ṣadaqātikum bi-l-manni wa-l-adhā* — "do not nullify your charity with reminders of it and with hurt" (2:264). There, *mann* is poison. It is the thing that **cancels** a good deed. You gave food to the hungry, and then you ruined it by reminding them they owed you.

So one root holds two faces: the pure gift that lifts you up, and the reproachful reminder that crushes you down.

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself: which one is Allah doing in our ayah?

When *Allah* says *mananna* — "We conferred favor" — is He reminding Musa of a debt to shame him? Is He saying "look what We did for you"?

Here is the staggering thing. When Allah names His favor, **it never shames the one who received it.** Because the *mann* that diminishes a person only works between equals — between two human beings, where reminding you of a gift implies *you should have been able to do it yourself, and you failed, and you needed me.* That sting only lands when there's a pretense that you were ever self-sufficient.

But before Allah, there was never any pretense. You were nothing. You were *not* before He made you something. So when He says *mananna ʿalaykum* — "We conferred favor upon you" — there is no insult buried in it, because there is no fiction of your independence to wound. He is simply telling the truth: *everything you are, We gave you.* And the only correct response to that truth is not humiliation. It is **worship.**

This is why the same word that destroys a good deed when *we* use it is the most beautiful word in the world when *Allah* uses it. *Mann* from a human says: *you are indebted and small.* *Mann* from Allah says: *you are loved and carried.* The word didn't change. The Giver did.

What this single word reveals: **before Musa won anything, before he survived anything, he was already a recipient of grace — and the grace was the foundation, not the prize.**

### Element Two — The Choreography of "Them": Who Shares in What

Now I want to show you something that you can only see in the Arabic, and once you see it you will not be able to unsee it. It is the most precise piece of grammar in the whole passage, and it is doing enormous spiritual work.

First, a teaching point. Arabic does something English cannot. English has *singular* and *plural* — one, or many. But Arabic has a third number in between: the **dual**, *al-muthannā*. A specific grammatical form for exactly *two*. Not one, not "many" — *two, precisely.* And Arabic also marks this on its pronouns. "Them two" is *-humā*. "Them, three-or-more" is *-hum*. Two different endings. The language is constantly, precisely telling you *how many.*

Now watch the count rise and fall across these verses. Watch it like a camera zooming out and back in.

It opens on **two**: *mananna ʿalā Mūsā wa-Hārūn* — the favor falls on Musa and Harun. The dual. Two brothers.

Then — *wa-najjaynāhumā wa-qawmahumā* — "We saved **them two** and **their people**." The frame widens. The salvation does not stay with the brothers. It spills outward to include an entire nation. The two, and then their people. Al-Jalalayn names them explicitly: *Banū Isrāʾīl* — the Children of Israel. And the distress they were rescued from — *al-karb al-ʿaẓīm*, the great distress — al-Jalalayn defines with surgical precision: *istiʿbād firʿawn iyyāhum*. The enslavement of Pharaoh over them. Not hardship in the abstract. **Institutional subjugation**, a system designed to break a people across generations. Al-Muyassar sets another word alongside it: *madhalla* — humiliation, the systematic stripping of dignity that accompanied the bondage. And al-Tabari folds two distinct griefs into that single phrase: the accumulated anguish of living as property under Pharaoh's house, and the drowning that annihilated their oppressors — the very fate that could just as easily have swept the Israelites too. Two distresses. One word. One act of God that reversed both.

Then the camera pulls all the way back — *wa-naṣarnā**hum*** — "We helped **them**." Not *humā* now. *Hum.* The plural. The whole people. **فَكَانُوا۟ هُمُ ٱلْغَٰلِبِينَ** — *"so **they** became the victors."* The victory belongs to everyone. The crowd. The nation. The deliverance and the triumph are shared wide.

[PAUSE]

And then — watch closely — the camera snaps back in. Tight. To just two.

*wa-ātaynā**humā**-l-kitāb* — "We gave **them two** the Book."
*wa-hadaynā**humā**-ṣ-ṣirāṭa-l-mustaqīm* — "We guided **them two** to the straight path."
*wa-taraknā ʿalay**himā** fi-l-ākhirīn* — "We left, upon **them two**, among the later ones."
*salāmun ʿalā Mūsā wa-Hārūn* — "Peace upon **Musa and Harun.**"

Do you see it? **Read the asymmetry.**

The **rescue** was shared with the whole people. The **victory** was shared with the whole people. But the **Book**, the **guidance to the straight path**, the **lasting legacy**, and the **eternal peace** — those snap back to just the two.

The people were saved. The people won. But not everyone who is saved receives the Book. Not everyone who escapes the sea is given the straight path. Not everyone who shares in the victory has *salām* declared over their name among the later generations.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what this is telling you, because it is uncomfortable and it is precious.

**Deliverance is not the same as guidance.** Being rescued is not the same as being guided. The same people Allah pulled out of the great distress — the same nation that crossed the sea on dry land and watched their oppressor drown — that very nation would later argue, complain, build a calf, and refuse to enter the land. They shared the *rescue*. They did not all hold onto the *guidance*.

How many people does Allah save from a disaster, a sickness, an accident, a near-death — who walk out the other side and never turn back to Him? The rescue was real. The plural *naṣarnāhum* — "We helped them all" — was real. But the *ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, the staying on the straight road afterward — that narrowed. It always narrows.

The widening pronoun is Allah's mercy: *I will save even those who will later forget Me.* The narrowing pronoun is Allah's truth: *but the Book and the straight path are held by those who carry them.*

And notice the absence — read the gap. Why is the *salām* at the end given only to the two?
