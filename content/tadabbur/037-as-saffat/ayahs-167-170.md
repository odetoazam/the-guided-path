# The Reminder They Asked For

## Introduction: A Promise Made to an Empty Room

Let me begin with something almost everyone has done at least once.

You are lying awake at night, and you tell yourself a story about who you would be — *if only.* If only I had grown up in a religious household. If only I had a teacher like the ones the early Muslims had. If only I lived in a different time, a purer time, before all this noise and distraction. If only I had wealth, so I wouldn't have to chase it and could finally devote myself to worship. If only the proof were clearer. If only the sign were undeniable.

*Then* — you tell yourself — *then* I would be the believer I was always meant to be. Then I would pray every prayer on time. Then I would give freely. Then I would be sincere.

[PAUSE]

It is one of the most comfortable thoughts the human mind produces. And it is comfortable for a precise reason: **the version of you in that story never has to actually do anything.** He lives in a condition that has not arrived. He is righteous in a world that does not exist. And because that world does not exist, his righteousness can never be tested, never be measured, never fail.

Now hear what Allah recorded about a people who said almost exactly this — and then watch what He did with it.

> [ayah:37:167] وَإِن كَانُوا۟ لَيَقُولُونَ
> [ayah:37:168] لَوْ أَنَّ عِندَنَا ذِكْرًا مِّنَ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ
> [ayah:37:169] لَكُنَّا عِبَادَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ
> [ayah:37:170] فَكَفَرُوا۟ بِهِۦ ۖ فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ

> *Wa in kānū la-yaqūlūn. Law anna ʿindanā dhikran mina-l-awwalīn. La-kunnā ʿibāda-llāhi-l-mukhlaṣīn. Fa-kafarū bihi — fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.*

> "And indeed, they used to say: 'If only we had a reminder from the former peoples, we would surely have been the chosen servants of Allah.' But [when it came] they disbelieved in it — so they are going to know."

### Rebuild the World

Picture Mecca before the Prophet ﷺ received revelation. The Quraysh knew the broad shape of the story. They knew the Jews had a Book. They knew the Christians had a Book. They had heard, through caravan and conversation, that other nations possessed scripture, prophets, a direct line from the heavens. And they felt the sting of it — al-Qurtubi tells us this was their answer *when they were reproached for their ignorance.* When someone shamed them for having no guidance, this is what they reached for.

So they made a boast. A boast disguised as a complaint. *If only we had what they had. If only a reminder from the ancients had reached us too. We wouldn't be like these other nations who received their Books and corrupted them. We* — the Arabs, the people of memory, the people of the word — *we would have been Allah's purest, most devoted servants. Give us the Book and watch.*

Al-Muyassar preserves a temporal precision that makes this boast all the more devastating: these words were spoken *before your sending, O Messenger* — before the Prophet's mission came. Which means they had years, perhaps decades, to repeat this claim in the warm safety of its hypothetical. Years in which no scripture arrived to test them. Years in which the claim could only grow more confident the longer it went unchallenged. The counterfactual was their comfort, and they had grown accustomed to living in it.

[PAUSE]

This whole surah, As-Saffat, has been a courtroom. Prophet after prophet brought forward — Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Harun, Ilyas, Lut, Yunus — and after each one, the verdict on those who denied them. Nation after nation, the same pattern: a messenger came, most rejected him, the consequence fell. And now, at the very end of that procession, Allah turns the camera onto the people listening *right now,* in Mecca, and quotes their own words back to them.

There is something the surah has already prepared that deepens the cut. Just two verses before this passage — in the words of the angels themselves — we heard: *"We are those who stand in rows. We are those who glorify."* Not *we would stand in rows, if only.* Not *give us the condition and we will glorify.* Just: *we stand. We glorify.* Actual, unconditional, present-tense worship, from beings who possess no scripture because they need none — who were never sent a book, never demanded one, and are nonetheless fully devoted. The surah sets that declaration immediately alongside the Quraysh's *if only.* Genuine, unqualified servants in one frame. Conditional, hypothetical ones in the next.

Because they got their wish.

The reminder came.

### The Hook

And here is what should stop us cold. The grammar of these four short lines does something almost unbearable. It records the boast, preserves the exact mood and tense of the wish — and then, in three words, demolishes it. There is a single Arabic letter in this passage so small you could miss it entirely, and yet the entire meaning hangs on it: without that one letter, the verse would say the *opposite* of what it means. And there is a verb at the very end that is missing its object — Allah says "they will know" and then refuses to tell us *what* they will know.

A people separated from their own promise by the thinnest of margins. A threat left deliberately, terribly, unfinished.

Let us go slowly.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Letter That Decides Everything — وَإِن ... لَيَقُولُونَ

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about Arabic that the original listeners knew in their bones.

There is a tiny word in Arabic: **إِنْ** — *in.* Two letters. And it is one of the great ambiguities of the language, because it can mean two completely opposite things depending on context.

In its most common use, **in** is a **negative particle.** *In* means *not.* "Ma" and "in" are the two great negators. So if you hear "in kāna" your first instinct, your default reading, is: *"they were NOT."*

But **in** has a second life. It can also be the **lightened form of إِنَّ — *inna*** — the great emphatic particle, the word that means *"indeed, truly, certainly."* When Arabs used *inna* so often that its heavy doubled *nūn* wore down in speech, it could soften into a light *in* — what the grammarians call **in mukhaffafa min al-thaqīla**, "the *in* lightened from the heavy one." This is exactly what al-Jalalayn flags in the very first words of his commentary here: *«وإن» مخففة من الثقيلة* — "the *in* is the lightened form of the heavy *inna.*"

So now you see the problem. The same two letters. One reading says *not.* The other reading says *indeed.* Negation versus the strongest possible affirmation. **The exact opposite.**

[PAUSE]

How does the listener know which one is meant?

Here is the genius of the language. When *in* is the lightened *inna* — the affirmative one — Arabic requires a marker to enter the sentence, a little **lām** attached to the predicate, whose entire job is to *separate* the two readings, to wave a flag and say: *do not read this as negation; this is affirmation.* The grammarians named it perfectly: **lām al-fāriqa** — *the distinguishing lām, the lām that separates.*

Now look at the verse:

> وَإِن كَانُوا۟ **لَ**يَقُولُونَ
> *Wa in kānū **la**-yaqūlūn*

There it is. That single letter — the **لـ** fused onto the front of *yaqūlūn* — is the lām al-fāriqa. Al-Baghawi names it directly: *( ليقولون ) لام التأكيد* — "*la-yaqūlūn*: the lām of emphasis."

Strip that one letter away and the verse reads: *"they were not saying."* Restore it, and the verse roars: ***"they most certainly used to say."***

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The difference between *"they never said it"* and *"they absolutely said it"* — between innocence and indictment — is one letter. One stroke of the pen.

And now notice what Allah has done by choosing this exact construction. He could have used a hundred clearer ways to say "they used to say." Instead He chose the one form where the sentence trembles on the edge of its own opposite — where a single letter is the only thing holding affirmation apart from denial.

Why does that matter for *these* people?

Because that is precisely their condition. **These are people separated from their own claim by the thinnest possible margin.** They said "we would be the chosen servants of Allah." They were one letter — one breath, one decision — away from it. The Book came. The line between the believer they swore they'd be and the denier they actually became was as thin as a lām. And they fell on the wrong side of it.

The form mirrors the meaning. The grammar that records their boast is built out of the very ambiguity that destroyed them: how close *not* sits to *indeed.* How close the denier sits to the one who almost believed.

One clear statement before we move: **the structure of this sentence is a sentence about how little separates the soul that keeps its promise from the soul that breaks it.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: The Grammar of the Excuse — لَوْ

Let me teach you a second word, and again I want to give you the concept before the verse.

Arabic has more than one way to say "if." English flattens them all into one word, and we lose something crucial. There is **idhā** — the "if/when" of things expected to happen. There is **in** — the "if" of open possibility, maybe yes, maybe no. And then there is **لَوْ — *law.***

*Law* is special. The grammarians give it a name that is almost a sentence in itself: **ḥarf imtināʿ li-imtināʿ** — "the particle of *non-occurrence because of non-occurrence.*" In plain terms: *law* is the "if" of the **contrary-to-fact.** It is the "if" you use for a condition that *did not happen,* and therefore a result that *did not happen* either.

When you say in English, "If I *were* rich, I *would* travel the world" — you are telling the listener, by your grammar alone, that you are *not* rich and you are *not* traveling. The whole sentence lives in a world that isn't real. That is *law.*

Now hear the boast:

> **لَوْ** أَنَّ عِندَنَا ذِكْرًۭا مِّنَ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ
> لَكُنَّا عِبَادَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ

> ***Law*** *anna ʿindanā dhikran mina-l-awwalīn — la-kunnā ʿibāda-llāhi-l-mukhlaṣīn*

> "**If only** we had a reminder from the former peoples, we would surely have been the chosen servants of Allah."

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what the grammar is quietly admitting?

By choosing *law,* they have already confessed two things they did not mean to confess. First, that they *do not have* the reminder — *law* announces the absence of the condition. Fine; that was their complaint. But second — and this is the trap they walked into — *law* equally announces **the absence of the result.** The structure of *law* says: *the condition didn't happen, AND the consequence didn't happen.* "If I were rich I'd be generous" carries, buried inside it, the admission *I am not generous.*

So when they say "*law* we had a reminder, we *would have been* the chosen servants," the very particle they chose whispers underneath: ***and so we are not.***

Their excuse is grammatically honest even when they are not. They reached for *law* to sound noble — *look how devoted we would be* — and *law* turned around and testified against them: *you are devoted only in a world that does not exist.*

Now look closely at *what* they wished for. **Dhikran min al-awwalīn.** Al-Jalalayn does not leave this vague: he glosses *dhikran* directly as *kitāban* — a **scripture**, a revealed book — and *min al-awwalīn* as "from the books of the past nations." This was not a wish for inspiration floating down from heaven, nor for general moral reminders scattered through history. They wanted exactly what the Jews held in the Torah and what the Christians carried in the Gospels: a bound, transmitted, recognized holy text with prophetic lineage behind it. They wanted entry into the guild of the Peoples of the Book. *Give us our scripture, and then watch us.*

The Quran arrived. Not merely a book like the earlier ones — the Book that earlier scriptures had been preparing the world to receive, delivered in the very tongue of the people demanding it. And still it was not enough. Nothing would have been enough, because the scripture was never the real obstacle.

Notice also *where* they located the cause of their faith. **ʿindanā** — "*with us, in our possession.*" The root carries the sense of something *near, at hand, held.* Their sincerity, in their own telling, was waiting on an external delivery. *Give us the package and then watch us become servants.* They located the cause of their faith *outside themselves* — in a Book not yet arrived, in a condition not yet met.

And here is the quiet devastation of it. **A faith that is waiting for conditions is not faith. It is a transaction with terms.** They had turned worship into something owed to them upon delivery: *send the reminder, and we will pay you sincerity.* But sincerity that has a price and a precondition was never sincerity. It was a negotiation.

What this invites us to consider — and now I am stepping just past the bare grammar into reflection — is that *law* is the native tongue of every excuse the human heart has ever made. Every "if only" is a sentence in the mood of *law.* And every sentence in the mood of *law* contains, whether we hear it or not, the confession that the thing has not happened and — by our own grammar — *is not happening.*

The statement of this element: **they built their virtue inside a conditional that, by its very nature, never becomes real — and the grammar they chose confessed it for them.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Threat With No Object — فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ

Now the turn. Three lines of boast, and then:

> فَكَفَرُوا۟ بِهِۦ ۖ فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ
> *Fa-kafarū bihi — fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn*
> "But they disbelieved in it — so they are going to know."

Feel the speed of it. After all the elaborate architecture of the wish — the *law,* the *anna,* the "with us," the "chosen servants of Allah" — the response is brutally compressed. **Fa-kafarū bihi.** "So they disbelieved in it." One verb. The *fa* at the front means *and then, and so, immediately as a result.* No gap. The reminder they begged for arrived — *bihi,* "in it," and the commentators are unanimous that "it" is the Qur'an, the very *dhikr* they had demanded — and the instant it came, the same mouths that swore devotion produced denial.

The boast took three verses. The betrayal took three words.

[PAUSE]

And now the part I want you to truly stay with. The verse ends:

> فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ
> *fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn* — "so they will know."

Two things here. First, **sawfa.** Arabic has two ways to point at the future: the little prefix *sa-,* which announces something near and soon, and the fuller word *sawfa,* which stretches the future out — drawn, distant, but all the more certain for the distance. In the mouth of the All-Powerful, addressed to those who mocked and denied, *fa-sawfa* is not a neutral forecast. It is the calm of someone who is in no hurry because the outcome is not in doubt. *You will know. Take your time. It is coming.*

Second — and this is the heart of it — look at the verb **yaʿlamūn.** "They will know." And then... nothing. **The object is missing.**

In Arabic, *ʿalima* — to know — normally takes an object. You know *something.* But here Allah says only: *they will know.* And closes the surah. The object is not withheld by accident. This is one of the most deliberate silences in the Qur'an: leaving the object unspecified forces the listener's imagination to supply it, and what the imagination supplies is always larger and more final than any words could name.

What will they know?

The full weight of the boast they made and broke. The distance between the servant of Allah they swore they would be and the denier they chose to become. The meaning of every prophet they saw summoned through this surah and vindicated, every nation they watched rise and fall across its long procession. The answer to the question the surah has been asking since Nuh was saved and his people drowned.

The object is left blank because no single object is large enough. *They will know* — and the sentence refuses to reduce that knowing to any one thing. It is a door left deliberately open onto a space the hearer must step into alone.

[PAUSE]

There is one final layer in the boast itself that deserves attention before we leave the linguistic terrain. The phrase they used to describe what they would be: **عِبَادَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ** — "the chosen servants of Allah." The word *mukhlaṣīn,* with the *fatḥa* on the *lām,* is the passive participle — not those who *are* sincere by their own effort, but those who have been **singled out, purified, and chosen** by Allah Himself. Al-Muyassar glosses the phrase as "purely devoted in faith and worship" — but the passive form claims even more than purity. It claims divine selection. They were not merely promising to try harder. They were forecasting that Allah would *elect* them, mark them out from all other servants, declare them His — *if only the condition were met.*

They spoke of divine election as if it were a reward already earned in advance, inside a hypothetical that had not arrived. And when the condition arrived, and the election was extended, they refused it.

## PART TWO: THE SELF THAT WAS NEVER TESTED

### The Architecture of the Imagined Self

Here is what the passage has now made undeniable: the Quraysh did not fail because they lacked faith. They failed because they had *already assigned themselves faith* — in a protected space where faith would never cost them anything, where the conditions for it would never arrive, where the promise could live forever in the safety of the unfulfilled hypothetical.

Their error was not ignorance. It was a particular kind of self-knowledge that is worse than ignorance: the certainty about who you would be in a world that does not exist.

[PAUSE]

The verb *kānū la-yaqūlūn* — "they **used to** say" — is a habitual past, not a single outburst. A repeated pattern. A rehearsed position. Which means they had said these words out loud, heard themselves say them, felt the claim confirmed by their own voice, perhaps been affirmed by those around them. The imagined self had become, in their own minds, as real as the actual one — perhaps more real, because the imagined self was unblemished. It never failed, never wavered, never had the morning come and the prayer not happen.

This is the precise mechanism of deferred virtue: the more times you say it without being tested, the more you believe it. The promise accretes weight. The hypothetical self gains definition. And when the actual test arrives, there is always a reason why *this* particular test does not count. *The reminder that came isn't quite the kind of reminder I imagined. The prophet isn't quite the figure I pictured. The Quran isn't exactly what I meant when I said a scripture from the former peoples.* The imagined condition was always more perfect than any real one could be.

[PAUSE]

What the Quraysh's case reveals — and what makes this passage terrifyingly personal — is that the imagined self is not merely a comfort. It is a **defense mechanism.** While it exists, the actual self cannot be indicted. You are never *really* a denier, never *really* complacent, never *really* someone who would turn away from Allah — because look, look at what you would do *if only.* The hypothetical functions as an alibi for the real.

This is why the structure of the surah's response is so precise. Allah does not argue with the theology of their claim. He does not say: *you were wrong to think you'd be chosen servants.* He simply records the boast — and then records the action. **Fa-kafarū bihi.** So they disbelieved in it. The gap between the claim and the conduct is left completely uncommented, completely unexplained. The silence between *"we would have been the chosen servants"* and *"they disbelieved in it"* is the verdict. No further analysis is needed. The contrast is the argument.

### The Mirror

Because this passage is a mirror.

Not for the Quraysh. They are gone. The mirror is for whoever reads it now.

What are the conditions you have named? What is the *law* that your faithfulness is waiting for? Not as an abstract question — but specifically, practically, honestly.

*If only my life were less chaotic, I would be more consistent in prayer.* Law — by the time you say it, the grammar confesses: I am not consistent now.

*If only I had grown up learning Arabic, I could really engage with the Quran.* Law — the condition has not arrived, and neither has the engagement.

*If only I had a community that supported me, I would be a different kind of Muslim.* Law — the community is not here, and neither is the identity that was waiting for it.

[PAUSE]

These are not the thoughts of bad people. They are the thoughts of every person who has ever tried to take themselves seriously as a spiritual being and found the gap between the self they imagine and the self they are to be genuinely painful. The imagined self is not mere vanity. It is sometimes the clearest picture you have of who you are supposed to be.

But the Qur'an has now shown us the danger. **The imagined self, left in the conditional, does not motivate. It insulates.** It is warm and it is comfortable and it is the most convincing lie the human heart manufactures, because it is built out of genuine longing. You do want to be better. You do mean it — in the world where the conditions are right. But that world is not where you live.

You live in the world where the Quran has already arrived. Where the reminder has come. Where the *dhikr* they said they wanted is in your hands — the very scripture they demanded and spurned, delivered to you across fourteen centuries — and the question it asks of you is not hypothetical.

The question the *law* asks of you is not: would you have believed if you had been tested then? The question is: what are you doing *right now,* in the conditions you have, with the Book that came?

Because *fa-kafarū bihi* means: the test was not hypothetical for them. It arrived. And the surah's terrifying implication is that the gap between *"I would have"* and *"I did"* is exactly one letter wide.

[PAUSE — major]

### The Mercy Hidden in the Warning

There is something this passage does not say, and the silence is intentional.

It does not say: therefore no one is forgiven. It does not say: the Quraysh represent the end of the story. It says *fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn* — they will know — and leaves the time and the content of that knowing entirely in Allah's hand.

The threat is real. The consequence is coming. But the surah has spent its entire length telling us about prophets who were vindicated *and* people who were destroyed — and the only thing that separated those outcomes was the decision made in the moment of the actual test, not the hypothetical one. And some among those who made the worst decisions in Mecca later turned. The boast was not destiny. The denial was not permanent. The Book they spurned became the Book they carried. Even *fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn* — even that deferred, unnamed knowing — can be the knowing of mercy rather than punishment, if the turn comes before the final accounting.

This is what makes the passage bearable to sit with. It is not a verdict. It is a warning about a pattern. And warning, by its very nature, is extended to those for whom the outcome is not yet fixed.

[PAUSE]

The *law* of your life has not closed yet. The condition you named — whatever it is — is not the real barrier. The *dhikr* has arrived. It is not merely *at hand* in some general sense — it is *ʿindaka,* with you, in the word you are reading right now. The question is the same one that the surah has been asking since the first prophet stood to deliver his message and was refused:

**What will you do now that it has come?**

## Closing: The Lām That Separates

We began with one letter. A single *lām* fused to the front of a word, holding the meaning of a sentence from tipping into its opposite. Remove it and you have negation. Keep it and you have the strongest possible affirmation. That is the lām al-fāriqa — the letter that separates.

The passage ends with people who fell on the wrong side of the very ambiguity their grammar described. The letter was there. The lām that could have held them in *indeed* failed to hold them in reality. Between "they most certainly said" and "they most certainly believed" lay the thinnest of margins — and they did not cross it.

But the lām is also a lesson for the listener. The distance between the soul that fulfills its promise and the soul that breaks it is not a canyon. It is not a vast unbridgeable gap. It is — in the metaphor the grammar itself offers — a single letter. A single decision. A single moment in which the actual condition arrives and you choose to meet it rather than wait for a better one, a cleaner one, a more convenient one.

The imagined self is compelling. The hypothetical faith is vivid and warm. But it lives in the mood of *law.* And the Quran, with precise mercy, has told you exactly what *law* means: the condition did not come, and so the result did not come.

Except that the condition has come. The *dhikr* the nations were waiting for — the scripture the angels need not wait for because they already stand in rows and glorify — is the one you are reading.

*Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.* They will know.

**Make it so that what you know is mercy.**
