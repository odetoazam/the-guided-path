# The Sound Heart and the Timeless Party

## Whose fire did you catch from?

---

### Introduction: The Portrait Gallery

Let me set the Arabic before you first, and then we'll walk into it slowly.

> وَإِنَّ مِن شِيعَتِهِۦ لَإِبْرَٰهِيمَ ۞ إِذْ جَآءَ رَبَّهُۥ بِقَلْبٍ سَلِيمٍ ۞ إِذْ قَالَ لِأَبِيهِ وَقَوْمِهِۦ مَاذَا تَعْبُدُونَ ۞ أَئِفْكًا ءَالِهَةً دُونَ ٱللَّهِ تُرِيدُونَ ۞ فَمَا ظَنُّكُم بِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*Wa inna min shīʿatihi la-Ibrāhīm. Idh jāʾa rabbahu bi-qalbin salīm. Idh qāla li-abīhi wa qawmihi mādhā taʿbudūn. A-ifkan ālihatan dūna-llāhi turīdūn. Fa-mā ẓannukum bi-rabbil-ʿālamīn.*

*"And indeed, among his party was Abraham — when he came to his Lord with a sound heart, when he said to his father and his people: 'What is it that you worship? Is it falsehood — gods other than Allah — that you desire? Then what is your assumption about the Lord of the worlds?'"*

Now picture where you are standing in the surah.

Surah As-Saffat is doing something deliberate. It is hanging portraits on a wall, one after another. Nuh. Then Ibrahim. Then Musa and Harun. Then Ilyas. Then Lut. Then Yunus. Each portrait has the same frame around it — most of them close with a salutation, *salāmun ʿalā* — *peace be upon* this one, *peace be upon* that one. It is a gallery of the rescued. The surah is making an argument the way a museum makes an argument: walk down this corridor, look at face after face, and notice that every single one of them was mocked, and every single one of them was right.

You have just left the room of Nuh. The flood. The ark. The handful saved while a world drowned. And the surah closes Nuh's portrait — and then immediately says: **wa inna min shīʿatihi la-Ibrāhīm** — *and indeed, among his party was Abraham.*

Here is the first strange thing, and I want you to hold the strangeness rather than smooth it over. Between Nuh and Ibrahim there are, by the reckoning that al-Jalalayn records, more than two and a half thousand years. Twenty-six centuries. Hud came in between. Salih came in between. Whole civilizations rose and turned to dust in the gap. And yet Allah reaches across that ocean of time and says of Ibrahim: *he was of Nuh's party.*

[PAUSE]

What does it mean to belong to someone you never met, who died before your great-great-great — keep going for a hundred generations — grandfather was born?

And then, before Ibrahim says a single word to anyone, before the famous arguments, before the smashed idols, before the fire that became cool and peaceful — before any of that — Allah tells us the one thing He wants us to know about him: **he came to his Lord with a sound heart.**

Not with arguments. Not with courage. With a heart.

Here is the hunger I want to plant in you before we begin. We are about to meet the greatest debater in the history of monotheism — a man who out-reasoned an entire civilization. And the Qur'an refuses to lead with his mind. It leads with his heart, and it leads with his lineage. Why? What is Allah telling us about where a person like this actually *comes from*? Hold that question. We are going to earn the answer.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: شِيعَة — The Kindling That Catches

Before we touch the word, let me give you an everyday picture, because the word will not open without it.

You have tried to start a fire. A real one — wood, not a switch. And you know that you cannot simply hold a match to a thick log. The log will not catch. It is too dense, too slow. So what do you do? You gather the small stuff first. The thin dry twigs, the kindling, the shavings. You lay the small pieces *against* the big log. You light the small. The small catches instantly — and *its* flame, sustained, is finally hot enough that the great log begins to burn. The little ones ignite the big one. And once the big one is burning, the whole thing is one fire. You can no longer say where the small wood ends and the log begins. It is all flame now.

Hold that image. Now we open the word.

Allah says **min shīʿatihi** — *of his shīʿah.* We translate it loosely as "his party," "his followers," "his kind." But where does the word come from?

The classical scholars give us a chorus of meanings, and I want you to hear them as layers, not as competitors. Ibn ʿAbbas says *shīʿah* here means **the people of his religion** — those of the same dīn. Mujahid says it means **those upon his path, his way, his sunnah** — those walking the same road. So far: same belief, same road.

But then al-Aṣmaʿī, one of the great early masters of the Arabic language, tells us where the word is actually *built* from. He says **shīʿah** is connected to **shiyāʿ** — and *shiyāʿ* is the name for exactly that small kindling wood, **the little pieces of firewood that are placed together with the large ones so that the whole takes light.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that does to the verse.

To be of someone's *shīʿah* is not merely to agree with them. It is to be **the kindling laid against their flame.** It is to be the small piece of wood that catches fire from the same source — and once you have caught, you are no longer separate from the blaze. You and the one whose fire you caught are now one fire.

Notice something this image refuses to let you keep. We tend to imagine influence as a chain of contact: I must have met you, studied under you, shaken your hand, for your fire to reach me. But fire does not work that way. Fire is not handed person to person like a baton. **Fire is caught.** A piece of kindling on the far side of the hearth catches from the same blaze as one lying right against the log. Proximity in space is not the point. Catching the same flame is the point.

And now the twenty-six centuries make sense.

Ibrahim never met Nuh. He could not have. An ocean of time separated them — al-Jalalayn names it as more than two and a half thousand years, with Hud and Salih and a drowned world in between. By every ordinary measure of belonging — same town, same tribe, same lifetime — Ibrahim had *nothing* to do with Nuh. And yet Allah says: **he was of his shīʿah. He caught the same fire.**

[PAUSE]

Belonging, in the language of revelation, is not a matter of when you lived. It is a matter of what you caught fire from.

Now there is a beautiful disagreement among the scholars here that I will not flatten for you, because the disagreement itself teaches. To whom does the "his" refer in *his party*? Most read it as **Nuh** — he is the one just mentioned, the portrait we just left, and al-Qurtubi says this is the more apparent reading precisely because of that: the surah just spoke of Nuh, so Ibrahim is being placed in *Nuh's* line. But al-Kalbi and al-Farrāʾ record another reading: that the "his" points forward to **Muhammad ﷺ** — that Ibrahim was of *the Prophet's* party, his kind, those who carry the same message.

Do not choose between them as if only one camera can be right. Look at what happens when you let both stand. Reading backward, Ibrahim is the kindling that caught Nuh's fire across twenty-six centuries. Reading forward, Ibrahim is himself the flame that, thousands of years later, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his community would catch from. **The same man is both — fire caught and fire passed on.** That is what a link in a chain of light actually is. It receives and it transmits. It is warmed by the one before and it warms the one after, and the heat is all one heat.

[PAUSE]

So when this verse says *of his party* — it is quietly drawing a single line of fire from Nuh, through Ibrahim, through every prophet in this gallery, all the way to the Messenger ﷺ, and — if you will let it — all the way to the small dry piece of wood that is you, lying somewhere on the edge of the hearth, wondering whether you have caught.

What this one word reveals: **your real lineage is not your bloodline and not your century. It is the fire you caught from.**

[PAUSE — let it settle]

### Element Two: قَلْبٍ سَلِيمٍ — The Heart That Stopped Turning Away

Let me again teach the concept before we use it, because two words here are quietly built from the *same idea*, and if you do not see the idea first, you will miss the architecture entirely.

The idea is **turning.**

In Arabic, the word for heart — *qalb* — comes from the root **q-l-b**, and that root means *to turn over, to flip, to overturn, to revolve.* This is not my invention; it is the plain lexical sense — the same root gives you *inqilāb*, an overthrow; *taqlīb*, the turning over of a thing in your hands; *munqalab*, the place you turn back to. The Arabs did not call the heart by a word for "feeling" or "center." They called it by a word for **the thing that keeps turning.** Because that is what the heart does. It turns. It is restless. It flips from intention to intention, from love to fear, from this morning's certainty to this evening's doubt. The organ at the core of you is named after its instability.

Now hold that, and receive the word Allah places beside it.

**bi-qalbin salīm** — *with a sound heart.* *Salīm* is from **s-l-m** — the very root of *Islām*, of *salām*, of *muslim*. Its core sense is **wholeness, safety, soundness, freedom from defect** — a thing that is intact, undamaged, at peace, surrendered. A *salīm* heart is one that is whole, unbroken, free of disease, secured.

Do you feel the tension Allah has built into just two words? He takes the organ whose very *name* means *the turner, the unstable one* — and He describes the highest possible state of it as **salīm: at rest, whole, safe.** The miracle of Ibrahim is not that he had a special organ other people lack. He had the same restless, turning heart you have. The miracle is that his turning had **come home.** The thing built to flip had stopped flipping away. It had turned, finally, in one direction and stayed.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, that Allah says *bi-qalbin salīm* — *a* sound heart — indefinite. Not *with his heart, which was sound.* The grammar makes it sound like a *kind* of heart, a quality being held up for inspection: he arrived carrying **this thing, a sound heart**, as though presenting it. And where did he carry it? **jāʾa rabbahu** — *he came to his Lord* with it. He did not come to his Lord with his accomplishments. He did not come with his arguments, his victories, his smashed idols, his survived fire. He came carrying one thing, cupped in his hands like an offering: a heart that was whole.

Why does that matter so much? Because the Qur'an tells us elsewhere, with terrifying clarity, exactly which currency is accepted at the end. *"The Day when neither wealth nor children will benefit — except one who comes to Allah with a sound heart"* (26:88-89). The same phrase. *Qalb salīm.* It is the *only* thing the verse says will pass through. Not wealth. Not even children. One thing clears customs on that Day, and Ibrahim is shown to us already carrying it — *idh jāʾa rabbahu bi-qalbin salīm* — he came to his Lord with it long before the Day came.

[PAUSE]

So what *is* a sound heart? The scholars describe it through what it is *free of* — free of shirk, free of doubt, free of the diseases of envy, rancor, and grudge, free of attachment to anything set up as a rival to Allah. But hold the kindling image from before and the turning image from just now, and you will feel a fuller answer. A sound heart is a heart whose turning has finished. It has caught the right fire and it no longer flips away from it. It is *salīm* — safe — because it is no longer being yanked in twenty directions by twenty false claimants on its love.

And here is where the architecture of this passage begins to glow — but I will not finish that thought yet. Keep one hand on this word, *turning*, because Allah is about to use it again, in its ugliest possible form, just two verses later. When He does, you will see that this whole passage is built on a single hinge.

What this word reveals: **the one thing you will carry to your Lord is the condition of your heart — and a sound heart is simply a heart that has stopped turning away.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: أَئِفْكًا — The Inverted World, and the Question Left Hanging

Now watch what Ibrahim does. And watch *how* he does it, because the method is the message.

He turns to his father and his people — the people he loves, the people who raised him — and he does not begin with a verdict. He does not say *you are wrong.* He asks a question: **mādhā taʿbudūn** — *what is it that you worship?*

Let me pause on the *form* of this. A prophet who already knows the answer is asking a question he doesn't need answered. Why? Because a question does something an accusation cannot. An accusation puts a wall up. A question puts a mirror up. *What is it, exactly, that you worship?* — said gently, it forces a person to actually *look* at the thing in their hands and describe it out loud. And the moment you have to describe a stone you carved yesterday as the "god" you bow to today, something in the saying of it begins to crack.

This is Ibrahim's whole method — he reasons *with* them, not *at* them. Elsewhere in the Qur'an we see the same man asking, *does it hear you when you call? does it benefit or harm you?* (26:72-73). He is a teacher who refuses to hand you the conclusion; he hands you the question and lets the conclusion detonate inside you. That is a mercy. The hardest truths are the ones we have to arrive at ourselves, and the kindest teacher is the one who walks you to the edge and lets you see.

Then comes the word I have been holding back. **A-ifkan ālihatan dūna-llāhi turīdūn** — *Is it falsehood — gods besides Allah — that you desire?*

**Ifk.** Hear it. أَئِفْكًا. The verse opens it with the hamza of questioning stacked right onto it — *a-if* — two glottal stops slammed together, a hard *k* landing at the end. It is a word you almost recoil from as you say it. It does not flow like *salīm* flowed — soft *s*, liquid *l*, humming *m*, the very sound of peace. *Ifk* is jagged. It sounds like rejection, like spitting something out. The phonetics are doing the theology: the sound of the word is the recoil from the thing.

And what does *ifk* mean? Its root, **ʾ-f-k**, means **to turn a thing away from its proper state — to overturn it, to invert it, to pervert it.** It is the same root that names the *muʾtafikāt* — the "overturned" cities of Lut, flipped upside down. *Ifk* is not just "a lie." It is **reality turned upside down.** It is taking what is true and flipping it on its head.

[PAUSE]

Now stand back and look at the hinge of this entire passage. I told you to keep one hand on the word *turning.*

In verse 84, Ibrahim comes to his Lord with a **qalb** — a heart, from *q-l-b*, *the turner* — and it is **salīm**, sound, its turning come to rest.

In verse 86, he names the sin of his people as **ifk** — from *ʾ-f-k*, *the inversion*, the world turned upside down.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The whole passage is built on the axis of *turning.* The sound heart and the idol are two states of the same restless organ. **A heart is a thing that turns. When it turns toward the truth and settles, it is *salīm* — sound, safe, whole. When it turns *away* from the truth, when it inverts reality and bows to what its own hands carved, that is *ifk* — overturning.** Shirk, in this passage's own vocabulary, is not first an error of the head. It is **the heart turning the wrong way.** It is the *qalb* doing what a *qalb* does — turning — but turning into an *ifk*, an inversion, instead of into *salām*, a peace.

That is why Allah led with the heart. Ibrahim could out-argue an empire because his heart had already turned the right way and stopped. The arguments were downstream. The fire he smashed and the fire that cooled for him — all of it flowed from a *qalb* that had become *salīm* and therefore could see clearly that the idols were *ifk* — the world stood on its head.

And one more thing about the grammar here, because it is exquisite. Look at the word order: **a-ifkan ālihatan dūna-llāhi turīdūn.** In ordinary order it would be *do you desire gods besides Allah, [which are] falsehood?* But Ibrahim does not put it in ordinary order. He drags the word **ifk to the very front**, before everything else, before he even names what the falsehood *is.* *Is it FALSEHOOD — [these] gods besides Allah — that you want?* He puts the inversion right in their faces first. The fronting is a spotlight. He will not let *falsehood* hide politely at the end of the sentence as a description; he makes it the first thing they have to look at. *You want — what? Falsehood. Let's start there.*

[PAUSE]

And then — the last verse. And here we must read an *absence,* because the silence at the end of this passage is the loudest thing in it.

**Fa-mā ẓannukum bi-rabbil-ʿālamīn** — *Then what is your assumption about the Lord of the worlds?*

*Ẓann* is assumption, opinion, conjecture, the picture you carry in your mind of how something is. And Ibrahim asks: **what is your *ẓann* about the Lord of all the worlds?** What do you imagine He is? What do you suppose He will do? When you have spent your life bowing to overturned reality, to carved falsehood — what exactly do you *think* about the One who is actually Lord of every world there is?

And then — nothing.

He does not answer it. The passage ends. The question hangs in the air, unanswered, and we are pulled into the next scene.

[PAUSE]

Read that absence carefully. Ibrahim gave them an argument they could resist — *what do you worship? is it falsehood you want?* — those they could argue back against. But the last question, the one about the Lord of the worlds, he does not finish *for* them. He cannot. Because the answer to *what do you think the Lord of all the worlds is, and what do you think He does with those who invert His reality and bow to stones* — the answer is too heavy to be *told.* It can only be *realized.* It is the kind of question that, if you actually let it land, you do not answer with your mouth. You answer with your whole life. The unfinished sentence is the trap closing. He has walked them, by questions, right up to the edge of the abyss of their own assumption about God — and then he steps aside and lets the silence do what no argument could.

What these verses reveal: **idolatry is reality turned upside down; the cure begins not by being told the truth but by being asked the one question you have been avoiding — and being left, mercifully, alone with it.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have watched three words open. *Shīʿah* — the kindling that catches the same fire across centuries. *Qalb salīm* — the restless heart whose turning has finally come home. *Ifk* — the same turning gone wrong, the world stood on its head, with a final question left deliberately unfinished.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words, but about the shape of a human life — about yours.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Only Thing You Are Allowed to Bring

Let me start where you actually live.

You are, right now, optimizing something. Almost everyone is. You have a thing you are trying to make sound — to make *salīm*, whole, free of defect. For some of us it is the body: the sleep score, the resting heart rate, the bloodwork, the protein. For some it is the career: the title, the trajectory, the next rung. For some it is money: the portfolio rebalanced, the runway extended, the number that means *safe.* We are a civilization of optimizers. We have dashboards for our steps and our savings and our screen time.

Now notice something. Of all the things we monitor, the one we monitor *least* is the one this verse says is the only one that travels. We track the heart's *beats per minute.* We do not track the heart's *direction.* We will spend an hour analyzing why we slept poorly and not one minute analyzing what our heart bowed to today.

And the Qur'an, with almost shocking economy, sweeps the whole dashboard off the table. *The Day when neither wealth nor children benefit* — there go two of the things we are most desperate to optimize, money and legacy, the portfolio and the bloodline, *neither of them* clears the gate — *except one who comes to Allah with a qalb salīm.* One item. One thing makes it through. And here is Ibrahim, the model, shown to us *already carrying it* — **jāʾa rabbahu bi-qalbin salīm** — he came to his Lord with a sound heart.

[PAUSE]

Let the strangeness of that land. We met Ibrahim expecting to be told about the greatest deeds of the greatest monotheist. And the headline Allah chose was not a deed at all. It was a *condition.* Not *what he did* — *what he was carrying.* Because in the end you do not bring Allah your résumé. You bring Him your heart, and everything else was only ever the heart's fingerprints.

Now, what does it actually mean for *your* heart to be *salīm*? Remember the root: whole, safe, intact, free of defect, surrendered. And remember the diseases the scholars named: shirk, doubt, envy, rancor, grudge. Put those together and a hard truth emerges. A sound heart is not a *busy* heart. It is not a heart with the most knowledge or the most spiritual experiences. It is a heart that is *free* — free of rivals, free of grudges, free of the small idols we don't even call idols. The colleague's success you cannot be happy about. The grudge you have rehearsed so many times it has its own armchair in your chest. The quiet certainty that your security comes from your salary and not your Lord. Each of these is a small turning-away. Each is the *qalb* doing its turning — and turning toward the wrong thing.

Here is the part that should comfort you and unsettle you at once. You were *built* to turn. The heart is *qalb* — the turner. So the goal was never to have a heart that doesn't move. The goal is a heart that has caught the right fire and *keeps turning home.* Soundness is not stillness. It is a thousand small corrections, all in the same direction. Every time you catch your heart turning toward a grudge and turn it back toward mercy — that is the *qalb* becoming *salīm.* Not once. Continuously.

[PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow? One concrete thing. We do a daily review of our calendars and our inboxes. Try a thirty-second review of your heart's *direction.* Tonight, ask one question — not *what did I do today*, but *what did my heart turn toward today, and was any of it an idol?* The grudge, the envy, the false security, the thing you feared more than you feared Allah. Name it. And turn it back. That is not a grand act of worship. It is just maintenance on the only thing you are allowed to bring.

### Theme Two: Whose Party Are You In?

Now the other thing you are, right now, whether you admit it or not: you are *lonely for a people.* 

It is one of the deepest aches of this era. We have more contact and less belonging than any humans who ever lived. We scroll past a thousand faces a day and know none of them. We are members of everything and *of* nothing. And so we reach for tribe wherever we can find it — the team, the brand, the political camp, the fandom, the comment section that finally agrees with us. We are starving to be *of someone's party.* To be *shīʿah* of *something.*

Hold that ache against this verse. **Wa inna min shīʿatihi la-Ibrāhīm** — *and indeed, of his party was Abraham.* Across twenty-six centuries. A belonging that did not require a shared lifetime, a shared land, a shared language, or a single meeting. Ibrahim belonged to Nuh the way kindling belongs to the log it caught fire from — not by contact, but by **catching the same flame.**

[PAUSE]

Do you understand what this offers you?

Your contemporaries are not your only possible people. The people lying next to you on the hearth — same office, same family table, same feed — may have caught a completely different fire, or no fire at all. And meanwhile there is a blaze that has been burning since Nuh, that Ibrahim caught, that every prophet in this surah's gallery caught, that the Prophet ﷺ caught and fanned into the brightest flame the world has seen — and that fire is *still lit.* It did not go out when the last prophet died. The Qur'an is the hearth where it still burns. And the question this verse puts to you is not *who are your friends* and not *what is your tribe.* It is: **whose fire did you catch from?**

Because that — not your passport, not your surname, not your generation — is the party you are actually in.

Notice the mercy folded into the kindling image. A small dry twig has no impressive qualities. It is not the log. It will never be the log. But laid against the flame, it catches *just as fully* — it becomes, for its size, the very same fire. You do not need to be a prophet to be of the prophets' party. You need only to be dry enough, close enough, and willing to catch. The smallest believer warming her hands at the same flame as Ibrahim is, in the only sense that lasts, *of his shīʿah.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the paradox I will not resolve for you, because it is meant to stay alive. To join this timeless party costs you, almost always, your immediate one. The very next thing Ibrahim does after being named of Nuh's party is to turn to **his father and his people** — *li-abīhi wa qawmihi* — the nearest people on his own hearth — and gently, lovingly, refuse the fire *they* were tending. He could not be of Nuh's party across twenty-six centuries without breaking, in some way, with the people across the dinner table. The fire you catch determines the party you are in — and sometimes the people you were born beside caught a different one. That is the loneliness no algorithm can fix and the belonging no death can sever, both at once.

So what changes tomorrow? Stop auditing your belonging by proximity and start auditing it by flame. Ask of the voices you let near your heart — the feeds, the friends, the inputs — *what fire is this one carrying, and do I want to catch it?* Because you are kindling. You *will* catch from whatever you lie closest to. The only choice you get is what you lie down beside. Lie down beside the hearth that has been burning since Nuh. Read the words where that fire still lives. You will catch. That is what kindling does.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now let me show you how the whole passage was built, because you can only see it now that we have done the work.

These five verses turn on a single hinge, and the hinge is the idea of **turning** itself.

- It opens with **belonging by caught fire** — *shīʿah*, the kindling — establishing that what you are joined to is whatever your heart has caught from.
- It moves to the **heart that turned and came home** — *qalb salīm* — the restless turner (*q-l-b*) brought to rest and wholeness (*s-l-m*).
- It names the disease as the **heart that turned and inverted** — *ifk* — reality stood on its head (*ʾ-f-k*), the same turning gone wrong.
- And it ends with the **unanswered question** — *fa-mā ẓannukum* — leaving you alone with the only assumption that matters.

The two roots at the center — *q-l-b* and *ʾ-f-k* — are *both* roots of turning. That is not a coincidence; it is the entire design. **The human heart is a thing that turns. Faith is that turning come home and made safe — *salīm*. Idolatry is that same turning made into an inversion — *ifk*.** Every heart is turning right now, including yours. The only question is *toward what.*

### Bringing It Together

And so the linguistic discoveries and the two themes turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The *qalb salīm* — the only thing you may carry to your Lord — and the *shīʿah* — the party you belong to by caught fire — are the **same reality.** Your heart turns toward a fire; the fire it catches becomes your party; the soundness of your heart simply *is* the rightness of the fire it caught and held. To have a sound heart is to be of the right party. To be of the right party is to have caught the fire that makes the heart sound. There are not two questions here. There is one: **what has your heart caught fire from, and has it stopped turning away?**

### Questions to Carry

1. If, like Ibrahim, the one thing recorded about you were not your deeds but *what your heart was carrying* — what would the record say you came with?
2. You are kindling lying beside something right now. What fire are you closest to — what are the daily voices and inputs your heart catches from — and is it the fire you would choose to become?
3. Where in your life is there an *ifk* — a small inversion you have made normal, something carved by your own hands that you quietly bow to?
4. Ibrahim's heart was *salīm* — free of grudge and rancor. Whose armchair is still sitting in your chest? What grudge have you let live there?
5. Ibrahim left them alone with one question: *what is your assumption about the Lord of the worlds?* If you answered honestly, not with your mouth but with how you actually live — what *is* your *ẓann* about your Lord?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The only thing you carry to your Lord is the turning of your heart — and a heart is *sound* when it has caught the timeless fire and stopped turning away, *overturned* when it bows to what its own hands carved.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to smash idols in a marketplace. The invitation is quieter and harder. It is to notice that your heart is, this very moment, turning — and to turn it, gently and again and again, toward the fire that has been burning since Nuh, that Ibrahim caught, that still burns in these words. You are kindling. You were made to catch. Lie down beside the right flame, and let it have you.
