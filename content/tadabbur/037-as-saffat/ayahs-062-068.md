# The Welcome They Chose

## Introduction: A Question Asked at a Banquet

Let me set the table for you — literally.

For nearly twenty verses before our passage, Surah As-Saffat has been doing something it rarely lets up on: it has been describing a feast. The people of Paradise are reclining on couches, facing one another. A cup is passed around — white, delicious, no headache in it, no exhaustion, no quarreling drunkenness. There are companions of modest gaze. There is conversation. And then, in the middle of all this ease, one of the people of Paradise leans forward and says something almost startling: *"I once had a close companion."* A friend. Back in the world. A man who used to say to him, *"Are you really one of those who believe? That when we die and become dust and bones, we will actually be brought to account?"*

And the believer, now in the Garden, looks down — the verse says he looks — and sees that very man in the midst of the Blaze. And he says: *"By Allah, you nearly ruined me."*

Hold that scene. A man at a banquet, remembering the friend who almost dragged him down, now watching the two roads end in two completely different places.

It is *into* this moment — the cups still being passed, the conversation still warm — that Allah turns and asks the question that begins our passage:

> [ayah:37:62] أَذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ نُّزُلًا أَمْ شَجَرَةُ ٱلزَّقُّومِ
>
> *Adhālika khayrun nuzulan am shajaratu-z-zaqqūm.*
>
> **"Is that better as a welcome-feast — or the tree of Zaqqum?"**

Now, here is what I need you to understand before we go a single word further, because the whole passage turns on it.

In Mecca, when this was revealed, the leaders of Quraysh had found something in the Quran they thought was a knockdown argument. The Quran had spoken of a tree that grows in the Fire. And they laughed. *A tree? In fire? Fire is the very thing that consumes trees. Muhammad tells us Hell burns everything, and then tells us a tree survives inside it — and not only survives, it bears fruit, and people eat from it.* It was reported that one of them, in open mockery, called for dates and butter and said to his companions, *"Come, let us 'zaqqum' ourselves"* — turning the terrible name into a punchline at a snack table.

Alongside that punchline ran a more formal theological objection, preserved in the classical commentary tradition: *"Your companion tells you there is a tree in the Fire — and fire consumes trees."* The logic sounded airtight. Worth pausing on, too: *zaqqum* was not a word the Quran invented. Al-Jalalayn records that it referred to actual plants already known in Arabia — bitter, vile shrubs native to Tihama, among the most repellent vegetation in the region. Take a word already saturated with that intensity of aversion and plant it inside a Fire said to devour everything, and the mockery wrote itself. The rational argument and the dining-table joke were the same test, in two different registers of contempt.

So this passage is not a quiet meditation dropped into empty air. It is Allah's response — calm, precise, and devastating — to people who turned His warning into a joke.

And here is the hook. I want you to notice that Allah does not answer their mockery by explaining the physics of the tree. He does not say *"here is how fire and wood can coexist."* He does something far stranger and far deeper. He takes the very word they would use for a generous host's table — and He hands it to the Fire.

Stay with me. By the end of this, I think you will see that the most frightening word in these seven verses is not *zaqqum*, and not *jahīm*, the Blaze. It is a small, warm, hospitable word that you would expect to hear at a wedding.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: نُزُل — The Word for a Guest's Welcome

Let me teach you a concept first, before we touch the verse.

Imagine a guest is traveling toward your home. They have been on the road. Before they arrive, you prepare something for them — not their whole stay, just the *arrival meal*. The thing that is set out the moment they cross your threshold so they know they are welcome. In many cultures this is sacred. You don't let a guest stand in the doorway. You bring out the best you have, immediately, as a sign: *you are received here, you are honored here, sit, eat.*

Arabic has a precise word for exactly this. **نُزُل** — *nuzul*.

The root is **ن-ز-ل** (n-z-l), and its core meaning is *to descend, to come down, to alight*. A traveler who stops for the night *yanzilu* — he "comes down" off his mount and settles. From this same root you get the place where you alight, and crucially, *the provision laid out for the one who has just alighted.* Al-Qurtubi, commenting on this very verse, defines *nuzul* in the language as **"the provision that has breadth and abundance to it"** — *ar-rizq alladhī lahu saʿah* — and he ties it to the expression *"the welcome was set up for the people."* It is the host's spread. The reception. The hospitality-feast.

Al-Jalalayn arrives at the same idea from a slightly different angle: *nuzul* is what is prepared for the one who alights, *"whether guest or otherwise."* Not only the invited; anyone who descends and lands finds something set out before them. The provision precedes the person.

So when Allah describes Paradise just before our verse and then asks **أَذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ نُّزُلًا** — "is *that* better as a *nuzul*" — He is calling the entire reward of the Garden by this one warm, social, human word. Paradise is being framed not as a wage you earned, not as a transaction, but as **the welcome-feast a Host sets out for an arriving guest.** Al-Muyassar captures it exactly: *"better as a reception and a gift from Allah."* The Garden is Allah's hospitality.

Let that land for a moment. The reward of the righteous is described in the language of *being welcomed home.* Of a door opening. Of a table already set because Someone was expecting you.

[PAUSE]

Now — notice what He does next. Watch closely.

**أَمْ شَجَرَةُ ٱلزَّقُّومِ** — *"or the tree of Zaqqum?"*

Grammatically, the word *nuzul* governs both halves of the question. *Is that better as a nuzul... or [is] the tree of Zaqqum [the better nuzul]?* Al-Baghawi makes the implied word explicit: the tree of Zaqqum is *"that which is the **nuzul** of the people of the Fire."* The reception of Hell. Its welcome-feast.

Do you feel what just happened?

The same word — *nuzul*, the host's loving spread for the arriving guest — is now placed over the Fire's table. The Fire, too, has a "welcome." The Fire, too, "receives" its arrivals. There is a meal waiting the moment they alight. **Hell is described as a host.**

This is not decoration. This is the most precise and most terrible move in the passage. Because the wrongdoers spent their lives refusing one invitation — and in refusing it, they did not become guestless. They simply accepted a different host. Everyone is received somewhere. Everyone alights somewhere. Everyone is handed a *nuzul.* The only question the verse forces — and it is unbearable in its simplicity — is: **whose welcome did you walk into?**

And then the cruelty of the irony deepens, because a *nuzul* is, by its nature, *the best of what the host has, offered up front, as honor.* So when the Fire's *nuzul* turns out to be a bitter, choking, devil-headed tree — the verse is telling you that this is the Fire's idea of *honoring its guests.* This is the welcome mat. It only gets worse from here.

One word. *Nuzul.* It does the work of an entire sermon. Hold onto it.

## Element Two: خَيْرٌ — The Comparison That Is Not a Comparison

Here is a second concept, and it is subtle, so let me build it slowly.

In English, when I ask *"is this better, or that?"* I am genuinely setting two things side by side and asking you to weigh them. The question implies the two are at least in the same category — both are options. *Is the train better, or the bus?* You can imagine choosing either.

The Arabic word **خَيْر** — *khayr* — functions here as a comparative: "better." (Arabic uses *khayr* and *sharr* as the comparative and superlative forms — "better/best" and "worse/worst" — without needing the usual *afʿal* mold; this is standard and documented.) So **أَذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ** is literally *"Is that better...?"*

Now feel the strangeness. Allah is asking: *Is the eternal Garden — the rivers, the cups, the companions, the peace, the face of your Lord — "better" than... a bitter tree that grows out of the floor of Hell?*

**Better?**

[PAUSE]

This is the rhetorical device at the heart of the verse, and Arabic rhetoric has long recognized it: the question whose answer is so obvious that the asking itself becomes the message. The two things are not actually comparable. There is no scale on which the Garden and the Zaqqum sit as two options. So why phrase it as a comparison at all? Why say *"better"* — as if Zaqqum had a case to make?

Here is what this invites us to consider. The verse is not really asking *us.* You and I, sitting here, do not need to be convinced that Paradise beats a tree of fire. The question is built to expose **the people who lived as though it were a real comparison.** Because that is exactly what the deniers did. With their *choices* — not their words, their choices — they answered this question backwards. Every time a man chose the cheap, the cruel, the arrogant, the heedless, over the patient and the true, he was casting a vote. He was saying, with his life: *I'll take the tree.* He didn't say it out loud. He may have laughed at the very idea of the tree. But his feet were walking toward it.

The verse that immediately follows — *"Indeed, We have made it a trial for the wrongdoers"* — reveals that this was by design. Classical commentators understood the choice as intentional: an image calculated to provoke the objection that it was impossible, so that those who chose their own reasoning over revelation would demonstrate, in that very choice, where they stood. Al-Jalalayn identifies the wrongdoers of that verse as specifically "the disbelievers of Mecca when they said: fire burns trees" — meaning the trial of the Zaqqum was not an argument that misfired. The scoff was the filter. And the wrongdoers sorted themselves.

So the word *khayr*, "better," is a mirror. It quietly asks: *if the choice is this obvious, then look at your life — which one have you actually been choosing?* The terror of the verse is not that the comparison is hard. It is that it is so easy, and people still get it wrong, every single day, with their hands and their tongues and their time.

Notice, too, the dignity in it. Allah does not mock them back. They mocked Him with dates and butter; He responds with a measured question that grants their "choice" the formal courtesy of being weighed. *Is that better — or this?* It is the calm of absolute certainty. You do not need to raise your voice when the answer is the floor of the universe.

## Element Three: كَأَنَّهُۥ رُءُوسُ ٱلشَّيَٰطِينِ — Describing the Unseen by the Unseen

Now the third feature, and this one is a marvel of how the Quran speaks about realities we have never met.

The verse describes the fruit of the tree:

> [ayah:37:65] طَلْعُهَا كَأَنَّهُۥ رُءُوسُ ٱلشَّيَـٰطِينِ
>
> *Ṭalʿuhā ka-annahu ruʾūsu-sh-shayāṭīn.*
>
> **"Its budding fruit is as if it were the heads of devils."**

*Ṭalʿ* is a beautifully specific word — it is the early budding spathe of a date palm, the sheath of new fruit just emerging, just *coming up* (the root carries the sense of *rising, appearing*). So even the tree's "produce," its emerging crop, is being pictured for us. And what is it compared to? *The heads of devils.*

Now stop and ask the question a careful reader must always ask: **how is this a description?**

Think about it. A description, a simile, is supposed to take something unfamiliar and anchor it to something you already know. *His courage was like a lion's.* You've seen — or can vividly imagine — a lion. The comparison lands because the second thing is more familiar than the first.

But here Allah describes the fruit of a tree no living human has seen... by comparing it to **the heads of devils** — which no living human has seen either. You have never seen a *shayṭān's* head. Neither had the Arabs of Mecca. So on the surface, this simile explains the unknown by means of the unknown. It should fail. It should tell you nothing.

And yet — does it?

[PAUSE — stay here]

Here is the wonder. It tells you *everything.* Because there is one thing every human heart already knows about the heads of devils without ever having seen one: **they are the very emblem of repulsion, malice, and horror.** The image works not by giving you a shape but by giving you a *feeling.* You don't need the data. You already recoil. The simile bypasses your eyes and goes straight to your dread. It hands you not a picture but a *response.*

And this is the deeper genius. The Quran is teaching you how the unseen world actually operates on the soul. The realities of the Hereafter are not less real for being unseen — they are *more* real, so real that the only honest way to convey them is to reach past your senses and grip your instincts directly. When the imagination has nothing to picture, the *heart* still knows. Some truths you were built to feel before you were ever shown.

There is something else hidden here, and the classical commentators noticed it. The Arabs *did* have, in their lore, frightening associations with certain ugly, poisonous desert plants they would liken to demonic things. So the verse meets them at the edge of their own imagination — and then pushes past it into the genuinely unseen. It uses the furthest reach of what they can imagine to gesture at what they cannot. That is mercy inside the warning: He speaks to them in images their own hearts already supplied, so that even in describing Hell, He is meeting them where they are.
