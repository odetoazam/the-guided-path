# The Call That Was Finally Answered

## Introduction: A Man Who Called for Nine Hundred Years

I want you to imagine a voice.

Not a young voice. A voice that has been calling so long it has worn grooves into the air. A voice that called at dawn and called at dusk, called in public and called in private, called openly and then called in whispers, called for a year, then ten, then a hundred, then five hundred — and the Quran tells us, in another place, that this calling went on for **nine hundred and fifty years**.

That is Nuh, peace be upon him.

[PAUSE]

We need to sit inside that number before we can hear this passage, because the whole of these eight ayat turns on a single reversal — and you will miss the reversal if you don't first feel the silence it broke.

For nearly a thousand years, Nuh *called*. He called to his people. And the Quran preserves, with almost unbearable tenderness, what that calling was like. *"My Lord, I have called my people night and day, but my calling only made them flee further... Every time I called them so that You might forgive them, they put their fingers in their ears and wrapped themselves in their garments."* (71:5-7). Fingers in the ears. Garments pulled over faces. A man speaking, and an entire civilization deliberately, physically, refusing to let the sound reach them.

Think about what that does to a person. Not for a week. Not for a year. For *centuries*. To open your mouth and have the world close its ears. To be the most patient man who ever lived and to watch your patience purchase, season after season, nothing but mockery. They laughed at him as he built the ship on dry land. They walked past and they jeered.

This is the world you must inhabit before the text opens. A lifetime — many lifetimes — of speaking into a wall.

And now read the first word Allah chooses to tell us about Nuh in this passage. It is not "Nuh built." It is not "Nuh preached." It is not even "Nuh believed."

It is: **وَلَقَدْ نَادَىٰنَا نُوحٌ** — *And Nuh called Us.*

[PAUSE]

The man whose calling was ignored for nine and a half centuries — turns. And he calls in a different direction. He stops calling the deaf and calls the One who hears. And what happens in the very next breath of the ayah is the thing I want us to spend this hour with.

Because Allah does not say "and We heard him." He does not say "and We answered." He says something stranger, something that breaks the frame of the sentence open:

**فَلَنِعْمَ ٱلْمُجِيبُونَ** — *and how excellent are We as Responders.*

Here is the hook. Allah is about to tell the story of a rescue — the Flood, the Ark, the drowning of a world. But before any of that, He pauses the narration to *praise Himself for answering*. Why? Why would the One who owns all power stop, mid-story, to celebrate the act of responding to a single old man's call?

Stay with that question. It is the door into everything.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: نَادَىٰنَا — The Call That Reaches

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the *form* of this verb is carrying weight that the translation cannot.

In Arabic, a simple action and a *directed, reciprocal* action often look different. There is a verb-pattern — scholars call it Form III, the pattern of *mufāʿala* — that takes a plain action and bends it toward another party. It builds directionality and relationship into the very shape of the word. The plain verb *kataba* means "he wrote." But shift it to this third form, *kātaba*, and now it means "he corresponded with someone" — writing that is *aimed*, that expects a reader, that lives in a relationship between two parties.

Now hold that, and look at the verb Allah uses for Nuh's call: **نَادَىٰ** — *nādā*, from the root *n-d-w*. This is precisely that third form. It is not the bare verb for making a sound. It is the form of calling that is *directed at someone*, that reaches across a distance toward a specific listener, that presumes a relationship on the other end.

Let's sit with this.

For centuries, the same root of "calling" described Nuh's relationship to his people — he called *to* them, *at* them, *toward* them. And the tragedy was that the call was directed but never *received*. He aimed his voice and it fell, every time, into ears stuffed with fingers. The directionality was real; the arrival was not.

And then here, in this ayah, the same shape of verb — directed, aimed, reaching toward a listener — is pointed in a new direction. **نَادَىٰنَا.** He aimed his call at *Us*. And notice what Arabic does that English cannot: the object is fused right onto the verb. *Nādā* + *-nā*. "Called-Us." There is no gap between the calling and the One called. The grammar itself closes the distance. In one word, the voice and its destination are pressed together with no space between them.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The whole passage could have begun, "When the people of Nuh denied, We drowned them." That is the *event*. But Allah does not begin with the event. He begins with a *relationship* — a directed call landing on its destination. The grammar tells you, before any content arrives, that this is a story about a voice that finally reached its target.

And now read what is **not** there.

Look at the ayah again: *And Nuh called Us, and how excellent are We as Responders.* Where is the *content* of the call? What did Nuh actually *say*? The ayah does not tell us. It is withheld.

This is not an accident, and it is not a gap in Allah's knowledge. Elsewhere in the Quran the words are given — *"My Lord, I am overcome, so help me"* (54:10); *"My Lord, do not leave upon the earth a single dweller from among the disbelievers"* (71:26). The classical commentators, al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi among them, fill in this very content here. So the words exist. Allah *chose* not to place them in this sentence.

Why withhold them? Because in *this* telling, the content is not the point. **The arrival is the point.** Strip away what was said, and what is left is the pure fact: a call went up, and it landed. After nine hundred and fifty years of aiming his voice at people who would not receive it, Nuh aimed it once at Allah — and the next word in the universe was a response. The silence around the words makes the answering louder.

Al-Jalalayn, working in his characteristically compact register, identifies the specific cry: Nuh called with the words *"My Lord, I am overcome, so help me"* (54:10). Al-Tabari reads the summons more broadly, as the full arc of Nuh's petition that runs through Surah Nuh — the night-and-day calling, the closed ears, the garments pulled over faces — culminating in his appeal for divine judgment on those who had refused for centuries to relent. Either way, the content lives elsewhere in the Quran. Its absence here is precise and deliberate: in this telling, Allah wants the eye not on what was asked, but on the naked fact that the asking arrived.

This is what this single verb-form reveals: *some calls are directed but never received; and there is One toward whom no directed call ever fails to arrive.*

## Element Two: فَلَنِعْمَ ٱلْمُجِيبُونَ — When the Answerer Praises the Answer

Now we come to the phrase I promised would break the sentence open. Let me again teach the structure before we feel it.

Arabic has a special category of verb that exists *only to praise*. The most famous is **نِعْمَ** — *niʿma* — from the root *n-ʿ-m*, the same root as *niʿma*, "blessing," and *naʿīm*, the bliss of Paradise. *Niʿma* is not a normal verb that describes an action in time. It is a verb of *commendation* — it means, roughly, "how excellent is...!" It is the grammar of applause. When an Arab wanted to hold something up and say *this — this is the finest of its kind* — they reached for *niʿma*.

And Allah prefaces it with two intensifiers stacked together: **فَـ** (the *fa* of immediate consequence — "and so, at once") and **لَـ** (the *lām* of emphatic affirmation — "truly, certainly"). *Fa-la-niʿma.* "And so — truly — how excellent...!"

Now feel the shape of what is happening. Nuh calls. And the response is not described as a transaction. It erupts as **praise** — and the One doing the praising is *Allah praising Himself*.

**فَلَنِعْمَ ٱلْمُجِيبُونَ** — *and how excellent are We as Responders!*

[PAUSE]

Let this land. We are used to the creature praising the Creator. The whole of worship is the small one extolling the Great. But here the direction inverts. Here Allah, who needs nothing, who is praised by every atom of existence, stops the narrative to commend *His own act of answering a servant's call*.

The word for "Responders" is **ٱلْمُجِيبُونَ** — *al-mujībūn*, from the root *j-w-b*. And this, too, is a built-up form — Form IV, the pattern *ajāba*, the verb that means specifically *to answer a call, to respond to a summons*. It is the precise counterpart to *nādā*. Nuh *called* (*nādā*); Allah is the One who *answers the call* (*mujīb*). The two words are made for each other — a question-shape and an answer-shape, locked together across the ayah like a key in a lock. And indeed, *al-Mujīb*, "The Responder," is one of the names by which Allah is known.

Now here is what's remarkable, and you have to slow down to see it. **The plural.** *Al-mujībūn* is plural. "We are the best of Respond-*ers*." This is the plural of divine majesty — the same "We" that runs through the passage, *najjaynāhu* (We saved), *jaʿalnā* (We made), *taraknā* (We left). It is not many gods; it is the single God speaking in the register of overwhelming greatness. But notice what the plural *does* here. It makes the responsiveness feel *vast*. As if all the resources of all the heavens stood ready, and the moment one old man's voice went up, the entire majesty of the Divine turned toward it and answered.

Al-Tabari's gloss on the phrase preserves a temporal precision that deserves to be heard: *"how excellent were We as Responders to him when he called Us, for We answered him."* When he called — not after he called, not eventually, not at the appointed time. The answering is grammatically coincident with the calling. In the classical reading there is no gap, no interim silence, no waiting period imposed between the cry and its reception. From the divine side, the call and its answer are a single movement. Which makes the praise stranger still: this is not celebration of a response that finally came after a delay. It is celebration of a responsiveness so total that arrival and answering exist, from Allah's side, as one.

[PAUSE]

Now ask the question from the introduction again, because we are finally ready for it. *Why would Allah pause a story of cosmic power to praise Himself for answering?*

Here is what I think this invites us to consider. Power that *acts* is impressive. But power that *responds* is intimate. Anyone with might can flood a world. Only a Lord who *cares* stops to answer the one who called. Allah is not, in this ayah, celebrating that He *can* drown a civilization. He is celebrating that He *answers*. Of all the things He could highlight about Himself in this moment — His might, His justice, His irresistible decree — He chooses to spotlight the attribute of *being reachable*. The best thing about this God, the thing worth a verb of praise, is that **when you call, He responds**.

What this single phrase reveals: *the most exalted thing the Most High says about Himself, in the moment of rescuing a prophet, is not that He is powerful — but that He is the One who answers.*

## Element Three: ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ and ٱلْءَاخَرِينَ — One Letter Between the Remembered and the Othered

This last element is subtle, and it is the kind of thing that lives in the *sound* of the Arabic and vanishes the instant you translate it. So let me ask you to listen with your ears, not just your mind.

The passage closes with two destinies, and Allah names them with two words that are *almost the same word*. Watch.

[ayah:37:78] وَتَرَكْنَا عَلَيْهِ فِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرِينَ

[ayah:37:82] ثُمَّ أَغْرَقْنَا ٱلْـَٔاخَرِينَ

*Al-ākhirīn.* *Al-ākharīn.* Say them aloud. They are built from the very same root — *a-kh-r*, the root of "afterness," of what comes last or behind. They differ by a single vowel. One letter of difference between them. And yet they open onto two completely opposite eternities.

[PAUSE]

**ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ** — *al-ākhirīn* — means *those who come after*: the future, the later generations, all the ages that would follow Nuh down to the end of time. And what does Allah "leave" for Nuh among them? The ayah does not even say the word at first — it is, again, a meaningful absence: *We left, upon him, among the later ones...* — left *what*? And then the next ayah delivers it: **سَلَٰمٌ عَلَىٰ نُوحٍ** — *"Peace be upon Nuh."* That is what was left. A greeting of peace, planted in the mouth of every generation that would ever come.

**ٱلْءَاخَرِينَ** — *al-ākharīn* — means *the others*: the remaining ones, the leftover people who rejected him. And what is left for *them*? Nothing. Not a name. Not a word. Just: *We drowned the others.* They are not even granted an identity. They are "the others" — *al-ākharīn* — the anonymous remainder, swallowed by the water and by the sentence both.

Al-Muyassar, in its gloss on the rescue of ayah 76, names what was saved with a precision this passage itself leaves implicit: *"him and his family and the believers with him"* — delivered from the harm of the rejectors and from the drowning in the great flood both. This matters for the two words we are holding. "The others" drowned in ayah 82 are not merely Nuh's enemies by temperament or tribe. They are those who chose not to be among the believers, who stood outside the circle of faith the Ark carried across the water. Their namelessness is not incidental to the rhetoric — it mirrors a choice they made. They refused to enter the community that preserves names, and so the Quran, with exact justice, preserves none of theirs.

Do you hear the architecture of it? **One root. One letter of difference. Two eternities.** The man they mocked is *al-ākhirīn*'s inheritance — a blessing whispered down the centuries. The civilization that mocked him is *al-ākharīn* — "the others," drowned, nameless. The near-identical sound makes the opposite fate ring all the louder.
