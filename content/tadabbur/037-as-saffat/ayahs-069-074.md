# Driven by Footprints, or Chosen by God

### As-Saffat 37:69–74

> إِنَّهُمْ أَلْفَوْا آبَاءَهُمْ ضَالِّينَ ۝ فَهُمْ عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ يُهْرَعُونَ ۝ وَلَقَدْ ضَلَّ قَبْلَهُمْ أَكْثَرُ الْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا فِيهِم مُّنذِرِينَ ۝ فَانظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُنذَرِينَ ۝ إِلَّا عِبَادَ اللَّهِ الْمُخْلَصِينَ

*Innahum alfaw ābāʾahum ḍāllīn — fahum ʿalā āthārihim yuhraʿūn — wa laqad ḍalla qablahum aktharu-l-awwalīn — wa laqad arsalnā fīhim mundhirīn — fanẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu-l-mundharīn — illā ʿibādallāhi-l-mukhlaṣīn.*

**"Indeed, they found their fathers astray, so in their footsteps they are rushed along. And certainly, before them, most of the ancients had gone astray, though We had surely sent warners among them. So look how was the end of those who were warned — except the chosen servants of Allah."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Room Where This Is Spoken

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand in the room where these verses are being spoken.

We are deep inside Surah As-Saffat — *The Ranged Ones* — a surah that opens with rows of angels standing in disciplined ranks, swearing by their own order. And then the surah does something it will do again and again: it takes us to the Day of Judgment and lets us *overhear* the people of the Fire. We hear them talking. We hear them arguing. We hear them turning on one another.

Just before our passage, Allah has described the tree of Zaqqum — the tree that grows from the bottom of the Hellfire, its fruit like the heads of devils — and the boiling drink, and the people forced to eat from it. It is one of the most physically vivid passages of torment in the whole Quran.

And then — right at the peak of that horror — Allah pulls the camera back and asks a single question. Not *what* is happening to them. **Why.** What did these people actually *do* to arrive here?

[PAUSE]

And here is what is so arresting. He does not say: *they murdered, they stole, they oppressed.* He does not list a catalogue of crimes. He gives us one diagnosis, almost gentle in its phrasing, and devastating in its content:

*"Indeed, they found their fathers astray — so in their footsteps they are rushed along."*

That's it. That's the root. Not a dramatic rebellion. Not a clenched fist raised against the heavens. Something quieter. Something that feels, honestly, far too ordinary to land someone in the Fire. They simply **inherited** a direction and **kept walking in it**.

Now here is the strand I want you to hold onto, because it runs like a golden thread through this entire surah. As-Saffat keeps returning to one phrase. After story after story — after Nūh, after Ibrāhīm, after Mūsā, after Ilyās — Allah keeps carving out one exempt group with the exact same words: *ʿibād Allāh al-mukhlaṣīn* — **the chosen, purified servants of Allah.** It appears in verse 40. In verse 74, our verse. In verse 128. In verse 160. Four times. It is the refrain of the entire surah. It is the door that keeps appearing in the wall of ruin.

[PAUSE]

So here is the hook. Here is what I need you to be hungry for.

In this short passage, there are two words — only two — that share a single hidden grammatical feature. One describes the people of the Fire. The other describes the saved. And when you see what that shared feature is, you will understand something about freedom, inheritance, and salvation that you may have spent your whole life getting backwards.

Both of these groups — the damned and the saved — are described as being *acted upon*. Neither is the one doing the verb. Both are, grammatically, **passive.**

Stay with me. Let's earn it.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Alfaw*: The Word for Inheriting Without Choosing

Let me teach you something before we open the word.

In English, we have a difference between two kinds of "finding." There's the *finding* of a scientist who searches for years and finally discovers a cure — active, effortful, earned. And there's the *finding* of a child who wakes up one morning and "finds" that the sky is blue, that water is wet, that the people in the house are his family. He didn't discover any of it. It was simply *there* when his eyes opened. He came upon a world already arranged.

Arabic has words that sit on both sides of that line. There is a word for finding-after-seeking. And there is a word for coming-upon-what-is-already-there.

Now look at how Allah describes these people:

> إِنَّهُمْ أَلْفَوْا آبَاءَهُمْ ضَالِّينَ
> *innahum **alfaw** ābāʾahum ḍāllīn*

The verb is **أَلْفَوْا — *alfaw*.** Its root is *lām-fāʾ-wāw*. And the classical commentators are completely united on its meaning. Al-Jalālayn glosses it simply: *wajadū* — "they found." Al-Baghawī, the same: *wajadū*. Al-Qurṭubī adds the crucial texture — *ṣādafūhum kadhālik fa-qtadaw bihim* — **"they came upon them in that state, and so they imitated them."**

[PAUSE]

*Ṣādafū* — they *came upon*. This is not the finding of a seeker. This is the finding of someone who simply opened his eyes and his fathers were already there, already facing a direction, already walking. *Alfaw* is the verb of the inherited world. The world that is handed to you before you are old enough to ask whether it is true.

Al-Muyassar, characteristically spare, names what the fathers were upon with a precision worth pausing over: *al-shirk wa-l-ḍalāl* — shirk and misguidance. Not merely a vague wandering. Not a confused search that veered off course. The fathers were not morally uncertain in some general way; they were engaged in the specific theological collapse of associating partners with God. And this — this particular orientation, this named error — is what *alfaw*, what these people *came upon*. A world already pointed away from the One, with the architecture of association built into its very foundations before they drew a single breath.

Notice what is **absent** here. This is the kind of gap the Quran wants us to feel.

There is no verb of investigation. There is no moment where they *weighed*, *questioned*, *examined*, *tested*. The Quran does not say "they considered their fathers' way and chose it." It says they *found* it — and then immediately, the next word —

> فَهُمْ عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ يُهْرَعُونَ
> **fa**hum...

That **فَ — *fa*** at the start of verse 70 is the *fāʾ* of immediate consequence. In Arabic it means "and so, instantly, with no gap." Finding → following. There is no space between the two. No pause for reflection. No night spent wondering. The *fa* slams the door on the very possibility of thought.

Let's sit with this, because it is the indictment in a single letter. What Allah is showing us is not a people who reasoned their way to a wrong conclusion. That, at least, would be the failure of an active mind. This is something the mind never even touched. They *came upon* a direction and were *instantly* moving in it. The faculty Allah gave them — the faculty of *naẓar*, of looking and weighing, which He will command directly in just three verses (*fanẓur* — "so **look**") — that faculty was never switched on.

**What this single word reveals:** their misguidance was never chosen. It was *received*, and never once examined. And a thing you never examined is a thing you never truly owned — yet, terrifyingly, you will still answer for it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Yuhraʿūn*: The Passive of Being Driven

Now we come to the first of our two hidden words. And to feel it, you need one small piece of grammar — but I promise it will be worth it.

In every language there is a difference between the *active* voice and the *passive* voice. "I closed the door" — that's active; I am the one doing it. "The door was closed" — that's passive; something happened *to* the door. The door is not the doer. The door is the receiver of the action. And often, in the passive, *the doer disappears entirely.* "The door was closed" — by whom? The sentence doesn't say. The agent is hidden.

Arabic marks this beautifully, by changing the vowels inside the word itself. And Allah does something extraordinary here. Watch:

> فَهُمْ عَلَىٰ آثَارِهِمْ يُهْرَعُونَ
> *fahum ʿalā āthārihim **yuhraʿūn***

The word **يُهْرَعُونَ — *yuhraʿūn*** — is in the **passive voice.** It is built, as the grammarians say, *mabnī lil-majhūl* — constructed for the unknown agent. The form is not "they rush." It is **"they are *being rushed* — they are *driven* — they are *hurried along*."**

Feel the difference. The Quran does not say *they hasten in their fathers' footsteps*, as though they were eager joggers choosing their pace. It says they **are being driven** down those footsteps. Something is pushing them. Some force has its hand at their back, and they are stumbling forward whether they will it or not.

And the lexicon makes it even more vivid. The root *h-r-ʿ*, in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, carries the meaning of *al-sawq al-shadīd* — fierce driving — being herded forward at speed. Al-Jalālayn opens the word from a different angle entirely: *yuza'jūn ilā ittibāʿihim fayusriʿūn ilayh* — **"they are disturbed, agitated, toward following them, and so they hasten to it."** The verb *az'aja* carries the specific sense of making someone so unsettled, so *displaced* from rest, that forward motion into the crowd becomes the path of least resistance. This is conformity pressure named in its innermost mechanism: not the whip of explicit command but the low-grade agitation of being out of step, of watching everyone around you walk in one direction while you stand still — until the discomfort of standing still exceeds the discomfort of following. *Iz'āj.* You were made restless. And so you ran. *(Some of the early authorities also note a shade of trembling or shaking in the root's lexical range — the way someone is pushed who has lost control of their own legs — though the core attested meaning the mufassirūn rest on is being driven swiftly forward.)*

[PAUSE]

Now place the two verses side by side and watch the picture complete itself:

> *They **found** their fathers astray — so down their fathers' tracks they **are being driven**.*

What are they being driven down? **آثَار — *āthār*.** Footprints. Tracks. Traces.

Stay with that word for a moment, because it is doing quiet, devastating work. You can only follow the *footprints* of someone who has already walked — and walked *away*. Footprints are what a person leaves *behind* them as they depart. The fathers are *gone.* They have walked off the edge of the world. And these people are running — being *driven*, being *agitated into forward motion* — along the indentations in the sand left by men who are no longer there to ask, no longer there to defend the path, no longer there to say whether it ever led anywhere at all.

They are chasing the traces of the departed. At full speed. With a hand at their back. And no one — grammatically *no one*, because the agent is hidden — is named as the one pushing.

[PAUSE]

**What this word reveals:** the people of the Fire are not portrayed as defiant masters of their own rebellion. They are portrayed as the *driven*. As people whose legs are moving but whose will was never consulted. The most chilling thing about misguidance, the Quran is telling us, is how little of it feels like a decision.

### Element Three — *Al-Mukhlaṣīn*: The Passive of Being Chosen

And now — after all that — we reach the second hidden word. The word that turns the whole passage on its hinge.

Allah has shown us the warners sent (*arsalnā fīhim mundhirīn*), and the end of those who were warned (*ʿāqibatu-l-mundharīn*), and then He commands us — *fanẓur* — **look** — at how that end turned out. And then, the very last word of the passage, the door in the wall:

> إِلَّا عِبَادَ اللَّهِ الْمُخْلَصِينَ
> *illā ʿibādallāhi **al-mukhlaṣīn***

**"Except the *mukhlaṣīn* servants of Allah."**

The root is *kh-l-ṣ* — purity, the state of a thing being clear of all admixture. Pure gold. Pure water. And the form here is Form IV, *akhlaṣa* — to make a thing pure, to purify it, to select it out and devote it entirely.

Now — here is the entire passage, hinging on a single vowel.

In the recitation we recite — the reading of Ḥafs — the *lām* in this word carries a *fatḥa*: **al-mukhla**ṣ**īn.** And that single vowel makes the word a **passive participle.** Not *mukhli**ṣ**īn* — "the ones who *make themselves* pure" — active. But *mukhla**ṣ**īn* — **"the ones who *have been made* pure. The ones whom Allah Himself purified and chose."** *(Some of the qurrāʾ do read the active* mukhliṣīn*; classical commentary holds both as true facets — the servant strives for sincerity, and Allah is the One who grants and seals it. But the reading before us, and the one the lexical form points to, is the passive: those whom Allah selected.)*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

Go back to *yuhraʿūn.* **Passive.** They *are driven.*
Now look at *al-mukhlaṣīn.* **Passive.** They *are purified.*

**Both groups are acted upon. Neither is the doer of their own defining verb.**

The people of the Fire did not, in the end, drive themselves — they *were driven*, herded down the tracks of the dead. And the people saved from the Fire did not, in the end, purify themselves into safety — they *were purified*, selected out, claimed by the hand of God.

[PAUSE]

This is the architecture of the entire passage, and we will return to it. But hold it now, freshly, as the grammar just delivered it: **the human being, in this passage, is never the unmoved mover of his own destiny. He is always being moved. The only question — the entire question — is by *what*.**

---

### The Bridge

Look back at what these three words have built.

*Alfaw* — they came upon a direction they never examined.
*Yuhraʿūn* — and were driven down it, passively, at speed, by an unnamed and unnamed force.
*Al-mukhlaṣīn* — while the saved were, just as passively, purified and chosen by a named hand: Allah's.

The grammar has shown us something the surface translation completely hides: that this is not, at its deepest level, a story about *choosing* the wrong thing. It is a story about *being moved.* About the forces that propel a human life forward when the human himself has gone to sleep at the wheel.

## PART TWO: WHAT THE TWO PASSIVES REVEAL

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about the people of the Fire, not just about an ancient tribe in the Arabian Peninsula — but about the structure of the human situation itself.

[PAUSE]

There is a question about freedom buried at the center of this passage, and the Quran has not buried it carelessly. It is buried the way a seed is buried — not hidden, but placed precisely so that something can grow.

The question is this: if the people of the Fire were **passive** — driven, herded, thrust forward by inherited momentum — in what sense are they accountable? And if the mukhlaṣīn are equally **passive** — chosen, purified, selected by Allah — in what sense did their faith cost them anything?

This is not a new question. The early scholars heard it in these very words. And the Quran does not answer it by resolving the tension. It answers it by *sharpening* it — by placing two passives side by side and commanding you, in the space between them, to *look.*

> فَانظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَاقِبَةُ الْمُنذَرِينَ
> *fanẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu-l-mundharīn*

**"So look how was the end of those who were warned."**

[PAUSE]

*Fanẓur.* **Look.** Second person singular. Not a collective address. Not "O people." **You.** The one reading. The one listening. At this precise moment in the passage — between the passive of being driven (*yuhraʿūn*) and the passive of being chosen (*al-mukhlaṣīn*) — Allah inserts a **command to your faculty of active sight.**

And here is what I want you to feel about that placement. The warners (*mundhirīn*) were sent — this is Allah's testimony about Himself, sealed with the oath-particle *laqad* — and the people had a choice about what to do with a warner. They could *naẓar*. They could look, weigh, examine, be disturbed and sit with the disturbance rather than run from it into the crowd. They could let the warner interrupt the *iz'āj*, the low-grade agitation of conformity, long enough to ask: wait. Where does this track lead?

The *fanẓur* is addressed to you, the reader, because you are being told: **this faculty is still live in you.** The warner has come. The book is in your hands. The only question is whether you will do what the people of the Fire did not do — pause. Look. Actually look.

Ibn Kathir, commenting on verse 71, draws the line with precision: most of the ancients went astray because they worshipped other gods alongside Allah. The error of the fathers was not vague moral confusion. It was a specific theological orientation — the singularity of God diluted with other allegiances, precisely what al-Muyassar identified in the single word *ḍāllīn*: they were upon *shirk*. And the sons inherited it without ever asking: who told you this? Who verified it? When did anyone in this chain actually stop and examine the direction they were all facing?

[PAUSE]

The warner is the interruption in the chain. Every prophet Allah sent — and He swears He sent warners among all of them (*wa laqad arsalnā fīhim mundhirīn*, the *laqad* making this a sworn testimony) — was, in structural terms, a moment where the *iz'āj* of conformity was given a competitor: the *iz'āj* of truth. A disturbance of a different kind. Not the low-grade unsettlement of being out of step with the crowd, but the acute disturbance of encountering something that reaches your *fiṭra*, your primordial nature, and says: *you already know this is true.*

The tragedy is that this sharper disturbance was rejected. The *iz'āj* of following the fathers won. And the passive of being driven swallowed any opening the passive of being chosen might have found.

## PART THREE: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE EXCEPTION

But there *were* exceptions. There always were. And the Quran does not let this pass without marking it.

*Illā* — "except." One word that cracks the universality open. Most of the ancients went astray. The people driven down their fathers' footsteps came to ruin. *Except* — and here comes the phrase that appears four times in this surah like a recurring door —

*ʿibād Allāhi al-mukhlaṣīn.*

And we are back to the question: what made them different?

[PAUSE]

We have established that *al-mukhlaṣīn* is a passive participle — these are not people who purified themselves into eligibility. They are people who were purified, selected, claimed. The hand that did this belongs to Allah. The verb makes this non-negotiable.

But let us not be careless here, because the Quran is not.

Some of the reciters read not *mukhlaṣīn* (passive: those whom Allah purified) but *mukhliṣīn* (active: those who themselves sincerely devoted). Classical commentary holds both as truthful facets of the same reality — the servant strives for sincerity, Allah is the One who grants it and seals it. The two readings are not in contradiction; they map onto the doctrine of *tawfīq* — that human striving and divine grant are two sides of one coin, neither sufficient without the other.

This saves us from a misreading that the passage itself would reject. The *mukhlaṣīn* were not passive in the way that stones are passive — inert, unresponsive, acted upon without any inner movement. They were passive the way a person who has *opened their hands* is passive: they did the part that was theirs to do — the turning, the striving, the *naẓar*, the sitting with disturbance rather than running from it — and then they received. The choosing happened. But it happened *to someone who had already stopped being driven.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the structure underneath the structure.

The people of the Fire were passive in the way that **objects** are passive. A stone rolling down a hill is passive. It did not examine the hill. It did not weigh the direction. It moved because the slope was there and the momentum was there and no force ever interrupted the roll.

The *mukhlaṣīn* were passive in the way that **recipients** are passive. A person receiving a gift is passive in the moment of receiving — the gift comes to them, they do not manufacture it. But something prepared them to receive it. Something in them, by grace, remained *open.* They did not run from the warner. They let the *iz'āj* of truth land.

Both are acted upon. But one is acted upon *from below* — by the momentum of inherited error. The other is acted upon *from above* — by the selecting hand of the One who sees what no one else sees.

[PAUSE]

And this — I want to suggest — is why the phrase *ʿibād Allāhi al-mukhlaṣīn* appears exactly four times in this surah. Once for the general principle (37:40). Once for our passage (37:74). Once for the followers of Ilyās (37:128). Once as the surah's closing benediction (37:160). The surah moves through the historical gallery — Nūh's people, Ibrāhīm's people, Mūsā's people, Ilyās's people — and in every story the same pattern: most were driven down the fathers' tracks and came to ruin. And in every story, the same door appears in the same wall.

*Illā.*

The exception is not a reward for extraordinary virtue. It is an account of how God reaches into the momentum of human history and *selects*. The refrain is not triumphalist. It is something closer to relief — the breath a reader takes when the tunnel finally opens.

## PART FOUR: THE QUESTION THIS PASSAGE ASKS *YOU*

We have walked through the grammar. We have stood inside the theology. Now the passage turns and looks at us.

And it asks — or rather, Allah asks, because *fanẓur* is His command — the only question that matters in this context:

**Are you being driven, or are you being chosen?**

[PAUSE]

Before you answer too quickly, let me make the question harder.

Being driven does not feel like being driven. That is the entire point. The people rushing down their fathers' footsteps were not, we can safely assume, aware of being herded. They were, in their own experience, *belonging*. They were *part of something*. The footsteps of the fathers were not an external force acting on them like wind against a sail — they were the very substance of their identity. The tradition they were being driven by was, to them, simply *reality*.

This is what makes the Quranic diagnosis so disquieting. It does not say they were lying to themselves. It does not say they were bad people who chose badly. It says they *found* a direction and were *driven* down it — made restless until they moved, *yuza'jūn*, agitated into forward motion. The finding was effortless. The being-driven felt like walking. The grammar reveals what the experience concealed.

[PAUSE]

So how does one tell the difference?

The surah has already given us the answer, in the single word standing between the two passives: *fanẓur.* **Look.**

Not just observe. Not just notice. The classical commentators understand *naẓar* in its deepest sense as the movement of the intellect toward an object with the intention of *judging* it — of producing, in the end, a verdict. This is not passive seeing. This is active examination. It is the faculty that the people of the Fire never turned on. It is the faculty Allah commands the reader to activate at this precise moment.

And the command is not abstract. The surah has populated it with content: *look how was the end of those who were warned.* The *naẓar* being commanded is historical. It requires you to look at what happened before you. To take the footprints of the ancients — not to *run down them*, but to *examine them.* To ask: where did this path lead? Is this the direction I want to be moving in? Does this inherited orientation, examined honestly, point toward the Real?

[PAUSE]

This is the place where *ikhlāṣ* begins. Not in the dramatic moment of choosing faith over disbelief in some grand existential crisis. In the quiet, ordinary, difficult act of **examining what you've been handed.**

The tradition you were born into. The theology you absorbed before you could question it. The emotional associations that make certain ideas feel true before you've tested them. The social cost of questioning something everyone around you has agreed to stop questioning.

The *fanẓur* is asking: have you ever actually *looked* at these things? Or have you, like the people rushing down the footsteps, simply found them there — and been set moving by the agitation of conformity before you ever thought to stop?

## PART FIVE: THE TWO PASSIVES, HELD TOGETHER

We have arrived at the thing I asked you to stay hungry for.

Both groups — the driven and the chosen — are passive. Neither is the unmoved mover of their own destiny. The grammar insists on this with a completeness that is almost uncomfortable.

And yet they arrive at radically different ends.

What is the difference between them?

[PAUSE]

The passive of *yuhraʿūn* is a **groundless passive.** The agent is hidden — *mabnī lil-majhūl*, constructed for the unknown. Something drives them. Something pushes the hand at their back. But the doer is unnamed, unowned, invisible. They are moved by a force they never named, never faced, never had any relationship with. They simply moved. And the direction they moved in was the direction of *shirk* — a world already oriented away from God, which they came upon (*alfaw*) before they could evaluate it, and which then drove them (*yuhraʿūn*) the rest of their lives.

The passive of *al-mukhlaṣīn* is a **grounded passive.** The One who does the choosing is not hidden. The word itself — *mukhlaṣīn* — contains the acknowledgment that Allah is the doer. The form points back to Him. They were purified *by* Someone, selected *by* Someone, claimed *by* Someone whose name they knew. Their passivity is not the passivity of objects. It is the passivity of the known-by.

[PAUSE]

To be driven by an unnamed force is to be something the Quran, in its inimitable way, calls *mayyit* — spiritually dead. The will is present but disengaged. The legs move but the person is absent from their own motion.

To be chosen by the Named is to be *ḥayy* — alive. To be in a relationship with the One who is acting on you. To know yourself as the object of divine selection and to have, in that knowledge, arrived at the most clarifying orientation a human being can inhabit.

[PAUSE]

This is why the surah's refrain — *illā ʿibāda Allāhi al-mukhlaṣīn* — appears with such rhythmic regularity. It is not celebrating a spiritual elite. It is marking a structural exit point in the human situation. At every point in history where most were driven down the footsteps of the dead, there were those who were being differently acted upon. Not because they were more powerful, not because they were beyond the pull of inherited momentum, but because something in them — something that grace itself had prepared — had stopped long enough to *look.*

And in that looking, the divine selection could reach them.

## CONCLUSION: The Door in the Wall

I want to return to where we began. The room where these verses are spoken.

We are deep inside As-Saffat, surrounded by images of the Hereafter. The tree of Zaqqum has just been described — its fruit like the heads of shayāṭīn, consumed by people who cannot stop, washed down with boiling ḥamīm. Al-Ṭabarī reads these verses together as a unit: the passage of punishment (verses 67–68) flows directly into the passage of diagnosis (verses 69–70) without pause or break. The structure is deliberate. Allah shows the consequence first, then the cause. And when the cause arrives, it lands with the force of everything we have just witnessed: *they found their fathers astray, and so they were driven.* The punishment, in a terrible irony, mirrors the crime. In life, they consumed the religion of their ancestors without ever examining what they were swallowing. In the Hereafter, they consume the Zaqqum and the boiling drink in precisely the same way — forced, passively, with no choice.

[PAUSE]

Then Allah draws back and asks: why? And the answer He gives is not murder, not theft, not explicit cruelty. The answer He gives is the quietest, most ordinary thing in the world.

*They found their fathers astray. And so they were driven down the tracks.*

The horror of this is not dramatic. That is the point. The Fire, in As-Saffat's account, is populated not primarily by villains but by sleepwalkers. People who moved through their entire lives in the passive voice, receiving a direction without examining it, being disturbed into forward motion without knowing they were being disturbed, following footprints without ever wondering where they led.

And into this picture — four times, like a door that keeps appearing in a wall — Allah cuts the *illā*. The exception. The *mukhlaṣīn*. The ones who were differently passive. Not the ones who escaped being acted upon — no one escapes that, we are all acted upon — but the ones who were acted upon *by the Named.*

[PAUSE]

The surah does not explain exactly how one moves from the first passive to the second. The grammar doesn't give us a transition map. But it does give us one command, standing at the precise hinge between the two passives: *fanẓur.*

**Look.**

It is the smallest intervention. It is, in one sense, nothing more than turning your faculty of examination *on.* But it is the faculty that distinguishes the *mukhlaṣīn* from the driven — not because looking earns salvation, but because looking is how you stop long enough for the choosing to reach you. The fathers were upon *shirk wa ḍalāl*, and the sons ran down those tracks without ever stopping to ask: is this true? The warner came. The faculty of *naẓar* was never engaged. And the door stayed closed.

The Quran is in your hands. A warner has come. The end of those who were warned stands before you in the historical record of every people to whom a prophet was sent.

*Fanẓur.*

Are you looking?
