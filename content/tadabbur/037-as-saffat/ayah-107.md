# And We Ransomed Him

### Surah As-Saffat, Ayah 107

> **وَفَدَيْنَٰهُ بِذِبْحٍ عَظِيمٍ**
>
> *wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm*
>
> **"And We ransomed him with a great sacrifice."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Knife

Let me ask you to stand somewhere uncomfortable before we read a single word.

Stand on a hilltop outside Makkah, in a time so old that the Kaʿbah has not yet been built by the hands that will build it. An old man is here — older than you imagine, a man who has already lost a homeland, already walked away from his father, already been thrown into a fire and walked out of it. A man who waited for a child until waiting itself became a kind of body he carried. And when the child finally came — when Allah finally answered the prayer *"My Lord, grant me from among the righteous"* — this old man held in his arms the single thing in all the world he could not bear to lose.

And then he dreamed.

He dreamed that he was slaughtering that very son.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that means before we move. This is not a man being asked to give up wealth. Not a man being asked to leave a city, or fast a month, or pray through a night. This is a father being shown himself, in a dream that prophets do not get to dismiss as ordinary dreams, with a knife at the throat of the boy he prayed years of his life to hold.

And here is what the surah does, just before our ayah, that should stop your breath. It does not show us Ibrahim agonizing alone. It shows him *turning to the boy* and telling him the dream. *"O my son, I have seen in a dream that I am slaughtering you, so look — what do you see?"* And the boy — old enough to have walked and worked beside his father, young enough to still be *the* beloved — answers:

*"O my father, do what you are commanded. You will find me, if Allah wills, among the patient."*

[PAUSE]

Two surrenders. The father's and the son's. The Qur'an gives them a single word for what happened next: *falammā aslamā* — "when they had both submitted." And then — the boy laid down on his forehead. The face pressed to the earth. The hand raised.

This is the moment the whole world has been holding its breath toward.

And into that frozen instant — the raised hand, the bound boy, the submitted hearts — Allah speaks three words.

Not thirty. Not a sermon on reward. **Three words.**

*wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm.*

Here is the hook I want you to carry into everything that follows. We tend to read this ayah as a *relief* — the happy ending, the boy is saved, exhale. But there is something hidden inside these three words that is far stranger and far more beautiful than relief. Because the One who provides the ransom in this verse is the very same One who issued the command. **Allah commanded the sacrifice. And then Allah ransomed it.** What kind of test is set up by the same hand that sets you free from it? What is Allah actually after, if it was never the blood?

Let's find out — one word at a time.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Fadaynā* — The Word "Ransom" and Who Pays It

Let me teach you a concept before we touch the ayah, because the whole verse hinges on it and most of us have lost the word in English.

In Arabic there is a word, *fidya*, and the verb that comes from it: *fadā / yafdī*. It does not simply mean "to save." It means something far more specific. **A *fidya* is a ransom — a substitute price paid to free a captive.**

Picture the old economy of it. Someone is taken — held, bound, owed. They cannot free themselves. And someone *else* steps in and pays a price, offers a substitute, and the captive walks free *on the strength of what was paid in their place*. The captive does nothing. The captive cannot do anything. Freedom comes entirely from outside them, through a price they could never have raised themselves.

That is *fidya*. Not rescue in general — **substitution.** A life standing in for a life. A price in the place of a person.

Now look at our word: **وَفَدَيْنَٰهُ** — *wa-fadaynāhu*.

Let's open it slowly.

The root is *fa-dā-ya* (ف د ي) — exactly this ransom-meaning, attested across the classical lexicons. The verb here is the simple Form I perfect: a completed act, already done, sealed. And then notice the ending: **-nā** — "We." And **-hu** — "him."

*We ransomed him.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with that pronoun. *We.* Who is the "We"?

It is Allah.

And here is the thing the grammar will not let you look away from. The whole drama, for a hundred verses, has been about what *Ibrahim* will do. Will Ibrahim obey? Will Ibrahim's hand fall? The camera has been on the human being and his agonizing, surrendering will. And then at the climax — the verb flips. The subject of the sentence is no longer Ibrahim. **The subject is Allah.** *Fadaynā* — *We* ransomed.

Ibrahim raised the knife. **Allah paid the ransom.**

Let's sit with the strangeness of this. In every human ransom story, the captor and the ransomer are *enemies* — one holds, the other pays to free. But here the One who "bound" the son to this altar — through the dream, through the command — is the *exact same One* who now provides the price to release him. The Hand that tested is the Hand that ransoms.

Notice what that tells us about the entire nature of the test. **If Allah was always going to provide the substitute, then Allah never wanted the boy.** The command was never an appetite for blood. The Lord of the worlds does not need a slaughtered child. So what was He drawing out? He was drawing out the *aslamā* — the submission of two hearts to the point of the forehead pressed to the earth. Once that was real — fully real, knife-in-hand real — the outward act became unnecessary, because the outward act was never the point. The surrender *was* the offering. The ram was just the receipt.

[PAUSE]

And here is the tafsir confirming the ransom-language directly. Al-Muyassar renders it plainly: *"We rescued Ismail, and made in his place a great ram."* The word the scholars reach for is *badal* — a substitute, a stand-in. Al-Jalalayn: *"And We ransomed him"* — the one who had been commanded to be slaughtered — *"with a ram."* Al-Tabari frames it as *jāzā* — a recompense — saying Allah made the slaughter of a great ram stand *in the place of* what had been commanded, delivering the son from it. The grammar of substitution is not an English overlay. It is sitting in the root.

A note the classical scholars were careful to preserve: the pronoun *hu* — "him" — carried a genuine disagreement among the early commentators about *whom* was being ransomed. Al-Jalalayn records both positions openly: some held it was Ismail; others held it was Isḥāq — *qawlān*, two opinions. Al-Tabari's collection of transmissions includes early authorities on both sides, and his own framing names Isḥāq. Al-Muyassar, and the Prophetic tradition, lean toward Ismail. What is remarkable — and perhaps deliberately so — is that the Qur'an itself says only *hu*: *him*. As if the grammar of surrender is what the verse insists on, not the name carried within it. The question of which son has occupied scholars across centuries. The ayah holds them both in a single pronoun and continues.

So the first word has already given us something we will spend the rest of our lives unpacking: **the One who asks everything of you is the same One who provides what you could never provide yourself.** The captive does not pay his own ransom. He cannot. Hold that. We will need it.

---

### Element Two: *Dhibḥ*, Not *Dhabḥ* — A Single Vowel That Changes Everything

Now I want to show you something so small that you would never see it in translation, and so precise that it tells you the Qur'an is built to a tolerance we can barely measure.

First, the concept. In Arabic, the same three root letters can be vowelled in different ways, and the vowelling is not decoration — it changes the *kind* of word it is. There is a difference, for instance, between a word that names an *action* and a word that names the *thing the action produces*. In English think of "the grinding" versus "the grain that's been ground." Same root idea — flour — but one word is the *act*, the other is the *result you can hold in your hand*.

Arabic does this with our root, *dha-ba-ḥa* (ذ ب ح), the root of slaughter.

- With a *fatḥa* — **dhabḥ** — it is the *maṣdar*, the verbal noun: the *act* of slaughtering.
- With a *kasra* — **dhibḥ** — it is the name of *the thing slaughtered*: the sacrificial animal itself, the offering you can see and touch.

Now look at what Allah chose: **بِذِبْحٍ** — *bi-dhibḥin*. With a *kasra*. **The thing slaughtered. The offering itself.**

This is not my reading. This is al-Qurṭubī, precisely: *"al-dhibḥ is the name of the slaughtered thing — its plural is dhubūḥ — like ṭiḥn (the ground meal) is the name for what has been ground. And al-dhabḥ, with fatḥa, is the verbal noun [the act]."*

[PAUSE]

So feel the choice. Allah did not say "We ransomed him with a *slaughtering*." He said "We ransomed him with a *slaughtered offering*." The emphasis is taken off the *act* — the knife, the blood, the violence — and placed on the *gift*: the concrete, present, provided substitute standing there on the hilltop.

Do you see the mercy folded into a single vowel?

The entire scene was building toward an *act* — *dhabḥ*, the slaughtering. Ibrahim's hand was raised to perform exactly that. And Allah, in naming what He provides, deliberately uses the *other* word — *dhibḥ*, the thing, the gift, the offering — as if to say: **the act you were braced to commit is not what I was ever asking for. Here, instead, is a gift.** The vowel itself moves us from the violence of the verb to the grace of the noun.

And there is a sound to it, too. Say it: *dhibḥ.* It ends in that guttural *ḥ*, a sound cut off short in the throat — the very place a blade would fall. The word carries the throat-sound of slaughter even as its grammar carries the meaning of *gift*. The Qur'an lets you hear the blade and receive the mercy in the same breath.

Now press deeper into what this particular *dhibḥ* actually was — because al-Jalalayn preserves a detail that should stop you cold. This was not a random animal that happened to wander onto the hilltop. Al-Jalalayn records that it was *a ram from Paradise* — *min al-janna* — and then names its lineage: it is the very offering that Habil had once brought, the sacrifice that Allah accepted from Adam's son at the very dawn of human offering. That accepted ram was preserved in the unseen, until Jibreel descended with it to this valley and Ibrahim slaughtered it saying the takbīr.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means. At the very beginning of human sacrifice — before every temple, before every altar, before the Kaʿbah itself was built — two brothers presented their offerings. One gave his best, and Allah accepted it. The animal entered the record of accepted things. And it did not simply cease. The acceptance of Allah is not a moment that expires.

Across all the centuries between Adam's sons and Ibrahim's ordeal, that accepted offering was held in trust — in Paradise, the only place that keeps things without loss — waiting for the greatest single act of human surrender ever made. The first sacrifice accepted by Allah became the ransom for the son of the Friend of Allah. The *dhibḥ* on that hilltop was not improvised. It carried within it the entire arc of human offering: from the first son who gave his best with a pure heart, to the prophet who laid down his most beloved, forehead to the earth.

One vowel. The difference between an act you dreaded and a gift you were given. But now we know: this gift had been waiting in Paradise since before this valley existed, pre-ordained, preserved in acceptance, brought by the same angel who carries divine speech. And Ibrahim, when his hand finally fell, said *Allahu Akbar* — and we have been saying it at the moment of sacrifice ever since.

### Element Three: *ʿAẓīm* — "Great" That Has Nothing to Do With Size

Now the last word, and it is the one we are most likely to read lazily. **عَظِيمٍ** — *ʿaẓīm.* "Great."

Here is the trap. When we hear "a great sacrifice," our minds reach for *size*. A huge ram. An enormous beast. We picture the magnificence as bulk. And the classical scholars saw us coming and shut that door before we could walk through it.

Let me teach the concept. The Arabic *ʿaẓīm*, from the root *ʿa-ẓu-ma* (ع ظ م), does carry the sense of "great" — but Arabic "greatness" runs along two completely different axes. There is greatness of *juththa* — physical bulk, body, mass. And there is greatness of *qadr* — worth, rank, honor, standing. The same word, two utterly different kinds of "great." One you weigh on a scale. The other you cannot weigh at all.

So which one is it here?

Al-Qurṭubī is unambiguous: *"ʿaẓīm — meaning great in worth (ʿaẓīm al-qadr); it does not mean great in body (lam yurid ʿaẓīm al-juththa)."* And he tells us *why* it was great in worth — two reasons, and notice they are both about meaning, never about mass: *"because the sacrificed one [the prophet's son] was ransomed by it, or because it was accepted (mutaqabbal)."*

And he brings al-Naḥḥās to confirm the linguistic range: *"ʿaẓīm in the language is used for the large (al-kabīr) and for the noble (al-sharīf); and the people of tafsir hold that here it is for the noble, or the accepted."*

[PAUSE]

Let that reorganize the whole picture. The greatness of this offering was **never in the animal.** A ram is a ram. Its bones weigh what bones weigh. Its greatness — its *ʿaẓīm*-ness — came entirely from two things that no scale can measure:

**One: what it stood in for.** It was great because it ransomed a prophet's submitted son. Its worth was borrowed from the worth of what it saved. A thing becomes precious by *what it is permitted to redeem*.

**Two: that it was accepted.** *Mutaqabbal.* Allah received it. And there is nothing in creation greater than the thing your Lord *accepts*. The same ram, unaccepted, is mutton. Accepted, it is *ʿaẓīm* — its greatness conferred from above, not earned from below.

And now we can read the *ʿaẓīm* of this particular ram with even more depth. It was great because it stood in for a prophet's son. It was great because Allah accepted it here, on this hilltop. And it was great because it had already been accepted once — Habil offered it, and Allah received it — long before Ibrahim was born, long before this test was set. It arrived on that hilltop already bearing the mark of divine acceptance. The *ʿaẓīm* upon *ʿaẓīm*, greatness layered in ways that only become visible when you read what al-Jalalayn preserved about where this animal came from.

Now hold these three words together for a moment, because they have quietly built a single sentence about reality. *Fadaynā* — Allah provides the ransom; the subject has shifted from the human to the Divine. *Dhibḥ* — and what He provides is a gift, not a violence, a thing you can receive, not a thing you must perform; brought from Paradise by Jibreel, carrying the lineage of the first accepted offering. *ʿaẓīm* — and its greatness is measured by worth and acceptance, never by size.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Ransom You Could Never Have Paid

Let me start where you actually live, far from any hilltop.

There is a particular kind of fear that sits underneath the lives of people who love something deeply. It is the fear of *the call you don't want to receive.* The parent who lies awake listening for the car in the driveway. The person who loves someone with a fragile heart and counts their breaths in the dark. We build whole lives around things we cannot bear to lose, and somewhere in us, quietly, we know: I am not in control of keeping this. The thing I love most is precisely the thing I cannot guarantee.

And so we do something very human. We *clutch.* We grip the beloved thing so tightly that we are no longer enjoying it — we are guarding it. We turn the gift into a hostage. And in the gripping, we slowly forget who gave it to us in the first place.

Now look at Ibrahim — because Ibrahim was given the most extreme version of this fear that a human being has ever been given. Not "you might lose him." *"Give him up. With your own hand."*

And here is what Ibrahim does, and it is the opposite of clutching. He *opens his hands.* He brings the most beloved thing in his life and lays it, unclenched, before the One who gave it. Father and son both — *aslamā*, they submitted. They let go of the grip.

And the instant the grip is truly, fully released — *fadaynāhu* — Allah catches what was falling.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the structure of mercy here? **The ransom came only after the hands were open.** Not before. As long as Ibrahim was gripping, there was no room for Allah to give. The open hand that was willing to release what was most beloved is the same open hand into which Allah placed the ram. The surrender did not *lose* the son. The surrender is what *received* him back.

This is the deepest possible answer to the question the ayah poses. If you read it as merely a relief — "and then God called it off, phew" — you miss what was actually transacted. Ibrahim did not get his son back *despite* the willingness to surrender. He got him back *because of* it. The surrender was the offering. Allah's contribution was the receipt.

[PAUSE]

The captive, remember, does not pay his own ransom. He cannot. That is the definition of *fidya*. And so here: Ibrahim could not save his son. The son could not save himself. What freed the boy was *paid from a source outside both of them* — the ram from Paradise, the gift already waiting since the time of Habil, brought by Jibreel at exactly the moment the hearts were fully open. The entire miracle depends on something Ibrahim could not provide and could not predict: a generosity that was already prepared before he raised the knife, waiting in the unseen for this act of surrender to make room for it.

What does that tell you about the tests you are living inside right now?

If you are holding something you are terrified to lose, this ayah is not asking you to be indifferent to it. Ibrahim loved his son. The text never pretends otherwise — the very *weight* of the command came from the depth of that love. But there is an invitation buried in *fadaynāhu*: **open the hands that are clutching what you love most, and find out what the One who gave it is capable of doing with open hands**. The ransom was already prepared before you were born. It is waiting in the economy of a mercy you cannot see from inside the fear.

### Theme Two: What Accepted Things Become

I want to take you somewhere the tafsir leads, because it is too extraordinary to pass over.

We have established that this *dhibḥ* was no ordinary animal. Al-Jalalayn preserves the tradition: a ram from Paradise, the very offering Habil had once given, accepted by Allah, preserved in the unseen, brought by Jibreel at the moment it was needed.

Let me sit with that. Because the first recorded act of sacrifice in human history — before every temple, before every altar, before the Kaʿbah itself was built — was Habil's offering. Two brothers brought their gifts. And the Qur'an records what happened: *"Allah accepted from one and did not accept from the other."* The one who gave his best, with a pure heart, was received. That animal entered the record of accepted things.

And it did not disappear.

When Jibreel descended with a ram onto that hilltop, he was not bringing a coincidental substitute. He was bringing *the first accepted offering back into the world*, at the moment when the greatest surrender of human history was being made. The ram that closed Habil's story of faithfulness became the ram that completed Ibrahim's story of faithfulness. **Accepted things have a future.**

[PAUSE]

Think about what you give that is pure — truly given without calculation, truly surrendered. The logic of this narrative suggests that acceptance does not simply complete an act. It *preserves* it, in ways and on timelines you cannot track. The offering that enters the register of *mutaqabbal* — accepted — does not expire. You may not live to see what it opens.

This is not a guarantee of worldly return. Habil was killed immediately after his sacrifice was received. The acceptance of his offering did not protect him from his brother's violence. But his offering was not undone. It came back — across all the centuries between Adam's sons and Ibrahim's ordeal — in the hands of Jibreel, at the exact moment the world needed it most. **The accepted gift echoes forward into times and places you cannot imagine from where you stand.**

What does this do to how you think about the small, uncelebrated acts of sincerity in your life? The prayer no one saw, given with your whole heart. The sacrifice made quietly, without record. The giving that cost you something and was never acknowledged. The logic of this ram says: these do not simply pass. They are held. And the One who holds them is the same One who sent Jibreel down a hillside with a gift that had been waiting since the beginning of human offering.

---

### Theme Three: *ʿAẓīm* From Below, *ʿAẓīm* From Above

There is a temptation, when we reach the end of this story, to think: what made this *great* was Ibrahim's act of willingness. His sacrifice, his obedience, his love — the human drama of it.

And that is true. But the Qur'an places *ʿaẓīm* on the *dhibḥ* — the provided sacrifice — not on Ibrahim's willingness. The greatness the ayah names is the greatness of what *Allah gave*, not what Ibrahim offered.

Why does that matter?

Because there are two economies of greatness, and we live inside one while being invited into the other.

The economy we naturally understand is **greatness from below** — greatness earned, greatness demonstrated, greatness recognized. You do something extraordinary, and it is acknowledged as extraordinary. Ibrahim's willingness was extraordinary. No one disputes this. The Qur'an itself names it: *"Indeed, this is the clear trial."*

But the *ʿaẓīm* in our ayah is **greatness from above** — greatness *conferred*. The ram was not great because it was large. It was great because of what it was permitted to do: stand in for a beloved son. And because Allah accepted it. The greatness came down onto the offering; it did not rise up from the offering.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what you bring to Allah — your prayers, your fasts, your grief, your trying-again-after-failing. None of it is "great" by weight. A *rak'ah* is a rak'ah; a prostration takes a minute; a du'ā is breath and words. But the ram was mutton until it was *accepted*. And the moment it was accepted, it was *ʿaẓīm* — great before the Lord of the worlds, great not because of what it weighed but because of Whose hands received it.

What makes your *ibāda* great is not its size. It is not its duration or eloquence or visible effect. It is whether the One who received it counted it as *mutaqabbal*. And the Qur'an is telling us, through the story of this ram, that the thing which most inclines Allah toward acceptance is the same thing Ibrahim and his son demonstrated in those final moments before the knife: **the open-handed, forehead-to-the-earth, all-in surrender** — the *aslamā* — that precedes the offering.

The outward act is the receipt. The inward surrender is the offering. And the *ʿaẓīm* descends from Him.

## CLOSING: The Knife That Taught a Nation How to Say His Name

Every year, at the turn of Dhul Hijjah, the world returns to this hilltop.

Millions slaughter. The ram reappears in every city, in backyards and butcher shops and on open ground outside city walls, and the words on the lips are *Allahu Akbar* — just as Ibrahim said them when the knife finally fell, not on his son, but on the gift Jibreel had carried from Paradise. He said *Allahu Akbar*, and every sacrifice since has carried that sound forward. A billion voices saying: *You are greater. What I hold is yours. My hands are open.*

This is what *ʿaẓīm* looks like from inside time. The three words of this ayah have been echoing for millennia, producing a practice that is not about blood — the Qur'an is explicit: *"It is not their meat nor their blood that reaches Allah, but it is piety from you that reaches Him"* (22:37) — but about returning, annually, to the posture of opened hands.

[PAUSE]

We do not slaughter because Allah needs the animal. We now know that. The test was never about the blood. We do not slaughter to reenact a trauma. We slaughter because a father and a son, on a hilltop, demonstrated something once about the relationship between surrender and reception — about how the hands that release what they love most are the very hands into which a gift from Paradise falls — and the world decided: *this should not be forgotten. We should do this every year. We should remember what our hands are for.*

The ram from Paradise. Habil's accepted offering, preserved across centuries. Brought by Jibreel. Slaughtered by Ibrahim, saying *Allahu Akbar*. Three words from Allah in response to two surrendered hearts.

*wa-fadaynāhu bi-dhibḥin ʿaẓīm.*

And We ransomed him — with a gift that had been waiting since the beginning of offering — great not in weight but in worth, great not in size but in acceptance, great because the One who conferred the greatness is the One to whom all greatness belongs.

[PAUSE]

Before you leave this ayah, let me ask you to do just one thing.

Identify the thing you are clutching — the beloved thing you have quietly, almost involuntarily, stopped returning to Allah with open hands. The thing you hold so tightly that the joy of it has been replaced by the fear of losing it. You know what it is. You have always known.

Now imagine — not performing, just imagining — opening those hands. Not losing it. Opening. Placing it, unclenched, before the One who gave it.

This is what the hilltop was about. Not the knife. The open hands.

And somewhere, already prepared, already accepted, already in transit — a *dhibḥ ʿaẓīm* is waiting.
