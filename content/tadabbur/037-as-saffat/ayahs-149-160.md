# The Verdict You Were Never Qualified to Issue

## Surah As-Saffat 37:149–160

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first to the sound of it. Let it move through you before you understand a single word.

> [ayah:37:149] فَٱسْتَفْتِهِمْ أَلِرَبِّكَ ٱلْبَنَاتُ وَلَهُمُ ٱلْبَنُونَ
>
> *Fa-staftihim, a-li-Rabbika l-banātu wa lahumu l-banūn.*
>
> **So ask them for their verdict: Does your Lord have the daughters, while they have the sons?**

And then, eleven verses later, the passage lands here:

> سُبْحَٰنَ ٱللَّهِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ ۝ إِلَّا عِبَادَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ
>
> *Subḥāna llāhi ʿammā yaṣifūn. Illā ʿibāda llāhi l-mukhlaṣīn.*
>
> **Glory be to Allah, far above what they describe — except for the chosen, purified servants of Allah.**

### Rebuild the world

You have to understand where this falls in the surah to feel the force of it.

For roughly a hundred and forty verses, Surah As-Saffat has been a procession. One prophet after another, marching through. Nuh, who was answered when he called. Ibrahim, who laid his son down to the knife and was ransomed. Musa and Harun, rescued from the great distress. Ilyas, who stood against an entire nation. Lut. Yunus, swallowed and brought back from the belly of the dark. And after each one — like a drumbeat, like a refrain — Allah says: *salāmun ʿalā…* peace be upon him. *Innahu min ʿibādinā l-muʾminīn* — he was of Our believing servants.

A parade of the rescued. And what do they all have in common? They served Allah, and Allah alone. No partner. No daughter. No intermediary. Pure.

Now the procession stops. The music changes. Allah turns — and this is sudden, it has the feel of a head snapping around — from the honored dead to the living men of Makkah sitting in their assemblies. The same men who, when one of them was told *your wife has given birth to a girl,* his face would darken and he would withdraw from people in shame. Those men. Those very men were saying, about the most exalted creatures in the heavens, the angels — *those are God's daughters.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with the obscenity of that for a moment, because the Quran is about to. A man who would bury a daughter alive in the sand rather than raise her — assigning daughters to the Lord of the worlds. Keeping the sons, the pride, the inheritance, for himself. And handing upward, to God, the thing he himself could not stand to receive.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to watch for. Allah does not answer this belief the way you'd expect — He does not simply say *you are wrong.* He does something stranger and far more devastating. He puts them in a chair. He hands them a gavel. He says: *fine. Issue the ruling. You're the judge here — render your verdict.*

And by the time the passage is done, they have convicted themselves. Not with an argument from outside. With their own preferences, read back to them.

There is a word buried in the first verse that tells you this entire passage is a courtroom. And there is a word in the middle that tells you their crime is not lying in the ordinary sense — it is something the Arabic language reserves for a very specific kind of catastrophe. Let's go and find them.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Istaftihim*: "Ask them for their ruling"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word, because if I just translate it you'll miss everything.

In Arabic there is a family of words built on the root *f-t-y*. From it you get *fatā* — a young man, in his prime, full of strength. And from the same root you get a word you already know: **fatwā.** A legal ruling. A formal, authoritative verdict issued by someone qualified to issue it. When you go to a scholar and you say *what is the ruling on this matter,* the act of asking is called *istiftāʾ*, and the answer he gives is the *fatwā.*

Now hold that. Because the verb Allah uses here is in a very particular shape.

> فَٱسْتَفْتِهِمْ

The corpus confirms this is **Form X** of the verb — *istaf-ʿala.* And Form X in Arabic very often means *to seek or request the thing the root names.* If the root is *ghafara*, to forgive, then *istaghfara* is to **seek** forgiveness. If the root is to-do-with-rulings, then *istaftā* is to **seek a ruling.**

So *istaftihim* does not mean "ask them a question." It means:

**Go to them and request their legal verdict. Treat them as the authorities. Solicit their fatwā.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land. Allah is commanding His Prophet ﷺ to walk up to the polytheists of Makkah and address them *as if they were a panel of jurists.* As if they sat on a bench. As if they held qualified knowledge of the unseen architecture of the heavens and could be consulted on it.

Do you feel the blade inside that? It is the deepest sarcasm in the most dignified possible register. There is no mockery in the surface words at all. The surface words are pure deference — *please, render your ruling.* And that deference is the whole weapon. Because to ask someone for a fatwā is to grant them, out loud, an authority — and the instant you watch what they do with that authority, you see they have none.

The classical commentators — al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi, and the Jalalayn all agree on this — call this *suʾāl tawbīkh.* A question of **rebuke.** Al-Qurtubi notes something else worth holding: this command, *fa-staftihim,* he says is connected back to the opening of the surah — *muʿṭūfun ʿalā mithlihi fī awwali s-sūrah* — even though, in his words, the distance between them is vast. The whole surah, with its oath-taking angels arranged in ranks and its parade of pure servants, has been building to *this* cross-examination.

And watch how the courtroom holds together once you've seen the frame. Five verses later Allah says:

> [ayah:37:154] مَا لَكُمْ كَيْفَ تَحْكُمُونَ

*Taḥkumūn* — from *ḥ-k-m*, the root of *ḥukm,* a judicial ruling, the root of *ḥākim,* a judge. He opened by inviting their *fatwā,* and now He turns to them mid-session: *how are you reaching this verdict?* It is one continuous legal scene. *Render your ruling… now explain to the court how on earth you arrived at it.*

And then the final move a judge makes when a claimant has asserted something with no basis:

> أَمْ لَكُمْ سُلْطَٰنٌۭ مُّبِينٌۭ ۝ فَأْتُوا۟ بِكِتَٰبِكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ
>
> *Or do you have clear authority? Then bring your document, if you are truthful.*

*Bring your evidence. Enter your exhibit into the record. Show the court the written warrant for this claim.* And of course there is no document. There never was. The bench falls silent.

But the indictment has one more layer, and it lands even earlier — in verse 150, before the demand for a document is even made: *wa-hum shāhidūn* — **while they were witnesses.** Al-Tabari unpacks this precisely: were these men *present* at the creation of the angels? Did they stand there and observe Allah creating them female, such that they can offer eyewitness testimony? The logic is devastating in its simplicity. You cannot testify to what you did not see. You have no scripture on the matter. You have no firsthand witness. You have no *sulṭān mubīn* — no clear authority of any kind. The courtroom has closed every exit. First the epistemological door — *you weren't there.* Then the textual door — *produce your book.* Then the judicial door — *how did you reach this ruling?* Every avenue of legitimate evidence runs out at once.

So what does this single word, *istaftihim,* reveal? **That the gravest claims human beings make about God are very often verdicts we have no standing to issue — and the fastest way to expose a baseless verdict is not to argue with it, but to make the one issuing it state his evidence out loud.**

### Element Two — *Ifk*: not a lie, an inversion

Now to the word at the center. And this one I need to teach carefully, because in English we have nothing quite like it.

> [ayah:37:151] أَلَآ إِنَّهُم مِّنْ إِفْكِهِمْ لَيَقُولُونَ
>
> *Unquestionably, it is out of their **ifk** that they say…*

We translate *ifk* as "lie" or "falsehood." That's not wrong, but it is thin, and it misses the engine inside the word.

The root is *ʾ-f-k.* Go to Lisān al-ʿArab, go to al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt,* and you find the root does not primarily mean "to speak an untruth." It means **to turn a thing away from its proper direction. To overturn. To flip something onto the side it was not meant to lie on.**

The clearest fingerprint of this root is elsewhere in the Quran. The cities of the people of Lut — the ones turned upside down, lifted and inverted so that their roofs became their foundations — are called *al-muʾtafikāt.* The **overturned** cities. Same root. *Ifk* is what happens when something gets flipped over so that up becomes down.

So *ifk* is not merely saying a false thing. **It is taking reality and turning it upside down.** It is the lie that inverts the order of things.

And the same word — let this give you a chill — is the word the Quran uses in Surah an-Nur for the great slander against ʿĀʾisha, may Allah be pleased with her: *alladhīna jāʾū bi-l-ifk.* Why that word and not an ordinary word for lie? Because that slander took the purest reality and tried to turn it into its filthy opposite. It flipped truth onto its head. That is *ifk.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at what they did, and see if *ifk* is not the only word that fits.

They took the angels — beings of light, the highest servants in the heavens, *creatures*, brought into being by Allah — and they called them His **offspring.** That is inversion number one: the created turned into the begotten. The servant flipped into the son.

Inversion number two, and this is the one the passage keeps pressing: they assigned the daughters to God. *Daughters.* The very thing that, among them, was despised. The thing that darkened a man's face. The thing some of them buried in the dirt. And the sons — the prized, the celebrated — they kept for themselves.

> أَلِرَبِّكَ ٱلْبَنَاتُ وَلَهُمُ ٱلْبَنُونَ
>
> *For your Lord the daughters — and for them the sons?*

Read the asymmetry slowly, because the Arabic builds it deliberately. Notice the order. The first thing mentioned for the Lord is *al-banāt,* the daughters — pushed to the front. And the thing kept for the self is *al-banūn,* the sons. They have taken what they value least and shoved it upward toward the Most High, and clutched what they value most to their own chests.

The classical commentators don't soften this. Al-Muyassar's concise gloss is stark: they assigned to Allah the daughters *allatī yakrahūnahunna* — **whom they hate.** Not dislike. Not merely prefer less. The Arabic verb is *karaha* — deep revulsion, finding something reprehensible. And the sons? *Alladhīna yurīdūnahum* — whom they *want*, whom they *desire*. Al-Jalalayn then names what they appropriated for themselves in a single precise word: *al-asnā* — **the most excellent, the highest-ranked in their own scale of worth.** Hated: handed upward. Most excellent: clutched below. That is not a preference — that is a moral universe inverted.

That is *ifk* in its purest form. The whole cosmic order turned upside down. The One deserving of all perfection is handed the leftovers; the creature keeps the crown. It is the *muʾtafikāt* — the overturned cities — but performed in the realm of belief instead of stone.

And watch the very next verse tighten the screw:

> [ayah:37:153] أَصْطَفَى ٱلْبَنَاتِ عَلَى ٱلْبَنِينَ
>
> *Aṣṭafā l-banāti ʿalā l-banīn — Has He **chosen** daughters over sons?*

The verb is *iṣṭafā,* Form VIII of the root *ṣ-f-w* — the root of *ṣafwa,* the choicest part of a thing, the cream skimmed from the top, the select and the pure. *Iṣṭafā* is not "to pick" in some neutral way; it is *to single out as the very best.* So the question drips: did God *select as His finest, His elite,* the daughters — the very ones you yourselves discard?

And look at how the word is spelled, because here the Arabic does something you can see with your own eyes. The verb *iṣṭafā* normally opens with a soft connecting *hamza* — *hamzat al-waṣl,* the little elidable catch that only gets you into the word. Here that connecting hamza has been swallowed and replaced by the **hamza of interrogation** — the *alif* of the question, carrying a full *fatḥa: a-ṣṭafā.* Al-Jalalayn notes exactly this: the interrogative hamza is opened and *stands in for the deleted connecting hamza.* And al-Tabari adds why it lands the way it does — when the Arabs aim the interrogative at *tawbīkh,* at rebuke, they fix that questioning *alif* firmly at the front. So even the *orthography* is a rebuke. The word is spelled like a raised eyebrow.

And here is what is so surgically precise. Allah does not even need to refute the theology head-on in this verse. He just reflects their own value-system back at them. *You despise daughters. You assign daughters to God. Now — how do you rule?* The contradiction is theirs. They built it. He simply holds up the mirror.

This is why the verse that follows is so devastatingly flat:

> مَا لَكُمْ كَيْفَ تَحْكُمُونَ
>
> *What is wrong with you? How do you reach such a ruling?*

There's almost a pause for breath in it. Like a judge taking off his glasses and looking at the claimant in genuine disbelief. *Walk me through your reasoning here. Because you have just assigned to the Lord of the heavens the one thing you yourselves cannot bear to receive.*

What does *ifk* reveal? **That the worst thing we do to God is not merely to speak falsely about Him — it is to invert reality so completely that we project our own contempt upward and call it theology.** And we'll come back to that, because that inversion is not ancient. It is alive in every heart, including ours.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — *Subḥān Allah… illā ʿibāda llāhi l-mukhlaṣīn*: the glory, and the exception

But before the glory, one last charge is entered into the record — the strangest of them all:

> وَجَعَلُوا۟ بَيْنَهُۥ وَبَيْنَ ٱلْجِنَّةِ نَسَبًۭا
>
> *And they invented, between Him and the jinn, a kinship.*

Here the classical commentators genuinely part ways over what this *nasab* — this claimed lineage — actually was. Al-Tabari reports that the scholars of interpretation *differed:* some said the mushrikūn had gone so far as to claim that Allah and Iblīs were *brothers.* Al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar read it differently — that "the jinn" here are the **angels** themselves, called *jinnah* from the root *j-n-n,* to conceal, *because they are veiled from human sight* (the same root that gives us *jannah,* the garden hidden beneath its canopy, and *junna,* a shield) — and the invented kinship is the same old claim: *these hidden ones are His daughters.* Either way, a bloodline has been drawn between the Creator and the created.

And then the verse turns and does something quietly devastating:

> وَلَقَدْ عَلِمَتِ ٱلْجِنَّةُ إِنَّهُمْ لَمُحْضَرُونَ
>
> *And the jinn already know that they — the ones who say this — will be brought forth.*

*Lamuḥḍarūn.* Another passive participle — hold onto that word, because it is about to matter enormously. *Muḥḍar* is one who is *made to be present, summoned, hauled in.* Al-Jalalayn is explicit: it is the ones who *make* this claim who will be *brought* — to the Fire. The very beings they slandered as God's kin already know the fate awaiting the slanderers. The witnesses the defense called have turned state's evidence.

Now the close. And there is a grammatical feature here so subtle and so beautiful that most people read straight past it.

After the entire dismantling, the passage delivers the only verdict that was ever true:

> [ayah:37:159] سُبْحَـٰنَ ٱللَّهِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ
>
> *Subḥāna llāhi ʿammā yaṣifūn.*

*Yaṣifūn* — from *w-ṣ-f,* to describe, to ascribe attributes, to render a portrait of someone. The whole passage has been about *descriptions of God* — false ones. Daughters. Offspring. Kinship with the jinn. And now Allah gathers them all up and pronounces Himself **above** them: *subḥān* — utter transcendence, distance, purity from every deficiency.

Notice the structure of the phrase itself. *Subḥān* is not a verb — it is a verbal noun, a *maṣdar,* used as a declaration, functioning as its own complete sentence. It does not say *Allah is glorious* — it performs the glorification in the act of utterance. You say *subḥān* and the act is already ringing out, complete, beyond what any following word can amplify. And *ʿammā* — *ʿan + mā* — sweeps everything in: *from whatever they describe, however they describe it, in all the ways human speech reaches toward Him and falls short.* The verb *yaṣifūn* is present tense and open-ended — the descriptions keep coming, generation after generation, each one grasping — and the *subḥān* rises above all of them, all at once.

*Glory be to Him, far beyond everything they describe.*

You'd expect the passage to end right there. The hammer falls. Full stop.

But it doesn't end there. There is one more verse, and it begins with one of the most important small words in the Arabic language:

> [ayah:37:160] إِلَّا عِبَادَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْمُخْلَصِينَ
>
> *Illā* — **except** — the chosen, purified servants of Allah.

*Illā* is an exception particle. It carves something out of the preceding statement. But the question — and this is where you must slow down — is: *excepted from what?*

The verse says: glory be to Allah, far above what they describe — *except the purified servants.*

Are the purified servants excepted from the description? Meaning their descriptions of God are not among the false ones? Or are they excepted from the preceding company — the people issuing baseless rulings, assigning daughters, inventing kinship with the jinn?

The answer is both, and that is not ambiguity but precision. The *mukhlaṣīn* are those who have been lifted entirely out of the architecture of *ifk.* They do not project. They do not invert. They do not issue verdicts they were never qualified to issue. Their *vaṣf* — their description of God — is not *ifk.* So they stand apart from the crowd and apart from the error simultaneously.

The classical grammarians give this its technical name. Al-Jalalayn parses the *illā* here as *istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ* — a **disconnected** exception, the kind where the thing carved out was never truly inside the category to begin with. On that reading, the *mukhlaṣīn* were never among the false describers at all; the sense bends toward *but as for the purified servants — they* declare Him free of everything these others ascribe. They are not the surviving remnant of a guilty class. They are a different class entirely.

And al-Muyassar, glossing this very last word of the passage, reaches back — and picks up the exact verb the mushrikūn had abused. The purified servants, he says, are *the ones Allah chose* — *alladhīna ṣṭafāhum.* *Iṣṭafā.* The root *ṣ-f-w* once more: the choosing of the choicest. In verse 153 the polytheists were mocked for imagining that God *iṣṭafā* — selected as His finest — the daughters they themselves despised. And here, at the close, we are shown who God actually *did* select as His pure and His chosen: not a gender, not a category the human imagination assigns Him, but the servants He Himself refined. The word they misused to build their *ifk* is the very word that names the ones who escaped it.

[PAUSE]

Now the most important thing about this word. The grammar.

*Al-mukhlaṣīn* is a **passive** participle of the Form IV verb *akhlāṣa* — to make something pure, to refine it, to draw it out from dross. In Form IV, you are the agent of that refining. But *mukhlaṣ* is passive. It is not *mukhlisīn* — those who *are sincere, who purify themselves.* It is *mukhlaṣīn* — **those who have been purified. Those whom Allah has refined.** The agent is God. They are the object of His action, not the subject of their own.

Sit with that distinction, because the whole passage hinges on it.

The polytheists of Makkah were intensely *active* in their theology. They were *doing things* — assigning categories, issuing rulings, constructing genealogies between God and the jinn, deciding which gender to hand up to the Creator. They were the agents of their own religious imagination, projecting it outward and upward with great confidence. And they arrived at *ifk*: the upside-down world.

The *mukhlaṣīn* are the opposite in structure, not merely in content. They did not seize their own purity. They were *selected into it.* They were *refined* — the way silver is refined, the way grain is winnowed — by a process whose agent is Allah alone.

[PAUSE]

And now go back to the parade that opened the surah.

Nuh: answered when he called out. The form is passive — he *was answered.* Ibrahim: ransomed at the last moment — *We ransomed him.* Musa and Harun: *We saved them from great distress.* Ilyas: his people were sent peace from above. Yunus: *drawn out* from the belly of the fish, *taken into mercy.* The prophets did not rescue themselves. They were rescued. They did not purify themselves. They were purified.

*Mukhlaṣīn* is the grammar of the entire surah's theology. The servants who appear at the end of this passage are not those who argued most cleverly about the divine nature or who issued the most rigorous fatwās. They are the ones about whom a different verb is used — the passive verb. The ones Allah chose to refine. The ones who, having been slowly emptied of their own projections, became fit vessels for the *vaṣf* that is true: the description that flows from God Himself, not from the human appetite to define and manage Him.

What does *illā l-mukhlaṣīn* reveal? **That the answer to motivated reasoning about God is not more and better reasoning — it is a different kind of being. One you do not arrive at by effort alone, but by being, in some irreducible sense, re-made.**

## PART 2: THE MIRROR TURNED INWARD

We said earlier that *ifk* is not ancient. Let's make good on that.

Here is the structure the passage has given us. You have a claim about God. That claim is built on something — a preference, a fear, an inheritance from the culture you were born into, a desire to keep certain things comfortable and close. And because the source of the claim is not revelation but your own interiority, the claim inverts: the thing you most desire gets kept at the human level, and upward you send a diminished thing, a projected thing, a thing shaped like your convenience or your contempt.

The Quraysh kept the sons and sent up the daughters. The structure of *their* projection was visible because their contempt was visible — written on their faces every time a girl was born, legible to everyone in the room.

Our projections tend to be less visible. But they follow the same grammar.

We construct a God who always agrees with our politics. We construct a God who is angry at exactly the people we are angry at, whose mercy extends exactly as far as our circle and not one inch further. We construct a God whose demands land conveniently on others and not on us specifically. We assign to Him our cultural assumptions, our anxieties about the world, our need for the universe to ratify what we already believed before we opened the text. We keep the sovereignty — the right to define and categorize and judge — for ourselves, and we hand upward something shaped like religion but built from preference.

And then we issue the ruling.

[PAUSE]

The passage has a question for you. A restrained one, but it does not leave:

*Mā lakum, kayfa taḥkumūn.* What is the matter with you? How are you reaching this verdict?

Not the verdict about the angels. Yours. The one you carry in your chest about who God is, what He values, who He loves, what He finds acceptable. Where did that verdict come from? Were you *present* when it was established? Is there a *book* you can place before the court? Or did it come from — and here is where the word *ifk* cuts quietly — your own arrangement of what you value most and what you find hardest to receive?

[PAUSE]

This is not an accusation. It is an invitation to a different relationship with uncertainty.

The *mukhlaṣīn* — the purified ones — are not those who arrived at a perfectly accurate theology through rigorous effort. They are those who were *emptied.* Whose projections were, over time, drawn out of them. Whose *ifk* — the inversions they didn't even know they were carrying — was slowly set right, not by their own cleverness, but by something working on them from outside themselves. This is why *subḥān* is not merely a phrase for the opening of *tasbeeh.* It is a posture of the soul. A practised returning to: *He is above what I can manage to think about Him. My description is not the destination. Let me hold it more loosely than I held it yesterday.*

This is why the passage begins with the courtroom and ends with glory. Because the movement it wants to produce in you is from the aggressive *istiftāʾ* posture — *I issue verdicts on divine matters; I know how to rule* — to the passive *mukhlaṣ* posture: *I have been, or I am being, purified of the need to control what cannot be contained. I return to He who is above all description.*

The gavel falls.

> سُبْحَٰنَ ٱللَّهِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ
>
> *Glory be to Allah, far above everything they describe.*

And in that *everything,* there is space — quietly, without self-condemnation — to include our own incomplete descriptions too. And still keep turning toward Him.
