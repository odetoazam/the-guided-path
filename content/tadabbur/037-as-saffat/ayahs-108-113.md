# The Praise He Left Unspoken: Ibrahim and the Reward That Outlives Death

## Introduction: Standing at the Foot of the Mountain

Let me give you the words first, slowly, before we go anywhere with them.

> [ayah:37:108] وَتَرَكْنَا عَلَيْهِ فِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرِينَ
> *wa taraknā ʿalayhi fil-ākhirīn*
> **And We left for him, among the later generations —**

> [ayah:37:109] سَلَـٰمٌ عَلَىٰٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ
> *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm*
> **"Peace be upon Ibrahim."**

> [ayah:37:110] كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ
> *kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn*
> **Thus do We reward those who do good.**

> [ayah:37:111] إِنَّهُۥ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ
> *innahu min ʿibādinā al-mu'minīn*
> **Indeed, he was of Our believing servants.**

> [ayah:37:112] وَبَشَّرْنَـٰهُ بِإِسْحَـٰقَ نَبِيًّا مِّنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ
> *wa bashsharnāhu bi-Isḥāqa nabiyyan min aṣ-ṣāliḥīn*
> **And We gave him good tidings of Ishaq — a prophet, from among the righteous.**

> [ayah:37:113] وَبَـٰرَكْنَا عَلَيْهِ وَعَلَىٰٓ إِسْحَـٰقَ ۚ وَمِن ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا مُحْسِنٌ وَظَالِمٌ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ مُبِينٌ
> *wa bāraknā ʿalayhi wa ʿalā Isḥāq — wa min dhurriyyatihimā muḥsinun wa ẓālimun li-nafsihi mubīn*
> **And We blessed him, and Ishaq. And among their descendants is the doer of good — and the one clearly unjust to his own self.**

Now let me rebuild the ground you have to be standing on for these words to land.

A few verses earlier, something happened that the human heart can barely hold. An old man — a man who waited his whole life for a son, who prayed *rabbi hab lī min aṣ-ṣāliḥīn*, "my Lord, grant me one of the righteous" — finally has the boy. And in a dream, he sees himself slaughtering him. He tells the boy. And the boy, this child, says: *yā abati fʿal mā tu'mar* — "father, do what you are commanded; you will find me, God willing, among the patient."

Then the two of them — both of them — submit. The father lays his son down on his forehead. The knife is real. The arm is raised. And at the edge of the unbearable, the call comes: *qad ṣaddaqta ar-ru'yā* — "you have already fulfilled the vision." A great sacrifice is sent down in the boy's place.

[PAUSE]

That is where we are standing. The knife has just been lowered. The boy is alive. The father is still trembling. And now — right now — Allah turns from the action and speaks the reward. Six verses of reward.

Here is the question I want you to carry up this mountain with us. When you have just passed the hardest test a human being has ever been asked to pass — when you have proven, with a raised knife, that there is nothing in this world you will not surrender for your Lord — **what reward could possibly match that?**

What do you give the man who proved he would give you everything?

The answer Allah gives is not what you would guess. It is stranger, and quieter, and infinitely larger. And part of it is sitting in your own mouth right now, every single day, and you may never have noticed.

Stay with me.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Taraknā* — "We Left." The Verb of a Man Who Left Everything.

Before we touch the word, let me give you the idea underneath it.

There are two ways to lose something and two ways to leave something. You can *lose* a thing — it slips from you, against your will, and it's gone. Or you can *leave* a thing — you set it down on purpose, you walk away from it, you choose. And when you leave something behind you, one of two things is true: either you've abandoned it and it's dead to you, or you've *planted* it — you've left it in the ground so that it stays there, growing, long after you've moved on.

Hold those two senses of "leaving." We'll need both.

Now look at the first word Allah chooses:

**وَتَرَكْنَا** — *wa taraknā* — "And We left."

The root is **ت-ر-ك** (*t-r-k*): to leave, to leave behind, to leave something standing in a place. It's a simple, first-form verb — *taraka, yatruku* — the most ordinary word for leaving. And here it's in the first-person plural of majesty: *We* left.

Now — here is the thing I need you to notice, and it's a thing about **absence**.

In Arabic, this verb wants an object. *We left* — left *what?* You leave a *thing*. The sentence is grammatically reaching out its hand for a noun, and look —

Allah does not give one.

*Wa taraknā ʿalayhi fil-ākhirīn.* "And We left, upon him, among the later generations." Left *what* upon him? The verse simply does not say. The object is missing. There is a hole in the sentence exactly where the gift should be named.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators all step into that silence and supply the same word. Al-Jalālayn glosses *taraknā* here as *abqaynā* — "We preserved, We kept in place" — and then supplies the object: *thanā'an ḥasanā*, beautiful praise. That gloss, *abqaynā*, matters. To *leave* a thing and to *preserve* a thing are close in meaning but not identical. When you leave something, it may or may not stay — it depends on forces outside you. When you preserve something, you are actively holding it in place against everything that would wear it down across time. The classical word choice suggests Allah is not merely *not removing* the praise from his name. He is *maintaining* it, upholding it, ensuring it survives every wind of history that would try to erode it. Al-Muyassar renders it simply but with its full force: *wa abqaynā li-Ibrāhīma thanā'an ḥasanan fī al-umami baʿdahu* — "We preserved for Ibrahim beautiful praise among the nations that came after him." Al-Qurṭubī goes further and tells you what that praise sounds like in practice: *fa-mā min ummatin illā tuṣallī ʿalayhi wa tuḥibbuhu* — **"there is no nation except that it sends prayers upon him and loves him."** Every community. No exceptions.

So the scholars agree: what Allah "left" — preserved, kept alive — was *beautiful, undying mention.*

But ask yourself — if that's what it means, why didn't Allah just *say* it? Why leave the noun out?

Here is what the silence does. **The moment you name the gift, you have measured it.** Say "We left him *beautiful praise*," and the praise has edges; it is a certain amount of a certain thing. But leave the word out — leave the verb open with its hand extended — and the gift expands to fill every space the listener can imagine. Praise, yes. And love. And imitation. And the fact that his name is a station of prayer in every mosque on earth. And that nations who agree on nothing agree on him. The unnamed object becomes **whatever good can possibly cling to a human name across all of time.** By saying less, Allah gave more. The blank is the size of the gift.

And now feel the second thing — the thing that makes this verb almost unbearably fitting.

Who is Ibrahim? Ibrahim is the man who *left.* He left his father. He left his people and their idols. He left his homeland — *innī muhājirun ilā rabbī*, "I am emigrating to my Lord." He left his wife and his infant son in a barren valley with no water, on a command, and walked away while she called after him. His entire life is a series of leavings. He is the great *tārik* — the one who lets go.

And what does Allah say in return? With the very same verb of his whole life —

**"And We left upon him."**

[PAUSE]

He left everything *for* Allah. So Allah left something *on* him that no power on earth can erase. He was the one who let go — and so he became the one who is *held onto*, forever, by every generation that comes after. The man who kept nothing is the man who is never put down.

That is what one word reveals: *the leaving you do for Allah's sake is the only kind of leaving that comes back to you and stays.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm* — Peace With No Verb, No Edges, No End.

Now we arrive at the gift itself — at the words Allah "left." And the first thing to notice is that the sentence breaks.

Let me teach you something small about Arabic that opens this completely.

Arabic has two kinds of sentences. One has a verb in it — *"the peace descended,"* *"We greet him with peace"* — and the moment you put a verb in, you've put the action **in time**. Verbs happen. They start, they occur, they finish. The other kind of sentence has no verb at all — just a thing, and what is said about it. *"Peace upon Ibrahim."* No verb. Grammarians call this a nominal sentence, and its quality is **permanence, fixedness, a state that simply *is*** — not an event that occurred, but a reality that stands.

Now look at what Allah chose:

**سَلَـٰمٌ عَلَىٰٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ** — *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm.*

No verb. He did not say "We send peace upon Ibrahim" — which would be an action, done once, at a moment. He said, simply, **"Peace — upon Ibrahim."** A standing reality, with no clock attached to it. It does not happen and finish. It *is*, and keeps being. Al-Jalālayn anchors this beautifully in two words: *salāmun minnā* — "peace from Us." Not a wish sent upward by creation, but a declaration issued downward from the One who holds authority over every condition of existence. A thing Allah declares does not require further maintenance. It simply stands.

Now look closer, at the word *salām* itself.

**سَلَـٰمٌ** — *salāmun*. The root is **س-ل-م** (*s-l-m*): wholeness, soundness, safety, being made intact and free of defect. And notice: it is *indefinite*. Not *as-salām*, "the peace" — but *salāmun*, "peace," with the open tail of the indefinite, the *tanwīn*. In Arabic, when you leave a noun indefinite like this in a statement of blessing, it doesn't make it smaller — it makes it *vast and unbounded.* It means: peace **of a kind beyond specifying**, peace without a named limit, *some peace whose extent you cannot fence in.* A whole, sound, complete safety, of an amount no one will measure.

Al-Muyassar captures this amplitude precisely: the *salāmun* of this verse is *taḥiyyatun li-Ibrāhīma min ʿindi Allāhi, wa duʿā'un lahu bi-as-salāma min kulli āfa* — "a greeting to Ibrahim from Allah, and a supplication for his safety from every affliction." That phrase, *min kulli āfa* — from *every* affliction — gives the boundlessness a shape. The indefinite noun is not vague; it is exhaustive. The *salām* covers every category of harm, leaves no variety of blight or diminishment or corruption unclaimed. It is a protection with no exceptions clause.

[PAUSE]

So put it together. A verbless sentence — meaning *timeless.* An indefinite noun — meaning *boundless.* A divine source — meaning *authoritative and permanent.* A coverage of every affliction — meaning *complete.* Allah did not give Ibrahim a peace that happened on a Tuesday in the desert. He gave him **a peace with no edges in space, no end in time, and no category of harm left unaddressed.**

And here is where I need to show you something that I think will change how you hear this passage for the rest of your life.

Go back to the first verse. *We left upon him, among the later generations* — something. The praise, the undying mention. And then the very next words tell you what that "something" actually *is*:

**"Peace be upon Ibrahim."**

Do you see what just happened? The thing Allah "left" among the later generations is *these exact words.* The reward is not a description of praise somewhere else. **The reward is the sentence you are reading.** Allah left, on the tongues of everyone who would ever come after, the words *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm* — and then He printed those very words right here, in the Book that the later generations would recite until the end of the world.

Which means — *you.* When you recite this verse, when you say *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm*, **you are the later generation.** You are the reward being paid out. You are the promise of verse 108 coming true *in your own mouth.* The ayah does not just *describe* the gift — it *enacts* it. Every time it is read, the leaving renews itself. The praise He left is still being left, right now, through you.

[PAUSE]

And lift your eyes for one moment to the whole surah, because this is not happening only here. Walk back through As-Saffat and you hear it tolling like a bell: *salāmun ʿalā Nūḥ* — peace upon Nuh (37:79). *Salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm* — here. *Salāmun ʿalā Mūsā wa Hārūn* — peace upon Musa and Harun (37:120). *Salāmun ʿalā Ilyāsīn* (37:130). And then, at the very end of the surah, the bell rings one last time over all of them together: *wa salāmun ʿalā al-mursalīn* — and peace upon all the messengers (37:181).

This is a refrain. It is the chorus of the whole surah. And what is a refrain? **It is something built to be repeated** — something the later generations would carry on their tongues. The very *form* of the surah is the reward. Allah designed a song of peace that He knew would be sung forever, and He wrote His servants' names into the chorus. That is what it means to be "left among the later generations." You become part of the song the world cannot stop singing.

What this one verbless, boundless sentence reveals: *the reward for the one who gave up everything in time was a peace that lives outside of time — and the proof of it is that you are pronouncing it right now.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Ẓālimun li-nafsihi mubīn* — The Wrongdoer Who Wrongs No One But Himself.

We come now to the last verse, and to a turn that should stop you cold.

Allah has just poured out honor. *We blessed him, and Ishaq.* The verb is from **ب-ر-ك** (*b-r-k*) — *barakah* — and its root sense is something *settling down and staying*, abundance that takes up residence and does not leave. The preposition Allah chooses is *ʿalā* — upon — not *fī* (in) or *li* (for). To bless *upon* someone is for the blessing to land and remain, like a weight that holds. He blesses the father and He blesses the son, and notice the dual that follows: *wa min dhurriyyatihimā* — "and from the offspring of **the two of them.**" The blessing flows down into a whole future, a river of descendants.

And then, at the very peak of the honor, in the same breath, Allah says something He did not have to say:

**وَمِن ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا مُحْسِنٌ وَظَالِمٌ لِّنَفْسِهِۦ مُبِينٌ**
*wa min dhurriyyatihimā muḥsinun wa ẓālimun li-nafsihi mubīn*
**"And among their descendants is the doer of good — and the one clearly unjust to his own self."**

Right here, at the summit of blessing, He tells you: the blessing on the parents **does not automatically descend into the children.** Among the very offspring of the two most honored fathers, there will be the *muḥsin* — the doer of good — **and** the *ẓālim* — the wrongdoer.

Sit with how startling that placement is. This is the moment for unbroken praise of the lineage. And instead Allah cuts in with a sober truth, because the truth is more merciful than the flattery would have been.

Now look at the asymmetry — because this is where the verse becomes precise to the point of tenderness.

Watch how Allah describes each.

The *muḥsin* — the doer of good — is named with nothing additional attached. Just *muḥsinun*. He does good. His doing is unqualified, uncontained. It reaches outward into the world. It benefits others. It carries no limiting preposition.

But the *ẓālim* — the wrongdoer — carries a prepositional phrase built like a vessel to hold the harm inside: *li-nafsihi*. **To his own self.** The wrong turns inward. It does not overflow. It does not leak into the blessing or poison the river of the lineage or render the *barakah* on Ibrahim and Ishaq void. He wrongs no one but himself.

And then: *mubīn* — **clear, manifest, unmistakable.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel the full weight of what just happened. You might hear *mubīn* as a condemnation — and yes, in one sense it is. The wrongdoing is not hidden. He cannot shelter under the family name, cannot invoke Ibrahim's legacy to excuse himself. The *ẓulm* is visible and attributable and his alone.

But *mubīn* also does something more merciful. It seals the boundary. The wrong is clear *and contained.* Everyone can see exactly what it is, exactly whose it is — which means no one has to spread it outward, enlarge it, make it about the family or the lineage or the blessing on the fathers. The clarity of *mubīn* is a kind of protection for everyone else. It says: *this is his account, not yours.* The sin is attributed with precision so it cannot be inherited.

[PAUSE]

And here is the deeper point this verse is making, placed exactly where it is for a precise reason.

Someone in every generation will hear this passage and think: *Ibrahim's family. The blessed lineage. The prophetic line. Surely those descended from him carry some of that light automatically.* And this verse — right here, in the moment of highest honor — says quietly but firmly: **No.** The blessing is real. The *barakah* is real. But between the ancestor's blessing and the descendant's soul, there is a door that only the descendant can open. *Muḥsinun wa ẓālimun* — doer of good *and* wrongdoer — both emerge from the same blessed root. They are not determined by it.

This is the verse that closes the door on spiritual inheritance as automatic entitlement. And it opens another door: if you cannot *inherit* faith from the greatest lineage in human history, then you also cannot be *condemned* by the lineage you actually come from. The accounting is personal. The standing before Allah is personal. Ibrahim's descendants are not saved by being Ibrahim's descendants; and the rest of humanity is not disadvantaged by not being Ibrahim's descendants. The same principle that strips entitlement from the blessed strips condemnation from the unblessed. **You stand on your own account.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Four: *Bashsharnāhu bi-Isḥāq* — The Second Opening of the Hand.

There is one more thing you need to see, and it requires standing back from the passage to notice its architecture.

Allah announces, in verse 112, the glad tidings of Ishaq — *wa bashsharnāhu bi-Isḥāqa nabiyyan min aṣ-ṣāliḥīn* — as if it is *new information.* A new gift, a new announcement, positioned after the sacrifice narrative has already closed. And this placement was not lost on the classical scholars.

Al-Tabari documents a major disagreement among the early commentators — one of the most significant *ikhtilāfāt* in the entire Abrahamic narrative of the Quran: which son was nearly sacrificed? Some held it was Ishaq. Others — and among them many of the earliest and most authoritative voices — held it was Ismail. And the *argument from this very verse's placement* was one of the classical evidences for the latter position.

If Ishaq had been the one laid on the ground with the knife above him, why would the glad tidings of Ishaq arrive here, after the sacrifice, as if they were something new? You do not announce a person's prophethood as fresh news after you have just told the story of his near-death. The sequential logic is strange. But if the son ransomed with a great sacrifice was Ismail — the one born of Hagar, the first son, the one Ibrahim was commanded to leave in the barren valley — then the announcement of Ishaq here makes perfect sense. It is a *second* opening of the divine hand: Ismail was the son of the sacrifice; Ishaq is the son of an additional glad tiding, another mercy layered upon the mercy.

The scholars who held this view were not marginal. The classical disagreement is real, well-documented, and has never been fully resolved. What we can say with confidence is this: the *positioning* of the Ishaq announcement — after the sacrifice, as a new glad tiding — carries weight. The Quran is precise about what it places where. Here, the sequence itself is part of the meaning. And the meaning is abundance: even after everything has already been given, the hand opens again.

[PAUSE]

Notice too the title given to Ishaq in this announcement: *nabiyyan min aṣ-ṣāliḥīn* — "a prophet from among the righteous." The gladness of the tidings is doubled. He is not merely promised — he is already characterized as both prophet and righteous before he has lived a day. This is the divine way of announcing a child: not just that he will be born, but what he will be, what he will carry. It is a gift named not only in its existence but in its quality.

And then *bāraknā ʿalayhi wa ʿalā Isḥāq* — "We blessed him and Ishaq." Father and son. Tied together with a blessing that the preposition *ʿalā* — upon — makes tangible: it lands on them, it rests on them, it holds. This is not a general well-wishing. This is the *barakah* that, according to the root *b-r-k*, *takes up residence and stays.* A camel kneels and does not rise. The blessing settles and does not depart.

So what do you have by the end of verse 113? Two men blessed. A blessing that holds fast. And then a lineage explicitly freed from the assumption that the blessing on the fathers descends automatically into every son. The passage is architecturally complete: gift, gift, gift — then clarity about what the gift is and is not.

## Part Two: The Question That Was Never About Ibrahim

Now let me return to the question I asked you at the beginning, because we are finally standing somewhere you can see its answer.

**What do you give the man who proved he would give you everything?**

We have now gone word by word through six verses of response, and here is what the response contains:

An unnamed gift — the size of a blank, which is the size of every good thing that could cling to a name across all of time.

An undying, verbless peace — from Allah Himself, covering every category of harm, with no clock attached to it.

A second glad tiding — Ishaq, the prophet from among the righteous — arriving as if the first gift were not already enough.

A blessing that lands and holds on two men and flows into a lineage.

And then — most startlingly, most honestly, most mercifully — the truth that this blessing is not a guarantee. It flows; it does not compel. The children will be who the children choose to be.

[PAUSE]

What you are watching Allah give Ibrahim is not a trophy. It is not a certificate. It is not even a crown. What He gives him is *a position in the ongoing story of creation itself.* Every time a human being says *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm*, he is there. Every time a prophet after him builds on the foundation of submission he laid — and they all do — he is there. Every time the word *muslim* is spoken and someone understands it as *one who surrenders* — that understanding traces its lineage to the man who surrendered first, most completely, at the edge of the impossible.

The reward for giving up everything was not *getting back everything.* That would be too small. The reward was becoming *part of everything.* The man who left everything became the man who is everywhere.

[PAUSE — major pause]

And here is the part that is not about Ibrahim.

The verse that declares his reward also declares the principle: *kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn* — **"Thus do We reward those who do good."** *Kadhālika* — "thus," "like this," "in this manner." What was given to Ibrahim is named as an instance of a general rule. It is not the exception reserved for one tested man in one desert in one moment of history. It is the *type* of reward Allah gives — which means it is available as a type.

This is not a claim that every person who practices *ihsān* will have their name recited in a surah. The parallel is not that precise. The parallel is structural: **what you leave for Allah does not disappear.** It is preserved, *abqaynā*, kept in place by the same power that kept Ibrahim's name in place. The leaving you do for Allah — the relationship you end, the comfort you surrender, the position you walk away from, the attachment you set down — does not slip into nothing. It lands somewhere in the unseen, and it stays.

The question the passage leaves you with is not a theological one. It is a personal one.

**What are you leaving?**

[PAUSE]

Ibrahim's entire life was structured by *tark* — by deliberate, faithful letting go. What he was willing to set down, Allah picked up and kept. The principle is not complicated. The application — in a life where the attachments are real, where the leavings are costly — is the hard thing. But the verse exists precisely to tell you that the cost is not wasted. It is deposited. It returns in a form and at a time that your present moment cannot see.

## Conclusion: The Praise Already in Your Mouth

Let me bring you back to where we started.

You are reading a verse that contains a man's reward. The reward is *these words.* The verse describes what was left for him among the later generations — and then the very next line is it: *salāmun ʿalā Ibrāhīm.* You are reading the reward. You are, right now, being the later generation in whose mouth the leaving lives.

Every time you recite Surah As-Saffat, or hear it, or read it — you are paying out what Allah promised. You are the mechanism of the miracle. The word *taraknā* stretches from the moment Ibrahim lowered the knife in the desert to the moment you are in right now, and it is still continuous, still unbroken, because people like you are still saying the name.

[PAUSE]

And the sober note at the end — the *muḥsinun wa ẓālim* — is not there to dampen anything. It is there to keep the passage honest. The man who earned the undying peace, the man whose name you carry on your tongue, is not your guarantor. He is your *model.* His reward is not transferable. His example is.

What he modeled was a specific kind of trust: *that the thing you surrender to Allah is not lost but held.* He modeled it at the knife's edge, with his son's face beneath him and the command of his Lord above him. He modeled it in the barren valley where he walked away from Hagar and the infant and did not look back. He modeled it in the homeland he left and the father he left and the people he left. His whole life was a demonstration that the leaving done for Allah is the *safest possible leaving* — the only leaving that returns to you, in the form of a peace no enemy can take, in a story no time can end.

*Kadhālika najzī al-muḥsinīn.* Thus do We reward those who do good.

The "thus" is the invitation. The "We" is the guarantee. The "muḥsinīn" is the door — and it is open, and your name is not required to already be written in a surah for you to walk through it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Go back now to the beginning of these six verses. Stand where we started — at the foot of the mountain, knife just lowered, boy just spared, father still trembling.

*What reward could possibly match that?*

Now you know the answer. Not gold. Not kingdoms. Not even the return of the son, though the son was returned.

**A peace, from Allah, without edges or end, preserved across all of time, pronounced right now in your mouth.**

That is the reward. That is the leaving that stays. That is what Allah does with everything you give Him.
