# The One Already Walking

## Introduction: A Man With His Back to the Fire

Let me put you somewhere first. Before a single word of Arabic, I want you to stand where Ibrahim is standing.

The fire has just gone out.

His people built it to kill him. They piled the wood so high that — the narrations tell us — they could not even get close enough to throw him in; they had to launch him by catapult. And then the command came down to the flames themselves: *"O fire, be coolness and peace upon Ibrahim."* He walked out untouched.

Now here is the part we rush past. **What do you do the morning after your whole society tried to burn you alive — and failed?**

You are vindicated. The miracle is undeniable. If there were ever a moment to stay, to plant your flag, to say *"See? My God is real and yours are nothing"* — it is now. The crowd has seen it with their own eyes. Stay, and you might win.

Ibrahim does not stay.

He turns around. He leaves the land of his birth, the graves of his ancestors, the only home, the only language, the only faces he has ever known — and he walks out into nothing. No map. No companions named. No destination you could point to on the ground.

And as he walks, he says six words. Listen to them in Arabic, even if the sounds are unfamiliar:

> **وَقَالَ إِنِّى ذَاهِبٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّى سَيَهْدِينِ**
>
> *Wa qāla innī dhāhibun ilā rabbī sayahdīn.*
>
> "And he said: Indeed, I am going to my Lord. He will guide me."

Here is the question I want to hang over this entire reflection, and I am not going to answer it yet. I want you to feel its weight first.

**How do you walk somewhere when you do not know where you are going?**

Not as a poetic flourish. As a literal problem. Ibrahim says *"I am going."* Present tense. He is in motion *now.* And in the same breath he says *"He will guide me"* — future tense. The guidance hasn't come yet.

[PAUSE]

So he is already walking. And the directions are still on their way.

Most of us would call that recklessness. The Quran calls it the foundation of faith. By the end of this, I want you to understand the difference — and I want you to know which one you are living by.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: ذَاهِبٌ — The Word That Is Already Moving

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because the whole insight lives in a distinction English blurs.

In English, "go" is just a verb. *I go. I will go. I went.* You change the tense and you're done. But Arabic has a category English barely has — a form called the **active participle**, the *ism al-fāʿil.* It does not name an *action.* It names a **doer caught in the doing.**

Think of the difference between *"she sings"* and *"she is a singer."* "She sings" is something she does. "She is a singer" is something she *is* — it has soaked into her identity. The active participle is that second thing. It takes a verb and turns it into a state of being. Not *"he runs"* but *"he is a runner — a running-one."*

Now look at what Ibrahim does *not* say.

He does not say **سَأَذْهَبُ** (*sa-adhhab*) — "I will go." The future verb. The thing you say when departure is still a plan.

He does not say **ذَهَبْتُ** (*dhahabtu*) — "I went." The past verb. The thing you say when it's over.

He says **ذَاهِبٌ** — *dhāhib.* The active participle. **"I am a going-one."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment, because it is doing something the other forms could never do.

A future verb — *"I will go"* — keeps you on the near shore. It is a promise about tomorrow, and tomorrow can always be postponed. A past verb seals it behind you. But the active participle? It places him **inside the motion right now.** Going is no longer something Ibrahim *will do* or *has done.* It is what he *is.* The leaving has stopped being an event on his calendar and become a fact about his person. He is not a man planning a journey. **He is a journey.**

And notice — it is **indefinite.** *Dhāhibun*, with the *tanwīn*, the little *-un* at the end. Not *"the going-one,"* a fixed title, but simply *"a going-one,"* open, ongoing, unbounded. There is no full stop in this word. The motion doesn't terminate at a city wall. It just… continues.

Here is why this is load-bearing and not decoration. **You cannot postpone an identity.** You can postpone a plan forever — *I'll leave that job next year, I'll quit that habit after Ramadan, I'll make that change when things settle down.* The future verb is the language of the comfortable delay. But Ibrahim reached for the one grammatical form that **cannot be deferred,** because it describes not what he intends but what he already is. He didn't say "I will become someone who leaves." He said: *the leaving is already me.*

When the heart finally moves, it does not speak in the future tense. It speaks in the present participle.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: إِلَىٰ رَبِّي — The Destination That Is a Person

Now follow where the participle points. *Dhāhibun ilā…* — "going to…" — to *what?*

In Arabic, *ilā* is the preposition of destination. It demands an endpoint. *Going to* — your mind leans forward, waiting for the place. Going to where? To which land? To what city?

And here the Quran does something I need you to see by its **absence,** because what is missing is the whole point.

He does not name a place.

He says: **إِلَىٰ رَبِّي** — *ilā rabbī* — **"to my Lord."**

Now — the scholars tell us where his feet were actually headed. Al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, and al-Qurtubī all identify the physical destination as **al-Shām,** the blessed land, the region of Syria-Palestine. Al-Qurtubī calls this ayah *"the foundation of hijra and withdrawal"* — *aṣl fī al-hijra* — and says Ibrahim was the first human being to migrate for the sake of his Lord. Al-Baghawī links it to the parallel verse in Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt, *"Indeed, I am a migrant to my Lord."* So there *was* a real geography. His body was going to Shām.

Al-Jalālayn frames the departure with a legal precision that the verse's brevity can obscure: Ibrahim is *muhājir ilayhi min dār al-kufr* — "migrating to Him from the land of disbelief." This is not merely travel. It is a recognized category: the movement away from an environment where one cannot freely worship, toward the One being worshipped. Ibrahim leaves not because Babylon lost its beauty or his people became too difficult to endure, but because the land itself had become an obstacle between him and his Lord. His feet were moving, but the engine of the migration was entirely interior.

But that is not what the ayah says. **The ayah names no land at all.** Out of everything Ibrahim could have put after *ilā* — a country, a direction, a safety — he puts a **Person.** *To my Lord.*

Stay with this asymmetry, because it is exquisite. Al-Qurtubī even preserves a second reading from the early scholars: that *"I am going"* here means *going with my deeds, my worship, my heart and my intention* — *dhāhibun bi-ʿamalī wa-ʿibādatī wa-qalbī wa-niyyatī* — a departure of the soul, not only the body. The grammar leaves room for both at once. His feet are walking to Shām. But the **destination he names** is not a place on any map. It is a relationship.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter? Because **a place can fail you.** Shām could be conquered. A house can burn — his old one nearly took him with it. Every geographic destination is a thing that can be taken away, blocked, destroyed, or proven disappointing when you arrive. If your destination is a *place,* your peace is permanently hostage to whether you reach it and whether it holds.

But Ibrahim has made his destination **the one endpoint that cannot be taken, blocked, or lost.** *I am going to my Lord.* You cannot bomb that road. You cannot close that border. Wherever his feet land — Shām, Egypt, Makkah, the open desert — he has already arrived, because the One he is travelling toward is present at every point on the journey.

He didn't choose a *where.* He chose a *Whom.* And the moment your destination is a Person rather than a place, **you can never be lost** — only closer or farther in the heart, and that distance is the only distance He cares about.

Notice the possessive, too — *rabbī,* **my** Lord. Not *al-Rabb,* "the Lord," a theological abstraction. *My* Lord. The same intimacy a child reaches for in the dark. He has just been thrown out by his father, his people, his entire human web of belonging — and the one bond he asserts, the one tie that holds, is *mine.* He loses every human "my" in a single day and clings to the only *"my"* that was never his to lose.

There is one more detail al-Jalālayn lets slip that is easy to miss: when Ibrahim reached the Holy Land, *then* he prayed. Ayah 100 — the prayer for a righteous child — came after the journey was complete, not during it. He did not walk out of Babylon bargaining with God for companions on the road. He walked the full migration on nothing but *dhāhib* and *sayahdīn* — identity and trust — and only at the destination did he turn and ask. He arrived empty-handed, and *then* he opened his palms.

## Element Three: سَيَهْدِينِ — He Steps Before He Is Shown

Now the third word, and this is where the whole ayah detonates.

**سَيَهْدِينِ** — *sayahdīn* — "He will guide me."

Break it open. There are three pieces fused into one word.

First, the **سـ**, the *sīn* — a tiny prefix that does one job: it throws the verb into the **future,** and specifically the *near, certain* future. Not "He might guide me." Not "perhaps, one day." *Sa-* is the prefix of *imminence.* "He is about to guide me. The guidance is coming."

Second, the verb **يَهْدِي** — *yahdī,* from the root *hadā,* to guide — and it is in the **imperfect** tense, the ongoing, the not-yet-completed.

And third, listen to the very end of the word. By the rules of Arabic, "He will guide **me**" should end in a clear long *yā* — *sayahdī-**nī**.* But the Quran writes it, and Ibrahim says it, as **سَيَهْدِينِ** — *sayahdīn* — the final *yā,* the *"me,"* thinned almost to nothing, dropped to a quiet *kasra* underneath. This is a known feature of the Quran's verse-endings; the *yā* of *"me"* is pared away so the line lands clean. But hear what it *does* to the word, even if it's a matter of rhythm: the *"me"* in *"He will guide me"* is the softest, most surrendered sound in the sentence. The self shrinks. The "me" almost disappears into the promise.

And what precisely is the guidance promised? Al-Muyassar opens the word's full scope: Ibrahim trusts that his Lord will guide him to what is good *in his religion and in his worldly life.* Both. *Dīn* and *dunyā.* Not merely spiritual direction — not simply which road to take to reach the mosque — but the comprehensive stewardship of a Lord who cares about every dimension of the life He gave. The one who walks *ilā rabbī* is not surrendering his worldly affairs to chance while his soul is tended. He is trusting that the same Lord who guides the heart also holds the road, the provision, and the landing.

Now — put it all together with what came before, and feel the collision.

**ذَاهِبٌ** — *I am already going.* Present. Active. In motion. The participle of a man whose feet are moving right now.

**سَيَهْدِينِ** — *He **will** guide me.* Future. Not yet. The directions have not arrived.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it?

**He is already walking before the guidance comes.**

The order is everything. A reasonable person — a *careful* person, the person every instinct in us tells us to be — waits for the guidance *first.* Show me the way, *then* I'll step. Give me the plan, the map, the certainty, the five-year strategy, and *once I can see the road,* I will start walking it. That is how we all want to live. *Sayahdīn,* then *dhāhib.* Guidance first, motion second.

Ibrahim inverts it. He is *dhāhib* — already in motion — and *then* he says *sayahdīn* — the guidance is on its way. **He steps off the edge, and on the way down he says: my Lord will show me where to land.**

This is not recklessness. This is the deepest logic of *tawakkul,* of trust. Because look at *what* he is trusting. He is not trusting a plan — he has none. He is not trusting a place — he named none. He is trusting **the Guide Himself.** And here is the secret the grammar is screaming: **guidance is given to the one who is already walking.**

You cannot steer a parked car. *Hidāya,* guidance, is course-correction — it is the gentle hand on the wheel that says *not that way, this way.* But the hand only works if the wheels are turning. The one who waits, frozen, for total clarity before he moves will wait forever, because Allah does not hand the map to the stationary. He hands it to the traveller. **First you become *dhāhib.* Then comes *sayahdīn.*** First the step of trust into the dark — and the light arrives *to meet the foot already in the air.*

[PAUSE]

Let me say it as plainly as I can, because this is the hinge of everything.

The guidance was never the *condition* for the journey. The journey was the *condition* for the guidance.

### The Bridge

So look at what these six words have shown us. A man who made *going* his identity, not his plan. A man who chose a Person, not a place, as his destination — and so could never be lost. And a man who put his foot down into total darkness *trusting that the light would come because he had stepped.*

But this is not really about words. The grammar is just the doorway. Now let us ask what this reveals not about language — but about your life, and the leap you are probably refusing to take right now.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Leap You Take Before You Can See the Landing

Let me start where you actually live, far from any desert.

There is a thing you know you need to do. You've known it for a while.

Maybe it's leaving a job that is slowly hollowing you out, but the paycheck is real and the next thing is uncertain. Maybe it's ending a relationship that everyone but you can see is wrong, but the loneliness on the other side terrifies you more than the misery you're in. Maybe it's walking away from a sin that has its hand around your throat — and you can't imagine who you'd even *be* without it. Maybe it's a move, a marriage, a calling, a confession, a reconciliation. Maybe it's just *praying again* after years away, and you're frozen because you can't see how the whole rest of your life reorganizes itself once you start.

And what stops you — every single time — is the same thing. **You want to see the landing before you jump.**

You tell yourself it's prudence. *I just need to know it'll work out. I just need a little more certainty. Show me the plan, show me the guarantee, and then I'll move.* And so you stand at the edge, year after year, fully convinced that the day the map arrives is the day you'll finally step.

But the map never arrives to the one standing still.

[PAUSE]

This is the lie at the center of our paralysis: that clarity precedes the step. That you are being responsible by waiting. That the person who moves without a guarantee is reckless — and the person who stays frozen is wise.

Ibrahim was not wise by that definition. He was *something else entirely.* He understood that faith is not the absence of uncertainty; it is the willingness to move *inside* the uncertainty, trusting that the One who set you on the road is the same One who knows every bend of it. You do not need the map when you are walking with the Mapmaker.

And notice what the *sīn* in *sayahdīn* actually promises. It does not say: "Before you take a step, I will download the full itinerary." It says: *as you go, guidance will come.* The guidance is for the journey. It arrives in instalments, at each junction where the choice must be made, not handed over in full before you have left the house. And it covers everything — religion and worldly life both, as al-Muyassar makes plain. God is not waiting only to adjust your piety. He is attending to the whole road. But He attends *to the traveller,* not to the one who decided to wait on the bank until conditions improved.

If the guidance were handed over entire — every twist and difficulty charted in advance — it would not be *trust.* It would be tourism.

**Faith is not knowing the road. Faith is knowing the Guide.**

And Ibrahim modelled something the Quran returns to again and again: the willingness to be *in motion toward the Lord* before the next step is lit. That is precisely what the Quran praises in Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt: *"Indeed, I am a migrant to my Lord."* Not "I am a man who has planned a migration." A migrant. Already mid-crossing. The action soaked into his identity before the destination had been reached.

So the question this ayah asks you is not *"where are you going?"* It is *"are you already moving?"*

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: The Prayer of the Migrant — *Rabb, Gift Me*

He arrives. No detail of the road is given. No mention of how long the journey took, who he met, how he survived. Just: he left, and then — upon reaching the Holy Land — he prayed.

> **رَبِّ هَبْ لِى مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ**
>
> *Rabb, hab lī min al-ṣāliḥīn.*
>
> "My Lord, grant me from among the righteous."

I want you to hear the grammar of *hab* before you reach for its meaning, because the root says something about the posture of this prayer that a translation flattens.

The verb comes from **وَهَبَ** — *wahaba* — to give freely, to bestow as a pure gift. Not *aʿṭinī,* "give me as my due." Not *irziʿnī,* "provide me from what is owed." *Hab* — *gift me.* The root carries the sense of liberality itself: what you give not because anyone earned it but because you choose to give. When Ibrahim says *hab,* he is standing before his Lord with empty hands and naming the posture explicitly: *I am asking for a gift. I am making no claim. Whatever You send is what You give freely, and I receive it as such.*

Think about where this man has just come from. He survived a fire. He walked out of his homeland. He arrived — alone, it seems, no family mentioned, no companions yet named — at a land he had never seen. If anyone had grounds to *demand* a companion, an heir, a child to make the sacrifice of the migration worthwhile, it was this man. But he does not speak the language of debt. He doesn't say *"I left everything for You; You owe me this."* He says: *gift me.*

And then notice what he does not ask for.

He does not ask for **a son.** He does not say *"give me a walad"* (child) or *"give me a dhakar"* (male heir). He says: **give me from among the righteous** — *min al-ṣāliḥīn.* The *min* — "from among" — slots him into a category of people, not a demographic. Ibrahim is not requesting biological completion. He is asking for a companion on the path. He is asking for someone who belongs to the world of *ṣulḥ* — of righteousness, of wholeness, of alignment with what God loves. A partner in the walk, not merely a body to inherit his house.

[PAUSE]

This is the prayer of a man whose whole life has reorganized around a relationship with his Lord, and who now asks for one thing from that Lord: someone to walk this road with who also knows the road. He gave up the companions he had — the people who threw him in the fire. He is asking for the kind he never had.

And he asks for it empty-handed, on the far side of a migration he completed on trust alone, with the word that means *gift.*

That is the only way to ask for anything from the One who gives everything.

## Theme Three: The Answer That Outpaces the Question

Then God answers. And the answer is precise in a way that is easy to hurry past.

> **فَبَشَّرْنَاهُ بِغُلَامٍ حَلِيمٍ**
>
> *Fa-bashsharnāhu bi-ghulāmin ḥalīm.*
>
> "So We gave him good tidings of a forbearing boy."

Two things are happening here, layered on top of each other.

First: **the tiding precedes the arrival.** *Bashsharnāhu* — We brought him glad tidings — is the announcement before the child. God gives the news before He gives the gift, so that Ibrahim lives in the joy of the coming thing before he holds it. This is itself a form of generosity: the pleasure of anticipation granted as part of the blessing.

Second, and this is what requires slowing down: **Ibrahim asked for *righteous.* God announced *forbearing.***

He said *min al-ṣāliḥīn* — from among the righteous. God replies with *ḥalīm* — the forbearing one.

These are not synonyms, and the difference is not incidental. *Ṣāliḥ* is broad: morally upright, aligned with what is good, a person of wholesome character. *Ḥalīm* is specific: it names the capacity to bear what is placed on you without breaking. To absorb pain, to hold grief, to face a trial of impossible weight — and remain composed. Grounded. Even willing. Classical commentators note that *ḥilm* is among the most prized of human qualities precisely because it does not merely describe who you are in times of ease; it describes who you remain under the heaviest pressure.

Why *ḥalīm* and not *ṣāliḥ?*

Because God already knows what He is about to ask.

Read the very next ayah — 37:102 — and hear what Ismail says when his elderly father tells him of the dream of slaughter: *"O my father, do what you are commanded. You shall find me, if Allah wills, among the patient."* That is *ḥilm* made visible. Not abstract righteousness, but the precise, targeted forbearance of a young man who can look at the blade and say: *if that is what my Lord wants, then I am ready.* God announced the quality the trial would require before either father or son knew the trial was coming.

[PAUSE]

What God gives is not merely a response to the prayer — it is a *prophecy embedded in the gift.* The glad tidings of *ḥilm* are, at the same time, the quiet reveal that this child's *ḥilm* would be needed. In answering Ibrahim's prayer, God had already seen the sacrifice on the mountain; in describing the gift, He was already describing what the test would demand. The word *ḥalīm* is the seed. Ayah 102 is the harvest.

Ibrahim asked for a companion in righteousness. God gave him a partner in the supreme test — and told him, years before the dream, exactly what quality that partner would carry into it.

This is the pattern of divine response to the *wahb*-prayer: God gives not what we name but what we need, in the direction we could not have anticipated. Ibrahim named *ṣāliḥ.* God sent *ḥalīm.* The answer exceeded the question in the most precise direction — not more of the same, but the specific quality that the unwritten chapter of Ibrahim's life was going to require.

And we do not know, when we pray with empty hands and open palms, what chapter is still unwritten for us.

# PART 3: CLOSING — The Three Postures of the Migrant

So we come back to where we started: a man standing with the fire behind him and his face toward a horizon with nothing on it but his trust.

And we have found three things in his six words.

**First: the posture of *dhāhib.*** Make the going your identity, not your plan. Stop living in the future verb — *I will leave, I will start, I will change.* Let it become who you are. The departure that is merely planned can be postponed indefinitely. The departure that has become your identity has already happened at the level of the heart, and the body will eventually follow.

**Second: the destination of *ilā rabbī.*** Stop naming places. Name a Person. The peace that depends on arriving somewhere — a salary, a marriage, a country, a status — is permanently held hostage to whether you get there and whether it holds. The peace of *ilā rabbī* cannot be bombed, blocked, or taken away. Wherever you land, you are already there, because the One you are travelling toward is already at every point on the road.

**Third: the trust of *sayahdīn.*** Move first. The guidance comes to the traveller, not the planner. This is not a call to abandon prudence; it is a call to stop using the demand for certainty as a permanent excuse for stillness. Take the step you know you need to take. The *sīn* of imminence is a promise: as you move, guidance will come — in your religion and in your worldly life, for the road and for what waits at the end of it.

[PAUSE]

And then, when you have walked — when you have arrived somewhere you did not know you were going, having been guided in ways you did not plan — open your hands. Say *hab.* Ask not for what you have earned or what would balance the ledger, but for what He freely gives. Ask not for a category of achievement but for a companion in the walk. Someone who knows the road because they also chose the Person, not the place.

And then trust that the answer, when it comes, will be more precise than your asking — carrying in it qualities for tests you have not yet been told about, gifts shaped for chapters not yet written.

Ibrahim left a fire behind him and found, eventually, a son.

The son he received was not described as wealthy or powerful or learned or influential. He was described as *ḥalīm.* Forbearing. The classical commentators hear in this a kind of divine foreknowledge woven into the announcement: God named the quality the moment He sent the gift, because He already knew which mountain was waiting.

**When you walk to your Lord, He does not just guide your feet. He shapes the gifts He sends you for the road ahead — even the road you cannot yet see.**

That is *tawakkul.* Not the absence of fear. Not the absence of uncertainty. But the settled conviction — the *dhāhib* conviction — that the Guide is already at work, that the guidance is on its way, and that every step of trust is met by a Lord who knew, before you took it, exactly where it would land.

[PAUSE]

Go, then. Become a *going-one.* The directions are on their way.
