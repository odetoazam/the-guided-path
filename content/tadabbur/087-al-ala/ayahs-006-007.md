# We Will Make You Recite: The Promise Hidden Inside a Fear of Forgetting

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

**سَنُقْرِئُكَ فَلَا تَنسَىٰٓ ۝ إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ يَعْلَمُ ٱلْجَهْرَ وَمَا يَخْفَىٰ**

In transliteration:

*Sanuqri'uka falā tansā. Illā mā shā'a-llāh. Innahu ya'lamu al-jahra wa mā yakhfā.*

And in plain English:

*"We will make you recite, so you will not forget — except what Allah wills. Indeed, He knows what is declared and what is hidden."*

Thirteen words. A promise, an exception, and a statement about what Allah sees. That's the whole of it. And by the time we finish, I want you to feel that these thirteen words were not written for a man who had everything under control. They were written for someone who was afraid.

### Let's rebuild the world this landed in

Go back. Early Mecca. The revelation is new, and it is terrifying in its weight. A man receives words — not words he is composing, but words arriving, from beyond himself, in bursts, sometimes heavy enough that they press the breath out of him.

And here is the thing we forget about the Prophet ﷺ in this period: **he was afraid of losing them.**

There's a parallel passage, in Surah Al-Qiyamah, where Allah tells him directly: *lā tuḥarrik bihi lisānaka li-ta'jala bih* — "Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it" (75:16). Picture that. The angel is reciting, and the Prophet's lips are already moving — chasing the words, trying to catch them before they're gone, trying to nail each one down out of a deep, human fear: *what if I lose this? What if it slips? This is the most precious thing that has ever been entrusted to anyone, and it is passing through me, and I cannot write, and I cannot guarantee I will hold it.*

Al-Qurtubi preserves exactly this in his commentary — that the Prophet ﷺ would repeat the revelation, anxious, *mukhāfata an yansā*, out of fear that he would forget. Mujahid reports the same picture: he would labor over it, afraid it would escape him, until he was told — in effect — *kufītahu*, "you have been spared that."

So sit inside that fear for a moment, because it is not exotic. It is the most human thing in the world. You have felt it. The fear of losing something precious that you cannot fully control. The grasping. The repeating. The check-and-re-check.

And into that fear, Allah does not say: *try harder. Concentrate. Develop a system.*

He says: **سَنُقْرِئُكَ** — *We* will make you recite.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then we'll spend the next hour earning it.

When the Prophet ﷺ was terrified of forgetting, the answer that came was not an instruction. It was a transfer. The verb is not *you will memorize*. The verb is **We will make you recite.** The action moves out of his hands and into the hands of his Lord.

And then — just when the promise reaches its most absolute, most comforting peak — *"so you will not forget"* — Allah does something strange. He adds a clause. **إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.** *Except what Allah wills.*

Why would you put an asterisk on a gift of security? Why introduce the smallest seed of "except" into a promise that was finally letting him breathe?

Stay with me. Because that asterisk is not a withdrawal of the gift. It might be the most important word in the verse.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We're going to open three things in these verses. The shape of the verb *We will make you recite*. The grammar of *so you will not forget* — and a distinction in that one short phrase that changes everything. And the strange exception, *except what Allah wills*, sealed by *He knows the declared and the hidden*.

### Element One: سَنُقْرِئُكَ — "We will make you recite"

#### First, a small grammar lesson — the kind you already know without knowing you know it

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns. Take a simple verb and you can reshape it to mean *cause* the action rather than *do* the action. English does this too, just clumsily. "He learned" versus "she taught him" — taught is just the causative of learned; *she made him learn*. "The water boiled" versus "I boiled the water" — *I caused it to boil*. Same root idea, but one form puts the action in your hands and the other puts it in someone else's.

Arabic does this with elegance. The base verb here is **قَرَأَ** (*qara'a*) — to read, to recite. Simple. *He recited.* Now Allah doesn't use that. He uses a reshaped form — what grammarians call Form IV — **أَقْرَأَ** (*aqra'a*). And this form, in this context, carries the causative weight: not *to recite* but **to make someone recite.**

This isn't my invention. Listen to how the classical commentators gloss the word. Al-Baghawi: *sanu'allimuka* — "We will teach you." Al-Jalalayn the same. The two great lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — anchor *aqra'a* in exactly this causative meaning: to bring recitation about *in* someone. So when we say "causative" here, we're not reaching; we're reading the form the way the tradition read it.

#### Now open the word

**سَنُقْرِئُكَ.**

Pull it apart and watch how much is packed into it.

There's a **سـ** at the front — *sa-* — the particle of the future. *We will.* A promise pointed forward in time. Not "We have made you," not "We are making you" — **We will.** This is a commitment Allah is signing.

Then **نُقْرِئُ** — *nuqri'u* — "We make to recite," in the first person plural. The "We" of divine majesty. And notice who is the *subject* of this verb. Not the Prophet. Allah. The Prophet is not the one doing the action here.

Then **ـكَ** — *-ka* — "you." The Prophet is the **object.** The one to whom the action is done.

Let's sit with that arrangement, because it is the entire consolation in miniature. The man was moving his tongue, *doing*, grasping, working to hold the words. And the grammar of the answer quietly takes him out of the driver's seat. **Allah is the subject. The Prophet is the object. The recitation is the thing done to him, in him, for him.**

#### Notice what this does to the fear

He was afraid because he thought the holding was *his* job. And any job that is yours can be failed. You can study and still blank. You can love something and still lose it. As long as the preservation depended on his grip, the fear was rational.

The verb dissolves the premise of the fear. It says: *this was never your task. The reciting itself — We will plant it in you. We will cause it to live on your tongue. You are not the keeper of this treasure. You are the one in whom We are keeping it.*

Here's what's remarkable. The cure for his anxiety was not to make him stronger. It was to tell him the truth about who was actually carrying the weight. The relief did not come from a better grip. **It came from being told he was held.**

[PAUSE]

And there's one more layer in this single word. Remember where we are in the surah. Just a few ayahs earlier: *alladhī khalaqa fa-sawwā, wa-lladhī qaddara fa-hadā* — the One who created and proportioned, who measured out and guided. The God of this surah is the God of **taqdīr** — of measuring, ordering, putting each thing in its precise place. The sun in its orbit, the pasture brought green and then made into dark debris — all of it *measured.*

And now that same measuring hand turns toward the inside of a human heart and orders *the revelation* there with the same precision. The God who proportioned the cosmos will proportion His words inside you. **سَنُقْرِئُكَ** is the law of the heavens applied to the chambers of one man's memory.

So what does this one word reveal? *That the preservation of the Quran was lifted, from the very beginning, out of human hands and placed in divine ones — and that the proof of God's care for His word is that He would not even trust it to the love of the one who loved it most.*

### Element Two: فَلَا تَنسَىٰ — "so you will not forget"

#### Again, a small distinction first — and this one is the hinge of the whole verse

In Arabic there is a tiny word, **لَا** (*lā*), "no / not." And it does two completely different jobs depending on the grammar around it.

Sometimes *lā* is a **command** — a prohibition. *Don't do this.* When it commands, it does something specific to the verb after it: it clips its ending. *Lā taf'al* — "do not do."

But sometimes *lā* is simply a **statement of fact about the future** — a negation, not an order. *This will not happen.* And when it merely negates, the verb stays in its full, flowing, indicative form.

Two different *lā*s. One says *don't.* The other says *won't.* And they look almost identical. The whole meaning of a sentence can turn on which one you're hearing.

#### Now look at the verse

**فَلَا تَنسَىٰ.**

The **فَـ** (*fa-*) at the front means "so" or "and as a result" — it ties this directly to what came before. Because We will make you recite, *therefore* this follows.

And then **لَا تَنسَىٰ.** Notice how the verb ends — **تَنسَىٰ**, *tansā*, ending in that long, open *ā*, the full indicative form, untouched, unclipped.

Stay with that ending, because it is telling you which *lā* this is. This is not the *lā* of command. This is the *lā* of statement. The verse is **not** saying: *"...so do not forget!"* — an order barked at an anxious man, one more burden, one more thing to fail at.

It is saying: **"...so you will not forget."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference? Sit in it.

"Do not forget" is a demand. It leaves the outcome on your shoulders. It is exactly the kind of pressure that *causes* the anxiety — *don't mess this up, don't drop it, the most important words in human history and it's on you not to lose them.*

"You **will** not forget" is a guarantee. It takes the outcome off your shoulders entirely. It is not asking anything of him. It is *promising* something to him.

This is the grammatical seal on the consolation we found in the first word. **سَنُقْرِئُكَ** said: *We are the ones doing the reciting in you.* And **فَلَا تَنسَىٰ** completes the logic: *and therefore — as the natural consequence of Our doing it — forgetting will not happen to you.* The not-forgetting is not a task he must achieve. It is a *result* of God's action that He is reporting in advance.

The man was straining his tongue to hold the words. And Allah essentially says: *put your tongue down. The holding is done. Not because you'll grip harder, but because We've already taken it from your grip.*

What does this phrase reveal? *That God's response to our fear of losing what is precious is not a sterner command to hold tighter — it is the announcement that He has already secured what we were afraid to lose.*

### Element Three: إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ — "except what Allah wills"

#### The device: the exception clause

There's a tool in Arabic called *istithnā'* — exception. The little word **إِلَّا** (*illā*), "except," carves something out of a general statement. *Everyone came except Zayd.* The "except" pulls one piece back out of the whole.

We use these all the time, and usually they feel like a *reduction.* "You can keep all of it — except this part." The exception takes something *away.* So when you hear it land right after a beautiful, absolute promise — *you will not forget* — your instinct is to brace. Here comes the catch. Here comes the fine print.

#### Open it

**إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.** *Except what Allah wills.*

So the promise was: *you will not forget.* And then, immediately: *except what Allah wills* you to forget.

Let's be honest about how this first strikes us. It seems to *undo* the gift. He was just told, beautifully, completely — *you will not forget.* He could finally exhale. And then a clause arrives that seems to crack the door of fear back open. *Except... what God wills.* So I might forget after all?

Why would Allah do this? Why put a seam in a seamless comfort?

Stay with me, because here is where the whole verse turns from consolation into something deeper.

#### The exception is not a withdrawal. It is a tether.

Think about what an *unconditioned* promise would have implied. If it simply read *you will not forget,* full stop, with no clause at all — then the not-forgetting would have become an independent fact, a property the Prophet now *owned,* a guarantee running on its own, no longer needing God moment to moment. The gift would have detached from the Giver. It would have become *his.*

The clause **إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ** prevents exactly that. It keeps the gift permanently plugged into the will of the One who gave it. It says: *you will not forget — and the reason you won't is not some mechanism you now possess, but My active, living will, every single moment, choosing to preserve it in you.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the reversal? The clause that looked like it was *introducing* insecurity is actually the source of the deepest security. Because a promise that runs on its own can run out. But a promise sustained breath-by-breath by an unlimited, loving will *cannot* run out — as long as the One willing it is alive, and He is *al-Ḥayy*, the Ever-Living.

The classical commentators, by the way, read the "except" with two complementary lenses, and both are true at once. Al-Muyassar notes it points to whatever Allah, in His wisdom, may choose to have abrogated or lifted — *li-maslaḥa ya'lamuhā,* for a benefit He knows. So even the rare instance of a verse being withdrawn happens *by His will,* not by the Prophet's failure. And on the other side, the clause safeguards the truth that the not-forgetting was never an automatic machine — it was, and remains, an ongoing act of divine choosing. Two camera angles. One reality: **the preservation belongs to God, start to finish, with no clause where it slips out of His hands and into mechanism or chance.**

So the asterisk is not the fine print that takes the gift back. **The asterisk is the cord that keeps the gift attached to the Giver forever.**

#### And then the seal: إِنَّهُۥ يَعْلَمُ ٱلْجَهْرَ وَمَا يَخْفَىٰ

Now watch how Allah closes. *Indeed, He knows the declared and what is hidden.* **إِنَّهُۥ يَعْلَمُ ٱلْجَهْرَ وَمَا يَخْفَىٰ.**

Why end here? Why, after talking about reciting and forgetting, suddenly mention what is *open* and what is *hidden*?

Read it as carefully for its absences as for its presences. Look at the two objects of "He knows." The first is **ٱلْجَهْرَ** — *al-jahr* — "the declared," a defined, fixed noun, sitting still. The second is not a parallel noun. It is **مَا يَخْفَىٰ** — *mā yakhfā* — "what is hidden," a verb, an ongoing action, *that-which-keeps-being-concealed.*

Stay with that asymmetry. The open is named flatly, because the open is easy — anyone can know what is declared out loud. But the hidden gets a *verb,* because hiddenness is active, restless, endlessly slipping into the dark. And He knows *that* too — not as a static list, but in its very motion.

Now connect it back. What is **forgetting**? Forgetting is exactly the moment when something that was *jahr* — present, spoken, declared on your tongue — slides into *khafā* — the hidden, the inaccessible, the place you cannot reach. Forgetting is the open becoming hidden inside you.

And the verse seals itself by saying: *the One who promised to preserve this in you is the One who sees both rooms.* He sees the words on your tongue, and He sees the dark place they could vanish into. **Nothing you could lose is hidden from the One who is keeping it.** The exact thing you fear — slippage into the unreachable dark — is fully lit before Him.

So the exception, *except what Allah wills,* is not arbitrary and it is not threatening. It is governed. It is in the hands of the One who sees the declared *and* the hidden — who knows precisely what is held and what could be lost, and wills accordingly, with perfect knowledge and perfect wisdom.

What do these last words reveal? *That the clause of divine will is not a blind spot in the promise — it is the promise placed in the hands of the only One who can see in the dark.*

### The bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. A verb that takes the Prophet out of the subject's seat — *We* will make *you* recite. A negation that is a guarantee, not a command — you *will* not forget. An exception that does not weaken the gift but tethers it forever to the Giver. And a seal that places the whole thing under the gaze of the One who sees the open and the hidden.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the fear you carry in your own chest.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The gift of not having to hold it all yourself

#### Start where you live

You know the feeling of grasping.

It's the parent lying awake, replaying everything they might forget to do for their child. It's the student the night before the exam, certain that the more they clutch the material the safer it is, and the clutching itself making them lose it. It's the person who loves someone and lies there afraid — *what if I lose them, what if this slips through my fingers, I have to hold on tighter.*

We are, all of us, terrified of losing what is precious. And our instinct is always the same: **grip harder.** Repeat it. Check it again. Stay vigilant. Carry it personally, because if I don't carry it, who will?

And there is a particular exhaustion to that posture. The exhaustion of believing that the survival of everything precious depends on the strength of *your* grip. You become the sole load-bearing wall. And load-bearing walls live in fear of cracking.

The Prophet ﷺ — the best of creation — stood exactly there. Moving his tongue. Repeating out of fear. Carrying the most precious thing ever entrusted to a human being, and carrying it *as if its survival depended on his grip.*

#### Now let the verse illuminate it

And Allah did not say: *grip better.*

He said: **سَنُقْرِئُكَ.** *We will make you recite.*

Feel what that does. It doesn't deny the preciousness. It doesn't minimize the stakes. It just relocates the responsibility. *You were never the load-bearing wall. I AM. You are not the keeper of this. You are the kept.*

This is the difference between two entire ways of living. One is the life of the grip — *everything depends on me, so I must never loosen.* The other is the life of trust — *I will do my part fully, with my whole tongue and heart, and the outcome rests with the One who actually holds it.*

And notice — this is not passivity. The Prophet ﷺ still recited. Still received, still conveyed, still gave everything. The trust did not replace the effort; it *removed the terror from* the effort. He could now recite *freely,* with an open hand, because the catching was no longer his to fear. Al-Qiyamah said it outright: *don't move your tongue to hasten it... then, when We have recited it, follow its recitation* (75:16-18). First the fear is named. Then the order is reversed: *you don't chase Us. We give, and you receive.*

[PAUSE]

#### Build it further

There's a movement here that goes deeper than reassurance. Think about *why* Allah would not even leave preservation to the love of the one who loved the Quran most.

Because love is not the same as power. The Prophet ﷺ loved the revelation more than anyone ever has. And love that intense will still *grasp* — will still lie awake, will still fear, will still move the tongue at night. Love alone could not guarantee the words. Only the One who is *al-Ḥafīẓ,* the Preserver, could.

And here is the tender thing: by taking the preservation into His own hands, Allah was not insulting the Prophet's love. He was *protecting him from the agony of a love that fears loss.* He was saying: *you may love it freely now, without the dread, because keeping it safe was never on your shoulders.*

There is a mercy in being relieved of a weight you were never meant to carry.

#### What does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practical turn. Tomorrow there will be something you are gripping — some precious thing you believe survives only by the force of your holding. A relationship. A reputation. Your children's future. Your own faith, even — that you must white-knuckle your way to keeping.

The invitation of **سَنُقْرِئُكَ** is not to stop caring or stop working. It is to do your part with an open hand and then to consciously, deliberately hand the *outcome* back to the One who actually holds it. To say, in the language of this verse: *I will recite. The keeping is Yours.*

Try it as an actual practice. When you catch yourself gripping — the lying-awake, the over-checking, the dread — name the thing, and then say: *Yā Ḥafīẓ, You are the One who keeps. I am the one who is kept. I do my part; the holding is Yours.* Watch what leaves your body when you mean it.

### Theme Two: The exception that protects you

#### Start where you live

We have a deep discomfort with exceptions.

When someone makes us a promise, we want it whole. We want no asterisk, no "unless," no "except." The clause feels like a trapdoor. *You said you'd always be there — except?* The exception reads, to us, as the place where the promise might betray us. We want guarantees with no fine print.

And this runs all the way down. We want a life with no exceptions. We want the plan to hold without contingency. We want to know, locked-in, that the precious thing is *ours,* permanently, no clauses, no surprises. The "except" is the thing we are most afraid of in every promise we are given.

#### Now let the verse illuminate it

And then comes a promise from God Himself — *you will not forget* — and even He, who could make it unconditional, *adds the clause.* **إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ.** *Except what Allah wills.*

Here is the reversal we found in the language, now as a way of living. We assumed the clause was the weak point of the promise. It is actually the strongest point.

Because think about what you are really asking for when you ask for a promise with *no* exception, no reference to God's will. You are asking for a guarantee that runs on its own, detached from God — a thing you possess, that no longer needs Him. And anything detached from God is, by definition, fragile, because everything except Him is contingent. **A promise with no "except what Allah wills" is a promise resting on something other than God. And that is the only kind of promise that can truly fail.**

The clause **إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ** is therefore not the trapdoor. It is the foundation. It says: *this gift is not floating free where it could be lost; it is anchored, every moment, in My living will.* The asterisk is the anchor chain.

[PAUSE]

#### Build it further

This is why the believer is taught to say *in shā'a-llāh* — "if Allah wills" — over everything. We sometimes say it as a verbal tic, a way of softening a plan. But look at what it really is. It is the deliberate attaching of every promise and intention to the will of God — *exactly* the move Allah Himself makes inside this verse.

The Quran even rebukes this directly elsewhere: *do not say of anything, "I will surely do that tomorrow," except [that you say] "if Allah wills"* (18:23-24). Why? Not to make us timid. To keep every one of our plans *tethered* — to stop us building a single promise on a foundation other than Him.

So the spiritual maturity the verse is teaching is this: **stop fearing the "except." Learn to love it.** The "except what Allah wills" in your own life — the contingency you cannot control, the outcome you had to release — that is not the leak in your security. It is the place where your security stops depending on your own grip and starts depending on His will. The asterisk is where the relationship with God actually lives.

And remember how the verse sealed it: *He knows the declared and the hidden.* The "except" is not blind. It is not random fate. It is governed by the One who sees both rooms — the open and the dark, what you can hold and what you cannot. So when something slips into the hidden — a memory, a plan, a person, a hope you couldn't keep — it did not slip past *Him.* It moved, with His knowledge, by His will, under His sight.

#### What does this change about tomorrow?

Tomorrow you will make plans, and somewhere in them is a thing you desperately want guaranteed with no clause. The invitation here is to consciously *write the clause yourself.* To say *in shā'a-llāh* and actually mean the theology of it: *I am attaching this to Your will, because Your will is the only thing strong enough to hold it.*

And when the exception falls — when something you wanted preserved is *not* preserved, when the thing slips into the hidden — the invitation is to meet it not as betrayal but as the clause you already accepted: *this was always "except what Allah wills." And the One who willed it sees the open and the hidden. It is not lost. It is with Him.*

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture

Now stand back and see the shape of these two ayahs, because they are built with a precision worthy of the surah of *taqdīr.*

Watch the movement:

1. **سَنُقْرِئُكَ** — God acts. (He is the subject; the Prophet is acted upon.)
2. **فَلَا تَنسَىٰ** — the result in the human. (You will not forget — a guarantee, not a command.)
3. **إِلَّا مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ** — the gift is tethered back to God's will.
4. **إِنَّهُۥ يَعْلَمُ ٱلْجَهْرَ وَمَا يَخْفَىٰ** — and that will is sealed under God's total sight.

It begins with God and ends with God. The human experience — *you will not forget* — sits in the middle, **cradled on both sides** by divine action and divine knowledge. The Prophet's security is not standing on its own legs at any point in this structure. It is held, before and after, by his Lord. The very architecture enacts the meaning: *you are surrounded; you are kept; the gift never once leaves the hands that gave it.*

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of our journey are the same insight from two directions.

The **language** showed us a man taken out of the subject's seat, a promise that is a guarantee not a burden, an exception that tethers rather than weakens, a sight that misses nothing.

The **themes** showed us a heart released from the exhaustion of the grip, and a heart taught to love the very "except" it used to fear.

They are one truth approached from two sides: **The things you are most afraid of losing were never yours to hold in the first place — and the moment you accept that, with all its clauses and contingencies, is the moment you are finally safe.** The Prophet feared losing the words. He was told: *We hold them. You won't lose them. And even the "unless" is in Our hands, under Our sight.* That is the most secure a human being can ever be — not because there are no exceptions, but because the exceptions belong to God.

### Questions to carry

- What is the precious thing you are gripping right now as if its survival depends on your hold? What would it feel like to do your part with an open hand and place the keeping where it actually rests?
- Where have you been carrying, as a personal duty, a weight that was never yours to bear alone?
- When you say *in shā'a-llāh,* are you tethering your plans to God's will — or just softening your sentences?
- Think of something you wanted preserved that slipped into the hidden — a memory, a plan, a person. Can you meet it as "except what Allah wills," seen and held by the One who knows the open and the hidden, rather than as something simply lost?
- The Prophet ﷺ, the best of creation, was reassured rather than rebuked for his fear. What does that tell you about how God meets *your* fear?

### One-sentence distillation

*The same Lord who measured out the cosmos will Himself carry His word in your heart — so that what felt like your fragile grip was always His unbreakable promise, held by one clause in the hands of the One who sees everything you cannot.*

### The closing invitation

Something can shift after sitting with these thirteen words. Not a new task — the opposite of a task. A loosening. The quiet recognition that the most precious things in your life are not surviving on the strength of your hold, and were never meant to. That you are invited to recite, to love, to work, to give — fully, freely — with the keeping handed back to the One who keeps. And that even the exceptions, the unless-es, the things that slip away, are not leaks in your security but the very places where your security stops being about you and starts being about Him.

You are not the load-bearing wall. You never were. *We will make you recite. So you will not forget.*

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You who recite Your words into the hearts of those who could never hold them alone — make us reciters with open hands and quiet hearts. Take from us the terror of the grip, and teach us to love the clause that tethers every gift to You. You who see the declared and the hidden — keep what we are most afraid to lose, in the place where nothing is ever truly lost: with You.

Āmīn.
