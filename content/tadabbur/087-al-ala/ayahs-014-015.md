# The Farmer's Word for Success

### Surah Al-A'la 87:14–15

> **قَدْ أَفْلَحَ مَن تَزَكَّىٰ ۝ وَذَكَرَ ٱسْمَ رَبِّهِۦ فَصَلَّىٰ**
>
> *Qad aflaḥa man tazakkā, wa dhakara isma rabbihi fa-ṣallā.*
>
> "He has certainly succeeded who purifies himself, and remembers the name of his Lord and prays."

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me take you somewhere first. Before we touch a single word.

I want you to imagine the world this surah was first spoken into. Makkah. Early revelation. A society whose entire scoreboard of human worth was visible, countable, and held in the hand. Camels. Caravans. The clink of trade. Sons — sons especially, because sons were the currency of survival and standing. A man's success was something you could *see across the marketplace*: the size of his herd, the length of his lineage, the gold that moved through his fingers. Worth was weighed. Worth was hoarded. Worth was loud.

And into this world — this world of accumulation and noise — a surah descends that opens with a strange command. Not "gather." Not "build." But *Sabbiḥi sma rabbika l-aʿlā* — "Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High." Lift your gaze off the scoreboard. There is a higher Name.

The surah moves. It speaks of creation, of the One who measured and guided, who brings forth the green pasture and then turns it to dark debris. It speaks of a reminder that will benefit the one who has awe in his heart, and that the wretched one will dodge. And then — just before it delivers its great diagnosis of what is wrong with the human being — it stops. It gives us a portrait. A photograph of the one who *made it*. The one who, when the final accounting is done, will be standing in the light.

And here is the strange thing. [PAUSE]

When Allah finally tells us who succeeded — who *truly* won, in the only game that was ever real — He does not reach for the language of the marketplace. He does not say "rich." He does not say "powerful." He reaches for a word that belonged to the poorest, dirtiest, most overlooked man in that entire society.

He reaches for **the farmer's word.**

### The Hook

There is a word in these verses for *success* — the word **أَفْلَحَ**, *aflaḥa* — and it does not mean what you think it means. It is not the word for the one who gathered the most. It is the word for the one who *broke open the hardest ground.*

And once you see where this word comes from — once you see the calloused hands it belongs to — you will never again be able to measure your own life the way the world taught you to.

Stay with me. Let's go to the soil.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: قَدْ أَفْلَحَ — The Certainty Before the Verb

Before we get to the farmer, look at the very first word. **قَدْ** — *qad*.

Let me teach you what this little word does, because in English we have nothing quite like it.

In Arabic, when you place the particle *qad* directly in front of a verb in the perfect (past) tense, it does something to that verb. It seizes it. It clamps down on it and says: *this is settled. This is confirmed. This is not a maybe.* If I tell you in Arabic *jāʾa* — "he came" — that is a report. But if I say *qad jāʾa* — "he has indeed come" — I have shut the door on every doubt. It is the difference between "the train arrived" and "the train has most certainly arrived."

Now notice the grammar here, because it is doing something quietly astonishing.

**قَدْ أَفْلَحَ** — "*qad aflaḥa*" — "he has *certainly* succeeded."

Allah is describing a kind of person who, in the timeline of your life, has not finished the race yet. The one who purifies himself, remembers, and prays — that is an ongoing, unfinished human being. He is *still working.* And yet Allah does not describe his success in the future tense. He does not say "*sa-yufliḥ*" — "he *will* succeed." [PAUSE]

He says it in the **perfect tense, sealed with *qad*.** *He has already, certainly, succeeded.*

Sit with this. The work is not done — but the verdict is already in. The moment a soul turns toward this path, Heaven writes the outcome in the past tense. As if to say: *the matter is so certain that We will speak of your victory as something already accomplished, before your hands have even finished the work.*

This is what it feels like to be loved by the One who controls the ending. Your success is not a hope you are reaching toward. It is a fact already filed in the records of the Most High, waiting for time to catch up to it.

That is the *qad*. Now — the word it is guaranteeing.

### Element Two: أَفْلَحَ — The Splitting of the Hard Ground

Here is the word. **أَفْلَحَ.** *Aflaḥa.* "He has succeeded."

Let me give you its grammatical shape first, and then let me take you to its root, because the root is where the whole sky opens.

Grammatically, *aflaḥa* is a verb in **Form IV** — the form built by adding a prefixed *alif* to the base, the form that, in this case, makes the meaning **transitive and brought-into-effect**: to attain, to bring about success, to come into prosperity. The certified morphology confirms it: root **f-l-ḥ**, Form IV, perfect tense.

But *where does this root live?* What field did Allah pull this word from?

The root **ف-ل-ح**, *f-l-ḥ*, in the deepest soil of the Arabic language, means **to split, to cleave, to break open.** And the person who does this for a living — the one whose entire trade is built on this root — is the **فَلَّاح**, the *fallāḥ*: **the farmer. The tiller. The one who splits the earth.**

[PAUSE]

Let this land.

The Arabic word for *success* — the word Allah chose to describe the saved soul, the winner of the only game that matters — is built from the **same root as the word for the farmer.** Success, in the language of the Quran, is not the merchant's word. It is not the conqueror's word. It is **the farmer's word.**

Why does this matter? Why would the All-Wise, choosing one word to crown the victorious of all humanity, choose the vocabulary of the man with dirt under his fingernails?

Because look at what the farmer actually *does.*

The farmer comes to a piece of ground that is hard. Crusted. Closed. Unyielding. And his job — the very meaning of his name — is to take a blade and **break it open.** To cleave the resistant earth so that something can be buried inside it, so that life can take root in the dark, so that eventually, after patience he cannot rush, something green breaks through toward the light.

The farmer does not *gather* success. He does not find it lying on the surface, ready to be picked up and counted. He **splits the hard ground and waits for what grows.**

And *this* — Allah is telling you — *this* is what it is to succeed as a human being. Your *falāḥ*, your success, is not what you accumulate on the surface of your life. It is what you are willing to *break open* inside yourself. The hard crust over the heart. The compacted, resistant ground of the ego. *Aflaḥa* — the one who succeeded — is the one who took the blade to his own hardened soil and split it so that something could grow.

Notice what this quietly destroys. Every definition of success the marketplace ever sold you — *more, bigger, louder, mine* — is **acquisitive.** It is about pulling things toward you. But the Quran's word for success is **agricultural.** It is about breaking yourself open so that life can enter. One is hoarding. The other is cultivation. [PAUSE]

The world tells you the successful man is the one with the fullest hands.

Allah tells you the successful man is the one with the **broken-open ground.**

### Element Three: تَزَكَّىٰ — The Verb You Cannot Do to Yourself by Halves

And now watch how perfectly the next word continues the farming.

**مَن تَزَكَّىٰ** — "the one who *tazakkā*" — "who purifies himself."

First, that little word *man* — **مَن** — "whoever." Notice the absence here, because absence in the Quran always speaks. Allah does not say "the rich man who purified himself," or "the Arab," or "the man of such-and-such tribe." He says **مَن** — *whoever.* It is left wide open. No name, no lineage, no precondition except the action itself. In a society obsessed with *who your father was*, Allah throws the gate open to anyone willing to do the work. The scoreboard is erased. Only the verb remains.

Now the verb itself. **تَزَكَّىٰ.**

Its root is **z-k-w** — and here is the first wonder. This is the root of **zakāh**, and the root carries *two* meanings braided into one: **purification** *and* **growth.** To *zakkā* something is both to cleanse it and to make it *increase.* Think about that — in the Arabic imagination, becoming pure and becoming *more* are the same motion. The same word. You do not shrink when you purify; you *grow.* The pruned vine bears more fruit. The cleansed soil yields more life. **Purity is not subtraction. Purity is fertility.** [PAUSE]

And do you see how it keeps the farming alive? *Falāḥ* broke open the ground. *Zakāh* is the growth that comes up out of it. The same field. The same image, sustained across two words: split the soil, and watch it *grow green and pure.*

But now the grammar — and this is the part I need you to hold.

The certified morphology marks *tazakkā* as **Form V.** Let me teach you what Form V does.

Form V in Arabic — the *tafaʿʿala* pattern — is the **reflexive** form. It takes an action and turns it back **onto the doer himself.** It carries the sense of *taking something on yourself*, of a gradual, effortful, self-involved becoming. When you put a verb into Form V, you are saying: *the one who does this, does it to himself, with effort, over time.*

So Allah does not say *zukkiya* — the passive — "he *was* purified," as though purity were something done *to* you, poured over you while you stood still. [PAUSE] He says **تَزَكَّىٰ** — Form V — **"he purified *himself.*"**

Feel the weight of that grammatical choice. No one can do this verb for you. Your teacher cannot purify you. Your parents cannot purify you. Your scholar, your shaykh, your community — none of them can press the blade into *your* hardened ground. The reflexive form locks the door from the inside. *Tazakkā* — he turned the work upon himself, by himself, through patient effort, day after day after day.

This is the farmer again, isn't it? No one can till your field while you sleep. The crust over the heart does not break because someone *told* you about breaking it. You take the blade. You do it to your own ground. And you do it the way Form V insists — not in one heroic blow, but **gradually, reflexively, as a thing you slowly become.**

Here is what these three words have built. *Qad aflaḥa* — the success is already certain, sealed in the past tense by Heaven. *Falāḥ* — and that success is the farmer's success, the splitting of hard ground. *Tazakkā* — and the splitting is something you do to *yourself*, the purification that is also a growth, the cleansing that is also an increase.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Definitions of Success — and Which One Is Lying to You

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, running quietly in the back of your mind right now, a scoreboard. Everyone does. It tracks things. Maybe it tracks income. Maybe it tracks the apartment, the title at work, the number on the scale, the followers, the comparison between where you are and where your old classmate is. You did not choose to install this scoreboard. The world installed it in you before you could object. And it runs *constantly*, whispering a single question under everything you do: *Am I winning?*

Here is what is so quietly violent about that scoreboard: it is **acquisitive.** Every metric on it measures what you have **gathered.** And so it can never, ever let you rest — because there is always more to gather, and someone has gathered more than you. The acquisitive scoreboard is a treadmill with no off switch. This is *exactly* the disease the very next verses of this surah name: *bal tu'thirūna l-ḥayāta d-dunyā* — "**but you prefer the worldly life**" — *wa l-ākhiratu khayrun wa abqā* — "while the Hereafter is better and more lasting" (87:16–17).

Do you see why verse 14 had to come *first*? Allah shows you the true scoreboard — *qad aflaḥa man tazakkā* — and *then* He names the false one you've been preferring. He shows you the farmer's success, and then He points at your hands full of dunya and says: *but this is the one you chose.*

Now bring back the word. *Falāḥ.* The farmer's success.

The farmer's success is not acquisitive. It is **generative.** The farmer is not winning a race against the field next door. He is breaking his own ground, planting his own seed, waiting on his own harvest. His success is measured by **what grows out of him**, not by what he drags toward him. And — here is the mercy of it — *that* scoreboard has no treadmill. The farmer is not impoverished because someone else's field is greener. His harvest is *his.* His relationship with the soil is *his.*

So here is the question these verses press into your chest: **What if you have been measuring your entire life on the wrong scoreboard?** What if success was never the size of the pile, but the depth of the breaking-open? What if the people you quietly envy are winning a game that, on the Day the truth is shown, will not even be played — and the quiet, unglamorous work of splitting your own hard ground was the only race that was ever running? [PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tomorrow, when the scoreboard whispers its question — *am I winning?* — answer it with the farmer's question instead. Not "*what did I gather today?*" but "**what did I break open in myself today? What grew?**" One honest act of cleaving your own hard ground — one prayer you didn't want to pray and prayed anyway, one resentment you tilled under, one ego-crust you split — *that* is *falāḥ.* Heaven has already written it in the past tense.

### Theme Two: The Three Verbs, in Order — Why Purification Comes Before Prayer

Now let me show you something about the *sequence*, because the order of these verbs is itself a teaching.

Look at the whole portrait across both verses. Three verbs. In a deliberate order:

1. **تَزَكَّىٰ** — he purified himself.
2. **وَذَكَرَ ٱسْمَ رَبِّهِ** — and remembered the name of his Lord.
3. **فَصَلَّىٰ** — *and so* he prayed.

We tend to think the spiritual life runs the other way. We think: *first I'll perform the rituals, and the rituals will purify me.* And there is truth in that. But notice what Allah places first. **Purification.** The breaking of the inner ground comes *before* the remembrance, and the remembrance comes before the formal prayer.

Let me bring this where you live. You know the experience of standing in prayer with a heart that is *crusted over.* The body bows. The tongue moves. But the ground inside is hard and closed, and nothing reaches down into it. You finish, and you feel you were never really *there.* Why? Because you brought an untilled field to the planting. You tried to grow something in ground you had not first broken open.

The order in these verses is the farmer's order. **First you split the soil** — *tazakkā*, the inner clearing, the cleansing of the heart from what hardens it: the shirk, the grudges, the appetites the early commentators all name. **Then the seed goes in** — *dhakara sma rabbihi*, the remembrance of the *Name*. And notice: it is *the Name.* This verse loops all the way back to verse 1 — *sabbiḥi sma rabbika l-aʿlā* — "glorify the **name** of your Lord, the Most High." The surah opened by commanding remembrance of the Name; here it shows you the man who actually *did* it. The seed planted in the broken ground is the Name of your Lord. **And then — *fa-ṣallā* — it grows into prayer.**

Look at that little **فَ** — *fa* — "and *so*." It is the *fa* of consequence, of natural result. He remembered the Name, *and so* — as the inevitable green shoot of a planted seed — **he prayed.** The prayer is not bolted on from the outside. In a soul that has been broken open and seeded with the Name of God, prayer **grows.** It comes up out of the ground on its own, the way wheat comes up out of a tended field.

The classical readings only deepen this. Some of the early scholars, as al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī report, read this verse through the lens of *ʿĪd*: *tazakkā* — he gave the *zakāh al-fiṭr*, the purifying charity — *fa-ṣallā* — *and so* he went out and prayed the festival prayer. Hold that image as one more camera angle on the same truth: you cleanse and give *first*, and the prayer flows *out* of the cleansing. The purified ground produces the standing before God.

[PAUSE — major pause]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Before your next prayer, give it sixty seconds of *tilling.* Don't walk to the prayer mat with a crusted heart and expect the seed to grow. Take one minute first to break the ground — to forgive the one thing you're holding, to name and release the grudge or the greed sitting hard in your chest, to remember *whose Name* you are about to stand before. Plant the Name in cleared soil. Then watch how differently the prayer *grows.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back and see the whole field at once.

The **linguistic journey** showed us three things hidden in the words: that *qad* seals our success in the certain past tense before the work is even finished; that *aflaḥa* is the **farmer's word** — success as the splitting of hard ground, not the gathering of a pile; and that *tazakkā* is **Form V**, the reflexive verb, the purification-that-is-also-growth which **no one can do for you.**

The **thematic journey** showed us the same truths from the other side: that the world's acquisitive scoreboard is the very *dunya* the next verse warns us we "prefer," while the farmer's generative success is the only race actually being run; and that the spiritual life has an *order* — you break the ground, you plant the Name, and the prayer grows up out of the cleared and seeded soil.

They are the same insight, approached from two directions. **The grammar is the theology.** *Falāḥ* is agricultural; therefore success is cultivation, not accumulation. *Tazakkā* is reflexive; therefore the harvest is yours and the labor is yours and no one can stand in your field for you. The whole surah is one sustained metaphor of a soul as a piece of ground — and these two verses are the moment Allah shows you the farmer who tended it well, standing in his harvest, while the One who owns the ending says of him, in the sealed and certain past tense: *he has already won.*

### Questions to Carry

1. Which scoreboard did I wake up on this morning — the one that counts what I *gathered*, or the one that asks what I *broke open*?
2. There is a piece of hard, crusted ground inside me right now that I have been avoiding tilling. I know what it is. What blade have I been refusing to pick up?
3. *Tazakkā* is Form V — no one can do it for me. Whom have I been quietly waiting to purify me, when the verb was always reflexive?
4. When I last stood in prayer and felt nothing reached the ground, had I tilled the soil first — or did I bring an untouched field to the planting?
5. If Heaven has already written my *falāḥ* in the certain past tense the moment I turn toward this work — what, exactly, am I waiting for?

### One-Sentence Distillation

True success is not the pile you gather on the surface of your life but the hard ground you break open within it — a splitting and cleansing you must do to yourself, into which the Name of your Lord is planted, and out of which prayer grows.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to overhaul your life tonight. The farmer does not plant the whole field in a day. But you can pick up the blade once. You can find the one crusted patch of ground you have been walking past — the grudge, the appetite, the pride, the prayer you keep postponing — and you can *split it open.* Just that one. And know, on the authority of the One who sealed it in the perfect tense, that the moment you do, your *falāḥ* is no longer a hope. It is already written. The harvest is only waiting for time to catch up.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who taught us that success is the breaking-open of hard ground — give us the courage to take the blade to our own crusted hearts. Purify us in the way we cannot purify ourselves, and where we have labored, let the cleansing be a growth. Plant Your Most High Name in the soil we clear, and let our prayer grow up out of it, green and reaching toward You. Write our falāḥ, and let us live long enough to stand in its harvest. Āmīn.*
