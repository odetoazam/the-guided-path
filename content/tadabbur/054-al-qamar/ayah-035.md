# Rescue Reclassified: The Past-Tense Gratitude That Earns a Present-Tense Reward

## Surah Al-Qamar, Ayah 35

**نِّعْمَةً مِّنْ عِندِنَا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي مَن شَكَرَ**

*Niʿmatan min ʿindinā. Kadhālika najzī man shakar.*

**"As a favor from Us. Thus do We reward the one who is grateful."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: One Verse of Light in a Corridor of Warnings

Before this verse can land, you have to hear the surah it lives in.

Surah Al-Qamar is one of the most relentless surahs in the Quran. It opens with the moon split in two — a sign so overwhelming that the Meccans who saw it could only call it sorcery and turn away. And then the surah does something almost unbearable. It walks the listener down a corridor of ruined civilizations, one door after another.

The people of Nuh — drowned. ʿAad — torn from the earth by a screaming wind, "as if they were trunks of uprooted palm trees." Thamud — destroyed by a single blast, left "like the dry, trampled twigs of a pen-builder." And after each ruin, the same haunting refrain tolls like a bell: *fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa-nudhur* — **"so how were My punishment and My warnings?"**

Imagine hearing this in Mecca, as a small, persecuted community of believers. Story after story, the same rhythm: a people denied, a people destroyed, a bell tolled. The surah is a drumbeat, and the drum is judgment.

Then we reach the fourth story. The people of Lut. The storm of stones descends on the city. And in the middle of the destruction, the previous verse carves out an exception: *illā āla Lūṭ* — "except the family of Lut. We saved them in the last hours of the night."

And then — for one verse — the drumbeat stops.

**نِّعْمَةً مِّنْ عِندِنَا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي مَن شَكَرَ**

*As a favor from Us. Thus do We reward the one who is grateful.*

Seven words. In a surah of tolling bells, this is the only verse in the entire sequence of destroyed nations that states a principle of *reward*. One verse of light in a corridor of warnings.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to carry into this reflection. Inside these seven words, Allah places two verbs side by side — and He puts them in different tenses. One of them is finished. One of them never finishes. And the question of *which one is which* may quietly rearrange how you understand everything Allah has ever done for you.

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Ended and the Verb That Didn't — *najzī man shakar*

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs carry time.

Arabic has essentially two verb forms for time. The **perfect** (*al-māḍī*) describes an action that is complete — done, sealed, behind you. "I called you yesterday." The **imperfect** (*al-muḍāriʿ*) describes an action that is ongoing or recurring — unfolding now, continuing into the future, happening again and again. "I call you every morning."

In English we have to pile up helper words to capture that second meaning — "I keep calling," "I am continually calling," "I will go on calling." Arabic does it with a single form. When the Quran chooses the imperfect, it is often painting something that does not stop.

Now look at the second half of our ayah:

**كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِي مَن شَكَرَ** — *kadhālika najzī man shakar.*

Two verbs. *Najzī* — "We reward." *Shakara* — "was grateful."

Now notice something.

*Shakara* — the human act, the gratitude — is in the **perfect tense**. Completed. A finished act, sealed in the past. The one who *was* grateful. The gratitude happened; it has an end-point.

*Najzī* — the divine act, the rewarding — is in the **imperfect tense**. Ongoing. Continuous. Recurring. "We reward" — present tense, and still rewarding, and rewarding again.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. The grammar itself is drawing a picture of a transaction that is wildly, beautifully unequal.

The human side of the exchange is finite. You were grateful — an act with edges, a beginning and an end. A moment of *alhamdulillah* whispered in a parked car. A prostration of thanks. A season of your life where your heart genuinely knew where its blessings came from. Completed acts. Past tense.

And the divine side? It never closes. *Najzī.* We reward — and keep rewarding. The verb has no finish line built into it.

**A completed, finite act of gratitude is answered by a reward that grammatically never ends.**

Stay with me, because there's a second layer. The verse says *man shakar* — "whoever was grateful" — and then stops. In Arabic, the verb *shakara* normally wants an object: you thank someone *for* something. Here, Allah leaves both unspecified. Not "whoever thanked Us for the rescue." Not "whoever thanked Us for surviving." Just — *whoever was grateful.* Full stop.

When Arabic withholds an expected object, the meaning widens to fill the space. The promise is not tied to one blessing, one rescue, one moment in one city. Any gratitude. Anywhere. From anyone. The door is left open exactly as wide as the grammar leaves it.

And notice what *najzī* withholds too — the reward itself is never named. "Thus do We reward" — with what? The verse doesn't say. The rescue of Lut's family is the example in front of us, but the reward itself is left as open as the gratitude. Two unspecified objects, facing each other across the verse, each one an open door.

**One word of this ayah tells you that your gratitude can be finished; His response to it is not.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: Not Just "From Us" — From Our *Presence*: *min ʿindinā*

Let me teach you another distinction, and again, let's start in your world.

There is a difference between receiving a letter *from a company* and receiving a letter *from the CEO's own desk*. Both technically come "from" the same source. But one passed through systems, departments, automated processes. The other came from the presence of the one who signed it. The "from" is the same word; the *provenance* is a different universe.

Arabic has a way of marking this difference. The word **ʿinda** is a locative noun — it means "at," "with," "in the presence of." *ʿIndī kitāb* — "I have a book," literally "at my presence is a book." When *ʿinda* combines with *min* ("from"), you get *min ʿindi* — not just "from," but **"from the presence of."**

Now hear the verse again:

**نِّعْمَةً مِّنْ عِندِنَا** — *niʿmatan min ʿindinā.*

Allah could have said *niʿmatan minnā* — "a favor from Us." Shorter. Simpler. Grammatically complete. Instead He says *min ʿindinā* — "a favor **from Our presence**."

Here's what this invites us to consider — and I'll mark this carefully as a reflection the wording opens up, beyond the bare grammar. The destruction in the previous verse is described as *sent*: "We sent upon them a storm of stones." Sent — dispatched, like a projectile released toward a target. But the rescue is not described as sent. The rescue is *min ʿindinā* — it carries the language of nearness, of provenance from the presence itself.

The punishment travels *away* from Him toward the guilty. The favor comes *from where He is* to the grateful.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. Lut's family was a handful of people fleeing a doomed city on foot, in the dark, in the last hours of the night. By every visible measure, they were the most abandoned people on earth — exiled from their home, their city burning behind them. And the Quran's label for that exact moment of their lives is: *a favor from Our presence.*

The night they lost everything was the night the language of divine nearness was wrapped around them.

**The people who looked most abandoned were described with the Quran's vocabulary of closeness.**

Hold onto this word — *ʿinda*. It is going to return at the end of this reflection in a way I am not going to spoil yet.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Hanging Word — Why *niʿmatan* Has No Sentence of Its Own

One more concept, and this one is subtle and beautiful.

In Arabic, a noun's ending tells you its job in the sentence. The ending *-an* (the accusative case) signals that the word is acted upon, or explains *why* or *how* an action happened. But here's the thing about our verse: it opens with **نِّعْمَةً** — *niʿmatan*, "a favor," in the accusative — **and there is no verb in this verse for it to attach to.**

The word hangs. It reaches backward, across the verse boundary, and grabs hold of the action in the *previous* ayah: "We saved them in the last hours of the night… **as a favor from Us.**"

The classical scholars noticed this hanging word and explained its grammar from slightly different angles — and rather than treating their views as competing answers, watch how they sketch one reality from multiple camera angles.

Al-Jalalayn reads *niʿmatan* as a verbal noun — *inʿāman*, "an act of favoring": the rescue happened *as an act of Our favoring*. Al-Qurtubi reads it as a direct object — the favor bestowed *upon Lut and his daughters* themselves. And al-Baghawi paraphrases the meaning with a striking verb: *jaʿalnāhu niʿmatan minnā ʿalayhim* — "**We made it** a favor from Us upon them."

Camera angle one: the rescue was an act of divine favoring. Camera angle two: the favor landed on specific, named human beings. Camera angle three: Allah *designated* the event as favor — He classified it.

Put the angles together and you see the composite picture: **the verse is not adding a favor to the rescue. The verse is re-naming the rescue itself.** The escape through the dark, the exile, the loss of the city — the entire event is being held up and given its true label: *this was niʿmah.*

And one more whisper from the grammar — I'll flag this as a rhetorical observation the indefinite form invites. *Niʿmatan* is indefinite: not "the favor," but "a favor." In Quranic rhetoric, indefiniteness can magnify — *a favor* of such a kind, beyond easy definition. As if even the rescue of an entire family from annihilation is filed under "one instance" of what His favor looks like.

**This single hanging word performs a re-classification: what looked like a desperate escape was, in Allah's own ledger, a gift.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So look at what the language has built. A finite past-tense gratitude answered by an unfinished present-tense reward. A rescue stamped not just "from Us" but "from Our presence." And a hanging accusative that reaches back and re-labels a night of loss as a night of favor.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Specifically: about the nights *you* have escaped from, and what you decided they meant.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Survivor's Question

There is a question that visits almost everyone who lives long enough, and it usually arrives quietly, at night, after the danger has passed.

*Why was I spared?*

The car accident that took the other lane. The diagnosis that came back benign when someone else's didn't. The layoff list your name wasn't on. The flight you missed. The version of your life that almost happened — addiction, ruin, a marriage that would have destroyed you — that swerved away at the last moment for reasons you cannot fully reconstruct.

Modern psychology has a name for the shadow side of this question: survivor's guilt. But there's another side, less discussed and more dangerous — call it **survivor's pride**. It's the quiet story we tell ourselves afterward: *I was spared because I was smart. Because I worked harder. Because I'm the kind of person these things don't happen to.* The escape gets absorbed into the ego's résumé.

And between guilt and pride there's a third option our culture offers: *luck*. Nothing meant anything. You rolled well. Move on.

Guilt, pride, or dice. Those are the modern menu options for the survivor's question.

[PAUSE]

Now place Lut's family inside that question. They walked out of a city that was destroyed behind them. Everyone they knew — neighbors, the society they'd lived among — gone by morning. If anyone ever had standing to ask *why were we spared?*, it was them.

And the Quran answers the question before they can even ask it. Not "you were spared because you were clever enough to leave." Not "you were spared by chance." One word, hanging at the front of the verse, reaching back to grab the whole event:

***Niʿmatan.*** A favor.

Your survival was not your achievement. It was not an accident. It was a gift, handed to you *min ʿindinā* — from His presence.

Feel what this does to the survivor's question. It dissolves the pride, because the rescue wasn't your résumé — it was His gift. And it transforms the guilt, because a gift is not an injustice to those who didn't receive it; the verse stands inside a surah that has spent thirty-four verses establishing that those who were destroyed were *warned*, repeatedly, and chose denial. And it annihilates the dice, because *niʿmah* is the opposite of randomness — it is intention, aimed at you, from the presence of Someone.

But here's the movement that matters most. If survival is a gift, then the gift implies a Giver, and a Giver implies a response. The verse tells you exactly which response keeps the channel open: *kadhālika najzī man shakar.* Thus do We reward the grateful.

Not "thus do We reward the deserving." Not "the strong," "the clever," "the careful." **The grateful.** The ones who looked at their own survival and read its true label.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this changes about tomorrow. You have already survived things. Some you know about; most you don't — the Quran's claim is that your existence is currently resting on a thick stack of rescues you never noticed. The practical discipline this verse teaches is small and concrete: **take one survival — one — and re-label it.** Tonight, name something you escaped, out loud, and file it where Allah files it: *that was niʿmah, min ʿindik* — that was a favor, from Your presence. Not luck. Not my competence. Yours.

That act of re-labeling has a name. It's called *shukr*. And the verse has just told you what *shukr* triggers.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Gratitude Is Not a Transaction — It's an Operating System

Our culture is, right now, in the middle of a gratitude renaissance. Gratitude journals. Gratitude apps. Three-things-I'm-thankful-for, every morning, backed by genuinely impressive research: gratitude practice measurably improves sleep, mood, resilience, even cardiac health.

And yet almost all of it has a strange hollow at the center. Grateful — *to whom?*

Modern gratitude is a feeling without an address. You're encouraged to be thankful *for* your health, your family, your morning coffee — but the "to" line is left blank, because filling it in would require a theology. So gratitude becomes a wellness technique: a transaction you perform on your own nervous system for your own benefit. You thank no one, and the thank-you note is addressed to yourself.

The Quran has no objection to the benefits — it just refuses the blank address line. And in this verse, it quietly shows you how much bigger *shukr* is than a journaling habit.

[PAUSE]

Look at how the classical commentators unpack *man shakar* — "the one who was grateful." Al-Jalalayn glosses it: *the one who believed in Allah and His Messenger and obeyed them.* Al-Baghawi transmits the words of the early commentator Muqatil: *"whoever affirmed Allah's oneness — He will not punish him along with the idolaters."*

Stop and feel how strange that is for a moment. The verse says *grateful*, and the commentators say *believer*. The verse says *shakara*, and the tafsir tradition reads *tawḥīd* — pure monotheism itself.

Is that a stretch? It is not — and the Quran's own vocabulary proves it. Listen to how the Quran names the opposite of gratitude. In Surah Al-Insan: "We guided him to the way — either *shākiran* (grateful) or ***kafūran***" (76:3). The opposite of grateful is *kafūr* — from the same root as *kufr*, disbelief. And the root meaning of *k-f-r* in the classical lexicons is *to cover*. The disbeliever and the ingrate are the same word because they are the same act: **covering over what you know you received, and where you know it came from.**

Now the commentators' reading snaps into focus. *Shukr* isn't one virtue on a list. It is the *orientation* underneath all the virtues — the settled recognition of where things come from. And *kufr* isn't primarily an intellectual position; it is ingratitude wearing a philosophy. Which means the gratitude journal with the blank address line and outright denial differ in degree, not in kind: both stop one step short of the Source.

This is why the verse can hang the entire principle of divine reward on this single verb. *Kadhālika najzī man shakar* — because *man shakar*, read fully, means: the one whose inner compass points correctly. The one who, when good arrives, traces it home.

[PAUSE]

And now bring back the grammar from our first element, because this is where it detonates. The gratitude is perfect tense — finite, human-sized, an act with edges. The reward is imperfect tense — unfinished, divine-sized, ongoing. This is the exact architecture of the famous promise in Surah Ibrahim: *la-in shakartum la-azīdannakum* — "if you are grateful, I will surely increase you" (14:7). Gratitude in, increase out — and the increase generates new blessing, which invites new gratitude, which triggers new increase. It isn't a transaction. It's a *spiral*. A feedback loop with no ceiling, where the only finite component is the one you contribute.

The practical edge for tomorrow: keep the gratitude practice — but **fill in the address line.** When the *alhamdulillah* leaves your lips, let it actually travel somewhere. Aim it. The wellness industry discovered the engine of gratitude; the Quran has always known its destination. And only gratitude that arrives somewhere can be *answered* by Someone — *najzī*, ongoing, unfinished, again and again.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

I asked you to hold onto a word. *ʿInda* — presence.

Our verse, in the middle of the surah, says the rescue came *min ʿindinā* — from Our presence. Now listen to how Surah Al-Qamar *ends*. After the corridor of ruined nations, after the final scenes of judgment, the very last verse of the surah describes the destination of the righteous:

**فِي مَقْعَدِ صِدْقٍ عِندَ مَلِيكٍ مُّقْتَدِرٍ**

*"…in a seat of truth, in the presence of (ʿinda) an All-Powerful Sovereign."* (54:55)

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The favor comes *from* His presence in verse 35. The grateful are seated *in* His presence in verse 55. *Min ʿindinā* … *ʿinda malīkin muqtadir*. The rescue and the destination carry the same word. What was sent *from* the presence eventually draws the grateful *back to* the presence. The gift was never just the escape from the burning city — the gift was a thread, and the thread leads home.

And step back once more, to the whole surah. Five destroyed nations. One tolling refrain. And embedded in the fourth story — only there — a single verse stating why anyone is ever *saved*: thus do We reward the grateful. In a surah that is ninety-five percent warning, the one verse of reward is not missing. It is *rationed* — placed like a single lamp in a long dark corridor, so that you cannot mistake what the way out actually is.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery from two directions. The grammar showed us a finite past-tense *shakara* answered by an infinite present-tense *najzī*, a rescue re-labeled *niʿmah* by a hanging word, a provenance marked *from His presence*. The themes showed us what that means lived: your survivals are gifts, not achievements; gratitude is not a wellness transaction but the orientation of a soul that knows its Source; and the response to a finite human thank-you is a divine reward that grammatically never ends.

One reality, twice seen: **He gives from His presence, and the only thing He asks you to complete is the recognition — the rest, He keeps open forever.**

### Questions to Carry

- What is one survival in your life that you have been filing under "luck" or "my own competence" — and what would change if you re-filed it as *niʿmah min ʿindih*?
- When you feel grateful, where does the feeling *go*? Does your gratitude have an address?
- Lut's family received the language of divine nearness on the worst night of their lives. Is there a loss in your past that might have been a rescue wearing different clothes?
- If Allah's reward is ongoing and yours was a single completed act — what does that tell you about who you are dealing with?
- The surah rations its light: one reward-verse among five ruins. When you speak to your own family about Allah, is your ratio of warning to hope drawn from fear — or from His example?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Your survival was never your achievement — it was a favor from His presence, and the finite gratitude that recognizes this is answered by a reward that never learns how to stop.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, find one rescue in your past — one road not taken, one harm that swerved — and say over it, deliberately, what this verse says: *this was a favor, from His presence.* Not as a ritual. As a re-classification. You are not being asked to manufacture a feeling. You are being invited to read your own life with corrected labels — and to discover that the corrected labels were love letters all along.
