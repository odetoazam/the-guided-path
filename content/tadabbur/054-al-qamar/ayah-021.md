# The Question Allah Asks Twice

## فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ

**Fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur**

*"So how was My punishment — and My warnings?"*

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Drumbeat in Makkah

Before we can hear this ayah, we need to hear the surah it lives in — because Surah Al-Qamar does not speak the way other surahs speak. It pounds.

Picture Makkah in the middle years of the message. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling for years now, and the response from Quraysh has hardened into a posture: *show us a sign.* And then a sign comes — the surah opens with it. The moon, the most stable object in their night sky, the thing they set their calendars by, splits. And what do they say? *Siḥrun mustamirr* — "passing magic" (54:2). They look at a torn sky and file it under *tricks*.

So the surah changes register. It stops arguing and starts reading case files. The people of Nūḥ. ʿĀd. Thamūd. The people of Lūṭ. The house of Pharaoh. One after another, each nation that heard a warner and called him a liar, each one closed out with the same hammering refrains — and the whole surah locked into a single relentless rhyme, verse after verse ending on that same clipped sound: *mustamirr... dusur... nudhur... muddakir*. If you have ever heard Al-Qamar recited aloud, you know the effect. It is not a melody. It is a drumbeat. It is the sound of something approaching.

Our ayah sits inside the second case file. ʿĀd — the towering people of the south, the ones even Quraysh told stories about, the nation of Iram whose ruins had already become a proverb. Two verses earlier, Allah described what came for them: a *ṣarṣar* wind — a screaming, frigid wind — on a day of unbroken calamity, **plucking people from the ground like uprooted palm trunks** (54:19–20). Not bending them. Not burying them. Plucking them — as if a human being planted on the earth were no harder to remove than a dead tree.

And then, while that image is still hanging in the air, comes our ayah:

**فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ** — *So how was My punishment, and My warnings?*

Here is the thing you need to notice, and it is the question this entire reflection exists to answer:

**You have already heard this exact sentence.** Word for word, letter for letter — three verses earlier, in 54:18. Allah asked this precise question *before* describing the wind. Now He asks it again *after*.

Why would the Speaker whose speech contains nothing wasted ask the identical question twice, four verses apart?

Stay with that. Because the answer is going to turn out to be the secret of how the Quran works on a human heart — and it may change how you read every ayah you have ever memorized.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: كَيْفَ كَانَ — A Question in the Past Tense

Before we open this word, let's establish something about questions — because not all questions are requests for information.

Think about how this works in your own speech. A teacher returns an exam and asks a student who didn't study, *"So how did that go?"* The teacher has the grade sheet in her hand. She is not gathering data. The question is doing something else entirely: it is making the student *say it* — or at least making the student stand inside the answer for a moment. Arabic rhetoric has a name for this: *istifhām* that has left behind the job of asking and taken up the job of confronting. The classical scholars describe such questions as serving *taqrīr* — pressing the listener to affirm what is already established — and *tahwīl* — magnifying the dread of it.

Now look at the ayah. **Kayfa kāna** — "How *was*." Past tense. The verb كَانَ, perfect tense, completed action. This is not "how will My punishment be," a threat aimed forward. The event is over. The wind has come and gone. The palm-trunk bodies are already lying in the text, two verses back.

So ask yourself: who answers a question like this?

Notice something. Allah asks — and then does not answer in words. There is no verse following that says "it was severe." (The tafsir al-Muyassar spells out what is implied — *it was immense, painful* — precisely because the text itself leaves it unstated.) The answer is not given because the answer was **already shown**. Verses 19 and 20 *are* the answer. The screaming wind is the answer. The uprooted bodies are the answer. The question arrives after its own answer, the way a teacher holds up the graded exam and says: *so — how did that go?*

And here is what is remarkable: a question with no possible verbal answer forces the listener to answer with something other than words. You answer *kayfa kāna ʿadhābī* with a feeling in your chest. The grammar has trapped you into responding from the heart, because the mind has nothing left to supply.

**This single past-tense question turns every listener, in every century, into a witness being asked to testify about something they just watched.**

### Element Two: عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ — One Punishment, Many Warnings

Two nouns, joined under one question. Let's open them slowly.

**ʿAdhābī** — "My punishment." From the root ʿayn–dhāl–bāʾ. And do not rush past that little letter at the end: the yāʾ, *my*. Allah attaches the punishment to Himself. Not "the punishment," not "their punishment" — **My** punishment. The wind that came for ʿĀd was not weather. It was not a tragic climate event that a sufficiently advanced civilization might have engineered around. It carried a signature.

**Wa nudhur** — "and My warnings." Root nūn–dhāl–rāʾ, the root of *nadhīr*, warner — the very title the Quran gives the Prophet ﷺ.

Now, what exactly is *nudhur*? Here the classical commentators preserve a genuine discussion, and rather than choosing sides, let's do what the tradition itself invites: treat each opinion as a camera angle on the same reality.

Al-Qurṭubī records the reading of the grammarian al-Farrāʾ: both words are *maṣdars* — verbal nouns — so *nudhur* means "My warning" as an act: *My act of warning you*. On this angle, the question reads: *how was My punishment, and how was My warning?* — that is, **did My warning turn out to be true?** The question vindicates the warner. Every prophet who was mocked as a fearmonger is being publicly proven right.

Al-Qurṭubī also records the second reading: *nudhur* is the **plural** of *nadhīr* — warnings, or warners, many of them — the way *nakīr* relates to *inkār*. On this angle, the question reads: *how was My punishment, and how were My many warnings?*

Hold both angles up together and look at what they jointly reveal — because composite, they are saying something neither says alone.

**The punishment is singular. The warnings are plural.**

ʿAdhāb — one word, one event, one day. *Nudhur* — a multitude. Hūd عليه السلام did not warn ʿĀd once. He warned them for a lifetime. Warning upon warning, year upon year, patience upon patience — and then *one* punishment. The grammar itself is an audit of divine mercy: count the warnings, then count the punishments. The ledger is never balanced. It is overwhelmingly, lopsidedly weighted toward warning.

Let's sit with this. When we read the punishment narratives, we tend to feel the severity of the single day and miss the leniency of the decades that preceded it. But the ayah pairs them deliberately, under one *kayfa*, forcing you to evaluate them together: *how was My punishment — and My warnings?* As if to say: you cannot assess the justice of the storm without first counting the years of forecasts that were ignored.

### Element Three: The Vanishing Letter

Now for something you can only see if you look at the ayah's final word very closely — and only hear if you listen to the whole surah.

We said *ʿadhābī* ends with the yāʾ of possession: عَذَابِى — "My punishment," the *my* fully written. Now look at the second noun: وَنُذُرِ. The meaning is "My warnings" — the commentators are explicit that the possessive is intended, and the kasrah at the end stands in its place. But the yāʾ itself — the written letter that says *my* — **is not there.** It has been elided.

This elision is documented, classical Arabic. At verse-endings — the *fawāṣil* — the Quran regularly trims the final yāʾ of "my," leaving the kasrah as its trace; you find the same pattern in فَحَقَّ عِقَابِ ("so how was My retribution," 38:14) and elsewhere. And the immediate effect here is sonic: it keeps the drumbeat. *Mustamirr. Dusur. Nudhur. Muddakir.* The entire surah marches on that clipped, single-syllable rhyme, and *nudhuri* with a full yāʾ would have broken stride. The rhythm of approaching judgment does not pause — not even for a pronoun.

That much is grammar and sound, firmly attested. But let me offer you something further — and I will flag it honestly as an invitation to consider, not a claim of the grammarians:

Look at the asymmetry. **"My punishment" keeps its letter. "My warnings" loses it.** The punishment arrives in the text whole, fully possessed, nothing trimmed. The warning arrives clipped, quiet, its claim on us written so lightly that the letter itself has slipped away.

Is that not exactly how these two things arrive in a life? Punishment — consequence — lands complete. It never abbreviates itself. But warnings come to us *trimmed*: the quiet unease before the bad decision, the verse that pricked you and was forgotten by evening, the friend's gentle word you half-heard. Warnings whisper in elided letters. Consequences print in full.

What this invites us to consider is that the very orthography of the ayah is rehearsing its message: **if you only respond to what arrives at full volume, you will only ever respond to punishments.**

### The Bridge

So look at what the construction has shown us. A question in the past tense that can only be answered by the heart of a witness. A singular punishment weighed against plural warnings, exposing the mercy hidden inside the threat. And a vanished letter that makes the warning quieter than the consequence — on the page itself.

But we have not yet touched the strangest fact about this ayah: that it is a *repetition*. The identical sentence, twice in four verses. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the person hearing them.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Same Words, A Changed Reader

There is a drawer, somewhere in your home or your phone, that holds a message you have read twice — once before, and once after.

Maybe it is the last text from someone you lost, read again the week after the funeral. Maybe it is the doctor's letter you skimmed when you felt fine, reread the day the second test came back. Maybe it is something smaller: advice from a parent you rolled your eyes at when you were twenty, which ambushed you at thirty-five with the full weight of being true. The words did not change. **Not one letter changed.** And yet the second reading was a different event entirely — because between the two readings, something happened to *you*.

Everyone carries at least one of these. The message that was information the first time and a wound — or a mercy — the second.

Now watch what Surah Al-Qamar does with this universal human experience.

Verse 18: *ʿĀd denied — so how was My punishment and My warnings?* The question sounds, and at that moment you have heard only a verdict, no scene. The question functions as a door: *you are about to find out.*

Verses 19–20: the scene. The screaming wind. The day of unbroken calamity. Human beings ripped from the earth like hollow palm trunks — *aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿir* — and notice, a palm trunk without its crown is a body without distinction, anonymous, horizontal, finished.

Verse 21 — our ayah: *So how was My punishment and My warnings?*

**The same question. But you are no longer the same listener.** Three verses ago, you heard it as a headline. Now you hear it as an examination — because now you have *seen* the wind, and the question quietly turns to you and asks you to testify. The first asking opened the case. The second asking calls you to the stand. Identical words; transformed meaning; and the only thing that changed in between was the experience deposited inside the one listening.

Here's what is remarkable: this is not merely a rhetorical technique inside the surah. **It is a description of how the entire Quran is designed to work across a lifetime.**

The Quran does not change. The muṣḥaf on your shelf has the same letters it had when you were nine years old, sounding out Surah Al-Fātiḥah. But you have read *iyyāka naʿbudu wa iyyāka nastaʿīn* before and after desperation. You have read the verses on patience before and after the year that required it. And you know — you have felt — that the second reading was not a repetition. The ayah was lying in wait for the version of you who would finally be able to hear it.

This is why the Quran repeats, and why its repetition is never redundancy. Allah asks *fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur* twice in four verses because **a question asked of an innocent listener and a question asked of a witness are two different questions** — even when the words match. And He repeats His stories and refrains across the surah, across the whole Book, because He is not addressing one static reader. He is addressing every future version of you, each of whom will arrive at the same verse carrying different scars, and each of whom deserves to find the verse waiting.

The people of Quraysh heard the stories of ʿĀd around their fires for generations — as folklore, as the first asking. The Quran retells the story so that they hear it a second time as something else: as their own file being read aloud in advance.

**The practical edge of this:** choose one ayah you memorized young — one so familiar your tongue runs it without your heart attending — and reread it deliberately *through* something you have lived since. Bring the divorce, the diagnosis, the child, the failure, the unexpected ease — bring it to the verse and read again. You are not reviewing. You are giving the ayah its second asking. Tadabbur, at its core, may be exactly this: returning to unchanged words as a changed person, and letting the difference measure you.

### Theme Two: The Mercy of Still Being Asked

In hospitals, in aviation, in engineering firms, there is a practice with a cold name: the post-mortem. After a failure — a crash, a collapse, a fatal error — the people involved sit in a room and walk through exactly what went wrong, decision by decision, warning by ignored warning. The practice has one unspoken premise so obvious it is never stated:

**Only the living attend the post-mortem.**

The review of the disaster is conducted by those the disaster did not take. And the entire value of the room lies in that fact — the people examining the wreckage are the ones who can still do something differently.

Now return to the ayah and ask the question we have been circling all along: *to whom is this addressed?*

Not to ʿĀd. ʿĀd cannot answer. The wind has already carried them out of the conversation; you cannot ask "how was My punishment?" of the people who *are* the answer. The question sails over the wreckage and lands on whoever is reading the file — Quraysh in Makkah, and you, here, now.

Let's sit with what that means. **The fact that you are being asked is itself the proof that it is not yet your story.** Allah is conducting the post-mortem of ʿĀd in front of you — walking you through the warnings, the denial, the wind — and the entire transaction only makes sense because you are still in the category of people who can answer with their lives rather than with their ruins. The question is severe in its content and tender in its address. It says, underneath the thunder: *you are still here. You can still hear this. Which means, for you, this is still a warning — not yet a verdict.*

And then — listen to what comes immediately next, because the surah does not leave you in the dread. The very next verse, 54:22:

**وَلَقَدْ يَسَّرْنَا الْقُرْآنَ لِلذِّكْرِ فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ**
*"And We have certainly made the Quran easy for remembrance — so is there any who will remember?"*

Look at the sequence. *How was My punishment and My warnings?* — and before the echo fades — *We have made the Quran easy... is there anyone who will take heed?* The terrifying question is followed instantly by the open door. As if to say: ʿĀd's warnings came through Hūd, and they are gone; *your* warning is this very Book, and it has been deliberately made easy — easy to recite, easy to carry, easy to return to — so that no one standing at the post-mortem can claim the lessons were locked away.

Here is the paradox worth not rushing to resolve: the most frightening verses in the Quran are, by the mere fact of reaching you, acts of mercy. A warning that arrives is a punishment that has not. *Nudhur* — plural, patient, repeated — is what Allah sends for decades. *ʿAdhāb* — singular — is what comes only after every warning has been spent. To be on the receiving end of the question *fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur* is to be standing, by definition, on the warned side of the line and not the punished side. The dread you feel reading it is the feeling of the door still being open.

**The practical edge of this:** the next time something in your life functions as a warning — a near-miss, a health scare, a marriage strained but not broken, a Ramadan that exposed how far you'd drifted — resist the instinct to merely feel relieved and move on. Conduct the post-mortem while you are still the one conducting it. Ask, concretely: *what was this the trimmed, quiet, elided-letter version of?* Then answer with one changed behavior — one — within the week. ʿĀd's tragedy was not that they received no warnings. It was that they received only warnings, plural, and treated every one as noise.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now that you have done the work, you are ready to see the design — and it is worth seeing, because it can be counted directly from the text.

The refrain *fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur* tolls through Surah Al-Qamar like a bell. For the people of Nūḥ, it sounds once — **after** their flood (54:16). For Thamūd, once — **before** their blast (54:30). But for ʿĀd, and only for ʿĀd, it sounds **twice: before the wind (54:18) and after it (54:21).** One story in the surah is bracketed by the question on both sides — the punishment scene is framed, entered through the question and exited through the same question.

The surah, in other words, *built* the experience we described in Theme One. It did not merely tell you that identical words transform when the listener changes — it ran the experiment on you. It asked you the question cold, showed you the wind, and asked you again, so that you would feel, in the four-verse interval of your own reading, the difference between hearing a warning and witnessing its fulfillment. Why this design lands on ʿĀd specifically, the text does not say — though one may notice that ʿĀd's punishment is the wind: the invisible force, the warning you cannot see until it is upon you. That much is an invitation to ponder, not a verdict.

### Bringing It Together

Every path we walked arrives at the same place. The past-tense question with no verbal answer makes you a witness. The singular punishment against plural warnings reveals the mercy hidden in the threat. The elided yāʾ shows warnings arriving quieter than consequences. The doubled refrain proves that the same words mean more to the one who has seen. And the address itself — a question only the living can receive — is the open door. The linguistics and the themes are one insight from two directions: **Allah's warnings are engineered to be heard twice — once as words, once as truth — and His mercy is the gap between the two hearings, where repentance lives.**

### Questions to Carry

- Which ayah in your life is still waiting for its second asking — words you memorized before you had lived enough to hear them?
- If you audited your last five years the way this ayah audits ʿĀd — warnings plural, consequence singular — what were the *nudhur* you filed under noise?
- Warnings arrive with the letter trimmed: quiet, brief, easy to miss. What is the quietest warning currently present in your life, and what would it look like written out in full?
- The question in this ayah can only be received by someone still able to change. What does it mean, concretely, that you were able to read it today?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah asks the same question before the storm and after it, because words of warning only become real to a heart that has watched them come true — and His mercy is that He still asks you while you can still answer.**

### The Closing Invitation

This week, give one familiar verse its second asking. Choose it tonight. Read it through what you have lived. And when something in your days arrives quiet and clipped — the elided-letter kind of warning — treat it, just once, with the seriousness you would give it written in full.

### Duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who warned in plurals and punished only once — make us of those who hear Your words the second time before the storm forces a third. Let Your Quran, which You made easy for remembrance, find in us someone who remembers, and let every warning that reaches us be enough — so that we meet You among those who were asked, and answered with their lives.
