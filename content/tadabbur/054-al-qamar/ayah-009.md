# They Called Him Mad — and Allah Called Him "Our Servant"

## Surah Al-Qamar, Ayah 9

[ayah:54:9] ۞ كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ فَكَذَّبُوا۟ عَبْدَنَا وَقَالُوا۟ مَجْنُونٌ وَٱزْدُجِرَ

*Kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin fa-kadhdhabū ʿabdanā wa-qālū majnūnun wazdujir*

"Before them, the people of Nuh denied — they denied Our servant and said, 'A madman!' — and he was driven away."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Night the Moon Split and Nothing Changed

Before we can hear this ayah, we have to stand where its first listeners stood.

Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling his people for years. And then something happens that should have ended the argument forever: the moon splits. Not a metaphor, not a vision — the surah opens with it as a past event: *iqtarabat is-sāʿatu wanshaqqal-qamar* — "the Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split."

Hold that moment. The single most reliable object in the pre-modern sky — the thing every Arab navigated by, counted months by, named their caravans' departures by — tears in two above their heads.

And what do they say?

*Siḥrun mustamirr* — "continuing magic" (54:2). Just more of the same trickery. They had a filing cabinet for everything this man did, and the file was labeled *sorcery*, and even the moon went into the file.

Now feel the Prophet's position ﷺ. What do you do when the *moon splitting* isn't enough? When the most spectacular sign conceivable gets shrugged into a category? There is a particular kind of exhaustion in that — the exhaustion of a person who has given everything, shown everything, and been met with a label instead of a response.

It is into *that* exhaustion that Ayah 9 descends. Al-Qurtubi says these narratives of past nations came as *ta'nīs* — companionship — and *taʿziya* — consolation — for the Prophet ﷺ. Allah does not respond to the moon-deniers by arguing with them. He turns to His messenger and says, in effect: *let Me tell you about the first time this happened.*

And here is the hook — the thing I want you to carry as a question into everything that follows. In this ayah, the people of Nuh hurl the ugliest word they have at him: *majnūn*. Madman. And Allah, narrating their insult, refuses to even repeat Nuh's name in that breath. He calls him something else instead. Two words. And those two words are the entire answer — not just to Nuh's tormentors, but to every person who has ever been mocked for what they believe.

What are those two words, and why do they change everything?

Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: كَذَّبَتْ... فَكَذَّبُوا — Why Does Allah Say "Denied" Twice?

Before we open this, let's learn something about how careful speech works.

When a skilled writer repeats a word, the repetition is never lazy. If a witness in court says "he hit him — and then he *hit* him," the second word is not the first one over again. The first establishes the fact; the second carries the weight of continuation, of escalation, of *again*. Repetition in masterful language is never a copy. It is a second blow.

Now look at our ayah. It opens: *kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥ* — "the people of Nuh denied before them." Then immediately: *fa-kadhdhabū ʿabdanā* — "so they denied Our servant."

Denied... so they denied. The same verb, the same intensive Form II — *kadhdhaba*, which doesn't mean "to doubt" but "to actively declare a liar" — twice, back to back, joined by *fa*, the Arabic connector of consequence.

Why?

This question is old. Al-Qurtubi preserves al-Zamakhshari raising it directly: *what is the meaning of "so they denied" after "they denied"?* And the answer he records opens in two directions — and notice, as we walk through them, that these are not competing alternatives. They are two camera angles on the same crime.

The first angle: **denial upon the heels of denial** — *takdhīban ʿalā ʿaqibi takdhīb*. Every time one denying generation passed away, another denying generation followed it. Remember how long Nuh stood among them — the Quran tells us elsewhere: nine hundred and fifty years (29:14). Children were born into mocking him, raised mocking him, grew old mocking him, and trained their children to mock him. The first *kadhdhabat* is the people as a body across time; the second *kadhdhabū* is each wave, each century, renewing the verdict. The repetition is doing what no single verb could: it is making you feel **duration**. Not an event of denial — a *civilization* of denial.

The second angle, also preserved by al-Qurtubi from al-Zamakhshari: they had already denied the very idea of messengers — rejected prophethood at the root — *so when Our servant came, of course they denied him too*. The first denial is the closed door; the second is what happens to the specific person who knocks on it. They didn't evaluate Nuh and find him wanting. They had decided before he ever spoke that no one like him could exist.

**Stay with that second reading, because it describes something you have seen.** A person who has pre-decided that "no one can tell me how to live" does not actually hear any individual who tries. The specific rejection was finished before the specific encounter began. The *fa* — "so" — is the sound of a foregone conclusion: they denied [the very possibility], *so naturally* they denied [the man].

And one more texture, small but documented: the first verb is feminine — *kadhdhabat* — agreeing, as al-Jalalayn notes, with the collective sense of *qawm*. A people, as a single mass, denied. Then the second verb shifts to plural — *kadhdhabū* — *they*, individually, denied. The crowd becomes persons. Because on the Day of Judgment, "my society denied him" will not be anyone's defense. The grammar moves from the mass to the members — from the mob to the men inside it.

So this is what the doubled verb reveals: **denial is never a single act. It is an inheritance, a momentum, a door closed before the knock** — and yet the grammar insists on counting every individual hand that held it shut.

### Element Two: عَبْدَنَا — The Name That Isn't There

Let's learn another principle before we open the next word: in the Quran, what is *missing* can speak as loudly as what is present. When Arabic expects a name and gets a title instead, the substitution is the message.

Now — Nuh has already been named in this ayah: *qawmu Nūḥ*, "the people of Nuh." So when the next clause needs to say who they denied, the easy word, the available word, the *already-used* word is right there: Nuh.

And Allah does not use it.

*Fa-kadhdhabū ʿabdanā.* "So they denied **Our servant**."

Notice something. The name appears when Nuh is being identified relative to *them* — they are "the people of Nuh." But the moment the sentence turns to the crime — the denial, the insult, the wound — Allah withdraws the name and substitutes a relationship: *ʿabd*, servant, bound to *nā*, **Us**.

Why does this matter? Think about what happens in your own speech when someone insults a person under your protection. A stranger mocks someone, and you say: "That's **my son** you're talking about." You don't say his name. The name is irrelevant in that moment — what you are announcing is *whose he is*. You are telling the mocker that the insult has not landed where they aimed it. It has landed on **you**.

*ʿAbdanā* is Allah doing exactly this. The people of Nuh thought they were dismissing a strange old man in their town square. Allah's wording reveals what they were actually doing: laying hands on something that belongs to the Lord of the worlds. The denial of the servant becomes an offense against the Master — and the Master says so by erasing the name and writing His own claim over it: *Ours.*

And there is a second layer here, and it is the tender one. *ʿAbd* — servant, slave — sounds to modern ears like a demotion. In the Quran's grammar of honor, it is the opposite. It is the title Allah uses for His messengers at their highest moments — for Muhammad ﷺ on the night journey: *subḥānalladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi* — "glory to the One who took **His servant** by night" (17:1). When the whole world calls you *majnūn*, Allah's answer is not to list your achievements. It is to say: *he is Mine.* That single possessive pronoun outweighs nine hundred and fifty years of mockery.

Here's what's remarkable when you put the two halves of the ayah side by side. *Their* word for Nuh: **majnūn**. *Allah's* word for Nuh: **ʿabdunā**. The entire ayah is a courtroom where two names for one man are entered into the record — and only one of them is spoken by the Judge.

### Element Three: وَازْدُجِرَ — The Verb With No One Behind It

One more concept, and this one is about a tool English barely has: the **unnamed passive**. Arabic can build a verb where the doer is grammatically deleted — not hidden, *deleted* — so that the act stands alone with its victim. "He was driven away." By whom? The verb refuses to say.

The last word of the ayah is *wazdujira* — and it is doing several things at once, so let's slow down.

First, its shape. The root is *z-j-r* — to rebuke, to repel, to drive off harshly, the word used for shouting a beast away. The form is the eighth — *iftaʿala* — which here intensifies and absorbs the action into its object. And listen to what happened to the word's own body: the form's *t* collided with the *z* of the root and hardened into a *d* — *iztajara* became **izdujira**. This is documented, regular Arabic phonology — but sit with the result: the word itself is bruised. It comes out of the mouth in a harsh cluster — *izd* — a sound you almost have to push past your teeth. A word about being roughly driven off that is itself rough to say.

Second, its voice. *Izdujira* is passive: "he **was** repelled." The mob that did it goes unnamed. Nine and a half centuries of shouting, threatening, blocking, mocking — and in Allah's final record, the perpetrators don't even merit a subject position in the sentence. They wanted to erase Nuh; the grammar erases *them*. What survives is only the suffering and the one who bore it.

Now — what exactly did they do to him? Here the classical commentators give us a composite sketch, and each report adds a layer:

Al-Jalalayn says: *intaharūhu bil-sabbi wa-ghayrihi* — they rebuked him with insult **and other than it**. That quiet phrase, "and other than it," covers everything words can't.

Al-Muyassar fills in the shape of the threat: they rebuked him **warning him of every kind of harm if he did not stop his daʿwah**. So *izdujira* is not just abuse — it is abuse with an ultimatum attached: *be silent, or else.*

Al-Baghawi makes the "or else" explicit by connecting it to their own recorded words elsewhere in the Quran: *"If you do not desist, O Nuh, you will surely be of those who are stoned"* (26:116). The driving-away had a destination: execution.

And then al-Baghawi preserves a fourth angle, from Mujahid, that turns the word inside out: *izdujira* may not be Allah's report at all — it may be the **end of their quoted speech**. On this reading, they said: "A madman — *and he has been driven out of his senses*," *ustuṭīra junūnan* — unhinged, carried off, possessed. The accusation and the expulsion become one sentence in the mouths of the mockers.

Don't rush to pick one. Watch what the composite shows you: on every reading, the same machinery is at work. **First the label, then the license.** Call him *majnūn* — and once he is *majnūn*, you may shout at him, threaten him, stone him, because none of it counts as injustice anymore. You don't owe a madman an answer. You don't owe a madman *anything*. The word *majnūn* is not a description. It is a permission slip.

And notice the chilling economy of their insult. In the Arabic, they don't even say "he is a madman." They say one word: *majnūnun*. The subject is omitted — a documented and common Arabic structure, a predicate with its subject elided — but feel its effect here: the dismissal is so total it doesn't spend a full sentence on him. Nine hundred and fifty years of calling, answered with a single flicked word.

So gather what the language has shown us. A denial doubled to span generations and pre-decisions. A name withdrawn and replaced with Allah's own claim of ownership. A one-word slur, and a bruised passive verb whose perpetrators are deleted from the record.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself: about what dismissal actually is, and about where a human being's worth actually lives.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Label Is the Escape Hatch

Start somewhere familiar.

You are in a discussion — at work, in your family, online — and you make a point someone cannot answer. Watch closely what happens next, because it almost never happens that they say "I cannot answer that." Instead, something shifts. They stop addressing your *argument* and start addressing *you*. "You're being dramatic." "You've gone down a rabbit hole." "You're obsessed." The conversation about *what is true* quietly becomes a conversation about *what is wrong with you*.

Psychologists have a name for the genus this belongs to: pathologizing the dissenter. It is the cheapest move in the human repertoire, because it converts an unanswerable challenge into a diagnosable condition. A *claim* demands evaluation. A *condition* only demands management. The moment they can file you under a disorder, they never have to open your file again.

Now return to the people of Nuh, and see how precisely this ayah X-rays the move.

Nuh brought them a claim — the most consequential claim possible: *there is One God; I am sent; a reckoning is coming.* A claim like that, made consistently, soberly, for nine hundred and fifty years, by a man whose honesty they had no record against — that claim is *unbearable* if you don't want it to be true. You cannot refute it; his life is the evidence. You cannot ignore it; he will not stop. There is only one exit left.

*Majnūnun.*

A madman. And just like that, the pressure is off. The moon can split over Makkah and it's "continuing magic"; a man can plead with perfect coherence for ten centuries and he's "possessed." Do you see that these are the same technology? **The label is not a conclusion they reached. It is an escape hatch they built** — because the alternative to "he is mad" was "he is right," and "he is right" would have cost them everything: their idols, their hierarchy, their unaccountable lives.

This is why the accusation of madness follows the prophets like a shadow through the Quran — Nuh here, and the Prophet ﷺ in nearly the same words: *wa yaqūlūna innahu la-majnūn* — "and they say: he is surely mad" (68:51). The previous surah had already answered it: *mā anta bi-niʿmati rabbika bi-kāhinin wa-lā majnūn* — "you are not, by the grace of your Lord, a soothsayer or a madman" (52:29). The repetition across messengers isn't coincidence. It is a signature. Wherever an undeniable truth meets an unwilling heart, this exact word gets reinvented.

And here is where the ayah turns its light on *us* — because we are not always Nuh in this story. Be honest about the moments you have been the crowd. The reminder that made you uncomfortable, so you decided the reminder-giver was "extreme." The practicing relative whose consistency indicted your inconsistency, so you filed them under "fanatic." The ayah you scrolled past because engaging it would have demanded change, so you told yourself that whole way of thinking was "for fundamentalists." Every one of those labels was an *izdujira* — a driving-away — performed not with a mob's fists but with a flick of the mind.

The question this theme leaves at your door is surgical: **the last time you dismissed someone who unsettled you — did you answer their claim, or did you diagnose them?** Because only one of those is thinking. The other is fleeing with extra steps.

**Practical application:** This week, when you feel the reflex to label someone whose words disturb you — *extreme, naive, brainwashed, obsessed* — pause and force yourself to do the harder thing the people of Nuh refused for 950 years: answer the actual claim. Write it down if you must: *what did they actually say, and is it true?* If it's false, you'll now know why. And if you can't show it's false — sit with what that means.

### Theme Two: Whose You Are, Not What They Call You

Begin again in the modern world, with a quiet epidemic.

We have built, in this generation, the most elaborate reputation-machinery in human history. Profiles, metrics, followers, reviews — a permanent, public, numerical answer to the question *what do people think of me?* And the documented result is not confidence. It is fragility. When your sense of self is assembled from other people's reactions, every stranger holds a piece of you. One comment can ruin a day; one wave of mockery can ruin a year. The self becomes a stock price — and everyone else is the market.

The deep assumption under all of it is this: *I am what I am called.*

Now watch the ayah dismantle that assumption with two words.

The market rendered its verdict on Nuh. Not a niche opinion — the *unanimous* verdict of his entire civilization, sustained across roughly thirty generations: *majnūn*. If identity is what others call you, then Nuh, by every metric his world possessed, *was* a madman. He had no defenders, no faction, no platform. He is, by the numbers, the most comprehensively rejected man who ever lived — the Quran records that as he built the ark, every group of nobles that passed by ridiculed him (11:38).

And against that entire market, one voice: *ʿabdanā.* **Our servant.**

Here is the move the ayah makes, and I want you to feel its structure. It does not say Nuh was *actually* respected. It does not say the mockery didn't happen — *izdujira* confirms it happened, brutally. The ayah concedes the entire worldly verdict... **and then renders it irrelevant by changing the court.** What they call you is a fact about *them*. Whose you are is a fact about *you*. And only one of those facts survives.

Sit with this — because it explains something about Nuh that nothing else can. How does a man absorb mockery for nine hundred and fifty years without breaking? Not stoicism; the Quran lets us hear him complain to his Lord — *"My Lord, I called my people night and day, and my calling only increased them in flight"* (71:5-6). He felt every wound. What he had was not numbness. **What he had was a center of gravity located outside their reach.** They could touch his reputation, his comfort, his safety — they could *zajara* him from every gathering — but his identity was stored where their hands could not go: in *ʿabdunā*, in being His. You can drive a man out of every place except the one relationship he actually lives in.

And now the ayah quietly extends its hand to you. Because *ʿabd* is not a title reserved for prophets — it is the one identity every believer already holds. The same possessive is offered to you five times a day: you stand in prayer precisely as *ʿabd*, and in that standing, every other verdict on you is suspended. The colleague's condescension, the family's disappointment, the algorithm's indifference, the ex-friend's slander — none of it is admissible in the court where you actually reside.

This is not a license for arrogance — "everyone who criticizes me is the people of Nuh." Sincere criticism from those who wish you well is a gift; the ayah is not about feedback, it is about *takdhīb* — denial aimed at the truth you carry, mockery whose purpose is to make you put it down. Learn to tell them apart: criticism wants you *better*; *takdhīb* wants you *silent*.

But when it is the second kind — when you are mocked precisely for the prayer, the hijab, the honesty, the refusal to lie for the company, the unwillingness to laugh at what shouldn't be funny — then this ayah was sent ahead of you, like supplies cached on a hard road. They called the best of creation *majnūn*. The insult you're absorbing has a lineage, and so does the answer: you are not what you are called. **You are whose you are.**

**Practical application:** The next time mockery or dismissal lands on you for something you did for Allah's sake — and you feel the heat rise in your chest — say silently the two-word answer Allah gave on Nuh's behalf: *I am His servant.* Then ask one question before responding: *am I about to defend my ego, or His truth?* Answer only for the second. The first was never under threat.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now step back and look at the whole verse at once, because its pieces lock together with terrible elegance.

The ayah is built as a perfect exchange of verdicts:

- **They** speak one word about him: *majnūn* — severing him from sanity, from society, from credibility.
- **Allah** speaks one word about him: *ʿabdanā* — binding him to the Lord of the worlds.

One word to cut him off; one word to take him in. And around this exchange, the verbs carry the timeline: *kadhdhabat... fa-kadhdhabū* — denial stretched across generations — ending in *izdujira*, the bruised, passive, perpetrator-less verb of a man driven out.

But the architecture doesn't end at the ayah's edge. The very next verse (54:10) records what the driven-out man did: *fa-daʿā rabbahu annī maghlūbun fantaṣir* — "so he called his Lord: I am overcome, so take vengeance." The one they expelled from every human door walked through the one door they couldn't close. And the verse after that, the sky itself opens. The people who drove away one man were themselves driven from existence by water — and the verdict *majnūn* drowned with them, while *ʿabdunā* is still being recited in your mouth tonight, fourteen centuries and several millennia after they thought they had the last word.

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey turn out to be one discovery seen from two sides: **the words people throw at the truth-carrier are deleted from history's record — like the deleted subject of *izdujira* — while the word Allah attaches to him is preserved in the only Book that doesn't decay.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When someone's words unsettle me, what is my honest first move — engaging their claim, or reaching for a label that excuses me from it?
2. Is there a "Nuh" in my life right now — someone whose consistency quietly indicts me — whom I have filed under *extreme* so I don't have to listen?
3. Where is my identity actually stored? If every external verdict on me — every opinion, metric, and reputation — were erased tonight, what would remain?
4. Can I tell the difference, in my own life, between criticism that wants me better and *takdhīb* that wants me silent?
5. Nuh endured 950 years on the strength of *whose* he was. What sustains my endurance — and will it last under that kind of weather?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the world's unanimous verdict on a truthful man was "madman," Allah overruled it with a single possessive — *Our servant* — teaching every mocked believer that identity lives not in what you are called but in Whose you are.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, or in your next prayer, try this: as you stand, before you raise your hands, consciously locate yourself — not by your job, your reputation, your follower count, or anyone's opinion of you, but by the one title this ayah dignifies above every insult ever spoken: *ʿabd*. Stand as His. Stay there a moment. Notice how much of what wounded you this week simply has no jurisdiction in that room.

### Du'a

O Allah, You called Nuh "Our servant" when his whole world called him mad — write us among Your servants, and let Your naming of us outweigh every name we are called. When truth makes us strangers, anchor our hearts in belonging to You; and when we are tempted to dismiss what discomforts us, give us the courage to answer honestly instead of fleeing behind a label. You hear the one who is overcome when he calls — so hear us, and suffice us, as You sufficed him.

*Ameen.*
