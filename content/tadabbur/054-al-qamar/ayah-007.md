# The Eyes That Would Not Lower Themselves

## Surah al-Qamar, Ayah 7

[ayah:54:7] خُشَّعًا أَبْصَـٰرُهُمْ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ كَأَنَّهُمْ جَرَادٌ مُّنتَشِرٌ

*Khushshaʿan abṣāruhum yakhrujūna mina l-ajdāthi ka-annahum jarādun muntashir*

"With eyes humbled, they will emerge from the graves as if they were locusts scattering."

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Moon Splits, and Nobody Moves

Before we can hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, we have to stand where they stood.

Makkah. The Quraysh have just witnessed something that should have ended every argument: the moon — the most stable, most ancient, most reliable object in their sky — split. The surah opens with it: *iqtarabati s-sāʿatu wa-nshaqqa l-qamar.* The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split.

And what did they say? *Siḥrun mustamirr* — "passing magic." Magic that will fade. They looked at a torn sky and filed it away as a trick.

Sit with that for a moment, because it's the emotional climate this whole surah breathes. These are people whose **eyes have stopped working as instruments of truth**. Not their physical eyes — those saw the moon perfectly well. Something behind the eyes refused. They saw, and they declined to see.

So Allah tells His Messenger ﷺ in ayah 6: *fa-tawalla ʿanhum* — turn away from them. The conversation with the deniers is, for now, over. And then, in the same breath, the scene changes. A Day is named: *yawma yadʿu d-dāʿi ilā shay'in nukur* — the Day the Caller calls to something terrible, something the mind has no category for.

And then comes our ayah. The answer to the people who looked at a split moon and shrugged.

Here is what I want you to notice before we go any further — and this is the question we'll spend the next hour answering: **this ayah about the most catastrophic crowd scene in human history doesn't begin with the crowd. It doesn't begin with the graves. It doesn't begin with the Day. It begins with their eyes.**

Of everything Allah could have shown us first about the resurrection of the deniers — the terror, the noise, the earth breaking open — He shows us a gaze.

Why?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: خُشَّعًا أَبْصَـٰرُهُمْ — The State Arrives Before the Action

Before we open this phrase, let me teach you a structure that Arabic uses with surgical precision, because the whole first half of this ayah depends on it.

Arabic has a grammatical tool called the **ḥāl** — the circumstantial accusative. It answers the question: *in what state* did the action happen? In English we do something similar when we say, "Trembling, she opened the letter." Notice what that word order does to you. You meet the trembling before you meet the letter. The state colors everything that follows. If I instead say, "She opened the letter, trembling," the information is identical — but the experience isn't. In the first version, the trembling is the point.

Now look at our ayah.

The main action is يَخْرُجُونَ — *they emerge*. That's the event: bodies coming out of the earth. But Allah does not start there. He starts with the state: **خُشَّعًا أَبْصَـٰرُهُمْ** — *humbled, their eyes*.

The condition of their eyes is fronted before we are told they're even moving. Grammatically, this is the ḥāl placed ahead of its sentence. Rhetorically, it means: before you see them stand, before you see the graves open, before you see the swarm — **see their eyes.** The Day is introduced through a gaze.

Now let's open the word itself. *Khushshaʿ* comes from the root خ-ش-ع — the same root as *khushūʿ*, the word every praying Muslim knows. Al-Qurtubi defines it here plainly: *al-khushūʿ fī l-baṣar al-khuḍūʿ wa-dh-dhilla* — khushūʿ in the gaze is lowliness and abasement. Al-Jalalayn glosses it with one word: *dhalīlan* — abased.

But here is what should stop you. **Khushūʿ is ascribed to the eyes, not to the people.** Allah did not say *khāshiʿīna* — "they emerge humbled." He said their *eyes* are humbled. Al-Qurtubi asks exactly the question we're asking — why attach humility to the eyes? — and answers: **because the trace of honor and the trace of humiliation appear in a person's gaze before anywhere else.** He then stacks the evidence from across the Quran: *abṣāruhā khāshiʿah* — "its eyes humbled" (79:9); and the description of the people of loss on that Day, *khāshiʿīna mina dh-dhulli yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyy* — "humbled by disgrace, looking on with a stolen glance" (42:45).

A stolen glance. You know this. Every human being knows this. Shame doesn't announce itself in words — it announces itself in the inability to look up.

[PAUSE]

There's a second layer here, and it's preserved in how the Quran was recited. Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both record that Abu ʿAmr, Hamzah, and al-Kisāʾī — al-Baghawi adds Yaʿqūb — read this word as **خَاشِعًا** *(khāshiʿan)*, in the singular, while the others read **خُشَّعًا** *(khushshaʿan)*, the intensive plural. Al-Baghawi explains the grammar: when a participle comes before its plural subject, Arabic permits singular or plural, masculine or feminine — he even quotes a line of old poetry to prove it: *rijālun ḥasanun awjuhuhum* — "men, fair their faces." And he records that in the reading of Ibn Masʿūd it was *khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum*.

That's the documented grammar. Now let me offer you what this invites us to consider — and I flag it as reflection, not as rule: the community of reciters carried *both* a singular and a plural over this scene. Heard one way, a single quality of abasement lies over the whole crowd like one shadow. Heard the other way, it is countless individual pairs of eyes, each humbled on its own. The scene is both. One disgrace; billions of separate gazes carrying it.

And one more thread before we close this element — because the Quran explains itself. There is another ayah where this exact phrase appears, *khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum*, and there Allah tells us **why** the eyes are in that state. Surah al-Qalam, 68:43: their eyes humbled, disgrace covering them — *wa-qad kānū yudʿawna ilā s-sujūdi wa-hum sālimūn* — "for they used to be called to prostrate while they were sound and able."

There it is. The eyes that are forced down on that Day are the eyes that **refused to go down voluntarily in this one.** Khushūʿ was offered to them as an honor — *qad aflaḥa l-mu'minūn alladhīna hum fī ṣalātihim khāshiʿūn*, "successful are the believers, those who in their prayer have khushūʿ" (23:1–2). The same word. The exact same word. What the believer gives freely in salah is what will be extracted from the denier by force.

**What this single fronted phrase reveals: khushūʿ is not optional. It is only voluntary or involuntary. Every eye will know it — the only question this ayah leaves open is whether yours meets it as worship or as sentence.**

### Element Two: ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ — The Word That Only Appears When the Earth Opens

The Quran has more than one word for a grave. The common one is *qabr* — you know it from *ziyārat al-qubūr*, from *al-maqābir* in Surah at-Takāthur. But the word here is different: **الأَجْدَاث**, the plural of *jadath*.

Here is the remarkable thing, and it is checkable: the Quran uses this word **exactly three times** — here in 54:7, in Surah Yā-Sīn 36:51, and in Surah al-Maʿārij 70:43. Go and look at all three. In Yā-Sīn: the Trumpet is blown, *fa-idhā hum mina l-ajdāthi ilā rabbihim yansilūn* — "and behold, from the ajdāth to their Lord they stream." In al-Maʿārij: *yawma yakhrujūna mina l-ajdāthi sirāʿan* — "the Day they emerge from the ajdāth in haste."

Every single time this word appears, **bodies are coming out of it.** Not being lowered in. Not being visited. Not resting. Out.

Stay with me here, because this is how the Quran teaches through vocabulary. The *qabr* family of words appears in contexts of burial, of visiting, of dwelling. But when the Quran wants to show you the grave at the precise moment it stops being a grave — the moment it becomes a doorway — it reaches for *jadath*. In the Quran's usage, an *ajdāth* is not where the dead stay. It is what the living-again leave.

What this invites us to consider: every grave you have ever stood beside — every janazah, every cemetery you've driven past — you were not looking at an ending. You were looking at a departure lounge. The deniers of Makkah used to say, *a-idhā mitnā wa-kunnā turāban* — "when we are dust, will we really be brought back?" This one word quietly answers them: the place you think of as the final word, Allah names only in the act of being exited.

And al-Muyassar adds the detail that makes the scene move: they come out *fī intishārihim wa-surʿati sayrihim li-l-ḥisāb* — in their spreading and the speed of their walk **to the reckoning**. The graves don't open onto freedom. They open onto an appointment.

**What this single word reveals: the grave is the only prison whose every Quranic mention as *jadath* is a record of release — and the release is a summons.**

### Element Three: جَرَادٌ مُّنتَشِرٌ — The Scattering That Carries the Name of the Resurrection

Now the simile. And before we touch the locusts, let me teach you one thing about Arabic verb forms, because there is a quiet miracle sitting inside the second word.

Arabic builds families of meaning out of three-letter roots. The root here is **ن-ش-ر** — *n-sh-r* — whose basic meaning is to spread, unfold, unroll. From Form I you get *nashara*: to spread something out. The word in our ayah, **مُنتَشِر** *(muntashir)*, is the active participle of **Form VIII** — *intashara* — and Form VIII characteristically turns the action back on the doer: not "spread out *by someone*," but **spreading out of itself**. A cloth is spread; a swarm *spreads itself*. No individual locust is steering. The spreading simply happens *through* them — a mass in motion that no member of it is directing.

Hold that, and now watch what else this root does in the Quran. *Wa-idhā ṣ-ṣuḥufu nushirat* — "when the scrolls are unrolled" (81:10). *Thumma idhā shā'a ansharah* — "then, when He wills, He resurrects him" (80:22). *Wa-ilayhi n-nushūr* — "and to Him is the resurrection" (67:15).

Do you see it? **The Arabic word for resurrection — *nushūr* — is cut from the same root as the word describing how the locusts scatter.** The simile is not decorated onto the event; it is woven from the event's own name. The deniers spreading across the earth like insects *are* the nushūr — their very scattering spells the word they spent their lives denying. That is not my flourish. That is the lexicon.

[PAUSE]

Now, the locusts themselves. Why *jarād*?

Anyone who has read accounts of a locust swarm knows the two things it does to the human mind: it overwhelms by **number** — the sky darkens, the ground seethes, counting becomes meaningless — and it unsettles by **directionless motion**. A swarm boils. It goes everywhere and nowhere.

The classical commentators saw both. Al-Muyassar emphasizes the number and the speed: like locusts *muntashir fī l-āfāq* — spreading through the horizons, rushing to what they've been summoned to. Al-Jalalayn emphasizes the inner state: *lā yadrūna ayna yadhhabūna mina l-khawfi wa-l-ḥayra* — **"they do not know where they are going, from fear and bewilderment."** Put the two together — and this is how scholarly difference works, not as a multiple-choice question but as two camera angles on one scene — and you get the full picture: viewed from above, a swarm moving with terrifying speed and singleness; viewed from inside, a creature that has no idea where it is being carried.

And then ayah 8 resolves the paradox: *muhṭiʿīna ilā d-dāʿ* — racing, necks craned, **toward the Caller**. The swarm that knows no direction has exactly one direction, and it isn't choosing it. All their lives the call came and they scattered away from it. Now the call comes and they scatter *toward* it — because on that Day, away no longer exists.

One last thread, offered explicitly as a reflection the concordance invites rather than a ruling of tafsir: the word *jarād* appears in only one other place in the entire Quran — Surah al-Aʿrāf 7:133, where locusts are among the signs **sent against** Pharaoh's people when they denied. In this world, locusts were a plague sent upon the deniers. In the next, the deniers themselves become the locusts. The instrument of the sign and the people who refused the sign converge into a single image.

**What this simile reveals: a life spent scattering from the dhikr ends as scattering itself — countless, hurried, bewildered — and even the bewilderment obeys, streaming toward the One they would not answer.**

### The Bridge

Step back and look at what the language has done. A fronted ḥāl that makes the gaze the headline of the Day. A grave-word the Quran reserves for the moment of exit. A simile whose own root letters spell *resurrection*, describing a swarm that races without choosing.

The construction is airtight. Now let's ask what it reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Gaze Is the Ledger

Think about the last time you couldn't meet someone's eyes.

Maybe it was a person you'd wronged and hadn't apologized to. Maybe it was someone who knew something true about you that you were still pretending wasn't true. You could control your words around them. You could control your posture, your small talk, your smile. The one thing you could not fully control was your gaze. It kept sliding away. We even have the phrase for the opposite condition: *she can look anyone in the eye* — and we all understand it has nothing to do with vision and everything to do with a clear conscience.

Interrogators know this. Parents know this. Every human culture reads the eyes as the one place where the inner ledger leaks into the visible world.

The Quran knew it first, and says it without apology. Al-Qurtubi's comment on our ayah is essentially a statement of this universal law: honor and abasement *show in the gaze*. The Day of Judgment, in this ayah, doesn't begin with an announcement of anyone's deeds. It doesn't need to. **The verdict is already legible in the eyes before a single page of the record is opened.** Khushshaʿan abṣāruhum — the eyes come out of the graves already carrying the sentence.

Now let the ayah press further. *Why* are these particular eyes humbled? We heard the Quran's own answer from 68:43 — they were called to prostrate, while sound and able, and they would not. Their eyes are not being humiliated arbitrarily. Their eyes are being given, permanently, the one posture those eyes refused to take when it would have been beautiful.

Here is the paradox I want you to sit with, without rushing past it: **the lowered gaze appears twice in the Quran's story of the human being — once as the mark of the best of people, and once as the mark of the worst.** The believer in salah, eyes down on the place of sujūd, heart down with them — *khāshiʿūn* — and Allah opens Surah al-Mu'minūn by calling that *success*. And the denier on the Day, eyes down because they can no longer physically rise — *khushshaʿan* — and it is ruin. Same root. Same muscles. Same angle of the neck.

The difference is a single variable: **was it chosen?**

This is one of those places where the Quran quietly reorganizes your entire picture of worship. Khushūʿ in prayer can feel, on a tired Tuesday, like a burden — one more thing to summon. This ayah reframes it completely: khushūʿ is not a tax. It is a *rehearsal with the lights on*. Reality is coming in which every eye will be humbled before its Maker. Salah is the offer to do, five times a day, in dignity and love, what will otherwise be done to you once, in terror. The believer's lowered gaze in prayer is not weakness — it is someone paying, voluntarily and at a discount, a debt that will otherwise be collected in full.

And the contrast Allah Himself draws elsewhere completes it: on that same Day, there will be other faces — *wujūhun yawma'idhin nāḍirah, ilā rabbihā nāẓirah* — radiant faces, **looking at their Lord** (75:22–23). Some eyes lowered forever; some eyes lifted to the most beautiful sight in existence. And the fork between those two destinies runs, in part, through what you do with your eyes *tonight*.

**The practical edge, and it is very concrete:** in your next salah, do one thing — keep your eyes on the place of your sujūd and tell yourself why: *I am lowering these voluntarily, now, so they need never be lowered for me.* And outside salah, take the Quran's other command to the gaze — *qul li-l-mu'minīna yaghuḍḍū min abṣārihim*, lowering the gaze from what is forbidden (24:30) — as part of the same training. Where your eyes go in private is the first draft of where they'll be able to go on that Day.

### Theme Two: Gathered as a Swarm, Judged Alone

Stand on a bridge over any city at 8:40 on a weekday morning and watch the commuters. Or watch footage of a crowd crush, or even just watch your own thumb move a feed upward at midnight, in rhythm with a hundred million other thumbs. Modern life has made us experts in a strange condition: **moving fast, in enormous numbers, in directions almost none of us individually chose.**

Psychologists have a word for the inner side of this — *deindividuation* — the way a self dissolves into a crowd, how responsibility thins out as numbers grow, how a person in a swarm will move in ways the person alone never would. We treat it as a modern discovery. This ayah painted it fourteen centuries ago in five words: *ka-annahum jarādun muntashir.*

Because notice what the locust simile takes away from them. A name. A face. A history. Every one of those bodies coming out of the ajdāth was once somebody — a chief of Quraysh, proud of his lineage, a man whose arrival reorganized a gathering. The Makkan elite who heard this surah measured everything by standing: whose name carried weight, who walked at the front. And Allah shows them their future as *insects in a cloud*. Not even wolves or stallions — something defined by being uncountable. The aristocracy of Makkah, rendered indistinguishable. Form VIII *muntashir*, remember: spreading *of itself*, no one steering. The men who steered everything, steering nothing — not even their own feet.

Let the irony of the swarm's direction land fully. In this life, the call — *the* call, the dhikr this surah keeps offering with its refrain, *wa-laqad yassarnā l-Qur'āna li-dh-dhikr* — came to them, and their movement was always *away*. Scatter, deflect, "passing magic," change the subject. An entire civilization's worth of motion organized around not-answering. And now look at ayah 6 through 8 as one sweep: the Caller calls — and the same people pour out of the earth and stream *toward* him, *muhṭiʿīn*, necks extended, unable to do anything else. The swarm's one trajectory is the summons. **A lifetime of running from the call, and the very first act of the next life is running to it.**

Here is the paradox to sit with — and again, don't resolve it too fast: the gathering is the most collective event that will ever happen, and the judgment it leads to is the most solitary. *Wa-kulluhum ātīhi yawma l-qiyāmati fardan* — "and every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone" (19:95). Gathered as a swarm; judged one by one. The crowd is real, and the crowd is no shelter. On that Day the swarm gets you to the courtroom — and dissolves at the door.

And this is where the ayah turns and looks directly at us, because the deniers of Makkah are not the only people who have ever lived inside a swarm. Ask yourself, gently but honestly: how much of my motion is *muntashir* — self-spreading, crowd-shaped, unchosen? The career direction I never actually decided. The opinions I hold at the same temperature as my timeline. The way my evenings dissolve into whatever everyone's evenings dissolve into. None of these are the denial of the Quraysh — but they are made of the same material: **motion without deliberate answer to the Caller.**

The believer's life, this surah suggests by contrast, is the act of *individuating before the Day individuates you* — stepping out of the swarm now, while stepping out is still possible, and answering the call as a person, with a name, by choice. That's what the surah's refrain is begging for: *fa-hal min muddakir* — is there any one — any single one — who will remember?

**The practical edge:** pick one current of your life this week where you know you're moving with the swarm rather than by decision — one habit, one consumption pattern, one silence — and make a single deliberate, chosen move against it, and say bismillah over it. Not to be contrarian. To rehearse being *fardan* — a self that can stand alone before its Lord — before standing alone is no longer a rehearsal.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Camera Pulling Back

Now that we've done the work, step back and watch the ayah as a single shot — because it is filmed, and the camera moves in one continuous pull.

It opens in extreme close-up: **a pair of eyes** — *khushshaʿan abṣāruhum*. Then it pulls back to mid-shot: **bodies climbing out of the earth** — *yakhrujūna mina l-ajdāth*. Then all the way out to an aerial wide: **a swarm covering the horizons** — *ka-annahum jarādun muntashir*.

From the most intimate, individual thing a human being has — a gaze — to the most anonymous thing a human being can become — a speck in a swarm. And both ends of the shot say the same thing from opposite directions. The close-up says: nothing you hid inside survives; it is written on your eyes. The wide shot says: nothing you built outside survives; your name, rank, and face dissolve into the cloud. What remains, at every distance, is only one thing: **how you answered the Caller.**

That is also why the linguistic discoveries and the themes were never two separate journeys. The fronted ḥāl *is* the ledger-gaze theme: grammar putting the eyes first because reality puts the eyes first. The Form VIII *muntashir* *is* the swarm theme: a verb-form of self-propelled scattering describing lives of unchosen motion. And the root n-sh-r, holding "scattering" and "resurrection" in the same three letters, is the whole ayah in miniature: what they called random dispersal — dust, decay, a crowd, a feed — was always, secretly, spelled with the letters of the nushūr.

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you. Don't answer them now. Let them sit in the eyes and ask themselves at the right moments.

1. When I stand in salah tonight, can I honestly say my lowered gaze is *khushūʿ* — or is it just habit wearing khushūʿ's posture?
2. Whose eyes can I currently not meet — and what does that tell me is still unrepaired in my ledger?
3. If every Quranic mention of the *jadath* is an exit, what am I doing with the strange gift of still being on this side of mine?
4. Where in my life am I *muntashir* — moving fast in a direction I never actually chose?
5. The Caller is already calling — the surah's refrain asks *fa-hal min muddakir*. Today, concretely, what did my answer look like?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**On the Day the call can no longer be refused, the eyes that would not humble themselves will come out of the graves already humbled, and the people who spent their lives scattering away from the dhikr will scatter — countless, bewildered, unstoppable — straight toward the Caller.**

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah offers you a trade, and the terms are almost embarrassingly generous: lower your eyes now, in love, for moments at a time — or have them lowered then, in dread, forever. Answer the Caller now, as one person stepping deliberately out of the swarm — or answer Him then, as one locust among billions who no longer has a choice. The same khushūʿ, the same call, the same direction of travel. The only thing this ayah leaves in your hands is the *manner* of it. Take the voluntary version. It is the entire difference between the two kinds of lowered eyes.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who will raise us from the ajdāth with our state written in our eyes — make our eyes humble before You in this life, in prayer and in private, so they are never humbled against us in the next. Let us be of those who answer Your Caller today, walking by choice, so that on the Day the swarm streams toward Him, we come not bewildered but recognized — among the faces made radiant, looking at their Lord. Āmīn.
