# The Past Tense of the Future
## Surah Al-Qamar 54:1

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**ٱقْتَرَبَتِ ٱلسَّاعَةُ وَٱنشَقَّ ٱلْقَمَرُ**

*Iqtarabati-s-sāʿatu wa-nshaqqa-l-qamar*

**"The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split."**

Four words. Two verbs, two nouns. That is the entire ayah.

[PAUSE]

### Rebuild the World

Before we touch a single word, come stand in Makkah at night.

There is no electricity in this world. No streetlights, no glow on the horizon from a distant city. When the sun sets over Makkah, the world goes genuinely dark — and the sky takes over. The moon is not a decoration in this world. It is the calendar. It is the clock. Caravans time their journeys by it. Months begin and end by it. Debts come due by it. In a world without artificial light, the moon is the single most stable, most public, most *shared* object in human experience. Everyone sees the same moon. No one owns it. No one can touch it.

Which is exactly why the Quraysh chose it.

The narration is preserved in both Sahih collections. Anas ibn Malik reports — and al-Baghawi transmits this with his full chain back to al-Bukhari — that **the people of Makkah asked the Messenger of Allah ﷺ to show them a sign**. Not a recitation. Not an argument. They wanted something no poet could compose and no magician could stage. They pointed, in effect, at the one object in their world that was beyond all human reach.

And the moon split.

Two halves — al-Jalalayn names the geography: one part over Mount Abu Qubays, the other over Qayqaʿan. Anas describes the people of Makkah seeing the mountain of Hira *between* the two pieces. And the Prophet ﷺ said one word to the people watching: **"Ishhadū"** — *Bear witness.*

Now hold that moment. The most public object in their universe has just answered a request they themselves made. And into that stunned silence, this surah descends — and it opens not with the moon, but with something else entirely:

*The Hour has drawn near.*

One more thing about where this ayah sits. The surah before this one, An-Najm, ends with the words **azifati-l-āzifah** — "the Imminent has drawn near" (53:57) — and then a command to prostrate. Al-Qurtubi explicitly connects our ayah to that closing line. So the Quran's own sequence runs: *the Imminent has drawn near... fall down before Allah... **The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split.*** One surah ends on approaching nearness; the next opens by tightening it. There is a strand running through these Makkan surahs, and its name is *proximity*.

### The Hook

Here is the question I want you to carry for the next hour.

This ayah describes two events. One of them — the splitting of the moon — had just happened. The other — the Hour — has *still not happened*, fourteen centuries later.

And yet the Arabic puts **both verbs in exactly the same tense**. The grammar refuses to distinguish between the event behind them and the event ahead of them.

That refusal is not an accident. It may be the entire message of the ayah.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: ٱقْتَرَبَتِ — The Past Tense of What Hasn't Happened

Before we open this word, let me teach you something about how Arabic handles time — because it does something English almost never dares to do.

In Arabic rhetoric, when a future event is absolutely certain, the language is permitted to speak of it **in the perfect tense — the tense of completed events**. Classical scholars of balāghah treat this as a known device: the past tense used for the future to express *taḥqīq* — settledness, doneness. The event is so guaranteed that the grammar files it with history.

We have a faint echo of this in our own speech. Imagine an employee who has just insulted the CEO in front of the entire board. A colleague leans over and whispers: *"You're finished."* Not "you will be finished." *Finished.* The firing hasn't happened yet — but everyone in the room knows it is no longer a question of *if*. The future has already hardened into fact, and the language registers it.

Now look at the first word of our surah.

**ٱقْتَرَبَتِ — iqtarabat.** A perfect-tense verb. *It has drawn near.* Past tense. Completed. Filed.

And what is the subject? **ٱلسَّاعَةُ — the Hour.** The one event in all of existence that, by definition, has not yet occurred.

Let's sit with this. The Quran could have said *taqtaribu-s-sāʿah* — "the Hour draws near," ongoing, approaching. It could have said *sa-taqtaribu* — "it will draw near." Instead it reaches for the grammar of the already-done. Al-Jalalayn glosses it in two words: *qarubati-l-qiyāmah* — the Resurrection has come close. Al-Muyassar: *danati-l-qiyāmah* — it has drawn near. The commentators don't soften the tense. They let it stand.

But there is a second layer in this word, and it lives in its shape.

Notice something: the root here is **ق-ر-ب** — *qurb*, nearness. The simple verb would be *qaruba* — "it became near." But the Quran uses the Form VIII verb — *iqtaraba* — the same fuller form that opens Surah Al-Anbiya: *iqtaraba li-n-nāsi ḥisābuhum* — "Their reckoning has drawn near for mankind" (21:1). Al-Qurtubi reads our word as equivalent in force to *azifat* in *azifati-l-āzifah* — the verb of something pressing in, looming. The fuller form is the form the Quran consistently chooses when nearness is not a mild fact but an **encroachment** — a nearness that has entered your space and concerns you personally.

Here's what's remarkable: how can the Hour be "near" when more than fourteen hundred years have passed since this ayah was revealed?

Al-Qurtubi preserves the answer the early community gave. He transmits from Qatadah, from Anas: the Prophet ﷺ taught that **most of the dunya has already elapsed**. Nearness, in this reading, is not measured from the revelation forward — it is measured against everything behind. If the whole of created time is a road, the ayah is telling you the road is mostly walked. What remains, compared to what has passed, is the last stretch.

And stay with me, because this changes what "near" even means. We instinctively measure nearness in units of time: years, centuries. The ayah measures it differently — **by proportion and by certainty.** A thing is near when nothing remains between you and it except arrival. The Hour has no precondition left, no unfinished prerequisite. The final prophet has come. The final book is in your hands. Every door between humanity and the Hour has already been opened. The only thing left is the walking.

So this single word does two things at once. Its **tense** declares the Hour as settled as the past. Its **form** declares the Hour as close as a thing pressing against the door.

One word in, and the ayah has already collapsed the comfortable distance between you and the end of everything.

### Element Two: ٱنشَقَّ — The Verb With No One Named Behind It

Now the second half of the ayah. And to see what this verb is doing, I need to teach you one more piece of Arabic — a beautiful one.

Arabic verbs come in families of forms, and one of those forms — Form VII, the *infaʿala* pattern — has a very specific job. Grammarians call it **muṭāwaʿah**, which is hard to translate but easy to feel. It describes **what happens in the object when an action lands on it and the object yields.** You break the glass — *kasarta-hu*; the glass *inkasara* — it gave way, it came apart. The form describes the receiving end of an action: the moment something *complies* with what is done to it.

The literal sense of muṭāwaʿah is striking: it comes from *ṭāʿah* — **obedience**. The Form VII verb is, grammatically, the verb of a thing *obeying* the action that reached it.

Now look at the word.

**ٱنشَقَّ — inshaqqa.** Root **ش-ق-ق** — to split, to tear, to cleave. Form VII. The moon did not "split" the way an axe splits — the moon *came apart in compliance*. The action arrived, and the moon yielded.

And listen to the word itself. *Inshaqqa.* The hissing *shīn*, then the doubled, glottal force of the *qāf* with its shadda — *shaQQa* — the word carries a crack in its own body. You can almost hear the fracture in the phonetics.

But here is where I want you to slow down, because the deepest thing in this verb is not what it says. It's what it *withholds*.

Ask the obvious question: **who split the moon?**

Read the ayah again. *The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split.* No agent. No "Allah split the moon." No "We split the moon," though the Quran uses the majestic "We" constantly elsewhere. The verb form itself — the yielding form, the obeying form — describes only the moon's response. The Hand behind the response is **never named in the ayah.**

[PAUSE]

Why?

Let's think about what naming does. You name an agent when the agent is in question — when the audience might wonder *who*. But stand again in Makkah on that night. The Quraysh themselves asked for the sign. They watched the moon part over two named mountains. They saw Hira between the halves. There was exactly one Being in their entire theology — and they *did* affirm Allah as supreme creator — who could possibly have done this. To name Him would almost diminish the moment, the way explaining a thunderclap diminishes it.

The absence is the statement. The moon obeys, and the One it obeys is so unmistakable that grammar can leave His name silent. The ayah gives us a universe in which the most untouchable object in the sky behaves like a servant answering a summons — *inshaqqa*, it complied — and trusts us to know whose summons it was.

And notice the contrast with the first half of the ayah, because the two verbs are paired deliberately. The Hour gets a verb of **approach** — it draws near, like something walking toward you. The moon gets a verb of **rupture** — it gives way, like something coming apart at a command. Movement and obedience. The unseen future advancing; the visible sky yielding. Between those two verbs stands the listener — and the ayah asks, without ever asking: *the moon complied; will you?*

### The Bridge

So here is what the construction has shown us. A future event written in the grammar of the past. A past event written in the grammar of obedience, with the Agent left majestically unnamed. Four words that erase the line between what has happened and what must happen.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what evidence actually does to a human heart, and about how near "near" really is.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Sign That Wasn't Enough

I want to start somewhere very familiar.

Every one of us has said, or heard someone say, some version of this sentence: *"If God would just show me a sign — something undeniable — I would believe. I would change. I'd never doubt again."* It feels like the most reasonable sentence in the world. It frames doubt as an evidence problem: the heart is willing; the data is insufficient. Send better data, and faith follows automatically.

Modern psychology has spent decades quietly dismantling that sentence. Researchers who study belief revision keep finding the same uncomfortable pattern: when people are confronted with strong evidence against a position they are *invested* in, they do not usually update. They *upgrade their defenses*. The evidence gets reclassified — the source was biased, the experience was a trick, there must be some explanation. The goalposts don't just move; they were never bolted down in the first place. What looked like an evidence problem was an *investment* problem all along.

Now return to Makkah.

The Quraysh ran the experiment we all fantasize about. They named their price: show us a sign. Not a private vision — a public, cosmic, unfakeable event. And al-Muyassar records the sequence plainly: the kuffar of Makkah asked the Prophet ﷺ to show them a sign, **he made duʿā to Allah, and Allah showed them the splitting of the moon.** The request was granted. The control group became the test group. They saw, with their own eyes, the moon in two pieces.

And what did they say? You only have to read one ayah further: *"And if they see a sign, they turn away and say: continuing magic"* (54:2). **Siḥrun mustamirr.** Persistent sorcery. He's done it again.

[PAUSE]

Sit inside that for a moment, because it is one of the most psychologically precise moments in the entire Quran. They did not deny seeing it. They couldn't — they had asked for it, together, in public. Instead, they did what the invested heart always does: they found a *category* that let the evidence exist without costing them anything. "Magic" was that category. It conceded the event entirely and conceded nothing of themselves.

Here's what this forces us to confront: **a sign does not create faith. A sign reveals what was already in the heart.** Evidence is not a key that opens every lock; it is a mirror, and what it shows depends on who is looking. The same split moon made some hearts fall into sujūd and made others reach for the word "sorcery." The moon didn't differ. The investments did.

And this is where the ayah stops being about seventh-century Makkah and walks directly into your life and mine. Because we are still running the bargain: *if I had certainty, I would act.* If I truly felt death was real, I'd pray differently. If I could just be sure, I'd finally repent, finally repair that relationship, finally stop. We tell ourselves we are waiting for evidence.

But be honest — gently, but honest. Has there been no evidence? No janazah you stood in? No near-miss, no diagnosis in the family, no night where the Quran cracked something open in your chest and you felt, for one unguarded moment, that all of it was true? The signs have come. And like the Quraysh, we have categories ready: *I was emotional that night. Everyone feels that at funerals. It passed.* Sihrun mustamirr, in modern dress.

**The practical edge of this theme is one question:** the next time something pierces you — an ayah, a loss, a sudden awareness of your own mortality — refuse the category. Don't file it. Don't explain it. Treat it as what the moon was: a summons that deserves the answer *I bear witness*, not the answer *interesting trick*. Faith is not built by waiting for a bigger sign. It is built by being honest about the signs already given.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Near Is Not a Number

Let me tell you about a strange thing deadlines do to time.

You're given three months for a project. For the first ten weeks, the deadline is an abstraction — technically real, emotionally fictional. Time feels wide. Then something flips. Not at a fixed date — at a fixed *feeling*: the moment the deadline stops being information and becomes *presence*. Suddenly the same twenty-four-hour day is shorter. Your priorities reorganize themselves without being asked. Things that consumed you last month now seem absurd. Psychologists who study this note that the deadline didn't move — **your relationship to it did.** Nearness, for human beings, is not a measurement. It is an orientation.

People who receive serious diagnoses describe the same flip far more profoundly. Many say some version of: *I always knew I would die. The day they told me, I knew it differently.* The fact didn't change. The tense changed. Death moved from "will happen" to — listen to this — something closer to *has already drawn near*. The perfect tense. Their hearts discovered, under pressure, the exact grammar this ayah opens with.

**Iqtarabati-s-sāʿah.** The Hour *has drawn near.*

Now we can see why the Quran chose that tense, and why the early commentators refused to dilute it. The ayah is not making a failed prediction about calendar proximity — fourteen centuries have not embarrassed it, because it was never speaking the language of calendars. It is performing, in grammar, the flip that the diagnosis performs in a human life. It is trying to move the Hour, inside you, from the category of "information" to the category of "presence." From a fact you know to a nearness you *feel*.

And remember Qatadah's transmission from Anas, preserved by al-Qurtubi: most of the road is already behind humanity. But let me bring that down from the cosmic scale to the personal one — because for you, the ayah carries an even sharper edge. Whatever remains of cosmic history, *your* Hour — the moment your own deadline stops being abstract — is one heartbeat away at all times. The scholars say it simply: whoever dies, their Qiyamah has begun. On that scale, *iqtarabat* is not rhetoric. It is the most literal sentence you will ever read.

Here's the paradox I want to leave unresolved for a moment, because it deserves to sit: **we are the first generation in history to know, to the second, exactly what time it is — and we may be the worst in history at knowing what time it is.** Our clocks are atomic and our hearts are fictional. We can schedule a meeting three years out and cannot feel the nearness of the one appointment that is actually guaranteed.

[PAUSE]

The ayah's pairing offers the cure, and it is gentler than you'd expect. It does not say: *the Hour has drawn near, so be terrified.* It says: *the Hour has drawn near — and the moon has split.* The frightening claim is immediately leaned against something the listeners had *seen*. As if to say: you doubt the unseen nearness? Look at what just happened in the seen. The same Power that answered your eyes is the one telling you about your future. The Quran constantly builds this bridge — arguing for what cannot be witnessed through what can — and here the bridge is a broken moon hanging over two named mountains.

**The practical application is almost embarrassingly small, which is why it works.** Once a day — choose the moment; perhaps when you switch off the light — say the first half of this ayah to yourself in the perfect tense, the way the Quran says it. Not "the Hour will come someday." *The Hour has drawn near.* Let your heart practice the grammar. You are not trying to manufacture fear. You are trying to do, voluntarily and gently, what the diagnosis does involuntarily and brutally: reorganize today around what is actually true about tomorrow. People who live this way do not become morbid. They become *clear*. Watch what happens to a grudge, to a wasted evening, to a delayed apology, when it has to justify itself in the presence of *iqtarabat*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now step back and look at the whole ayah at once, because the two halves we studied separately were always one structure.

**ٱقْتَرَبَتِ ٱلسَّاعَةُ** — a *future* event, spoken in the past tense.
**وَٱنشَقَّ ٱلْقَمَرُ** — a *past* event, standing as proof of the future.

Do you see it? The ayah is a perfect chiasm of time. The future is written as past; the past is deployed for the future. The two verbs share one tense, one rhythm, one breath — *iqtarabat... inshaqqa* — because in the knowledge of Allah they share one reality: both are simply *done*. One has reached you. One is on its way. The grammar of certainty does not distinguish.

And where does that leave you, the listener? Exactly where the ayah's structure places you: **standing in the middle** — between a sign behind you that already happened and an Hour ahead of you that grammatically already happened. The Quraysh stood between a split moon and an approaching Hour and chose the word "sorcery." The ayah rebuilds that night, every time it is recited, and hands you their position — with their excuse removed.

### Bringing It Together

Everything we found in the language and everything we found in the themes turns out to be one insight seen from two directions.

The linguistic journey showed us tense collapsing the future into the past, and a Form VII verb showing the cosmos *obeying* an unnamed command. The thematic journey showed us that signs reveal hearts rather than convince them, and that nearness is an orientation, not a number. Put them together and the ayah's message stands whole: **certainty is not something you wait to receive; it is something you choose to inhabit.** The moon already yielded. The grammar already settled the question. The only unsettled thing in the entire ayah is the reader.

### Questions to Carry

1. What sign has already come into my life that I filed under a safe category — and what would change if I unfiled it?
2. If I am honest, what tense does my heart use for death — "will happen someday," or "has drawn near"? What does my calendar say my heart believes?
3. The moon yielded to its Lord in one instant. Which command have I been negotiating with for years?
4. What am I telling myself I would do "if I were certain" — and what is that bargain protecting?
5. When my Hour arrives and I am asked about the signs I was shown, what will I say I was waiting for?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**By writing the unarrived Hour in the tense of finished things and leaning it against a moon that visibly obeyed, this ayah strips away the last excuse of every heart that claims it is merely waiting for proof.**

### The Closing Invitation

This week, let one ayah re-tense your life. When you notice yourself deferring — the prayer you'll perfect later, the apology you'll make eventually, the return to Allah you've scheduled for some quieter year — say the first two words of this surah to yourself: *iqtarabati-s-sāʿah*. Not as a threat. As an orientation. The deadline has not moved in fourteen centuries; only hearts move. Let yours move toward it willingly, the way the moon moved when it was called.

### Du'a

O Allah, the moon split at Your command and the Hour approaches by Your decree — make our hearts among those that yield when You call, not those that name Your signs and walk away. Write certainty into us the way You wrote the Hour into Your Book: settled, finished, near. And when our own hour draws close, let it find us already facing You.

*Āmīn.*
