# The Label in Escrow

## Surah Al-Qamar 54:26

**سَيَعْلَمُونَ غَدًا مَّنِ ٱلْكَذَّابُ ٱلْأَشِرُ**

*Sa-yaʿlamūna ghadan man al-kadhdhābu al-ashir*

**"Tomorrow they will know who the liar is — the insolent one."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Verdict Already Delivered

Before we can hear this ayah, we have to stand where it was first heard.

Surah Al-Qamar is one of the most rhythmically relentless surahs in the Quran. It moves like a drumbeat through the ruins of nations — the people of Nuh, then ʿAd, then Thamud, then Lut, then Pharaoh — and after each ruin, the same two refrains toll like a bell: *How then were My punishment and My warnings?* And: *We have certainly made the Quran easy for remembrance — so is there anyone who will remember?*

The first listeners were the Quraysh of Makkah. They were doing, in real time, exactly what each of these ancient nations had done: looking at a man they had known for forty years — a man they themselves had named *al-Amin*, the trustworthy — and deciding, now that he carried revelation, that he must be a liar.

So the surah brings them to Thamud. And Thamud's objection, in the ayah just before ours, is worth hearing in full, because our ayah is an answer to it. Thamud said of Salih:

*"Has the reminder been cast upon him — out of all of us? No — rather, he is a liar, an insolent one."* (54:25)

Listen to the psychology in that sentence. They don't engage the message. They engage the *selection*. Why him? Why not one of us — one of the powerful, the established? And from wounded pride, they reach their verdict: **kadhdhābun ashir** — a habitual liar, swollen with self-conceit.

They have passed judgment. The case, as far as Thamud is concerned, is closed.

And then Allah speaks one line. Six words in Arabic. He does not argue. He does not defend Salih. He does not present counter-evidence.

He simply takes their exact verdict — their precise words — and attaches a date to it.

Here is what you need to listen for, and it is stunning once you see it: **the accusation in ayah 25 and the accusation in ayah 26 are the same two words — but they are not grammatically identical.** One tiny change has been made. And that change is the entire judgment of God compressed into a definite article.

Let's go find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Returned Accusation — *kadhdhābun ashir* becomes *al-kadhdhābu al-ashir*

Before we open the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic handles the difference between an *accusation* and an *identity*.

In Arabic, as in English, there is a world of difference between calling someone "a thief" and calling someone "**the** thief." If a shopkeeper says "there's a thief in this market," he's making a loose charge — somebody, somewhere, fits the description. But if a detective gathers everyone in a room and says "one of you is **the** thief," something has changed. The definite article *al-* turns a floating accusation into a fixed role — a title that exists, that is real, and that *someone in this room must wear*. The only remaining question is who.

Hold that. Now look at the two ayahs side by side.

Thamud's verdict in 54:25: *bal huwa **kadhdhābun ashir*** — "rather, he is a liar, an insolent one." Indefinite. No *al-*. A charge flung at Salih the way mud is flung — carelessly, to see if it sticks.

Allah's response in 54:26: *sa-yaʿlamūna ghadan man **al-kadhdhābu al-ashir*** — "tomorrow they will know who **the** liar, **the** insolent one is."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened?

Allah did not deny that a *kadhdhāb ashir* exists in this story. He **accepted their category**. He took the label they manufactured, stamped it with the definite article — made it official, made it singular, made it a title that *will* be worn — and then placed it in escrow. The word *man* — "who" — leaves the title hanging in the air between two parties: Salih, and Thamud. One of them is the liar drunk on self-conceit. Tomorrow names him.

This is a pattern of divine rhetoric that, once you see it here, you will start seeing across the Quran: **Allah lets the accuser write the indictment, and then lets reality decide who stands in the dock.** Thamud chose the words of their own condemnation. Nobody imposed the charge sheet on them. They drafted it themselves, in their own dialect, with their own intensifiers — and Allah simply countersigned it and set the court date.

Stay with me on the words themselves, because both were chosen by Thamud with venom, and both return with terrible precision.

***Kadhdhāb*** is not "liar." Arabic has *kādhib* for that — someone who told a lie. *Kadhdhāb* is the intensive pattern, *faʿʿāl* — the pattern Arabic uses for professions and compulsions: *najjār*, a carpenter, someone whose life is carpentry. A *kadhdhāb* is someone whose life is lying — a structural, habitual, wall-to-wall fabricator. Thamud didn't accuse Salih of an error. They accused him of being constitutionally false.

And ***al-ashir*** — from the root *ʾ-sh-r*, which the lexicons tie to the recklessness born of excess: the insolence of someone so swollen with comfort and self-regard that he no longer feels the weight of anything. It is arrogance with a specific cause — *too much ease*.

Now notice something. [PAUSE]

Who, in this story, were the people of too much ease? Who carved palaces out of mountains, secure and wealthy, as the Quran tells us elsewhere? Who looked at a prophet and could not believe that heaven would bypass *their* eminence, *their* establishment? **Thamud described themselves and mailed the description to the wrong address.** The word *ashir* fit exactly one party in this story — and it was not the shepherd-prophet calling them to water rations and humility.

When Allah returns the phrase with the definite article, He is not inventing an irony. He is *revealing* one that was already there. The label was always a self-portrait. Tomorrow just turns it around to face the painter.

**What this single grammatical shift reveals: Allah does not always answer slander with denial. Sometimes He answers it with a definite article and a deadline.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Ghadan* — Why "Tomorrow"?

Here is a question that bothered the classical commentators, and their answer is one of the most quietly beautiful things in the tafsir tradition.

The punishment of Thamud did not arrive the next morning. The she-camel came first, then the test, then the three days' respite, then the scream. And the fullest "knowing" — the kind no one can argue with — waits for the Day of Resurrection. So why does Allah say ***ghadan*** — *tomorrow*?

Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both record the answer, and it comes from how Arabs actually spoke. *Ghadan* was used **ʿalā al-taqrīb** — for nearness — because of the proverb-like habit of the Arabs regarding consequences: *inna maʿa al-yawmi ghadan* — "with every today, there is a tomorrow." Al-Qurtubi even quotes a line of pre-Islamic poetry to prove the idiom: *whoever was not dead today — dies tomorrow.* In that way of speaking, "tomorrow" doesn't mean twenty-four hours. It means: **closer than you think. Already on the calendar. Adjacent.**

Now let the commentators show you *which* tomorrow, because they differ — and the difference is not a contradiction. It is a composite sketch, each scholar holding a camera at a different angle on the same approaching reality.

Al-Jalalayn points the camera far: *tomorrow* means **the Hereafter** — they will know at the ultimate unveiling, when they are punished for denying their prophet. Al-Baghawi points it near: *tomorrow* is **when the punishment descends on them in this world** — the scream that left them crumpled in their homes — though he also records al-Kalbi's view that it means the Day of Resurrection. Al-Muyassar simply refuses to choose: they will see who the insolent liar is **when the punishment strikes them in this world, and on the Day of Resurrection.**

Lay the angles over each other and you get the full picture: *ghadan* is not a timestamp. It is a **direction**. Every tomorrow in their future — the morning the camel was killed, the morning the scream came, the morning of the graves opening — all of them lean toward the same answer to the same question. Truth has only one trajectory; "tomorrow" is its name for *soon, and certain, and yours.*

And here, notice what this does to Thamud's timeline versus Allah's timeline. Thamud's verdict was instant — *bal huwa kadhdhābun ashir*, delivered the moment their pride was stung, no waiting period, no deliberation. Allah's verdict takes the one thing their verdict refused to take: **time.** Theirs was fast and false. His is slow and final. The whole moral universe of this ayah lives in that contrast of speeds.

**What this single word reveals: divine justice is rarely instant, but it is always adjacent — and "tomorrow" is how Allah says "this is already in motion."**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Sa-yaʿlamūna* — The Punishment Is Knowing

One more element, and it is the first word of the ayah — which means it is the headline.

Ask yourself what word you would expect here. Thamud has slandered a prophet of God. The surah is a litany of destructions. You would expect: *they will be seized*. *They will be destroyed*. *They will taste*. The surah uses all of those verbs elsewhere.

Instead, the sentence opens with the root *ʿ-l-m*: **sa-yaʿlamūna** — *they will know.*

Sit with this. The threatened consequence — the thing held over their heads — is not, in this sentence, pain. It is **knowledge**. And there is a precise reason why, and it lies in what their crime actually was. Thamud's crime in ayah 25 was not violence; that comes later, with the camel. Their crime in ayah 25 was a *false claim to knowledge*. "He **is** a liar" — stated as fact, as something they knew. They didn't say "we suspect," "we doubt," "we are not convinced." They issued a verdict with the confidence of a court that has seen all the evidence.

So Allah's response is measure-for-measure with surgical precision: you claimed to know — **so knowing is what you will be given.** The punishment fits the crime so exactly that the punishment and the crime share a verb. They counterfeited certainty; they will be paid in the real thing. And real certainty, arriving too late, for the man who built his life on the counterfeit — that *is* the punishment. The scream killed Thamud's bodies. *Sa-yaʿlamūna* describes what happened to them a moment before: the instant the sky changed and every one of them understood, with total clarity, who the *kadhdhāb ashir* had been all along.

Notice also what the verb is **not** given: a comfortable object. Arabic lets "know" take a simple object — *they will know the truth*, *they will know their error*. Allah doesn't supply one. What follows the verb is an open question — *man al-kadhdhābu al-ashir*, "who is the liar, the insolent one?" The sentence itself withholds the answer. Allah does not say "they will know that Salih was truthful," even though that is transparently the import. The grammar leaves the question standing — because **the answer is not the sentence's job. The answer is tomorrow's job.** The ayah is constructed to end the way an unresolved chord ends, leaning forward into the silence where the resolution will come. Even the listener is made to wait — made to feel, for one suspended moment, the open-endedness that Thamud should have felt and didn't.

One more layer, and it comes from the reciters. There are two canonical readings of this word, and the classical commentators — al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both record this — preserve them side by side. Ibn ʿĀmir and Ḥamzah read it ***sa-taʿlamūna*** — "**you** will know" — making it Salih's own voice, turned to face his people, the warning delivered eye to eye. The other reciters read ***sa-yaʿlamūna*** — "**they** will know" — making it Allah's voice speaking *to Salih about them*, third person, over their heads.

Don't treat these as rival options. Hear them as two channels of the same broadcast. In one reading, the threatened are *addressed* — warned to their faces, given every chance. In the other, they are *discussed* — already spoken about rather than spoken to, the way one speaks about a case that is closed. And there is consolation hidden in that second reading: it means this sentence is also something Allah said **to His prophet**. To the slandered man himself: *they will know, tomorrow, who the liar is — you don't have to prove it; I have taken the case.* Every believer who has ever been lied about has needed exactly that sentence.

**What this verb reveals: the deepest punishment for false certainty is true certainty — arriving on a morning when it can no longer save you.**

### The Bridge

So here is what the construction has shown us. An accusation, flung indefinite, returned definite. A timeline — *tomorrow* — that turns consequence from an abstraction into an adjacency. And a verb that makes *knowing itself* the reckoning, while the grammar deliberately refuses to hand us the answer early.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what accusations actually are, and about what time actually does.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Accusation as Autobiography

Let me start somewhere that has nothing to do with Thamud.

Modern psychology has a name for one of the oldest moves of the human soul: **projection**. It is the mechanism by which we take what is intolerable in ourselves and relocate it in someone else. The chronically dishonest person is the first to call others fake. The controlling person diagnoses everyone around them as controlling. It works because it is efficient: accusing you of my flaw lets me *condemn* the flaw — satisfying my conscience — without ever *owning* it. The courtroom convenes, the verdict is guilty, and I walk out free, because I made sure someone else was sitting in my chair.

You have watched this happen. You have likely had it done to you — been accused, with strange heat, of precisely the thing your accuser was doing. And if we are honest in this room, we have done it. The flaws that most enrage us in others deserve a long, careful look, because rage that specific usually has a return address.

Now go back to the cliffs of Thamud and watch ayah 25 again with this lens.

A people made insolent by ease — *ashir* in the most lexically exact sense, reckless with comfort, carving their security into mountain rock — look at a man calling them to restraint and accountability. And out of all the words in the Arabic language, the word their souls reach for is... *ashir*. They reach for *kadhdhāb* — habitual falsifier — to describe the one man in the valley whose words would prove true. The accusation is not just wrong; it is wrong with the specific, mirror-image wrongness of projection. **They read their own diagnosis aloud and attached the wrong name to it.**

And now feel the restraint — the almost frightening economy — of the divine response. Allah does not refute the diagnosis. The diagnosis was *correct*. There was, in fact, an insolent liar in that valley. Allah's entire intervention is to add the definite article and ask the question they should have asked themselves: ***who?***

[PAUSE]

This is the part that should reach into our chests. Because the ayah, read this way, is not only a threat to ancient deniers. It is a standing principle: **the labels we manufacture do not evaporate. They wait.** Every verdict we pass goes into escrow with our name on the envelope as a possible recipient. "Tomorrow they will know who" — the *who* is open at the moment of accusation, and it is closed at the moment of truth, and the accuser does not control which way it closes. Thamud assumed that saying "he is the liar" settled the matter. They learned that saying it had only opened a file.

There is a practical edge to this, and it is sharp. Before the next verdict leaves your mouth — about a person, a family member, a scholar, a community — let this ayah install a half-second delay, and in that half-second, one question: *if Allah returned this exact sentence with a definite article and asked "who?", am I certain the answer isn't me?* That question, asked honestly even a few times, will quietly retire most of our accusations. Not because the people around us are innocent — but because we will discover how often the heat of our charge was borrowed from our own file.

**The core insight: an accusation is never only a description of the accused. It is always also a deposition by the accuser — and Allah keeps both on record until tomorrow.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Speed of False Certainty and the Patience of Truth

Begin, again, in our own world.

We live in the first era of human history in which a verdict can be globally distributed in less time than it takes to read the evidence. The pull-to-refresh, the quote-post, the instant take — our entire information culture is built to reward whoever *knows first*, not whoever knows *truly*. And we have all felt the specific pleasure of instant certainty: the clean, settled feeling of having decided. The mind hates open files. A verdict — any verdict — feels like relief.

Thamud's sentence in ayah 25 has exactly that texture. *Bal huwa kadhdhābun ashir.* "No — rather, he's just an insolent liar." Feel the *bal* — "rather" — doing its work: it sweeps the entire question off the table. Has revelation really come to him? — *bal* — don't even sit with the question; here's the verdict; file closed. It took them one breath. They got the relief of certainty at the price of its truth.

And against that one breath, Allah sets one word: ***ghadan***. Tomorrow.

Here is the paradox I want to let sit without rushing to resolve it: **the side with all the power of proof is the side that chooses to wait.** Allah could have vindicated Salih in that instant — could have made the sky answer the slander before its echo faded. Instead, the response to instant false certainty is *scheduled* true certainty. The she-camel comes in the very next ayah — and with her, a trial, a watching period: *"so watch them and be patient"* (54:27). Patience is commanded *to the prophet* — the innocent party is the one told to wait. Why?

Let's sit with this.

Because time, in the moral structure of this ayah, is not a delay of justice. Time is the *instrument* of justice. Instant vindication would have proven Salih right, but it would have proven nothing about Thamud — they could forever say they were never really tested, never given room to choose. The interval between the accusation and *ghadan* is the space in which Thamud gets to demonstrate, freely and completely, who the *kadhdhāb ashir* actually is. They fill that space with the hamstrung camel. The waiting period wasn't mercy delayed; it was the evidence being allowed to complete itself. **Truth can afford to wait precisely because waiting only ever works in its favor.** Falsehood needs the verdict *now*, because falsehood's position only degrades with time.

And this is where the ayah quietly turns and looks at us — at the slandered, the misjudged, the ones holding an accusation they cannot disprove tonight.

There is a person listening to this who has been called something they are not — in a family, a workplace, a community, a marriage — and who lies awake drafting the defense, the thread, the message that will finally prove the truth *today*. To that person, this ayah hands the strangest gift: **a release from the burden of immediate vindication.** *Sa-yaʿlamūna ghadan.* They will know. Tomorrow. The sentence is in Allah's voice in the dominant reading — spoken over the heads of the accusers, as reassurance to the accused. You are not required to win the argument on the accuser's schedule. You are required to be, patiently, what the truth will reveal you to be — and to let *ghadan* do the work that *ghadan* was created to do.

The practical application is concrete: identify the one place in your life where you are straining hardest to force a verdict *today* — to make someone see, admit, concede. Tonight, formally hand that file to its actual Owner. Say it in your du'a in so many words: *this is Yours; I will do what is right and wait for Your tomorrow.* Then watch what that does to your sleep, your speech, and your dignity in that conflict. The believer who has truly absorbed *sa-yaʿlamūna ghadan* argues less and endures better — not from weakness, but because they have seen the case schedule.

**The core insight: false certainty is fast because it has to be; true certainty can wait because every tomorrow belongs to it.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now step back and look at what these two ayahs form when you set them side by side — because the structure is itself the message.

Ayah 25 ends: ***kadhdhābun ashir*** — indefinite.
Ayah 26 ends: ***al-kadhdhābu al-ashir*** — definite.

A mirror — with one alteration. Thamud's sentence and Allah's sentence close on the same two words, so that in recitation the accusation is heard twice in two breaths: once leaving the mouths of the accusers, once suspended in the air with a *man* — "who?" — in front of it and a *ghadan* — "tomorrow" — behind it. The structure performs the meaning: **what you say comes back.** Not paraphrased. Not approximately. Verbatim, upgraded to a title, and dated.

And between the saying and the returning sits the whole machinery we uncovered: a verb that makes knowledge the reckoning, a grammar that withholds the answer, a tomorrow that is a direction rather than a date.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two directions. The definite article *is* the doctrine of projection — the label fixed and waiting for its true owner. The word *ghadan* *is* the doctrine of patient truth — consequence as adjacency, not abstraction. The verb *yaʿlamūn* *is* the measure-for-measure justice — counterfeit certainty repaid in the real thing. Grammar and reality are saying the same sentence, because the One who governs reality is the One who chose the grammar.

### Questions to Carry

- Which accusation have I made most often, with the most heat — and what would I find if Allah put a definite article on it and asked me *who*?
- Where in my life am I demanding vindication *today* that Allah may have scheduled for *tomorrow*?
- When I dismissed someone with *bal* — "no, rather, he's just..." — what question was I refusing to sit with?
- Has my ease made me *ashir* in ways I would only recognize if comfort were withdrawn?
- If certainty about the truth of this Quran arrived for me right now, with full force — what would I change before tonight?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Every accusation flung at truth becomes a title held in escrow, and on a tomorrow already adjacent, Allah returns it — definite, dated, and addressed to its rightful owner.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, you are invited to do one small, severe thing: retrieve a single verdict you have issued — choose the one you said with the most confidence — and reopen the file. Not to flagellate yourself. Simply to ask the question Thamud never asked: *who?* — and to let the answer take longer than one breath. That half-second of reopened inquiry, practiced as worship, is this ayah living in you.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who return every word to its source and every label to its owner — guard our tongues from verdicts that describe ourselves, and guard our hearts from the ease that breeds insolence. Make us patient with Your *tomorrow*, content to be vindicated on Your schedule and not our own. And when true knowing comes to us, O Allah, let it come as light in this life that saves us — not as clarity on a morning too late to act. Amin.
