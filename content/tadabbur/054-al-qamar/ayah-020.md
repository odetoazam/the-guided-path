# Uprooted from the Depths: When the Tallest Trees Fall

## Surah Al-Qamar, Ayah 20

**تَنزِعُ ٱلنَّاسَ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٖ مُّنقَعِرٖ**

*Tanziʿu n-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿir*

"It wrenched the people away, as if they were trunks of palm trees torn out from the root."

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Surah of Warnings, A People of Towers

Before we can hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, we have to stand where they stood.

Surah Al-Qamar opens with the moon split in the sky — a sign so enormous that the people who saw it called it passing magic and walked away. And then the surah does something relentless. It takes the Quraysh of Mecca — confident, established, certain that history bends around them — and it walks them through a graveyard of civilizations. The people of Nuh. ʿĀd. Thamud. The people of Lut. Pharaoh. One after another. And after each grave, the same two refrains toll like a bell: *how were My punishment and My warnings?* — and then, astonishingly, mercy: *We have made the Quran easy for remembrance, so is there anyone who will remember?*

Punishment and invitation. Grave and open door. Over and over.

Our ayah sits inside the second of these graves — the story of ʿĀd. And here is what the first listeners knew about ʿĀd that we tend to forget: ʿĀd were not a footnote to the ancient Arabs. They were *the* legend. The people of towering construction, of unprecedented physical strength, the people who looked at their own bodies and their own buildings and asked, as another surah records it, **"Who is stronger than us in might?"** (41:15). When an Arab wanted to describe something ancient and mighty, he reached for ʿĀd.

The previous verse has already told us what was sent against them: a *rīḥan ṣarṣaran* — a wind that screams, on a *day of relentless misfortune*. Now comes verse 20, and it is the only visual we are given. One frame. One image, chosen by Allah to carry the entire weight of how the strongest people in human memory ended.

And the image is this: human beings scattered across the earth like the torn-out trunks of palm trees.

Here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows. The Quran could have shown us anything — the wind itself, the collapsing buildings, the darkness. Instead it shows us *palm trees*. Specifically: not standing palms, not broken palms, but palms **wrenched out from the root**, and — stranger still — not even whole palms, but *aʿjāz* — trunks, stumps, the lower portions only.

Why stumps? Why roots? Why this image, of all images, for these people, of all people?

Stay with me. The answer is buried — quite literally — in the grammar.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تَنزِعُ — The Verb That Refuses to Become Past Tense

Let me teach you something about how Arabic tells stories, because without it, the first word of this ayah will slip past you.

Every language lets you choose your distance from an event. In English, "the storm destroyed the town" and "the storm *is tearing* the town apart" describe the same event — but they put you in completely different places. The first is a report. You're reading it in tomorrow's newspaper, safe, with coffee. The second is a live feed. You're *there*.

Arabic does this with tense. Past-tense verbs (*faʿala*) report; they seal an event in history. But classical Arabic has a documented rhetorical move — the grammarians discuss it as evoking the event as though presently witnessed — where a narrator describing the *past* suddenly shifts into the *imperfect* tense, the tense of ongoing action. The event is over. The verb refuses to admit it. The scene is dragged out of history and made to happen in front of you, *now*.

Now look at the sequence in these two verses.

Verse 19: **أَرْسَلْنَا** — *We sent* the wind. Past tense. Sealed. A report.

Verse 20: **تَنزِعُ** — *it wrenches out* the people. Imperfect. Present. Live.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? Allah opens the account from the divine vantage point — *We sent* — the decree, completed, filed in the record of history. And then the camera drops out of eternity and into the storm, and the tense drops with it. You are no longer being told about a wind that *once* tore people from the earth. You are standing in the wind *while it tears them out*. The verb is unfinished because, for the listener, the scene must be unfinished. It is happening as you recite it. It happens every time anyone recites it.

And the verb itself — *tanziʿu*, from the root **ن-ز-ع** — is not a gentle word. This root means to wrench, to strip, to pull something out of the place it is embedded in, against resistance. The Quran uses this same root for Shaytan *stripping* the garment from Adam and Hawwa (7:27), and for the angels who *wrench out* souls at death (79:1). *Nazʿ* always implies that the thing being removed was *attached* — rooted, gripping, holding on — and that the removal overpowers the grip.

So before the palm tree simile even arrives, the verb has already told you the people were holding on to something.

Now — holding on to *what*? Here the ayah goes quiet. It says the wind wrenched out *the people* — but wrenched them out *of what*? Arabic could easily have specified: from their homes, from the ground, from their shelters. **The object of extraction is left unsaid.** And when the Quran leaves a gap like this, the classical scholars do something beautiful with it. Listen to them — not as competing answers, but as four cameras filming the same storm.

Al-Qurtubi records one view: the wind tore them **from beneath their own feet** — the very ground betrayed them, the way a palm is torn from its soil. He records Mujahid's view: it lifted them from the earth and **threw them down on their heads**, so that — as al-Baghawi and al-Muyassar also describe — their necks were crushed and their heads separated from their bodies. Another view in al-Qurtubi: it tore them **from their houses** — the shelters they trusted. And a fourth, the most haunting: the people of ʿĀd, seeing the storm coming, **dug pits in the earth and climbed down into them** — and the wind reached into the pits and pulled them out, one by one, leaving the empty holes behind. Al-Qurtubi additionally records a narration, transmitted through Muhammad ibn Kaʿb, that the wind tore people even from their graves — a report we note as he transmits it, without elevating its status.

Hold those four frames together. From under their feet. From the air, headfirst. From their houses. From holes they dug with their own hands.

**The ayah leaves the object unspecified because no specification would survive.** There was nothing to be wrenched *from* — no ground, no home, no pit — that could hold them. The grammar's silence is the theology: when this extraction comes, the question "wrenched from where?" has no answer, because *where* has stopped existing as a category of safety.

### Element Two: أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٖ — Why Stumps, and Not Trees

Now the simile. And I need you to notice that the simile is *wrong* — deliberately, devastatingly wrong — in a way that takes a moment to see.

If you wanted to compare the people of ʿĀd to palm trees, the natural comparison writes itself. ʿĀd were famous for two things: height and strength. The date palm is the tallest, strongest thing in the Arabian landscape. Tall people, tall trees. Al-Qurtubi notes exactly this — they were likened to palms *li-ṭūlihim*, for their height. The simile should be: *as if they were palm trees, fallen.*

But that is not what the ayah says. It says **أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٖ** — the *aʿjāz* of palms. *Aʿjāz* is the plural of *ʿajuz* — al-Baghawi pauses to give you the morphology, *ʿajuz* pluralizing like *ʿaḍud/aʿḍād* — and it means the **rear, lower, bottom portion** of a thing. The base. The trunk after the crown is gone. Ibn Abbas, as al-Baghawi records, read it as *uṣūluhā* — their root-ends.

So the image is not fallen trees. The image is **headless trunks**.

Why? Al-Baghawi gives the unbearable answer, and al-Muyassar and al-Jalalayn confirm it: because the wind, hurling them down headfirst, **separated their heads from their bodies — and what remained on the ground were bodies without heads**, the way a palm trunk lies on the ground when its crown has been cut away. The simile is not poetic decoration. It is forensic. It describes precisely what a witness walking through the aftermath would have seen, and it finds the one object in the Arab visual world that looks exactly like that.

Let's sit with this.

[PAUSE]

A palm tree's identity is its crown. Everything the palm *is* — its fruit, its shade, its silhouette against the sky, the reason you planted it — lives at the top. The trunk exists to hold the crown up. A trunk without its crown is instantly, visibly *pointless*: all that height, holding up nothing.

And a human being's *ʿajuz* — the body without the head — is the same statement made in flesh. The head is where the face was. Where the eyes were that looked at their buildings with pride. Where the mouth was that asked *who is stronger than us?* The wind did not merely kill ʿĀd. It edited them — removed exactly the part of them that had done the boasting — and left the strength lying on the ground, intact and useless, like trunks that no longer hold anything up.

**The ayah shows you power with the personhood removed.** That is what *aʿjāz* means here, and no other word would say it.

One more layer, and it comes from a gap between two surahs. This same simile appears one other time in the Quran — in Surah al-Ḥāqqah (69:7), describing the same people: *ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin* **khāwiyah** — palm trunks, *hollow*. Here in Al-Qamar, the palm is *munqaʿir* — uprooted. There, *khāwiyah* — empty inside. Al-Jalalayn notes the grammatical surface of the variation — *nakhl* is a collective that Arabic allows to be treated as masculine or feminine, and each surah's choice harmonizes with its own verse-endings — the masculine *munqaʿir* rhyming with Al-Qamar's *-ir/-ur* cadence, the feminine *khāwiyah* with al-Ḥāqqah's.

But notice what the two readings give us when laid side by side, like two exposures of one photograph: in one surah ʿĀd are trunks **torn out from below**; in the other, trunks **hollowed out from within**. Uprooted *and* empty. The destruction Allah describes is total in both directions — nothing beneath them, nothing inside them. What this invites us to consider is that these were always the only two places strength could have lived: in your roots, or in your core. ʿĀd had neither. They had only height.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: مُّنقَعِرٖ — Torn Out From the Very Bottom

The last word of the ayah, and the deepest. Literally.

First, the concept — because Arabic morphology is going to do something here that English simply cannot. Arabic verbs come in patterns, *forms*, and each form shapes the meaning. Form VII — the pattern *infaʿala* — is the pattern of **undergoing**. It describes a thing not acting, but being acted upon so completely that the action becomes its state. *Kasara* — he broke it; Form VII *inkasara* — it *got broken*, it gave way. The thing in a Form VII verb has no agency. It receives.

The Leeds morphological corpus confirms what the word's shape shows: **مُنقَعِر** is a Form VII participle, from the root **ق-ع-ر**.

Now the root. *Qaʿr* in Arabic is **the bottom** — the floor of a well, the bed of the sea, the deepest reachable point of anything. When the classical lexicons describe *inqaʿara* for a tree, they mean it was torn out *min qaʿrihā* — from its bottom-most point, root system and all. Al-Baghawi glosses it: cut away from its place, fallen flat on the earth. Al-Muyassar: like a palm *ripped out from its aṣl* — its base.

Put the form and the root together and feel the full weight of this single word:

Not *fallen* — a fallen tree might be replanted.
Not *broken* — a broken tree still has living roots; it can shoot again.
**Munqaʿir**: passively, helplessly extracted from the deepest point of its anchoring, so that nothing of it remains in the earth at all.

And here is what makes the word choice surgical rather than dramatic. Of every tree the Arabs knew, the date palm is the *hardest* to uproot. Farmers of that world knew this in their hands: you can cut a palm, burn a palm, but pulling one out of the ground, roots and all, is almost beyond human strength — the root mass grips the earth like a buried fist. **The palm is the Arab's living definition of rootedness.** So when the Quran needs an image for what this wind did, it selects the one tree whose uprooting is nearly impossible — and uproots it with a single word.

Say the word aloud: *mun-qa-ʿir*. The qāf and the ʿayn — two of the deepest, most guttural sounds the human throat can produce — scrape against each other in the middle of the word. The word for being torn out from the depths is itself dredged up from the depths of the throat. The sound performs the meaning.

[PAUSE]

So conclude this element with the full sentence the morphology has built: the people who asked *who is stronger than us* are answered with a Form VII — the grammar of pure passivity. They do not act in this ayah. They do not resist, flee, or even fall. They *undergo*. The strongest people on earth appear in their own death scene without a single active verb to their name.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Step back and see what the language has assembled. A verb in the living present, so the scene never stops happening. An extraction with no named source, because nothing could hold them. A simile of headless trunks, because their strength outlived their selves by a few seconds. And a final word that reaches to the bottom of the earth — *qaʿr* — to tell us that even their deepest roots came up.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because this ayah was never about ʿĀd's weather. It is about where *you* think your roots are.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Pits We Dig — On the Architecture of False Security

Begin somewhere familiar. Not in ancient Arabia — in your own life, this week.

Every one of us is, right now, digging pits. We don't call them that. We call them *plans*. The emergency fund with six months of expenses. The second income stream. The insurance policies, the backup of the backup, the career hedge, the property, the network of people who could help us if things went wrong. None of these are sins — many are wisdom. But watch what happens *inside* you as each one is completed: a small voice exhales and says, *now I'm safe*. Brick by brick, hedge by hedge, we are building a feeling. The feeling that we have personally engineered our own un-uprootability.

Psychologists have a name for the engine underneath this: the illusion of control — the deeply human tendency to believe that our preparations regulate outcomes that are, in fact, not in our hands. The feeling of safety scales with the *effort* we put into preparing, not with actual safety. Dig a deeper pit, feel more secure — whether or not the pit can hold.

Now return to that detail preserved in al-Qurtubi, the one I asked you to hold: the people of ʿĀd **saw the storm coming**. They had time. And what did they do with their final hours? The most technologically capable civilization of their age took their famous strength and their famous engineering and they *dug holes in the ground and climbed inside*.

It is the most human moment in the entire story. Faced with what was coming, they did what we do: they made a plan. They executed it competently. They went down into their pits with their hearts probably slowing a little — *now I'm safe* —

— and the ayah says **تَنزِعُ**. The wind reached into the pits and *wrenched them out*. And al-Qurtubi adds the detail that should keep us awake: the wind took the people **and left the pits intact**. The empty holes remained, dotting the landscape — a field of perfectly engineered, perfectly useless shelters.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the ayah is *not* saying. It is not saying don't prepare; the Prophet ﷺ taught tying the camel. The ayah is aimed at something more precise: the moment your preparation quietly changes jobs — from *means* you take while relying on Allah, into the *thing you actually rely on*. The pit is fine. The pit as your *rabb* is the catastrophe. ʿĀd's pits failed not because they were badly dug but because they were asked to do something no created thing can do: hold a person whom Allah has decreed to be taken.

And notice — this is the same lesson the verb taught us linguistically. *Nazʿ* implies a grip being overpowered. The unnamed object of extraction implies that *nothing* — ground, house, pit — could be that grip. The grammar and the story converge on a single sentence: **anything you hold instead of Allah is a pit that will be left empty.**

What does this change tomorrow? Try this — it is small and it is honest. Tonight, name your pits. Actually list them: the two or three things that, if you imagine losing them, make your chest tighten. That tightening is diagnostic; it shows you where your sense of rootedness actually lives. Then take each one and consciously re-assign its job in a sentence: *"This is a means I am grateful for. It is not what holds me."* You will feel the difference between saying it and meaning it. That gap is your work.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Height Without Roots — What the Headless Trunk Knows About Us

Start again in our world, because this theme lives closer to us than ʿĀd's desert.

We live in the great age of height. Everything in modern life is a metric of vertical extension: followers, valuation, title, square footage, the curated profile. We have built an entire civilization around the crown of the palm — the visible top, the part that photographs well against the sky. And our growth strategies are crown-first: become visible, become impressive, become tall, and assume the roots will somehow keep pace.

ʿĀd were the first civilization of height. *Iram of the lofty pillars, the like of which was never created in the lands* (89:7–8). Bodies taller, buildings taller, and — this is the spiritual mechanism — they converted their height into their theology: *who is stronger than us in might?* Height became their evidence that they were un-uprootable. The taller the palm grew, the less it thought about its roots.

Now hold the ayah's image up against them like a mirror, because every element of it is aimed.

They were proud of their *height* — so the simile grants them height: the palm, tallest thing in the land. It concedes their entire boast. *Yes. You were tall. Tall as palms.*

They located their identity in strength — so the simile shows their strength perfectly preserved: the trunk, the strongest part of the palm, lying on the ground *intact*. The wind didn't even bother to break them. Their strength was never the obstacle.

And then the two edits. The crown — gone: *aʿjāz*, trunks only, the part that held the face and the boast removed. And the root — gone: *munqaʿir*, extracted from the *qaʿr*, the very bottom. What remains is the exact thing they worshipped, isolated: **pure mass. Pure height. Holding up nothing, anchored to nothing.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox I want to leave unresolved for a moment, because it deserves to ache: *the palm's height was real.* The Quran doesn't mock their strength or call it illusion — it was created strength, real engineering, genuine human magnificence. The ayah's claim is more unsettling than "your power was fake." Its claim is: **your power was real, and it was never the question.** The question was only ever the roots. And on the day the question was asked, the tallest trees in the land turned out to be the most spectacular things to fall.

Now let the Quran complete the picture, because it does — in another surah, with the same tree. *Have you not seen how Allah sets forth a parable: a good word is like a good tree —* **aṣluhā thābit** *— its root is firm, and its branches are in the sky?* (14:24). Read those two trees side by side and the whole Quranic doctrine of height appears. The Quran is not against branches in the sky. It is not against tallness, achievement, building, reach. The believer's tree *also* touches the heavens. The difference between the two trees is invisible from the outside — it is entirely underground. *Aṣluhā thābit* versus *munqaʿir*. Root, firm — versus root, torn out. Same height. Opposite endings.

So the question the ayah hands you is not *how tall are you?* It is: **what is your height attached to?** When you grew — in career, in knowledge, in platform, in reputation — did your roots deepen at the same rate? Or have you been growing crown-first, the way ʿĀd built, the way our age teaches: visible extension racing ahead of invisible anchoring?

There is a practical test, and it is uncomfortably simple. Your roots are whatever remains when the visible is subtracted. So ask: if the title, the audience, the portfolio were wrenched away tonight — *tanziʿu* — what would be left standing in me? Whatever answers that question — your salah when no one sees it, your truthfulness when it costs, your connection to Allah at 4 a.m. — *that* is your root system. And the maintenance schedule for roots is daily, hidden, and unimpressive, which is exactly why we neglect it. Tomorrow, before you add one more inch of crown — one more visible achievement — give the first hour to the *qaʿr*. The part of the palm nobody sees is the only part the wind couldn't argue with.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of One Frame

Now step all the way back, and watch the pieces assemble into a single design.

The ayah is one frame of film, eight words long, and it is constructed like a sentence-length palm tree. At the front, the living verb — *tanziʿu* — the wind, present tense, forever happening. At the center, the human beings — *al-nās* — already passing, in the very moment of the verb, from people into image: *ka-annahum*, "as if they were." And at the end, where Arabic places its final weight, the word that reaches lowest: *munqaʿir*, from *qaʿr*, the bottom of everything. The ayah literally descends as it proceeds — from the wind above, through the people, down to the torn root. **The verse's word order is the storm's trajectory.**

And the linguistic discoveries and the thematic ones turn out to be one insight seen from two directions. The grammar said: an extraction so total that "from where?" has no answer; a Form VII of pure passivity; strength preserved, selfhood removed. The themes said: every false root — the pit, the height, the plan — fails at once and completely; and what remains of a person who rooted in himself is exactly what remained of ʿĀd: real, impressive, horizontal. The physical palm was never the subject. It was the stepping stone to the unseen question every soul carries: *what am I actually rooted in — and would it hold?*

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Don't answer them quickly.

1. If I listed the three things that make me feel secure, how many of them are pits — created things asked to do a Creator's job?

2. In the last five years, which has grown faster in me: my visible height, or my hidden roots? What's the evidence?

3. ʿĀd saw the storm coming and used their final hours to dig. When warnings reach me — an ayah that unsettles, a janazah, a near miss — what do I instinctively reach for first: my plans, or my Lord?

4. What would remain standing in me if everything visible were subtracted tonight?

5. Surah Al-Qamar follows every grave with the same open door: *We have made the Quran easy for remembrance — is there anyone who will remember?* The question is grammatically still open. Am I answering it?

### One Sentence

**Strength that is not rooted in Allah is not protection from the fall — it is only the height of it.**

### The Invitation

This ayah does not ask you to abandon your pits or cut down your height. It asks you, gently, to relocate one thing: the *trust*. Keep the plans; move the reliance. Keep growing; grow downward first. Let the visible life be branches — and this week, every day, give something deliberate and hidden to the roots: the prayer no one sees, the Quran read slowly before the world wakes, the quiet *tawakkul* spoken over what you cannot control. The wind that is coming for every life — death, if nothing sooner — will ask only one question, and it will ask it at the *qaʿr*.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who hold every root in every depth — make our roots in You firm, *aṣluhā thābit*, so that no wind of this world or the next can wrench us out. Do not leave us leaning on pits we have dug with our own hands; let our strength bow before Yours while it is still attached to our hearts. And make us among those who heard the question — *is there anyone who will remember?* — and answered with their lives. Amin.
