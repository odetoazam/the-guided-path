# The Verdict Written in the Passive Voice

## Surah Al-Qamar 54:45

> **سَيُهْزَمُ ٱلْجَمْعُ وَيُوَلُّونَ ٱلدُّبُرَ**
>
> *Sayuhzamu-l-jamʿu wa yuwallūna-d-dubur*
>
> "The assembly will be routed, and they will turn their backs."

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Battlefield Report Filed Years Early

Before we can hear this verse, we have to stand where it was first heard.

Makkah. Years before the hijrah. The Muslims are not a community yet — they are a problem. A few dozen people, many of them enslaved or poor, some of them being starved in a boycott, some of them beaten in public. They have no army. They have no treasury. Most of them cannot walk through the marketplace without calculating who might hurt them today.

And across town stands Quraysh — wealthy, armed, ancient, connected. When pressed about this new prophet and his warnings, the surah tells us their answer. It is the verse immediately before ours, 54:44:

> أَمْ يَقُولُونَ نَحْنُ جَمِيعٌ مُّنتَصِرٌ — "Or do they say: We are an assembly bound to prevail?"

Listen to what their argument actually is. It is not "our case is true." It is not "our gods have answered him." It is: **we are many.** That's the whole credential. *Naḥnu jamīʿ* — we are a gathered mass. Touch one of us and you touch all of us.

And into that — into a city where the believers are outnumbered something like a hundred to one — comes our verse. A flat, unhedged statement about a future military outcome:

**The assembly will be routed, and they will turn their backs.**

All four of our classical commentators — al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi — say the same thing about where this landed: the day of Badr. Years after these words were first recited in Makkah, an army of roughly a thousand Qurayshi fighters broke and fled before three hundred and thirteen badly equipped believers, and the verse that had been sitting in the surah all along was suddenly a description of the morning news. Al-Qurtubi adds quietly: *and other occasions besides it.*

But here is what should stop us — and this is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows.

The Quran could have said this so many ways. "You will defeat them." "Allah will destroy them." "Their assembly will fall to the believers." Instead, the verse does three strange things. It announces the defeat **without naming who inflicts it.** It uses the **definite article** — not *an* assembly, but *the* assembly — as if pointing at something. And it ends with a **singular noun where every rule of sense expects a plural**: they will turn *the back* — one back — not *their backs*.

None of that is accident. By the end of this reflection, I want you to see that those three choices are not stylistic flourishes. They are the verdict itself.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: سَيُهْزَمُ — A Defeat With No Defeater

Let's start with something you already know from your own language, even if you've never named it.

There is a way of speaking that grammarians call the **passive voice** — in Arabic, *al-mabnī lil-majhūl*, literally "built upon the unknown." It's the difference between "the boy broke the window" and "the window **was broken**." Same event. But in the second sentence, something has happened to the doer: he has vanished from the grammar entirely. The sentence contains the wound but not the hand.

We usually use the passive to hide things. Politicians love it — "mistakes were made." But the Quran uses it for something else entirely, and this verse is one of the cleanest examples in the Book.

*Sayuhzamu.* The root is **hazama** — to rout, to break an army so completely that it stops being an army and becomes a crowd of individuals running. And the form here is passive imperfect: **"it will be routed."**

Now notice something. Routed — *by whom?*

The verse doesn't say. Read it again. The believers are not in this sentence. The Prophet ﷺ is not in this sentence. There is no army, no swords, no angels — grammatically, there is no agent at all. The assembly simply... breaks. The defeat arrives in the verse the way it would arrive at Badr: from a direction Quraysh could not see.

Stay with me, because there's a second layer stacked on top of this one. The verb doesn't begin with the passive — it begins with a single letter: **sa-** (سَـ). In Arabic, this prefix attaches to a verb to mark the future as *settled* — not "might happen," not "if conditions hold," but *will*. One letter, and the entire future tense locks into place.

So the full weight of this one word is: **a defeat that is certain, inflicted by no one you can point to.**

Why does that matter? Because of who was listening. If the verse had said "you will defeat them," every Makkan would have laughed — *these* people? The enslaved, the boycotted, the beaten? The claim would have been measured against the believers' visible strength and dismissed. But the verse never stakes the outcome on the believers' strength. It removes them from the sentence. The defeat is decreed *over the heads of both armies*, the way weather is decreed.

And here the classical readers preserved something beautiful. Al-Qurtubi records that while the overwhelming majority read *sayuhzamu* — "it will be routed," passive, agent hidden — the reader Ruways, transmitting from Yaʿqūb, read it **سَنَهْزِمُ — *sanahzimu*: "We will rout the assembly."**

Don't treat these as competing options. Treat them as two camera angles on one reality. The majority reading gives you the scene as Quraysh would experience it: a collapse with no visible cause. The minority reading lifts the veil and shows you the unnamed hand: *We*. The hidden agent of one reading is named in the other. Together they say: the defeat will look agentless from the ground — and it will be Allah's act from above.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing in this verse's grammar that I need you to see before we move on, because it's the asymmetry the whole verse turns on.

The defeat is **passive** — done *to* them. But the verse has a second verb, and it is fully **active**: *yuwallūna* — "they will turn." This is Form II of the root *waliya*, the form of deliberately turning something to face a direction. They are the subject. They do this themselves.

Read the two halves together and feel the cruelty of the grammar — and I mean cruelty in the precise sense of truth without anesthesia:

**Their defeat is not their act. Their flight is.**

In the entire verse, the assembly — this proud, gathered, prevailing mass — is granted exactly one action of its own. Running away. Everything else is done to them. That is what the verse says becoming an enemy of Allah does to human agency: it doesn't erase your will; it reduces your will's entire remaining jurisdiction to the direction of your retreat.

### Element Two: ٱلْجَمْعُ — Answered in Their Own Word

Here's a rhetorical move you've experienced even if you've never named it: being answered in your own vocabulary.

A child says, "Everyone is going!" and the parent answers, "Then *everyone* can explain it to their parents." The power of the reply is that it doesn't import new words — it picks up the speaker's own word, turns it over, and hands it back with the weight reversed. Rhetoricians treat this echo as one of the sharpest instruments in argument, because it leaves the opponent nowhere to stand: the ground they chose is the ground they lose on.

Now go back one verse. Quraysh said: *naḥnu jamīʿun muntaṣir* — "we are a **jamīʿ**, a gathered force, bound to prevail." Root: *jīm-mīm-ʿayn*. Gathered-ness. Mass. Plurality as power.

And our verse answers: *sayuhzamu **l-jamʿu*** — "**the jamʿ** will be routed."

Same root. Their word, picked up and handed back. They chose the battlefield of vocabulary — *we are the gathering* — and the verse meets them precisely there: very well, *the gathering* is what will break.

And notice the definite article — **al**-jamʿ, *the* assembly. Not "an assembly," not "assemblies of disbelievers" in general. The *al-* here functions like a pointing finger: *that* assembly. The one you just mentioned. The one bragging in the previous verse. The Quran is not making a general observation about crowds; it is taking delivery of a specific boast and stamping a verdict on it.

Let's sit with what this means about their argument. When the Quran wants to refute a *claim*, it refutes the claim — the Book is full of arguments, evidences, challenges. But Quraysh in 54:44 made no claim about truth. Their entire position was *numbers*. And to an argument made of numbers, the verse doesn't respond with a counter-argument. You cannot reason with a headcount. You can only break it.

So the verse does the only thing that answers a crowd: **it promises the crowd's dissolution.**

What this single word reveals: the Quran heard exactly what Quraysh's confidence was made of — not conviction, but congregation — and aimed its verdict at the congregation itself.

### Element Three: ٱلدُّبُرَ — The Plural That Flees as a Singular

Now the strangest word in the verse, and the one our commentators spent the most ink on.

*Wa yuwallūna **d-dubur*** — "and they will turn **the back**."

Stop. *They* — plural verb, a whole army — will turn... *the back*. Singular. Every instinct of grammar expects *al-adbār*, "their backs," plural — and in fact that is exactly the form the Quran uses elsewhere for armies fleeing. So why, here, do a thousand men turn *one back*?

The classical grammarians were not embarrassed by this question; they answered it carefully, and both al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi preserve the answer. Two layers.

First: *al-dubur* here is what Arabic calls an **ism jins** — a genus noun, a singular that stands for the whole class. Al-Qurtubi compares it to the way Arabs say *dirham* and *dīnār* for money in general. Al-Baghawi gives the idiom: it's like saying "we struck *the head* of them" when you mean heads — the singular carries the plural when one stands naturally for many. Documented, attested, standard Arabic.

Second — and this is the detail both of them state explicitly — the singular was chosen **li-ajli ruʾūsi-l-āy**: *for the sake of the verse-endings.*

You have to hear Surah Al-Qamar recited to feel this. Fifty-five verses, and virtually every single one lands on the same closing sound: a clipped syllable ending in **-r**. *Mustamirr. Nudhur. Munhamir. Dusur. ʿAqir. Wa-zdujir.* Verse after verse after verse, like a drumbeat that never breaks — a surah about warnings that *sounds* like a warning, the same knock on the same door, fifty-five times. The plural *adbār* would have broken the drumbeat. The singular *dubur* keeps it: *...wa yuwallūna-d-dubur.* Knock.

So the grammar is settled: genus noun, in service of the surah's relentless rhyme. That is the documented fact, and we stand on it.

But now — and I want to flag clearly that what follows is what this construction *invites us to consider*, the meaning that becomes visible once the grammar is secure — look at what this singular does inside *this particular verse*.

These are the people whose entire boast was plurality. *We are a jamīʿ.* We are many. And the verse has already granted them their plurality once — *yuwallūna*, "they turn," fully plural verb, every man choosing flight individually. But the moment the verse shows us what they turn... the plural collapses. Not backs. **The back.** One undifferentiated surface, as if the proud gathering, in the instant of its breaking, stops being a thousand men and becomes a single fleeing thing.

This is the arc of the whole verse compressed into its last word. They entered as a *jamʿ* — gathered, plural, loud. They exit as a *dubur* — one back, receding. The crowd that made each man feel large is, from the vantage of the verse, one shape running away. **Defeat does not just scatter the crowd. It reveals that the crowd was always one thing — and that one thing was never strength.**

### The Bridge

So look at what the construction of nineteen letters has shown us. A defeat decreed in the passive — certain, and inflicted by no hand you can see. A boast answered in its own vocabulary — *the* assembly, that one, the one you just mentioned. And a plural that flees as a singular — the gathering unmasked as one breakable thing.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because the *jamʿ* did not die at Badr. You have stood inside one. You may be standing inside several right now.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Armor of Numbers

Think about the last time you held an opinion you weren't sure of — and then discovered that everyone around you held it too. Feel what happened in your chest. The question didn't get *answered*. It got *quieter*. Nothing about the truth of the matter changed; what changed is that you were no longer alone with it, and being not-alone *feels* like being right.

Psychologists have studied this for seventy years and given it names — social proof, conformity cascades, the experiments where a person will deny what their own eyes see rather than disagree with a unanimous room. But you don't need the studies. You know the feeling from the inside. "Everyone I respect thinks this." "No one I know believes that." We treat the headcount as evidence. We wear the crowd as armor.

And here is the uncomfortable precision of Surah Al-Qamar: when Quraysh — ancient, sophisticated, poetic Quraysh — were finally pressed to state their position against revelation, the position they stated was *exactly this and nothing more*. Not "he is wrong because..." Not "our fathers' religion is true because..." Just: *naḥnu jamīʿun muntaṣir*. We are many, so we will be fine.

It is the oldest argument in the world, and it is not an argument at all. It is a feeling wearing the costume of an argument.

Now watch what the verse does to it. It does not say the crowd is *wrong* — the surah spent forty verses establishing that. It says something colder: the crowd is *breakable*. **Sayuhzamu.** The armor you are wearing is made of other people, and other people run.

Let that move through a few rooms of your own life.

The deen you hold — how much of it is *yours*, held because you stood before Allah's words and were convinced, and how much is borrowed warmth from the people around you? This question cuts in every direction, by the way. A person can be in the *jamʿ* of practicing Muslims and still be wearing the crowd rather than carrying conviction — and discover it only when the crowd is removed: the move to a new city, the university where no one prays, the workplace where the deen is a curiosity. The defeat of *that* inner assembly doesn't happen on a battlefield. It happens quietly, the first month no one is watching.

And the reverse: the person who silences the pull they feel toward Allah because *their* crowd — secular, ironic, certain — would find it embarrassing. Same armor. Same breakability.

The Quran has a fixed teaching about where every crowd ends, and it is one of the most solemn verses in the Book — 19:95: *and every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone.* The *jamʿ* is a temporary arrangement. It does not enter the grave with you. It does not stand with you at the Reckoning. Every gathering you have ever hidden inside has a dissolution date.

So here is the practical edge, and I'd ask you to actually do this rather than nod at it: **take one conviction you act on — one — and ask whether it would survive the emptying of the room.** If everyone who currently agrees with you vanished tomorrow, would you still pray? Still believe? Still hold that position you post about? Whatever survives the empty room is yours. Whatever doesn't was never conviction. It was *jamʿ* — and you now know the verdict written over every *jamʿ* that stands on nothing else.

### Theme Two: Carrying a Verdict Before Its Evidence

There is a particular human ache that doesn't have a good English name: the gap between a promise and its fulfillment. Anyone who has waited for test results, for a child to come home, for a marriage to heal, for a duʿā to be answered — you know the texture of that gap. The promise is real, but the evidence is not here yet, and every visible thing argues against it.

Now consider what this verse asked of the first people who memorized it.

It was recited in Makkah — our commentators are unanimous that its fulfillment came at Badr, which means there were *years* between the words and the morning they described. Years in which a believer would stand in salah, recite *sayuhzamu-l-jamʿu*, "the assembly will be routed" — and then walk out into a city where the assembly was doing just fine. The assembly controlled the wells, the trade routes, the Kaaba itself. The assembly was beating his friends. The verse and the visible world flatly contradicted each other, and the believer was asked to recite the verse anyway, in the certain past-tense confidence of the *sa-* prefix: this *will* happen. Sealed. Done, merely not yet displayed.

It is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb himself later recalled wondering, when this verse came down, *which* assembly — what gathering could this mean, and when? Even the strongest of them carried the verse as a sealed envelope. And a narration recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī describes the morning the envelope opened: the day of Badr, the Prophet ﷺ in his armor, reciting this very verse — *sayuhzamu-l-jamʿu wa yuwallūna-d-dubur* — as the promise and the evidence finally collided in front of everyone.

Here's what I want you to notice about those in-between years, because this is where the verse becomes about your life and not about military history.

**The verse was equally true the entire time.** It was true when the Muslims were starving in the boycott. It was true on the worst day of persecution. Its truth never fluctuated with the visible evidence — only its *visibility* did. The believers in Makkah were not waiting for the verse to *become* true. They were waiting for the world to catch up to it.

Now: you carry verses like this. Right now. Allah has issued verdicts in His Book with that same settled certainty — that He forgives all sins for the one who returns to Him; that with hardship comes ease; that whoever has taqwā of Allah, He will make a way out for them; that not an atom's weight of good is lost. These are not poetry. They are the same grammatical species as *sayuhzamu*: sealed verdicts about a future you cannot yet see.

And the question of the Makkan years is your question: **do you recite the verdict with the confidence of its grammar, or with the confidence of your circumstances?** When the evidence is absent — when the duʿā has gone unanswered for years, when the hardship has no visible ease attached — the Makkan believer's posture is available to you: the promise is already true; the display is simply scheduled.

The practical application is almost embarrassingly concrete. Take the divine promise you find hardest to believe *for yourself* — for most people it is forgiveness; for some it is relief — and the next time you ask Allah for it, change your inner posture from *petition* to *collection*. You are not trying to talk Allah into something. You are presenting a promissory note He wrote. That is not arrogance; it is *tasdīq* — believing Him the way Badr believed this verse.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now step back, because the verse sits inside a structure, and the structure delivers a final blow we haven't yet absorbed.

Watch the sequence:

- **54:43** — the surah turns from four destroyed nations to the live audience: *"Are your disbelievers better than those? Or do you have immunity in the scriptures?"*
- **54:44** — their answer: *"We are an assembly bound to prevail."* The boast.
- **54:45** — our verse: the verdict. *The assembly will be routed.*
- **54:46** — *"Rather, the Hour is their appointed time — and the Hour is more calamitous and more bitter."*

Read verse 46 again. **Bal** — "rather," "no, more than that." The Quran issues the most astonishing battlefield prophecy in its pages — a prophecy that would be fulfilled to the letter — and then immediately *waves it off as the lesser matter*. Badr, the verse is saying, is not the appointment. Badr is the deposit. The rout of the assembly in this world is a small, visible installment of a verdict whose full payment is the Hour.

This is why the verse could afford its passive voice. A verdict that hides its agent at Badr will unveil Him completely on the Day when no agent but Him remains.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two directions.

The grammar showed us: a defeat with no named defeater, a boast answered in its own word, a plural crowd that flees as a single back — and an assembly whose only remaining act of free will is the direction of its retreat.

The themes showed us: every crowd we hide inside is breakable, and every promise Allah has issued is already true in the gap before its evidence.

Put them together: **the verse is a portrait of two kinds of certainty.** Quraysh's certainty was made of headcount, and it was grammatically dismantled before it was militarily dismantled. The believers' certainty was made of a single letter — *sa-* — and it outlasted years of contradicting evidence until the world surrendered to it. One certainty was loud and breakable. The other was quiet and already settled. The verse asks you, every time you recite it, which kind you are carrying.

### Questions to Carry

1. Which of my convictions would survive the emptying of the room — and which exist only because the room is full?
2. When I imagine standing firm in my deen, am I imagining standing in a crowd — or standing alone the way I will actually stand on the Day each one comes to Him alone?
3. Is there a promise of Allah I have quietly downgraded from *verdict* to *comfort* because its evidence hasn't arrived yet?
4. The assembly's last free act was choosing the direction of its flight. When I flee difficulty — and I do — which direction am I turning: away from Allah, or toward Him?
5. If Badr is the deposit and the Hour is the appointment, what am I treating as the appointment that is really only a deposit?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**When your only argument is the crowd, the verdict is already written in the passive voice — the gathering will break, and the one act left to it will be the turning of a single back — while the believer's quiet certainty, carried for years against all visible evidence, is simply truth waiting for its morning.**

### The Closing Invitation

This week, find the one place in your life where you are wearing other people as armor — one opinion, one habit, one silence — and quietly replace the crowd with a reason. Not publicly. Not dramatically. Just take that one thing and re-found it on something that will still be standing when you come to Him alone. And take one promise of His and begin treating it as already true.

### Duʿā

O Allah, You routed the assembly that trusted in its numbers and kept Your promise to a handful who trusted in Your word — make our certainty from the second kind, not the first. Do not let us hide from You inside any crowd, and do not let the delay of Your promise's evidence shake our grip on Your promise. And on the Day each of us comes to You alone, let us arrive as those who turned toward You every time we fled. Āmīn.
