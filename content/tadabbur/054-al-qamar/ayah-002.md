# The Verdict Before the Evidence

## Surah Al-Qamar, Ayah 2

**وَإِن يَرَوْا۟ ءَايَةً يُعْرِضُوا۟ وَيَقُولُوا۟ سِحْرٌ مُّسْتَمِرٌّ**

*Wa in yaraw āyatan yuʿriḍū wa yaqūlū siḥrun mustamirr*

"And if they see a sign, they turn away and say: 'Magic — continuing on.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Night the Sky Answered

Before we can hear this ayah, we have to stand where its first listeners stood.

It is Makkah, years before the migration. The Prophet ﷺ has been preaching for over a decade, and the leaders of Quraysh have settled into a comfortable strategy: demand proof. Proof is a wonderful thing to demand, because it puts you in the judge's seat. You fold your arms. You set the bar. And as long as the bar isn't met, you owe nothing — not belief, not change, not even attention.

Al-Qurtubi records a narration from Ibn Abbas describing what happened next. The mushrikūn gathered around the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and made their demand specific: *if you are truthful, split the moon for us — one half over Mount Abū Qubays, one half over Quʿayqiʿān.* Name the mountains. Set the stage. Make it impossible.

And he asked: *if I do it, will you believe?*

They said yes.

He asked his Lord — and the moon split.

Stop and feel this. Not a vision. Not a metaphor. The one fixed, serene object in every human night sky — the thing your grandmother and her grandmother and every shepherd in every desert had watched their whole lives — pulled apart above their heads. Al-Qurtubi observes that this very ayah is evidence that they *saw* it: "and if they see a sign, they turn away" — the turning away presupposes the seeing.

The first ayah of this surah records the event: *the Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split.*

The second ayah — our ayah — records the response.

And here is what should stop you cold before we read a single word of it: the response is not recorded in the past tense.

**Here's the question to carry into everything that follows:** this ayah contains only seven words, and three of them are verbs. The entire psychology of human denial — the kind documented in research labs fourteen centuries later — is encoded in the *order* those three verbs appear. And then the ayah ends on one final word that the greatest commentators of this ummah could not agree on. They give it meanings that flatly contradict each other.

What if the disagreement is the point? What if Allah chose a word whose ambiguity *performs* the very confusion it describes?

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Three Verbs, and the Order That Indicts

Before we open the ayah, let me teach you something about conditional sentences — because this ayah is one, and its structure is where the first discovery lives.

Every language has if-then statements. *If it rains, the streets flood.* And notice what an if-then statement claims: not that something happened once, but that there is a *mechanism*. A reliable link. If-then statements describe how a system behaves. When I say "if you compliment him, he deflects," I'm not reporting an event — I'm describing his character. I'm telling you what he *does*, dependably, every time.

Now look at what Allah does in the very first word.

**وَإِن يَرَوْا۟** — *wa in yaraw* — "and if they see..."

The previous ayah used the past tense: *inshaqqa al-qamar* — the moon **split**. A completed, dateable event. One night. One sky. But our ayah shifts into the imperfect tense — *yaraw*, they see; *yuʿriḍū*, they turn away; *yaqūlū*, they say — the tense of ongoing, repeating action, wrapped inside a conditional.

Let's sit with this. The moon split *once*. But Allah does not say "they saw it and turned away." He says: **if they see a sign — any sign — they turn away.** The grammar has moved from event to *pattern*. From one night to a standing law of their hearts. The split moon isn't being treated as a story that ended badly. It's being treated as a *sample* — one data point revealing a permanent mechanism.

And notice the word **ءَايَةً** — *āyatan* — "a sign." Indefinite. Not *the* sign. Not "the splitting of the moon." Just... a sign. Any sign. Allah has deliberately un-specified the most spectacular miracle in the Makkan period. Why?

Because if He had said "they saw the moon split and turned away," you could imagine a defense: *that particular sign was confusing, ambiguous, maybe an eclipse, maybe a trick of the eye.* The indefinite closes that door. The problem was never *this* sign. Raise the moon, lower the moon, split it in halves or quarters — **the response was written before the sign arrived.**

[PAUSE]

Now — the order. This is the heart of it.

Read the three verbs in sequence: *yaraw* — they see. *Yuʿriḍū* — they turn away. *Yaqūlū* — and they say.

See it? **The speech comes after the turning.**

If these were honest people reaching a wrong conclusion, the order would be: they see, they *examine*, they *conclude*, and the conclusion makes them turn away. Investigation first, verdict second, departure third. That's how a judge works.

But that is not the sequence Allah records. They see — and they turn away — and *then*, with their backs already turned, they issue the verdict: "magic."

The explanation is not the *reason* for the turning away. The explanation is the *justification manufactured after* the turning away. The back turns first. The mouth catches up. By the time the word "siḥr" leaves their lips, it isn't a conclusion at all — it's a press release for a decision already made.

**Allah has recorded, in the sequence of three verbs, that their reasoning runs backward.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: Yuʿriḍū — The Geometry of a Closed Heart

Now let's open the middle verb, because it's hiding a physical picture.

**يُعْرِضُوا۟** — *yuʿriḍū* — comes from the root **ع-ر-ض** (*ʿ-r-ḍ*), and the base meaning of this root, documented across the classical lexicons, is *width, breadth, the side of a thing* — *al-ʿarḍ* as opposed to *al-ṭūl*, length. From this root Arabic builds the verb form used here — Form IV, *aʿraḍa* — which means, with beautiful literalness, **to give something your side.** To rotate your body so that what faces the thing is no longer your face, but your flank. Your shoulder. The edge of you.

Stay with that image, because it's more precise than our English "turn away."

When something faces you, you are in relationship with it. Your eyes can meet it. You can be addressed. *Iʿrāḍ* is the deliberate breaking of that geometry — you don't flee, you don't attack, you don't argue. You simply rotate. You make yourself unaddressable. The thing is still there, still speaking, still shining — but it now falls on the side of you, the part with no eyes and no ears.

Here is the principle this whole methodology runs on: when the Quran gives you a physical image, the image is a stepping stone. The body's rotation is pointing at something that has no shape — **the orientation of a heart.** A heart, too, has a face. A heart can be turned toward a truth or can give it the shoulder. And the ayah is describing people whose hearts performed that rotation *before the light arrived* — so that when the moon split, the light landed on the side of them.

Now notice something — an absence.

In ordinary Arabic usage, *aʿraḍa* is completed with *ʿan*: you turn away **from** something. Turn away from the sign. From the Messenger. From the message. The language expects an object.

**Allah names none.**

*Yuʿriḍū* — they turn away — full stop. Turn away from *what*? The ayah leaves the question hanging, and that absence is doing deliberate work. Had Allah specified "they turn away from the sign," the iʿrāḍ would be contained — a rejection of one thing. By withholding the object, the turning away becomes **total and objectless**. It is no longer something they do *to* a sign. It is something they *are*. A standing posture. A direction of the soul that precedes every individual encounter — which is exactly what the conditional grammar of Element One already told us. The missing object and the if-then structure are two witnesses giving the same testimony.

What does a person look like when their iʿrāḍ has no object? Like someone for whom no conceivable evidence exists. Not because evidence is weak — but because the part of them that evidence would land on has been rotated out of the light.

[PAUSE]

So: the sequence runs backward, and the turning has no object. One question remains. When such people finally do speak — what do their words actually do? The last two words of the ayah answer that, and they're the strangest of all.

### Element Three: Mustamirr — The Word That Won't Hold Still

**سِحْرٌ مُّسْتَمِرٌّ** — *siḥrun mustamirr.* "Magic, mustamirr." Their entire verdict on the splitting of the moon. Two words.

*Mustamirr* is built from the root **م-ر-ر** (*m-r-r*) — and it comes in Form X, a verbal pattern you can hear in the word itself: *musta-*. Form X typically carries the sense of a state sought, entered, or fully established. The question is: established in *what*? Because the root *m-r-r* opens in more than one direction — and this is where the commentators part ways.

Listen to them disagree. Don't rush past this. Each one is a camera angle, and we're going to assemble the photograph.

**Camera angle one: it means "passing — about to vanish."** Al-Baghawi reports this from Mujāhid and Qatādah: *mustamirr* means *dhāhib* — going, departing — from the base sense of *marra*: **to pass by**. The same root that gives you *marra al-rajul* — the man passed by. Tafsir al-Muyassar renders the whole phrase this way: magic that is *bāṭil, dhāhib, muḍmaḥill* — false, fleeting, dissolving, with no permanence. On this reading the Quraysh are saying: *don't worry about it. It's a trick, and tricks expire. Wait a week and the moon will be ordinary again and so will he.* It's the language of reassurance. They are comforting *themselves*.

**Camera angle two: it means the exact opposite — "continuous, unending."** Al-Jalalayn records this reading: *dāʾim* — perpetual. On this reading the Quraysh are saying: *here he goes again. One 'miracle' after another — it never stops. This is just more of the same ongoing sorcery.* It's the language of weary dismissal. The very *persistence* of the signs — which should have been their proof — is repackaged as evidence of fraud: *real wonders are rare; this man produces them constantly; therefore it's a production.*

**Camera angle three: it means "strong — overwhelming."** Al-Baghawi reports this from Abū al-ʿĀliyah and al-Ḍaḥḥāk, and al-Jalalayn carries it too: *mustamirr* from *al-mirrah*, strength — and al-Baghawi gives us the picture underneath: *marra al-ḥabl* — said of a **rope** when it is twisted firm and tight; *amrartuhu* — I twisted it hard until it held. On this reading the Quraysh are conceding the sign's force: *this is magic of terrifying skill — magic that surpasses all magic.* It's the language of a witness who felt the power of what he saw and needed a category to put it in that wasn't "God."

Now stand back and look at all three angles at once.

It will pass. It will never stop. It is overwhelming. **These cannot all be sober assessments — they contradict.** A thing cannot be dismissed as fleeting and resented as perpetual in the same breath. And the classical scholars were not confused; they were each faithfully reading a word that genuinely opens in all three directions.

So ask the deeper question. Why would Allah, who chooses every letter, seal this ayah with a word that splinters into contradictory meanings?

**Because incoherence is the fingerprint of a manufactured explanation.**

Truth-seeking converges: people honestly examining the same evidence drift toward one account. Denial *diverges* — because denial doesn't need a coherent story, it needs *any* story, today's story, a story per mood. Wave it off as a fading trick on Monday; groan that it never ends on Tuesday; whisper that it's the most powerful sorcery you've ever seen on Wednesday — the content changes daily because the content was never the point. The word *mustamirr* shimmers between meanings because **the excuse it carries was always interchangeable.** Allah chose a word whose instability *performs* the instability of the people saying it.

And one more thing — listen to the word with your ears. *Mustamirrrr* — it ends on a doubled, rolled *rāʾ*, a sound that doesn't close but keeps vibrating, keeps going, trails on. A word about continuation that refuses to stop moving in your mouth. Even the sound won't hold still.

### The Bridge

So here is what the construction has shown us. A conditional that turns one night into a permanent law. A verb sequence that runs backward — verdict after departure. A turning-away with no object, because it *is* the object. And a closing word that means three contradictory things, because the people saying it would have said anything.

The grammar has given us an anatomy. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About *us*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lawyer in Your Head

Let me start somewhere that has nothing to do with seventh-century Makkah.

Sometime in the last few decades, psychologists studying human judgment kept colliding with the same uncomfortable result. We assume our minds work like judges: evidence comes in, we weigh it, a verdict comes out. But experiment after experiment showed the machinery running in the other direction. People reach conclusions *first* — driven by loyalty, fear, identity, desire — and then the reasoning mind goes to work **not as a judge but as a lawyer**, hired after the fact to build the best available case for a verdict already signed. The field gave it a name: *motivated reasoning*. Show people evidence against a belief they're invested in, and watch them get *better* at finding flaws — sharper, more creative, more energetic — than they ever are with evidence they like. Intelligence doesn't cure it. Intelligence *funds* it. The smarter the client, the better the lawyer.

And the most unsettling part: the lawyer works in-house. The press release he drafts is delivered *to you*, and you experience it as your own honest verdict. Nobody feels themselves doing motivated reasoning. It feels exactly like thinking.

Now go back to the three verbs.

*Yaraw* — the evidence arrives. *Yuʿriḍū* — the client turns away; the verdict is in. *Yaqūlū* — and now the lawyer stands up and delivers the argument: "magic."

Fourteen centuries before anyone ran a study, this ayah recorded the sequence with clinical exactness — **the speech after the turning, the brief after the verdict.** And the conditional grammar adds what the laboratory also found: this is not an event but a mechanism. *If* they see a sign — any sign, every sign — the machine produces the same output, because the machine's output was never a function of its input.

Here is the part that should unsettle us, though. It's easy to read this ayah at the Quraysh. The ayah is *about* them. But the mechanism it diagnoses did not die in Makkah — it ships standard with every nafs, including the one reading this.

Where does the lawyer practice in your life? Think honestly. The ayah whose plain meaning would cost you a habit you love — how fast did "context" and "scholars differ" arrive, and did they arrive *after* you'd already decided to keep the habit? The sincere criticism from a spouse, a parent, a friend — did you weigh it, or did you feel your inner body rotate, give it the shoulder, and *then* hear yourself composing the rebuttal: *they're exaggerating, they don't understand, they do it too*? Notice the order. The rotation, then the rhetoric. *Yuʿriḍū*, then *yaqūlū*.

The Quraysh saw the moon in two pieces and were saying "siḥr" before their necks had finished turning. We are not exempt from their machinery merely because we believe their moon.

**The practical discipline this ayah teaches is the audit of sequence.** When you find yourself explaining away something uncomfortable — a verse, a criticism, a sign in your own life — stop and run one question: *which came first in me, the examination or the verdict?* Did I look into this and then turn away, or did I turn away and then dress it in reasons? You cannot always stop the lawyer from standing up. But you can learn to recognize his voice — he is suspiciously fast, suspiciously fluent, and he only ever argues one side.

### Theme Two: The Word "Just" — Labeling as a Shield Against Reality

Begin again in our world, not theirs.

There is a small word we deploy constantly, almost invisibly, and it may be the hardest-working word in the modern vocabulary of avoidance: **"just."**

It's *just* a coincidence. She's *just* emotional. It's *just* a phase. That feeling in prayer? *Just* brain chemistry. The awe you felt under the night sky? *Just* an evolved response. Notice what the word does. It never denies the experience — it couldn't; the experience happened, you felt it, everyone saw it. Instead it does something far more efficient: **it concedes the event while confiscating its meaning.** Yes, that happened — *but it makes no claim on you.* "Just" is not an argument. It is a label whose entire function is to let you stop looking.

Now look again at the Quraysh's two-word verdict, and see the genius of their word choice.

They did not say the moon never split. Al-Qurtubi's point cuts here: the ayah itself testifies that they *saw* it. Denying the event was impossible — too many eyes, too much sky. So they reached for the one word that could absorb the event whole: **siḥr.** Magic. Because "magic," as a category, is purpose-built for exactly this: it accepts that something astonishing occurred — and reroutes it away from God. *Yes, the moon split. Yes, we saw it. It's sorcery.* The label costs them nothing and saves them everything: their gods, their trade, their fathers' graves, their seats at the head of Makkah. Two words, and the most violent intrusion of heaven into earth in their lifetimes is filed away in a drawer marked *handled*.

Siḥr was their "just." **The label is a shield — and what it shields you from is the meaning of what you have already seen.**

And here the third element of our linguistic journey returns with its full weight. Remember the three faces of *mustamirr*: it'll pass; it never stops; it's overwhelmingly strong. Three different shields grabbed from the same rack, depending on the day's anxiety. When you need to feel safe: *it's fading magic, it'll pass.* When you need to feel justified: *it's constant magic, he never quits.* When you cannot deny what your own chest felt: *it's mighty magic — mightier than any magic.* The shield changes shape with the threat. That is how you know it's a shield and not a description: **descriptions are accountable to the thing; shields are accountable only to the fear.**

There's a hidden tragedy in the third reading, the rope-reading — *marra al-ḥabl*, the rope twisted tight and strong. Some of them, the ones behind that reading, stood close enough to the truth to feel its strength. They didn't experience the sign as weak. They experienced it as a rope — firm, tightly wound, strong enough to hold. Strong enough to pull a man out of jahiliyyah, if he'd grip it. And their response to feeling its strength was to praise the craftsmanship of the sorcery. They felt the rope and called it a trick of weaving — and let it pass through their open hands.

So the question turns to us, where it always must. **What have you already seen — and labeled?**

Because this is the precise shape of the danger for a believer. Our danger is mostly not denying that the Quran is true. It's something quieter: the moment in salah when something cracked open in your chest — filed as *just tiredness, just emotion*. The ayah that landed on your exact situation the week you needed it — *just an algorithm-like coincidence of mushaf pages*. The narrow escape, the door that opened, the dream that warned you — labeled, filed, survived. Each label individually defensible. And the sum of them: a life in which Allah has been speaking the whole time, and every message was marked *handled* on arrival.

**The practical discipline:** catch yourself at the label. The next time you hear yourself say "just" — or its dressed-up cousins: *probably nothing, makes sense actually, classic coincidence* — about something that moved you, stop. Don't fight the label with another argument; the lawyer wins arguments. Instead, hold the label open for ten seconds and ask one question: *if this* were *a sign — if this were addressed to me — what is it asking?* You don't have to answer immediately. You just have to refuse to let the drawer close on schedule. The Quraysh's catastrophe wasn't that they saw magic in the sky. It's that the drawer closed in the time it takes to say two words.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now step back and look at the whole ayah from above — because its very shape is its meaning.

The sentence opens with seeing and a sign: *wa in yaraw āyatan* — the half that belongs to Allah. Light arriving. It closes with two human words: *siḥrun mustamirr* — the half that belongs to them. And in between, at the hinge of the sentence, stands the pivot on which everything turns: *yuʿriḍū* — the rotation.

Look at the asymmetry across that hinge. On one side: an āyah — a word the Quran uses for the moon split in the sky and for its own verses alike, a tear in the fabric of the ordinary. On the other side: two muttered words of category and dismissal. A split moon, answered by a filing label. The sentence itself enacts the diminishment — reality enters enormous and exits shrunk to a phrase — and the only thing that happened in between was the turning of a heart.

And the two halves explain each other. The turning (*iʿrāḍ*) is fixed — objectless, characterological, the same every time. The talking (*mustamirr*) is fluid — three contradictory meanings, a new excuse per day. **Denial is rigid in its direction and infinitely flexible in its justifications.** That is the complete anatomy, and you need both halves to recognize it — in history, and in the mirror.

### Bringing It Together

The grammar and the themes were never two discoveries. The backward verb sequence *is* motivated reasoning, written in syntax. The objectless Form IV *is* the standing posture that precedes all evidence. The unstable final word *is* the interchangeable shield. Allah did not merely describe the psychology of denial in this ayah — He *built* it into the sentence, so that the structure indicts what the words report.

### Questions to Carry

- In the matter where I feel most defensive these days — which came first in me, the examination or the verdict?
- What is the most recent thing that genuinely moved me toward Allah — and what label did I put on it within the hour?
- Where in my life is there an *iʿrāḍ* with no object — a standing rotation of the heart that decides my response before anything arrives?
- If my explanations for avoiding something keep changing while my avoidance stays constant — what does that tell me about which one is real?
- What rope have I felt the strength of — and called a trick of weaving?

### One Sentence

**Denial does not follow from what we see; it decides, in advance, what we are allowed to see — turning first, explaining after, and changing the explanation as often as needed so that the turning never has to change at all.**

### The Invitation

This week, just once, reverse the sequence of the three verbs. When something arrives that asks something of you — an ayah, a criticism, a sign — let the seeing finish before the turning begins. Stand face-on for ten seconds longer than your comfort wants. Not every such moment is a split moon. But the discipline of facing is built in small rotations, and so is the disease of *iʿrāḍ*. The Quraysh did not become people who could dismiss a divided sky overnight; they rehearsed on smaller signs until the great one bounced off them. Rehearse the opposite.

### Du'a

O Allah, You split the moon for people who turned away, and You have split open meanings for us in Your Book night after night — do not let our hearts give Your signs the shoulder. Make us people who face what You send before we name it, and strip from our tongues every label we use to escape You. O Turner of hearts, fix our hearts toward Your dīn, and when Your rope brushes our hands, let us grip it. Āmīn.
