# The Single Cry: When Carved Stone Becomes Trampled Straw

## Surah Al-Qamar, Ayah 31

**إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً فَكَانُوا۟ كَهَشِيمِ ٱلْمُحْتَظِرِ**

*Innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-kānū ka-hashīmi l-muḥtaẓir*

"Indeed, We sent upon them a single blast, and they became like the dry, trampled twigs of a fence-builder's pen."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Ruins They Rode Past

Before we open this ayah, you need to stand where the first listeners stood.

It is Mecca, in the years of mockery. The Quraysh have heard the warnings and laughed. Surah Al-Qamar opens with the moon itself split as a sign — and their response, recorded in its second verse, is to call it passing magic. So the surah answers them with a drumbeat. Not one story of a destroyed nation, but four, struck one after another like a fist on a door: the people of Nuh. Then 'Ad. Then Thamud. Then the people of Lut. And between each story, the same two refrains toll like a bell: *How then were My punishment and My warnings?* And: *We have certainly made the Quran easy for remembrance — so is there anyone who will remember?*

Now here is what makes the Thamud section different from the others for the people hearing it.

The Quraysh had *seen* Thamud's ruins.

Their trade caravans to Syria passed directly through al-Hijr, the rock city of Thamud. They had ridden past those facades carved into living mountain — doorways cut into cliffs, chambers hollowed from solid stone, architecture meant to outlast centuries. And it had outlasted centuries. The stone was still standing. The carvers were not.

The Quran elsewhere describes exactly what those homes meant to the people who made them: *"They used to carve homes from the mountains, feeling secure"* (15:82) — **āminīn**, secure. That was the whole point of cutting your house into a mountain. Floods cannot wash it away. Raiders cannot burn it. Wind cannot topple it. Thamud had solved, as completely as any ancient people ever solved it, the problem of safety.

So when this surah reaches Thamud, the listeners are not hearing about strangers. They are hearing about a place they have camped in. People who demanded a sign — and were given one, a she-camel brought out as a test. People who were told simply to leave her alone, to share the water with her. People who instead called their companion, and he took the blade and killed her. People who were then given three days.

And then comes our ayah. One verse. What happened when the three days ended.

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin — and this is the question that should pull you through everything that follows. The destruction of an entire civilization, masters of stone, takes Allah **half a verse** to describe. And the image He chooses for their ending is not fire, not rubble, not even bones. It is something so small, so domestic, so *humble*, that you have probably stepped on it without noticing: the dried twig-fragments that fall from a shepherd's thorn fence and get crushed under the feet of sheep.

People who carved mountains became the litter of a sheep pen.

Why that image? Why *single*? And why does the verse spend its first half entirely on Allah's action and its second half entirely on what remained?

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً — "A Single Blast"

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic counts.

In English, if I say "I heard a shout," the singularity is just grammar — of course it was one shout; I would have said "shouts" otherwise. But Arabic has a way of taking that built-in singularity and *underlining* it. The word صَيْحَةً (*ṣayḥatan*) is already singular — one cry. The grammar needs nothing more. But Allah adds the adjective وَٰحِدَةً (*wāḥidatan*) — "single," "one" — to a word that was already one.

When a language states what the grammar already implied, it is never wasting breath. It is pointing. Think of the difference in English between "he asked once" and "he asked **exactly** once." The information is the same. The emphasis changes everything — suddenly the *count* is the story.

So look at the ayah. **The count is the story.**

إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً — *Indeed, We sent upon them a single blast.*

Let's sit with what this singularity is doing. Thamud were not a village. They were a civilization advanced enough in engineering to hollow out mountains — and the Quran addresses them through their prophet Salih as a people given orchards, springs, palm trees, skilled carving (26:146-149). Undoing a civilization, by every human measure, is the largest project there is. Armies besiege cities for years. Empires take generations to fall.

One sound. Not two. Not a barrage. The text insists you register the number.

And notice something else — notice what the verse does *not* tell us. The ṣayḥah arrives indefinite, undescribed. Whose cry was it? How loud? What did it sound like? The Quran leaves the agent unnamed. Classical commentary fills in a narration here — al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi (reporting from ʿAṭā'), and the Tafsir al-Muyassar all relay that it was the cry of Jibril — but the Quranic text itself withholds it, and I think the withholding is deliberate. Because the moment you name and describe the instrument, the listener's attention goes to the instrument. *What a mighty angel, what a terrible cry.* The verse refuses you that. It keeps your eyes on the only two facts that matter: **We sent**, and **one**.

Stay with me, because this is where the surah does something extraordinary.

Nineteen verses later, Surah Al-Qamar closes with this declaration: وَمَآ أَمْرُنَآ إِلَّا وَٰحِدَةٌ كَلَمْحٍۭ بِٱلْبَصَرِ — *"And Our command is but one — like the blink of an eye"* (54:50).

The same word. **Wāḥidah.** The surah plants it here, in the dust of Thamud, and then harvests it at the end as a universal law. What you witnessed in verse 31 was not an exception, the surah is saying — it was a *sample*. One cry for Thamud, because one is all the command ever is. There is no divine effort scale. There is no "harder" miracle. The distance between Allah's command and its completion is not short — it is *nonexistent*, the blink of an eye, and even that comparison is a concession to our need for images.

Here's what's remarkable when you hold those two verses together: we instinctively measure power by effort expended. The heavier the lift, the more strength we credit. This verse breaks that instrument across its knee. A power that ends a civilization with one sound is not displaying *great* effort. It is displaying that effort, for this Power, **is not a category that exists.**

One word. One cry. One civilization, finished.

### Element Two: هَشِيم — The Word That Crunches

Now the second half of the verse — and here I need to teach you two things: a sound, and a shape.

First, the sound. Say the word aloud: **hashīm**. *Ha-shīm.* Feel what your mouth does — the breathy *hā*, then the dry rustle of the *shīn*. Arabic has many words whose phonetic texture imitates their meaning, and this is one of the clearest in the Quran. *Hashīm* sounds like dry stalks crunching underfoot. You cannot say it wetly. The word itself has had the moisture pressed out of it.

Second, the shape. The root is *h-sh-m*, which carries the meaning of crushing something dry and brittle. And the word comes on the pattern *faʿīl* — a pattern Arabic frequently uses with a **passive** sense: the thing to which the action was *done*. *Jarīḥ* is the wounded one. *Qatīl* is the slain one. *Hashīm* is **the crushed thing** — not the crusher, not the crushing, but what is left *after*.

So when the verse says فَكَانُوا۟ كَهَشِيمِ — *they became like hashīm* — the grammar itself has already stripped Thamud of agency. In the first half of the verse, Allah acts. In the second half, Thamud do not even get a verb of their own suffering. They simply *became* — became the object, the residue, the thing acted upon.

But there is a deeper layer in this word, and al-Baghawi preserves it for us. He records that the Arabs called *anything* that had once been moist and then dried out *hashīm* — كل شيء كان رطبًا فيبس, "everything that was once tender and then withered."

Let's sit with this. **Hashīm is not a word for things that were always dead.** It is a word specifically for things that were once alive — green, supple, full of water — and are now dry enough to crumble. The word carries its own past tense inside it. Built into *hashīm* is the memory of the life it used to have.

Now hold that against Thamud. A people described in the Quran as given springs and gardens — wetness, greenness, life. A people *flowing* with vitality and skill. The word chosen for their end does not merely say they died. It says they were *desiccated* — that everything moist and living in them was withdrawn, and what remained was so brittle that the next pressure, any pressure, would crumble it.

And one more turn of the lens, because al-Baghawi preserves multiple readings from the early generations, and they layer rather than compete. Ibn 'Abbas, in his report, pictures the twigs fallen from a sheep-fence, trampled by the flock. Ibn Zayd pictures decayed trees, crumbled so fine that *the wind scatters them*. Qatadah pictures something like charred, crumbling bones. Put the three images side by side and watch what they share: in every one, the thing has become **too light to resist anything**. Sheep's hooves. Wind. A touch. Thamud's whole identity was *weight* — stone, mass, permanence. Every camera angle the early commentators offer shows us the same final state: weightlessness. The people of the mountain could now be carried off by a breeze.

### Element Three: ٱلْمُحْتَظِرِ — The Fence-Builder's Irony

The last word of the verse is its quietest and, I think, its most devastating. But to see it, you first need to learn what the word means in the most ordinary, dusty, practical sense — because the commentators insist on grounding it there.

ٱلْمُحْتَظِرِ (*al-muḥtaẓir*) comes from the root *ḥ-ẓ-r*, which means to enclose, to fence in — the same root gives *ḥaẓīrah*, a pen or corral. The Tafsir al-Jalalayn explains the word with a small portrait of desert life: the muḥtaẓir is **the man who builds an enclosure for his sheep out of dry branches and thorns, to protect them from wolves and predators.** And the *hashīm* of the muḥtaẓir — the specific image our verse invokes — is what falls from that thorn fence and gets trampled into fragments under the animals' feet. Ibn 'Abbas, as al-Baghawi reports, describes exactly the same scene.

So the final image of the verse, in full: Thamud became like the bits of dried thorn that drop off a sheep-pen fence and are crushed under hooves. Not the fence. The *droppings* of the fence. The part so worthless that even the shepherd who gathered the branches never gives it a thought.

Now, the grammatical shape — and here Arabic rewards close attention. *Muḥtaẓir* is a Form VIII active participle. Form VIII characteristically folds the action back toward the doer: it is the form of doing something *for oneself*. The muḥtaẓir is not building fences as a trade, for others — he is the one who encloses *for his own benefit*, making a pen for *his own* flock, securing what is *his*. The Jalalayn's wording captures this precisely: he makes the enclosure *li-ghanamihi* — for his sheep.

Stay with me now, because everything we have built converges here.

What is a ḥaẓīrah? It is **security architecture**. A barrier you erect around what you love to keep predators out. And what was Thamud? *"They carved homes from the mountains, feeling secure"* (15:82). Thamud were, on the scale of civilizations, the ultimate muḥtaẓir — the ultimate enclosure-builders, the people who took security architecture to its logical extreme. Not thorn fences. Mountains.

And the verse ends them *inside someone else's enclosure* — not as the protected flock, not even as the protecting fence, but as the debris of the fence, underfoot.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer, and it comes from a genuine scholarly difference that al-Qurtubi records — a difference in recitation that, like so many, turns out to be two camera angles on one reality. The majority read the word with a kasrah — *al-muḥtaẓir* — the **person**, the fence-builder. But al-Hasan al-Basri, Qatadah, and Abu al-'Aliyah read it with a fathah — *al-muḥtaẓar* — the **enclosure itself**, the pen. On the first reading, Thamud are like the broken twigs *belonging to the man who built for protection*. On the second, they are like the broken twigs *of the very structure of protection*. One reading indicts the protector; the other indicts the protection. Together they close every exit: neither the one who builds the fortress nor the fortress itself survives the single cry.

**What this one word reveals:** Allah did not merely destroy Thamud. He chose, as their epitaph, the vocabulary of *their own project* — enclosure, protection, security — and showed it trampled.

### The Bridge

Step back and look at what the language has shown us. A count that insists on *one*. A word for their remains that carries the memory of lost life and the texture of dryness in its very sound. And a closing image drawn, with surgical irony, from the architecture of self-protection.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Fortress and the Twig — What We Build to Feel Safe

Let me start somewhere that has nothing to do with Thamud.

Think about what you have personally built to feel secure. Be honest and specific. For most of us it is a layered system, assembled over years: the savings account with its target number. The insurance policies. The qualifications that make us employable, the reputation that makes us recommendable. The relationships we keep in good repair partly — admit it — because they are there if things go wrong. The pension. The second income. The neighborhood chosen for its safety. We rarely see this system whole, because we built it one piece at a time, each piece reasonable. But stack it up and look at it, and it has a shape.

It is a ḥaẓīrah. A thorn fence around the flock of everything we love.

And here is the thing — building it is not condemned. The shepherd in the Jalalayn's portrait is not a villain; gathering branches against wolves is wisdom, and providing for those in your care is part of this religion. Thamud's sin was not masonry. They were not destroyed for carving homes; they were destroyed for *hamstringing the she-camel* — for taking the blade to the sign of Allah when it inconvenienced their water schedule. The carving enters the story for a different reason.

It enters because of what it did to their *hearing*.

Follow the sequence. A people whose every wall says *you are safe* are a people who can listen to a warner and feel nothing move in their chest. Salih warned them; the surah records their answer — they called him deluded, a liar, and asked why revelation should fall on him of all people (54:24-25). That is not the voice of people who have weighed the evidence. It is the voice of people whose stone walls have been whispering to them for generations: *nothing can reach you here.* The fortress did not make them safe. It made them **deaf**. The security was not the crime; it was the anesthetic that made the crime feel survivable.

And so the punishment came specifically as a *sound*. Sit with that. For a people insulated by stone, Allah did not send what stone defends against — no flood, no army, no fire to be walled out. He sent the one thing no wall has ever stopped. A wall can stop water, wind, and spears. It has never once stopped a sound. The ṣayḥah passed through their carved doorways as if the mountains were tents — and the people who could not be reached by the warning voice of a prophet were reached, perfectly and instantly, by another voice.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this stops being about Thamud.

Every one of us has felt the moment a piece of our fence cracks — the test result that comes back wrong, the industry that collapses, the institution that was too big to fail, failing. And notice your own reaction in those moments, because it is diagnostic: the *vertigo*. The feeling of the floor tilting. That vertigo is the honest moment. It is the instant you discover how much of your calm was leaning on the fence rather than on the One the fence cannot keep out — or hold back.

The verse is not telling you to tear down your fence. It is asking you a quieter and harder question: **when you feel safe, what exactly is generating the feeling?** If the answer is the savings, the stone, the system — then your security is made of the same material as Thamud's, and the verse has told you that material's true name. *Hashīm.* The word that remembers it was once alive. Every fence is already drying.

**The practical application:** This week, when you notice the specific comfort of your own security system — the glance at the account balance, the solidity of the front door locking — let it trigger one deliberate sentence: *ḥasbiyallāh* — Allah is my sufficiency, and this fence is His gift, not my god. You are not abandoning the fence. You are relocating your weight off it. The shepherd keeps his pen; he just stops believing the thorns are what really hold back the wolf.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: One — Living Before an Effortless Power

The second theme begins with the strange mathematics of how we trust.

Notice how human help works: it is always *budgeted*. When you ask someone powerful for something, you instinctively calculate the cost to them — their time, their capital, their attention — because their power is a finite tank, and every act of help drains it. This is why we ration our asking. We save the big requests for emergencies. We feel guilt asking twice. We rank our needs into "worth bothering them about" and "I'll handle this myself." Every relationship with created power is a relationship with a *limited supply*, and we have all internalized the etiquette of scarcity so deeply that we don't notice we're doing it.

Now bring that instinct — and watch it shatter — against *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* read in the light of 54:50: *Our command is but one, like the blink of an eye.*

The verse showed us divine power with the effort-meter visible: a civilization ended, and the meter read **one**. Not "one, with strain." Not "one, but a mighty one drawn from deep reserves." One, the way a glance is one. The surah then generalizes it: this is not how Allah acted on Thamud; this is how Allah's command *is* — toward everything, always. There is no big request and small request on the receiving end. Splitting the moon and turning a heart, ending Thamud and mending your affairs — none of it costs anything, because cost requires effort and effort requires limitation, and *He is not limited*.

Let's sit with what this does to fear, first.

If divine power required mobilization — armies gathered, energies summoned — then there would be warning, and distance, and time. The terror of this verse is that there is none. Between Thamud's third day and their last moment stood no visible preparation at all. Whatever standing arrangements you are counting on — the stability of your country, your health's quiet cooperation, the ordinary sun rising on ordinary obligations — all of it continues by a permission that can be withdrawn without effort, *because its continuation was never effortful to begin with.* The believer who absorbs this walks differently: not in paranoia, but in a kind of awakeness. The ground is not solid. The ground is *upheld*.

But now turn the same truth over, because it has a second face — and this is the turn the heart must not miss.

**The effortlessness cuts both ways.** The same "one" that ended Thamud is the "one" behind *kun fa-yakūn* — Be, and it is. You, sitting here with your impossible situation — the marriage that seems past repair, the child who has drifted, the debt with no visible exit, the sin you've repented from a hundred times — you have been applying creature-mathematics to Allah. Somewhere inside, you have ranked your needs as "too big," budgeted His power like a rich friend's patience, felt the quiet shame of asking again. The verse demolishes the entire framework. There are no big requests. Difficulty is a property of *our* position, never of His response. The du'a you've been embarrassed to keep making costs Him nothing to answer — *nothing* — and the paradox is this: the most terrifying verse in the Thamud story, properly absorbed, makes asking easier. The power that should make you tremble is the same power that should make you bold in supplication.

Don't rush past this paradox. Let it sit. Fear and hope, *khawf* and *rajā'*, are not two moods to alternate between. They are one accurate perception of one reality: a power without effort, facing you. Which face it shows depends on which side of the cry you stand.

**The practical application:** Identify the one du'a you have quietly downgraded — the request you stopped making with conviction because it felt too large. Tonight, make it again, but first say to yourself, in plain words: *what I am asking costs Him nothing.* Then ask like you believe it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Verse

Now that the work is done, step back and look at the verse's own construction — because the shape of the sentence preaches the same sermon as its words.

The verse is two halves on a hinge.

**First half:** إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً — every word is divine action. *We* — emphatic. *Sent* — Our verb. *A single cry* — Our instrument, counted.

**The hinge:** فَ — the Arabic letter *fā'*, one letter, which signals immediate consequence. Not "and then, after some time." Immediately. The entire interval between divine command and civilizational erasure is given exactly one letter of the verse — because that is how much time it occupied.

**Second half:** كَانُوا۟ كَهَشِيمِ ٱلْمُحْتَظِرِ — and here Thamud finally appear, and look at what they get. No verb of resisting, fleeing, or even dying. One verb: *they became.* Then a simile of debris.

The verse opens with the most real word in existence — *Innā*, the divine We — and closes with just about the least significant object in the desert: fence-litter under sheep. The sentence itself is a slope from absolute Being down to near-nothingness, and Thamud's entire story is the slide.

And the two themes we walked are the two halves seen from inside: the second half — *hashīm al-muḥtaẓir* — is the fate of every security built from created material; the first half — *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* — is the effortless power that determines when. The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were one journey. The grammar was the theology all along.

### Questions to Carry

- What is my ḥaẓīrah — and when I feel calm, is the calm coming from Allah or from the fence?
- Has any comfort in my life made me harder of hearing — slower to take a warning seriously because I feel insulated from consequences?
- Where am I still applying creature-mathematics to Allah — rationing my du'a as if His power were a budget?
- Thamud heard their warner for years and were unmoved, then heard one ṣayḥah and were finished. Which voices am I currently not moved by — and what am I assuming about how much time I have?
- If everything once-moist becomes hashīm, what am I watering in myself that cannot dry out — and what am I watering that can?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Everything you have built to protect yourself stands only by the permission of the One whose single word can turn carved stone into trampled straw — so lean your weight on Him, and let the fence be only a fence.

### The Closing Invitation

This week, let one moment of manufactured safety — the door locking, the balance loading, the contract signing — become a doorway instead of a wall: pause inside it, and hand the feeling of safety back to its true Owner. Not because the fence is evil, but because you now know what fences are made of. That small, repeated relocation of trust is what this verse, by Allah's permission, came to work in you.

### Du'a

O Allah, You whose command is but one, like the blink of an eye — do not leave us leaning on thorn fences while wolves of heedlessness circle our hearts. Make us moist with Your remembrance in a world that dries everything, and when we ask You, let us ask like people who know that nothing we need has ever cost You effort. Āmīn.
