# The Command Without a Verb: When "One" Is All It Takes

## Surah Al-Qamar, Ayah 50

**وَمَآ أَمْرُنَآ إِلَّا وَٰحِدَةٌ كَلَمْحٍۭ بِٱلْبَصَرِ**

*Wa mā amrunā illā wāḥidatun ka-lamḥin bil-baṣar*

"And Our command is but a single one — like the glance of an eye."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before This Ayah

Before we can hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, we have to stand where they stood.

It is Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ has been warning his people, and his people have made a sport of asking *when*. When is this Hour you keep threatening us with? When is this punishment? The previous surah, An-Najm, had ended on an ominous note: *azifat al-āzifah* — "the approaching one has approached" — and the Makkans walked away from it laughing. Then this surah opens with something that should have ended the laughing forever: **"The Hour has drawn near, and the moon has split"** (54:1). They saw a sign in the sky itself — and they called it passing magic.

So Surah Al-Qamar takes them on a tour. Five stops. The people of Nuh. 'Aad. Thamud. The people of Lut. The house of Pharaoh. Five nations who also heard warnings, also asked *when*, also kept living as though the answer were *never*. And after each ruin, the same haunting refrain rings out: **"And We have certainly made the Quran easy for remembrance — so is there anyone who will remember?"**

Notice something about those five stories. Each one took generations to build up to. Nuh preached for centuries. Thamud were given a she-camel and a waiting period. The warnings stretched across lifetimes. But when the moment of the command itself finally came — how long did *it* take?

For Thamud, the surah tells us exactly: **"We sent upon them a single blast — ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan — and they became like the dry stubble of a pen-builder"** (54:31). One blast. One.

Hold onto that word. *Wāḥidah.* One. The surah is going to hand it back to us.

Because now, at ayah 49, the surah stops telling stories and starts stating principles: **"Indeed, all things We created with qadar — with precise measure"** (54:49). And then comes our ayah. Eight words in English. Six words in Arabic. And inside those six words is one of the most startling descriptions of divine action in the entire Quran.

Here is the hook, and I'll say it plainly: **this ayah describes the most powerful act in existence — the command of Allah — and it does so without using a single verb.**

Not one. An ayah about ultimate *action* with no action word in it.

That cannot be an accident. Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Sentence With No Verb

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds sentences — because Arabic gives a speaker a choice that English mostly hides.

In Arabic, you can build a sentence around a verb — the grammarians call this *jumlah fiʿliyyah*, a verbal sentence. "Zayd stood up." "The rain fell." The moment you use a verb, you have smuggled in something else without noticing: **time**. Every Arabic verb carries tense in its very bones — it happened, it is happening, it will happen. A verb is an event with a timestamp.

But Arabic has a second option: the *jumlah ismiyyah*, the nominal sentence — a sentence built entirely from nouns. "The sky — blue." "Allah — One." No verb anywhere. And classical Arabic rhetoric recognized what this does: a nominal sentence doesn't narrate an event. It states a *reality*. It describes what something **is**, not what it *does* — a truth that doesn't unfold, doesn't begin, doesn't end. It simply holds.

Think of the difference between a video and a photograph. A verb is video — it plays, it takes duration. A nominal sentence is a photograph — it doesn't take time; it simply *is*.

Now open the ayah.

**وَمَآ أَمْرُنَآ إِلَّا وَٰحِدَةٌ كَلَمْحٍۭ بِٱلْبَصَرِ**

Go through it word by word. *Mā* — a negation particle. *Amrunā* — a noun: "Our command." *Illā* — an exception particle. *Wāḥidatun* — a noun: "one." *Ka-lamḥin* — a noun with "like" attached: "like a glance." *Bil-baṣar* — a noun: "by the eye."

Four content words. Every single one a noun. The peer-reviewed morphological analysis of this ayah confirms it flatly: verbs present — **none**.

Let's sit with this.

The subject of this sentence is *amr* — command. Command is the most action-saturated concept there is. When the Quran wants to describe Allah creating, it usually reaches for the famous verbal formula: *kun fa-yakūn* — "Be! — and it is." Verbs. Events. But here, in the ayah whose entire subject is how fast divine action happens — the verbs vanish.

Why?

Stay with me. A verb would have placed Allah's command *inside* time. "Our command happens quickly" — that sentence, with its verb, would mean the command is an event like other events, just a faster one. A sprinter among joggers, but still on the same track.

The verbless sentence says something far more radical: **the command of Allah is not an event on the track at all.** It does not "happen quickly." It does not "happen" in the way things happen to us — beginning, middle, end. The ayah's grammar performs its own meaning: just as the sentence contains no unfolding, the command contains no unfolding. From our side, things take time. From His side — and here language itself starts to strain — there is no "taking."

The ayah about instantaneous action is built from a grammar that has removed time itself.

**The form of the sentence is the first miracle of the sentence.**

### Element Two: "Nothing... Except One" — and the Word That Isn't There

Now I want to show you two things woven together in the middle of this ayah: a structure, and a silence.

First, the structure. Arabic has a rhetorical device the scholars call *ḥaṣr* — restriction. You build it with two particles: *mā* ("not") at the front, *illā* ("except") in the middle. "Not X except Y." It's the difference between saying "I trust Ahmad" and saying "I trust *no one* but Ahmad." The first is information. The second slams every other door shut. Whatever sits after *illā* stands alone in a cleared room.

This ayah is built on exactly that frame: *mā amrunā illā* — "Our command is nothing... except..."

Except what? One word: **wāḥidatun.** "One."

And here is where you must learn to read the Quran's silences as carefully as its speech. *Wāḥidatun* is an adjective in form — feminine, singular — and feminine adjectives in Arabic lean against a noun. "One" *what*? One time? One word? One act? The ayah doesn't say. **The noun is missing.** Arabic ears expect it, reach for it — and Allah withholds it.

When the classical commentators came to this gap, they did what careful readers do: they supplied the missing noun. And watch what happened — they didn't all supply the same one.

Al-Jalalayn and al-Qurtubi read it as *marratan wāḥidatan* — "**one time**." The command is never issued twice. No repetition, no revision, no second attempt. Al-Qurtubi puts it simply: Our command is but a single instance.

Al-Muyassar, and al-Baghawi in his second reading, supply *kalimatan wāḥidatan* — "**one word**." And they name the word: *kun*. "Be." When We will a thing, We say one word — and the thing is, without delay, without negotiation. Al-Baghawi adds a piercing phrase: *lā murājaʿata fīhā* — there is no going back and forth in it. No deliberation. No drafts.

Now — and this matters for how we read our scholars — these are not competing answers where one must be wrong. They are camera angles on the same reality. Stand at al-Qurtubi's angle and you see the command's **unity in number**: it never needs repeating, because nothing resists it. Stand at al-Baghawi's angle and you see its **unity in substance**: it is a single utterance, "Be," with no machinery behind it. One time. One word. The same oneness, photographed from two sides.

But here's what's remarkable — go one step further. Ask *why* Allah left the noun out at all. He could have said "one word." He could have said "one time." He said only: **one.**

Because the moment you supply a noun, you limit the oneness to that noun. Leave it open, and the "one" expands to fill every reading at once: one word, one time, one act, one will — *one, absolutely*. The absence is not a gap in the meaning. **The absence is the meaning.** The command of Allah is so utterly singular that even the noun describing its singularity would have been one word too many.

A restriction structure that clears the room — and then, standing alone in the cleared room, a single word meaning "one," with nothing leaning on it.

The grammar has drawn a picture of tawhid in action.

### Element Three: The Glance — *Ka-Lamḥin bil-Baṣar*

One more doorway. The ayah ends with a simile: *ka-lamḥin bil-baṣar* — "like a glance of the eye."

Start with the word itself. *Lamḥ.* Al-Qurtubi, drawing on the classical lexicon al-Ṣiḥāḥ, defines it precisely: *al-lamḥ* is **looking with haste** — the hurried look, the flicker of sight. And he points to where Arabs actually used the word: *lamaḥa al-barq* — **lightning flashed.** The same root serves for the dart of an eye and the flash of lightning across a night sky. *Lamḥ* is not "seeing." It is not "watching." It is the fastest thing the eye does — the flick, the flash, the instant of sight before sight even settles.

Say the word aloud: *lamḥ*. One syllable. It opens and shuts like the thing it names. The word for the flash *is* a flash. The Quran chose a sound that performs its own meaning.

Now ask the deeper question — the one this whole reflection has been building toward. The Quran constantly does this: it speaks of unseen realities by pointing at things we can touch and see. Stars, winds, wombs, lightning. The physical image is never the destination; it is the stepping stone. So: why, of all things, compare the divine command to a *glance*?

Think about what a glance is in your own experience. It is the one act you perform that has **no felt duration**. Everything else you do takes time, and you feel it taking time — walking, speaking, even thinking. But the glance? You look at the far mountain, and you are *already there*. No journey. No transit. Sight is the closest thing in human experience to action without process. Allah is speaking to us about a reality we cannot conceive — timeless action — so He points at the nearest thing to it that we *can* feel: the instant your eye flicks and the horizon is simply yours.

He argues for the unseen through the seen. The glance is the stepping stone; the command is the destination.

But now — stay with me, because the classical scholars saw one more layer, and it overturns the simile from inside.

Al-Baghawi transmits a report through ʿAṭā', attributed to Ibn Abbas, explaining this ayah: *"My decree in My creation is **faster** than the glance of an eye."*

Faster. Not "as fast as." **Faster.**

Read that against the ayah and something extraordinary comes into focus. The simile says "like a glance" — but the reality, as the early community understood it, *exceeds* the glance. So the comparison was never a measurement. It was a **concession**. Allah reached for the fastest thing in our experience not because His command matches it, but because our experience contains nothing faster to point at. The glance is not the ceiling of the command's speed. It is the ceiling of *our comprehension*.

The simile is Allah stooping to the limits of the human eye — the same eye, *al-baṣar*, that the ayah names — to describe something the eye could never clock. Al-Muyassar captures the spirit: the thing is, *and not even the blink of an eye intervenes*.

So here is what this single phrase reveals: even when Allah describes Himself to us, the description is mercy before it is information. He measures His speech to the size of our seeing.

### The Bridge

Look at what the language has shown us, all together now. A sentence about action with no verb — because the command does not unfold in time. A restriction structure guarding a single word, "one," whose noun is deliberately withheld — because the command's oneness overflows every noun. And a simile of the glance that the early commentators tell us the reality outstrips — because our experience has no faster thing to offer.

Three constructions, one message: **divine action has no process.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About your waiting. About your fear. About the Hour.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Progress Bar That Isn't There

Let me start somewhere very far from seventh-century Makkah: the loading screen.

You know the progress bar. The thin line creeping across the screen — 40%, 60%, 80%. Here's something engineers have admitted openly: many progress bars are not measuring anything. They are animations. The system shows you a bar filling gradually because humans *distrust instant results*. If the task completed in a flash, you'd suspect it hadn't really run. So the machine performs slowness — fakes a process — to make the result feel earned, feel *real*.

Sit with that, because it exposes something deep in us. **We believe in process. We believe duration is what makes things real.** Real change is slow change. Real healing takes years. Real transformation has stages, milestones, percentages. Anything instant feels cheap, suspicious, impossible.

And then this ayah walks in and says: the deepest layer of reality has no progress bar. *Wa mā amrunā illā wāḥidah.* The command is one. No stages. No percentages. No bar creeping toward completion.

Now place this ayah next to its neighbor, because Allah placed them next to each other deliberately. Ayah 49: "Indeed, all things We created **with qadar** — with precise measure." Ayah 50: "And Our command is but one, like the glance of an eye."

Feel the tension between them? Qadar speaks of measure, proportion, intricacy — everything calibrated, everything in its decreed place and time. That *sounds* like process. That sounds like the longest project imaginable. And the very next breath says: and the command behind all of it — **one**. Like a glance.

Here is the paradox, and I won't rush to resolve it: the universe *experiences* unfolding — seasons turn, children grow, civilizations rise across centuries — while the command *behind* the unfolding never unfolds at all. The slowness is real from where we stand. And from where He commands — there is no "from where." One word. Both are true. The Quran lets them sit side by side, ayah 49 and ayah 50, and asks us to hold both.

Now bring this home, to the place where you actually live this tension: **the wait.**

You have made a du'a — maybe for years. Healing that hasn't come. A door that hasn't opened. A heart — maybe your own — that hasn't softened. And somewhere in the long middle of the waiting, a quiet, corrosive thought forms: *this is taking so long because it is hard.* Hard for the situation to change. Complicated. Too many moving parts. Maybe — whisper it — too hard even for Allah to untangle quickly.

This ayah reaches into that exact thought and breaks it.

Nothing is "taking long" because it is difficult. Difficulty would require process, and the command has none. The sea splitting for Musa and the kettle boiling in your kitchen are not different sizes of task on Allah's side — *mā amrunā illā wāḥidah* admits no sizes. One word covers both. When the answer to your du'a arrives in its appointed moment, it will not arrive panting from a long journey. It will arrive the way sight arrives at the mountain: instantly, the moment the glance is cast.

So what is the waiting, then? The waiting is qadar — ayah 49 — the measured wisdom of *when*. It is never the command struggling. The delay lives entirely in the measure, never in the power. And that changes the texture of waiting itself: you are not waiting for Allah to *manage* to do something. You are waiting for the moment His wisdom has already chosen — and when that moment comes, the distance between "not yet" and "done" will be thinner than a blink.

**What this changes tomorrow:** the next time the wait grows heavy, catch the thought *"it's too complicated"* — and replace it with this ayah's arithmetic. There is no complicated. There is only *not yet* — and *one word*. Make your du'a with the confidence of someone asking the One for whom granting it costs less than a glance costs you.

### Theme Two: The Phone Call

Begin again where you live.

Everyone past a certain age carries one of these memories. An ordinary afternoon — errands, emails, nothing. Then the phone rings. And in the time it takes to register a voice saying the words it is saying, your life divides into *before* and *after*. A diagnosis. An accident. A loss. People who have lived through such a call all describe it the same way: *everything changed in a second.* The world that took decades to build — routines, plans, assumptions — was rearranged in the duration of a glance.

We know this about life. We've all either lived it or stood next to someone living it. And yet we walk around structurally convinced it won't happen *today* — that the big interruptions send advance notice, that there will be a ramp, a warning period, a progress bar.

Now hear who this ayah was first aimed at. The Makkans demanding a schedule for the Hour. *When? Give us the date. Show us the build-up.* And al-Baghawi preserves a second report attributed to Ibn Abbas — through al-Kalbi — reading the ayah exactly along this line: *"Our command for the coming of the Hour, in its speed, is like the flicker of sight."*

Do you see what the ayah does to their question? They asked *when*, assuming the Hour was an event like other events — something that approaches, that casts a shadow ahead of itself, that you can watch coming and adjust for. The ayah dissolves the assumption underneath the question. The Hour does not approach the way a caravan approaches, visible on the horizon for hours. It arrives the way a glance arrives: **there is no "almost."** One moment the world; the next moment the command — and no moment between them in which to become a different person.

And the surah has already shown us this in flesh and stone. Remember Thamud: *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* — one blast — "and they became like dry stubble" (54:31). The surah planted the word *wāḥidah* in a story before placing it in a principle. Their years of mockery, their society, their carved homes in the mountains — and the transition out of all of it took *one*. Our ayah is that story, universalized: what "one blast" was for Thamud, "one word" is for everything.

Here is the uncomfortable depth, so let's not flinch from it: **the glance-speed of the command means the person you are at the arrival is the person you answer as.** No ramp. No grace period appended at the end. The mockers' plan — believe later, repent at the visible approach — requires an approach that is visible. The ayah cancels the visibility. Whatever preparation happens must happen *now*, in the long, slow, qadar-paced meanwhile — because the transition itself will not be long enough to say one extra word in.

But don't leave this theme in fear, because the same blade cuts the other way — and this is where the ayah turns to face the believer with mercy.

If the world can change against you in a glance — it can change *for* you in a glance. The same instant that ends the heedless world answers the desperate du'a. Rescue is also *wāḥidah*. Forgiveness is also *wāḥidah*. The Quran says of the Hour's nearness elsewhere that the matter of the Hour is "but as a glance of the eye, or even nearer" (16:77) — and the believers' relief, when it comes, comes by the same economy: one word. The night that took years to descend can lift before morning. Nothing in your life is more than one *kun* away from transformation — and that is not a motivational slogan; it is the plain grammar of this ayah.

**What this changes tomorrow:** live with the glance in both directions. In the direction of accountability: ask, tonight, *if the transition came mid-sentence, mid-scroll, mid-grudge — is this the sentence, the scroll, the grudge I want to be caught holding?* And in the direction of hope: name the situation you've quietly filed under "permanent" — and unfile it. Nothing is permanent in a world governed by a one-word command. The only permanent things are the ones *He* has said are permanent.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now step back and look at the whole surah from above — because the placement of this ayah is itself a discovery.

Surah Al-Qamar is built from long stories and short executions. Nuh's people: generations of preaching — then the floodgates, and it's over. 'Aad: a screaming wind — and they're felled like hollow palm trunks. Thamud: *one blast.* Lut's people: a single dawn. Pharaoh's house: seized in one overwhelming grasp. Every story has the same shape — **a long, patient lead-up and an instantaneous end.** Process on the human side; no process on the divine side.

Then, at the close, the surah states its own pattern as a pair of principles, back to back:

**54:49** — everything by qadar: *the long, measured lead-up.*
**54:50** — the command is one, like a glance: *the instantaneous execution.*

The two ayahs are the surah's five stories, distilled. And the word that links them — *wāḥidah* — was planted in the Thamud story (54:31) precisely so that when you reach 54:50, your ear says: *I've heard that word before. I've seen what "one" does.* The surah taught you the word in a story before asking you to believe it as a doctrine. That is the architecture: narrative first, principle after — soil before seed.

### Bringing It Together

And now see how the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight approaching from two directions.

The grammar removed the verb — and the theme told you why: the command has no process, so your waiting is never Allah "working on it." The grammar withheld the noun after "one" — and the theme told you why: the oneness covers every category, the sea and the kettle, the blast and the blessing. The simile of the glance was a concession to the eye — and the theme told you why it matters: because the transition it describes is too fast for the eye it was scaled down for, too fast to repent inside, too fast to despair correctly about. Language and life, saying one thing:

**Process belongs to creation. Instantaneity belongs to the Command.**

You live inside the qadar. You are governed by the *wāḥidah*.

### Questions to Carry

2. If the transition out of this world takes less than a glance, what am I holding right now — a grudge, a postponed tawbah, an unspoken apology — that I am gambling I'll have time to put down?
3. What have I labeled "permanent" that this ayah just unlabeled?
4. When I make du'a tonight, will I ask like someone petitioning an overloaded bureaucracy — or like someone addressing the One whose entire response costs one word?
5. The Quran "made easy for remembrance" asks five times in this surah: *is there anyone who will remember?* Tonight — is there?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Everything you experience as slow is held by a command that has never taken time at all — so the delay is always wisdom, never difficulty, and the change, when it comes, will be faster than your eye.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, take one wait — the heaviest one — and carry it back to Allah in different words than usual. Not "Ya Allah, this is so hard." But: *"Ya Allah, I know this is not hard for You. It is one word for You. So I am no longer asking You to manage it — I am asking You to say it, and I will wait inside Your measure until You do."* See what that does to the weight.

### Du'a

*O Allah, whose command is one word and faster than our seeing — we hand You every door that has stayed shut and every night that has stayed long, knowing none of it is heavy for You. Make us ready for the glance in which everything changes, and when Your "Be" comes for what we have begged You for, let it find us still trusting, still asking, still among those who remembered.*

*Ameen.*
