# The Horse and the Heart

## On the Ingratitude We Witness in Ourselves

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> [ayah:100:6] إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ لِرَبِّهِۦ لَكَنُودٌ
> *Inna l-insāna li-Rabbihi la-kanūd*
>
> [ayah:100:7] وَإِنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ ذَٰلِكَ لَشَهِيدٌ
> *Wa-innahu ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd*
>
> [ayah:100:8] وَإِنَّهُۥ لِحُبِّ ٱلْخَيْرِ لَشَدِيدٌ
> *Wa-innahu li-ḥubbi l-khayri la-shadīd*

And in English, as closely as English can carry it:

> *Indeed, mankind is, toward his Lord, deeply ungrateful. And indeed, he is, to that, a witness. And indeed he is, in his love of wealth, intense.*

Three short verses. Thirteen words. And in those thirteen words sits one of the most precise diagnoses of the human heart anywhere in human language.

But to feel why these verses land the way they do, we have to back up. Because these three verses do not begin the surah. They *answer* it.

### Rebuild the world

Imagine the desert before dawn. The sky is still dark, that cold blue-grey before the sun. And then — sound. Hooves. Not the gentle plod of a camel caravan, but the thunder of warhorses at full gallop. Allah opens this surah with an oath, and He swears not by the rider, not by the warrior, not by the sword. He swears by the *horses*.

*Wal-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥā* — by the runners, panting. You can hear it: *ḍabḥā* — the rough, heaving breath of an animal that has thrown its whole body into the run. *Fal-mūriyāti qadḥā* — striking fire, sparks flying as iron-hard hooves strike stone in the dark. *Fal-mughīrāti ṣubḥā* — charging at dawn. *Fa-atharna bihi naqʿā* — and they kick up a storm of dust. *Fa-wasaṭna bihi jamʿā* — and they plunge, all of them, right into the heart of the enemy gathering.

[PAUSE]

Sit in that scene for a moment. What is the horse *doing*? It is giving everything. Lungs burning, hooves striking sparks, body hurled into danger it does not understand and gains nothing from. The horse has no stake in the battle. It will not collect the spoils. It does not hate the enemy. And yet it spends itself completely — out of nothing but loyalty to the one on its back, the one who fed it, raised it, trained it.

Five verses of pure, breathless devotion. An animal, with no intellect, no scripture, no promise of paradise — pouring out its entire self in faithfulness.

And then the oath lands. Allah has built this whole thundering scene for one purpose: to set up a mirror. And He turns it on us.

*Inna l-insāna li-Rabbihi la-kanūd.*

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us cold. The horse — which has so little — gives so much. And man — who has been given *everything* — is described, by his Maker, in a single word so heavy that the classical scholars argued for centuries over how much darkness it contains.

What is that word? And why, of all the things Allah could say about us first, did He choose to begin with our ingratitude — and then immediately add that *we already know it*?

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that the scholars could not exhaust — *kanūd* (كَنُود)

Before we open this word, I want to teach you something about how Arabic builds intensity — because without it, *kanūd* will look like an ordinary word, and it is anything but.

In Arabic, you can take a simple meaning and *pour more of it in* by changing the shape of the word. There is a family of patterns called **ṣiyagh al-mubālagha** — the forms of exaggeration, of intensity. Think of the difference in English between someone who *lies* and a *liar* — and then a *compulsive liar*. Same root act, but the word has swollen to describe not an action but an entire character, a settled nature.

One of those intensive patterns in Arabic is **فَعُول** (*faʿūl*). When you cast a meaning into this mold, you are no longer describing something that happens once. You are describing what someone *is*, deeply and by habit.

Now hold that, and look at the word Allah chose:

> **لَكَنُودٌ** — *la-kanūd*

The root is **ك-ن-د** (k-n-d), and the word sits in exactly that intensive mold, *faʿūl*. So before we even ask what it means, the *shape* is already telling us: this is not a passing mood. This is a disposition. A grain in the wood.

Now — what does the root itself carry? Here is where it becomes remarkable, because the classical lexicographers and mufassirūn did not give us one clean definition. They gave us a cluster, and every angle adds a layer.

Let's sit with them, one at a time.

**Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid, and Qatāda** — as al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both report — said *kanūd* means **kafūr jaḥūd** — one who actively denies and covers over the blessings of Allah. Not someone who merely forgets — someone who *denies*. To receive a gift and then behave as though it never came.

**Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī** gave us a definition so psychologically exact it could be lifted straight into a modern textbook. He said *kanūd* is:

> *the one who counts the calamities and forgets the blessings.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. *Counts the misfortunes. Forgets the favors.* We will come back to this — because this is not just a definition of an ancient word. This is a description of the human mind that science only caught up with in the last fifty years.

**Al-Kalbī** noticed something fascinating about dialect. He said that *kanūd*, in the tongue of the tribes of Muḍar and Rabīʿa, means the *ungrateful one* — but in the tongue of Kinda and Ḥaḍramawt, it means the *disobedient one*. So the word holds both: a heart that denies and a limb that rebels. Ingratitude inside, disobedience outside.

**Abū ʿUbayda** opened yet another window. He said *kanūd* describes one who is *qalīl al-khayr* — barren of good — and then he gave the image that I cannot get out of my head. He said: *al-arḍ al-kanūd* — **kanūd land is land that grows nothing.** Soil you pour water into, plant seed into, tend — and it returns you nothing. Dead earth.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? The word for the ungrateful human is the *same word* used for dead soil. A field that takes the rain and gives back no harvest. That is the picture Allah is painting of a heart that receives blessing after blessing and produces no gratitude. Rain falls. Nothing grows.

And there is one more. A narration reported by Abū Umāma al-Bāhilī, in which the Prophet ﷺ was asked about *al-kanūd*, and he described him as the one who *eats alone, withholds his gift, and strikes his servant.* (This is a narration carried in the tafsīr tradition — al-Qurṭubī records it — and you'll find scholars discussing its chain; we take it as illustration, not as a definitive legal text.) But notice the *portrait*: the kanūd is someone who hoards his enjoyment, closes his hand, and is harsh to those beneath him. Ingratitude is never a private feeling. It leaks out into how you treat everyone around you.

So now lay all of these on top of one another — not as competing options, but as a single composite face:

- He **denies** the blessing (Ibn ʿAbbās).
- He **remembers every wound and forgets every gift** (al-Ḥasan).
- He **disobeys** the Giver (al-Kalbī).
- He is **barren soil** — takes everything, returns nothing (Abū ʿUbayda).
- He **hoards, withholds, and is harsh** (the narration of Abū Umāma).

It is worth pausing here on a point of genuine ikhtilāf within the classical tradition. Al-Jalalayn reads *al-insān* in this verse as specifically *al-insān al-kāfir* — the disbeliever who actively rejects Allah's blessings. On that narrowing, the verse's harshest charge falls on outright rejection, not on ordinary human weakness. But the weight of the tradition — visible in al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's universal psychological portrait and in Abū ʿUbayda's image of barren earth — treats the kanūd condition as the baseline of all human souls: a pull that faith does not uproot by decree but must wage daily war against. The believer reading these verses is not exempt from their address. He is instead handed a mirror. The question the verse asks is not "are you a kāfir?" but "how much of this kanūd — honestly, in the quiet — do you find in yourself?"

That is *kanūd*. One word. And it is not in a mild form — it is in the intensive *faʿūl* mold, which means: this is not what man occasionally does. According to the verse, left to his own nature, *this is what man is.*

What this single word reveals: **ingratitude is not an event in the human heart. It is the default setting.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: Where the word is placed — *li-Rabbihi* before *kanūd*

Now I want you to notice not just *what* is said, but the *order* in which it is said. Because Arabic word order is not casual. In Arabic, the normal, expected place for a phrase like "to his Lord" would be *after* the description. You'd expect: *inna l-insāna la-kanūdun li-Rabbihi* — "man is ungrateful, to his Lord."

But that is not what Allah said. He said:

> **إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ لِرَبِّهِۦ لَكَنُودٌ**
> *inna l-insāna **li-Rabbihi** la-kanūd*

He pulled *li-Rabbihi* — "toward his Lord" — *forward*, ahead of the description. In Arabic rhetoric this is called **taqdīm**, the fronting of a word out of its expected slot. And the rule is simple and powerful: **when something is moved forward, it is because attention is being forced onto it.** Fronting creates emphasis, and often restriction.

Let me give you an everyday version. If I say, "He lied to his mother," that's a statement about lying. But if I say, "To his *mother* — to her — he lied," I have just made the whole sentence about *who* he betrayed. The wound is now in the relationship, not just the act.

That is exactly what the fronting of *li-Rabbihi* does here. The verse is not mainly informing you *that* man is ungrateful. It is forcing your eyes onto *the one he is ungrateful toward.*

And now look at the word chosen for that one. Not *Allāh*. Not *al-Khāliq*, the Creator. The word is **Rabbihi** — **his Rabb.**

Let's open that. *Rabb* comes from a root, **r-b-b**, that carries the meaning of nurturing something *from a small, helpless state, stage by stage, all the way to completion.* A *rabb* is the one who takes what is nothing and patiently raises it to fullness. It is the word a child's whole upbringing lives inside. The one who fed you when you could not feed yourself. Who carried you when you could not walk. Who watched over every stage of you becoming you.

And here the classical commentators add a quiet but important clarification about what, precisely, this ingratitude is aimed at. Al-Muyassar, unfolding the terse phrase into plainer words, does not read it as ingratitude toward the Lord's bare essence — he reads it as ingratitude *li-niʿam Rabbihi*, toward *the blessings* of his Lord. He supplies the word the verse leaves unspoken: what man denies and covers over is the unbroken stream of favors. And notice how neatly that closes the circle with the barren soil. The rain that falls on the *kanūd* land *is* the niʿam. Man is the field; the blessings are the water that keeps falling; and the charge of the verse is that the water comes and comes — and nothing grows.

[PAUSE]

So now feel the full weight of the construction. Allah did not say, "Man is ungrateful to a distant deity." He said: man is ungrateful **specifically to the One who raised him from a drop to a person, who sustained every breath, who nurtured him stage by stage to fullness.** And He fronted that relationship so you cannot look away from it.

This is the difference between a stranger forgetting your birthday and your own child forgetting you ever existed. The fronting of *li-Rabbihi* is what turns a fact into a heartbreak.

And there is the buried mirror from the opening of the surah. The horse was loyal to the one who raised and fed it — a mere rider, a mortal owner. Man is *disloyal* to the One who raised and fed him — and that One is his Rabb. The animal honored its small lord. Man dishonors his Great One.

What this placement reveals: **the tragedy is not abstract ingratitude — it is ingratitude aimed precisely at the One whose entire relationship to us is nurture.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The walls of emphasis — and the verse that says "you already know"

Step back and look at the architecture of just the first verse. Arabic has a way of building emphasis like stacking stones, and here Allah stacks them three high:

1. **إِنَّ** (*inna*) — "Indeed," the particle of certainty that opens the sentence.
2. **لَ** (the *lām* of emphasis) — fused onto *kanūd* as *la-kanūd*, a second confirmation.
3. **كَنُود** itself in the intensive *faʿūl* mold — the meaning already maximized.

Three layers of stress around one diagnosis. *Inna* … *la-* … and the intensive form. It is as if Allah is saying: *do not soften this, do not explain it away, do not tell yourself "not me." This is firm. This is true. This is heavy.*

Now — a person might hear this and protest: "But surely we don't *mean* to be ungrateful. We just forget. We get distracted. We're not *aware* of it." And Allah, in the very next verse, closes that door before we can walk through it:

> **وَإِنَّهُۥ عَلَىٰ ذَٰلِكَ لَشَهِيدٌ**
> *wa-innahu ʿalā dhālika la-shahīd*
> "And indeed, he is, to that, a witness."

The word is **shahīd** (شهيد), from the root **sh-h-d** — to witness, to testify, to see with your own eyes and be able to swear to it. A *shahīd* is not someone who heard a rumor. A *shahīd* is the eyewitness in the courtroom whose testimony settles the matter because *they were there.*

And notice — this word too is in an intensive form, *faʿīl*. Now read the two verses together with their matching emphasis:

- *la-**kanūd*** — he is deeply ungrateful.
- *la-**shahīd*** — he is fully a witness *to* it.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? Allah did not only say we are ungrateful. He said **we are eyewitnesses to our own ingratitude.** Some of the mufassirūn read the witness here as man himself — he testifies against his own soul, because deep down he *knows*. Others read it as Allah being witness over man's state — and the ambiguity is itself a mercy and a warning: whether it is your own conscience or your Lord's gaze, *the ingratitude is seen.* It is not hidden, not even from you.

This is the cruelest and most honest part of the diagnosis. We are not ungrateful *by accident*, like someone who genuinely never knew. Somewhere inside, in the quiet, when the noise dies down — we know. We know how much we've been given. We know how little we return. The verse simply names what the conscience already whispers.

Al-Jalalayn, commenting on this verse, sharpens the precise mode of the testimony: man witnesses against himself *bi-ṣunʿihi* — through his own conduct, through the texture of his deeds. It is not only the still voice of conscience speaking privately in the dark. The testimony is enacted in the open — written into every moment when gratitude was the available response and was passed over anyway. The pattern of choices is itself the evidence filed in the courtroom. Al-Muyassar captures this from a complementary angle, rendering the verse's meaning as man being *muqirr* — a confessor, one who formally acknowledges. In the Islamic legal tradition, *iqrār* carries full evidentiary weight on its own; it needs no corroboration from outside. Man's acknowledgment of his own ingratitude is not extracted under pressure. In the honest moments, when the noise is gone, it rises almost voluntarily.

What these emphatic walls reveal: **our ingratitude is not innocent forgetting. It is a thing we do with the lights on.**

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have built. *Kanūd* told us ingratitude is our default nature — deep as the grain in wood, dead as barren soil. The fronting of *li-Rabbihi* told us the tragedy is not against a stranger but against the very One whose whole relationship to us is raising us into existence. And the double emphasis of *shahīd* told us we are not doing this in the dark — the testimony runs through our deeds, the confession surfaces in our quietest moments, and it is seen.

Three verses, and they have not yet finished.

Because there is one more piece of the architecture. One more verse. And it answers a question we didn't know we were asking: *why?*

Why does man do this? What is the mechanism behind the kanūd? And the third verse — built with exactly the same triple architecture of *inna*, the *lām* of emphasis, and the intensive mold — does not soften the answer.

## PART 2: THE THIRD VERSE

### Element Four: *Shadīd* and the real meaning of *al-khayr*

> **وَإِنَّهُۥ لِحُبِّ ٱلْخَيْرِ لَشَدِيدٌ**
> *wa-innahu li-ḥubbi l-khayri la-shadīd*

The triple architecture is still there. *Inna* — indeed. The *lām* fused onto *shadīd* — another layer of emphasis. And *shadīd*, from the root **ش-د-د** (sh-d-d): to be firm, tight, fierce, severe. In its intensive *faʿīl* form, it describes not a momentary flare of feeling but a gripping, habitual intensity — the quality of a man who is not merely fond of something but *seized* by it. *Shadīd* is the word you reach for when the attachment has become structural, when it runs all the way down.

And what is he seized by? **لِحُبِّ ٱلْخَيْرِ** — by his love of *al-khayr*.

Now here is a question worth sitting with. The word *al-khayr* in Arabic means *good* — goodness, benefit, excellence. So why have the translators rendered it as "wealth"?

Because this is one of those places where the Quran's word choice is doing something very precise, and the classical scholars were unanimous in noticing it. Al-Muyassar, reading this verse, does not say man loves *al-khayr* in some abstract sense. He says man is intensely attached to *al-māl* — to wealth, property, money. That substitution is not arbitrary. It reflects a sustained observation across the tafsīr tradition: when *al-khayr* appears in Quranic contexts of accumulation and the pull of the dunya, the referent is wealth. The same usage appears in 2:180, where *al-khayr* unambiguously means the property one leaves behind at death.

[PAUSE]

And you can feel why Allah chose *al-khayr* rather than simply *al-māl*. If He had said "man fiercely loves money," you and I would nod and feel slightly superior — because surely we are not *crude* enough to simply love money. But *al-khayr*? "The good"? That is exactly how the human heart actually frames its attachment to wealth. It does not say to itself: *I love my accumulation more than I love my Rabb.* It says: *I am pursuing what is good — for me, for my family, for my future.* The word *al-khayr* captures the self-justification baked into the attachment. The heart wraps its gripping love in the language of virtue, calls it prudence, calls it care for dependents, calls it building something lasting — and this is precisely what the verse is diagnosing.

Now notice — the phrase is fronted again. *Li-ḥubbi l-khayri* comes before *shadīd*, just as *li-Rabbihi* came before *kanūd* in verse 6. The same rhetorical move, pointed in the opposite direction:

> Toward his **Rabb** — *kanūd.* Weak.

> Toward his **love of wealth** — *shadīd.* Fierce.

[PAUSE]

This is the mechanism the third verse is revealing. Man is not kanūd for no reason. He is kanūd *because* he is shadīd. The heart that is locked in habitual, gripping attachment to the accumulation of this world simply has no room left for the orientation that gratitude requires. They are competing pulls: the tighter the grip on the gift, the more the Giver recedes from the heart's awareness. You cannot be simultaneously *shadīd* in clinging to what you have been given and *shākir* — radically grateful — toward the One who gave it. The two states displace one another.

This is why the third verse is not an afterthought or a separate observation. It is the *explanation*. Verse 6 stated the disease. Verse 7 said you already know it — through your deeds, through your own admission. Verse 8 says: here is the root.

What this verse reveals: **man is not ungrateful by accident. He is ungrateful because his fierce love of what he calls "the good" has quietly occupied the place where gratitude toward the Giver should live.**

## PART 3: THE PORTRAIT COMPLETED

### Three verses, one circuit

Step back now and look at the full picture these three verses have assembled.

Verse 6: Man is *kanūd* toward his Rabb — intensely, habitually ungrateful, aimed precisely at the One who nurtured him into existence, whose every relationship to man is the relationship of a nurturer to what he has raised. And this ingratitude is not passive forgetting. It is active, grained into the character, barren earth that takes the rain and returns nothing.

Verse 7: And he is *shahīd* — a witness, an acknowledger — to this very condition. His own deeds testify against him. His own conscience, in the honest moments, admits it. The testimony is not hidden from him; it is enacted in his conduct, and surfaces almost on its own when the noise dies down.

Verse 8: And the cause is *shadīd* love of *al-khayr* — a fierce, habitual grip on wealth, which he calls "the good," which crowds out the gratitude that should flow toward the One from whom all of it came.

Read together, these three verses trace a complete circuit — not just a diagnosis but a mechanism:

*The fierce love of the gift drives out the gratitude toward the Giver, and man knows it, and does it anyway.*

### Al-Ḥasan's observation and what science found

Recall what al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī said: *kanūd* is the one who counts the calamities and forgets the blessings. This is not poetic license. In the last century, researchers studying human cognition identified something they called the "negativity bias" — the brain's documented tendency to register losses, threats, and harms more strongly and durably than equal-magnitude gifts and gains. It is wired in, they argue, because in ancestral environments, missing a threat was fatal, while missing a blessing was merely unfortunate.

What al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī named in the second century of Islam, and what the Quran named before him, is the same structural feature of the human mind: left to its defaults, it will organize itself around what went wrong. The misfortune is counted and filed. The blessing fades. And this is not a defect peculiar to bad people — it is the baseline condition of the human soul, the very condition the Quran calls *kanūd*, and it is what gratitude as a *practice* — not merely a feeling, but an act performed against a natural gradient — must constantly push against.

This is also why *shukr* in the Islamic tradition is not mere acknowledgment. It is effort. The Prophet ﷺ would stand in the night prayer until his feet swelled, and when ʿĀʾisha asked why he exerted himself so — given that Allah had forgiven whatever was past and whatever was to come — he said: *"Should I not be a grateful servant?"* — *a-fa-lā akūna ʿabdan shakūrā?* The *shakūr* — the intensely, habitually grateful one — is the mold in which the antidote to the kanūd is cast. Both words are intensive forms. Both describe what someone *is*, not what they occasionally do. The question these three verses leave with us is which one we are building, day by day, with or against the current of the default.

[PAUSE]

### The horse returns

And now, finally, we can look at what the oath was doing.

Allah did not swear by the horse to admire it. He swore by it to build the mirror.

The horse pours everything into service — not for its own gain, not for any reward it can collect, not because it understands the battle or negotiated the terms. It does so because it was raised in a bond of trust and responds to that bond with its whole body. Lungs burning. Sparks flying from stone. Into the dark, into the dust, into the heart of the enemy gathering.

And then the camera cuts to man.

Who was raised — not by a mortal rider, but by the Rabb of all worlds. Whose every breath was a sustained act of divine nurture. Whose very capacity to love *al-khayr* with such fierce intensity was itself a gift from the same Rabb he neglects. And who spends the whole of that *shadīd* — that fierce, gripping energy — on the accumulation of what he calls "the good," while the One who gave him everything barely receives a backward glance.

The horse is *shadīd* in its loyalty. Man is *shadīd* in his love of his own goods. The same intensity. The same mold. Opposite directions.

[PAUSE]

This is what makes the surah's structure so devastating. It is not an accusation lobbed from outside. It is a mirror, built from five verses of pure animal devotion, angled precisely so that we see ourselves in comparison. And the question it leaves — unspoken in these three verses but pressing right through them into the verses that follow — is not "are you guilty?"

The question is: *what would it mean to be the horse?*

## CLOSING

These three verses are the jawāb al-qasam — the answer to the oath. But they answer with a question. If you want to know why Allah swore by the warhorses panting in the dark, striking sparks from stone, hurling themselves into the battle for a master who gave them nothing but their care — now you know. The oath was a setup. The setup was a mirror. And the mirror is asking whether the creature who has language and consciousness and scripture and *iqrār* — who already *knows*, whose very deeds testify against him — will turn itself, finally, in the direction of the One who raised it.

The surah will go on. The graves will be opened. The contents of the chests will be laid bare. And the Rabb who nurtured man from nothing will be *khabīr* — fully, intimately informed — of what man chose to do with what he was given.

These three verses live just before that reckoning. They are not the threat. They are the mercy that precedes it: the invitation, right now, while the kanūd can still become the shakūr, while the heart that clutches *al-khayr* so fiercely can still loosen its grip and turn — to look at the horse, and look at yourself, and choose.
