# The Horse That Held Nothing Back

## Surah Al-Adiyat, verses 1–5

> [ayah:100:1] وَٱلْعَـٰدِيَـٰتِ ضَبْحًا
> [ayah:100:2] فَٱلْمُورِيَـٰتِ قَدْحًا
> [ayah:100:3] فَٱلْمُغِيرَٰتِ صُبْحًا
> [ayah:100:4] فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِۦ نَقْعًا
> [ayah:100:5] فَوَسَطْنَ بِهِۦ جَمْعًا

*Wal-ʿādiyāti ḍabḥā. Fal-mūriyāti qadḥā. Fal-mughīrāti ṣubḥā. Fa-atharna bihī naqʿā. Fa-wasaṭna bihī jamʿā.*

**By the chargers that run panting, striking sparks of fire from the rock, raiding at the break of dawn, kicking up a cloud of dust, and plunging into the very heart of the gathered host.**

---

## Introduction: Stand on the Ridge Before the Light Comes

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand somewhere.

It is the last hour of the night. The kind of darkness that is not black but a deep, cold grey, the hour the Arabs called the time when you cannot yet tell a white thread from a black one. You are on a low ridge of broken ground. Below you, in a hollow, an enemy camp sleeps — fires burned down to embers, sentries dozing, the whole gathered host of them unaware. They think the dark is protecting them.

And behind you, breathing, are the horses.

Not standing still. You can hear them. A row of warhorses, reined back hard, every muscle wound like a spring, and from their chests comes a sound — not a neigh, not a snort exactly — a deep, rhythmic, rasping *huff* as the air is forced through them. They are straining against the bit. They want to go. They have been bred for ten thousand years for this one moment, and they know it is coming.

Then the rider lets go.

And the whole world becomes motion. Hooves come down on flint and stone so hard and so fast that **sparks fly into the dark** — actual fire, struck from the ground by iron-hard hooves. The grey light cracks open into dawn at the exact moment the line of horses crests the ridge. A wall of dust rises behind them like a curtain being torn. And then they are *inside* the camp, in the very middle of the gathered enemy, before a single sleeping man has reached for his sword.

[PAUSE]

That is the scene. Five verses. Eleven words. One unbroken cinematic shot, from the held breath in the dark to the explosion in the centre of the camp.

Now here is the thing I need you to hold onto, because everything depends on it.

**Allah is swearing an oath.** These five verses are not a description for description's sake. They are *Allah taking a vow* — the way a person says "I swear by my mother" — except the One swearing is the Lord of all the worlds, and what He chooses to swear *by* is this: a horse, charging, holding nothing back.

When Allah swears by something, He is lifting it up in front of you and saying: *look at this. Look carefully. I am calling this thing to witness.*

So the question that will pull us all the way through tonight is this one: **Why would the Lord of the universe raise His hand and swear by a sweating, panting, half-wild warhorse?** What is this creature being called to witness against? What did it do — what did it give — that you and I are failing to give?

Hold the question. We are going to earn the answer.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Sound That *Is* the Meaning — ضَبْحًا (Ḍabḥā)

Let me teach you something about language first, with no Arabic at all.

There is a small family of words in every human language that do not just *describe* a sound — they *are* the sound. In English: *buzz. Hiss. Thud. Crackle. Whoosh.* You don't have to define "buzz" to a child. The word does the work in your mouth. Your tongue and lips actually perform a small version of the thing. Linguists call this *onomatopoeia* — when the body of the word imitates the body of the meaning.

Now hold that idea, and look at the very first thing Allah tells you about these horses. Not their colour. Not their number. Not their riders. The first thing is a *sound*:

**ضَبْحًا** — *ḍabḥā.*

Say it. Slowly. *Ḍabḥ.* Feel where it lives. It does not live on your lips. It lives at the **back of the throat** — that heavy, emphatic *ḍād* (a sound Arabic is so uniquely known for that Arabic itself is called *lughat al-ḍād*, "the language of the ḍād"), and then the *ḥā*, a breath pushed out from deep in the chest. *Ḍ-a-ḥ.* It is a word you cannot say gently. It forces air out of you.

And what does it mean?

The classical scholars are remarkably specific here. Qatāda said: *taḍbaḥ idhā ʿadat* — the horse makes this sound *when it runs.* Al-Farrāʾ, the great grammarian, defined it precisely: **الضبح صوت أنفاس الخيل إذا عدون** — *ḍabḥ is the sound of the horses' breathing when they gallop.* It is the panting itself. The forced, rhythmic heave of breath from the chest of an animal running at the absolute edge of its capacity.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with what Allah has done.

He had countless ways to begin. He could have said "by the swift horses," "by the noble horses," "by the horses of war." Instead, the very first sound to reach your ear is a word **that sounds exactly like an exhausted animal forcing breath through its chest.** *Ḍabḥā.* The word pants. You cannot pronounce it without doing, in miniature, the very thing it names.

Notice something even Ibn ʿAbbās paused over. He observed that *almost nothing in creation makes this particular sound* — he named the horse, and a couple of other creatures, as the only ones. And the lexicographers added something quietly devastating: the root *ḍ-b-ḥ* is connected to the idea of something being *altered or scorched by fire* — they would say *ḍabaḥat-hu al-nār*, "the fire changed its colour." The panting is not casual breathing. **It is the sound of a body being changed by exertion.** A body running so hard that its very breath has become audible evidence of how much it is spending.

The first word, and you already know: this creature is holding *nothing* back.

Stay with this, because there is one more layer, and it runs underneath the entire surah.

## Element Two: The Drumbeat of the Endings — The Hammering of *-an*

Read the five verses out loud, and listen not to the meaning but to the *rhythm* of how each one ends:

ḍabḥ**ā** · qadḥ**ā** · ṣubḥ**ā** · naqʿ**ā** · jamʿ**ā**

Five words. Five identical endings. That long, open *-ā* sound (in Arabic, the *tanwīn* of the accusative — *ḍabḥan, qadḥan, ṣubḥan, naqʿan, jamʿan*) falling at the close of every single line like a hoof striking the ground.

[PAUSE]

I want you to feel what is happening, because it is one of the most extraordinary marriages of sound and sense in the entire Qur'an.

You are reciting a surah **about galloping horses** — and the recitation itself *gallops.* Each verse is short, hard, and ends on the same percussive beat, so that when you say them in sequence — *ḍabḥā, qadḥā, ṣubḥā, naqʿā, jamʿā* — your own mouth produces the rhythm of hooves at full charge. **Da-DAH. Da-DAH. Da-DAH.** The form of the words *performs the meaning of the words.* The surah does not describe a cavalry charge from the outside. It puts you on the horse.

This is what I mean when I say, in this tradition, that the sound is never decoration. Here the rhythm is *load-bearing.* If Allah had revealed the same five facts in long, flowing, gentle sentences, you would have *understood* the charge. But you would not have *felt* it in your chest the way you feel it when these five hammer-blows land one after another. The meaning is delivered not only to your mind through definitions, but to your body through rhythm.

And ask yourself *why.* Why make you feel the charge in your body? Because the entire point of this surah, as we will see, is about what a body *gives* — total, spent, holding-nothing-back exertion. Allah is not going to *tell* you about total exertion as an abstraction. He is going to make your own tongue gallop until you feel, physically, what it is to pour everything out. The form is preparing your heart for the argument.

## Element Three: The Camera That Stops Describing and Starts *Acting* — From Participle to Verb

Here is the most subtle thing in the passage, and it rewards every second we spend on it. To see it, I need to teach you one small piece of grammar — gently, with an everyday example.

In Arabic — and in English — there is a difference between a word that *describes an ongoing quality* and a word that *reports a completed action.*

Think of the difference between calling someone **"a runner"** and saying **"she ran."**

"A runner" is a description. It is a standing, ongoing identity — *the running one.* It hovers. It doesn't tell you when. Arabic has a special form for exactly this: the *active participle*, the *ism al-fāʿil* — "the one who does X."

"She ran," on the other hand, is a *finished event.* It happened. It is done. It is a verb in the past tense — what Arabic calls the *fiʿl māḍī*.

Now watch the camera move through these five verses.

The first three verses are all **participles** — ongoing descriptions, "the ones who…":

- **الْعَادِيَات** (*al-ʿādiyāt*) — "the charging ones" (root *ʿ-d-w*, to run, to gallop)
- **الْمُورِيَات** (*al-mūriyāt*) — "the spark-striking ones" (the ones who strike fire from stone with their hooves)
- **الْمُغِيرَات** (*al-mughīrāt*) — "the raiding ones" (the ones who launch the dawn raid)

Three frames, all held in that hovering, ongoing, *cinematic-slow-motion* tense. The charging. The sparking. The raiding. You are watching it unfold, suspended, as if in slow motion. *The ones who run… the ones who spark… the ones who raid…*

And then — verse four — **the grammar breaks into a sprint.** The participles stop, and suddenly you get a *verb, past tense, completed action*:

- **فَأَثَرْنَ** (*fa-atharna*) — "**then they kicked up** [the dust]" — done. It happened.
- **فَوَسَطْنَ** (*fa-wasaṭna*) — "**then they plunged into** the very middle" — done. They are inside.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the gear-change?

For three verses, the camera describes — it lingers, it characterizes, it holds the horses in a state, a quality, an identity: *the chargers, the spark-strikers, the raiders.* And then, exactly at the moment of impact, **the language stops describing and starts** ***doing.*** "They kicked up the dust. They plunged into the centre." Past tense. Finished. *It is over. They are already inside the camp.*

This is the genius of it. The shift from the hovering participle to the snapped-off past-tense verb *is the arrival itself.* Grammatically, you have been watching in suspended slow-motion — and then time collapses, the action completes, and before you can brace for it, the horses are in the middle of the enemy. **The grammar accelerates faster than you can follow** — which is *precisely* what a cavalry charge does to the men it hits.

And notice the little particle gluing each frame to the next: **فَ** (*fa-*). Not *wa* ("and"), which simply lists. *Fa-* means "and then — immediately, as a consequence." *Fal-mūriyāt… fal-mughīrāt… fa-atharna… fa-wasaṭna.* And then, and then, and then — each frame triggering the next with no gap, no pause, no breath. The very particles are sprinting.

Three participles holding the breath. Two verbs that are the impact. The grammar itself is the charge.

### The Bridge

Step back and look at what these three discoveries have shown us.

The **sound** of the words pants and gallops. The **rhythm** of the endings beats like hooves. The **grammar** holds you in slow motion and then collapses into the impact. Every single linguistic tool in the passage is bent toward one effect: to make you *feel*, in your own body, a creature spending itself completely — breath, muscle, fire, dust, and finally its whole self thrown into the deadliest place on the field, the centre of the enemy.

The words have done their work. Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about language, but about *you.* Because remember the question we have been carrying: **why is Allah swearing by this animal at all?**

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Creature That Withheld Nothing — and the One Who Withholds Everything

Let me begin not in seventh-century Arabia, but somewhere you already live.

Think about the last time you watched someone give *everything* to something. A mother awake for the fourth night with a sick newborn, running on no sleep, no thanks, no recognition — just giving, because the giving is who she is now. An athlete in the final hundred metres, lungs on fire, who somehow finds *more* and empties the tank completely, leaving nothing in reserve. A rescue worker going back into a collapsing building one more time.

There is something in us that goes quiet in front of that. We recognize it as *sacred.* Total exertion. Holding nothing back. The complete pouring-out of a self into a purpose.

Now — here is what Allah does. He takes the purest image of that total pouring-out He can find, and He builds it for you in five verses. The warhorse.

Think about what this horse is *doing* across these verses. It runs until its very breath becomes an audible, scraping sound (*ḍabḥā*). It drives its hooves into stone so hard that **its own body strikes fire** (*qadḥā*). It charges, at dawn, *toward* armed men — not away from danger but straight into it (*ṣubḥā*). It throws up a wall of dust with the violence of its motion (*naqʿā*). And then it does the thing no instinct of self-preservation should ever allow: **it plunges into the exact centre of the gathered enemy** (*jamʿā*) — the single most lethal place on the entire battlefield.

[PAUSE]

This animal is giving *everything.* Its breath, its strength, its body, and finally its very life — thrown into the deadliest spot on earth.

And now ask the question that the surah is quietly setting up: **Why? Why does the horse do this?**

Not for itself. The horse has no quarrel with the men in that camp. The horse will gain nothing from the raid — no spoils, no land, no honour. The horse does it for **one reason only: because its master asked.** Out of loyalty to the human being on its back, this creature overrides its own deepest instinct for survival and pours its entire self out.

Hold that. Now turn the page — because the very next verse, verse 6, is where the oath finally lands. After five verses of this magnificent, self-spending loyalty, Allah delivers the thing He swore the whole oath *for*:

**إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لِرَبِّهِۦ لَكَنُودٌ**
*"Indeed, the human being is, to his Lord, ungrateful."*

[PAUSE]

There it is. *That* is why He swore by the horse.

Feel the contrast Allah has constructed, because it is merciless and it is meant to be. **The horse pours out everything for a human master who gave it relatively little** — some hay, some water, a stable. And the human being, who was given *everything* — existence itself, eyesight, a beating heart, breath in the lungs, a mind that can read these words, a world arranged entirely for his benefit — **that human being withholds gratitude from the Divine Master who gave him all of it.**

The animal that owes almost nothing gives everything. The human who owes everything gives almost nothing.

That word in verse 6 — **كَنُود** (*kanūd*) — deserves a moment. It is not the ordinary word for "ungrateful." The lexicographers explain *kanūd* as a specific, ugly kind of ingratitude: the one who **counts and remembers every hardship, and forgets every blessing.** They said the *kanūd* is "the one who eats alone, beats his servant, and withholds his gift." It is the person who, when one thing goes wrong, says "why is this happening to *me*" — having said nothing across the ten thousand mornings his heart beat without being asked, his eyes opened without being told, his lungs filled without his permission. The *kanūd* notices the single thorn and is blind to the entire garden.

[PAUSE]

And here is the mercy hidden inside the severity. Allah did not have to swear an oath to tell us this. He swore *by the horse* so that we would not just be *told* we are ungrateful — we would *see it,* set against the most beautiful possible image of the opposite. He held up a mirror, and the mirror is a sweating, faithful, self-spending animal, so that we would look at it and feel the gap between what that creature gives its master and what we give ours.

**What does this change about tomorrow morning?**

Here is the practical thing, and it is small enough to actually do. The *kanūd* forgets blessings and remembers complaints. So reverse it, deliberately, once a day. Before you ask Allah for anything — before the request, before the complaint, before "why is this hard" — *count three things He gave you that you never asked for and never earned.* The breath you are taking right now. The eyes reading this line. The fact that you woke. Just three. Out loud if you can. You are not informing Allah of His blessings; you are retraining a heart that, left alone, drifts by default toward *kanūd.* The horse cannot choose its loyalty. You can. That is the entire dignity of being human — and the entire weight of it.

## Theme Two: Why the Unthinking Animal Is Called to Witness Against You

Now I want to open something deeper, and a little uncomfortable. Stay with me.

We tend to think of ourselves as the crown of creation — and the Qur'an does honour the human being, given the trust (*amānah*) that the heavens and earth declined to carry. But notice what happens repeatedly in revelation: **when Allah wants to indict the human being, He often calls a creature with no reason and no choice to stand as witness.**

Why? Why would the *unthinking* animal be the one held up against the *thinking* human?

Let me give you the modern version of this discomfort first, because you have felt it.

You have, at some point, watched a dog wait at a window for an owner who barely deserves it. You have seen loyalty in an animal so total and so uncalculating that it embarrassed you — because you knew that you, with your vast mind and your endless capacity for reasoning, hesitate and bargain and withhold in your own relationships in ways that simple creature never would. There is something about the *purity* of an animal's devotion that exposes the *calculation* in our own.

That is exactly the nerve this oath touches.

The horse in these verses has no concept of reward in the next life. It has no scripture, no prophets, no intellect to weigh duties, no free will to choose loyalty over self-interest. It simply *is* loyal, completely, by its nature. **And that becomes the indictment.** Because the human being has been given everything the horse lacks — reason, revelation, the capacity to recognize the Giver, the freedom to choose — and uses those very gifts to construct *reasons to withhold.* We are the only creature that can know its Lord and still turn away. The horse cannot betray its master. We can. And often do.

[PAUSE]

Here is the layer beneath that. There is a thread running through the Qur'an: *everything in creation is already in a state of submission and praise.* The sun runs its course in obedience. The mountains and birds glorify with Dāwūd. The thunder praises with His praise. **All of creation is, in a sense, the loyal warhorse** — pouring itself out in the function its Lord assigned it, holding nothing back, never once asking "why me."

And into the middle of that vast, harmonious, self-spending obedience walks the human being — the *one* creature given the freedom to *not.* The one creature that can look at the Giver of all things and say, in effect, "what have you done for me lately." The *kanūd.*

So the oath is doing something more profound than shaming us. **It is showing us where we belong.** The horse is not above us — we are honoured above it. But the horse is showing us the *posture* we were meant to have toward our Lord: total, trusting, holding-nothing-back devotion. The animal is living, by nature and without choice, the life we were invited to live *by choice* — which is the only kind of devotion that has any worth. A loyalty you cannot help is instinct. A loyalty you *choose,* against your own self-interest, against your *kanūd* nature — *that* is worship. That is the thing the angels were told to bow before.

[PAUSE]

So the animal is not your superior. The animal is your *teacher* and your *witness.* It witnesses what wholehearted exertion looks like, so that on the Day when *the tongues, hands, and feet bear witness,* you will already have been shown the standard — not by a scholar, not by a sermon, but by a panting horse at dawn.

**What does this change about tomorrow?**

Find the one place in your life where you are giving Allah your *leftovers* — the prayer you rush at the end of the day when you are too tired to mean it, the charity you give only from surplus, the dhikr you do only when nothing better is on. And in that *one* place, this week, give Him the charge instead of the leftovers. Give Him the dawn raid — the prayer you wake *early* for while you are still strong, the giving that costs you something, the remembrance you do *first* and not last. Not everywhere. Not perfectly. Just one place where you stop withholding and pour out. The horse gives its best hour — the dawn — to its master. Give Him one of yours.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: The Whole Oath Was a Trap of Mercy

Now stand back and see the structure whole, because it is breathtaking in its design.

Five verses of oath. One verse of verdict. And the oath was *built* for the verdict.

Watch the architecture:

- **Verses 1–3** (the participles): the horse in its *ongoing nature* — charging, sparking, raiding. *This is what it IS.*
- **Verses 4–5** (the verbs): the horse in its *completed sacrifice* — it kicked up the dust, it plunged into the centre. *This is what it DID.*
- **Verse 6** (the landing): **"Indeed, the human being is, to his Lord, ungrateful."**

Everything in those first five verses — every panting breath, every struck spark, every grain of dust, the final suicidal plunge into the enemy's heart — *all of it was constructed as the backdrop against which your ingratitude becomes visible.* You cannot see *kanūd* in the dark. You need a light to see it by. **The blazing, self-spending loyalty of the warhorse is the light Allah lit so that you could finally see the shape of your own withholding.**

That is why the oath had to be so beautiful, so kinetic, so total. A small loyalty would not have exposed a small ingratitude. He gave you the *most* magnificent image of holding-nothing-back He could find — so that the gap between that creature and you would be impossible to look away from.

## Bringing the Two Directions Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery, approached from two sides.

The *language* made you **feel** total exertion — your tongue panted (*ḍabḥā*), your mouth galloped (*the hammering endings*), the grammar collapsed into impact (*the participles becoming verbs*). The *themes* made you **understand** what that total exertion was *for* — to expose, by contrast, the human being who gives his Lord everything except his whole heart.

Allah did not want you to *learn* about ingratitude. He wanted you to *feel* the loyalty you lack, in your own chest, in your own mouth — and then catch your breath at verse 6 and realize the mirror was pointed at you the whole time.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I the *kanūd* — counting the one hardship and forgetting the ten thousand gifts? Can I name the specific blessing I have stopped seeing precisely because it never failed me?
2. The horse gives its master its *best* hour, the dawn. What hour am I giving Allah — my best, or my leftovers?
3. The animal pours itself out by *instinct.* I am asked to do it by *choice.* In what relationship — with Allah, with people — am I withholding precisely because I *can?*
4. If a panting warhorse is a more complete servant of its master than I am of mine, what exactly is my reason — I, who was given reason itself?
5. When my tongue, hands, and feet bear witness on the Day, what will they testify that I gave — the charge, or the hesitation?

## The One Sentence

**The creature that owes almost nothing pours out everything for its master — so that you, who owe everything, might finally see what you have been withholding from yours.**

## The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to become a horse — mindless, instinctual, unfree. You are being asked for something *higher* than the horse can ever reach: a loyalty that is *chosen,* given against your own nature, offered with the very reason and freedom that make withholding possible. That is the only devotion worth anything. The horse cannot help its faithfulness. You can. And so when you *choose* to pour yourself out — early, willingly, holding nothing back — you do something no animal in all of creation can do. You become the servant who was *worth* creating. Not because you had to. Because you decided to.

Let something shift. Not everything. One thing. One place where the leftovers become the charge.

## A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, You who gave me the breath I never asked for and the eyes I never earned — forgive the *kanūd* in me, the heart that counts its troubles and forgets Your gifts. Teach me the loyalty of the creature that holds nothing back, and let me give You not my leftovers but my dawn. Make me, by choice, the servant I was made to be. *Āmīn.*
