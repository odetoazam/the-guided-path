# Hikmah — The Gift That Contains Every Other Gift

## The Ayah

[ayah:2:269] يُؤْتِى ٱلْحِكْمَةَ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَمَن يُؤْتَ ٱلْحِكْمَةَ فَقَدْ أُوتِىَ خَيْرًا كَثِيرًا ۗ وَمَا يَذَّكَّرُ إِلَّآ أُو۟لُوا۟ ٱلْأَلْبَـٰبِ

*Yuʾtī al-ḥikmata man yashāʾu. Wa man yuʾta al-ḥikmata fa-qad ūtiya khayran kathīrā. Wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā ūlū-l-albāb.*

**He gives wisdom to whom He wills. And whoever has been given wisdom — has indeed been given abundant good. And none will be mindful of this except those of inner cores.**

---

## Rebuilding the World

This ayah sits in the middle of one of the most practically-focused sections of the Quran. The surrounding ayahs in Surah Al-Baqarah are about *spending* — how to give charity, whom to give it to, what the Shayṭān whispers to block that giving, what Allah promises in return. The passage is about the flow of wealth between human beings.

Ibn Kathīr, drawing on Ibn ʿAbbās, notes that the command just before this one was to spend not from the dregs of one's wealth but from its *purest and finest* — "for Allah is pure and good, and accepts only what is pure and good." Charity, in other words, was already being framed as more than the transfer of money; it was an act that requires discernment — what to give, from where, and to whom. So when the discussion of wealth pauses, it pauses on exactly the quality that good giving silently depends on.

And right in the middle, Allah pauses the discussion of money and delivers one of the most startling statements in the Quran about what the highest gift actually is.

It is not wealth. It is not even children, or health, or power. It is *ḥikmah.*

[PAUSE]

Before we can receive this ayah, we have to understand what happens when this word lands in the Arabic language.

*Ḥikmah* is a word the pre-Islamic Arabs used. It is in their poetry. It is in their proverbs. It is in the speech of their wise elders, who were called *ḥukamāʾ* — wise ones. The Arabs valued ḥikmah. They had their own poets and sages who were known for it.

But Allah, in this ayah, says something that would have stopped the Arab listener mid-conversation. *He gives* ḥikmah. He does not say Arabs produce it, or study it, or cultivate it, or inherit it from their elders. He says He *gives* it. To whom? *To whom He wills.* Not to whom deserves it. Not to whom works for it. To whom *He wills.*

The ayah takes what the Arabs already considered the most prestigious human quality and places its distribution entirely in Allah's hand.

And then the second half lands. **Whoever has been given this — has been given abundant good.** Not some good. Not a good alongside other goods. *Khayran kathīrā* — "abundant good." The phrasing implies that this single gift, *ḥikmah,* contains inside it a multitude of other goods. To have it is to have a treasury. To lack it, even while possessing many other apparent blessings, is to lack what makes blessings coherent.

[PAUSE]

**The Hook:**

This ayah is short. Twenty-two tokens. But inside it, Allah has placed a claim that would, if you truly absorbed it, reorganize your prayer life for the rest of your life. Because if this one gift contains abundant good — if every other gift is somehow subordinate to it — then it becomes the primary thing to ask Allah for. And most of us have never done that.

By the end of this reflection, I want you to understand what *ḥikmah* actually is, why it is described as the container of other goods, why Allah gives it specifically to those described in a particular way — and why, after you hear this ayah tonight, you may never end a duʿāʾ the same way again.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Yuʾtī* — The Giving That Is Purely Gift

Before we open the word *ḥikmah,* look at the verb that introduces it.

*Yuʾtī.* Root: **أ-ت-ي**. Form IV. Imperfect tense. The Form I verb *atā* means "to come." Form IV — *ātā* — means "to cause to come, to bring, to bestow."

Notice what this form does. It does not mean "He rewards." It does not mean "He grants in proportion to effort." It means "He causes to come to them" — a pure gift language. The verb places ḥikmah in the category of things that *arrive* at a person's doorstep from Allah, not things a person constructs through their own effort.

[PAUSE]

And then: *man yashāʾ.* "Whom He wills."

Classical tafsirists spent careful time on this phrase. The question arises naturally: why doesn't Allah simply say *He gives wisdom to those who seek it,* or *to those who are righteous,* or *to those who strive?* Why *man yashāʾ* — "whom He wills"?

The answer the classical commentators reach is that ḥikmah is precisely the kind of gift that *cannot* be purely earned. Some gifts are commensurate with effort — if you work, you get a salary. If you study, you gain knowledge. If you practice, you gain skill. But ḥikmah is different. There are people who study their whole lives and do not acquire it. There are people who have never attended a single class and speak it as if they drink it from an unseen well.

*Man yashāʾ* preserves this mystery. Ḥikmah is not arbitrary — Allah's will is never arbitrary, and classical Muslims have always understood His will to be guided by His infinite knowledge and mercy. But it is also not mechanical. Allah gives ḥikmah to those whose hearts have a certain readiness — and that readiness itself is also His gift. The gift of ḥikmah and the capacity to receive it are both *from Him.*

[PAUSE]

This is a crucial insight. Because it means you cannot buy ḥikmah. You cannot earn ḥikmah through study alone. You cannot force it open through effort. What you can do is *ask for it,* and then *create the conditions in yourself* that Allah typically chooses for its arrival — humility, consistent remembrance, honest seeking, the removal of the veils of arrogance and self-regard. But even these preparations are not causes. They are dispositions that invite without compelling.

Ḥikmah, in the deepest theological sense, is a gift Allah *initiates.*

One clear statement: **The verb *yuʾtī* and the phrase *man yashāʾ* together place ḥikmah outside the transactional economy of ordinary achievement. It is a gift, given at Allah's will, to those He has prepared to receive it — often beyond what they would have earned by effort alone.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: What Ḥikmah Actually Is

Now we come to the word itself. *Ḥikmah.* Root: **ح-ك-م.**

In Arabic, the root ح-ك-م carries a family of related meanings. *Ḥakama* — to judge, to decide, to rule. *Ḥukm* — a judgment, a ruling. *Ḥākim* — a judge, a governor. *Aḥkam* — to make firm, to perfect.

And then there is the noun *ḥikmah.* It is the quality of being able to *make the right judgment* — to perceive what a situation actually calls for and to respond fittingly. It is not merely intelligence. It is not merely knowledge. It is not even morality in isolation. It is the *union* of knowledge and right action — knowing *what* to do *when* to do it *in the way* most aligned with truth.

[PAUSE]

Classical scholars and philosophers — Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in his famous *Mufradāt* — built nuanced definitions of ḥikmah. Each of them saw something slightly different, and the composite is richer than any single definition.

Al-Rāghib defined *ḥikmah* as "knowledge arrived at through reason that is congruent with reality" — meaning, knowledge that accurately corresponds to what is, combined with the capacity to act from that knowledge.

Ibn Kathīr defined it more practically as *understanding of the Quran* — the capacity to see into the meanings of Allah's speech and to live from them.

Al-Qurṭubī and others emphasized that ḥikmah includes *placing things in their proper place* — knowing what is appropriate to what, what belongs with what, which response fits which moment. A wise person does not just know things — they know *what the situation requires.*

[PAUSE]

Here it is worth being honest that the early authorities did not settle on a single definition — al-Ṭabarī, gathering them, records a genuine *ikhtilāf* over what ḥikmah means in this very ayah. His own preferred reading is that it is *al-iṣābah fī al-qawl wa-l-ʿamal* — "hitting the mark in speech and action." That phrase comes from the root **ص-و-ب**, the same root used of an arrow striking its target. On this reading ḥikmah is not a stock of information at all; it is the aim that *lands* — the right word at the right moment, the right deed in the right measure. Al-Muyassar carries this gloss forward almost verbatim: Allah gives *al-iṣābah fī al-qawl wa-l-ʿamal* to whom He wills. Others among the earliest authorities, including Ibn ʿAbbās, read ḥikmah here as *the Qurʾān and the understanding of it* (al-Qurʾān wa-l-fiqh bihi) — the line Ibn Kathīr follows. And still others said it is *prophethood* itself. This is real disagreement, not one meaning in several dresses; what follows draws together the readings rather than pretending to close the question.

Let me build the composite.

**Ḥikmah is the capacity to see what is real, to know what is true, and to respond to each situation with what actually fits it — all three together.**

[PAUSE]

Now stay with what this composite reveals.

You probably know people who know many things but cannot apply any of them. They can quote the texts, cite the cases, name the principles. Yet in real situations, they fail to recognize what is happening, or they apply the wrong rule, or they do the right thing at the wrong time. They have *ʿilm* — knowledge. They lack ḥikmah.

You also probably know people who have not studied much, but who seem to see into situations with uncanny clarity. They know what to say to the grieving friend. They know when silence is required. They recognize, before others do, that a relationship is becoming unhealthy or that a decision is about to be a mistake. They speak little, and when they speak, their words often carry weight beyond their apparent content. These people have ḥikmah. They may or may not have ʿilm in the technical sense. But they have the capacity to perceive reality accurately and respond fittingly.

*Ḥikmah* is the second — elevated and made whole by knowledge where knowledge is available, but not dependent on credentialed knowledge alone. It is the *inner seeing* that knows what to do.

[PAUSE]

And this is why Allah describes ḥikmah as *khayr kathīr* — abundant good. Because consider what ḥikmah does for every other gift a person might have.

- Wealth *without* ḥikmah — often corrupts. Wealth *with* ḥikmah — feeds the poor, builds mosques, sustains families.
- Power *without* ḥikmah — destroys. Power *with* ḥikmah — shelters the weak.
- Knowledge *without* ḥikmah — becomes pedantry, arrogance, or idle dispute. Knowledge *with* ḥikmah — becomes benefit to the community.
- Beauty *without* ḥikmah — breeds vanity. Beauty *with* ḥikmah — reflects Allah's artistry without taking credit.
- Even worship *without* ḥikmah — can become empty ritual. Worship *with* ḥikmah — becomes what Allah actually wanted from it.

Do you see? Ḥikmah does not merely sit alongside other goods. It *activates* them. It is the gift that makes other gifts useful — and without which other gifts turn, quietly, against the one who holds them.

This is why Allah says *khayran kathīrā* — abundant good. Not because ḥikmah is a lot of good among many goods. Because ḥikmah is the one gift whose presence multiplies every other gift, and whose absence causes every other gift to diminish or deform.

One clear statement: **Ḥikmah is not one blessing among many. It is the blessing that makes other blessings actually serve you. Without it, every other gift you hold is at risk of turning against you.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Ūlū al-Albāb* — Those of Inner Cores

The ayah ends with a specific phrase that tells you something about who actually responds to these words.

*Wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā ūlū-l-albāb.* "And none will be mindful of this except those of inner cores."

Let me teach you this phrase. Because it appears in sixteen places in the Quran, and each time it appears, it marks a specific category of person.

*Lubb.* Plural *albāb.* Root: **ل-ب-ب.** The Arabic word for the *core* of something — the kernel, the center, the substance that is left when everything peripheral is removed. The *lubb* of a nut is the nutmeat inside the shell. The *lubb* of a fruit is the flesh, not the skin. The *lubb* of a matter is its essence, not its wrapping.

*Ūlū-l-albāb* — "those of inner cores" — are people who have a functioning *lubb.* They have access to the inner essence of things, not merely their surfaces. They perceive meaning beneath appearance.

[PAUSE]

Classical scholars, including Ibn Kathīr and al-Qurṭubī, noted that Allah does not say *ūlū al-ʿuqūl* — "those of intellects." *ʿAql* (intellect) is a broader word — every sane adult human has ʿaql. The Quran specifically uses *albāb* here because it is naming a narrower category. Not everyone who has an intellect has a functioning *lubb.* The *lubb* is the intellect *that has penetrated to essences.*

[PAUSE]

A word of honesty about the translation. Most classical commentators gloss *ūlū-l-albāb* plainly as "those of intellect" or "those of sound understanding," and the rendering "inner cores" used here leans deliberately on the concrete image inside the root — *lubb* as kernel — rather than on that standard gloss. The two are not in conflict so much as in focus. And al-Muyassar catches the nuance exactly: those who take heed are *aṣḥāb al-ʿuqūl al-mustanīra bi-nūr Allāh wa-hidāyatihi* — "possessors of intellects illumined by the light of Allah and His guidance." Not raw intellect, then, but intellect with a light in it. That is precisely the gap between *ʿaql* as bare reasoning and *lubb* as the core that has been lit from within — and it quietly tells you that the *lubb* is, in the end, not something you merely sharpen but something Allah illumines. Which folds back into everything the ayah has been saying: even the ground that receives the gift is itself lit by the Giver.

And the verb paired with *ūlū-l-albāb* — *yadhdhakkar* — is Form V of the root **ذ-ك-ر**, meaning "to remember, to take heed, to be mindful." Form V is reflexive-intensive. It does not mean "remember a fact." It means "to actively bring into one's own consciousness, to internalize, to let the awareness shape you."

Put together: *wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā ūlū-l-albāb* — **"none will actively internalize this teaching except those who have access to the inner essence of things."**

[PAUSE]

This is a warning inside a promise. The promise is that ḥikmah is *khayr kathīr.* The warning is that *most people will hear this and forget it immediately.* Most will not absorb what Allah has just said. The ayah can be recited, translated, memorized — and still pass through a person without landing.

The only ones in whom the ayah *settles* are those with a functioning *lubb.* And one of the classical readings is this: a *functioning lubb* is the soul that has not been buried under layers of distraction, ego, and surface-level living. The core is still accessible. When a profound truth lands on such a soul, the soul recognizes it.

The ayah is doing something subtle. It is telling you, the listener, that you should ask Allah not only for ḥikmah itself, but for the *lubb* that can receive it. Because the gift and the receiver are both Allah's gifts, and the receiver must be ready for the gift to do its work.

One clear statement: **The ayah is a two-tier request. Ask for ḥikmah — but know that ḥikmah lands only in a functioning *lubb.* Ask for both. Ask for the gift and for the receiving-ground.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

We have walked through three things. The verb *yuʾtī* and the phrase *man yashāʾ* — ḥikmah as pure gift. The word *ḥikmah* itself — the capacity to see reality, know truth, and respond fittingly, which multiplies every other gift. And *ūlū-l-albāb* — the soul with a functioning core, ready to receive.

Now let us ask what this ayah means for how a believer actually lives.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Reorganizing Your Duʿāʾ Around What Actually Contains Good

Let me start where you already live.

If you listen honestly to the duʿāʾ you make most weeks, what does the list look like? For most people, it looks something like this: *Allāhumma, grant me rizq. Grant me good health. Grant me a righteous spouse. Grant me success in my work. Grant me safety in my travels. Grant my parents mercy. Grant me paradise.*

These are all legitimate asks. The Quran itself, and the Prophet ﷺ in his recorded duʿāʾ, ask for each of these. There is nothing wrong with asking for them.

But watch what this ayah is quietly saying. If ḥikmah is *khayr kathīr* — abundant good — then it may be that *much of what you are specifically asking for* is actually downstream of ḥikmah.

[PAUSE]

Consider. You ask Allah for rizq. But rizq without ḥikmah is often squandered, hoarded, or used unjustly. Rizq *with* ḥikmah becomes exactly the amount you need, used in the ways that benefit you and others, and is a trust you carry rather than a burden.

You ask Allah for a righteous spouse. But the capacity to recognize a righteous spouse, to cultivate a loving home with them, and to navigate the inevitable hard moments — that is ḥikmah. A person *without* ḥikmah can marry someone wonderful and still destroy the marriage. A person *with* ḥikmah can make a difficult situation flourish.

You ask Allah for success in your work. But *without* ḥikmah, success corrupts. Many people who have "succeeded" by worldly measure have lost more than they gained in the process — their children, their health, their relationship with Allah. Success *with* ḥikmah is success that does not cost you your soul.

You ask Allah for safety. But safety in the body without ḥikmah in the heart is not safety. A person in a safe country can still be spiritually destroyed. A person in a war zone, if they have ḥikmah, may walk through it with their soul intact.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the pattern? Ḥikmah is the *X-factor* inside every specific blessing. Without it, the blessing is often an unopened box — you have the gift but cannot access what is inside. Or worse, the blessing becomes a trial that you handle poorly.

The ayah is inviting a reorganization of your duʿāʾ. Not a *replacement* — you still ask for rizq, spouse, health, protection. But you add, at the top of the list, what actually makes all of these work: *Rabbi, hab lī ḥikmah. My Lord, grant me wisdom.*

Because if Allah grants you ḥikmah, the other asks become much more likely to land productively. And if Allah withholds ḥikmah, the other asks, if granted, may turn against you.

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ explicitly made this ask. In the famous duʿāʾ preserved in the authentic ḥadīth, he asked: *Allāhumma innī asʾaluka ʿilman nāfiʿan — O Allah, I ask You for beneficial knowledge.* Beneficial knowledge is knowledge wrapped in ḥikmah. The qualification *nāfiʿan* — "beneficial" — is the ḥikmah component of knowledge. Knowledge that benefits is knowledge held with the wisdom to use it rightly.

And we also have the Prophet's ﷺ famous duʿāʾ for Ibn ʿAbbās: *Allāhumma faqqih-hu fī-d-dīn wa ʿallim-hu-t-taʾwīl.* "O Allah, give him deep understanding in the religion and teach him taʾwīl." *Taʾwīl* here means the ability to see beneath the surface to the actual meaning — the hallmark of ḥikmah. The Prophet ﷺ did not pray for Ibn ʿAbbās to memorize more ayahs. He prayed for him to receive the capacity to *understand to depths.* That is the duʿāʾ for ḥikmah.

[PAUSE]

**The core insight:** The duʿāʾ you have been making has been mostly for specific outcomes. This ayah is inviting you to add, at the top of every duʿāʾ, the request for the meta-gift that makes every other gift actually serve you: *Rabbi, hab lī ḥikmah.*

**Practical application:** For one week, start every duʿāʾ session with this specific ask — *Allāhumma, āti-nī al-ḥikmah. O Allah, give me wisdom.* Say it in Arabic if you can. Before any other request. Before any specific ask. Let this be the foundation under everything you request afterward. And notice, at the end of the week, whether your relationship to your own requests has shifted.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The *Lubb* That Receives — Clearing the Ground for the Gift

Now the second theme, which is harder and more inward.

The ayah says ḥikmah lands only in *ūlū-l-albāb* — those with functioning inner cores. This means the cultivation of your *lubb* is the prerequisite you control. The gift comes from Allah; the readiness to receive it is — at least in part — something you can work on.

[PAUSE]

What damages the *lubb*? Classical spiritual writers — from Al-Ghazālī to Ibn al-Qayyim — identified several layers of what covers the *lubb* and makes it unable to receive ḥikmah:

**Surface-level living.** When your life is entirely on the outer layer — running from task to task, reacting to phones, responding to stimuli — the *lubb* is never accessed. The outer shell becomes so active that the core is never visited. Many modern lives run like this for decades without the person noticing.

**Kibr — arrogance.** The arrogant person already thinks they see clearly. They do not need ḥikmah because they believe they have it. Allah does not typically give gifts to those who would not recognize them, because arrogance is exactly the filter that prevents recognition.

**Unexamined sin.** The believer who is committing sins without any accompanying repentance develops, over time, a film over the heart. The Prophet ﷺ described this: every sin leaves a dark point on the heart, and if not cleansed with repentance, the dark points accumulate until the heart itself becomes dark. A darkened heart has a diminished *lubb.*

**Distraction from dhikr.** The *lubb* is refreshed by regular remembrance of Allah. The soul that does not return to Allah frequently in the day loses its orientation. Without orientation, the *lubb* becomes dormant.

**Surrounding company.** If you spend most of your time among people who live entirely at the surface, your own soul will adopt that register. The *lubb* needs companionship that operates at the same depth. This is why the Prophet ﷺ said "a man is upon the religion of his close friend" — because the company sets the register of the soul.

[PAUSE]

And what *cultivates* the *lubb*? The inverse of the above:

**Regular silence.** Stepping out of the stream of stimuli long enough for the core to be accessible again. Even five minutes a day of deliberate silence — no phone, no conversation, no input — refreshes the *lubb.*

**Humility.** The honest acknowledgment that you do not know what you need to know. That you are in process. That Allah has what you do not have. This posture keeps the *lubb* open.

**Consistent repentance.** Regular, unceremonious, quiet turning back to Allah for the small sins that most of us would rather not examine. This keeps the film from accumulating.

**Dhikr.** Remembrance of Allah, repeated through the day, is the *lubb*'s food. It is what the core was made for. Without it, the core starves.

**Company of depth.** Deliberately spending time — even a little — with people whose speech operates at the level of essence, not surface. This can be through books if not in person. A single biography of a saint, read over weeks, can recalibrate the *lubb.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what is striking about this list. None of these practices guarantee ḥikmah. The gift remains Allah's to give. But each of them *clears the ground.* Each of them makes the soul more of the kind of soul Allah tends to give ḥikmah to.

This is the spiritual labor the believer can do. Allah will not reward you for the labor with automatic ḥikmah — that would violate *man yashāʾ.* But He will reward you with the labor itself, which is good, and with the possibility of ḥikmah, and often with glimmers and tastes of it as you walk. And if Allah wills, the full gift arrives — not because you earned it, but because your soul became a place it could land.

[PAUSE]

**The core insight:** You cannot force ḥikmah. But you can clear the ground in yourself where ḥikmah tends to arrive. The five practices — silence, humility, repentance, dhikr, company of depth — are not guarantees. They are preparations. The rest is Allah's to will.

**Practical application:** Pick one of the five. Not all. One. The one you most clearly lack right now. If your life is all surface and you never sit in silence, add five minutes of silence tonight. If you lack humility, examine honestly where kibr is showing up in your week. If you neglect istighfār, make it part of your daily routine. Build one practice for a month. Then consider which of the remaining four to add next. The *lubb* responds to consistent tending.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Look at the ayah one more time.

*Yuʾtī al-ḥikmata man yashāʾu.* He gives ḥikmah to whom He wills.
*Wa man yuʾta al-ḥikmata fa-qad ūtiya khayran kathīrā.* And whoever has been given ḥikmah — has indeed been given abundant good.
*Wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā ūlū-l-albāb.* And none will be mindful of this except those of inner cores.

The structure is: the source → the consequence → the hearers.

The source is Allah's will. The consequence is that the gift, when received, is equivalent to abundant good. And the hearers — the ones who actually register this claim — are those with functioning inner cores.

[PAUSE]

And look at what the architecture reveals about the ayah itself. The ayah is *testing* you even as it teaches you. By stating at the end that most people will not be mindful of this teaching, it is asking: *are you among the ūlū-l-albāb who will actually absorb what was just said? Or are you one of those in whom this ayah will pass through without changing anything?*

The ayah is self-referential. It describes ḥikmah, names its value, identifies who receives its teaching — and in doing so, sorts its own listeners into those in whom it lands and those in whom it does not.

This is part of why it ends with the phrase *wa mā yadhdhakkaru illā ūlū-l-albāb.* The phrase is not ornamental. It is the *test-sentence* of the ayah. If you hear it and something shifts in you, if you feel the weight of what was just said, if you are moved to reorganize even a small part of your duʿāʾ or your spiritual practice — you are, by the evidence of your own response, among those described. If you hear it and simply move on, unchanged, then the ayah has identified itself which group you are currently in.

### The Emotional Arc

We started with an ayah in the middle of a charity passage, but we found that the ayah is actually about the one gift that contains every other gift. We walked through the verb of pure bestowal. We walked through ḥikmah as the capacity to see reality, know truth, and respond fittingly. We walked through *ūlū-l-albāb* as those whose inner cores are accessible. We walked through a reorganization of duʿāʾ around ḥikmah as the meta-gift. And we walked through the cultivation of the *lubb* as the only labor a believer can do to prepare for the gift.

What we discovered is this: **Allah has told us, explicitly, what the most important single thing is to ask Him for. And most of us have never asked for it directly.**

### Questions to Carry

1. In an honest review of the last month of your duʿāʾs, how many times have you specifically asked Allah for *ḥikmah*? If the answer is "rarely" or "never" — what does that say about what you have been valuing in your requests?

2. Name one area of your life where you have many resources — talent, knowledge, money, relationships, time — but feel you are not using them in a way that fully serves either you or Allah. Is it possible that what is missing is not more resources but ḥikmah in their use?

3. Of the five conditions that damage the *lubb* — surface-level living, arrogance, unexamined sin, neglect of dhikr, company that stays at the surface — which is most active in your life right now? Naming it is the first step to clearing it.

4. The Prophet ﷺ prayed for Ibn ʿAbbās to be given *taʾwīl* — deep understanding. Is there a young person in your life — a child, a younger sibling, a student — for whom you could make the same duʿāʾ, asking Allah for *them* to be given ḥikmah? What would it look like to pray for the next generation's wisdom, not just their success?

5. If you were granted, tomorrow, a measurable increase in ḥikmah — what is the first thing in your life you would see differently, and what would you do differently as a result? Sit with this question honestly. The gap between your current vision and that imagined clearer vision is the distance ḥikmah would cover.

### One Thing to Remember

> **Ḥikmah is the gift that makes every other gift actually serve you. Wealth without it corrodes. Power without it destroys. Knowledge without it becomes pedantry. Health without it enables waste. Ask Allah for this first — and the rest of your duʿāʾ will stop competing with itself.**

### The Closing Invitation

If this ayah wants one thing from you tonight, it is this.

Before you sleep, add one phrase to your closing duʿāʾ. Just one. In Arabic if you can — *Rabbi, hab lī ḥikmah.* Or in English — *My Lord, grant me wisdom.* Say it with the weight of this ayah behind it. Say it knowing that if Allah grants this one request, the abundant good that would be given alongside it is greater than you can currently picture.

And then, after the duʿāʾ, choose one of the five practices — silence, humility, repentance, dhikr, company of depth — and commit, for the next seven days, to tending it. Not because it earns ḥikmah. Because it clears the ground where ḥikmah tends to arrive. You cannot compel the gift. You can prepare the soil.

Do this, and the ayah has done its work in you — you are, by your response, among the *ūlū-l-albāb* it named.

### Du'a

Allāhumma — You who give ḥikmah to whom You will — give it to us. Grant us the *lubb* that can receive it, clear the veils that prevent its arrival, and let us not be among those who hear this teaching and pass on unchanged. Teach us to ask for what actually contains abundant good, not only for the outer gifts that depend on it. And grant ḥikmah, through us, to those who will come after us — our children, our students, our communities — so that the gift You begin in one generation does not end there.
