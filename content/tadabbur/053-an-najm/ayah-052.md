# The First and the Worst: Why the People of Nuh Were Named Last

> وَقَوْمَ نُوحٍ مِّن قَبْلُ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا هُمْ أَظْلَمَ وَأَطْغَىٰ
>
> *wa-qawma Nūḥin min qablu — innahum kānū hum aẓlama wa-aṭghā*
>
> "And the people of Nuh before — indeed, it was they who were the more unjust and the more rebellious."

---

## INTRODUCTION: A List That Runs Backward

Let me put you inside the rhythm of this surah, because the rhythm is where the meaning begins.

An-Najm is fast. It moves like something falling. The very name of the surah — *the star* — opens with a star descending: *wan-najmi idhā hawā*, by the star when it plunges. And from that first plunge, the surah keeps moving downward, faster and faster, until near the end it arrives at a list. A list of nations that no longer exist.

Listen to the order Allah gives them.

First: **ʿĀd** — *wa-annahu ahlaka ʿĀdan al-ūlā* — He destroyed the former ʿĀd.

Then: **Thamūd** — *wa-Thamūda fa-mā abqā* — and Thamūd, and He left none of them.

Then, this ayah: **the people of Nuh** — *wa-qawma Nūḥin min qablu* — and the people of Nuh, **before**.

Now stop. Feel what just happened to time.

ʿĀd and Thamūd were ancient to the Arabs of Makkah. Their ruined cities were landmarks you could ride past in the desert. They were the old world. But the people of Nuh? They were older still. They were *before* ʿĀd. Before Thamūd. They sit at the very dawn of human rebellion, at the edge of the Flood, at almost the beginning of the story.

So the list is moving **backward**. From the old, to the older, to the oldest. Allah is walking us back up the river of time toward its source.

And when He arrives at the source — the first great nation to reject a messenger, the people of the very first warner — He does something He did not do for the others. For ʿĀd, He told us *what happened to them*: destroyed. For Thamūd, *what happened to them*: none left. But for the people of Nuh, He stops describing the punishment and tells us instead something about **who they were**:

*innahum kānū hum aẓlama wa-aṭghā* — indeed, it was they who were the more unjust, the more rebellious.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows.

Why are the *first* also the *worst*? In a list of doomed nations, the people of Nuh came earliest in time — and Allah singles them out as **more** wrong than all the rest. More unjust. More rebellious. Not equal. **More.**

What did they have that ʿĀd and Thamūd did not? What was given to them that turned, in their hands, into the heaviest indictment in the whole list?

The answer is hidden inside one small word — and inside one small pronoun that, by every rule of Arabic, did not need to be there. Let's go find it.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *min qablu* — The Word for the Beginning

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In Arabic, there are words that point. *Qabl* is one of them — it means "before." Normally, when you say "before," you say before *what*. Before the prayer. Before sunrise. Before ʿĀd. The word leans on something — it needs an object to point at, and in Arabic that object would normally pull the word into a particular grammatical case, ending in a small *i* sound: *qabli ʿĀd*, before ʿĀd.

But here, Allah does not say *qabli* anything. He says **qablu** — with a different ending, a *u* sound. And that ending is not random. In Arabic grammar, when *qabl* takes that *u* ending and stands alone, it means the thing it was pointing at has been **cut off and left unspoken** — and the listener is trusted to already know it. Grammarians call this state *al-qaṭʿ ʿan al-iḍāfah*, the severing from what follows.

Do you feel the effect? It is the difference between saying "before the others" and simply saying, with a kind of finality, **"before."** Just — before. Before everything you've been counting. Before the before.

*wa-qawma Nūḥin min qablu.*

The grammar itself is reaching backward into a time so early that there is nothing left to name on the other side of it. ʿĀd was before you. Thamūd was before ʿĀd. And the people of Nuh? They were simply **before** — at the headwaters, where the river of human disobedience first began to flow.

Stay with this, because it is doing more than marking a date.

When Allah places the people of Nuh at the very *beginning* of the list of rebellion, and then immediately calls them the **worst** of it, He is making a claim about the nature of the first wrong. The first ones to turn away did not turn away out of confusion in a crowded world full of competing messages. They turned away when the message was **fresh**, when the proof was **new**, when humanity was still young and close to its origin. There were no centuries of distortion to blame, no inherited skepticism, no worn-out familiarity. The light came to them clean — and they refused it clean.

[PAUSE]

That is what *min qablu* is quietly establishing. Not just *when* they lived. But that they had the least excuse — and chose the deepest wrong.

That single severed word, *qablu*, has set the table. Now watch what Allah serves on it.

---

### Element Two: *hum* — The Pronoun That Did Not Need to Be There

I want to teach you one of the most beautiful tools in the Arabic language, and once you see it, you will hear it everywhere in the Quran.

In English, to make a pronoun do something extra, we lean on it with our voice. "*They* were the worst." You can hear the emphasis only because I pressed on the word. Arabic does not need your voice. Arabic builds the emphasis right into the sentence, with a device the grammarians call **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — *the separating pronoun*.

Here is how it works. Watch closely.

To say "indeed they were more unjust," Arabic needs only:

*innahum kānū aẓlama* — indeed they were more unjust.

That sentence is complete. Grammatically whole. Nothing missing. You could stop there and every Arab would understand it perfectly.

But that is **not** what Allah said. Allah inserted one extra word, a second "they," right into the middle:

*innahum kānū **hum** aẓlama* — indeed they were — **they** — more unjust.

That extra *hum* is the separating pronoun. It is grammatically "unnecessary." It carries no new information. By the cold logic of grammar, it could be deleted and the meaning would survive.

So why is it there?

Because in the Quran's Arabic, this little inserted pronoun does three things at once, and you need to feel all three.

**First, it isolates.** *Hum* draws a circle around the subject and cuts them off from everyone else in the room. ʿĀd is standing here. Thamūd is standing here. The Arabs of Makkah are listening. And the *hum* steps the people of Nuh out of the line-up and sets them apart: **these ones — these specifically.**

**Second, it confirms.** The separating pronoun carries a flavor the grammarians describe as *taḥqīq* — locking the judgment in place so it cannot be argued with. Not "perhaps they were among the worse." But: **they were, decisively, settledly, the worse.** The verdict is sealed.

**Third — and this is the one I want you to sit with — it restricts.** The separating pronoun carries a whisper of *only*. It is as if Allah is saying: when you go looking for the most unjust, the most rebellious — you will keep arriving back at **them**. The worst was not spread evenly. It had a name. It had a face. It pooled, here, at the source.

[PAUSE]

And now notice the architecture of the sentence, because Allah surrounded this verdict with reinforcement on every side.

He opened with *inna* — "indeed," the particle of certainty.
He used *kānū* — "they **were**," the perfect tense, a completed and settled reality, a permanent fact of the record, not a passing phase.
He inserted *hum* — the separating pronoun, isolating and locking.
And only *then* did He deliver the verdict: *aẓlama wa-aṭghā*.

Four layers of emphasis stacked on top of one another before the judgment even lands. Allah does not normally pile certainty this high. When He does, He is telling you: **do not soften this. Do not look away from it. This is fixed.**

That tiny, "unnecessary" *hum* is the sound of a verdict being sealed.

---

### Element Three: *aẓlama wa-aṭghā* — The "More" With No "Than"

Now we reach the two words the whole ayah was built to deliver. And to feel them, you need one more piece of Arabic.

Arabic has a special form for comparison and superiority. It is called **af ʿal al-tafḍīl** — the form of preference. You already know examples without knowing it: *akbar*, greater; *aʿlam*, more knowing; *akram*, more generous. The shape itself — *a-ʿ-a-l* — means *more of this* or *the most of this*.

And here Allah gives us two of them, back to back:

**aẓlama** — from the root *ẓ-l-m*, injustice, *ẓulm* — so: *more unjust*, more given to putting things where they don't belong, more given to wronging.

**aṭghā** — from the root *ṭ-gh-y*, and this root is worth slowing down on. *Ṭughyān* is not ordinary sin. It is the sin of **overflowing the limit**. The same root describes a flood that bursts its banks — *lammā ṭaghā al-māʾu* (69:11), when the water *overflowed*. It describes the tyrant Pharaoh — *idhhab ilā Firʿawna innahu ṭaghā* (20:24), he has *transgressed all bounds*. *Ṭughyān* is rebellion that no longer recognizes a ceiling, water that no longer respects a riverbank, a self that has thrown off every restraint. So *aṭghā* is: *more boundlessly rebellious, more out of all bounds.*

Two elatives. More unjust. More rebellious-beyond-limit.

But here is the thing every Arabic ear notices and waits for. When you say "more," the question that immediately follows is: **more than what?**

More unjust — *than whom?*
More rebellious — *than whom?*

And Allah… does not say. He leaves the comparison **open**. *aẓlama wa-aṭghā* — and then the sentence simply ends. The "than" is severed, exactly the way *qablu* was severed at the start. The ayah opens with a cut-off "before" and closes with a cut-off "more than." Both ends left deliberately open.

[PAUSE]

So what do we do with an unfinished comparison? This is where the classical scholars gather, and — beautifully — they do not contradict each other. Each one simply fills the silence from a different angle, and together they form a single picture.

**One reading**, given by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī and the Jalālayn: *more unjust and rebellious than ʿĀd and Thamūd* — more than the very nations Allah just finished listing. The people of Nuh top a list of the doomed. The worst of the worst.

**A second reading**, which al-Qurṭubī explicitly preserves: the pronoun reaches over *all* of them — ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Nuh together — and measures them against the **Arabs of Makkah** who were sitting there listening to this very surah. As if to say to the Quraysh: *do not imagine you are the chosen exception. These were greater nations than you, more rebellious than you — and look where they are now.*

And **why** were the people of Nuh the more unjust? Here the scholars are united, and their reason is the heart of this entire reflection. Al-Baghawī says it plainly: *li-ṭūli daʿwati Nūḥin iyyāhum* — **because of the length of Nuh's call to them.** Al-Qurṭubī says the same: *li-ṭūli muddati Nūḥin fīhim* — because of the *length of Nuh's time among them*.

Nine hundred and fifty years. The Quran tells us elsewhere: *fa-labitha fīhim alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman* (29:14) — he remained among them a thousand years all but fifty. Nearly a millennium of one man, calling, warning, pleading, by day and by night, in private and in public, as he himself reports in Sūrat Nuh: *raise after raise of invitation, and it only made them flee.*

That is the missing "than." That is what fills the open comparison.

They were *more* — because they were given **more**. More time. More patience. More warning. More chances than any nation before or since. And every added year of mercy, refused, was added to the weight of the verdict.

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why the grammar severed both ends? The "before" was left open to say: *they were the first, with the least excuse.* The "more than" was left open to say: *they were given the most, and wasted the most.* The first word and the last word of the ayah are both reaching into the same silence — and in that silence sits 950 years.

---

### The Bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three things, and they are really one thing.

*Min qablu* placed them at the headwaters of human rebellion — the first, with the clean and undistorted proof.

*Hum*, the separating pronoun, sealed their verdict beyond appeal — *they*, decisively, settledly, the worst.

*Aẓlama wa-aṭghā*, the two open elatives, told us the measure of that worst — and the scholars handed us the reason it was sealed: not because they were given less, but because they were given **more**, and turned the gift into the indictment.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about a vanished people — but about time, and mercy, and the strange arithmetic of how a soul becomes lost.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When Mercy Becomes the Measure

Let me start where you live.

Think of the longest the runway anyone ever gave you. A teacher who kept extending the deadline. A parent who forgave the same mistake the seventh time. A friend who stayed when everyone else walked. A doctor who told you, gently, the same warning at three appointments in a row, watching you nod and change nothing.

Now ask yourself an uncomfortable question. Did that patience always make you better? Or did some part of you, quietly, begin to take it for granted? Did the very length of the runway start to feel like permission?

There is a strange law in the human heart, and you have felt it operate in yourself. **Patience extended without limit can be heard, by the lower self, as patience that does not matter.** The alarm that rings every day stops being an alarm. The warning you've heard a hundred times becomes background noise. The mercy that never runs out begins to feel like a mercy you never had to answer for.

This is the law that destroyed the people of Nuh.

[PAUSE]

Here is the part the world gets backward. We tend to think: *the longer God waited, the more lenient the final judgment must be. He gave them so long — surely that softens the account.* That feels intuitive. It feels fair.

But Allah inverts it. *innahum kānū hum aẓlama wa-aṭghā.* They were the **more** unjust — *because* of the long waiting. The 950 years did not soften the verdict. The 950 years **were** the verdict. Every sunrise Nuh greeted them and was rejected was not erased from the ledger. It was written into it.

Sit with the weight of that, because it overturns something we secretly believe. We treat respite as the absence of accountability — as if the time before the reckoning is empty, neutral, free. It is not. **Respite is not the suspension of the account. Respite is the account, being kept, in slow motion.**

Every day Allah gives you is not a day off from the record. It is a day *on* the record. The runway is not empty space before the test. The runway is the test.

And this is precisely why the people of Nuh, who came **first** — who had the *least* distortion to excuse them and the *most* time to respond — became the *worst*. They had every condition for faith and refused under the best possible circumstances, for the longest possible duration. There was no one to inherit blame from. There was nothing old or corrupted in the message. There was only a clear voice, for nearly a thousand years, and a settled, deepening, sealing "no."

[PAUSE]

So let me turn this gently toward you, because this ayah is not really about them.

What has been calling you, patiently, for years? You know what it is. The prayer you keep meaning to return to. The habit you keep meaning to leave. The reconciliation you keep deferring. The turn toward Allah you keep filing under "soon." And here is the trap the people of Nuh fell into, dressed in modern clothes: *the very fact that nothing has collapsed yet feels like proof that nothing needs to change.* The deadline keeps moving, so you assume there is no deadline.

But the deadline moving **is** the mercy. And the mercy is being recorded.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It means you stop reading God's patience as God's indifference. The space He has given you is not evidence that the call is optional — it is the most generous possible form of the call. The people of Nuh had 950 years and read it as permission. You do not know the length of your own runway. The only safe response to a patience this deep is to answer it *now*, while answering is still something you choose, rather than something the silence finally answers for you.

### Theme Two: The Inheritance No One Wants to Name

Let me give you a second door into this ayah, and it comes from al-Qurṭubī, who preserves a piece of the story so vivid it should stop your breath.

He describes *how* the people of Nuh became so entrenched over those centuries. It was not, he says, a single generation refusing once. It was a refusal that became an **heirloom**. Picture it: a man would take his small son by the hand — the boy barely able to walk — and lead him out to where Nuh was calling people to Allah. And he would point at the messenger and say to his child: *"Beware this one. He is a liar. My own father walked me out here when I was your size, and he told me exactly what I am telling you now."*

[PAUSE]

Read that again and feel the machinery in it. The disbelief was *transmitted*. Hand to hand. Father to son. Wrapped and handed down like a family ring. The boy did not arrive at rejection through his own reckoning with the truth. He inherited it, pre-formed, before he was old enough to weigh it. And one day he would take *his* son's hand and walk *him* out to the same spot, and say the same words. *The old one died on disbelief, and the young one was raised on his father's bequest.* That is al-Qurṭubī's image. That is how 950 years of refusal becomes a wall no messenger can pass.

Now bring it into your world, because this is one of the most quietly powerful forces in any human life, and it has a name in modern language: **intergenerational transmission.** We now understand, with a thoroughness the ancients felt but could not chart, how much of what we believe was never *decided* by us. It was absorbed. The dinner-table certainties. The "people like us don't do that." The contempt for a whole way of being that we never personally examined — we received it the way the boy received his father's pointing finger.

And here is the searching question this ayah forces. **How much of your relationship with Allah is yours — chosen, weighed, owned — and how much is simply an heirloom you have never unwrapped?**

This cuts in both directions, and I want you to feel both edges.

Some of you inherited *faith* as an heirloom. It was handed to you, pre-formed, and you have never once made it your own — never asked the question, never wrestled, never *chosen* it. And the danger there is real: an inherited faith you never owned can be put down as carelessly as it was picked up, because it was never truly in your hands to begin with.

And some of you inherited *distance* as an heirloom. A coldness toward the sacred, a reflexive eye-roll, a "we don't do that in this family" — handed to you by people who were handed it by people, in a chain none of you ever stopped to examine. And the tragedy of the people of Nuh is that **not one of them, for nine centuries, broke the chain.** Not one boy, grown to manhood, ever stopped and said: *wait — let me actually go and listen to this man myself, and decide.*

[PAUSE]

This is the *ẓulm* in *aẓlama*. Remember the root — *ẓulm* is putting a thing where it does not belong. And what greater misplacement is there than to hand your child a verdict about God that you yourself never tested? To pass down a "no" you never earned the right to say?

But here is the mercy folded inside the warning. **Every chain can be broken by one person who chooses to weigh the thing themselves.** The whole catastrophe of Nuh's people is the catastrophe of inheritance left unexamined for a thousand years. Which means the doorway out was always there, in any single generation. One man, taking his son's hand and walking him to Nuh and saying not "beware this liar" but *"let us listen, and let us think"* — would have changed everything downstream of him forever.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It asks you to do the one thing the people of Nuh never did across 950 years: *unwrap the heirloom.* Take whatever you were handed — faith or coldness, devotion or contempt — set it down on the table, and look at it as if for the first time. Ask: *do I believe this because it is true, or only because it was given to me?* The most dangerous inheritance is the one you have never once examined. And the most liberating act in this entire ayah is the one act its subjects never performed: to stop, and weigh, and choose for yourself.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A List That Climbs Down to the Root

Now stand back and see the whole shape, because the structure itself is the final argument.

Watch the sequence again: ʿĀd → Thamūd → the people of Nuh. The list runs **backward** through time — recent, older, oldest. And it does not stop at the most recent or the most famous. It keeps descending, *past* ʿĀd, *past* Thamūd, all the way down to *min qablu* — to the first, the source, the headwaters.

Why build it that way? Because Allah is not listing nations. He is tracing a disease back to its **origin**. The surah climbs down the family tree of human rebellion, generation past generation, until it reaches the root — and at the root it plants its heaviest word: *aẓlama wa-aṭghā*, the worst of all. The structure says: *the deepest wrong is not the newest. It is the oldest, the one given the most time and the cleanest proof, the one that became an inheritance.* The list runs backward precisely so it can end at the beginning — and show you that the beginning was the worst.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The grammar severed both ends of the ayah — a cut-off *qablu* at the start, a cut-off *aṭghā* at the end — and into both silences it poured nine and a half centuries. The *hum* sealed a verdict that the centuries had earned. And the themes told us what those centuries *were*: a mercy so long it was mistaken for permission, and a refusal so old it became an heirloom.

The first were the worst — not despite being given the most, but **because** they were given the most. That is the whole ayah. Mercy, misread, becomes the measure.

### Questions to Carry

1. What has been calling you, patiently, for so long that you have started to mistake its patience for permission?
2. Where in your life are you reading God's *delay* as God's *indifference* — treating the runway as empty when it is the test itself?
3. Which of your deepest convictions about the sacred did you actually *choose* — and which did you simply inherit and never unwrap?
4. If the chain of the people of Nuh could have been broken by one person in one generation who stopped to weigh the truth themselves — are you that person in your own chain?
5. When you stand at the end of your respite, will it have been the proof of your transformation — or the record of how long you delayed it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The people of Nuh were named the most unjust and most rebellious not because they received the least, but because they received the most — the longest mercy, the clearest proof — and turned a 950-year invitation into a verdict they sealed against themselves.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not a story about a drowned world. It is a mirror held up to the long patience God is showing *you* right now. The people of Nuh teach us, by their catastrophe, the one thing that could have saved them: that respite is not the absence of the account but the account being kept in slow motion — and that the only safe answer to a mercy this deep is to turn, today, before the silence answers for you. Do not let the gift become the indictment. Unwrap the inheritance. Weigh it. Choose Him while choosing is still yours.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who waited nine and a half centuries upon a people and waited longer still upon me — do not let me mistake Your patience for Your permission. Let the length of Your mercy be the measure of my turning toward You, never the measure of my turning away. And give me the courage the people of Nuh never found: to weigh the truth with my own heart, and to answer Your call before the time You have given me is spent. Āmīn.
