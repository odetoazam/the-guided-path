# The Wish You Cannot Own

### Surah An-Najm, Ayahs 24-25

> أَمْ لِلْإِنسَانِ مَا تَمَنَّىٰ ۝ فَلِلَّهِ الْآخِرَةُ وَالْأُولَىٰ
>
> *Am lil-insāni mā tamannā · Fa-lillāhi-l-ākhiratu wal-ūlā*
>
> **"Or shall man have whatever he wishes for? But to Allah belong the last and the first."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind These Eight Words

I want you to stand, for a moment, in the place where these words were first heard.

The Surah is *An-Najm* — "The Star." It opens with an oath by the star as it sets, and it moves quickly into something the Makkans had never heard before: a sworn, eyewitness account of the Prophet ﷺ at the furthest horizon, then at the Lote Tree of the Boundary. This is a Surah about *what is actually real* versus what people merely tell themselves is real.

And then, just before our two ayahs, the Surah does something almost confrontational. It names them. By name.

*Have you considered al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā? And Manāt, the third, the other one?* (53:19-20)

These were the three great goddesses of Arabia. Three names a Makkan would have grown up hearing in oaths, in lullabies, in the desperate prayers of his mother. And the Qur'an names them not to honor them — but to ask a devastatingly simple question about them: *do you get to assign daughters to God and sons to yourselves? These are nothing but names you named — you and your fathers.* (53:23)

And in that same breath, the Qur'an diagnoses the entire disease underneath the idol-worship:

> *They follow nothing but conjecture and what their souls desire.* (53:23)

**Conjecture. And what their souls desire.**

Hold onto that second phrase. *What their souls desire* — `mā tahwā al-anfus`. Because the very next thing Allah says — our ayah — takes that desiring soul and puts it under the light:

> *Or shall man have whatever he wishes for?*

Here is the hook. Notice what just happened. The Makkans had an idol named **Manāt**. And do you know what the word *Manāt* is built from? The same three letters — *mīm, nūn, yā'* — that build the word **tamannā**, *"he wishes."* They had carved a goddess out of the very act of wishing. They had built a stone shrine to the human longing for a guaranteed outcome.

And Allah, having just demolished the stone, now reaches *behind* the stone — to the longing itself. To the wish that built it.

[PAUSE]

What is it in us that needs to believe we will get what we want? And what happens to that need when it meets the One who actually owns the warehouse of every outcome — in this world and the next?

That is what these eight words are about. Let us not rush them.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَمْ — The Question That Is Not Really a Question

Let me teach you something about how Arabic asks before we open the word.

In English, when I say *"or,"* I am usually offering you a choice. *Tea or coffee?* Both are live options. But Arabic has a particular particle, **أَمْ** (*am*), that can do something stranger and sharper. The grammarians call one of its uses **`am munqaṭiʿa`** — the "disjunctive" or "severing" *am*. It carries the force of *bal* ("rather") fused with a question. It means something like: **"Or — wait — do you actually imagine that…?"**

Think of how we use it in everyday speech. A friend has spent forty minutes explaining a get-rich-quick scheme. You let them finish. Then you say quietly: *"…Or do you really think it's that easy?"* You are not asking a question you want answered. You are holding up a mirror. The "or" turns the whole speech back on the speaker and exposes the assumption hiding inside it.

That is exactly the *am* that opens our ayah.

> [ayah:53:24] أَمْ لِلْإِنسَـٰنِ مَا تَمَنَّىٰ

It is not the first question in this passage. It comes riding on the back of everything before it — the named idols, the followed conjecture, the desiring souls. So the *am* is saying: *"…Or — after all of that — does the human being actually get to have whatever he wishes for?"*

Notice what the particle does that a plain statement could never do. Allah does not say *"Man does not get what he wishes."* He could have. Instead He hands you the assumption — *whatever he wishes for* — lets you feel its weight in your own chest, and then the very grammar of the sentence collapses it. **You are made to watch your own wish fail to survive the question.**

This is mercy in the shape of grammar. A flat denial would bounce off us. But a question — a question we have to answer ourselves — gets *inside*. Every mufassir who handled this ayah heard that internal answer and wrote it down. Al-Jalālayn completes the unspoken half: *"…Is it so? It is not so."* Al-Baghawī phrases it as the disbeliever's own delusion exposed: *"Does the disbeliever imagine he has whatever he wishes and craves of the idols' intercession?"* The *am* hands you the imagined thing precisely so that you can feel it dissolve.

[PAUSE]

So before we have even reached the heart of the ayah, the *form* of it has already done spiritual work. It has caught us in the act of wishing — and asked us, gently, *who told you it was yours?*

---

### Element Two: تَمَنَّىٰ — The Architecture of a Wish

Now we arrive at the word the whole ayah turns on.

> أَمْ لِلْإِنسَانِ مَا **تَمَنَّىٰ**

*Tamannā.* "He wished. He longed for. He fantasized about."

I want to teach you two things about this word — one about its *shape*, and one about its *roots* — and each one opens a different door.

**First, the shape.** *Tamannā* is what grammarians call a **Form V** verb (*tafaʿʿala*) — built on a doubled middle root letter with a *ta-* prefix in front. This form, across Arabic, very often carries a sense of **deliberate, self-involved, repeated engagement** — the self doing something *to and for itself*, over and over. *Taʿallama* — to teach oneself, to learn through effort. *Tadhakkara* — to keep reminding oneself. *Takabbara* — to make oneself big, to swell with self-importance.

So *tamannā* is not a single passing thought. It is the soul **going to work on a wish**. Spinning it. Returning to it. Polishing it. It is the verb-form of the daydream you keep stepping back into.

Sit with that. The grammar already tells you this is not innocent. This is the mind that lies awake constructing the life it does not have, room by room.

**Second — and this is where the floor opens beneath us — the roots.** *Tamannā* comes from the three letters **م-ن-ي** (*m-n-y*). And in the classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib — that root carries a startling core meaning: **to measure out, to apportion, to allot a measure.**

From this single root, watch what Arabic grew:

- **Umniyah** — a wish. (Literally: a *measure* you assign to yourself.)
- **Manā / maniyyah** — death; the appointed fate. (The measure that is *actually* allotted to you.)
- **Manī** — the drop of fluid from which the human is measured out into being (you will meet this very word at the end of this same Surah, 53:46, and again in 75:37).
- And **Manāt** — the goddess of apportioned fortune, the idol named just five ayahs ago.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what is happening here? It is breathtaking.

A **wish** (*umniyah*) and **death** (*maniyyah*) and **the seed of your creation** (*manī*) all grow from the *same root* — the root of *measuring out an allotment.*

So here is what the word *tamannā* is quietly telling us. **To wish is to sit in the seat of the Apportioner.** It is to take the pen of measurement into your own hand and write yourself a quantity that reality has not written for you. *I should have this much. My life should be measured out like that.* The wish is a private accounting, a self-issued allotment.

But the very same root reminds you, in the very same breath, of the *real* allotments — the drop you began from, the death you are walking toward — **neither of which you measured.** You did not apportion the *manī* that made you. You will not apportion the *maniyyah* that ends you. The two great measurements of your existence, the first and the last, were written by another hand entirely.

And in between those two measurements you did not control, you spend your days *tamannā* — measuring out, in fantasy, a third world that you imagine *will* obey you.

That is the human condition in one verb.

And remember — five ayahs earlier they had built this very root into a *goddess*. **Manāt**: the deification of "the allotment I wish for." They took the most fragile thing in the human heart — the longing for a guaranteed measure — and carved it into stone and bowed to it. The Surah named that stone, broke it, and now turns to the longing that quarried it: *Or shall the human have whatever he measures out for himself?*

[PAUSE]

This is the moment to be honest about what the scholars saw in *what* exactly the human was wishing for, because they did not all see one thing — and the differences are themselves a teaching.

Al-Qurṭubī gathers the views like a man holding up a wish to the light and turning it. He reports that some read it as the idolaters wishing for **the idols' intercession** — and on this reading, names appear in the narrations: al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith in one tradition, al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra in another, and a third that simply says *all the disbelievers.* (Notice: the occasions of revelation here are *multiple and reported differently* — we should hold them as a cluster of narrations, not a single settled event.) But al-Qurṭubī keeps turning the wish: others read it as a man wishing for **sons rather than daughters**; others, wishing for **reward without any deed to earn it**; others still, wishing that **prophethood had landed on him** instead of on Muḥammad ﷺ.

Do not treat these as five rival theories where only one wins. Treat them as **five camera angles on a single face.** Intercession you did not earn. Sons you were not given. Reward without works. A station that was never yours. Strip the specifics away and what remains is identical in every case: **an outcome the soul has measured out for itself, demanded from a world it does not own.** Al-Muyassar says it cleanly — *the human does not have what he wished, neither of intercession nor of anything else his soul craves, for to Allah belongs the affair of this world and the Hereafter.*

Which is precisely where the ayah goes next.

---

### Element Three: فَلِلَّهِ الْآخِرَةُ وَالْأُولَىٰ — The Deed to Both Worlds

The human has just been caught measuring out a world for himself. Now comes the answer — and notice it does not arrive as a rebuke. It arrives as a *statement of ownership.*

> [ayah:53:25] فَلِلَّهِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةُ وَٱلْأُولَىٰ
>
> *So to Allah belong the last and the first.*

Two pieces of grammar are doing enormous work here. Let me teach the first one.

In Arabic, the normal, neutral way to say "X belongs to Y" places things in a certain order. But you can **move the owner to the front** of the sentence — *fa-lillāhi*, "to **Allah** belong…" — and the grammarians tell us that this fronting (`taqdīm`) of what would normally come later creates **restriction and exclusivity.** It no longer means merely *"both worlds belong to Allah."* It means **"to Allah — and to Allah** ***alone*** **— belong both worlds."**

Feel the force of that against everything before it. The human has spent his life *tamannā* — measuring, allotting, issuing himself deeds to outcomes. And the ayah responds not by arguing with any single wish, but by quietly producing **the actual title deed** — and the name on it is not yours. Every wish you ever issued was a check drawn on an account you do not hold. *Fa-lillāhi* — the front-loaded Name says it before it says anything else: **the warehouse of all outcomes is His, exclusively, and was never for a moment in your name.**

Now the second piece — and this one is an *absence*, an inversion, the kind of gap where the deepest meaning hides.

Read the order again: **الْآخِرَةُ وَالْأُولَىٰ** — *the last, and the first.*

That is backwards.

In ordinary speech you say "the first and the last." You count *one, two.* You name the beginning before the end. Allah here names **al-ākhirah — the Hereafter, the last — *before* al-ūlā — this world, the first.**

Why invert the natural order of time?

[PAUSE]

Because the entire Surah has been re-teaching us *what is actually real.* And in the ledger of the real, the Hereafter is not the *afterthought* that comes second — it is the *destination* that comes first in importance, first in priority, first in weight. The human wishes almost entirely inside *al-ūlā*, the first world — the promotions, the marriages, the vindications, the comforts. He treats the Hereafter as the appendix. The ayah turns the page upside down to set it right: **the last is named first, because the last is what matters first.** The dunyā you exhaust your wishes upon is, in the true accounting, *al-ūlā* — merely "the former one," the prelude, the smaller of the two.

And there is a quiet beauty even in the sound. Listen to how the two ayahs end:

*…mā taman**nā**.*
*…wal-**ū**lā.*

The wish — *tamannā* — and the world it grasps at — *al-ūlā* — **rhyme.** They chime together at the ends of the lines, the longing and the little world it clutches, sonically bound. But between them stands the answer that owns them both: *fa-lillāhi-l-ākhiratu* — to Allah belongs the *last.* The rhyme ties your wish to the small world; the meaning lifts the real prize out of your reach and into His hand.

---

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

So look at what these eight words have built.

A particle — *am* — that catches you in the act of wishing and lets your own answer expose you. A verb — *tamannā* — whose very root reveals that to wish is to sit, illegitimately, in the seat of the One who measures out *manī* and *maniyyah*, the first breath and the last. And a closing line whose fronted Name and inverted order hand you the title deed to both worlds with someone else's signature on it — and tell you which world was the real one all along.

The grammar has shown us its hand. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about *words* — but about the way we are actually living, today, this week, inside our own heads.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Parallel Life You Live in the Conditional Tense

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a version of your life that does not exist. You visit it often. It is the life where the conversation went the way you rehearsed it. Where the money came. Where the person stayed, or left, or never hurt you. Where your body cooperated. Where the parent understood. You can furnish this life in extraordinary detail. You know its rooms. And here is the strange thing: **you can spend more hours there, in some seasons, than in the life you actually have.**

Our age has built an entire economy on this. Scroll your phone for ninety seconds and you will be sold a "manifestation" — *wish hard enough and the universe delivers.* You will see, frame after frame, the lives you do not have, edited to a gloss, until the wishing becomes a low constant ache you stop even noticing. We have, like the people of Makkah, quietly **deified the wish.** We do not carve it into stone anymore. We carve it into a feed.

This is *tamannā.* Not a sin in itself — the Prophet ﷺ taught us to ask Allah for our wishes, and *du'ā* itself is wishing turned toward its true Owner. The danger is not the longing. The danger is the **soul that takes up residence inside the longing** — Form V, the deliberate, repeated, self-furnishing daydream — until it begins to feel that the wished-for outcome is *owed.* That reality has somehow defaulted on a debt. That you are entitled to the allotment you measured out for yourself.

And here the ayah does something I find almost tender. It does not say *stop wishing.* It does not shame the longing. It asks one quiet question: ***whose is it?***

> *Or shall man have whatever he wishes for?*

Watch the movement the ayah pulls you through. You begin clutching the wish — *I need this, I am owed this.* Then the *am* loosens your grip just slightly: *…or do you actually imagine it's yours to command?* Then the root of *tamannā* whispers: *you did not even measure out the drop that made you or the death that waits for you — what makes you think you measure out this?* And finally the answer arrives — not "you can't have it," but **"it belongs to Someone."** *Fa-lillāhi-l-ākhiratu wal-ūlā.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the relief hidden in that? The exhausting thing about the parallel life is not that we want things. It is the **illusion of personal authorship** — the belief that the outcome is ours to engineer, and therefore *ours to be tormented by* when it doesn't come. The ayah lifts the pen out of our cramped hand. *You were never the author of the allotment. The deed to both worlds is His.* The grief of the unanswered wish does not vanish — but it changes address. It stops being *"reality has cheated me of what was mine"* and becomes *"the One who owns all outcomes has, for now, measured it otherwise."*

**What does this change about tomorrow?** A single practice: when you catch yourself moving into the parallel life — the rehearsed conversation, the gilded feed, the *if only* — do not scold yourself out of it. Instead, take the exact wish you are holding and **say its true address.** Turn the *tamannā* into a *du'ā.* Move the very same longing from the imaginary world where you are the apportioner, into the open hand held up to the One who actually is. The wish was never the problem. The forgotten owner was.

---

### Theme Two: The Freedom on the Other Side of "It's Not Mine"

Now let me take you somewhere that, at first, sounds like bad news — and show you it is the best news in the ayah.

*To Allah belong the last and the first.* On a flat reading, that sounds like a wall. *You don't own anything. Sit down. Stop hoping.* If that is all you hear, you have heard the words but missed the *gift inside them.*

Let me show you the gift through an image that does real work.

Imagine two people carrying a heavy ledger everywhere they go. Inside the ledger is the running account of every outcome they want — the job, the child, the healing, the apology, the vindication. The first person believes the ledger is *theirs to balance.* Every morning they wake and check the columns: *Did I get closer? Did I lose ground?* They are the accountant and the auditor and the debtor all at once. They never put the ledger down. Even in their sleep, they are doing the sums. This is the life of *tamannā* without an owner — and it is **exhausting beyond description**, because the books never balance and the audit never ends.

The second person carries the same ledger, wants the same things — but they know something the first does not. **The account is not in their name.** It is held, every column of it, *al-ākhirah wal-ūlā,* by the One who measured out their first breath and holds their last. And so, having done their honest work — having striven, asked, knocked — they are able, at the end of the day, to **set the ledger down.** Not because they stopped caring. Because they finally understood it was never theirs to balance.

[PAUSE]

That is *tawakkul.* That is the entire doctrine of reliance on Allah, hidden in the geometry of one sentence. *Fa-lillāhi-l-ākhiratu wal-ūlā* is not the slamming of a door. It is the **lifting of a weight you were never built to carry.**

And notice — this is why the Qur'an inverted the order. *Al-ākhirah* first. Because the moment you remember that the *last* world is the real prize and the larger ownership, the desperate grip on the *first* world's outcomes loosens on its own. The promotion, the vindication, the answered prayer in *al-ūlā* — they shrink to their true size when you remember that the One holding *al-ākhirah* is the same One holding them. You wanted a measure of the small world so badly that you forgot the immeasurably larger one is also entirely in His gift — and entirely available to be *asked* for.

The same Surah will say, just fifteen ayahs later: *and that man shall have nothing but what he strove for* (53:39). Hold the two together. **You do not own the *outcome* — He does. But you are handed the *striving* — that is yours.** The ledger is His; the pen of effort is in your hand. So you strive with everything, and you release the result completely. That is not resignation. That is the only sane way to carry a wish.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Tonight, name one outcome you have been carrying as if it were *yours* to secure — one column in the ledger you keep auditing in the dark. Do your honest part toward it tomorrow, fully. And then, deliberately, **say the words and set it down:** *fa-lillāhi-l-ākhiratu wal-ūlā* — the last and the first are His; this, too, is in His account, not mine. See what it does to your sleep.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see how perfectly the two ayahs are built as a single hinge.

The first ayah is **the human reaching up** — *tamannā,* the soul measuring out a world for itself, the wish rising on the rhyme *-nnā.* The second ayah is **the divine answer coming down** — *fa-lillāhi,* the owner's Name fronted for exclusivity, the deed produced, the rhyme answered with *-lā.*

Up: the wish. Down: the ownership. And the *fa-* — "so, therefore" — is the hinge between them, turning a question into its only possible answer. The human says, in effect, *I have measured out my portion.* And the heavens reply, *the measure of both worlds was never in your hand.*

The linguistic discoveries and the lived themes turn out to be **one insight seen from two sides.** The grammarian's *am munqaṭiʿa* — the question that exposes a hidden assumption — is the same thing, in words, as the spiritual disease of the unexamined wish: an assumption of ownership we never inspected. And the fronted *fa-lillāhi* — exclusivity in syntax — is the same thing, in reality, as *tawakkul*: the lifting of a ledger we were never meant to balance. The root *m-n-y* binds it all: you did not measure the drop you came from or the death you go to — so the wish in between was never yours to guarantee, only yours to *entrust.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the one wish I have been silently treating as *owed* to me — as a debt reality has failed to pay?
2. When I move into the parallel life in my head, am I *asking* the Owner — or am I *being* the owner?
3. If I truly believed both worlds were already in a Hand wiser than mine, what would I stop carrying tonight?
4. The Surah says the last belongs to Allah *before* the first. Which world do my daily wishes actually live in — and which one was I made for?
5. Have I confused striving (which is mine) with controlling the outcome (which never was)?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Man spends his life measuring out, in fantasy, a portion of a world he does not own — but the deed to both worlds, the last and the first, rests in a single Hand that wrote his first breath and holds his last.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to stop wishing. You were not asked to. You are invited only to *move the wish to its true address* — to lift it out of the airless room where you are the apportioner, and place it, openhanded, before the One whose Name comes first in the sentence and whose ownership comes first in reality. Strive fully. Then set the ledger down. The portion was never yours to guarantee. It was always His to give.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, to You alone belong the last and the first. I confess how many worlds I have built in my own hand, and how heavy the wishing has become. Take the pen of measurement back into Your hand where it always belonged. Let me strive without grasping, ask without demanding, and trust that whatever You have measured out — the first and the last of it — is better than the portion I would have written for myself. Āmīn.*
