# Only Names: When We Worship the Labels We Invent

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the words before us — first in Arabic, then in sound, then in a plain English you can hold.

> إِنْ هِيَ إِلَّآ أَسْمَآءٌ سَمَّيْتُمُوهَآ أَنتُمْ وَءَابَآؤُكُم مَّآ أَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَا مِن سُلْطَٰنٍ ۚ إِن يَتَّبِعُونَ إِلَّا ٱلظَّنَّ وَمَا تَهْوَى ٱلْأَنفُسُ ۖ وَلَقَدْ جَآءَهُم مِّن رَّبِّهِمُ ٱلْهُدَىٰ

*In hiya illā asmāʾun sammaytumūhā antum wa-ābāʾukum, mā anzala-llāhu bihā min sulṭān. In yattabiʿūna illā-ẓ-ẓanna wa-mā tahwā-l-anfus, wa-laqad jāʾahum min rabbihimu-l-hudā.*

"They are nothing but names — names that you and your forefathers have invented — for which Allah has sent down no authority whatsoever. They follow nothing but conjecture and the cravings of their own souls — even though guidance has most certainly come to them from their Lord."

### Rebuild the world

To feel the weight of this, you have to stand where the first listeners stood.

Just a few breaths earlier in this surah, Allah has done something startling. He has *spoken the names of the idols out loud.* "Have you considered al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt, the third, the other?" (53:19-20). These were not abstractions to the people of Makkah. Al-Lāt had a shrine. Al-ʿUzzā had a sacred grove and custodians who tended her. Manāt stood on the road between Makkah and Madinah, and pilgrims would shave their heads at her station. Families had been visiting these stones for generations. Grandfathers had taught grandsons the rites. There were songs. There were festivals. There was an entire economy and an entire identity wrapped around these three.

And there was something deeper than identity. There was *certainty.* When you have done a thing for three hundred years, when your father did it and his father did it, the thing stops feeling like a choice. It feels like reality itself. The idols were not a theory the Makkans held. They were the floor the Makkans stood on.

Now picture the moment. Allah names them — al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt — and lets the names hang in the air. And then, in the very next breath, He reaches out and takes the floor away.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Watch carefully what He does *not* say. He does not say "they are false gods." He does not say "they are powerless." He says something stranger, something quieter, and something far more devastating than either.

He says: *they are only names.*

Not weak gods. Not lesser gods. Not gods at all. **Words.** Sounds the mouth makes. Labels that you yourselves stuck onto stone.

There is a whole science of the human heart folded into that one move — and a mirror that, by the end of this reflection, may be uncomfortably close to your own face. Because the question this ayah is really asking is not "why did *they* worship stones?" The question is: *what are the names you are following right now — and did anything ever actually come down to back them up?*

Let us go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Asmāʾ* — the hollow word, and the root that means "to rise high"

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small idea, because everything turns on it.

There is a difference between a **name** and the **thing the name points to.** You know this instinctively. The word "water" will never make you wet. You can write "fire" on a page a thousand times and the page will not burn. A name is a pointer. Its entire job is to direct you toward something real that stands behind it. A name with nothing behind it is not a small thing — it is a *broken* thing. It is a road sign pointing into an empty field.

Now hold that, and look at what Allah says:

> إِنْ هِيَ إِلَّآ أَسْمَآءٌ — *In hiya illā asmāʾ* — "They are nothing but names."

The grammar here is a sealed box. *In... illā* is one of the strongest restriction structures in Arabic: a negation (*in* here means "not") followed by an exception (*illā*, "except"). It means: erase everything, and leave only this one thing. "They are nothing — nothing at all — *except* names." Every other possibility is shut off. Not "they are names and also a little powerful." Only names. Names, full stop.

Now sit with *asmāʾ* itself.

The word for "name," *ism*, comes from the root **s-m-w** (سمو). And this is where the ground opens. The lexicographers record that this root carries the meaning of *sumuww* — **height, elevation, rising up.** It is the very same root from which we get *samāʾ* — the **sky**, the thing that is high above you. A name, in this understanding, is something *raised up* over a thing, lifted as a banner over it so it can be recognized from a distance.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The Makkans took stones from the ground and *raised names over them.* They lifted up "al-Lāt." They hoisted "al-ʿUzzā" — a name that literally means "the Mighty One," from *ʿizz*, might. They elevated "Manāt." They performed the act of *sumuww* — they made these things *high* in their speech and in their reverence.

But here is the linguistic blade. The same root that gives us the *name* you raised up gives us the *sky* from which real authority descends. And the next clause is going to tell us that from *that* sky — the actual heights, *as-samāʾ* — **nothing came down for these names at all.**

So you have a perfect, cruel symmetry hidden in a single root. *You* manufactured the height. You did the elevating, with your tongues. But the real height, the heavens, sent nothing down to meet your elevation. The loftiness was entirely yours, entirely on the surface of your speech — a banner raised over emptiness.

These are not mighty ones. They are mighty-sounding *words.*

### Element Two: *Sammaytumūhā* — the word built entirely of human hands

Now look at the very next word, because the Qur'an does something here that you can almost *feel* with your eyes before you understand it.

> أَسْمَآءٌ سَمَّيْتُمُوهَآ — *asmāʾun sammaytumūhā* — "names *that you have named them.*"

First, notice that the root **s-m-w appears twice in a row.** The noun *asmāʾ* (names), then immediately the verb *sammaytum* (you named). It is as if the ayah is saying: these things are *names* — and they got their name-hood from one place only: *your act of naming.* The noun and the verb lock together. There is no outside source. The naming begins and ends with you.

And the verb *sammā* is **Form II** of the root — the *faʿʿala* pattern, with the doubled middle letter. In Arabic, Form II often takes a simple root and makes it *declarative* or *factitive* — it is the form of *designating*, of *assigning a status by decree.* *Sammā* doesn't mean "to discover a name." It means "to *impose* a name," to declare "you shall be called such-and-such." It is the act of a person with a label and a will, sticking the label on.

Who, exactly, did this declaring?

Look at the word as a piece of architecture. *Sammaytumūhā.* Listen to how it accumulates: *sammay-* (the act of naming) *-tum-* (you, plural) *-ū-* (a connecting vowel) *-hā* (them). It is a long word, and it grows long precisely because it is *piled high with human pronouns.* "You-all" is in there. "Them" is in there. The whole word is a little tower of human agency. And then — *antum wa-ābāʾukum* — "**you** and **your forefathers**" — Allah adds the pronouns *again,* spelling out explicitly what the verb already carried: this is *yours.* All of it. The naming, the naming again, the namers named once more.

[PAUSE]

There is no divine fingerprint anywhere on these gods. Every syllable of their existence traces back to a human mouth and an inherited habit — *antum* (you, who chose it) and *ābāʾukum* (your fathers, who handed it down). The word is constructed to leave God *completely absent* from the manufacture of these "gods." And that absence is the whole point of the next clause.

This is the same root, remember, *s-m-w*, that appears in another famous moment — when Allah "taught Ādam the names, all of them" (*ʿallama Ādama-l-asmāʾa kullahā*, 2:31). There, the names came *from* Allah and pointed *to* real created things; the naming was a gift from above, anchored in reality. Here, the names come from *you* and point to *nothing.* The contrast is exact. There is a true naming that descends and corresponds to reality — and there is a counterfeit naming that we generate and that corresponds to our wishes. One is revelation. The other is projection. Hold that word — *projection* — we will return to it.

### Element Three: *Mā anzala... min sulṭān* — and the silence of the sky

Now the blade comes down.

> مَّآ أَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَا مِن سُلْطَٰنٍ — *mā anzala-llāhu bihā min sulṭān* — "Allah has sent down no authority for them whatsoever."

Let me teach one small piece of grammar, because it intensifies everything. The phrase is *min sulṭān* — literally "of any authority." That little word *min*, placed here after a negation, is what grammarians call *min* for **complete generality** (*istighrāq*). It doesn't just say "no authority." It says "not *one shred* of authority. Not the smallest fragment. Search the heavens — you will not find a single particle of warrant for these names." The negation is total and exhaustive.

Now, two words deserve our full attention.

First, *sulṭān* (سلطان), from the root **s-l-ṭ**. We hear this word today as "sultan" — a *ruler*, one who holds power. And that is exactly the texture it carries here. *Sulṭān* is not just "evidence" in a dry, academic sense. It is *commanding authority* — proof that has *power* over you, the kind of warrant a king's decree has, that *binds* and *compels* assent. The tafsir of al-Jalālayn and al-Qurtubī gloss it precisely: *ḥujjah wa burhān* — a conclusive proof, a binding demonstration. So Allah is saying: behind these elevated names, there is no king. No throne. No decree. No power that stands behind the title.

Think of it this way. A name is a title. *Sulṭān* is the kingdom that the title is supposed to rule. And Allah is telling them: you have crowned three stones with royal titles, but **there is no kingdom behind any of them.** A king with no realm is not a king. He is a man wearing a paper crown. Al-Lāt, "the Goddess" — a title with no throne. Al-ʿUzzā, "the Mighty" — might with no kingdom. You raised the banner, but the castle behind it was never built.

Second — and this is where you must slow down completely — look at the *verb*: *anzala.* "Sent **down.**" It is **Form IV** of the root *n-z-l*, the causative form: to *cause to descend.* Authority, in the Qur'an's vocabulary, is something that *comes down* — from above, from the heights, from the *samāʾ.*

And here is the silence I want you to hear. The whole verbal image is of looking *up* at the sky and waiting for something to descend — and **nothing descends.** The Makkans raised their names *up* (remember *sumuww*, elevation). They reached and reached upward with their reverence. And from the actual heights — nothing came down to meet them. The sky was silent over their idols. *Mā anzala-llāhu bihā min sulṭān.* Not a particle came down.

[PAUSE — let this land]

Now hold that silence in one hand. Because the ayah is about to fill your other hand with its exact opposite — and the contrast is the entire architecture of the verse.

### A bridge before we go deeper

So far the words have shown us three things, and they are really one thing seen three times. The idols are *only raised-up sounds* (*asmāʾ*). The raising was done *entirely by human hands* (*sammaytumūhā antum wa-ābāʾukum*). And from the real heights, *nothing ever descended to back them* (*mā anzala-llāhu... min sulṭān*).

A name you invented, propped up by nothing from above.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about three stones in Arabia, but about the way every human heart, including yours and mine, can come to worship its own inventions.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: We still name our cravings into gods

Let me start where you live, not where the idols stood.

You have never bowed to a stone. So it would be easy to read this ayah as a museum piece — a description of primitive people doing a primitive thing, safely seven centuries and an ocean away. But the Qur'an is not a history of other people's mistakes. It is a mirror. So let us find ourselves in it.

Think about the words we use to dignify the things we already want to do.

A man who cannot stop accumulating wealth, past every reasonable need, calls it "providing for my family." A person who cannot let go of a grudge calls it "having standards." Someone avoiding a hard responsibility calls it "self-care." A reckless appetite gets named "living authentically." Cruelty gets renamed "honesty." Cowardice gets renamed "being realistic." Endless distraction gets renamed "staying informed."

Watch what is happening. There is a craving — a thing the *nafs* wants. And then there is a *name* we raise up over it, a dignified, elevated label, so that we can serve the craving while feeling that we are serving something noble. We do the *sumuww* — we lift a banner of respectability over the want. And then we follow the *name.*

[PAUSE]

This is exactly what the ayah diagnosed. *Asmāʾun sammaytumūhā* — names that *you* invented. The idol-worshipper did not, in his own mind, bow to a rock. He bowed to "the Goddess," "the Mighty One." The name made the rock worship-able. The *label was the mechanism* — it converted a piece of stone into something a dignified person could kneel before. Strip the name away and you are left with what was always there: a stone, and a desire.

And this is what the modern mind calls **projection.** We take something inside us — a fear, a craving, an inherited assumption — and we cast it outward, dress it in an authoritative-sounding name, and then bow to it as if it came from outside us, as if it had *sulṭān*, real authority over our lives. But it never did. It came from us. We sent it out and called it down. The "god" was a mirror all along.

Here is the practical edge. The most dangerous idols in your life will never look like idols. They will look like *virtues,* because you will have named them well. The work this ayah asks of you is not "stop worshipping stones." You don't. The work is: **audit your names.** Take the thing you most insist is non-negotiable, the value you most loudly defend, and ask the ayah's question of it directly — *did anything actually come down to back this, or did I name it myself and inherit the rest from people who named it before me?* (*antum wa-ābāʾukum.*) Some of your names will pass. The whole point is that some will not, and you have never once checked, because the name was doing its job — it was keeping you from looking.

What changes tomorrow: pick *one* label you use to justify a behavior you privately suspect is just a want. Take the name off it for a single day. Look at the stone underneath. That act — removing the name and looking — is the beginning of *tawḥīd* in the inner life.

### Theme Two: The two masters of the heart — and the guidance that came anyway

Now the ayah tells us *what is actually steering* a person who follows invented names. And it names two masters, with surgical precision:

> إِن يَتَّبِعُونَ إِلَّا ٱلظَّنَّ وَمَا تَهْوَى ٱلْأَنفُسُ — *In yattabiʿūna illā-ẓ-ẓanna wa-mā tahwā-l-anfus* — "They follow nothing but conjecture, and whatever their souls crave."

Again that sealed-box construction: *in... illā,* "nothing except." Erase everything else. There are only two reins on this heart, and here they are.

**The first master is *ẓann* (الظن) — conjecture.** Not knowledge. *Ẓann* is the realm of "probably," "it seems to me," "everyone says," "it stands to reason." It is the guess that has dressed itself up as a conviction. The Makkans had no proof their idols intervened — they had a *feeling* that they did, a tradition that said so, a sense that it was probably right. And they followed that feeling as if it were certainty. The Qur'an elsewhere is blunt: *inna-ẓ-ẓanna lā yughnī mina-l-ḥaqqi shayʾā* — "conjecture avails nothing at all against the truth" (53:28, just a few verses later in this very surah). A guess can feel as solid as bedrock and still be standing on nothing.

**The second master is *hawā* — desire** — in the phrase *mā tahwā-l-anfus,* "what the souls *crave.*" And here is a root worth stopping the world for.

*Tahwā* comes from the root **h-w-y** (هوي). And the lexicographers record its base meaning: **to fall. To plunge downward.** It is the same root from which comes *hāwiyah* — the name of the abyss in Sūrat al-Qāriʿah, the bottomless pit (101:9). It is the root of a thing *dropping* through empty air.

[PAUSE]

Feel what the Qur'an has done by choosing this exact word for "desire."

It did not pick a neutral word for "wanting." It picked the word for *falling.* In the Arabic imagination, *hawā* — desire, craving, the pull of the *nafs* — is not a force that lifts you. It is the force of *gravity on the soul.* It *feels* like flight. It feels like freedom, like finally doing what you want. But the very name of it, in the language Allah chose, tells you the truth: it is a *fall.* You are not soaring. You are dropping. And like all falling, it accelerates, and it feels like nothing at all until the ground.

So look at the two masters together and see the cruel comedy of it. *Ẓann* — a guess that you've mistaken for solid ground. And *hawā* — a fall that you've mistaken for flight. The person following invented names thinks they are standing on certainty and rising toward freedom. In reality they are balanced on a guess and plunging on a desire. Both of their masters are lying to them about which way is up.

Notice, too — and the classical commentators paused on this — that the ayah *shifts who it is talking to.* A breath ago it was *you* — *sammaytumūhā antum,* "*you* named them," direct, in your face, an accusation. Now suddenly it is *they* — *yattabiʿūna,* "*they* follow." Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī both note this turn explicitly: *rajaʿa mina-l-khiṭābi ilā-l-khabar* — the discourse "returns from direct address to third-person report." This is the rhetorical move the scholars of *balāghah* call *iltifāt,* the sudden pivot of address.

Why pivot here? Sit with it. While it was about the *names,* Allah confronted them face to face — *you* did this. But the moment the subject becomes the *diseased inner mechanism* — the slavery to guesswork and craving — the address turns away, as if to say: *this one has turned his back on Me, so I will speak of him, not to him.* The grammar enacts the estrangement. The one who follows only his *hawā* has, in a sense, walked out of the room of direct address with his Lord. The shift from "you" to "they" is the sound of a back being turned. (And it is striking that an early reader, ʿĪsā ibn ʿUmar, is recorded as reciting it *tattabiʿūn,* keeping the second person — as if even the Qur'an's recitation history holds open the door the verse seems to close.)

And now — *now* — the other hand opens. Remember the silence of the sky, the *nothing* that came down for the idols? Listen to the final clause:

> وَلَقَدْ جَآءَهُم مِّن رَّبِّهِمُ ٱلْهُدَىٰ — *wa-laqad jāʾahum min rabbihimu-l-hudā* — "and guidance has *most certainly come* to them, from their Lord."

[PAUSE — major]

Hear the architecture. Over the idols: *mā anzala-llāh* — Allah sent down **nothing.** Over the human being: *laqad jāʾahum* — guidance has **most certainly come.** The *laqad* is an oath-strength affirmation — "by all means, beyond any doubt, it *has* arrived." One direction is empty; the other is full. From the false gods, silence. From the true Lord, *al-hudā* — **the** guidance, definite, specific, *the* very thing they needed.

And it came *min rabbihim* — "from **their Lord.**" Not "from Allah" in the abstract here, but *rabbihim* — from the One who is *their* Sustainer, who nurtured them, raised them, fed them through every day of the very lives they were spending in His denial. The word *Rabb* is the word of intimate cultivation, the One who grows a thing from nothing to fullness. Even to the people raising names over stones, *their Rabb* did not go silent. He sent the guidance. To them. By name.

This is the tragedy the whole ayah has been building toward, and it is not the tragedy you expect.

The disaster of these people was **not that the truth never reached them.** That would be a tragedy of circumstance — pitiable, but innocent. No. *Laqad jāʾahum.* It came. It most certainly came. The map arrived. The light arrived. The guidance from their own loving Lord was *placed in their hands.*

And they preferred the names they had made, the guesses they had inherited, and the falling they had mistaken for flight.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this stops being about them.

You and I do not live in an age of *missing* guidance. We live in an age of *ignored* guidance. The Qur'an is in your pocket, translated, recited, searchable, more accessible than to any generation in human history. *Laqad jāʾakum* — it has most certainly come to *you.* So the question this ayah leaves on your chest is not "have you found the truth?" The truth found you. The question is the one the verse implies in its terrible silence between the two clauses: *given that guidance has come — what are you still following instead?* Which guess? Which craving wearing a noble name? Which inherited "everyone knows" that you have never once held up to the light?

What changes tomorrow: the next time you are about to do something, and you reach for the reason — pause and ask *which master is this.* Is this *ʿilm* (knowledge — something actually came down to back it), or is it *ẓann* (a guess I've dressed as certainty)? Is this *hudā* (guidance, which lifts), or is it *hawā* (a craving, which falls)? You will not always like the answer. But you will, for the first time, be *looking* — and the entire disaster of this ayah was a people who never looked, because the names they'd invented were so very good at keeping them from it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the whole verse as a single built thing — because it is shaped like a descent and a refusal.

It moves in two columns. On one side, *the human reach upward into emptiness:*
- **Names** raised up (*asmāʾ,* from the root of *elevation*)
- raised by **human hands alone** (*sammaytumūhā antum wa-ābāʾukum*)
- with **nothing descending** to meet them (*mā anzala-llāhu... min sulṭān*)
- steered by **guesswork** (*ẓann*) and **falling** (*hawā*)

On the other side, in a single closing clause that answers all of it:
- **the true descent** that actually happened (*laqad jāʾahum... al-hudā*)
- from **their own Lord** (*min rabbihim*).

The verse is the portrait of a people looking up the wrong staircase. They climbed and climbed on names of their own making, reaching toward a sky that sent nothing back — while behind them, all along, *the real thing had already come down and was waiting* in their hands. The whole tragedy is in the geometry: they faced the empty heights and turned their backs on the full one.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The words showed us: a *name* (*sumuww* — raised up) with no *kingdom* behind it (*sulṭān* — authority that never descended). The themes showed us a *heart* that elevates its cravings with noble labels and then bows to them, steered by a guess and a fall. They are one truth. **An idol is any name we raise up over a desire so that we can serve the desire while feeling we are serving something high.** And the cure is one act, available to anyone: take the name off, look at what is underneath, and turn toward the guidance that — *laqad* — has most certainly already come.

### Questions to carry

1. What is the most respectable *name* I use to justify a thing I privately suspect is just a want? What is the stone underneath it?
2. Of the convictions I would defend most fiercely — how many came *down* to me with real *sulṭān,* and how many did I inherit from *ābāʾī,* those before me, and simply never examined?
3. When I last made a real decision, which master held the reins — knowledge, or *ẓann*? Guidance, or *hawā*?
4. *Hawā* means "to fall." Where in my life does something feel like soaring that the Qur'an would name a descent?
5. Guidance has *most certainly come* to me. So what, honestly, am I following instead — and why have I never made myself look at it directly?

### One-sentence distillation

Their gods were never gods — only names they raised over their own cravings, propped up by guesswork and the gravity of desire — and the real catastrophe was not that truth never reached them, but that it most certainly had, and they turned their backs to face the empty sky.

### The closing invitation

You are not being asked to smash a stone. You are being invited to do something quieter and far harder: to walk through the names you live by and ask, of each one, *did anything come down to back this?* Most of your life runs on names you have never audited. This ayah is the hand that gently turns your face from the empty staircase you've been climbing — and points, behind you, to the guidance that was in your hands the whole time. You do not have to find it. *Laqad jāʾakum.* It has come. You only have to turn around.

### A brief du'ā

O Allah, You who are *our Rabb* — the One who raised us from nothing and never once went silent over us — show us the names we have invented and mistaken for truth. Strip from our hearts every idol we have dressed in a noble word, and free us from the guess we have called certainty and the fall we have called freedom. And the guidance You have most certainly sent down — let us not be among those who held it in their hands and faced the other way.

*Āmīn.*
