# The One Behind the Laughter and the Tears You Cannot Hold Back

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by simply placing the words in front of you.

> وَأَنَّهُۥ هُوَ أَضْحَكَ وَأَبْكَىٰ

*Wa annahu huwa aḍḥaka wa abkā.*

*"And that it is He — He alone — who makes [one] laugh and makes [one] weep."*

Four words after the opening particle. One of the shortest statements in the whole surah. And in those four words, Allah does something to your understanding of yourself that, if it lands, you will not be able to undo.

Before we open it, I want you to come and stand where the first listeners stood.

### Rebuild the world

Surah An-Najm is one of the early Makkan surahs. It is fast. It is sworn. It opens with an oath upon a star as it falls — *wa al-najmi idhā hawā* — and it moves at the speed of revelation itself, short verses snapping one after another like a heartbeat under stress. The whole surah is a defense of what the Prophet ﷺ saw and received, against a city that called him deluded. It climbs all the way up to the Lote Tree at the boundary of the heavens, the highest point a created being has ever been shown, and then it comes back down to earth and starts settling accounts.

And right before our verse, the surah reaches its great turning point. Allah says: *"that man shall have nothing but what he strove for"* (53:39). *"And that to your Lord is the final end"* — *wa anna ilā rabbika al-muntahā* (53:42). Everything is running toward Him. Every road ends at His door.

Now — having told the human being where he is going — Allah turns and begins a list. A rapid, breathless litany, each line beginning with the same two words, *wa annahu huwa* — *and that it is He, He Himself.* It is He who makes you laugh and makes you weep. It is He who brings death and gives life. It is He who created the two mates, the male and the female. It is He upon whom rests the second creation. It is He who enriches and gives.

Pair after pair after pair. Opposites that the human mind keeps in separate drawers, and Allah keeps reaching into both drawers with the same hand.

And here is what I want you to feel about the world these words landed in. The people of Makkah were not atheists. They believed in Allah as the distant Creator. What they could not let go of was the *middle layer* — the gods, the patrons, the causes, the intermediaries who, they believed, actually ran the day-to-day. The thing that touches your life, they thought, is never God directly. It is always some power in between.

### The Hook

So watch what this list does. It walks straight into the most ordinary, most involuntary thing a human body does — it picks the laugh that bursts out of you before you can stop it, and the tears that come up your throat when you did not give them permission — and it says: *behind even that, there is no middle layer. That is Me.*

Here is the question I want you carrying through everything that follows. Of all the things Allah could have begun this divine list with — and notice, He begins it before even mentioning death and life — **why does He start with laughter and tears?** Why open the catalogue of His power not with the cosmos, not with the grave, but with the two things your own face does without asking you first?

Stay with that. Let's go and find the answer in the words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa annahu huwa* — when the language doubles down on "He, and no one else"

Let me teach you something small about how emphasis works before we touch the verse.

In English, if I want to stress that a particular person did something, I lean on my voice: "*He* paid for dinner." The stress carries the weight. But Arabic does not need your voice. Arabic has built emphasis directly into its grammar, with actual words whose only job is to say *this one and not another.*

Imagine I tell you in plain Arabic, *qāma Zayd* — "Zayd stood." Now imagine I add a second pronoun that grammatically I do not need: *Zaydun huwa qāma* — literally "Zayd, *he* stood." That extra *huwa*, "he," is not carrying new information. Zayd is already the subject. So what is it doing? Grammarians call it **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — the pronoun of separation, or the pronoun of emphasis. It is a wedge. It drives in between the subject and everything else and says: *this one, specifically, exclusively, him.* It walls the action off so that no one else can climb in and claim a share.

Now look at how our verse is built:

> وَأَنَّهُۥ **هُوَ** أَضْحَكَ

*Wa anna-hu* — "and that He…" The "He" is already there, tucked into the ending of *annahu.* The subject is already named. Allah is already the one being spoken about.

And then — *huwa.* "He." Again. The pronoun that grammar did not require. The wedge.

Notice something. There are now **two** ways of saying "He" stacked on top of each other in a four-word sentence: the *hu* attached to *anna,* and then the standalone *huwa.* Arabic does not waste words; the Quran least of all. So this doubling is not decoration. It is a door being shut. It is the language saying: *Him. And then, in case you reached for someone else — Him again. Only Him.*

Let's sit with what that excludes.

Al-Qurṭubī, reading exactly this verse, writes a sentence that I want to give you in full because it is one of the most piercing lines in the classical commentary on An-Najm:

> *"Dhahabat al-wasāʾiṭ wa baqiyat al-ḥaqāʾiq."*
> **"The intermediaries fell away, and the realities remained."**

The intermediaries. The middlemen. The whole crowded layer of *secondary causes* that we instinctively credit. He says: in the face of this *huwa,* they all dissolve — *fa-lā fāʿila illā huwa,* there is no doer except Him.

Now I need you to feel how radical that is, because we are exactly like the Makkans here; we have just renamed our idols.

When you laugh, what made you laugh? "The joke." "My friend's face." "The video." When you cried last, what made you cry? "The news." "The song." "What he said to me." We have a whole layer of intermediaries — triggers, causes, circumstances — and we hand them the credit instantly and automatically. The joke made me laugh. The loss made me weep. The cause did it.

And the verse walks up to that entire layer, and with one doubled pronoun, *huwa… huwa,* it says: the joke is real, the loss is real — but they are not the *doer.* They are the **occasion.** They are not the **author.** *Dhahabat al-wasāʾiṭ.* The intermediaries fall away. The joke was holding a door; it was never the One who walked through it to reach your chest and shake the laughter out of you.

And now consider the surah's architecture around this moment. The verses immediately preceding ours — 53:38 through 53:40 — had just announced something related but distinct: *"that no bearer of burdens bears another's burden, and that a human being has nothing except what he strove for."* Al-Ṭabarī reads that passage as addressed to those Makkans who had been assured by a patron — an idol-intercessor — that the patron would step forward and absorb their accountability, carry their weight at the reckoning. The whole idol-system rested on this belief: that a divine middle-agent stood between you and the consequences of your life. And then, with that argument still reverberating, the litany immediately begins: *wa annahu huwa aḍḥaka wa abkā.* The two movements are one movement, made across adjacent verses. On the front of the Final Reckoning: your intermediary cannot carry your burden. On the front of daily life: your intermediary does not author your joy and grief either. The same idols that claimed to run the machinery of cosmic justice turn out to be absent from the laugh happening in your chest right now. *Dhahabat al-wasāʾiṭ* — the intermediaries fell away on every front, at once.

[PAUSE]

This is the grammar of *tawḥīd.* This is *lā ilāha illā Allāh* spelled out at the level of a single involuntary breath. Not "there is no creator but Allah" as a slogan you agree with from a distance — but "there is no *doer* of this laugh, right now, in your own throat, but Allah."

One doubled pronoun. And the entire middle layer of your life, the layer you give all the credit to, quietly falls away.

### Element Two: *Aḍḥaka* and *abkā* — the form that moves from the act to its Author

Here is the second thing, and it lives inside the verbs themselves.

Let me teach the structure first, plainly, the way Arabic builds it.

There is a simple, base form of "to laugh": *ḍaḥika* — *he laughed.* It happened to him; it came out of him. And there is a base form of "to weep": *bakā* — *he wept.* These are the everyday words. The thing your face does.

Now Arabic has a gear it can shift the verb into. By adding a small alif at the front and reshaping the vowels, it converts the verb from *"doing the thing"* into *"making someone else do the thing."* Grammarians call it **Form IV**, the *afʿala* pattern, and one of its core functions — the function operating right here — is **causation.** *ʿalima,* he knew → *aʿlama,* he made-known, he informed. *Jalasa,* he sat → *ajlasa,* he made-someone-sit, he seated them.

So watch the gear shift on our two verbs:

- *Ḍaḥika,* he laughed → **أَضْحَكَ** *aḍḥaka,* **He made [someone] laugh.**
- *Bakā,* he wept → **وَأَبْكَىٰ** *abkā,* **He made [someone] weep.**

This is confirmed in the corpus: both verbs here are Form IV. And this is everything. Look at what the verse did *not* say. It did not say "Allah laughs and weeps." It did not even say, at this layer, that *you* laugh and weep. It reached past the act entirely, slipped the verb into the causative gear, and named **the one who produces the act in you.**

Do you feel the move? Your laughter belongs to you — it is *your* face, *your* breath, *your* helpless shaking. And the verse says: yes, and the One who reached in and *caused* it is not you. *Aḍḥaka.* He-made-you-laugh.

Here's what is remarkable about choosing these two verbs to carry the point. Of all the things a human being does, **laughter and weeping are the two we control the least.** Think about it honestly. You can decide to walk. You can decide to speak, to eat, to lie down. But can you decide, right now, from nothing, to genuinely laugh? Not perform it — *feel* it, the real involuntary bark of it? You cannot summon it. And can you, sitting in a moment of fine composure, decide to weep real tears for no reason? You cannot manufacture them either. Both of them *erupt.* They overrule you. They arrive announced by nothing and they leave when they choose. The polygraph, the lie detector, works precisely because some bodily responses cannot be faked or forced — and laughter and tears sit at the very top of that list.

So Allah takes the two human acts that feel **most beyond anyone's control** — including beyond *your own* control — and slides them into the causative form, and names the Causer. He is telling you: *the very fact that you cannot command these — that is the fingerprint. The thing in you that overrules even you, that is Me, working in you, closer than the cause you would have blamed.*

Al-Baghawī draws exactly this conclusion from the verb: *"this proves that everything a human does is by His decree and His creating — even the laughing, even the weeping."* If the laugh that bursts out beyond your control is authored, then nothing smaller than that is unauthored.

And now let me give you the **composite picture** the classical commentators built around these two verbs — not as competing options, but as the same light seen through three windows.

Look through the **first window** — the heart. Al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, and ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Muslim all read it at the level of your inner state: *He gladdened whom He willed, so they laughed; He grieved whom He willed, so they wept.* As ʿAṭāʾ puts it, *joy draws the laughter up, and grief draws the weeping up.* He does not merely move your face; He moves the **emotion underneath** the face, and the face only follows. The laughter is downstream of a joy He placed; the tears are downstream of a sorrow He allowed.

Al-Muyassar is precise about where this first window lives: He gladdened whom He willed *in this worldly life,* and grieved whom He willed in it. That tethering to *al-dunyā* — to this present life, not a future or cosmic one — matters. The second window (the Hereafter) and the third (the seasons of the earth) can feel elevated and distant. But the first window is happening *today,* in the chest of the person sitting with this verse. Which means the eschatological and cosmic readings are not abstractions floating above your immediate experience but interpretations *of* it, naming what is always already occurring in you right now.

Look through the **second window** — the Hereafter. Mujāhid and al-Kalbī read it forward, to the end of the road this whole surah is pointing at: *He will make the people of the Garden laugh in the Garden, and the people of the Fire weep in the Fire.* Every laugh now is a small rehearsal of a final laughter; every tear now, a small foretaste of a final weeping. The two emotions are not just states — they are *destinations* in seed form.

Look through the **third window** — the cosmos itself. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk reads it at the scale of the whole earth and sky: *He makes the earth "laugh" with blossoming plants, and the sky "weep" with rain.* The Arabs already called a meadow bursting into flower a "laughing" land. So the rain falls — the sky weeps — and the earth laughs back in green. The same pairing that governs your face governs the seasons.

Now hold all three windows open at once. Your private heart. The final Day. The turning of the whole world. And the verse says the **same four words** describe all three. The laughter in your chest, the laughter of the saved, and the laughter of a hillside in spring — *one Author, one verb, one hand.* You are not an exception to the system of the world. You are a small, living instance of it.
