# The Second Growing

## وَأَنَّ عَلَيْهِ ٱلنَّشْأَةَ ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ ۝ وَأَنَّهُۥ هُوَ أَغْنَىٰ وَأَقْنَىٰ

*wa anna ʿalayhi an-nashʾata-l-ukhrā · wa annahu huwa aghnā wa aqnā*

**"And that upon Him is the second bringing-forth. And that it is He who enriched and gave."**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

Let me put you somewhere first. Before a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

You are in Makkah. The surah we are inside — An-Najm — is one long, gathering storm of certainty. It opens with a star falling, *wan-najmi idhā hawā*, and it does not let the listener catch their breath. It defends the Prophet ﷺ against the accusation that he is lost, that he speaks from desire, that he made this up. *Mā ḍalla ṣāḥibukum wa mā ghawā* — your companion has not strayed, nor does he err. And then it climbs — through the vision at the lote tree, through the dismantling of every false god the Arabs prayed to — until it arrives at a stretch of verses that read almost like a heartbeat.

Listen to the rhythm of where we've landed. *Wa annahu huwa aḍḥaka wa abkā* — and that it is He who makes laugh and makes weep. *Wa annahu huwa amāta wa aḥyā* — and that it is He who gives death and gives life. *Wa annahu khalaqa-z-zawjayni-dh-dhakara wal-unthā* — and that He created the two mates, the male and the female. Clause after clause, each one beginning the same way, each one another line on a résumé that belongs to no one but God.

Now feel the climate this was spoken into. The people of Makkah could accept almost all of it. That Allah gives life and death? They half-believed it. That He created them the first time? They would not deny it — they knew they had not made themselves. There was exactly one claim in this whole list that they choked on. One.

That the dead would be brought back.

This was the laugh-line of the disbelievers. This was the thing they thought was so absurd it discredited the whole message. They would pick up a crumbling, weathered bone from the desert, crush it to powder in their fist, let the wind take it, and say: *who will revive these, when they are rotted?* To them, decomposition was the end of the argument. Scattered is scattered. Gone is gone.

And into that exact wound — into the one claim they mocked — Allah drops verse 47. He does not argue. He does not raise His voice. He simply adds it to the list, in the same calm rhythm as making someone laugh or cry, as if to say: *this belongs here, with all the rest of what I do.*

**Here is the hook.** When Allah names this thing they found impossible — the raising of the dead — He does not reach for the word you would expect. He does not call it a *rebuilding*. He does not call it a *manufacturing* or a *putting-back-together*. He reaches for a word that, everywhere else in the language, describes a *seed becoming a plant*. A word for *growing*. And then He does something even stranger with the grammar — He places this second growing not as something He *might* do, not even as something He *will* do, but as something that rests **upon Him**. A weight on His own side of the ledger.

Stay with me. Because once you see what word He chose, and where He placed it, the bone in the disbeliever's fist stops being an argument against resurrection — and quietly becomes an argument *for* it.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: النَّشْأَة — The Word for Growing

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word, because the whole verse turns on it.

Arabic, like every language, has more than one way to talk about a thing coming into existence. You can *make* something — that's *ṣanaʿa*, the verb of a craftsman at a workbench, assembling parts. You can *create* something — that's *khalaqa*, to bring out of nothing, to originate. And there is a third word, a quieter one, that does not belong to the workshop at all. It belongs to the garden.

That word is built on the root **n-sh-ʾ** (ن-ش-ء).

Think about where this root lives in ordinary speech. A *nāshiʾ* is a youth — a child in the middle of *growing up*, unfolding from small to full. When the Quran wants to describe the night prayer rising in the stillness, it says *nāshiʾat al-layl* — that which *arises*, *emerges*, in the depth of night. The root is about something coming up and out under its own unfolding — a sprout breaking soil, a child lengthening into a man, a thing *developing into being* rather than being bolted together.

Now look at the word Allah chose for what He will do to your scattered body.

**ٱلنَّشْأَةَ** — *an-nashʾah*. The noun of that very root.

[PAUSE]

Let that land slowly. The disbelievers were imagining resurrection as *reassembly* — and of course reassembly sounds absurd when the parts are dust on the wind. How do you find every grain again? How do you fit a powder back into a skeleton? They were picturing a man at a workbench trying to rebuild a shattered pot, and they were right to find it grotesque.

But Allah never offered them a workbench. **He offered them a garden.** The word He chose does not mean *I will collect your scattered pieces and glue them.* It means *I will make you grow again.*

Notice what that quietly does to the whole argument. A seed does not fear the winter. A seed is not anxious that its substance has dissolved into the dark soil, that it can no longer be seen, that it has — to every watching eye — *rotted*. The dissolving is not the failure of the seed. **The dissolving is the first half of the growing.** The farmer is not worried when the seed disappears into the ground. He buried it on purpose.

So when the man in Makkah crushed the old bone to powder and said *who could revive this?* — he thought he was holding the proof that resurrection is impossible. He was holding a seed and laughing at it for not being a tree. Allah's word answers him without raising its voice: *that powder in your hand is not the end of the process. It is the soil stage of it.*

And here is something remarkable, and you can verify it across the whole Quran. This is not the only place Allah uses this root for resurrection. In Sūrat al-Wāqiʿah He says: *wa laqad ʿalimtum an-nashʾata-l-ūlā fa-lawlā tadhakkarūn* — **you already know the first growing; will you not then reflect?** And in al-ʿAnkabūt: *thumma-llāhu yunshiʾu-n-nashʾata-l-ākhirah* — **then Allah brings about the latter growing.** The same root, again and again, deliberately. The Quran is consistent: it never frames your re-creation as *repair*. It frames it as a *second spring*.

Al-Jalālayn notes, almost in passing, that the very word carries two valid recitations — *nashʾah* with a lengthened vowel, and *nashʾa* with a shortened one. Both recitations are attested, both canonically valid. Both point to the same root. The Arabic language, in both forms of its breath, is saying the same organic thing: *something rises, grows, and becomes.* Even the pronunciation of the word lives.

The four commentators in front of us all read this verse as the second creation for the resurrection after the first creation — al-Jalālayn says *al-khilqah al-ukhrā lil-baʿth baʿd al-khilqah al-ūlā*; al-Baghawī, *al-khalq ath-thānī lil-baʿth yawma-l-qiyāmah*; al-Muyassar, *iʿādatu khalqihim baʿda mamātihim*. Their meanings converge. And al-Ṭabarī, in his commentary on the preceding clause of this same rhythmic chain — the one that says Allah *gave life* (*aḥyā*) — pauses to explain what that life-giving actually meant: breathing a soul into what was itself a *dead* thing, the *nutfah*, the inert fluid that carried no life of its own until He placed life into it. The first creation, in other words, did not begin with something already alive. It began with what was plainly lifeless. Life, from the very first, has always emerged from what appeared dead. This is not a new pattern Allah is introducing in verse 47. It is the pattern by which He already made you the first time.

But notice what the *word itself* adds beneath their gloss — a texture none of us would invent: the texture of **growth**. Of life that comes up, not parts that get bolted back. The scholars tell you *what* it refers to. The root tells you *what it feels like*. It feels like spring.

**What this one word reveals:** Allah did not answer the fear of decomposition by denying it. He answered it by renaming it. What you call *rotting*, He calls *planting*.

### Element Two: عَلَيْهِ — The Weight He Put on His Own Side

Now I want you to look at a word so small you would read straight past it. Two letters and a pronoun. **عَلَيْهِ** — *ʿalayhi* — "upon Him."

Let me teach you the preposition first, in plain life, before we bring it here.

In Arabic, the preposition **ʿalā** — "upon," "on" — does something specific when you attach it to a person and a thing-to-be-done. It makes that thing into a **weight that rests on them**. An *obligation*. A *debt owed*. If I say *ʿalayya dayn*, I am not saying "a debt is near me" — I am saying *a debt is **upon** me*, it sits on my shoulders, it is mine to discharge. If I say *ʿalayka an tafʿala kadhā*, I am saying *it is incumbent **upon** you to do such-and-such.* The word puts the thing on your account. It becomes something you owe, something that will not be settled until you pay it.

That is the word Allah chose for the second creation.

Read the verse again with this in your ears: *wa anna **ʿalayhi** an-nashʾata-l-ukhrā* — **and that the second growing is *upon Him*.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with how staggering this is. Allah does not say *He is able to* bring about the second creation — though He is. He does not say *He will* — though He will. He places it on the side of the ledger that reads *incumbent upon Me*. He writes your resurrection into His own column as a **debt He has taken on Himself**.

Now — be careful and be precise here, because this is holy ground and we do not exaggerate on it. No creature *imposes* a duty on Allah. Nothing has a claim against Him from the outside; He is asked about nothing and answerable to none. So what is this *ʿalayhi*? It is an obligation **He freely placed upon Himself** by His own promise. It is self-binding. It is the difference between *I could help you* and *I have given you my word.* And al-Qurṭubī catches exactly this when he comments on the verse: *waʿada dhālika, wa waʿduhu ṣidq* — **He has promised this, and His promise is truth.** The weight is the weight of a *promise made by the One who cannot break it.*

He is, if we can speak this way, the One who has bound His own honor to making sure you arrive.

Al-Muyassar phrases it with a directness that lands like a hand on the shoulder. The tafsir addresses the verse to the Prophet ﷺ himself: *wa anna ʿalā rabbika ayyuhā ar-rasūl iʿādata khalqihim baʿda mamātihim* — "upon your Lord, O Messenger, is the returning of their creation after their death." The promise was delivered to a man being called a liar and a madman in the streets of Makkah. To a man whose community was holding the argument of the old bone and finding it devastating. And the grammar of the verse was its answer: *the thing they call impossible — I have put it on My own account. I have taken it. You go on.*

Feel what that does to your fear of the Day. Most of us, when we think of resurrection and the standing before God, feel it as a threat hanging over us — an event we are dragged toward. But the grammar reframes the entire relationship. The Day you dread is not something being done *to* you against an indifferent sky. It is something your Lord has placed *upon Himself* to deliver. **Your return to Him is a debt He intends to pay.** He is not the prosecutor reluctantly hauling you in. He is the One who has bound His own honor to making sure you arrive.

And think about *who* He is for that promise to mean what it means. A promise is only ever as strong as the one who makes it. A man promises and dies before he can keep it. A man promises and forgets. A man promises and simply can't — the means slip out of his hands. Strip those three failures away — death, forgetfulness, inability — and you have the One making this promise. The promise of a Lord who does not die, does not forget, and is never unable, is not a *hope*. It is a *fact that has not happened yet.*

**What this small word reveals:** Allah did not merely tell you resurrection is possible. He moved it onto His own account and signed it. Your standing before Him is not a sword over your head — it is a **word He has given.**

### Element Three: ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ — "The Other," and the One He Left Unspoken

One more word, and this one teaches you to read a silence.

**ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ** — *al-ukhrā*. It comes from the root **ʾ-kh-r** (ء-خ-ر), the root of *ākhir*, "the last," and *ākhirah*, "the Hereafter" itself. As an adjective here it means *the other one*, *the second*, *the latter*. Allah calls this growing **the other growing**.

Here is the rhetorical move, and you have to listen for what is *not* said to hear it. When you call something "the other," you are leaning on something the listener already has in hand. You cannot have *the other* shoe without a *first* shoe. You cannot have *the second* act without a *first* act sitting in everyone's memory. The word *al-ukhrā* — "the latter," "the other" — **silently summons the first.** It says: *you already know the one I'm comparing this to.*

And look — Allah does not bother to name the first one here. He does not say *the first growing and the second growing.* He names only the second, and lets the first hang in the air as a given, because it is the one thing not even the deniers could argue with. **You are alive. You were grown once already.** Your existence is the first *nashʾah*, walking around, breathing, holding the very bone you crush in mockery. The first growing is not a claim Allah has to defend. It is standing right there in the form of the person doubting the second.

Do you feel the trap closing, gently? By naming only *al-ukhrā* — "the *other* one" — Allah forces you to supply *al-ūlā*, "the first one," from your own life. And the moment you have admitted the first, the second is no longer an outrageous new claim. It is just *the same thing, again.* This is precisely the argument al-Wāqiʿah makes explicit — *you already knew the first growing; will you not reflect?* The verse here in An-Najm makes the same argument *silently*, by a single word, by what it declines to repeat.

He just calls the next life *"the other one"* — and leaves the proof of it sitting in your own chest.

And there is a deeper thing folded into the choice of *ukhrā* over a plain "second." Because this root, ʾ-kh-r, is the root of *al-Ākhirah* — the Hereafter. The "other growing" is not just the second of two. It is the growing that *belongs to the other world*. It carries, in its very letters, the address of where you are going. The word for "again" and the word for "eternity" are cut from the same cloth. When you say *al-ukhrā*, you are not just counting — you are pointing toward another plane of existence that has been waiting in the horizon of language all along.

**What this word reveals:** Allah proves the impossible using the one thing you cannot deny — your own existence — and He does it without spending a single word to argue. He names the second; you must supply the first from your own memory of being alive. The argument is complete before you notice it has happened.

## PART 2 — THE LEDGER OF HIS GIVING

### وَأَنَّهُۥ هُوَ أَغْنَىٰ وَأَقْنَىٰ — And That It Is He Who Enriched and Gave

Now we move to verse 48, and I want you to notice something before I say a single word about it.

The verse that just told you Allah has taken your resurrection upon Himself is immediately followed by a verse about *provision*. About wealth and sufficiency and giving. This is not a detour. It is not a change of subject. It is the Quran placing two acts of divine ownership side by side: He owns your future — the second nashʾah, the return — and He owns your present. The grain in your pantry and the atom in your scattered bones are both on His ledger. The same Lord governs both. Neither is accidental, and neither is yours to secure on your own.

Feel the architecture. One God. One account. Everything you have and everything you will become, both written under His name.

**أَغْنَىٰ** — *aghnā*. The verb comes from the root **gh-n-y** (غ-ن-ي), the same root from which we get *ghanī* — the Self-Sufficient One, one of His names. To enrich. To make free from need. Al-Jalālayn glosses it plainly: *aghnā an-nās bil-kifāyah bil-amwāl* — "He enriched people with sufficiency through wealth." The word *kifāyah* matters here. It does not mean *lavish abundance* or luxury. It means *enough*. It means that the pressing need has been met. There is a feeling in every tongue for the specific relief of *enough* — not excess, not the weight of too much, but the arrival at *sufficient* — and that is what *aghnā* names. He brought you to the point where the lack stopped burning.

**أَقْنَىٰ** — *aqnā*. And here is where the two classical readings in our sources quietly diverge, and the divergence is itself the insight.

Al-Jalālayn reads *aqnā* as the giving of something more permanent: *aʿṭā al-māl al-muttakhadh qinyah* — "He gave the wealth that is taken as a lasting holding." A *qinyah* is not the meal you eat tonight. It is the property you own, the asset you keep, the possession that remains after the immediate need is met. If *aghnā* is the food on the table, *aqnā* is the field that keeps producing it. If *aghnā* is the relief of today's sufficiency, *aqnā* is the endowment of tomorrow's security. Two distinct gifts: the meeting of urgent need, and the granting of lasting resource.

Al-Muyassar, reading the same word, lingers on something different: *wa mallakahu lahum wa arḍāhum bihi* — "and He gave them ownership of it, **and made them satisfied with it**." The final phrase — *arḍāhum bihi*, He made them content, He pleased them with what they received — carries a different register entirely. On this reading, *aqnā* points not to a larger pile of wealth but to an inner state. Contentment. The settled satisfaction that makes what you already have feel like enough — which is, in many ways, the richer gift. To be given more is one kind of blessing. To be given peace with what you have is another kind altogether.

[PAUSE]

Sit with both readings, because they may not be alternatives so much as layers of the same truth. He enriched people — gave them sufficiency. And then He gave something more: either the lasting holdings that carry them forward, or the inner contentment that needs no carrying. The classical commentators do not force a choice between these, and perhaps the Quran does not either. The same root can bear both. And a Lord who gives you the field *and* the peace about the field has given you everything.

Now look at the grammar of the verse one more time: *wa annahu **huwa** aghnā wa aqnā* — "and that it is **He Himself** who enriched and gave." That *huwa* — "He Himself" — is emphatic, restrictive. Not luck. Not the market. Not your cleverness, your connections, your effort alone. **He Himself.** This is the same pattern running through the whole cascade of An-Najm — verse after verse, God reclaiming every act that the human mind might attribute to something else. He makes laugh. *He* makes weep. *He* creates the two mates. *He* gives life. And *He Himself* enriches.

You may have worked for what you have. That work is real and it counts. But the One who arranged the circumstances, opened the doors, moved the people, held the disasters at bay, and placed the sense of sufficiency in your chest when you arrived at enough — *that was Him.* The verbs are past tense — *aghnā*, *aqnā* — accomplished, completed, done. Not a future promise but a retroactive ownership claim. He looks back at everything in your life that functioned as provision, and signs His name to it. *That was Me.*

And now read verse 47 alongside verse 48, and feel the full span of what is being declared. The One who has placed your resurrection on His own account is the same One who has been enriching and endowing you all your life. His track record of provision is not separate from His promise of return. Both are signatures of the same Lord. If you have any evidence that He provides — a moment of relief, a sufficiency that came when you did not arrange it, a day when the need was met without your knowing how — then you have evidence for Who will fulfill the second nashʾah. He is not inconsistent. He does not enrich you here and abandon you there.

**What these two verses together reveal:** The God who wrote your resurrection onto His own ledger has been writing your provision on the same ledger all along. You were never self-sufficient for a single day. And you will not be abandoned for a single moment of what comes next. He has signed both accounts.

## CODA — The Bone in the Fist

Come back to the man in Makkah. He is still standing there, the powdered bone sifting through his fingers, his laugh at the ready.

He looked at the bone and saw the end of the argument. Allah looked at it and saw a seed. The man saw decomposition as the final verdict. Allah named it the soil stage of a growing that has not stopped.

What changed between the man's reading and God's? Not the bone. Not the dust. The same physical fact, in the same desert. What changed was the *word* placed over it. When you call something *reassembly*, you make the scattered-ness into an obstacle. When you call something *growing*, you make the scattered-ness into the process. *Nashʾah* — the word that carries a youth unfolding, a night prayer rising, a sprout clearing the surface — placed over the scattered bone, makes it something a farmer would recognize. A stage. A season. Not the end.

And notice how Allah placed this word. He set it in the middle of a list of things He does — things as ordinary as laughter and tears, as intimate as the determining of a child's sex before birth. He sandwiched the resurrection between the creating of male and female (53:46) and His own enriching and giving (53:48). He did not isolate it as the great anomaly, the terrifying exception, the claim that strains belief. He made it *one of His works* — the way you would list your own daily acts. I made them laugh. I made them weep. I made them in pairs. I will grow them a second time. I enriched them. I gave.

The list does not pause at the resurrection. It does not slow its breathing. It moves through it in the same *wa anna* rhythm, unbroken, as if to say: *this is not more difficult for Me than any of the others. You have already witnessed My other acts. Did laughter surprise you? Did a birth? Then why should a second spring?*

He enriched you once, and the verb is past tense — it is done and signed. He has put your resurrection on His own account — it is incumbent and it will be paid. The man with the bone was holding an argument. He was actually holding a seed. Allah has been writing both its past and its future into the same ledger all along.

The man can let go now.
