# The Pair From a Drop

## An-Najm 53:45-46

> وَأَنَّهُۥ خَلَقَ ٱلزَّوْجَيْنِ ٱلذَّكَرَ وَٱلْأُنثَىٰ ۝ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ إِذَا تُمْنَىٰ

*Wa annahu khalaqa l-zawjayni l-dhakara wa l-unthā · min nuṭfatin idhā tumnā*

**"And that it is He who created the two mates — the male and the female — from a drop of fluid when it is emitted."**

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Before we open these words, we have to stand where the first listeners were standing. Because if we don't, this ayah will sound like a simple biology lesson — *Allah made males and females, from a drop, fine, next* — and we will walk straight past one of the most devastating arguments in the whole Quran.

So come back with me to Mecca.

This is Surah An-Najm. It opens with an oath by **the star as it sets** — *wa l-najmi idhā hawā*. It is one of the most electric openings in the Quran, and the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah out loud, in public, near the Kaʿba. Tradition records that when he reached its final ayah — *fa-sjudū lillāhi waʿbudū*, "so prostrate to Allah and worship Him" — he went down into sajda, and the entire gathering went down with him, believers and idol-worshippers together, because the words had overwhelmed them. This is a surah that physically put people on the ground.

And what is it about? In its heart, this surah does two things. First, it defends the Prophet's vision — *mā ḍalla ṣāḥibukum wa mā ghawā*, "your companion has not strayed nor erred" — his true sight of revelation, the ascension, the lote-tree at the boundary. And then it turns and **demolishes the gods of the Quraysh.**

Listen to how it does it. A few dozen verses before our ayah, Allah says:

> *"Have you then considered al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā — and Manāt, the third, the other one? Is the male for you and for Him the female? That, then, would be an unjust division!"* (53:19-22)

Sit with what the Quraysh were doing. They worshipped three goddesses — **al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt** — and they called them the *daughters* of Allah. They assigned to Allah female offspring. And in the same breath, in their own homes, they buried their female infants alive and prized their sons. They kept the males for themselves and handed the "lesser" gender — in their eyes — up to God. The Quran exposes the contradiction with a blade: *you split male and female into higher and lower, you took the higher for yourselves, and you insulted your Creator with the rest.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold that. Because some twenty verses later, in the litany we are about to enter, Allah returns to the very same two words — *the male and the female* — and He does something with them that I want you to feel the force of, not just understand.

This is the **hook.** The Quraysh used "male and female" as a tool of contempt — a hierarchy, a put-down, a way of dividing the world into the worthy and the throwaway. And Allah is about to take those exact two words, *al-dhakar* and *al-unthā*, and place them side by side as **equal twin works of His hand, drawn from one and the same drop.** Watch what happens to a hierarchy when you trace both of its halves back to the identical humble origin.

There is a second thing buried here, and it is even quieter. The third goddess was named **Manāt**. And the last word of our passage is **tumnā**. Hold those two sounds in your ear — *Manāt*, *tumnā* — and let me earn that for you by the end. For now, just notice that the surah is not done with these idols. It is about to bury them inside a single emitted drop.

Let's go in slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We have three small windows to open in these two short ayahs. The first is a number. The second is a pair of names. And the third is a single verb that does almost all the theological work — and rhymes, sound for sound, with a dead goddess.

### Element One: *Al-Zawjayn* — The Word That Cannot Stand Alone

Let me teach you a piece of Arabic before we touch the ayah, because once you have it, the verse will open by itself.

In Arabic, nouns come in three numbers, not two. There is the singular — one. There is the plural — three or more. And uniquely, sitting between them, there is the **dual** — *al-muthannā* — a special grammatical form that means *exactly two*, no more, no less. English doesn't have this. We say "one star" or "stars," and if we want two we have to borrow the word "two" and prop it next to the plural. Arabic doesn't borrow. It bends the word itself into a shape that means *a matched pair*. You take *zawj* and you make it *zawjayn* — and the very skeleton of the word now says: **two-that-belong-together.**

So the word in our ayah, *al-zawjayn*, is already carrying its meaning in its bones before we even translate it.

Now the root. The word is from **z-w-j** (ز و ج). And here is what every Arab in that gathering knew in their gut, and what we have to slow down to recover. A *zawj* does not mean "a couple." A *zawj* means **one half of a pair.** Your spouse is your *zawj* — and so are *you*. One shoe of a pair is a *zawj*. One bird, when there is another it flies with, is a *zawj*. The word names a single thing **defined entirely by the fact that it has a counterpart.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land. A *zawj* is a thing that, by its very name, **cannot stand alone.** To call something a *zawj* is to say: it is incomplete by design. It points beyond itself. It was built to need.

And what does Allah say He created? Not "a male." Not "a female." Not even, simply, "males and females." He says *al-zawjayn* — **the two mates**, the pair-as-a-pair, two halves of one designed completeness. He did not create two independent things that happen to coexist. He created a *relationship*, a built-in incompleteness, a need stitched into the structure of the living world.

The classical commentators sharpen this in two illuminating directions. Al-Jalalayn glosses *al-zawjayn* not as "the two mates" (as in two individuals) but as *al-sinfayn* — **the two kinds**, the two categories of existence. Allah did not merely pair two creatures; He originated two entire orders of being — male-kind and female-kind — reaching across every species, every epoch, all the living world at once. The *zawjayn* is the architecture of biological life itself, not a single matched couple. And al-Ṭabarī, describing what Allah did with the zawjayn, reaches for a word that presses beyond ordinary making: *ibtadaʿa* — He **originated** them, brought them into being from absolute novelty, with no prior template to follow. Not assembled from existing stock, not fashioned by rearranging what was already there. *Ibtadaʿa* is radical origination — beginning-with-no-precedent. The two categories of creation are not a derivation of anything before them. They are a first act, in every generation, repeated billions of times.

Here's what's remarkable. Run this all the way out. Everything Allah creates, He creates as a *zawj* — as one-of-a-pair, as a thing that leans. Elsewhere He says it flatly: *wa min kulli shayʾin khalaqnā zawjayn* — "and of everything We created pairs" (51:49). Day leans on night. Land leans on sea. The male leans on the female. Nothing in the created order is self-sufficient. Everything is *zawj* — half of something, needing its other half.

And now ask the question the word is begging you to ask: **what is the one thing in existence that is NOT a *zawj*?**

What is the one Reality that has no counterpart, no matching half, no other it leans on, that stands utterly and completely alone, needing nothing?

> *Allah. Al-Wāḥid. Al-Aḥad. Al-Witr — the One who is single, never a pair.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? The grammar handed us tawhīd. The very moment you understand that to be created is to be a *zawj* — to be half, to be incomplete, to need — you understand in the same breath that **the only One who is not half of anything is Allah.** The word that describes you is the word that excludes Him. Your pairedness is the fingerprint of your createdness. And His singleness is the signature of His divinity.

The Quraysh wanted partners *for* Allah — they wanted Him to have daughters, consorts, a divine family. And the structure of this ayah answers them at the level of grammar: *pairing is the mark of the made thing. To be paired is to be a creature. The Creator is the one thing in all of being that is not a zawj.*

That is what this single word reveals. Hold it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Al-Dhakara wa l-Unthā* — The Naming That Levels

Now look at what follows the pair: *al-dhakara wa l-unthā* — **the male and the female.**

A grammar note first, simple but it matters. After Allah says *al-zawjayn* — "the two mates" — He could have stopped. The sentence is complete. Instead He adds *al-dhakara wa l-unthā* as what Arabic grammarians call a **badal** — a clarifying apposition, a restatement that zooms in. It's as if He says "the pair —" and then, so there is no ambiguity, "— I mean the male, and the female." He spells the pair out into its two named halves.

And here is the first thing to notice: **both nouns carry the definite article.** Not *dhakaran wa unthā* — "a male and a female," vague and indefinite. But *al-dhakara wa l-unthā* — **THE male and THE female.** Each is named, definite, given its full weight. Neither is a footnote to the other. The grammar places them on **one line, in the same case, with the same definiteness, joined by a simple "and."** Total parity. No hierarchy in the syntax. No "and also, secondarily, the female." Just *the* male and *the* female, side by side, two equal objects of one creating verb.

Now feel the rebuke reassemble itself.

Twenty verses earlier: *a-lakumu l-dhakaru wa lahu l-unthā* — "Is the male for you, and for Him the female?" (53:21). There, the Quraysh had ranked them — male above, female below, the higher kept for the self, the lower flung at God. The two words stood in a relationship of *contempt*.

Here: *al-dhakara wa l-unthā* — the male and the female — and now they stand in a relationship of **equal authorship.** Same verb — *khalaqa*, He created — reaches both. Same origin, as we're about to see — one drop — produces both. The hierarchy the Quraysh built has been quietly dismantled, not by argument but by *placement*. Allah set the two words down next to each other as twins of His making, and the ranking simply has nowhere left to stand.

[PAUSE]

There is a tafsīr precision here worth honoring, because the classical scholars read this with great care. Al-Qurṭubī notes that "from a drop" refers to **the children of Ādam** — the descendants — and *not* to Ādam and Ḥawwāʾ themselves, because the first two human beings were not formed from a *nuṭfa*; Ādam was shaped from clay, and Ḥawwāʾ from Ādam. And the scholars widen the lens: al-Baghawī and the *Muyassar* read *al-zawjayn* to include **the male and female of every living creature** — humans *and* the animals — *min kulli ḥayawān*. So the scope of this verse is enormous. Every paired, breathing, reproducing thing on this earth — every stag and doe, every drake and hen, every man and woman after the first parents — all of them, two by two, **created.** None of them self-made. The whole teeming, pairing, mating world is one signature, repeated billions of times: *made, made, made.*

What this single naming reveals: the categories the world uses to rank and to shame — male over female, this kind over that — collapse the instant you trace both halves back to the same Hand and the same drop. **He named them equal so that we would stop ranking them.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Idhā Tumnā* — The Drop You Did Not Command

Now the third window, and this is where everything we have built comes to a point. The pair, the male and the female, comes — *min nuṭfatin idhā tumnā* — **"from a drop of fluid, when it is emitted."**

Two words to open: *nuṭfa* and *tumnā*.

*Nuṭfa* (from **n-ṭ-f**) is one of the Quran's most deliberately humbling words. It does not mean a mighty seed or a noble essence. The root sense is a *single tiny drop* — the bead of water left clinging when you tip out a bucket, the last drop trembling on a lip. It is the Quran's chosen word for the most negligible quantity imaginable. And the Quran *insists* on this word for human origin, again and again, because it wants you to feel the gap. Elsewhere it makes the taunt explicit: *a-lam yaku nuṭfatan min maniyyin yumnā* — "Was he not a drop of fluid emitted?" (75:37). *From this you came.* The towering pair, male and female, the entire ranked and proud human race — **out of a drop you could not even see clearly.**

Now the verb: *tumnā*. And here we have to slow all the way down, because this one word is carrying the freight.

Let me teach the form first. Arabic verbs come in patterns — Forms I through X — and each pattern bends the basic meaning of a root in a known direction. Our verb is **Form IV** of the root **m-n-y** (م ن ي). Form IV characteristically makes a verb *transitive* or *causative* — it makes the action reach out and *do* something to an object. The base sense of this root in Form IV — *amnā* — is **to emit, to pour forth** the fluid. And our word, *tumnā*, is that Form IV verb **in the passive voice.** Not "it emits." **"It IS emitted."**

And both al-Jalalayn and the *Muyassar* are precise about where this emission is directed: *tusabbu fi l-raḥim* — it is **poured into the womb**. Not ejected into emptiness. Channeled, purposively, into its appointed place. The drop does not wander. It is poured — and the word *raḥim*, womb, shares its root with *raḥma*, mercy. From the very first moment, before you are anything at all, the drop that will become you is poured into the architecture of divine mercy.

Feel the passive. The drop does not emit itself by its own will, and *you* — the proud human reading this — were not the agent either. You were not consulted. You did not decide. There was a moment, before you were anything, when *you were what is emitted* — acted upon, set in motion, poured out — by a process you neither designed nor authorized nor were even present for. **The grammar strips you of authorship over your own existence.** The passive voice is the whole argument: *you did not make you.*

And al-Ṭabarī presses this further still. Reading the immediately preceding ayah — "He gives life" (53:44) — directly alongside ours, he is explicit about what "giving life" means in this context: **breathing the spirit into the dead drop** — *nafakha l-rūḥa fi l-nuṭfati l-mayyita*. The drop is not merely tiny. It is *lifeless*. It has no animating power, no will, no spark of its own. Before the divine act, you were not a dim or embryonic version of yourself. You were dead matter. And then a breath — not yours, not your parents', not any created being's — entered the drop, and the person who reads, and grieves, and argues, and loves, came into being. **Not just passive. Dead. Until He willed otherwise.**

[PAUSE]

And here is the layer beneath the layer — and I'll mark it honestly as an interpretive extension that the *lexicon* opens, not bare grammar. The root **م-ن-ي** is one of those astonishing Arabic roots that holds two meanings in one fist. Yes, *manā / amnā* means to emit, to pour out the drop. But the lexicographers — al-Rāghib among them — note that the deep sense of م-ن-ي is **to measure out, to apportion, to ordain.** From this root comes *munyah* and *umniyya* — a wish, a thing you measure out in your hopes. And from this root comes *maniyya* — **death, the appointed end, fate itself**, because death is what is *measured out* for you. The same root names the drop you begin from *and* the decree of your ending.

[PAUSE]

Do you hear it? The word for the beginning, *tumnā* — the drop emitted — sits on the same root as the word for the destiny, the appointed term that was *measured out* for you before you arrived. The Arabic is whispering that the drop and the decree are one act. **In the very moment you were emitted, you were apportioned.** The fluid that began you and the fate written for you come from the same divine measuring-out. You did not author the drop; you do not author the destiny. Both were *manā* — measured for you — by the One who is not a *zawj*.

What this verb reveals: your existence opens with a passive verb and a measured-out drop, which means it opens with a confession — *I am not the author here.* And if you were not the author of your beginning, the question the next ayah is about to ask becomes unanswerable for the proud: *who, then, do you imagine authors your end, and your return?*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Step back and see what the three windows have shown us, woven into two short ayahs.

The **number** — *al-zawjayn*, the dual, the pair — told us that to be created is to be half-of-something, to be incomplete by design, and therefore that the only One who is not a pair is Allah. The **naming** — *al-dhakara wa l-unthā* — took two words the world uses to rank and to shame, and laid them down as equals under one creating verb. And the **verb** — *idhā tumnā*, "when it is emitted," passive, measured out — stripped us of authorship over our own beginning and tied that beginning, by the root, to our ordained end.

Three windows. One message: **You are a made, paired, measured-out thing — and the One who made you is none of those.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about you, your pride, and the day you will be brought back.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Pair That Proves You Are Not God

Let me start where you live.

There is a hunger in the modern self that no previous generation has fed quite so aggressively: the hunger to be **self-made.** "Self-made millionaire." "I am the author of my own story." "I built myself from nothing." We have made a religion of self-authorship — the conviction that I am the origin of my own value, my own meaning, my own existence; that I owe nothing, lean on nothing, need nothing; that I stand alone.

And there is something in us that *wants* this to be true, because to need is to be vulnerable, and to be vulnerable is terrifying. So we perform self-sufficiency. We curate the image of the person who has it all handled, who depends on no one, who is complete.

Now hold that performance up against one Arabic word: ***zawj.***

The ayah says you are a *zawj* — one half of a pair. And the word means: *a thing built to be incomplete. A thing that points beyond itself. A thing that, by its very nature, leans.* You were not made to stand alone. You were made — biologically, at the level of the cell — to need a counterpart even to *exist*. The next generation does not come from you; it comes from you *and* an other, from the pair, from the *zawjayn*. Your own continuation is proof that you are not enough by yourself.

[PAUSE]

And here is the move I want you to feel. We usually experience our neediness as a *flaw* — something to hide, to overcome, to apologize for. The self-made myth treats dependence as a failure state. But this ayah reframes it completely. Your pairedness, your built-in incompleteness, your *leaning* — **this is not your shame. It is your signature as a creature. It is the very mark that distinguishes you from God.**

Think about it as a kind of relief. The pressure to be self-sufficient, self-originating, self-complete — that pressure is the pressure to be *God*. It is a weight no creature can carry, and we break ourselves trying. And the ayah lifts it off your shoulders with one word. *You are a zawj. You were never supposed to be the One. Only Allah is Witr — single, needing nothing, leaning on no one.* Your need is not a defect in the design. **Your need IS the design.** It is the truest thing about you, and it is pointing, every second, away from yourself and toward the only One who needs nothing.

The person who finally accepts that they are a *zawj* — half, leaning, incomplete — is finally free, because they have stopped auditioning for a role that was never theirs.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes where you go when you feel the lack. When you feel incomplete — and you will, today, in some specific way — you have two options. You can read the incompleteness as a problem to be solved by *more self* — more achievement, more control, more performance — and grind yourself against a wall that does not move. Or you can read it the way the ayah reads it: *this lack is the truest thing about me; it is the shape of a creature; it is an arrow.* And you can follow the arrow to its only destination — the One who is not a pair. **Turn your incompleteness into a direction, not a wound.** That is the practical inheritance of the word *zawj*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Drop Is the Proof of the Return

Now the second theme, and to feel it we have to read just one verse past ours — because Allah Himself does. Right after "the male and the female, from a drop when it is emitted," He says:

> *wa anna ʿalayhi l-nashʾata l-ukhrā* — **"And that upon Him is the second creation"** (53:47).

That word — *upon Him*, *ʿalayhi* — is breathtaking. The second creation, the resurrection, is *upon Him* — He has bound it to Himself as something owed, something certain. And notice *where* He places this promise: **immediately after the drop.** The drop is not a separate topic. The drop is the *argument* for the resurrection. Let me show you why, by going back to a denier standing in Mecca.

The thing the Quraysh could not swallow was resurrection. *We die, we rot, we become dust and scattered bone — and you tell us we will be put back together? Reassembled? Re-created?* It struck them as absurd. Impossible. The Quran records their sneer in many places: *who will give life to bones when they are decayed?* (36:78).

And the Quran's answer, again and again, is not a new miracle. It is a *memory.* **Look at how you started.**

You started as a *nuṭfa* — a drop. A thing so small, so formless, so utterly *not-you* that if you had seen it you would never have believed it could become a person who reads, weeps, argues, loves, and denies resurrection. And yet here you are. The One who drew **the entire paired world — male and female, human and animal, the whole ranked and reasoning race — out of a single emitted drop** is now telling you that bringing you back from dust is, if anything, *the easier of the two acts.* As the Quran says elsewhere, *wa huwa ahwanu ʿalayh* — "and that is easier for Him" (30:27).

[PAUSE]

Here is the argument in its full force, and feel how the passive verb *tumnā* drives it home. You did not author your first creation. *Tumnā* — you *were emitted*, acted upon, with no say, no presence, no power. You contributed *nothing* to your own beginning. So on what grounds do you imagine your *return* depends on your power, your consent, your participation? It never did the first time. The God who began you while you were a will-less drop does not need your cooperation — or your belief — to begin you again. **Your own origin is the receipt.** It is the proof, written into your body's history, that you are the kind of thing that gets brought into being from almost-nothing by a Will that is not yours.

And now bring back everything from Part One. The drop was *measured out* — *tumnā*, from the root that also names the *appointed term*, the decreed end. The same divine measuring that poured out your beginning has measured out your ending — and measured out, beyond the ending, your return. Beginning, end, and second beginning: one continuous act of a single Measurer who never lost the thread. You think death scatters you beyond recovery. But the One who apportioned the drop has *also already apportioned the gathering.* It is, in the deepest sense, the same decree.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this personal, because resurrection can feel abstract until you turn it on your own anxieties. Part of what makes the modern heart resist the return is the secret fear that we are *too far gone* — too broken, too scattered, too lost to ever be made whole. We look at the wreckage of a year, a decade, a self, and something whispers: *you cannot come back from this. There is no reassembling what you have become.*

And the ayah answers that whisper with the drop. *You think you are too scattered to be remade? You were once a single drop, emitted, formless, nothing — and He made you a whole person, paired, named, alive.* The God of the *nuṭfa* is in the business of making wholeness out of next-to-nothing. That is not a one-time trick. It is His signature. The same power that built a human from a drop can rebuild a heart from its ruins, and a body from its dust. **Nothing about you is too scattered for the One who started you as a drop.**

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes the weight of your deeds and the size of your hope at the same time. If the return is *real* and *upon Him* — certain, owed, coming — then nothing you do today is weightless; it is all traveling toward the second creation. And if the God of the drop can rebuild you from anything, then no failure today is final; the door of return is open as long as you are breathing. **Live as someone who will be put back together** — by the same Hand, with the same ease, that once made you from a drop you did not command.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now let me show you the structure, because we earned the right to see it only by doing the work first.

Go back to the beginning of the surah. The Quraysh worshipped three goddesses, and the third — *Manāt* — was the goddess of **fate and death.** Her very name comes from the root م-ن-ي / م-ن-و: *manā*, to apportion, to measure out a destiny. They imagined that this idol *manat* — decreed — the spans of their lives and the hour of their deaths. They had outsourced their fate to a carved stone named "the Apportioner."

And the surah took her apart. *"Have you considered… Manāt, the third, the other one?"* (53:20). *These are nothing but names you and your fathers invented.* (53:23).

Then, verses later, comes our passage. And listen to the last word again: *idhā* ***tumnā*** — "when it is emitted." Same root. **م-ن-ي.** The root of the goddess of fate is the root of the verb for the emitted drop.

[PAUSE]

Here is the architecture. The Quraysh said: *Manāt apportions our destinies.* And Allah replies, with a verb that rhymes with her name: *No. I am the One who apportioned the drop — tumnā — from which I made the male and the female, every pair on this earth. I measure out the beginning. I measure out the end — the maniyya, the appointed death. And upon Me is the second beginning. The Apportioning you ascribed to a stone is Mine, from the first drop to the final gathering.*

The dead goddess of fate is answered by the living God of the drop, in a single chosen word. *Manāt* could not emit so much as one *nuṭfa*. The One who *tumnā*'d the drop holds your whole arc — birth, death, and return — in His measuring Hand. That is the surah's hidden hinge, and it was sitting in the sound of the last word the whole time.

### Bringing It Together

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the two themes are one insight, approached from two sides.

The **grammar** said: you are a *zawj* — paired, incomplete, leaning — and you were *tumnā* — emitted, measured out, not the author. The **themes** said: therefore stop performing self-sufficiency, because your need is the mark of a creature and the arrow toward your Lord; and therefore stop fearing the return, because the God who made you from a drop you did not command will remake you with ease you cannot imagine.

It is the same single truth, said two ways: **You did not make yourself, and you will not unmake yourself. The One who paired you and poured you out from a drop holds your beginning, your ending, and your return — and He, alone in all of existence, is not a pair.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I still performing the role of the self-made, self-sufficient one — the role that was never mine, because I am a *zawj*, built to lean?

2. When I feel incomplete this week, will I read the lack as a wound to be hidden, or as an arrow pointing me to the only One who needs nothing?

3. I contributed *nothing* to my first creation — I was *tumnā*, emitted, acted upon. So why do I live as though my return, and my standing before Allah, depends on my being in control?

4. Is there a part of my life, my faith, or my self that I have written off as "too scattered to come back"? What does the God of the drop say to that?

5. What have I quietly handed over to my own "Manāt" — some idol of fate, luck, fortune, or self — that in truth belongs to the One who apportioned my every drop?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The God who paired the whole living world into male and female and drew that pairing from a single drop you never commanded has measured out your beginning, your end, and your return — so your incompleteness is your direction home, and your origin is the proof that you will be brought back.

### The Closing Invitation

You came in a pair, which means you were never the One. You came from a drop, which means you were never the author. And you are owed a second creation, which means you were never going to simply end. Let something shift in how you carry yourself tomorrow: a little less of the exhausting performance of self-sufficiency, a little more of the relief of being a creature who is held. Let your needs become prayers instead of shames. And when the fear of the return, or the fear of being too far gone, rises in you — answer it with the drop. *He made me from that. He will not lose me now.*

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who poured me out from a drop I did not command, and paired me, and named me — let me stop pretending I am the One. You alone are *Al-Witr*, single, needing nothing; I am a *zawj*, leaning, needing You. As You measured out my beginning, You have measured out my end and my return to You — so make my return a homecoming, gather what time has scattered in me, and remake from this dust a heart worthy of standing before You. *Āmīn.*
