# The City That Turned Itself Over: When Desire Becomes Gravity

> وَٱلْمُؤْتَفِكَةَ أَهْوَىٰ ۝ فَغَشَّىٰهَا مَا غَشَّىٰ
>
> *wal-muʾtafikata ahwā · fa-ghashshāhā mā ghashshā*
>
> **"And the overturned city — He cast it down. So there covered it that which covered."**

---

## Introduction: The Scroll That Was Read Aloud in Makkah

Before we open these words, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

Surah An-Najm was among the earliest revelations recited openly in Makkah. There is a report that it was the first surah the Prophet ﷺ recited publicly at the Kaʿbah, and that when he reached its final word — the command to prostrate — even the disbelievers around him fell into sajdah, almost against their will, overtaken by something they could not name.

So this is not a quiet, private surah. This is the Quran walking into the public square.

And notice *what kind* of surah it is. It opens with an oath by the star as it sets. It defends the Prophet ﷺ — "your companion has not strayed, nor does he speak from **desire** (هَوَىٰ)." It ascends through the night journey, the lote-tree at the boundary, the greatest signs of the Lord. And then, near the end, it does something striking: it pulls out an ancient document. It says — *have you not been told what is in the scrolls of Mūsā, and of Ibrāhīm who fulfilled?*

What follows is a list. A roll-call of nations Allah destroyed. ʿĀd. Thamūd. The people of Nūḥ. And then — these two ayahs.

Here is the world I need you to inhabit. The Makkans listening to this had a trade route. Twice a year their caravans went north, "a journey in winter and a journey in summer" (106:2). And that northern route ran *past the ruins*. Past the region of the Dead Sea, the overturned land of the people of Lūṭ. They had seen it. The stillness of it. A place where something catastrophic had happened, frozen in the landscape.

So when the Quran says *al-muʾtafikah* — "the overturned" — it is not describing a legend. It is pointing out the window of the caravan.

[PAUSE]

**Here is the hook.** In this list, every nation is given its name. ʿĀd is named. Thamūd is named. The people of Nūḥ are named. But when the recital reaches the people of Lūṭ, Allah does *not* name them. He calls them only by what happened to them: *the overturned*. And in the very next breath, when He describes the punishment that fell, He refuses to tell us what it was: *"there covered it — that which covered."*

Two refusals. A withheld name. A withheld description. Five words in Arabic, two of them deliberately empty.

Why would the most precise speech ever spoken suddenly go silent in exactly two places? That silence is where we are going to live today.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *al-Muʾtafikah* — The City Named After Its Own Collapse

Let me first teach you something about how Arabic builds a word, because it will change how you hear this one.

In English, when we name a thing, the name is usually arbitrary. "London" doesn't *mean* anything about London. But Arabic names things by their action or their quality. A noun can be carved directly out of a verb, so that the name carries inside it *what the thing does* or *what was done to it*.

Now, the word here is **ٱلْمُؤْتَفِكَة** — *al-muʾtafikah*. Its root is **أ-ف-ك** (*a-f-k*). And the basic meaning of this root is: **to turn something over, to overturn, to flip, to invert** — to take what was upright and put its top where its bottom was.

Listen to how al-Qurṭubī explains it: *"iʾtafakat bihim"* — it turned over **with them** — *"ay inqalabat wa ṣāra ʿāliyahā sāfilahā"* — it flipped, so that its highest part became its lowest part. *"Afaktuhu ay qalabtuhu wa ṣaraftuhu"* — I overturned it, that is, I flipped it and diverted it.

So *al-muʾtafikah* means: **the overturned one. The flipped-over city.**

Al-Jalalayn notes: *qurā qawmi Lūṭ* — not one city but *the villages* of Lūṭ's people, a cluster of settlements. An entire civilization condensed into a single name. Not "the destroyed settlements" or "the sinful cities." Just: *the overturned*.

But stay with me, because there are two things hidden in this word.

**First — the form.** This word is built on Form VIII (*iftaʿala*), the pattern *muʾtafikah*. Form VIII in Arabic very often carries a **reflexive or middle sense** — the action folding back onto the doer. Not simply "the city *that was* overturned" as if it were a passive victim, but, as al-Qurṭubī's phrase *iʾtafakat* suggests, **"the city that turned itself over."** The destruction is described as something the city *did to itself*, something latent in its own structure that finally gave way.

Sit with that. Allah does not name the city "al-mahdūmah" — the demolished. He does not use a purely passive form. He uses a form that whispers: *this collapse came from within.*

[PAUSE]

**Second — and this is what I want you to carry home — look at what else this exact root produces.**

The root أ-ف-ك, the root of "overturning," is the very same root of the word **إِفْك** — *ifk*. And do you know what *ifk* means?

It means **a lie. A slander. Falsehood.**

It is the word the Quran uses for *al-ifk al-mubīn*, the great slander (24:11). A lie, in the Arabic imagination, is not the absence of truth — it is **truth turned upside down**. It is reality *overturned*. To lie is to take the upright thing and flip it.

Now feel the full weight of the name. The people of Lūṭ were a society that had **inverted everything**. They had turned the natural upright. They had turned the moral upside down. They had taken the guest, who in their world was sacred, and made him prey. They had taken what was meant for life and bent it toward sterility. They lived in a permanent state of *ifk* — of overturned truth.

And so when the punishment came, Allah did not invent a new form of destruction for them. **He simply made physical what they had already made of themselves.** A people who had overturned reality were given a city that was overturned. The *muʾtafikah* — the inverted city — was the inevitable, honest, *external shape* of an inverted soul.

That is what this single word reveals: **the punishment was not added to the crime. The punishment was the crime, made visible.**

### Element Two: *Ahwā* — The Root That Means Both "to Fall" and "to Desire"

Now the second word: **أَهْوَىٰ** — *ahwā*. "He cast it down."

Again, let me teach the structure first.

The root is **ه-و-ي** (*h-w-y*). At its base, this root means **to fall, to drop, to descend, to plunge downward**. The intransitive verb *hawā* means *it fell*. You can hear the descent in the sound of the word itself — *hawā* — it has the shape of something dropping through air and exhaling on the way down.

The word in our ayah, *ahwā*, is **Form IV**. And here we must be careful and precise, because Form IV does several different jobs in Arabic. Here, its job is **causative-transitive** — and we know this not by guessing but because al-Qurṭubī tells us so explicitly. He says: *"hawā bi-l-fatḥ yahwī huwiyyan ay saqaṭa"* — *hawā* means *it fell* — *"wa ahwā ay asqaṭa"* — and *ahwā* means **He made it fall. He cast it down.**

So the picture the commentators paint — al-Jalalayn, al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī all agree — is this: the city was **raised up** first, lifted toward the sky, and then **dropped**. Made to fall from a height. *Ahwā* — He sent it plunging down.

Al-Jalalayn is specific about how: Jibrīl — the angel of revelation, the carrier of the word from heaven to earth — was commanded to do this. He lifted the cities toward the sky *maqluubah* — **inverted**, upside down — and then released them to the earth in that configuration. They traveled through the air above the region of the Dead Sea already in the position they would land in. *Al-muʾtafikah* was not just the name for what they became. It was the posture they held on their descent. The same angel whose name the Makkans knew as the source of this very surah was the instrument of that plunge.

That alone is a terrifying image. But now I have to show you the thing that makes this word a thunderclap.

[PAUSE]

This same root — ه-و-ي, the root of *falling* — is the root of one of the most important words in this entire surah. It is the root of **هَوَىٰ** — *hawā*.

And *hawā* means: **desire. Caprice. The lowly whim of the self. Following what you feel like, against what is true.**

Why would Arabic use *the same root* for "falling down" and for "desire"?

Because the ancient Arabs, and the Quran after them, understood something we are only now relearning: **desire is a descent.** Imam al-Rāghib al-Aṣfahānī explains in his *Mufradāt* that the whim of the self is called *hawā* precisely because it *casts down* (yahwī) its possessor — in this world into ruin, and in the next into the Fire. Desire is named after gravity. To follow your *hawā* is to obey a downward pull.

Now go back to the surah itself. Hold these in your hand:

- In the third ayah, defending the Prophet ﷺ: *"wa mā yanṭiqu ʿani **l-hawā**"* — "he does not speak from **desire**." The Prophet is the man who does *not* obey the downward pull.
- A few ayahs later, condemning the idolaters (53:23): *"in yattabiʿūna illā ẓ-ẓanna wa mā **tahwā** l-anfus"* — "they follow nothing but conjecture and **what the selves desire**." Same root. *Tahwā* — what the selves *fall toward*.

So the surah has already, twice, diagnosed the disease: **following hawā — letting your desire be your gravity.**

And now, at the climax, comes the verdict. The city of a people who lived for their *hawā* — *ahwā*. He made it **fall**.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel it? **The same root that named their sin names their sentence.** They followed the downward pull of desire — *hawā* — and so Allah gave their world to the downward pull — *ahwā*. Their *hawā* had already been pulling them down their whole lives. The punishment merely let the rest of the universe finally agree with the direction they had chosen.

This is not a pun. This is theology written into the bones of the language. **Sin has a direction. That direction is down. And a life lived in that direction is, eventually, simply allowed to arrive.**

### Element Three: *Mā Ghashshā* — The Sentence That Refuses to Finish

Now the second ayah: **فَغَشَّىٰهَا مَا غَشَّىٰ** — *fa-ghashshāhā mā ghashshā*. "So there covered it — that which covered."

Let me teach the device, because it is one of the most powerful in all of Arabic rhetoric, and once you see it you will see it everywhere in the Quran.

The verb **غَشَّىٰ** — *ghashshā* — is from the root غ-ش-ي, meaning **to cover, to envelop, to overwhelm, to shroud**. It is Form II, an intensifying form — not a light covering but a *total* enveloping, a smothering. The same root gives us *ghishāwah*, the veil over the heart, and *al-ghāshiyah*, the Overwhelming — a name for the Day of Judgment.

Now look at the structure of the sentence. *"There covered it"* — covered it with **what?**

Arabic, like English, expects an object here. *Covered it with* — fire? Stones? Darkness? The sentence is leaning forward, waiting for the noun that names the horror.

And what does Allah put in that slot? **مَا** — *mā*. The relative word: *"that which."*

*"There covered it — that which covered."*

[PAUSE]

He withholds it. He builds the entire sentence to point at the thing, and then, at the exact moment of revelation, He **names it only by the fact that it happened.** What covered them? *That which covered them.* The sentence circles back on itself and tells you nothing — and in telling you nothing, it tells you everything.

This is a deliberate device. The scholars of *balāghah* call it **ibhām** — intentional ambiguity, used for *taʿẓīm* (to magnify) and *tahwīl* (to terrify). It is the rhetoric of the unspeakable. When a thing is so enormous that no description could contain it, the most powerful move is to **refuse to describe it** and let the listener's own imagination fall into the gap.

Think of how this works in your own life. When someone who has witnessed something unbearable says, *"What I saw there… I can't even tell you,"* — that sentence terrifies you more than any detail could. Because your mind, given an empty room, fills it with everything it most fears. The undescribed is always larger than the described.

The classical commentators, for their part, did not stay with the silence. Al-Muyassar reaches into what companion-sourced tradition preserved and names what the Quran withheld: *al-ḥijāra al-mutatābiʿa al-nāzila ʿalayhim mina l-samāʾi ka-l-maṭar* — stones raining down upon them from the sky continuously, like rain. The same stones described in Sūrah Hūd (11:82). And yet — notice what happens when you know this. The knowing does not lift the dread of *mā ghashshā*. If anything, the image of heaven raining stones without ceasing, as if the sky had simply decided this was now the weather, deepens it. The named detail is always smaller than the unnamed space the Quran carved out. The scholars named what they could. The Quran's silence was the more unsettling choice.

This is the only other place the Quran uses almost this exact construction: of Pharaoh's army at the sea, *"fa-ghashiyahum mina l-yammi mā ghashiyahum"* — "there covered them, of the sea, that which covered them" (20:78). The same shrug of horror. The same closed door.

So Allah ends the recital of Lūṭ's people not with a description, but with a sealed envelope. *That which covered.* And the sentence does not finish — because the reality it points to **cannot be finished**.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Al-muʾtafikah* — a people defined by inversion, given an inverted city.
*Ahwā* — a people who followed the downward pull, made to fall.
*Mā ghashshā* — a horror so total it can only be named by its own occurrence.

Three precise instruments. But they are not just telling us about an old city by the Dead Sea. They are telling us about **the structure of reality itself** — about how sin works, how justice works, and what Allah chooses to say and chooses to leave unsaid. Let us ask now what these words reveal not about language, but about *us*.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Your Inner Orientation Is Building Your Outer World

Let me start where you actually live.

You have known people whose lives slowly collapsed. Not in a single catastrophe — but structurally. A person who, for years, made small inversions. Called the harmful thing "freedom." Called the obligation "oppression." Called the warning "negativity." Each inversion felt like nothing. A small turning of the upright. And then one day the whole structure came down, and from the outside it looked sudden — but it wasn't sudden. It had been *muʾtafikah* the whole time.
